Ancestral Sequence Reconstruction Provides Insights into the Structural Diversification and Neofunctionalization of T-superfamily Conotoxins in Conus
Authors: Samuel S Espino, Thomas Lund Koch, Joanna Gajewiak, Matías L Giglio, Maren Watkins, Helena Safavi-Hemami, Baldomero M Olivera, Mary O'Connell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDue to their remarkable diversity and rapid evolution, conotoxins—peptide toxins from predatory marine cone snails—provide a powerful system for exploring how gene diversification may contribute to the development of lineage-specific adaptations. We previously demonstrated that 2-loop Tau conotoxins represent an evolutionary innovation associated with mollusk-hunting behaviors in cone snails. Here, we investigate the evolutionary history of these toxins as a model to understand the mechanism of ancestral gene neofunctionalization, which may have contributed to the emergence of mollusk-hunting in cone snails. Using ancestral sequence reconstruction, we present a model in which ancestral T-superfamily conotoxins neofunctionalized into the 2-loop Tau conotoxins. Predicted ancestral sequences reveal an intermediate structure between the classic T-superfamily conotoxins and the derived 2-loop Tau forms. Notably, these ancestral intermediates acquired a new cysteine scaffold that facilitated a structural transition from a globular to a ribbon fold. This conformational shift was followed by sequence-level changes that presumably enhanced activity against molecular targets in mollusks. We propose that the emergence of 2-loop Tau conotoxins may have been one factor that contributed to the emergence of molluscivory, providing insight into how gene innovation may underlie ecological diversification.Keywords: ancestral sequence reconstruction, conotoxin, gene neofunctionalization, structural diversification, lineage specific adaptation, Cone snail

Introduction

Novel phylogenetic lineages often emerge in response to ecological opportunities. For instance, geologic upheavals can generate new ecological niches that existing organisms can colonize and subsequently speciate in (Williams and Duda 2008; Hou and Li 2018). To thrive and take advantage of such opportunities, organisms can adapt by restructuring and repurposing already present genes (Andersson et al. 2015). Here, we present a model illustrating how these processes likely occurred in mollusk-hunting Conus, culminating in the emergence of χ-conotoxins and the broader 2-loop Tau conotoxins, which the snails presumably use to capture prey.

The estimated 1,000 species in the genus Conus are presumed to occupy the ecological niche once thought to be dominated by the ammonites, one of the major predators in warm shallow seas (Olivera et al. 2017). These snails are broadly grouped into three categories based on their prey: worm-hunters (vermivores), fish-hunters (piscivores), and mollusk-hunters (molluscivores) (Kohn 1959; Rockel et al. 1995; Duda et al. 2001; Olivera et al. 2014). Cone snails use venom to capture prey and defend against predators and competitors (Olivera et al. 1985; Olivera 2002, 2006). Venom components are diverse and known to target voltage- and ligand-gated ion channels, receptors, such as G-protein coupled receptors and tyrosine kinases, and transporters (Hillyard et al. 1992; Craig et al. 1999b; McIntosh et al. 2000; Sharpe et al. 2001; Ellison et al. 2006; Platt et al. 2014; Safavi-Hemami et al. 2015; Espino et al. 2018; Espiritu et al. 2023).

The venom from each cone snail species is a complex mixture of mostly small (15 to 30 amino acids), gene-encoded, bioactive peptides known as conotoxins or conopeptides that are evolving under positive selection (Woodward et al. 1990; Duda and Palumbi 1999; Olivera 2006; Phuong and Mahardika 2018). As a result, distantly related species have a distinct complement of toxins with minimal sequence overlap (Olivera 1997; Thapa et al. 2014; Grandal et al. 2021). Closely related species, however, have been shown to express functionally homologous venom components that can be identical or differ by only a few amino acids (Barghi et al. 2015; Li et al. 2018). Conotoxins can be grouped into gene superfamilies, and each superfamily can be divided into pharmacological families (Terlau and Olivera 2004; Robinson and Norton 2014). Each superfamily is characterized by a highly conserved signal sequence, a relatively conserved propeptide region, and a hypervariable toxin region (Woodward et al. 1990; Olivera 2006). However, the pattern of cysteine residues in the toxin sequence is typically conserved within each gene superfamily (Hillyard et al. 1989; Woodward et al. 1990; Olivera 2006).

All living cone snails are thought to have evolved from ancestral worm-hunting snails, and most extant species have retained the ancestral worm-hunting trait (Duda et al. 2001; Duda and Palumbi 2004; Kohn 2018). Evidence based on superfamily gene expression patterns revealed that venom composition correlates with prey type in Conus (Koch et al. 2024). Although some gene superfamilies appear more confined to species with specific prey preferences or hunting strategies (Phuong et al. 2016), many superfamilies can be found across the entire genus, with prey-specific pharmacological families likely existing within superfamilies (Espino et al. 2024; Koch et al. 2024). An example is the T-superfamily conotoxins, which are expressed across all Conus feeding groups (Walker et al. 1999; Koch et al. 2024).

Classical/canonical T-superfamily conotoxins are characterized by two pairs of geminal cysteines (Cys) separated by 4 to 6 amino acids, forming a single intercysteine loop and adopting a globular conformation (Mandal et al. 2007; Gupta et al. 2010). Conotoxins in this superfamily have been shown to target ion channels, receptors, and transporters (Sharpe et al. 2001; Liu et al. 2007; Petrel et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2014), although most members have not been functionally characterized. The most intensively studied T-superfamily conotoxin is χ-MrIa from the mollusk hunter Conus marmoreus (Balaji et al. 2000; McIntosh et al. 2000; Sharpe et al. 2001). χ-MrIA defines a new pharmacological family in the T-superfamily—the χ-conotoxin family, which inhibits the norepinephrine transporter (NET) (Sharpe et al. 2001). NET is a biogenic monoamine transporter and a member of the solute carrier 6 family of Na+/Cl- dependent transporters and is closely related to the serotonin transporter (SERT) (Broer and Gether 2012). Recent research identified additional members of this family, including χ-AuID from Conus aulicus (Espino et al. 2024) and χ-PnID from Conus pennaceus (Espiritu et al. 2023).

χ-Conotoxins belong to the broader 2-loop Tau conotoxins, a subgroup within the T-conotoxin gene superfamily (Espino et al. 2024). While χ-conotoxins inhibit NET, other members of the 2-loop Tau subgroup have molecular targets that remain unknown. Two-loop Tau conotoxins are distinguished by a modification of the classical T-conotoxin cysteine framework of CC-X4-6-CC to a new structure represented by CC-Xj-C-Xk-C. Specifically, 2-loop Tau conotoxins are characterized by a CC-X4-CX1-2C motif (Espino et al. 2024), where X denotes an amino acid, and the subscripts represent the number of amino acids present in each intercysteine loop. This structural adaptation represents a significant functional and structural divergence within the T-superfamily.

We hypothesize that χ-conotoxins evolved by neofunctionalization of ancestral T-superfamily toxins with unknown pharmacology to inhibit a molluscan transporter that is homologous to NET. Our previous work has demonstrated that χ-conotoxins, and subsequently other members of the 2-loop Tau conotoxins, evolved exclusively within the monophyletic lineage of molluscivorous cone snails (Espino et al. 2024). The emergence of χ-conotoxins thus coincided with a key ecological transition from vermivory to molluscivory, suggesting that this molecular adaptation may have contributed to optimizing predatory efficiency, coinciding with the shift in prey type.

Although χ-conotoxins are unique to the venom of mollusk-hunting cone snails, the evolutionary history of χ-conotoxin remains unknown. Using ancestral sequence reconstruction (ASR), biochemical characterizations, and in vivo assays, we propose a possible mechanism of how a classical T-conotoxin was transformed structurally and biologically into a 2-loop Tau conotoxin. This ancestral conotoxin likely underwent rearrangement of the cysteine framework, resulting in the tendency to adopt the ribbon conformation while retaining activity in worms. After the ribbon conformation was established, further mutations resulted in low-level biogenic monoamine transporter activity. The optimization of this activity may have led to predatory efficiency in the novel prey, facilitating the emergence of χ-conotoxins and subsequently other members of the 2-loop Tau conotoxins. This transition may have contributed to the successful radiation of mollusk-hunting cone snails in the tropical waters of the Indo-Pacific.

Results

Ancestral Sequence Reconstruction (ASR) of 2-Loop Tau Conotoxins

To gain insights into the biochemical and biological mechanisms behind the emergence of χ-conotoxins and the broader 2-loop Tau conotoxins from the T-superfamily, we employed ASR. ASR allows for statistical inference of ancestral protein sequences from a gene tree, enabling the experimental reconstruction of evolutionary intermediates (Thornton 2004; Hanson-Smith et al. 2010; Merkl and Sterner 2016). By characterizing intermediates, one can explore the biochemical and biological properties of putative ancestral 2-loop Tau conotoxins and identify the specific mutations and structural modifications that may have contributed to a shift in activity. It is important to emphasize that reconstructed sequences represent model-based estimates rather than definitive ancestral states. Thus, while ASR can provide valuable insights into evolutionary patterns, the results must be interpreted with caution as model-based estimates that offer plausible evolutionary scenarios rather than definitive ancestral sequences.

Since the evolution of conotoxins to a large degree is defined by insertion/deletion (indel) mutations (Wu et al. 2013; Yang and Zhou 2020), we used ARPIP, an ASR approach that unlike most programs, specifically models insertions and deletions (Jowkar et al. 2023). Using this approach, we reconstructed ancestral sequences at key evolutionary nodes (Fig. 1a, Figure S1a and b) based on our previous reconstruction of 189 T-superfamily conotoxins, which includes the 2-loop Tau's and χ-conotoxins (Espino et al. 2024) (Figure S2). We selected the following three sequences for further analysis due to their significant position within the tree: ancestral 2-loop Tau_97 (anc2T_97), the common ancestor of all 2-loop tau conotoxins; ancestral 2-loop Tau_55 (anc2T_55), the ancestor of the omaria-cordigera clade (clade A) that features an unusual 2-loop Tau, Cdg1.2, from Conus cordigera; and ancestral 2-loop Tau_96 (anc2T_96), the ancestral sequence of 2-loop Tau conotoxins in clade B (Fig. 1a and c). Ancestral conotoxins derived from anc2T_96 (Figure S1a to c) were not further investigated as they display the cysteine scaffold characteristic of extant 2-loop Tau conotoxins (Fig. 1d) and are thus expected to fold into the ribbon conformation. Previous studies have demonstrated that conservative amino acid substitutions in loop 1 of χ-conotoxins preserve activity against NET, although they may affect the potency of transporter inhibition (Brust et al. 2009). Therefore, the conservative amino acid substitutions observed in loop 1 between the anc2T_96-derived ancestral conotoxins and extant χ-conotoxins (Figure S1c) suggest that these ancestral conotoxins may already possess inhibitory activity against NET. To validate our results, we compared the ARPIP output with ASR from IQ-TREE (Minh et al. 2020) by mapping the insertions and deletions predicted by ARPIP onto the IQ-TREE sequences, as IQ-TREE doesn’t model indels. The results were largely consistent between the two methods, although differences were observed in the predicted amino acids at the amino- and carboxy-termini. For example, IQ-TREE predicts, for anc2T_97, a phenylalanine (Phe) at the N-terminus with a likelihood of approximately 0.6, whereas ARPIP predicts valine (Val), which has a much lower likelihood of 0.03 in the IQ-TREE analysis. At the C-terminus, IQ-TREE predicts alanine (Ala) with a likelihood of 0.16, while ARPIP predicts glutamine (Gln) with a likelihood of 0.03. In anc2T_55, IQ-TREE infers arginine (Arg) with a likelihood of 0.5, at the C-terminus, consistent with a potential processing site, but ARPIP's alternative prediction of glycine (Gly) with a likelihood of 0.44 is nearly equally probable (File S1). The set of alternative predictions from IQ-TREE is presented in Figure S3. It is important to note that the likelihood values reported by IQ-TREE reflect its own substitution-based model and cannot be directly compared to those from ARPIP, which explicitly models insertions and deletions but does not provide per-residue likelihoods. While variation exists between ASR methods at the N- and C-termini, the amino acid sequences within the intercysteine loops—our primary focus—are consistent across both methods. Since we relied on ARPIP-predicted gaps, we base our reconstruction on its output.

Based on their cysteine arrangements, the ancestral conotoxins predicted from ARPIP; anc2T_97 and anc2T_55 appear to be structural intermediates between the classical Tau conotoxins and the extant 2-loop Tau conotoxins (Fig. 1a, c, and d). Anc2T_97, the predicted last common ancestor of all 2-loop Tau conotoxins, contains five amino acids between Cys2 and Cys3, which are typical of classic T-superfamily conotoxins, and a single proline residue between Cys3 and Cys4 (Fig. 1c and d). From anc2T_97, the 2-loop Tau branches into two clades (Fig. 1a, Figures S1a and S2). Anc2T_55 retained most of the structural features of anc2T_97 except for the deletion of glutamine at the C-terminus (Fig. 1c, Figure S1a and b). From this ancestral conotoxin, clade A 2-loop Tau conotoxins emerged. The cysteine pattern and intercysteine loop size in anc2T_96, the ancestor of clade B 2-loop Tau conotoxins, are identical to those of most known 2-loop Tau conotoxins (Fig. 1a, c, and d, Figure S2) (Balaji et al. 2000; McIntosh et al. 2000; Sharpe et al. 2001; Espiritu et al. 2023; Espino et al. 2024).

Our model, based on ASR analysis, proposes that during the evolution of χ-conotoxins and the broader 2-loop Tau conotoxins, the ancestral T-superfamily conotoxin underwent an expansion of loop 2 via the insertion of a proline residue between Cys3 and Cys4 in anc2T_97. Subsequent structural changes include the contraction of loop 1 (between Cys2 and Cys3) to four amino acids and further expansion of loop 2 in clade B. In clade A, loop 1 is similarly reduced to four amino acids, and proline in loop 2 is replaced by the bulkiest amino acid, tryptophan, in the extant conotoxin Cdg1.2. Additional mutations in the amino acid sequence likely improved in vivo activity, enhancing predatory efficiency against mollusks. However, the possibility that some structural divergence arose through neutral evolution cannot be eliminated.

Ancestral 2-Loop Tau Conotoxins Tend to Fold into the Ribbon Conformation

All described T-superfamily conotoxins have four cysteines. While the 2-loop Tau conotoxins have two intercysteine loops, most conotoxins in the T-superfamily (canonical/classical Tau conotoxins) have only one (Walker et al. 1999) (Fig. 1d). Classical Tau conotoxins are stabilized by disulfide bridges between Cys1 and Cys3 and between Cys2 and Cys4, forming a globular structure (Mandal et al. 2007; Gupta et al. 2010) (Fig. 1d). In contrast, the disulfide bridges in the 2-loop Tau conotoxins are formed between Cys1 and Cys4 and between Cys2 and Cys3, resulting in a ribbon conformation (Balaji et al. 2000; McIntosh et al. 2000; Espino et al. 2024) (Fig. 1d).

The resurrection of predicted ancestral conotoxins allowed us to gain insights into the structural transition from globular T-superfamily conotoxins to the ribbon conformation found in 2-loop Tau conotoxins. Specifically, we addressed the following two questions to investigate how structural diversity is possibly generated within the T-superfamily conotoxins: (i) What is the preferred folding conformation of the ancestral conotoxin and (ii) How do changes in loop sizes affect folding?

To address these questions, we first synthesized ancestral conotoxins anc2T_97 and anc2T_55 (Table 1) and constrained them to fold into globular or ribbon conformations. This was achieved by protecting specific cysteine residues in the linear conotoxin with acetamidomethyl (ACM) groups and forming the disulfide bridges in a two-step process (Fig. 2a). Second, we randomly folded the linear conotoxins without ACM protection and determined whether the conotoxins adopted a globular or ribbon conformation (Fig. 2b). Conotoxins with known conformation from step 1 served as standards for co-elution experiments using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Further, two-loop Tau conotoxins previously purified from venom have been shown to contain a post-translational modification (PTM) converting the proline residue in loop 2 to hydroxyproline (Balaji et al. 2000; McIntosh et al. 2000; Sharpe et al. 2001; Espino et al. 2024). We also synthesized ancestral conotoxin analogs in which the proline residue in loop 2 was modified to hydroxyproline. This allowed us to determine whether this PTM affects folding, as has been demonstrated for other conotoxins (Lopez-Vera et al. 2008). The C-terminus of anc2T_55 terminates in glycine, and in extant conotoxins, the Cα-N bond of this amino acid is cleaved, resulting in an amidated C-terminus (Ul-Hasan et al. 2013). Following this convention, anc2T_55 and the corresponding hydroxyproline analogs were synthesized with a C-terminal amide. Directed folding of globular anc2T_97 and anc2T_55 conotoxins produced multiple topological isomers (Figure S4a and e), while a single product was formed from the directed folding of the ribbon anc2T_97 and anc2T_55 conotoxins (Figure S4c and g). The topological isomers of the globular ancestral conotoxins could have resulted from the cis-trans isomerization of proline, which has been shown to occur in some conotoxins (Safavi-Hemami et al. 2010). Similarly, directed folding of the globular conotoxin analogs containing hydroxyproline also resulted in multiple topological isomers, but with a sharper HPLC peak (Figure S4b and f). This PTM does not appear to affect the folding of the ribbon conotoxins, as only a single product is formed in the folding reaction (Figure S4d and h).

Random (undirected) folding of ancestral conotoxins anc2T_97, anc2T_55, and their corresponding hydroxyproline analogs resulted in multiple products forming within 1 to 3 min (Fig. 2c and d). Co-elution experiments involving standard globular and ribbon conotoxins, as well as their hydroxyproline analogs, revealed the formation of both conformations, 1 min after the start of the folding reaction (Figure S5a to d). The ribbon conotoxin appeared to be the favored product because it was present in the folding reaction at a higher percentage, ranging from 19% to 25% (anc2T_97: 25.3 ± 0.6%, n = 3, anc2T_97 [P10O]: 21.7 ± 2.1%, n = 3, anc2T_55: 20.7 ± 2.3%, n = 3, anc2T_55 [P10O]: 19.0 ± 1.0%, n = 3; values represent mean ± SD), when compared to the other products of the reaction (Fig. 2f). It is important to highlight that the folding yields of the ribbon conotoxins are relatively low, and numerous peaks in the folding reaction cannot be definitively categorized as either globular or ribbon conformations, suggesting they may represent folding intermediates. Nevertheless, the introduction of the second intercysteine loop seemed to favor the formation of the ribbon conotoxin.

The anc2T_96 conotoxin was folded following the same strategy as anc2T_97 and anc2T_55. However, the globular form of anc2T_96 conotoxin was not synthesized because it has a cysteine framework identical to the extant 2-loop Tau conotoxins in clade B, which are known to adopt a ribbon conformation (Balaji et al. 2000; Espiritu et al. 2023; Espino et al. 2024) (Fig. 1a, c, and d). We synthesized the conotoxin with hydroxyproline in loop 2, consistent with the modification found in extant 2-loop Tau conotoxins (Balaji et al. 2000; Espiritu et al. 2023). The folding of anc2T_96 demonstrated a slower rate of final product formation compared to anc2T_97 and anc2T_55 conotoxins and their hydroxyproline analogs. Equilibrium among folding products was achieved within ∼10 min for anc2T_96 (Fig. 2e), whereas anc2T_97 and anc2T_55 conotoxins and their hydroxyproline analogs reached equilibrium among the reaction products in 1 to 3 min (Fig. 2c and d). The ribbon isomer was the predominant product for anc2T_96, with a final yield of 44.0 ± 2.8% (mean ± SD, n = 2) (Fig. 2e and f, Figure S5e). Some minor components of the reaction product may represent globular isomers; however, this was not further investigated. These results suggest that the opening of the second intercysteine loop, as observed in anc2T_97 and anc2T_55, favors the formation of the ribbon conformation, albeit with a relatively low folding efficiency. Further expansion of loop 2 from one to two amino acids and contraction of loop 1 from five to four amino acids, as observed in anc2T_96, significantly increased the folding efficiency into the ribbon form (Fig. 2f). Thus, the resurrection of ancestral conotoxins enabled the identification of sequence features that may have facilitated structural diversification within the T-superfamily conotoxins.

Extant Conotoxins and Chimeric Conotoxins Support the Role of Loop 2 in the Folding of 2-Loop Tau Conotoxins

Based on the folding chemistry of predicted ancestral conotoxins, we have shown in vitro that the formation of the second intercysteine loop led to a tendency to adopt a ribbon conformation. Furthermore, increasing the size of loop 2 from one to two amino acids in anc2T_96 significantly enhances the efficiency of forming the ribbon conotoxin. An important caveat is that the above conclusions are derived from a model-based prediction of ancestral conotoxins and not from a definitive extant conotoxin. Fortunately, the difference in loop 2 sizes between clade A and clade B conotoxins (Fig. 1a) can be used to test whether loop 2 size affects the efficiency of folding into the ribbon conformation. We predicted that a representative conotoxin from clade B, characterized by a larger second intercysteine loop, would fold more efficiently compared to the 2-loop Tau Cdg 1.2 in clade A, which has one amino acid in loop 2. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the folding characteristics of Cdg1.2 from Conus cordigera, a conotoxin from clade A, and χ-AuId, a conotoxin from C. aulicus, which belongs to clade B (Fig. 3a and b). The synthesis was conducted according to the strategy described in the preceding section. Notably, Cdg1.2 contains an N-terminal glutamine residue that is expected to undergo cyclization to pyroglutamate, a PTM previously documented in conotoxins from other gene superfamilies purified from venom (Craig et al. 1999a, 1999b; Vijayasarathy et al. 2018). Consequently, all Cdg1.2 analogs were synthesized incorporating an N-terminal pyroglutamate. χ-AuID was selected because it has been purified from venom and shown to exist in the ribbon conformation in vivo (Espino et al. 2024). We also synthesized chimeric conotoxins in which loop 2 of Cdg1.2 and χ-AuId were switched. We reasoned that if loop 2 indeed affects folding efficiency, switching loop 2 of Cdg1.2 with those from χ-AuID will make the chimeric Cdg1.2 fold like native χ-AuID, while substituting the second loop of χ-AuID with those from Cdg1.2 will result in the chimeric χ-AuID folding like native Cdg1.2. The in vitro folding of both conotoxins was consistent with the predictions derived from the folding results of the ancestral conotoxins. Cdg1.2, featuring a single amino acid in the second loop, adopted a ribbon conformation with a folding efficiency of 70.2 ± 2.5% (mean ± SD, n = 3) (Fig. 3d and f, Figure S6c and d) while the remaining products of the folding reaction consist of topological isomers of the globular conotoxin (Figure S6d). In contrast, 95.5 ± 1.9% (mean ± SD, n = 3) of the completed χ-AuId folding reaction corresponds to the ribbon conotoxin (Fig. 3c and f, Figure S6a and b). Folding of chimeric conotoxins resulted in the same conclusion that the size of the second loop increases the efficiency to fold into the ribbon conformation. Chimeric χ-AuID [F10W, des11O] yielded 69.4 ± 16.4% (mean ± SD, n = 6) of the ribbon conopeptide, while Cdg 1.2 [W10F, 11O] almost exclusively produced the ribbon conopeptide (99.0 ± 1.2%, mean ± SD, n = 6) (Fig. 3f, Figure S6e and f). These results support the role of loop 2 in the preferential folding of 2-loop Tau conotoxins into the ribbon conformation. Thus, changes in the cysteine framework from the ancestral T conotoxin to the 2-loop Tau conotoxin seem to be accompanied by a change in the energetics of the reaction, resulting in the ribbon conformation becoming the more thermodynamically stable form.

The conotoxins χ-AuId, Cdg 1.2, and the ancestral conopeptide anc2T_96 exhibit significant similarity in their primary structure. This observation may indicate that their tendency to fold into the ribbon conformation results from the similarity in the amino acid sequence, rather than from the presence of a second loop. To explore this hypothesis, we examined the folding characteristics of a 2-loop Tau conotoxin identified from venom, which, while differing in primary structure from the aforementioned conotoxins, maintains an identical loop size and cysteine pattern. Our prior research has established that 2-loop Tau conotoxins can be categorized into four groups, with χ-conotoxins constituting the largest group in mollusk hunters (Espino et al. 2024). Some more divergent 2-loop Tau conotoxins appear to be expressed only in specific clades or species. An example of a species-specific conotoxin is Fv1.2, which was identified from a cDNA clone obtained from the venom gland of Conus furvus (Fig. 3a). The conotoxin sequence predicted from this clone is highly divergent from χ-AuID, Cdg 1.2, and anc2T_96, especially in the first intercysteine loop; however, the loop size and cysteine framework are conserved (Fig. 3b). Thus, Fv1.2 serves as an ideal model for examining the contribution of sequence versus cysteine framework to folding.

We next purified a conotoxin with a mass that matched the calculated mass of Fv1.2 containing a hydroxyproline from C. furvus venom (Figure S7a to c). N-terminal Edman sequencing yielded the sequence GVCCAHTGCKOCP, with O representing hydroxyproline, confirming that the purified peptide is identical to the predicted conotoxin encoded by the Fv1.2 clone. We provisionally refer to this conotoxin as FvIa. The amino acid sequence of the FvIa precursor predicted from the Fv1.2 clone is homologous with other T-superfamily conotoxins (Figure S7e). Accordingly, Fv1.2 (FvIa) is a member of this gene superfamily. Native FvIa co-eluted with the synthetic ribbon FvIa, demonstrating that the native conotoxin is present in the ribbon conformation in vivo (Figure S7d). The random (undirected) folding of linear FvIa revealed that the predominant folding product, the ribbon conotoxin, was formed within ∼30 min and comprises 93.4 ± 5.4% (mean ± SD, n = 3) of the completed reaction mixture (Fig. 3e and f).

The results presented here, along with those obtained in the folding of the ancestral conotoxins, suggest that the tendency of 2-loop Tau conotoxins to fold into the ribbon conformation is predominantly determined by the size of the second intercysteine loop. Certain amino acids in loop 1, for example proline, which constrains free rotation around the peptide bond (Ho et al. 2005), may affect the equilibrium between the ribbon and globular conformation. However, proline is absent in loop 1 of the 2-loop Tau conotoxins identified in this study and in our previous work (Espino et al. 2024).

Activity of Ancestral 2-Loop Tau Conotoxins in the Gastropod A. californica and the Annelid Worm H. verbana

χ-Conotoxins are among the most highly expressed toxins in the venom glands of mollusk-hunting cone snails, and the conotoxin χ-AuID causes a strong physiological response upon injection into the mollusk Aplysia californica (Espino et al. 2024). Reconstructing the ancestral 2-loop Tau conotoxins enabled us to estimate the stage at which the predicted ancestral conotoxins acquired activity in mollusks. To investigate this, we tested the in vivo activity of the ancestral 2-loop Tau conotoxins and their analogs in the gastropod A. californica and the annelid Hirudo verbana, as representatives of cone snail prey.

The injection of χ-AuID into A. californica resulted in hyperactivity and impaired locomotion, presumably due to the inhibition of biogenic monoamine transporters in the snail (Espino et al. 2024). In contrast, injecting 10 nmol each of ribbon and globular anc2T_97, anc2T_55, anc2T_96, along with their corresponding hydroxyproline-containing analogs, did not significantly affect the locomotion of A. californica (Fig. 4a). Mollusk-hunting snails are suggested to have evolved from worm-hunting ancestors (Puillandre et al. 2012; Olivera et al. 2015; Kohn 2018), suggesting that the ancestral conotoxin of the snail-targeting 2-loop Tau conotoxins may exhibit activity in annelid worms instead. The injection of 10 nmol of anc2T_97 and its analogs into the leech H. verbana elicited a mild hypoactive phenotype with an onset approximately 60 min post-injection and a duration of 20 min. The anc2T_55 conotoxin appeared to be more potent at inducing hypoactivity in H. verbana, with the onset of symptoms at approximately 20 min post-injection and the effect lasting approximately 60 min. Interestingly, anc2T_96, which has a cysteine framework and intercysteine loop size similar to clade B 2-loop Tau conotoxins, exhibits no significant activity in these assays. We used χ-AuId as a reference to determine the effect of an extant peptide with activity in molluscan prey. χ-AuID induces hyperactivity in A. californica, consistent with our earlier findings (Espino et al. 2024), and interestingly, this conotoxin also induces hyperactivity in H. verbana. The activities of ancestral conotoxins against H. verbana are summarized in Fig. 4b.

In vivo activity of ancestral conotoxins in A. californica and H. verbana. a) Ancestral conotoxins and analogs at 10 nmol injection do not significantly affect locomotion of A. californica. χ-AuId serves as a positive control in these experiments. b) Ancestral conotoxins induce hypoactivity in H. verbana. The onset and duration of effects are indicated. Anc2T_55 [1,4: P10O) appears to be the most potent with the onset of effects starting at ∼13 min and lasting for ∼85 min.

Activity of Ancestral 2-Loop Tau Conotoxins and Cdg1.2 Against the NET

χ-Conotoxins inhibit NET (Sharpe et al. 2001; Espiritu et al. 2023; Espino et al. 2024). However, since NET is absent in mollusks (Adamo 2008), we hypothesize that the effects of χ-conotoxins observed in A. californica may instead result from the inhibition of the related serotonin transporter (aSERT). This hypothesis is supported in part by the conservation of the χ-conotoxin binding site in both NET and aSERT (Figure S8). Further, we have observed that 2-loop Tau conotoxins, which do not inhibit NET (e.g. FvIa), also fail to produce the characteristic activity seen with χ-conotoxins in A. californica in our assay (Figure S9). Therefore, we used χ-conotoxin activity against NET as a surrogate measure of their activity in mollusks. Consistent with our in vivo assays, we used ancestral conotoxins to investigate the emergence of χ-conotoxin activity. Anc2T_97 and anc2T_55 and their hydroxyproline analogs did not inhibit NET at 10 μM (Fig. 5a). In contrast, anc2T_96, the ancestor of clade B 2-loop Tau conotoxins, exhibited weak yet significant transporter inhibition, reducing NET activity by approximately 10% at 10 μM (Fig. 5a and b). These results indicate that the hallmark activity of χ-conotoxins, albeit weakly, first appeared in the anc2T_96 ancestral conotoxin.

NET activity of ancestral conotoxins and Cdg1.2. a) Ancestral conotoxins do not inhibit NET except for anc2T_96 [P10O], which shows a mild but significant inhibition of NET. b) Zoomed view of the inhibitory activity anc2T_96 [P10O] against the transporter. At 10 μM, the ancestral conopeptide decreases transporter activity to 90.0 ± 5.7% (mean ± SD, n = 3, *p < 0.04, t-test) relative to control. The activity of the same concentration of χ-AuId is shown for comparison (****p < 0.0001, t-test, n = 3). c) Dose–response curve illustrating that ribbon Cdg 1.2 inhibits NET with an IC50 = 2.53 ± 0.53 μM (mean ± SD, n = 3 independent experiments performed in triplicate).

The 2-loop Tau conotoxins form two clades among molluscivorous Conus. All identified χ-conotoxins belong to the larger clade B; however, it is not known whether Cdg1.2, a 2-loop Tau conotoxin from clade A, inhibits NET and thus, a χ-conotoxin. We tested Cdg1.2 for activity against NET and for activity in vivo against A. californica. The Cdg1.2 sequence includes amino acids in loop 1 that are important for NET activity, and we have shown that this conotoxin preferentially folds into the ribbon conformation. Thus, we hypothesize that the ribbon Cdg1.2 is a NET inhibitor. The NET inhibition assay validated our hypothesis and demonstrated that the ribbon Cdg 1.2 inhibits the transporter with an IC50 of 2.53 ± 0.53 μM (mean ± SD, n = 3) (Fig. 5c). A 10 nmol injection of ribbon Cdg1.2 into A. californica induced uncoordinated hyperactivity and impaired locomotion (Video S1, n = 3), phenotypes similar to that observed following the injection of χ-AuID (Espino et al. 2024). We propose the name χ-CdgIB for this conotoxin, following the established convention for conotoxin nomenclature (Olivera and Cruz 2001). These results indicate that both clades of the 2-loop Tau gene tree contain χ-conotoxins and support the importance of the χ-conotoxin family in the molluscivorous lifestyle.

Discussion

Conus venom is a complex mixture of conotoxins that play a crucial role in prey capture and serve as a deterrent against predators and competitors (Olivera et al. 1985; Olivera 1997; Olivera et al. 2017). Extant Conus species evolved from worm-hunting snails, where the ancestral venom repertoire repeatedly adapted to novel ecological niches and prey types (Duda and Palumbi 2004). Tracing a possible evolutionary history of conotoxins from their ancestral state to their current forms may reveal how structural and functional diversity arise, and how novel gene functions evolve and give rise to complex phenotypes.

The events that led to the emergence of the 2-loop Tau conotoxins in the T-superfamily are unusual in Conus and rarely described in detail. In most cases, neofunctionalization of pharmacological families is achieved by mutations of key amino acids in the toxin sequence while maintaining the cysteine framework characteristic of a gene superfamily (Woodward et al. 1990). One example similar to what we have described is the κA-conotoxins, where activity against voltage-gated potassium channels is achieved by the alteration of the cysteine framework typical of the A-superfamily conotoxins (Santos et al. 2004). However, the mechanism by which structural diversity and biological activity are generated in the A-superfamily remains unknown.

Our prior research has established that χ-conotoxins and the broader 2-loop Tau conotoxins represent a unique evolutionary innovation of mollusk-hunting Conus, derived from the classical T-superfamily conotoxins (Espino et al. 2024). The evolution of molluscivory in Conus is thought to have originated from vermivory (Duda et al. 2001). Thus, the emergence of molluscivory in Conus may have been accompanied by the evolution of χ-conotoxin bioactivity to optimize predatory efficiency toward a novel prey type. This process involves: (i) structural changes in the ancestral conotoxin, particularly the introduction of a second intercysteine loop; (ii) three-dimensional rearrangement through the formation of different disulfide bridges; and (iii) mutations of a cluster of amino acids to optimize activity against molecular targets in the novel prey. To gain insight into these processes, we used ASR along with biochemical characterizations and in vivo assays. This approach allowed us to predict most likely ancestral conotoxin structures and trace key chemical and biological innovations that led to extant χ-conotoxins and the broader 2-loop Tau conotoxins.

ASR using ARPIP revealed that the most likely common ancestor of 2-loop Tau conotoxins is the conotoxin designated as anc2T_97. A striking feature that makes this ancestral conotoxin different from classical Tau conotoxins is the appearance of a second intercysteine loop between cysteines 3 and 4. From this ancestral conotoxin, the branch that gave rise to the extant 2-loop Tau conotoxins split into two: the smaller omaria-cordigera group (clade A), derived from anc2T_55, and the more extensive clade B, derived from anc2T_96, and containing the majority of the extant 2-loop Tau conotoxins. The model presented here suggests that the formation of a second loop in the ancestral conotoxin has altered the folding chemistry of 2-Loop Tau conotoxins, favoring the ribbon conformation over the globular conformation characteristic of most classical T-conotoxins. An increase in the size of loop 2 further shifts the equilibrium toward the ribbon conformation, as initially shown by anc2T_96 and further supported by the folding behaviors of extant peptides χ-AuID and χ-CdgIB, along with their peptide chimeras, as well as the extant 2-loop Tau conotoxin FvIa, which possesses a sequence distinct from anc2T_96, χ-AuID, and χ-CdgIB.

Our model indicates that the formation of a second loop in T-superfamily conotoxins influences the folding chemistry, leading to a preference for the ribbon conformation. Air oxidation of χ-PnID, a χ-conotoxin from C. pennaceus, produced comparable quantities of both the globular and ribbon conotoxins, with only the ribbon conotoxin acting as a NET inhibitor (Espiritu et al. 2023). This finding, to some extent, contradicts the conclusions drawn in the current study. Interestingly, the ribbon χ-PnID is predominantly expressed in venom (Espiritu et al. 2023). This suggests that air oxidation of χ-PnID did not accurately reflect in vivo conditions.

The activity of the ancestral anc2T_97 and anc2T_55 conotoxins in the annelid H. verbana suggests that the ancestors of 2-loop Tau conotoxins were targeted toward annelid worms or at least exhibit activity in annelids. By focusing on clade B, we aimed to determine when the switch in activity from a worm-active conotoxin to a conotoxin with activity in mollusks occurred. This inflection point likely corresponds to the ancestral conotoxin anc2T_96. Although anc2T_96 did not show activity in the H. verbana or A. californica assays at the injected concentration, it exhibited weak activity against NET, a hallmark of χ-conotoxins. This low-level activity may have provided a foundation for natural selection to optimize and enhance χ-conotoxin activity. Research on χ-MrIA has shown that the amino acids -GYKL- in loop 1 are essential for activity against NET, with nonconservative amino acid substitutions in any of these residues leading to a loss of activity (Sharpe et al. 2003; Brust et al. 2009; Hu et al. 2024). Sequence comparison among anc2T_96, χ-MrIA, χ-CdgIB, and χ-AuID reveals that the first three amino acids -GYK- in loop 1 are conserved, while position 4 varies: it is occupied by the aromatic phenylalanine in anc2T_96, and by the aliphatic amino acid leucine in χ-MrIA, χ-CdgIB, and χ-AuID. This substitution at position 4 may underlie the weaker NET inhibition observed for anc2T_96. In ancestral conotoxins derived from anc2T_96, this position is mutated to the aliphatic amino acid methionine (Figure S1a to c), likely contributing to optimized NET activity and presumably enhanced efficacy against the in vivo target in prey.

All conotoxins previously identified as inhibitors of NET are classified within clade B. This study demonstrates that clade A includes a 2-loop Tau conotoxin that inhibits NET, χ-CdgIB. This distinct branch suggests the nonmonophyletic nature of the χ-conotoxins. The species expressing χ-CdgIB, C. cordigera, is a member of the Eugeniconus clade of mollusk-hunting Conus. Eugeniconus represents one of the earliest diverging clades of mollusk-hunters, having separated from the remaining molluscivores ∼15.6 million years ago (Puillandre et al. 2012; Espino et al. 2024). χ-CdgIB is distinct from the 2-loop Tau conotoxins in clade B in that it has a single bulky amino acid, tryptophan, in loop 2, replacing the proline found in loop 2 of its most recent ancestor, anc2T_55. Considering these distinctions, it is noteworthy that χ-CdgIB functions as a NET inhibitor and exhibits the same phenotypic effects in A. californica as χ-AuID from clade B. This further supports the assertion that mutations in loop 2 are relatively tolerated while maintaining activity against NET (Brust et al. 2009).

Two conotoxin families are characterized by the presence of two loops in their mature toxin sequences: the 2-loop Tau conotoxins and the α-conotoxins belonging to the A-superfamily (Santos et al. 2004). Although both families exhibit two intercysteine loops, the 2-loop Tau conotoxins predominantly adopt a ribbon form, while the majority of identified α-conotoxins assume a globular conformation (Tabassum et al. 2017). An exception is noted in the case of α-ImI, where research has demonstrated the presence of both globular and ribbon conotoxins in the venom, with the globular conotoxin exhibiting the highest level of biological activity (Safavi-Hemami et al. 2012). Additionally, the proline residue located at position 6 in α-ImI has been identified as a significant determinant influencing its folding into a globular conformation (Kang et al. 2005). The 2-loop Tau conotoxins and α-ImI are of comparable sizes, with both containing four amino acids in loop 1. α-ImI has three amino acids in loop 2, whereas 2-loop Tau conotoxins contain between one and two amino acids. Increasing the size of loop 2 in α-conotoxins favors the globular conformation (Tabassum et al. 2017). Thus, it appears that among conotoxins, there is a narrow window of loop 2 sizes in which the preferred fold is the ribbon conformation. In these conotoxins, the preferred conformation is typically the form with the highest level of bioactivity (Safavi-Hemami et al. 2012; Espiritu et al. 2023).

The data supporting our main conclusion regarding the effects of the second intercysteine loop on folding of 2-loop Tau conotoxins were derived from in vitro experiments and may not fully reflect the enzyme-catalyzed folding in vivo. However, the folding reactions presented here, although lacking enzymes, were conducted under conditions that are relevant in vivo, i.e. neutral pH and millimolar concentrations of reduced and oxidized glutathione (Price-Carter et al. 1996). While enzymes and most chaperones influence the kinetics of folding, they generally do not affect the thermodynamic stability of the final products (Berg et al. 2019; Xu 2022). Therefore, the ratio of products observed in our in vitro reactions is likely to reflect, at least in part, those found in vivo. Consistent with this, ribbon χ-PnID has been shown to be the predominant isoform expressed in the venom of C. pennaceus (Espiritu et al. 2023). Furthermore, under folding conditions similar to those used in this study, enzymes identified from cone snail venom duct increased the rate of product formation but, in most cases, did not alter the ratio of the folded products in vitro (Lopez-Vera et al. 2008; Safavi-Hemami et al. 2012; Safavi-Hemami et al. 2016; O'Brien et al. 2018).

Mollusk-hunting cone snails are faced with a unique biological challenge: the protective shell of their prey. To hunt successfully, molluscivorous Conus must develop a method to extract their prey from its shell. We propose that χ-conotoxins may have evolved to provide one solution to this biological problem. As demonstrated here and in our previous work (Espino et al. 2024), injection of a χ-conotoxin into the mollusk A. californica induced an uncoordinated, hyperactive phenotype. A hyperactive snail is more likely to emerge from its shell, thereby negating the shell's protective function and rendering it vulnerable to predation. In addition, χ-conotoxins impair locomotion, preventing escape even if the snail remains hyperactive—further increasing the cone snail's advantage. The primary predators of cone snails are other mollusks. Thus, a venom peptide targeted to other mollusks could serve a dual role in both predation and defense, blurring the line between venom components exclusively for defense or for predation.

This research proposes a model that depicts the evolutionary progression of 2-loop Tau conotoxins. The model illustrates a series of gradual and sequential changes that have led to the extant conotoxins, which play an important role in the molluscivorous lifestyle. One can envision a situation in which an ancestral worm-hunting cone snail experienced a random mutation that resulted in the insertion of a proline residue between Cys3 and Cys4 within a classic T-superfamily conotoxin. This consequential event introduced new folding chemistry and structural diversity into the T-superfamily conotoxins. The resulting novel scaffold underwent further mutations that eventually resulted in low-level activity against a presumed biogenic monoamine transporter in mollusks and was subsequently optimized for activity toward the novel target. Molluscivory evolved ∼18 Mya in the early Miocene (Espino et al. 2024). Major geological events during this epoch likely opened novel ecological niches, allowing the ancestral Conus, equipped with novel mollusk-active conotoxins in its venom, to evolve into the ∼100 species of extant mollusk-hunting cone snails (WoRMSEditorialBoard 2025).

Materials and Methods

Ancestral Sequence Reconstruction

ASR was performed using Ancestral Reconstruction under the Poisson Indel Process ARPIP (Jowkar et al. 2023) based on commit 175d4272ae2669994e508a4a96705ddc4b446a83. This method is specifically designed to account for insertion-deletion (indel) mutations. Many conventional ASR methods do not incorporate indels, making them unsuitable for studying conotoxin evolution, where indel-driven structural changes often underlie functional shifts (Wu et al. 2013; Yang and Zhou 2020). The reconstruction was conducted using a previously published gene tree of T-superfamily conotoxins, including 2-loop Tau- and χ-conotoxins with sequences available from https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.26876092.v1 (Espino et al. 2024). The tree with bootstrap support can be viewed in Figure S2. The model parameters were set as PIP (model = WAG01, μ = 0.01, λ = 10) to account for insertion and deletion events.

To compare the ASR from ARPIP, we also performed ASR with IQ-TREE (Minh et al. 2020). Unlike ARPIP, IQ-TREE does not take gaps into account. The best model according to ModelFinder was set to Q.mammal + I + G4 with the same alignment and tree as used for ARPIP. Since insertions and deletions are not modeled in IQ-TREE, we rolled over the indel mutations from ARPIP and predicted the anc2T_55, anc2T_96, and anc2T_97 for comparison. The predicted ancestral peptide sequences can be seen in Figure S3. Likelihood values are presented in Supplementary File 1. Anc2T_55 is denoted by Node154 in IQ-tree, anc2T_96 by Node134, and anc2T_97 by Node133.

Conopeptide Synthesis

The synthesis of χ-AuID was previously described by Espino et al (2024). In this study, we describe the synthesis of FvIA, Cdg1.2, χ-AuID [F10W;des11O], Cdg1.2 [W10F, 11O], as well as the ancestral sequences anc2T_99, anc2T_96, anc2T_55, along with their hydroxyproline analogs. Conotoxins were synthesized using an Apex 396 automated peptide synthesizer (AAPPTec; Louisville, Kentucky) applying standard solid-phase Fmoc (9-fluorenylmethyloxy-carbonyl) protocols. Conotoxins were constructed on preloaded resins as follows: FvIA on H-Pro-2-ClTrt-resin (substitution: 0.62 mmol/g; AAPPTec, Louisville, Kentucky), Cdg1.2 on Fmoc-Cys(Trt)-Wang LL resin (substitution: 0.3 mmol/g; NovaBiochem), anc2T_55 on Rink Amide-MBHA resin (substitution: 0.34 mmol/g; NovaBiochem), anc2T_96 and anc2T_96 and their analogs on Fmoc-Gln(Trt)-Wang LL resin (substitution: 0.3 mmol/g; NovaBiochem). All standard amino acids were purchased from AAPPTec. Side-chain protection for the amino acids was as follows: Cys, His, and Gln, trityl (Trt); Cys acetamidomethyl (Acm); Lys and Trp, tert-butyloxycarbonyl (Boc); Ser, Thr, Hyp, and Tyr, tert-butyl ether (tBu). A tenfold excess of standard amino acids was used. The coupling activation was achieved with one equivalent of 0.4 M benzotriazol-1-yl-oxytripyrrolidinophosphonium hexafluorophosphate and two equivalents of 2 M N,N-diisopropylethyl amine in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone as the solvent. Each coupling reaction was conducted for 60 min. Fmoc deprotection was carried out for 20 min with 20% piperidine in dimethylformamide.

Oxidative Folding of Linear Conotoxins and Random Folding Analysis

Linear and ACM-protected conotoxins were cleaved from 25 to 50 mg of resin by incubating with 1 mL of reagent K (11.0 M trifluoroacetic acid [TFA], 3.0 M 1,2-ethanedithiol, 4.0 M thioanisole, and 5.0 M phenol) for 2 h with constant shaking at room temperature. The cleavage reactions were filtered, and the conotoxins were precipitated from the filtrate with ice-cold methy-tert-butyl ether (MTBE). MTBE was decanted from the precipitated conotoxins, and the peptides were resuspended in a solution containing 35% acetonitrile (ACN) and 0.1% TFA in water. After resuspension, the ACN concentration was reduced to 15% with a solution of 0.1% TFA in water. Linear conotoxins were purified by semi-preparative HPLC (semi-prep HPLC) using a Vydac C18 column (5 μm, 10 × 250 mm2, 300 Å) in a two-buffer system consisting of buffer A (0.1% TFA in water) and buffer B90 (90% ACN, 0.1% TFA in water). Components of the linear conotoxin solution were eluted with a linear gradient of 1% B90/min starting at 15% for 35 min at a flow rate of 4 mL/min. Folding of the globular or ribbon conopeptide was accomplished in two disulfide-bridge-forming reactions. The first disulfide was formed by the reduction of the free thiols in a solution containing 1.0 mM oxidized glutathione, 1.0 mM reduced glutathione GSH, 100 mM Tris (pH = 7.5), and 0.1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for 2 h. The partially oxidized conopeptides were purified to >95% homogeneity by semi-prep HPLC using the same conditions described above. ACM-protected cysteines were oxidized to form the second disulfide bridge by incubation in a solution containing 24% ACN (v/v), 380 mM TFA, and 5 mM I2 for 10 min and quenched with saturated ascorbic acid solution. Completely oxidized conopeptides were purified to >95% homogeneity using semi-prep HPLC as described above. A 20 μL aliquot from the second semi-prep run was analyzed on analytical HPLC on a Vydac C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm2, 300 Å) following the same solvent gradient described above for the purification of the linear peptides at a flow rate of 1 mL/min to further verify homogeneity.

The complete oxidation of cysteine was verified by mass spectrometric analysis (Table S1).

To generate a linear conopeptide in which all the cysteines contain free thiols, either the globular or ribbon conopeptide was reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 65 °C for 30 min in a solution buffered to pH 8 with 0.5 M Tris base. The reduced conopeptide was purified by analytical HPLC on a Vydac C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm2, 300 Å) to >95% homogeneity. The components of the reaction mixture were eluted under the conditions described previously for semi-prep HPLC, with the exception that the flow rate was reduced to 1 mL/min. A total of 10 nmol of the reduced conopeptide was subjected to random folding, following the protocol described above, for the formation of the first disulfide bond. Aliquots were collected at designated time points, and the reaction was quenched with 10% formic acid. The progress of the folding reaction was monitored by analytical HPLC, following the previously described protocol. The HPLC peaks corresponding to the globular or ribbon conformations were identified based on their retention times, which were comparable to those of the standard globular or ribbon conotoxins analyzed earlier. The relative amount of conotoxin in each HPLC peak was determined by integrating the area under the peak using the built-in function in the Empower 3 software (Waters Corp., Milford, Massachusetts).

Identification and Sequencing of Clones Encoding Fv1a From C. furvus

cDNA from C. furvus was used as a template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with oligonucleotides corresponding to conserved regions of the signal sequence and 3′ UTR sequences of χ-prepropeptides. The resulting PCR products were purified using the High Pure PCR Product Purification Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, Indiana) following the manufacturer's suggested protocol. The eluted DNA fragments were annealed to pNEB206A vector using the USER Friendly Cloning kit (New England BioLabs, Inc., Beverly, Massachusetts, United States) following the manufacturer's suggested protocol, and the resulting products were transformed into DH5α competent cells. The nucleic acid sequences of these χ-encoding clones were determined according to the standard protocol for automated sequencing.

Extraction and Fractionation of C. furvus Venom

Conus furvus snails were collected in the Central Philippines. Venom ducts were dissected from 50 snails, and the venom was manually extruded (on ice) and lyophilized. A 50-mg portion of the lyophilized venom was extracted with 1 mL solution containing 30% ACN and 0.1% TFA. The suspension was kept on ice and homogenized by vortexing every 10 min. After 30 min, the suspension was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min, and the supernatant was separated from the pellet. Two further extractions were performed, and the supernatants from the three extractions were pooled and diluted with 3 volumes of 0.1% TFA. This solution was then fractionated on a Vydac C18 semi-preparative column (5 μm, 10 × 250 mm2). The venom components were eluted using a linear gradient of buffers consisting of 0.1% TFA (buffer A) and 90% ACN in 0.1% TFA (buffer B90) at a gradient of 1% B90/min and a flow rate of 4 mL/min. The absorbance at 220 and 280 nm was monitored, and individual peaks corresponding to individual fractions were collected.

Purification of FvIa From Semi-prep HPLC Fraction

FvIa co-elutes with the first major peak in the fractionation of the C. furvus venom. The conopeptide was purified using analytical HPLC on a Vydac C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm2, 300 Å) with a linear gradient of 10% to 30% B90 over 20 min at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The purity of the fraction containing Fv1a was verified by separate analytical HPLC using the elution profile described above, and the conotoxin was purified to >95% homogeneity. The mass spectrometry results of the purified conotoxin were consistent with the mass predicted from the cDNA clone Fv1.2 (Fv1a) and with the PTM of proline to hydroxyproline.

Reduction and Alkylation of the Purified Conotoxins

Putative FvIa was reduced using DTT and alkylated using 4-vinylpyridine (4-VP). The pH of the solution containing the conopeptide was buffered to pH 8 using 0.5 M Tris base. The conopeptide was reduced with 10 mM DTT at 65 °C for 30 min. After 30 min, a 0.8 μL volume of 4-vinylpyridine (4-VP) was added to the solution containing the reduced conopeptide. The alkylation of the reduced conopeptide was performed in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The reduced and alkylated conotoxin was purified from the reaction using an analytical reverse-phase HPLC column using the gradient described above for the purification of the putative conopeptide.

Conopeptide Sequencing

To verify the isolation of FvIa from venom, a portion of the purified conopeptide was sequenced by Edman degradation. Approximately 70 pmol of the alkylated conopeptide was sequenced at the DNA/peptide core facility at the University of Utah. The 3-phenyl-2-thiohydantoin amino acid derivatives were identified by HPLC.

Aplysia californica Bioassay

The A. californica assay was performed as previously described (Espino et al. 2024). Briefly, the snails were acclimated to the testing conditions for at least 24 h. The conotoxins were resuspended in normal saline solution (NSS) and stored overnight at 4 °C. On the day of the experiment, the snails were transferred to a smaller observation chamber, injected with 10 nmol conopeptide in 20 μL NSS, videotaped for 30 min and the behavior quantified as described previously. The injection was administered at the anterior of the snail, just above the junction of the parapodia.

Hirudo verbana Bioassay

Prior to the administration of the injections, 10 nmol of each dry conotoxin was resuspended in 12 μL of nanopure water, followed by vortexing and centrifugation. Groups of three leeches, each weighing 1.20 ± 0.22 g, (mean ± SD), were subjected to intramuscular injections at the dorso-posterior portion of the body, specifically two-thirds of the body length from the anterior end, with either 12 µL of water (as controls) or the conopeptide solution (10 nmol in 12 µL), using a G31-gauge 1-mL insulin syringe. After injection, the leeches were placed individually in small plastic tanks (10 × 11 × 17 cm3, W × H × D) filled with 300 mL of artificial pond water (1:10 dilution of Instant Ocean sea water). The effects of the conotoxins on the gross behavior of the leeches were monitored for 3 h.

Cell Culture

Human embryonic kidney cells stably expressing the human norepinephrine transporter (HEK293-hNET), a generous gift from the Blakely Lab (Vanderbilt University), were grown in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's media containing 10% (v/v) FBS, 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine and 250 μg/mL G418 at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidity-controlled incubator.

Norepinephrine Uptake Assay

The norepinephrine uptake for FvIa followed protocols described previously (Espino et al. 2024). For χ-CdgIB, the assay was performed using the neurotransmitter transporter uptake assay kit (Molecular Devices, San Jose, California). HEK-293 hNET cells were plated on a 96-well amine-coated plate at a density of 50,000 cells/well 24 h before the experiment. Conotoxins were resuspended in Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) containing 20 mM HEPES (HBSS-HEPES solution) to achieve the appropriate concentration. The conotoxin solution was stored overnight at 4 °C. The solutions were warmed to room temperature on the day of the experiment. The vial containing the lyophilized fluorescent dye/masking mix (R8173) was equilibrated at room temperature and reconstituted with 10 mL of the HBSS-HEPES solution (dye solution). Culture media was aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with 100 μL HBSS-HEPES solution. After the second washing, the cells were incubated with 100 μL of the conopeptide solution at 37 °C for 30 min. Following the 30-min incubation, 100 μL of the dye solution was added to each well, and the cells were further incubated with the conotoxin and dye solution for an additional 30 min. After incubation, the uptake reaction was quenched by placing the plate on ice. The amount of the fluorescent dye taken into the cell via the transporter was measured as the emission at 520 nm upon excitation at 440 nm in a fluorescent plate reader. In parallel, the activity of the transporter was measured in the presence of 1 μM desipramine to account for nonspecific uptake. After fluorescent measurement, the cells were lysed at −80 °C overnight. The protein concentration in an aliquot of the lysate was measured using Quick Start Bradford Protein Assay Kit (BioRad) following the manufacturer's protocol. Transporter activity was measured as the emission at 520 nm normalized against protein concentration in each well.
