Risk of Cardiovascular Disease With Co‐Occurring Covert Brain Infarction and Unrecognized Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Camiel V. J. Box, Julie A. E. van Oortmerssen, Martijn J. Tilly, Meike W. Vernooij, M. Kamran Ikram, Maryam Kavousi, Frank J. Wolters
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCovert brain infarction (CBI) increases the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), but optimal clinical management is uncertain. To enable tailored preventive measures, we aimed to determine whether the co‐occurrence of CBI with unrecognized myocardial infarction (UMI) identifies a subgroup at especially high risk of ASCVD.MethodsWe included participants from the population‐based Rotterdam Study, who were aged ≥45 years and without a history of stroke, transient ischemic attack or myocardial infarction. Participants underwent electrocardiography and brain magnetic resonance imaging between 2006 and 2012 and were followed up for first‐ever ASCVD (ie, composite of ischemic stroke and recognized myocardial infarction). We used Cox models adjusted for cardiovascular risk factors to determine ASCVD risk.ResultsAmong 4627 participants (mean age, 64.1 years; 57% women), 359 (7.8%) had CBI and 92 (2.0%) UMI. Compared with individuals without CBI, those with CBI were twice as likely to have UMI (odds ratio, 2.3 [95% CI, 1.2–3.9]). During a mean 9.9‐year follow‐up, 344 ASCVD events occurred. Compared with individuals with neither CBI nor UMI, individuals with CBI or UMI were at higher risk of ASCVD (hazard ratio [HR] for CBI, 1.7 [95% CI, 1.2–2.3]; and HR for UMI, 1.7 [95% CI, 0.9–3.3]), and individuals with co‐occurring CBI and UMI were at highest risk (HR, 5.6 [95% CI, 2.9–10.7]). Among 359 individuals with CBI, those with UMI were at higher risk of ASCVD than those without UMI (HR, 3.4 [95% CI, 1.7–6.7]).ConclusionsIndividuals with CBI are at increased risk of ASCVD, particularly in the presence of co‐occurring UMI. These findings mark a subgroup of patients with CBI that could benefit most from stringent preventive therapy.

Covert brain infarction (CBI) is a frequent incidental finding on brain imaging, occurring with increasing prevalence by age in 4% to 28% of individuals aged >45 years. 1 , 2 The presence of CBI signals a significant risk for future cardiovascular disease, with a 2‐fold increased risk of stroke and a 1.5‐fold increased risk of myocardial infarction. 3 , 4 However, optimal clinical management of CBI is undetermined. Current guidelines stress a lack of evidence on secondary stroke prevention measures after CBI and therefore recommend only routine primary stroke prevention measures in addition to investigation of underlying mechanisms. 5 , 6

The lack of evidence on the harms and benefits of cardiovascular risk reduction strategies in people with CBI pertains to optimal goals for blood pressure and lipid‐lowering intervention, as well as the use of platelet inhibitors. 7 Benefits of more stringent preventative treatment following diagnosis of CBI could outweigh adverse effects particularly in patients who are at high risk of clinical sequelae, including both cerebrovascular and cardiovascular events. 8 Considering the profound burden of coronary heart disease in patients with stroke and its associated risks, 9 , 10 assessment of coexisting unrecognized myocardial infarction (UMI) in patients with CBI might help to identify such a high‐risk group.

UMI accounts for an estimated 20% to 60% of all myocardial infarctions (MIs). 11 , 12 , 13 Like CBI, UMI is not detected during the acute phase, either because symptoms are lacking or not recognized as such. Diagnosis of UMI relies on pathological findings on ECG, myocardial imaging modalities, or autopsy. 14 UMI carries significant risk of major adverse cardiac events, stroke, and death, comparable to risks after clinically recognized MI. 14 Moreover, UMI may be more common in people with CBI, 15 but the clinical implications of their co‐occurrence is uncertain, as no published studies have determined risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) in patients with both CBI and UMI.

Therefore, we aimed to assess the concurrence between CBI and UMI in the general population and determine whether individuals with both CBI and UMI are at increased long‐term risk of ASCVD.

Study Population

This study is embedded within the Rotterdam Study, a prospective population‐based cohort study among 17 931 residents of the Ommoord neighborhood in Rotterdam, the Netherlands. The Rotterdam Study was initiated in 1990 with the inclusion of 7983 participants aged ≥55 years (Rotterdam Study‐I), and was subsequently expanded thrice, with an additional 3011 participants in 2000 (Rotterdam Study‐II), 3932 participants aged ≥45 years in 2006 (Rotterdam Study‐III), and 3005 participants aged ≥40 years in 2016 (Rotterdam Study‐IV). 17 Participants are examined in‐person every 3 to 5 years in a dedicated research facility, and are followed up continuously through automated linkage with health care providers, both regionally and nationally. For the current study, we included participants from the fifth examination round of Rotterdam Study‐I (2009–2011), the third round of Rotterdam Study‐II (2011–2012), and the baseline round of Rotterdam Study‐III (2006–2009).

Every examination round entails a home interview and 3 routine visits to the Rotterdam Study research center. ECG is performed during visit 1, and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) during visit 3. Of 7972 participants in the aforementioned examination rounds, 6794 were free of transient ischemic attack (TIA), stroke, or recognized MI at the current study's baseline. Of those, 759 did not undergo ECG, 19 had an ECG that was of insufficient quality, and 2115 opted out of the MRI or had contraindications. A total of 4631 (68.2%) participants underwent both ECG and brain MRI. Participants who completed both MRI and ECG were on average younger, more often men, and had lower burden of cardiovascular risk factors than those who did not (Table S2). An additional 4 participants did not provide consent for follow‐up, leaving 4627 (68.1%) participants for analyses (Figure 1). As ECG and MRI were done at different visits within the same examination round, the date by which participants had undergone both was defined as the baseline for this study; the median time between ECG and brain MRI was 5.3 (interquartile range, 3.7–8.0) weeks. We use the terms co‐occurring and co‐occurrence because they were discovered during the same research round, but this does not imply that the CBI and UMI happened at the same moment in time. The exact moment of their occurrence is unknown because they are, by definition, not discovered in their acute state.

Covert Brain Infarctions

MRI was done with a 1.5T scanner (GE Signa Excite; GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI), using a multisequence protocol as previously described. 18 Brain infarcts were rated on fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, proton density–weighted, and T1‐weighted sequences by trained researchers who were blinded to clinical details. Uncertain lesions were discussed with a consultant neuroradiologist. Intrarater agreement on infarct detection was previously assessed as good (κ=0.80). 19 The presence of brain infarcts on MRI was not reported back to participants or their health care provider, in line with the Rotterdam Study protocol on incidental findings. 20 We defined CBI as brain infarction on MRI in participants without a history of TIA or stroke. This likely excluded some participants who had CBI in addition to their clinical TIA or stroke, but the vascular territory of neurological symptoms can be challenging to determine, 21 and implications of those CBIs for preventative intervention are limited.

Unrecognized MIs

A 12‐lead ECG was conducted using an ACTA electrocardiograph (Esaote, Florence, Italy) at a sampling frequency of 500 Hz. All digital ECGs were processed using the Modular ECG Analysis System, which uses criteria based on the Minnesota codes to detect MI. 22 Electrocardiographic diagnosis of MI relied on pathological Q waves, supported by auxiliary criteria like the QR ratio and R‐wave progression. Two independent research physicians, blinded to clinical details, validated the diagnosis of electrocardiographic MI in ECGs that were identified by Modular ECG Analysis System as suggestive of MI. In case of discordance between readers, a consultant cardiologist‐electrophysiologist made the final diagnosis. We defined unrecognized MI (UMI) as a sign of MI on ECG in participants without a history of recognized MI.

In contrast to CBI, all definite ECG‐detected MIs in this study were reported back to participants and their general practitioners. In case of a newly diagnosed UMI, participants were advised to consult a cardiologist, who generally evaluate and treat UMI as chronic coronary disease. 23

Ascertainment of Incident ASCVD and Death

Within the Rotterdam Study, prevalence and follow‐up for stroke, TIA, MI, and death takes place via a combination of periodic in‐person assessment and automated coupling of the study database with medical records from the general practitioners. In the Dutch health care system, general practitioners serve as gatekeepers and therefore receive all relevant medical information from all of their patients' health care providers. Information on disease occurrence from medical records is reviewed by a consensus panel including a consultant cardiologist or vascular neurologist to assure consistent ascertainment of cases in the cohort.

Stroke was defined in accordance with the World Health Organization criteria as a syndrome of rapidly emerging clinical signs of focal or global disturbance of cerebral functions lasting 24 hours or longer or causing death with no apparent cause other than of vascular origin. 24 Distinction between hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke was made on the basis of imaging. If brain imaging was unavailable, the stroke type was considered undetermined.

Recognized MI was defined as the presence of MI symptoms such as cardiac pain or cardiogenic shock in combination with pathological findings of acute MI within 28 days of death, a rise or fall in cardiac biomarkers (ie, troponin and creatine kinase), or ECG changes indicative of MI. 25

Death was ascertained through linkage with municipal administration. Follow‐up for ischemic stroke and recognized MI was done until January 1, 2020.

Other Participant Characteristics

Smoking status and medication use were obtained through interview. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure were measured in the sitting position on the right arm using a random‐zero sphygmomanometer; the mean of 2 readings was used for analysis. Body mass index (kg/m2) was calculated from measurements of height and weight. Estimated glomerular filtration rates were calculated from creatinine in fasting blood samples using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula. 26 Triglycerides, total cholesterol, and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol were measured in fasting blood samples. From these, we calculated low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (mg/dL) using the Friedewald formula. 27 Because this formula is applicable only with triglycerides up to 4.5 mmol/L, low‐density lipoprotein was missing in 2.1% of participants. 28 Diabetes was defined as fasting glucose >6.9 mmol/L, nonfasting glucose >11.0 mmol/L, or the use of antidiabetic medication.

Statistical Analysis

Missing data (<1.0% for body mass index, blood pressure, smoking, and medication use, 2.1% for low‐density lipoprotein, and 2.8% for kidney function) were imputed using 5‐fold imputation.

First, we determined whether individuals with CBI were more likely to also have UMI by computing crude and age/sex‐adjusted odds ratios with 95% CIs from logistic regression models. Next, we determined the risk of ASCVD in individuals without CBI and UMI, with only CBI, only UMI, and co‐occurring CBI and UMI. We calculated incidence rates and used Cox proportional hazard models to determine hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. We defined our primary outcome, ASCVD, as a composite of ischemic stroke and recognized MI. The secondary outcomes were ischemic stroke and recognized MI separately. Incidence rates for all‐cause death were also calculated.

We constructed 3 models for every outcome: a crude model (model 1), a model including age and sex (model 2), and a model in which we additionally adjusted for well‐known cardiovascular risk factors to control for confounding, specifically, body mass index; systolic blood pressure; use of antithrombotic medication, blood pressure–lowering medication, and lipid‐lowering medication; smoking habits; estimated glomerular filtration rate; low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol; and diabetes (model 3). Participants were censored at death, loss to follow‐up, or January 1, 2020, whichever came first. Follow‐up for ASCVD and ischemic stroke was additionally censored when an undetermined stroke occurred. We examined a possible interaction between CBI and UMI in model 3 on a multiplicative scale by adding an interaction term and on an additive scale by calculating the relative excess risk due to interaction. 29

We tested the proportional hazards assumptions with Schoenfeld residuals, combined with plots of the β coefficients over time (Figure S1). Schoenfeld residuals in model 3 for ASCVD risk were marginally significant, but the plots of the β coefficients over time suggested only minimal violation of the proportional hazards assumption (Figure S1). Furthermore, the timing of diagnosis of a CBI or UMI with respect to their occurrence is highly variable, and a time interaction in the survival models therefore less meaningful. Hence, we decided not to stratify models by follow‐up time.

For graphical representation, we created covariable‐adjusted survival curves based on the Cox model (using g‐computations) and covariable‐adjusted cumulative incidence curves based on a Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard model. 30

We performed several sensitivity analyses. First, we explored sex differences by stratification and testing for statistical interaction on a multiplicative scale. Second, we repeated the survival analyses using subdistribution hazard models, to provide a prognostic estimand in addition to the causal estimand from the Cox models. 31 Third, we additionally corrected for a history of treatment for coronary artery disease by percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty or coronary artery bypass grafting. Fourth, we excluded participants with antithrombotic medication use.

To determine whether disclosing a UMI to participants could have influenced their medication use, we checked pharmacy records for antithrombotic and lipid‐lowering medication use at baseline and 90, 180, and 730 days after baseline.

All analyses were done in R Statistical Software version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), using the packages adjustedCurves, 30 interactionR, 32 mice, 33 riskRegression, 34 pammtools, 35 survival, 36 and tidycmprsk. 37 α was set to 0.05.

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

Of 4627 participants, 359 (7.8%) had a CBI, and 92 (2.0%) a UMI. The mean age of the study population was 64.1±11.0 years, and 57% were women (Table 1). Participants with both CBI and UMI used antithrombotic (44%) and lipid‐lowering medication (50%) more often than other participants (13% and 21% for those with neither CBI nor UMI, 20% and 25% for those with only CBI, and 29% and 25% for those with only UMI). This did not change substantially during the first 6 months after baseline visit (Figure S2). Participants with both CBI and UMI more often had a history of treatment for coronary artery disease (3/16 [19%]) than the other groups (115/4192 [3%] for those with neither CBI nor UMI, 10/343 [3%] for those with only CBI, and 7/76 [9%] for those with only UMI).

The Co‐Occurrence of CBI and UMI

Overall, the combination of CBI and UMI occurred in 0.4% of the population. Among people with CBI, the prevalence of UMI was 4.5%, compared with 1.8% in people without CBI. Individuals with CBI were more than twice as likely to also have a UMI compared with people without CBI (crude odds ratio, 2.6 [95% CI, 1.4–4.3]), similar after adjustment for age and sex (odds ratio, 2.3 [95% CI, 1.2–3.9]). This did not differ between women and men (age‐adjusted odds ratio in women, 2.15 [95% CI, 0.79–4.96]; and odds ratio in men, 2.50 [95% CI, 1.14–5.06]; P interaction=0.77).

Risk of First‐Ever Clinical ASCVD

During a total of 45 984 person years of follow‐up (mean follow‐up time, 9.9±3.1 years), 344 first‐ever ASCVD events, 187 first‐ever recognized MI and 171 first‐ever ischemic stroke occurred, and 671 participants died. The incident rate for ASCVD was highest in the participant with co‐occurring CBI and UMI (81.1 per 1000 person‐years) and lowest for the participants with neither CBI nor UMI (6.4 per 1000 person‐years; Table 2). After adjusting for age and sex, individuals with only CBI or only UMI had a similar 1.8‐fold increased risk of ASCVD, compared with reference participants with neither UMI nor CBI (Table 2). Risk was markedly higher in participants with co‐occurring CBI and UMI than for the other groups (Table 2). For example, age/sex‐adjusted risk was 9 times higher than in individuals with neither CBI or UMI (adjusted HR, 9.0 [95% CI, 4.8–16.9]), and nearly 5 times higher than in individuals with only CBI (adjusted HR, 4.8 [95% CI, 2.5–9.5]). These patterns were similar, with 3‐ to 6‐fold increased risks, after further adjustment for shared cardiovascular risk factors (Table 2; Figure 2). Figure 2A displays the fully adjusted survival curves. In the fully adjusted models, we found a significant interaction between CBI and UMI on an additive scale (relative excess risk due to interaction, 3.2 [95% CI, 0.2–8.3]), and a nonsignificant trend for interaction on a multiplicative scale (HR interaction term, 2.0 [95% CI, 0.8–5.2]; P interaction=0.16).

ASCVD risk estimates were similar when using fully adjusted subdistribution hazard models (adjusted HR for co‐occurring CBI and UMI versus neither CBI nor UMI, 6.5 [95% CI, 3.7–11.4]), adjusted cumulative incidence curves are presented in Figure 2B. When stratifying by sex, the ACSVD risk associated with co‐occurring CBI and UMI was numerically larger for women than for men (fully adjusted HRs, 8.8 [95% CI, 3.0–26.3] versus 4.8 [95% CI, 2.1–11.0]; Table S3), but the formal test for interaction was not statistically significant (P interaction=0.26). Risk of Ischemic Stroke, Recognized MI, and Death

When investigating ischemic stroke and recognized MI as separate outcomes, results were similar to the composite outcome of ASCVD. Participants with CBI and UMI had the highest incidence rate of ischemic stroke and recognized MI (ischemic stroke; 50.7 per 1000 person‐years; recognized MI, 37.6 per 1000 person‐years; Table 3). In fully adjusted Cox models, their ischemic stroke risk was 3.9 (95% CI, 1.7–9.1) times higher than participants with only CBI and 6.9 (95% CI, 3.2–15.3) times higher than participants with neither CBI nor UMI. As for recognized MI risk, participants with CBI and UMI had a 3.3‐fold higher risk (95% CI, 1.2–8.7) than participants with only CBI, and a 4.9‐fold increased risk (95% CI, 2.0–12.2) compared with those with neither CBI nor UMI (Table 3).

For the risk of stroke, we found a significant additive interaction between CBI and UMI (relative excess risk due to interaction, 5.1 [95% CI, 0.6–13.4]) and a nonsignificant trend for multiplicative interaction (HR of interaction term, 3.5 [95% CI, 0.8–14.4]; P interaction = 0.09). For the risk of recognized MI, there was some evidence for additive interaction, albeit not statistically significant (relative excess risk due to interaction, 2.0 [95% CI, −2.0 to 9.4]), and no clear evidence for a multiplicative interaction (HR of interaction term, 1.4 [95% CI, 0.4–4.8]; P interaction=0.61).

Participants with CBI and UMI also had the highest mortality rate, with an incident rate of 51.4 per 1000 person‐years. Participants with neither CBI nor UMI had an incident rate of 11.1, those with only UMI a rate of 19.7, and those with only CBI a rate of 29.6 per 1000 person‐years.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that individuals with covert brain infarction were more likely to also have a UMI. The presence of UMI in individuals with CBI markedly elevates the risk of clinical ASCVD, similar for ischemic stroke and recognized MI, and possibly even more in women than in men.

To the best of our knowledge, no published studies had previously investigated the risk of ASCVD with co‐occurring CBI and UMI. Multiple studies have explored the risks of stroke in individuals with CBI and the risk of recognized MI in individuals with UMI, separately of one another. Risks of stroke after CBI and recognized MI after UMI in our study were similar as described in earlier meta‐analyses. 3 , 14 , 38 Our results suggest that the concurrence of CBI and UMI confers additional risk not captured by either separately. Of note, the lower bound of the confidence interval for stroke risk in people with both CBI and UMI in our study exceeded the upper confidence limit for stroke risk in individuals with CBI from prior meta‐analysis, supporting the incremental stroke risk with co‐occurring UMI. 3 Similarly, a recent study among 2282 patients with clinical stroke also observed an increased risk of stroke recurrence in the presence of UMI. 39 These findings may be explained by an increased risk of thromboembolic stroke in patients with impaired cardiac function 40 or a persistent, shared prothrombotic or proinflammatory state. Moreover, co‐occurring CBI and UMI indicates more extensive large‐artery disease (ie, in multiple vessel beds) or a joint burden of large‐artery disease and small‐vessel disease, posing a dual threat for clinical ASCVD and particularly stroke.

The increased risk of ASCVD in patients with concurrent CBI and UMI may prompt special consideration in terms of clinical management in patients with CBI. Current guidelines for CBI by the European Stroke Organization and American Stroke Association recommend cardiovascular risk factor management in line with primary stroke prevention guidelines. 5 , 6 , 41 Treatment with platelet inhibitors is not routinely recommended in the absence of robust data from randomized controlled trials. TIA and stroke trials cannot be applied to chronic CBI, since the strongest benefit of intervention is shortly after the event and the moment of CBI occurrence is uncertain. 42 Awaiting further evidence, clinical decision making requires careful weighing of potential harm and benefits of more stringent preventive treatment, guided by personalized risk estimates. 7 , 43 The 3‐ to 4‐fold increased risk of ASCVD for individuals with a UMI in addition to their CBI, suggests that the number needed to treat to prevent future ASCVD may be much lower in patients with both CBI and UMI than in the group with CBI alone. Performing an ECG is already part of the guidelines to investigate atrial fibrillation in patients with CBI, 5 , 44 and thus provides an opportunity for identifying this high‐risk group. At present, CBIs often go unheeded and unmentioned to patients. 45 Simple risk stratification using an ECG may prompt a preventive approach with more stringent targets for blood pressure and lipid levels, and, we believe, a lower threshold for aspirin initiation in the context of further individual risk factors, bleeding risk, and patients' preferences. Although no randomized trials have been done specifically for treatment of UMI either, evidence of chronic coronary disease generally prompts cardiologists toward more progressive preventive regimens. 23 Based on our findings, new clinical trials in patients with CBI may tailor prespecified subgroup analyses to high‐risk patients.

Although individuals with CBI are twice as likely to also have UMI, 15 , 46 the co‐occurrence of CBI and UMI was still rare in our community‐based cohort (0.4%). Prevalence of UMI in individuals with CBI was higher, at 4.5%, which likely is an underestimation of UMI as ECG detects only transmural infarction, Q waves can resolve over time, and non–ST‐segment–elevation MI do not lead to Q waves. While ECGs are cheaper and easier and carry no contrast‐related risks, contrast enhanced MRI is more sensitive and often used as gold‐standard for UMI detection. Indeed, a meta‐analysis found that ECG has a sensitivity of 13.2% (and a specificity of 95.7%) with MRI as the gold standard, and that MRI‐detected UMI was associated with higher cardiovascular disease risk than ECG‐detected UMI. 14 We had no cardiac MRI data available in the Rotterdam Study population. Additional studies are needed to determine whether co‐occurring CBI and MRI‐detected UMI also carries higher risks of ASCVD. Such clinical cohorts with combined cardiac and brain imaging could also assess whether co‐occurrence is more common in patients with higher prior odds of vascular disease, compared with the general population. Echocardiography is another option for detecting UMI, but recent meta‐analyses did not find any studies that used echocardiography for this purpose. 14 , 47

Strengths of this study include its long follow‐up duration and robust methods for ascertainment of clinical events in a population‐based setting. Certain limitations to this study are also important to consider when interpreting the results. First, we may have missed UMIs on ECG if they were small or obscured by ECG abnormalities due to other disease, such as bundle branch block. Such misclassification would lead to underestimation of the prevalence of UMI and its association with clinical ASCVD. Second, although the interval between ECG and MRI was generally short (weeks), more prolonged intervals in some participants may have led to immortal person‐time bias and underestimation of ASCVD risk. Third, given their incidental nature, we cannot determine the time at which CBI or UMI occurred, and their timing and order of events may well influence subsequent ASCVD risk. Fourth, the reporting of incidental UMIs to participants and their general practitioners could have instigated preventative interventions to lower ASCVD risk. However, we found no substantial changes in antithrombotic and lipid‐lowering medication in response to diagnosis of UMI among affected study participants. Fifth, study participants were younger and healthier than those who did not undergo both MRI and ECG, which may have induced selection bias leading to reduced prevalence of CBI and UMI and incidence of ASCVD. Finally, participants were predominantly White individuals, which may limit generalizability to other races and ethnicities.

In conclusion, individuals with CBI are more likely to also have UMI, and co‐occurring UMI further increases the risk of clinical ASCVD. Investigating UMI in patients with CBI presents a valuable opportunity for clinicians to identify high‐risk patients with CBI who could potentially benefit most from stringent prevention.
