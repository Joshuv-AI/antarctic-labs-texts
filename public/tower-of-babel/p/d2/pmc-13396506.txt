Agricultural fungicides shape soil and sediment reservoirs of multidrug-resistant fungi
Authors: Zhao Qin, Qiang Wang, Qingxiang Yang, Qingqing Wang, Yongjie Li, Haitao Ma, Jin Gao, Xinlei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fungicide resistance in environmental fungi represents a growing One Health challenge, yet its ecological extent and clinical relevance remain poorly studied. Here, we integrate residue monitoring, community profiling, large-scale culture isolation, and mechanistic assays to investigate the resistance of fungi in agricultural soils and adjacent river sediments. The results indicate that fungicides are widely detected in environmental samples and pose substantial ecological risks, particularly carbendazim and tebuconazole. Resistant fungi are also widespread and account for a large proportion of the genera detected in fungal communities. Among >3,900 resistant isolates, we recover 358 multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains showing cross-resistance to multiple agricultural fungicides. Transcriptomic, qPCR, and molecular docking analyses suggest that environmental MDR phenotypes arise from synergistic effects of target-gene alterations, efflux pump overexpression, and adaptive stress responses. Notably, triazole exposure coordinately upregulates virulence genes and mycotoxin biosynthetic clusters, indicating transcriptional activation of genes associated with virulence and secondary metabolism. Together, these findings highlight agricultural landscapes as reservoirs of fungicide-resistant fungi and underscore the need to re-evaluate fungicide practices to protect crop health, ecosystem stability, and the effectiveness of clinical antifungals.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance is one of the most pressing global health challenges of the 21st century. Although bacterial antibiotic resistance has been extensively studied and tightly regulated, antifungal resistance has received far less attention despite its growing impact on agriculture, ecosystems, and human health. Fungi are ubiquitous pathogens capable of infecting plants, animals, and humans, and their control relies heavily on a narrow range of antifungal agents1. Clinical options are particularly limited, with azoles serving as a key frontline therapy. However, widespread resistance is undermining their effectiveness, leaving few alternatives for treating invasive fungal infections.

The public health burden of fungal resistance is alarming. Some invasive fungal infections (IFIs), such as Candida auris and Aspergillus fumigatus, could cause more than 40% mortality rates among immunocompromised patients2–5. Aspergillus fumigatus, a major airborne pathogen, is responsible for more than 200,000 invasive aspergillosis cases annually, and triazole resistance has risen steadily since the late 1990s6,7. Clinical surveillance in Europe has reported itraconazole resistance in some regions, and multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates are becoming increasingly common, with reduced susceptibility also observed to amphotericin B and terbinafine7,8. Equally concerning is the global emergence of Candida auris, an MDR yeast capable of causing hospital outbreaks with mortality rates comparable to bacterial “superbug”9. Although the specific MDR species in environmental and clinical settings may differ, the high occurrence of human-pathogenic fungi in the environment remains alarming. It is therefore important to determine whether agroecosystems constitute a major selective pressure driving the evolution and spread of antifungal resistance.

Agricultural fungicide use is increasingly recognized as a potential contributor to this trend. The global application of fungicides has more than doubled over the past decade, with triazoles accounting for nearly 17% of the market10,11. Azoles are applied extensively to protect crops from fungal diseases, but because they target the same enzyme (Cyp51) as clinical antifungals, their use can impose parallel selective pressures in the environment12. Azole and other fungicide residues have been detected in over 80% of agricultural soils and adjacent water systems, often at concentrations sufficient to sustain long-term selection pressure13–15. Such contamination creates favorable conditions for the emergence and persistence of resistant fungi. Notably, resistant Aspergillus fumigatus isolates carrying mutations identical to those found in clinical strains have been recovered from agricultural soils, providing direct evidence of cross-resistance between environmental and clinical settings16–18. Moreover, resistant fungi have been detected in patients without prior antifungal exposure, consistent with environmental origins18–21.

Despite these risks, key aspects of environmental fungal resistance remain unclear. Agricultural soils are often contaminated with multiple fungicides, creating complex selection pressures that may promote MDR. However, most studies have focused on single compounds and a narrow set of taxa, or have relied on high-throughput sequencing approaches that lack phenotypic resolution22–25. Few studies have systematically examined how chronic, multi-fungicide contamination reshapes fungal communities, promotes multidrug resistance, and drives the evolution of resistance mechanisms. This knowledge gap is particularly concerning because agricultural soils may act as long-term reservoirs of fungicide residues and resistant fungi, with the potential to disseminate highly resistant strains into food systems, aquatic environments, and ultimately clinical settings.

Here, we conducted a comprehensive investigation of fungicide contamination and antifungal-resistant fungal communities in soils from diverse agricultural fields and nearby river sediments. By isolating large numbers of resistant fungi and characterizing their resistance profiles, we provide an ecologically relevant, phenotype-based assessment of environmental antifungal resistance. Focusing on MDR strains, we further examine metabolic adaptations to fungicide stress, including regulation of resistance mechanisms and mycotoxin biosynthesis. Together, this work helps clarify the environmental dimension of fungal resistance and its implications for agricultural sustainability and public health.

Results

Assessment of fungicide residues, ecological risk, and fungal composition in agricultural soils and river sediments

The recovery rates of five fungicides were evaluated at different concentrations using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). All recovery rates were within the acceptable range (70–120%), demonstrating the accuracy of the method (Supplementary Table S1).

A total of 81 samples were used to detect the fungicide residues, including 42 from agricultural soil and 39 from adjacent river sediments. The detection frequencies of the five fungicides were different among samples: CBD was detected in 81 of 81 samples, ASB in 41 of 81 samples, MBT in 68 of 81 samples, TDM in 57 of 81 samples, and TCA in 40 of 81 samples. Measured residue levels ranged from non-detectable to 1.44 mg/kg across all compounds (Fig. 1a). Mean residues of CBD, ASB, MBT, TDM, and TCA in agricultural soils were 0.19, 0.52, 0.28, 0.12, and 0.06 mg/kg, respectively; corresponding means in sediments were 0.14, below the detection limit for ASB, 0.68, 0.02, and 0.001 mg/kg, respectively. In general, the concentrations of fungicide residues in agricultural soil were higher than in river sediments. Ecological risk assessment indicated that CBD posed a high risk at 70.37% of sites, followed by TDM with a high risk at 22.22% of sites. To illustrate this pattern, agricultural soil was used as an example for further analysis. Based on the MiSeq ITS amplicon sequencing data, the composition and diversity of fungal communities in these soils were determined. Alpha-diversity within single fungicide-resistant communities was highest in the ASB and TDM groups (Fig. 2b). Prevalent occurrence of MDR fungi with agriculture and clinical relevance

Community analyses based on ITS sequencing are insufficient for resolving the full extent of environmental fungal resistance. Phenotype-based culture assays are essential for clarifying functional resistance diversity. We isolated a broad panel of resistant strains from farmland soils and river sediments to better clarify the extent of resistance and underlying mechanisms. Fungal strains resistant to individual fungicides were initially isolated by screening on fungicide-supplemented media (n = 3994). After morphological dereplication, 1,261 phenotypically distinct strains were retained for further analysis. Of these, 630 isolates, accounting for 49.96% of the distinct strains, were classified as triazole-resistant. A total of 506 of the triazole-resistant isolates (80.32%) also exhibited resistance to ASB and CBD, indicating pronounced cross-resistance. These results highlight the widespread occurrence of MDR fungi in agricultural soils and river sediments, with a substantial proportion displaying co-resistance across multiple fungicide classes, including triazoles, strobilurins, and benzimidazoles. Molecular identification classified these triazole-resistant strains into various genera, including Meyerozyma spp., Aspergillus spp., Clonostachys spp., Fusarium spp., and Trichoderma spp. (Supplementary Table S2). Ubiquitous genera such as Aspergillus, Fusarium, Alternaria, and Talaromyces constituted the dominant group in the resistant community. Five representative multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates (VegeW19, YamS12, WHJ35, WHMI2, and VegeMI6) were selected for multilocus phylogenetic identification. Based on combined ITS, LSU and protein-coding gene analyses, these strains were affiliated with Aspergillus fumigatus, Fusarium oxysporum, Talaromyces purpureogenus, Alternaria alternata, and Meyerozyma guilliermondii, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1). Detailed locus information and sequence similarity metrics are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Sensitivity profiling of these multidrug-resistant fungal strains to agricultural fungicides revealed substantial resistance at concentrations exceeding typical levels in the field (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table S3). Notably, 97.20%, 68.22%, and 40.19% of the strains remained resistant to TDM, MBT, and TCA even at concentrations 10–30 times higher than the recommended field application rate. Azoxystrobin (ASB; 10 × FR) resistance was observed across 62.62% of the strains. Resistance to all tested fungicides across all concentrations was observed in 19.63% of strains (notably Aspergillus and Meyerozyma). Twenty environmental fungal isolates were selected for further susceptibility testing against eight clinically relevant antifungal agents: itraconazole (ITC), fluconazole (FLU), and voriconazole (VO), griseofulvin (AGF), miconazole (MCL), caspofungin (CAS), flucytosine (AFY), and amphotericin B (AMB) (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table S4). High resistance rates to VO (80.00%) and FLU (90.00%) were observed, whereas 60.00% of the strains remained sensitive to ITC. Resistance rates to AGF, MCL, AFY, AMB, and CAS were 95.00, 70.00, 55.00, 45.00, and 40.00%, respectively. Several isolates, such as Meyerozyma spp., Aspergillus spp., Fusarium spp., and Talaromyces spp., exhibited resistance to most antifungal agents tested, and two strains belonging to Fusarium spp. and Meyerozyma spp. were resistant to all tested clinical antifungals. To quantitatively validate resistance levels, we determined the MICs of five agricultural fungicides for the 85 MDR isolates using a standardized broth microdilution assay (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table S5). MICs spanned a wide range (0.125 – 64 µg mL⁻¹), with many isolates exhibiting values well above those typically observed in sensitive strains. In particular, MICs for triadimefon (TDM), myclobutanil (MBT), and azoxystrobin (ASB) were skewed toward higher values, confirming that resistance was not confined to growth at a single screening concentration but instead reflected elevated tolerance across multiple fungicide classes. qPCR analysis confirmed significant upregulation of genes closely related to the drug resistance pathway, including CYP51, ERG6, PDR, and ABCB1, after fungicide exposure (Supplementary Fig. S2). Amino acid sequence mutations in Cyp51 were translated from transcriptome assemblies of resistant isolates. Structural modeling based on these sequences suggested that resistant strains such as A. fumigatus may exhibit reduced predicted interaction strength between azoles and lanosterol 14α-demethylase (Cyp51). These structural differences were reflected in modeled increases in binding free energy (ΔΔG = + 0.11 to + 1.02 kcal/mol relative to the non-MDR strain) (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Table S7).

Concurrently, resistance may also be supported by enhanced efflux activity, which was inferred from structural modifications in the ABC transporters PDR and ABCB1. Key residues (Ile178, Ile252, Arg342, Glu996) were predicted to improve drug–transporter interactions, potentially facilitating azole extrusion (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table S8). This efflux potentiation acted synergistically with cell wall remodeling via the upregulation of DAL and other associated stress-response genes (Supplementary Fig. S3a). This resistance mechanism was not restricted to A. fumigatus. Similar mechanisms were observed in F. oxysporum, where PDR activity maintained functional dominance even under Cyp51-altered backgrounds (Supplementary Fig. S3b and Supplementary Tables S9, S10).

Flow cytometry was used to assess efflux activity in azole-resistant strains and quality-control strains. Fluorescence intensity decreased gradually over time in all strains. In the quality-control group, antifungal treatment resulted in higher fluorescence than in untreated samples. In contrast, fluorescence decreased after antifungal exposure in resistant strains, consistent with enhanced efflux activity. These results provide direct experimental evidence of increased drug efflux in resistant strains and align with the transcriptomic upregulation of efflux pump genes (Fig. 4d). Fungicide-induced upregulation of virulence and mycotoxin biosynthesis in MDR fungi

A particularly notable finding was the fungicide-associated upregulation of virulence and mycotoxin biosynthetic genes in response to triazole exposure in a subset of MDR fungi. In A. fumigatus, the entire fumagillin biosynthetic pathway (originating from farnesyl pyrophosphate) and fumitremorgin branch genes were coordinately activated (Fig. 5). Comparable expression patterns were observed in T. purpureogenus and F. oxysporum. The expression patterns inferred by qPCR and RNA-Seq were similar (Fig. 6a–c). Genes involved in fumagillin synthesis (AF470 and AF520) and fumitremorgin synthesis (FTMA and FTMF) were significantly upregulated under fungicide stress, suggesting transcriptional activation of these toxin pathways in A. fumigatus. Virulence-associated genes such as SIDA, PLB, and plc were also markedly upregulated, suggesting a potential shift toward increased pathogenic potential under fungicide stress. Discussion

Although bacterial antibiotic resistance has received global attention, fungi represent an equally critical, yet often overlooked, front in the resistance crisis. Drug-resistant fungal pathogens are emerging as a major public health concern, causing substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide. Our findings indicate that fungicide residues are widespread in agricultural soils, where they are associated with shifts in native fungal communities and the occurrence of antifungal resistance. Many fungi acquired MDR and exhibited cross-resistance to clinical antifungal agents. This development may pose a threat to agricultural productivity and complicate the treatment of human infections. Widespread fungicide residues detected in agricultural systems indicated pervasive environmental contamination of agricultural soils, with detection frequencies reaching 100% for certain compounds such as CBD and residue levels posing significant ecological hazards (Fig. 1a). Ecological risk assessment further underscores the severity of this issue, given that high-risk profiles for CBD and TDM were present across 70.37% and 22.22% of sampling sites, respectively (Fig. 1b). Previous studies have demonstrated that pollutants such as fungicides already existed in terrestrial environments, and pollutants derived from terrestrial environments, such as agricultural soils, can be transported into aquatic systems through processes including surface runoff, rainfall-induced erosion, and leaching, thereby contributing to water contamination13–15,26–28. Consistent with this, our detection of multiple fungicide residues in river sediments (Fig. 1a) highlights the accumulation and persistence of these residues despite natural dilution processes. Beyond environmental contamination, the widespread presence of fungicide residues may exert selection pressure on fungal communities and is associated with the occurrence of antifungal resistance12,18,20,29. Such residues were shown to increase the abundance of resistant fungi in soil and aquatic ecosystems29,30. Consistent with these trends, our study detected consistently high abundances of resistant fungi in both soils and sediments (Fig. 2a), confirming long-term exposure-driven adaptation. Notably, resistance was not restricted to rare taxa but was broadly distributed across a large proportion of genera within the fungal community. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the widespread presence of fungicide residues may affect soil health and water quality through diffuse pollution pathways but also fosters resistance within fungal populations.

Environmental fungicide-resistant isolates (n = 1261) revealed an alarming convergence of MDR across agricultural and clinical contexts. Strikingly, 80.32% of triazole-resistant fungi exhibited cross-resistance to azoxystrobin and carbendazim. These findings underscore the capacity of agricultural environments to select for MDR phenotypes with clear clinical relevance. Several isolates showed extreme tolerance to fungicides and resistance profiles that extended to multiple frontline antifungal drugs, consistent with studies of A. fumigatus and other environmental pathogens showing that agricultural azole use has driven cross-resistance to clinical azoles16–18,20,31,32. This pattern is also consistent with studies showing that some fungi can develop resistance to clinical antifungals without prior direct exposure to medical agents19–21. Notably, although not to species, the MDR strains identified here fall in the genera that are associated with invasive infections in immunocompromised hosts, such as Meyerozyma, Aspergillus, and Fusarium33–36. A. fumigatus, for example, is a leading cause of invasive aspergillosis, a severe systemic infection with reported mortality rates of ~ 30–85% even with treatment37. Similarly, although infections caused by M. guilliermondii are less common than those caused by dominant Candida species, they are associated with disproportionately high mortality among at-risk patients36. The detection of these taxa in agricultural settings highlights a critical One Health concern: opportunistic fungi originating in the environment may enter clinical settings via multiple routes, including direct contact, aerial and aquatic dissemination, and foodborne transmission. Adding to this complexity, recent evidence suggests that A. fumigatus can acquire triazole resistance through horizontal gene transfer, potentially accelerating the spread of resistance determinants across fungal populations38. Collectively, these results identify agricultural landscapes as potential reservoirs of highly resistant fungi that can persist in the environment and, under favorable conditions, contribute to resistant infections in clinical settings, with important implications for public health.

Our integrated transcriptomic, qPCR, and molecular docking analyses indicate that fungal MDR is maintained by a synergistic combination of target-site alterations and enhanced drug efflux, consistent with recent findings in fungal pathogens39,40. In azole-resistant isolates, reduced affinity of Cyp51 for triazole ligands, together with elevated ABC-transporter activity —particularly PDR —likely acts in concert to limit intracellular drug accumulation, a hallmark of azole resistance41. The primary azole target, lanosterol 14α-demethylase (Cyp51), was also upregulated under azole stress, which may increase ergosterol biosynthesis and promote membrane remodeling, thereby further reducing fungicide efficacy40,42. Canonical substitutions strongly associated with azole resistance in A. fumigatus (e.g., TR34/L98H and TR46/Y121F/T289A) were not detected in our isolates20,43. Structural modeling based on transcriptome-derived Cyp51 sequences suggested that these variants may influence the Cyp51 active-site conformation and alter hydrogen-bonding (Tyr53, His365, and Gly497) and hydrophobic contacts (Phe219 and Leu76) with azoles. Consistent with this, molecular docking predicted modest reductions in azole–Cyp51 binding strength, with ΔΔG shifts of + 0.11 to + 1.02 kcal/mol relative to the non-MDR strain. These results are based on in silico predictions and transcriptome-derived sequences, and thus require independent genomic and biochemical validation. In parallel, transcriptomic and qPCR analyses showed strong overexpression of efflux pump genes, including ABC transporters (PDR and ABCB1) and MFS transporters (MDR1), across resistant strains. Efflux potentiation was particularly pronounced in F. oxysporum, where PDR overexpression was associated with high-level resistance, supporting a central role for efflux in MDR phenotypes. By contrast, Talaromyces isolates preferentially activated an MFS-mediated route via MDR1 induction, underscoring taxon-specific diversification of efflux strategies. Given the broad substrate specificity of PDR, its overexpression likely cooperates with Cyp51 alterations to yield additive or synergistic resistance effects, as reported in clinical Candida and Aspergillus species44–47.

Although bacteria can produce antifungal metabolites that influence fungal community structure, multiple lines of evidence suggest that the widespread MDR observed here is primarily associated with persistent fungicide residues rather than bacterial antagonism. Relative to fungicide residues, endogenous bacterial antifungal compounds are expected to occur at much lower concentrations and are unlikely to impose the sustained selection required to generate stable MDR. Moreover, the resistant isolates exhibited broad cross-resistance across multiple fungicide classes and clinical triazoles—patterns not readily explained by exposure to bacterial metabolites. Transcriptomic and structural analyses further revealed canonical azole-resistance signatures, including Cyp51 structural alterations, upregulation of the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway, and marked induction of ABC transporters. These molecular responses are well-established consequences of fungicide selection pressure and are not known to be elicited by bacterial antifungal compounds, which more commonly target membranes or signaling pathways and have not been shown to drive Cyp51 mutations or the pronounced efflux-pump induction observed here48,49.

A particularly alarming outcome of this study is that fungicide exposure not only selects for MDR phenotypes but also induces transcriptional activation of virulence- and mycotoxin-related pathways. For the first time, we found that triazole stress coordinately activated the fumagillin and fumitremorgin biosynthetic pathways in A. fumigatus, with similar expression trends in T. purpureogenus and F. oxysporum. Transcriptomic analyses revealed strong upregulation of fumagillin genes (AF470, AF520), fumitremorgin genes (FTMA, FTMF), and qPCR analysis confirmed these expression trends. These results suggest that fungicide exposure may influence mycotoxin biosynthesis in mycotoxigenic fungi beyond its inhibitory effects on growth. In addition, several virulence-associated genes, including SIDA, PLB, and plc, were upregulated relative to the controls, implying a potential increase in pathogenic potential under fungicide stress. Although further confirmation is required, such stress-induced toxin and virulence responses in MDR fungi could have serious consequences, including favoring toxigenic fungi, threatening food safety, and increasing risks to human health. Taken together, these findings indicate that fungicide exposure can select for resistance traits while, in some strains, simultaneously elevating toxin-related and virulence-associated gene expression. Given widespread fungicide residues in soils and river sediments, the abundance of resistant fungi in agricultural environments, and the demonstrated cross-resistance to clinically important antifungals, a concerning picture emerges: agroecosystems may foster reservoirs of MDR fungi with elevated pathogenic potential. These findings have far-reaching implications. The linkage between fungicide residues, antifungal resistance, and transcriptional activation of pathogenicity-related pathways suggests that environmental selection pressures can directly shape pathogenic potential, with consequences for crop protection, food safety, ecosystem stability, and human health. The emergence of environmental fungi with both high resistance and increased virulence-related traits represents a dual threat that could compromise current clinical antifungal therapies and exacerbate the global burden of invasive fungal infections.

Our findings indicated that the widespread presence of fungicide residues is associated with a link between environmental contamination and clinical antifungal resistance. We demonstrated that agricultural soils containing fungicide residues served as reservoirs for fungi capable of resisting multiple agricultural fungicides while also exhibiting cross-resistance to frontline clinical antifungals. We showed that fungicide pressure induced transcriptional activation of virulence factors and mycotoxin biosynthetic pathways, thereby linking antifungal resistance with potential increases in pathogenic capacity. Agroecosystems, therefore, represent critical reservoirs and transmission nodes within the global antifungal resistance network that link environmental contamination to clinical treatment failures.

Methods

Sampling

Soil and sediment samples were collected in the summer of 2022 from agricultural fields and river systems in Henan Province, China. Agricultural soils were obtained from vegetable fields (tomato, cucumber, and lettuce), wheat fields, and yam fields in Xinxiang (35°13′–35°37′N, 113°82′–113°94′E) and Jiaozuo (34°73′–34°95′N, 112°90′–113°08′E). Sediment samples were collected from the Yellow River, the second-largest river in China, and its two tributaries, the Yiluo River and Qin River. Sampling locations along the Yellow River included five sites along the main stream: Mengjin (S-MJ), Gongyi (S-GY), Guobaide (S-GBD), Huayuankou (S-HYK), and Chenqiao (S-CQ) (34°85′–34°93′N, 112°62′–114°48′E). Additional sites included three points along the Yiluo River tributary (S-YR1, S-YR2, and S-YR3; 34°69′–34°80′N, 112°89′–113°04′E), three points along the Qin River tributary (S-QR1, S-QR2, and S-QR3; 35°08′–35°09′N, 113°28′–113°39′E), and two confluence points where the Yiluo River and Qin River join the Yellow River (S-YRH and S-QRH; 34°83′N, 113°07′E; 34°96′N, 113°48′E).

A systematic five-point sampling strategy was applied at each site to ensure spatially representative sampling. For agricultural soils, surface topsoil (0–15 cm) was collected using a sterilized soil auger, targeting the microbially active layer. River sediments were sampled from riparian zones, specifically from the upper 20–30 cm of the riverbed. At each site, three random quadrats were established, yielding a total of 81 samples. Immediately after collection, samples were sealed in sterile polyethylene bags, stored on ice, and transported to the laboratory within 12 h, after which they were stored at – 20 °C until further analysis.

Determination of fungicide residues and ecological risk assessment

The triazole fungicides triadimefon (TDM), myclobutanil (MBT), and tebuconazole (TCA), the benzimidazole fungicide carbendazim (CBD), and the methoxyacrylate fungicide azoxystrobin (ASB) were selected as representative compounds for this study (Yuanye Co., China). Residual levels of these fungicides were quantified in agricultural soil and river sediment samples. Standard curves were established for each fungicide using seven concentrations (0, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1.0 mg/L). Linearity was assessed by calculating the coefficient of determination (R2), which exceeded 0.99 for all tested compounds (Supplementary Table S1). The Limit of Detection (LOD) and Limit of Quantification (LOQ) were calculated following the guidelines of The Fitness for Purpose of Analytical Methods (2025). Data were processed with Chromeleon software. The method was validated for specificity, sensitivity, and accuracy. The LODs and LOQs achieved after nitrogen blow-down concentration were 0.002 - 0.011 μg/mL and 0.025 μg/mL, respectively. The method was further validated for accuracy using recovery experiments, with recovery rates ranging from 73.13% to 123.71%. Standard solutions of each fungicide were mixed with the extract, and peak areas from HPLC analysis were compared with the standards to calculate recovery rates. Environmental samples were pretreated with an optimized QuEChERS (Quick, Easy, Cheap, Effective, Rugged, and Safe) protocol, followed by C18 solid-phase extraction and concentration by nitrogen blow-down50. Quantitative analysis was performed using a Thermo Fisher Vanquish Core liquid chromatography system equipped with an AcclaimTM 120 C18 column, maintained at 35 °C. An isocratic elution was used, with a mobile phase of 60% acetonitrile and 40% water at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min.

The ecological risk assessment of fungicides at each sampling site was conducted by calculating the Risk Quotient (RQ), where ecological risk refers to the likelihood of a fungicide residue affecting the indicator organisms51. The measured residues at each sampling site were designated as the measured environmental concentration (MEC). Algae or aquatic plants and earthworms were selected as the sensitive test species for sediment and soil, respectively. The Predicted No-Effect Concentration (PNEC) was derived from the No-Observed-Effect Concentration (NOEC) for these species obtained from the PPDB database, prioritizing acute effect concentrations when available. Analysis of the fungal community

The composition of fungal communities was assessed by high-throughput sequencing (Shanghai Personalbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Total DNA was extracted from soil samples using the FastDNA™ Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Across samples, DNA yields typically ranged from 100 ng/μL to 200 ng/μL, with A260/280 ratios of ~ 1.8–2.0, indicating DNA of sufficient quality for PCR amplification.

The fungal ITS1 region was amplified using primers ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2 (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′). PCR amplicons were purified using AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, USA) and quantified with a NanoDrop 2000 fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of purified amplicons were pooled, and sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free amplicon workflow, including end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and size selection. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform using a 2 × 250 bp paired-end sequencing chemistry.

For each sample, 66,336 – 151,926 raw paired-end reads were generated. On average, 90.79% of reads passed quality filtering and denoising in QIIME2, yielding 2884 high-quality amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The sequencing datasets were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under accession numbers PRJNA1328220. Raw sequencing reads in FASTQ format were processed using QIIME2 version 2024.252. Reads were quality-filtered and denoised to generate ASVs following the QIIME2 pipeline. Representative sequences were taxonomically assigned using the UNITE database (version 9.0)53.

Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.0.1 (R Core Team, 2020). Microbial α-diversity was calculated using the vegan package (v3.5.3)54. Figures were generated using ggplot255 and dplyr56. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and model fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2). Visualization of microbial community composition analysis was completed using the Wekemo Bioincloud (https://www.bioincloud.tech)57 and the Tutools platform (http://www.cloudtutu.com) with the ggplot2 package for R.

Evaluation of the abundance of putative fungicide-resistant fungi across environmental samples

Isolation and identification of MDR fungi

Individual colonies obtained from fungicide-supplemented plates were carefully selected and purified through successive streaking to obtain pure isolates resistant to single fungicides. These isolates were then screened for putative MDR. Specifically, purified colonies were first inoculated onto ½-dextrose PDA medium containing the three triazole fungicides, TDM, MBT, and TCA, at the same concentrations used in the initial selection. Colonies that successfully grew under these conditions were subsequently transferred to ½-dextrose PDA medium supplemented with all five fungicides (TDM, MBT, TCA, CBD, and ASB). Strains capable of forming colonies on both media were designated as putative MDR candidates exhibiting cross-resistance.

Taxon assignment of MDR isolates was performed by amplifying the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region using primers ITS1 (5’-TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3’) and ITS4 (5’-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3’)59. Pure cultures were preserved as agar plugs in 30% (v/v) glycerol at – 80 °C. Fungal species were identified using a multilocus approach based on the ITS region and six additional loci. Genomic DNA was extracted using a Plant/Fungal DNA Kit (Simgen, Hangzhou, China) and quantified before PCR amplification. PCR was performed using gene-specific primers: ITS1/ITS4 for the ITS region; EF1-728F/EF2-986R for TEF-1; fRPB2-5F/fRPB2-7cR for RPB2; NL1/NL4 for LSU; Bt2a/Bt2b for BenA; gpd1/gpd2 for GAPDH; and CaM-F/CaM-R for CaM60–64 (Supplementary Table S11). PCR products were sequenced by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Resulting sequences were queried against the NCBI database, and a 98% similarity threshold was used for preliminary species-level identification.

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using IQ-TREE (v2.0) based on multilocus sequence data retrieved from NCBI GenBank or generated in this study (Supplementary Table S12). Sequences were aligned with MAFFT (v7.475) using default parameters65, and ambiguously aligned regions were removed using trimAI. The ITS region, TEF-1, BenA, and other loci were concatenated to generate a multilocus dataset. The best-fit substitution model and partitioning scheme were selected using PartitionFinder2 based on AICc66. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using maximum likelihood analysis with 1000 bootstrap replicates67. All analyses were conducted in the PhyloSuite platform68. The resulting tree was visualized in iTOL (v6.5), and support values were annotated69.

Antifungal susceptibility testing of MDR isolates

Sensitivity to agricultural fungicides

To assess the sensitivity of MDR isolates to agricultural fungicides, serial concentrations of TDM, MBT, TCA, CBD, and ASB were prepared at 1–30 × the recommended field application rates (Supplementary Table S13). Filter paper disks (6 mm in diameter) were soaked in each fungicide solution for 2 h, air-dried, and placed onto PDA plates previously inoculated with fungal suspensions. For sporulating molds, spore suspensions were prepared by gently scraping conidia from 7-day-old cultures grown on ½-strength dextrose PDA, resuspending them in sterile physiological saline, and adjusting the concentration to 106 spores/mL using a hemocytometer. For non-sporulating isolates (e.g., yeasts or molds that did not produce conidia), cell suspensions were prepared by collecting fresh colonies, resuspending them in sterile physiological saline, and adjusting the cell density to an OD600 corresponding to 106 cells/mL. Sterile filter paper disks without fungicide were placed in the center of each plate as negative controls. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 3-4 days. The sensitivity of isolates was classified based on inhibition zone size (d, mm) as follows: d ≤ 6, not sensitive; 6 < d < 10 (+), low sensitivity; 10 ≤ d < 15 (++), moderate sensitivity; 15 ≤ d < 20 (+++), high sensitivity; and d ≥ 20 (++++), extremely sensitive70.

Sensitivity to clinical antifungal drugs

To evaluate the response of MDR isolates to clinical antifungals, susceptibility testing was performed on Mueller–Hinton agar supplemented with 0.5 μg/mL methylene blue. Commercial antifungal disks (Liofilchem, Italy) included itraconazole (ITC), fluconazole (FLU), miconazole (MCL), flucytosine (AFY), amphotericin B (AMB), griseofulvin (AGF), caspofungin (CAS), and voriconazole (VO). For inoculation, 100 µL of each fungal suspension (106 spores/mL) was evenly spread across Mueller–Hinton agar plates using a sterile cell spreader to produce a uniform lawn. Then, antifungal disks were applied aseptically with sterile forceps, ensuring even spacing. Plates were incubated at 35 °C for 24 – 48 h. Zones of inhibition were measured, and susceptibility was determined according to the standardized interpretive criteria of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2010, 2018)71,72. Isolates were classified as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant according to the established breakpoints for each antifungal drug and fungal species (Supplementary Table S14).

The following reference strains were used as quality controls: Talaromyces purpureogenus (CICC 41734/CGMCC 3.12064), Meyerozyma guilliermondii (ATCC 6260), Alternaria alternata (CGMCC 3.4255), Aspergillus fumigatus (GDMCC 3.627/ATCC MYA-3627), and Fusarium oxysporum (GDMCC 3.696/CGMCC 3.18025).

Determination of MIC values in multidrug-resistant fungi

Antifungal susceptibility was evaluated by determining minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) using a broth microdilution method with minor modifications of CLSI guidelines (CLSI M27 and M38)73,74. Fungal isolates were grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 28 °C for 5–7 days. Spores were harvested in 0.01% (v/v) sterile Tween−20, filtered through a 40 µm mesh, and adjusted to 1–5 × 10⁴ spores/mL using a hemocytometer. Antifungal agents were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare 3200 µg/mL stock solutions and then serially diluted twofold in RPMI 1640 supplemented with MOPS (pH 7.0) to generate 10 concentrations ranging from 0.03 to 16 µg/mL. Because fungicides are applied at substantially higher concentrations in agricultural settings, MICs for fungicides were determined over a wider range (0.125 – 64 µg/mL) using the same dilution and assay procedure.

Assays were performed in 96-well microplates by adding 100 µL of each drug dilution and 100 µL of fungal suspension per well, together with a growth control (drug-free) and a blank control (sterile medium). Plates were incubated at 35 °C for 48 h for filamentous fungi or 24 h for yeasts, and the endpoint was defined visually as complete inhibition of growth (clear wells). Susceptible quality-control strains, including Talaromyces purpureogenus, Meyerozyma guilliermondii, Alternaria alternata, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Fusarium oxysporum, were included to verify assay performance. MICs were defined as the lowest concentration that completely inhibited visible growth, and isolates with elevated MICs were classified as fungicide-resistant.

Transcriptome analysis of MDR fungi under fungicide exposure

The MDR isolates Aspergillus fumigatus, Fusarium oxysporum, and Talaromyces purpureogenus, all of which were obtained in this study, were selected for transcriptome sequencing to investigate resistance mechanisms. Based on growth curves, fungicide treatment was applied at the mid–late exponential phase (36 h). Growth-rate assays under triazole exposure were performed to determine the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) for each strain. Spore suspensions (106 spores/mL) were inoculated into potato dextrose broth and incubated with shaking at 28 °C for 36 h. Fungicides were then added at their respective EC50 concentrations, and cultures were incubated for an additional 6 h (treatment group, n = 3). Untreated cultures served as controls (n = 3). Mycelia were harvested by centrifugation and subjected to RNA extraction for transcriptome sequencing and qPCR validation.

Total RNA was extracted from control and fungicide-treated mycelia using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). RNA quality was assessed, and libraries were constructed only from samples meeting the following criteria: concentration > 200 ng/μL, OD260/280 ratio 1.8–2.2, and total yield ≥ 5 µg. Eukaryotic mRNA was enriched with oligo(dT)-conjugated magnetic beads, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA using random hexamer primers. Second-strand cDNA was synthesized, end-repaired, adenylated at the 3′ end, and ligated to sequencing adapters. The resulting cDNA libraries were PCR-amplified and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X plus platform (Shanghai Majorbio Co., Ltd., China). Raw sequences were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the accession number PRJNA1328230.

Adapters, low-quality reads, and reads containing > 5% ambiguous bases (N) were removed using fastp (v0.23.2) with default parameters. For Aspergillus fumigatus, clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (A. fumigatus A1163; assembly GCA_000150145.1) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) with default parameters75–82. Mapped reads were assembled using StringTie (v2.2.1), and gene expression levels were quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) using RSEM (v1.3.3). For Fusarium oxysporum and Talaromyces purpureogenus, transcriptome analysis was conducted using a reference-free workflow75,78–80,83–89. Clean reads were de novo assembled using Trinity (v2.15.1) with default parameters. Redundant transcripts were reduced using CD-HIT (v4.8.1) (sequence identity threshold = 0.95), and assembly quality was evaluated using TransRate (v1.0.3) and BUSCO (v5.4.3). Functional annotation of assembled transcripts was performed by searching against the NR, COG, and KEGG databases using DIAMOND (v2.0.15) with an E-value cutoff of 1.0 × 10⁻⁵. Gene expression levels were estimated using TPM values calculated by RSEM. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the following thresholds: |log2FC | ≥ 1, p < 0.05, and false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05. Functional enrichment of DEGs was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses in DAVID, with FDR < 0.05 considered significant.

qPCR validation of differential gene expression

To validate the RNA-seq results and assess fungicide-induced transcriptional changes, representative genes were assayed, including ergosterol biosynthesis genes (ERG6, CYP51), drug efflux transporters (PDR, ABCB1), and stress response regulators (SIDA, PLB, plc, OMTA, OMTB, AF470, AF520, FTMA, and FTMF). All qPCR primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S15. Fungal cultures were prepared under the same conditions as the transcriptome sequencing experiment (36 h of growth followed by 6 h of fungicide exposure). RNA extraction and quality assessment were performed as described above. One µg of total RNA per sample was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. qPCR reactions were carried out with 1 μL of cDNA as template under the following thermal cycling conditions: 95 °C for 120 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method, with VAMP used as the reference gene for normalization due to its stable expression under fungicide stress90.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking was conducted to investigate the structural interactions between fungicides and target proteins (Cyp51 and PDR). Canonical protein sequences of Aspergillus fumigatus Cyp51 (XP_752137.1) and PDR (XP_755847.1), as well as homologs from Fusarium oxysporum, were retrieved from the NCBI database (KAH7214055.1, KAH7215207.1). Corresponding protein sequences from resistant isolates were obtained from transcriptome assemblies. Homology models of Cyp51 and PDR were generated using SWISS-MODEL. Because homology modeling of proteins carrying single-point mutations can introduce uncertainty in local structural predictions, particularly around the substituted residue, we interpreted these models cautiously and considered them as approximations of the true protein conformations. Ligand structures of triazole and other fungicides were downloaded from PubChem and energy-minimized prior to docking. Docking simulations were performed with AutoDock 4.2.6, and each docking experiment was run in triplicate to ensure reproducibility91. Top-ranked binding poses were evaluated based on binding free energy (ΔG) and key molecular interactions. Significant binding energy shifts (ΔG ≤ – 5.0 kcal/mol) between non-MDR strain and mutant proteins based on comparisons of structurally similar regions were interpreted as indicative of altered fungicide affinity, but we acknowledge that these predictions require experimental validation. Protein–ligand interactions were visualized and analyzed using PyMol software (version 2.6) and PLIP.

Multidrug efflux activity assay

Multidrug efflux activity in MDR fungi was assessed using a rhodamine 6 G (R6G) efflux assay as a proxy for azole-associated efflux activity92. Quality-control strains and MDR isolates were cultured in potato dextrose broth (PDB) at the mid–late exponential phase. Spores were harvested, filtered through a 40 µm mesh to remove hyphal fragments, and adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ spores/mL in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). To deplete intracellular ATP, suspensions were incubated in glucose-free PBS at 30 °C for 40 min. R6G was then added to a final concentration of 10 µM, and samples were incubated at 30 °C with shaking (100 rpm) in the dark for 30 min. Cells were placed on ice for 5 min, centrifuged twice at 4 °C, and resuspended in efflux buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% glucose), either without fungicide (control) or with fungicide supplementation (treatment). R6G efflux in response to azole fungicides was subsequently quantified by flow cytometry (Beckman CytoFLEX, USA). Each experiment included a non-fluorescent blank control.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes (n = 3 biological replicates per group) were selected based on established conventions in microbial ecology studies and practical constraints of experimental feasibility.

No other data were excluded from the analyses. All remaining data from biological replicates were included in the final dataset. All experiments were performed with biological replicates (n = 3) to ensure statistical reproducibility. Key findings were validated across independent experimental batches. Detailed protocols for sample processing, DNA extraction, qPCR, and sequencing are provided in the Methods section to facilitate replication by other researchers.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). For comparisons among multiple groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test was used. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact p-values and statistical test details are provided in the figure legends.
