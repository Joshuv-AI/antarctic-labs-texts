Guidelines for the management of aortic stenosis: A methodologic review of American college of cardiology/American heart association (ACC/AHA) and the European society of cardiology/European association for cardio-thoracic surgery (ESC/EACTS) recommendations
Authors: Milan Milojevic, Michael A Borger, Aleksandar Nikolic, Patrick O Myers, Faisal G Bakaeen
Published: 2025

Varying methodologies lead to different recommendations for management of aortic stenosis.

Central Message

Due to different development processes, ACC/AHA and ESC/EACTS diverge on key aortic stenosis recommendations. A unified development method is needed to align guidance and rebuild clinician confidence.

Aortic stenosis (AS) is a progressive and increasingly prevalent disease that burdens health care systems worldwide.1 The global prevalence of AS rose from 46 cases per 100,000 in 1990 to approximately 116 per 100,000 in 2019.2 This challenge is projected to continue increasing sharply, driven by population aging and improved diagnostics and access to care. In parallel, the pressing clinical and economic impact drives a focus on value-based care, emphasizing the importance of identifying patients most likely to benefit from specific therapeutic interventions.

Clinical practice guidelines are pivotal in supporting evidence-based decision-making and ensuring optimal patient care. However, even when developed from the same “robust” body of evidence, guidelines issued by different organizations can significantly diverge in their recommendations. This is particularly evident in the management of valvular heart disease, for which notable differences exist between the treatment recommendations for AS of the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) and those of the European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (ESC/EACTS).3,4

The ACC/AHA and ESC/EACTS guidelines represent the most widely referenced documents in medical literature for the management of AS. Although both provide structured patient care frameworks, their recommendations diverge substantially in several areas, notably in the choice between surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) and transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), as well as the age thresholds used to guide prosthetic valve selection once SAVR is indicated. These discrepancies carry substantial clinical implications and may erode clinician confidence in guidelines. The resulting uncertainty, particularly regarding treatment modality selection, may partly explain why current practice patterns are increasingly misaligned with formal recommendations.5, 6, 7

After the Food and Drug Administration approval of TAVI for low-risk patients in August 2019, registry data showed that by 2021, 47.5% of patients younger than 65 years of age underwent TAVI.8 More recent analyses suggest an even greater shift, with some cohorts reporting that more than one half of patients younger than 60 years are now treated with TAVI.9 This observed trend persists despite ACC/AHA guidelines strongly recommending SAVR over TAVI in low-risk patients younger than 65 years of age and even though an Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality (STS-PROM) score <4%, a proposed threshold of low procedural risk, does not equate to biological youth or predict long-term life expectancy across all age groups.10 Notably, patients younger than 65 years of age remain significantly underrepresented in low-risk TAVI and SAVR trials (eg, comprising only 7% in Placement of Aortic Transcatheter Valves 3 [PARTNER 3] trial), leaving a critical evidence gap that precludes even hypothesis-generating insights for future trial comparisons. This growing disconnect between practice patterns and formal guidance raises pressing questions about the root causes of such divergence. A major factor may be the absence of a shared development methodology between North American and European guidelines, which contributes to contradictory recommendations that undermine the credibility and unformal practical utility of society guidelines.

ACC/AHA versus ESC/EACTS Recommendations for AS

ESC/EACTS released an updated guideline in the August 2025 update, but because no parallel ACC/AHA revision exists, our analyses focus on the 2020 ACC/AHA and 2021 ESC/EACTS versions, issued 8 months apart, so that observed differences reflect the development of methodology, not newly published studies.

The 2020 ACC/AHA guideline provides comprehensive recommendations covering diagnosis, follow-up, medical therapy, and the choice between SAVR and TAVI. The 2021 ESC/EACTS guideline follows a similar structure but is more compact and narrower in scope, with recommendations focused mainly on determining who should undergo intervention, when, and by which approach. Both guidelines assign more than one half of their recommendations to Class of Recommendation (CoR) I (52% for ACC/AHA, 53% for ESC/EACTS) and 5% to Class III, reflecting strong “do” or “do not do” guidance. However, they differ markedly in the degree of scientific rigour covering these recommendations, as reflected by the assigned level of evidence (LoE): nearly two-thirds of ESC/EACTS recommendations are based solely on expert opinion (LoE C), compared with just 15% in the ACC/AHA document. Conversely, LoE A was assigned to 24% of ACC/AHA recommendations but only one in the ESC/EACTS guideline.

Decision Between SAVR and TAVI

The guidelines take broadly similar positions on when to intervene in severe symptomatic AS, yet differ noticeably on how to intervene and on prosthesis choice once SAVR is planned. Both groups agree that age, surgical risk, expected valve durability, and patient values should guide the TAVI-versus-SAVR decision, yet their age cut-offs differ markedly:

I. ACC/AHA: In patients aged younger than 65 years or with a projected life expectancy >20 years, SAVR is recommended (CoR I, LoE A). For patients aged ≥80 years or younger with a life expectancy <10 years and suitable transfemoral access, transfemoral TAVI is recommended (CoR I, LoE A). In the intermediate age group (65-80 years), either SAVR or transfemoral TAVI is recommended, with the modality to be selected through individualized shared decision-making (CoR I, LoE A).

II. ESC/EACTS: SAVR is recommended for patients younger than 75 years with low predicted surgical risk (STS-PROM or European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II <4%) (CoR I, LoE B). TAVI is recommended for patients aged ≥75 years or with high surgical risk (STS-PROM or European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II >8%) (CoR I, LoE A). For patients not meeting either criterion, the choice between SAVR and TAVI should be made based on clinical, anatomical, and procedural factors (CoR I, LoE B).

Prosthesis Choice for SAVR

Age thresholds for prosthesis selection also differ between the 2 guidelines. The ACC/AHA suggest mechanical valves for patients younger than 50 years, bioprosthetic valves for those older than 65 years, and individualized decision-making for those aged 50 to 65 years (all graded as CoR IIa, LoE B). In contrast, the ESC/EACTS guideline shifts these age boundaries approximately one decade older, advising mechanical valves for patients younger than 60 years (CoR IIa, LoE B), bioprosthetic valves for those older than 65 years (CoR IIa, LoE C), and either valve type between 60 and 65 years, on the basis of individualized consideration of factors such as anticoagulation tolerance, reproductive planning, and risk of reoperation (CoR IIa, LoE C).

Standards for Guidelines Development

A critical factor underlying the observed differences in recommendations lies in the methodologic frameworks adopted by the respective guideline developers. The development of clinical practice guidelines should follow rigorous adherence to standardized methodologic protocols to minimize bias. Various health care organizations and agencies have proposed standards to improve guideline development's rigour and reliability. Among the most recognized is the Institute of Medicine (IOM) framework, outlined in Clinical Practice Guidelines We Can Trust,11 which defines several core principles for trustworthy guidelines: (1) transparency, (2) balanced expert inclusion, (3) conflict of interests management, (4) systematic reviews, (5) critical evidence appraisal, (6) clear articulation of recommendations, (7) external review, and (8) timely updates. Despite the apparent clarity of these principles and the existence of tools to evaluate IOM compliance,12 many guidelines still fail to meet fundamental academic standards.13 Adherence remains inconsistent, often justified by publication delays and cost concerns,14 without mechanisms to enforce and incentivize methodological compliance and timely updates.

How ACC/AHA and ESC/EACTS Guidelines are Developed

Although IOM standards are hypothetically endorsed, guideline development in cardiovascular medicine remains inconsistent, with significant methodological variability. According to the ACC/AHA document, the Joint Committee on Clinical Practice Guidelines appointed a multidisciplinary writing group to represent the broader cardiovascular community.3 However, specific professional disciplines and their proportional representation were not formally defined. Fewer than 50% of members, including Co-chairs, could hold relevant conflicts of interest. Routine systematic reviews and formal risk-of-bias assessments were not required, although they could be commissioned for “critical” questions. Recommendations were approved by a simple majority (50% + 1) of eligible voting members. Notably, the only 2 surgeon members of the writing committee abstained from voting on the AS section as the result of industry relationships. Cost-value analyses were limited to selected interventions for which economic factors were expected to influence decisions, although the extent and interpretation of that influence remained unclear. The criteria for selecting external reviewers and validation procedures were not explicitly described. The reviewer panel included 43 members, primary content reviewers for the Joint Committee or ACC/AHA, with only 2 officially from the AHA and none from surgical societies endorsing the document.3

ESC and EACTS, by contrast, created a methodologic framework by aligning elements from their respective methodologies.4 The charter defined the writing committee as two-thirds ESC and one-third EACTS representatives. It also defined a methodologic team with 2 delegates per society with expertise in epidemiology and biostatistics, who were responsible for appraising observational studies and trials comparing TAVI and SAVR. Recommendations required at least 75% approval of eligible voting members. Co-chairs could have industry relationships if annual income remained less than €10,000; other members faced no specific income limits but were prohibited from direct industry employment. Given the variability in European reimbursement models, cost-effectiveness analyses were not used. The external review panel comprised 31 members, mirroring the composition of the writing committee (two-thirds ESC, one-third EACTS). One review coordinator from each society was designated and granted veto authority. The draft underwent 3 structured review rounds, including comments from 31 reviewers and more than 50 national cardiac societies, who could discuss Class I and III recommendations. After approval by the review coordinators, the document was submitted for endorsement by 31 ESC Guidelines Committee members and 12 EACTS Council members.

Clinical Impact and Underlying Causes of Discrepancies in Treatment Recommendations

Although differences between guidelines may appear modest, their clinical consequences are substantial. For example, a 5- to 10-year change in age thresholds can shift treatment allocation for 18% to 35% of low-risk patients, directly influencing SAVR versus TAVI decisions across a substantial patient population.8

These discrepancies are not incidental but reflect more profound methodologic divergences in guideline development. A prime example is the use of age-based thresholds. Despite their direct influence on clinical decision-making, these cut-offs are not directly supported by inclusion criteria from pivotal randomized trials. As a consequence, the strength of evidence assigned to these recommendations differs markedly: ACC/AHA assigns LoE A to all 3 age-based recommendations, whereas ESC/EACTS assigns LoE A to only one and LoE B to the others, although the underlying trials are graded mainly as high risk of bias.4 Further divergence stems from committee structure; ESC/EACTS requires cross-disciplinary consensus aligned with the heart team model, whereas ACC/AHA permits voting within a single speciality group, even in interspecialty disagreement. These methodologic differences influence recommendations credibility and may substantially contribute to increasing misalignment between formal guidelines and real-world practice.

Process Standardization and Necessary Steps Toward Improvements

To improve consistency and strengthen trust in future clinical guidelines, a more transparent and harmonized development process is needed. This begins with recognizing methodologic gaps and establishing clear, enforceable standards for rigorous guideline development. A key initial step is the creation of a framework for selecting multidisciplinary writing committees with balanced representation. Equally important is the rigorous evaluation of evidence through standardized risk-of-bias assessments and grading, along with ensuring access for independent methodological teams to “closed” industry-sponsored or proprietary data. The process should also include robust consensus-building procedures, and patient involvement to support shared decision-making, enhance credibility, and promote broader adoption across healthcare systems.

Achieving this ultimate goal requires collaboration among professional organizations, health care policymakers, and patient advocacy groups, where engagement remains limited. Nevertheless, promising developments are emerging. One example is the recent joint methodology developed by the American Association for Thoracic Surgery, EACTS, the STS and the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons.15 This initiative marks an essential step toward harmonizing methodologies and clinical practice guidelines across cardiothoracic surgical societies. However, given the type of guidelines these societies produce, their influence will likely remain concentrated on the periprocedural aspects of care. Broader diagnostic and therapeutic decisions are often shaped within frameworks established by larger nonsurgical organizations that operate under distinct methodological standards. Although this surgical-led initiative is highly valuable, achieving broader alignment will require integrating these efforts into interdisciplinary and cross-specialty guideline development frameworks.

In parallel to efforts toward methodologic harmonization in trans-Atlantic guidelines, clinicians worldwide continue to face a fragmented landscape of national and specialty society documents. Obtaining a complete overview is nearly impossible, as many guidelines are issued in local languages, appear on nonindexed platforms, and follow different update cycles; however, the proliferation itself highlights the need for process solutions rather than counting exercises. Accordingly, the creation of a public registry of cardiovascular guideline projects with planned update cycles and version control, intersociety methodological alignment (eg, shared evidence grading, risk-of-bias tools) with formal cross-endorsement pathways, and minimum transparency standards (eg, indexed availability). These measures would help in reducing duplication, minimize small but consequential divergences in recommendations, and improve consistency in clinical practice.

Conclusions

Clinical practice guidelines for the management of AS shape health care policy and inform thousands of treatment decisions each day. Yet the 2 most influential guidelines, from ACC/AHA and ESC/EACTS, show notable discrepancies, particularly regarding TAVI versus SAVR, thereby diminishing trust in guideline documents and create confusion across key stakeholders at the point of decision. In light of inconclusive evidence to guide lifelong management for most patients, these divergences largely reflect fundamental methodologic differences in guideline development, including the composition of writing committee, approaches to evidence assessment, consensus-building procedures, voting thresholds, and external review processes, which collectively contribute to misalignment between authoritative guideline sources.

With the updated ESC/EACTS guidelines released in August 2025,16 and the new ACC/AHA guidelines not expected until 2026, meaningful alignment remains unlikely. Without a shared development framework and formal, ongoing communication between the writing committees, there is a considerable likelihood that recommendations for the management of AS will diverge further. It is time for leading cardiovascular societies to establish open communication channels and engage in structured methodological dialogue among major guideline developers. Finally, adopting a unified, transparent framework is imperative to replace fragmented systems, restore clinician confidence, and ensure that patient care is guided by consistent, evidence-based, globally trusted recommendations.
