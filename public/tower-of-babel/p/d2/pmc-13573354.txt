Association between atherogenic index of plasma and MASLD in patients with type 2 diabetes: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Aysun Seker, Seydahmet Akın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is highly prevalent in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated as the logarithmic ratio of triglycerides to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), reflects atherogenic dyslipidemia; however, its association with MASLD specifically in patients with T2DM remains insufficiently characterized.MethodsIn this retrospective cross-sectional study, 216 adults with T2DM who underwent abdominal ultrasonography between January and September 2025 were evaluated. MASLD was diagnosed based on hepatic steatosis in the presence of metabolic dysfunction. Clinical, anthropometric, and biochemical variables were recorded. Comparative analyses, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses, and multivariable logistic regression models were performed to assess the association between AIP and MASLD and to compare its performance with other non-invasive metabolic indices.ResultsMASLD was identified in 163 patients (75.5%). Individuals with MASLD had higher body mass index (BMI), fasting glucose, triglycerides, uric acid, and AIP levels, and lower HDL cholesterol (all p < 0.01). ROC analysis demonstrated a modest discriminative ability of AIP for MASLD (AUC = 0.690, 95% CI 0.62–0.76, p < 0.001), with an optimal cutoff value of 0.068 yielding 66.9% sensitivity and 67.9% specificity. In logistic regression analyses, AIP showed a significant association with MASLD in both univariate analysis (OR 14.66, 95% CI 4.35–54.94, p < 0.001) and after adjustment for BMI and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) (OR 10.40, 95% CI 2.71–44.30, p < 0.001). Similarly, the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index remained independently associated with MASLD in adjusted models (OR 2.81, 95% CI 1.54–5.43, p = 0.001), whereas Fatty liver index (FLI) and Hepatic steatosis index (HSI) were not significantly associated with MASLD.ConclusionAIP was independently associated with MASLD in patients with T2DM; however, its discriminative performance was modest and did not exceed that of its individual lipid components. Introduction

MASLD is a recently adopted terminology that replaces non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), emphasizing metabolic dysfunction rather than alcohol exclusion as the key diagnostic criterion. According to current consensus definitions, MASLD is characterized by the presence of hepatic steatosis together with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, including overweight/obesity, T2DM, or metabolic dysregulation [1, 2]. MASLD encompasses a continuum from simple steatosis to advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma and has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver disease globally. Recent meta-analyses estimate that it affects nearly 30% of adults, with a consistent increase among younger and obese individuals [3–5].

Patients with T2DM represent a particularly high-risk population, with MASLD prevalence frequently exceeding 60–70% depending on the diagnostic methods and population studied [1, 6]. The coexistence of T2DM and MASLD accelerates fibrosis progression, elevates hepatocellular carcinoma risk, and substantially increases cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Beyond hepatic complications, this overlap is associated with poorer glycemic control, greater treatment intensity, and higher healthcare expenditures, underscoring the importance of early detection and risk stratification in diabetic populations [1].

The pathophysiological link between T2DM and MASLD is largely mediated by insulin resistance and atherogenic dyslipidemia. Insulin resistance promotes hepatic de novo lipogenesis, impairs lipid oxidation, increases free fatty acid flux to the liver, and enhances triglyceride accumulation within hepatocytes. Concurrently, elevated triglycerides and reduced HDL-C levels reflect disordered lipid metabolism and systemic metabolic stress, both of which contribute to hepatic fat deposition, lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signaling pathways that drive disease progression [7, 8].

Although liver biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard, its invasiveness and limited feasibility restrict routine clinical application. Ultrasonography is more accessible but lacks sensitivity for early-stage disease. Furthermore, commonly used non-invasive indices such as the Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4) and the AST-to-Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) were primarily developed to estimate advanced fibrosis rather than hepatic steatosis itself [9, 10]. In contrast, steatosis-specific indices such as FLI and HSI have been developed to estimate hepatic fat accumulation using routine clinical and laboratory parameters [11, 12].

AIP, defined as log10 (triglycerides/HDL cholesterol), is a simple composite marker that reflects atherogenic dyslipidemia and reflects the interaction between lipid metabolism abnormalities and insulin resistance [13–15]. Elevated AIP levels have been associated with obesity, hypertension, cardiometabolic risk, endothelial dysfunction, and systemic inflammation. Emerging evidence further suggests potential links between AIP and hepatic steatosis and MASLD-related cardiometabolic burden [16].

However, data regarding the independent relationship between the AIP and MASLD in patients with T2DM remain limited, particularly in studies comparing AIP with established steatosis- and fibrosis-based non-invasive indices [17]. Moreover, comparative evaluations of AIP against both fibrosis-oriented and steatosis-specific non-invasive indices in diabetic populations are scarce. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the association between AIP and MASLD in individuals with T2DM and to compare its discriminative performance with established non-invasive indices.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cross-sectional study included patients with T2DM who were followed at the internal medicine outpatient clinic of a tertiary-care hospital between January and September 2025. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the local institutional ethics committee (Approval No: 2025/010.99/21/25; Date: 30 October 2025). Due to the retrospective design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. The manuscript was prepared in compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged ≥ 18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM and available ultrasonographic and laboratory data obtained during routine clinical evaluation. Exclusion criteria were: (1) significant alcohol consumption (> 30 g/day for men and > 20 g/day for women), (2) known chronic liver diseases (hepatitis B or C infection, autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson’s disease, or hemochromatosis), (3) use of hepatotoxic medications (including amiodarone, methotrexate, or long-term corticosteroids), (4) active malignancy, systemic inflammatory disorders, or acute infectious diseases, and (5) incomplete clinical or laboratory data.

After applying the eligibility criteria, a total of 216 patients were included in the final analysis. Because the study included all consecutive eligible patients during the predefined study period, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. However, a post-hoc power analysis indicated that the final sample size provided more than 80% statistical power to detect moderate effect sizes.

Clinical and laboratory assessment

Demographic characteristics (age and sex), anthropometric measurements, and laboratory parameters were retrieved from electronic medical records. BMI was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m²).

Biochemical variables included fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-C, and uric acid levels. All laboratory measurements were performed after overnight fasting using standardized automated analyzers.

Medication history was reviewed, including the use of metformin, insulin, sulfonylureas, pioglitazone, statins, sodium–glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors.

Definitions

Metabolic Dysfunction–Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD)

MASLD was defined according to the 2023 international consensus criteria. Hepatic steatosis was assessed by abdominal ultrasonography and graded on a semi-quantitative scale from 0 to 3. Participants with steatosis grades 1–3 were classified as MASLD-positive, whereas those with grade 0 were considered MASLD-negative. As all participants had T2DM, the metabolic dysfunction criterion required for MASLD diagnosis was inherently fulfilled. Ultrasonographic examinations were performed by an experienced radiologist who was blinded to clinical and laboratory data.

Calculated indices

AIP, FLI, HSI, TyG, FIB-4, and APRI were calculated using previously validated formulas. The formulas and variables used for these indices are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed to assess the discriminative ability of AIP and other non-invasive indices for MASLD detection. The area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated. Optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index.

Comparative ROC analyses were additionally conducted to determine whether AIP provided incremental discriminative value beyond its individual lipid components. Therefore, the diagnostic performance of AIP was directly compared with a combined triglyceride and HDL-C model.

Binary logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between metabolic indices and the presence of MASLD. First, univariate logistic regression models were constructed to examine the crude associations between each metabolic index—AIP, FLI, HSI, TyG, FIB-4, and APRI—and the presence of MASLD.

Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression models were performed separately for each index to determine their independent associations with MASLD after adjustment for potential metabolic confounders. These multivariable models were adjusted for BMI and HbA1c, which are clinically relevant determinants of metabolic dysfunction and hepatic steatosis. In addition, because waist circumference was significantly different in the bivariate analysis, supplementary multivariable models including waist circumference as an additional covariate were performed where appropriate. Waist circumference was not additionally included in the FLI model because it is already incorporated into the FLI formula. Sensitivity analyses were also performed using multivariable logistic regression models further adjusted for cardiometabolic medication use, including statins, fibrates, metformin, insulin, pioglitazone, SGLT2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, sulfonylureas, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ACE inhibitors/ARBs), beta-blockers, and diuretics.

Prior to multivariable modeling, Pearson correlation analysis and variance inflation factor (VIF) analyses were performed to assess potential multicollinearity among candidate covariates. Because no evidence of problematic multicollinearity was identified, the selected covariates were retained in the final models. Odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for both univariable and multivariable analyses.

To further evaluate the discriminative ability of the metabolic indices for identifying MASLD, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Model performance and goodness-of-fit were additionally assessed using complementary pseudo-R² statistics, including McFadden’s R², Cox and Snell R², and Nagelkerke R². All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, given the number of MASLD cases in the study, the events-per-variable ratio exceeded 10 in the multivariable logistic regression analyses, supporting the stability and reliability of the constructed models.

Results

Demographic and anthropometric characteristics

A total of 216 patients with T2DM were included in the final analysis. Among them, 163 patients (75.5%) were classified as MASLD-positive and 53 (24.5%) as MASLD-negative based on abdominal ultrasonography. The mean age did not differ significantly between MASLD-positive and MASLD-negative groups (57.5 ± 9.7 vs. 56.7 ± 10.1 years, p = 0.62). Sex distribution was also comparable between groups (male sex: 47.9% vs. 50.9%, p = 0.73). In contrast, anthropometric measurements were significantly less favorable among patients with MASLD. Body weight was higher in the MASLD-positive group (86.61 ± 13.00 vs. 77.18 ± 10.34 kg, p < 0.001). Similarly, BMI was significantly greater in MASLD-positive patients (31.2 ± 4.7 vs. 28.1 ± 3.9 kg/m², p < 0.001). Waist circumference was also significantly higher in the MASLD-positive group (107.2 ± 14.16 vs. 102.7 ± 11.65 cm, p = 0.008), indicating a stronger association between central adiposity and MASLD. In addition, hypertension and dyslipidemia were more prevalent among MASLD-positive patients compared with MASLD-negative individuals (58.9% vs. 43.4%, p = 0.04; and 54.0% vs. 37.7%, p = 0.03, respectively). Atherogenic and steatosis-related indices

AIP values were significantly higher in MASLD-positive patients. Median AIP was 0.11 [0.02–0.21] in the MASLD-positive group compared with 0.03 [− 0.07–0.12] in the MASLD-negative group (p < 0.001). The distribution of AIP risk categories also differed significantly between groups: low-risk AIP was more common in MASLD-negative patients, whereas high-risk AIP was more frequent in MASLD-positive patients (p < 0.001).

Similarly, FLI and HSI were not significantly different between groups (p = 0.766 and p = 0.834, respectively). TyG was significantly higher in MASLD-positive patients (p < 0.001). Correlation analysis

In correlation analyses, AIP showed significant positive correlations with BMI (ρ = 0.283, p < 0.001) and HbA1c (ρ = 0.186, p = 0.006), whereas correlations with ALT, AST, FIB-4, and APRI were not significant. AIP demonstrated no meaningful correlation with FLI (r = 0.080, p = 0.291) or HSI (r = 0.120, p = 0.082) but exhibited a strong positive correlation with TyG (r = 0.845, p < 0.001). Overall, these findings indicate that AIP is more closely associated with atherogenic and insulin resistance–related metabolic burden than with fibrosis-oriented or conventional steatosis indices. Diagnostic performance analyses

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses demonstrated that AIP had a moderate ability to discriminate MASLD, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.690 (95% CI 0.62–0.76, p < 0.001). The optimal cut-off value of 0.068 was determined using the Youden index, yielding 66.9% sensitivity and 67.9% specificity. In contrast, fibrosis-oriented indices showed limited diagnostic performance; FIB-4 and APRI were not significant discriminators (AUC 0.561 and 0.589, respectively). In comparative analyses including additional metabolic indices, TyG exhibited a similar modest discriminative ability (AUC 0.681, 95% CI 0.588–0.773, p < 0.001). By comparison, FLI and HSI demonstrated poor diagnostic performance, with AUC values of 0.485 and 0.447, respectively, and were not statistically significant. The ROC curves of the evaluated metabolic indices (AIP, TyG, FLI, and HSI) for predicting MASLD are shown in Fig. 1.

Incremental value of AIP beyond triglycerides and HDL

Because AIP is derived directly from triglyceride and HDL cholesterol values, an additional analysis was performed to determine whether AIP provided information beyond its individual lipid components, as specifically requested by reviewers. Model A, including AIP alone, yielded an AUC of 0.690 (95% CI 0.604–0.776), whereas Model B, including triglycerides and HDL cholesterol separately, yielded an AUC of 0.686 (95% CI 0.600–0.772). DeLong comparison showed no significant difference between the two models (p = 0.6046). Thus, in this cohort, AIP did not demonstrate meaningful incremental discriminative value beyond its underlying lipid components. Logistic regression analyses

Univariate logistic regression analyses demonstrated that the AIP and the TyG were significantly associated with MASLD. AIP showed a significant crude association with MASLD (OR 14.66, 95% CI 4.35–54.94, p < 0.001), and TyG was also significantly associated with MASLD (OR 2.76, 95% CI 1.64–4.87, p < 0.001). In contrast, established steatosis indices, including FLI and HSI, were not significantly associated with MASLD in univariate analyses (FLI: OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.97–1.02, p = 0.781; HSI: OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.94–1.04, p = 0.674).

In multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for BMI and HbA1c, AIP remained independently associated with MASLD (OR 10.40, 95% CI 2.71–44.30, p < 0.001). Similarly, TyG maintained a significant independent association with MASLD after adjustment (OR 2.81, 95% CI 1.54–5.43, p = 0.001). By contrast, neither FLI nor HSI showed significant associations with MASLD in their respective adjusted models (FLI: OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.97–1.02, p = 0.749; HSI: OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.93–1.05, p = 0.766).

Overall, these findings indicate that among the evaluated metabolic indices, AIP and TyG demonstrated significant independent associations with MASLD, whereas FLI and HSI did not show meaningful predictive value in this cohort. The results of univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses evaluating the association between metabolic indices and MASLD are presented in Table 7. Additional logistic regression analyses including the fibrosis-based indices FIB-4 and APRI, as well as supplementary models further adjusted for waist circumference, yielded consistent findings. Neither FIB-4 nor APRI demonstrated a significant independent association with MASLD, and additional adjustment for waist circumference did not materially alter the associations of AIP, HSI, or TyG with MASLD (Supplementary Table S2). In additional sensitivity analyses further adjusted for cardiometabolic medication use, the independent associations of both AIP and TyG with MASLD remained essentially unchanged, whereas none of the medication variables showed an independent association with MASLD (Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

MASLD has emerged as the most common chronic liver disease worldwide and represents a major hepatic manifestation of systemic metabolic dysfunction. The burden of MASLD is particularly pronounced among individuals with T2DM, in whom the prevalence of hepatic steatosis frequently exceeds 60–70% depending on the studied population [18]. The coexistence of MASLD and T2DM is clinically important because it is associated with accelerated disease progression, increased risk of advanced fibrosis, and higher cardiometabolic morbidity and mortality [19]. For this reason, identifying simple and accessible metabolic markers that reflect the underlying metabolic disturbances associated with MASLD has become an important research priority [20].

In the present study, AIP values were significantly higher among patients with MASLD compared with those without ultrasonographic evidence of steatosis. In addition, AIP demonstrated modest discriminative ability for identifying MASLD and showed positive associations with markers of metabolic burden, particularly body mass index and glycated hemoglobin. Similarly, TyG index demonstrated a comparable level of discriminative performance, suggesting that both indices may capture overlapping aspects of metabolic dysfunction related to insulin resistance and dyslipidemia. The strong correlation observed between AIP and TyG further supports the possibility that these indices reflect related metabolic pathways. In contrast, fibrosis-oriented indices such as FIB-4 and APRI showed limited discriminatory performance in this cohort. These findings suggest that AIP may primarily reflect metabolic disturbances associated with hepatic steatosis rather than the severity of hepatic fibrosis. The relatively large odds ratio observed for AIP may partly reflect the logarithmic nature of this index.

The biological plausibility of these findings is supported by the central role of insulin resistance in the pathogenesis of MASLD. Insulin resistance promotes increased lipolysis in adipose tissue, resulting in enhanced free fatty acid flux to the liver. This increased substrate availability, together with enhanced hepatic de novo lipogenesis, leads to triglyceride accumulation within hepatocytes and the development of hepatic steatosis [8]. In parallel, dyslipidemia characterized by elevated triglyceride levels and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol is a hallmark metabolic abnormality in both MASLD and T2DM [18]. Because AIP is calculated from the logarithmic ratio of triglycerides to HDL cholesterol, it has been proposed as a surrogate marker of atherogenic dyslipidemia and systemic metabolic imbalance [21]. Therefore, elevated AIP levels may reflect the metabolic environment that predisposes individuals with diabetes to hepatic fat accumulation.

Several non-invasive indices have been developed to estimate hepatic steatosis or metabolic risk in clinical practice. FLI and the HSI are widely used tools that combine anthropometric and biochemical parameters to estimate the probability of fatty liver [11, 12]. Although these indices have shown acceptable performance in general populations, their diagnostic accuracy may vary in specific metabolic contexts. In the present study, neither FLI nor HSI demonstrated meaningful discriminative ability for identifying MASLD among patients with T2DM. By contrast, both AIP and TyG index showed measurable diagnostic performance. This finding may be related to the characteristic metabolic profile of patients with T2DM, in whom insulin resistance and atherogenic dyslipidemia are prominent features that may be better captured by triglyceride-based indices.

The relatively poor performance of FLI and HSI in our cohort may be explained by the high prevalence of MASLD and the metabolically homogeneous nature of the T2DM population, which may reduce the discriminatory capacity of indices derived from metabolic parameters. In addition, because diabetes is included as a component in the HSI formula, the presence of T2DM in all participants may have reduced the variability of this index and further limited its discriminatory capacity.

The TyG index is widely recognized as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance and has been associated with a variety of cardiometabolic conditions, including hepatic steatosis [22]. In the present study, a strong correlation between AIP and TyG was observed, suggesting that these indices capture overlapping aspects of metabolic dysfunction related to insulin resistance and dyslipidemia [23]. However, despite their biological relevance, both indices demonstrated only moderate diagnostic performance. This likely reflects the multifactorial nature of MASLD, in which hepatic steatosis arises from complex interactions between insulin resistance, adiposity, lipid metabolism, and genetic susceptibility rather than from a single metabolic pathway. This overlap may partly explain the comparable diagnostic performance observed for AIP and TyG in the present study.

An additional noteworthy finding of the present study was that serum ALT and AST levels remained within the normal range in both MASLD-positive and MASLD-negative patients despite the high prevalence of MASLD. This observation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that normal transaminase levels do not exclude hepatic steatosis, particularly in individuals with T2DM. Therefore, reliance on liver enzyme levels alone may lead to underrecognition of MASLD, highlighting the importance of integrating metabolic risk assessment with imaging-based evaluation.

Recent clinical guidance has emphasized structured screening strategies for MASLD in individuals with metabolic risk factors, particularly patients with T2DM who are at increased risk for progressive liver disease. Current recommendations support a two-step algorithm in which FIB-4 is used as an initial screening tool to identify individuals at risk for advanced fibrosis who require further evaluation. In a large recent analysis, a FIB-4 threshold of ≥ 1.3 identified a substantial proportion of patients with previously unrecognized advanced MASLD [24, 25]. While fibrosis-based indices remain central to current clinical algorithms, metabolic markers such as AIP may provide complementary information reflecting lipid-driven metabolic dysfunction. In this context, AIP may help characterize metabolic risk profiles associated with hepatic steatosis and could potentially complement fibrosis-focused screening strategies. However, AIP should not be considered a substitute for imaging modalities or established fibrosis-based risk assessment algorithms in the clinical evaluation of MASLD.

From a clinical perspective, the findings of the present study suggest that AIP may represent a simple and accessible metabolic marker reflecting dyslipidemia-related metabolic dysregulation associated with MASLD in patients with T2DM. Because AIP can be calculated from routinely available lipid parameters, it provides a practical summary measure of lipid-related metabolic imbalance, although its diagnostic contribution beyond conventional lipid parameters appears limited. Nevertheless, the moderate diagnostic performance observed in this study indicates that AIP should not be interpreted as a standalone diagnostic marker for MASLD. Instead, it may serve as a complementary metabolic indicator that reflects the broader cardiometabolic environment associated with hepatic steatosis. Importantly, the observed association between AIP and MASLD remained robust after additional adjustment for cardiometabolic medication use, suggesting that this relationship was not solely explained by differences in pharmacological treatment.

An important finding of this study is that AIP did not demonstrate incremental discriminative value beyond its individual lipid components. This suggests that AIP largely represents a reformulation of triglyceride and HDL cholesterol rather than a novel independent biomarker. This finding deserves particular attention when interpreting the clinical significance of AIP. Because AIP is mathematically derived from triglyceride and HDL cholesterol levels, its association with MASLD is expected to partially reflect the metabolic information contained in these conventional lipid parameters. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that AIP represents a superior or novel diagnostic biomarker. Instead, AIP should be regarded as a practical composite index that conveniently summarizes atherogenic dyslipidemia using routinely available lipid measurements. Furthermore, the association between AIP and MASLD remained essentially unchanged after additional adjustment for waist circumference, suggesting that the observed relationship was not solely explained by central adiposity.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding causal relationships between AIP and MASLD. Consequently, the temporal relationship between AIP elevation and the development of hepatic steatosis cannot be determined. Second, hepatic steatosis was assessed using ultrasonography, which has limited sensitivity for detecting mild steatosis compared with advanced imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging–based techniques. In addition, ultrasonography is an operator-dependent imaging modality, and all examinations were performed by a single experienced radiologist who was blinded to the clinical and laboratory data. Although this approach ensured methodological consistency, interobserver variability could not be assessed, which should be considered when interpreting our findings. Third, the study was conducted at a single center and included a specific population of patients with T2DM, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Furthermore, because the study was conducted in a tertiary-care outpatient clinic, the relatively high prevalence of MASLD in our cohort may partly reflect referral and selection bias. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to broader populations should be interpreted with caution. In addition, lifestyle-related factors such as diet, alcohol intake, and physical activity were not available in this retrospective dataset and therefore could not be included in the analysis. Finally, although several metabolic indices were evaluated, additional biomarkers and imaging-based parameters could further improve risk stratification for MASLD. Moreover, the high prevalence of MASLD in the study population may have influenced the diagnostic performance of certain indices.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides novel insights into the relationship between AIP and MASLD in patients with T2DM. By integrating multiple metabolic indices and evaluating their relative performance, this study contributes to the growing body of literature exploring simple metabolic markers associated with hepatic steatosis in diabetes. Future prospective studies involving larger and more diverse populations are needed to clarify the clinical utility of AIP and to determine whether combining metabolic markers with established fibrosis-based algorithms may improve MASLD risk stratification in patients with T2DM.

Conclusion

In patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, AIP was independently associated with MASLD. However, its discriminative ability was modest and comparable to that of its individual lipid components. These findings suggest that AIP primarily reflects underlying atherogenic dyslipidemia rather than providing additional diagnostic value beyond conventional lipid parameters. Therefore, AIP should be interpreted as a composite metabolic indicator rather than a standalone diagnostic tool for MASLD. Further prospective studies are needed to determine whether AIP may provide additional value when combined with established fibrosis-based and metabolic risk assessment algorithms.
