From interface to outcome: a 4I framework for AI-linked functionality in electronic health records
Authors: Ting Zhang, Nina Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElectronic health records (EHRs) are central to clinical documentation and care coordination, yet long-standing studies report persistent socio-technical problems such as poor usability, slow response, alert burden, interoperability gaps, and clinician cognitive burden. As EHRs increasingly incorporate decision support and other AI-related functionalities, the evidence base now spans both traditional EHR design/use and EHR-linked advanced functions. However, findings are often reported in ways that make it difficult to trace how interface and information conditions relate to clinicians’ interaction experiences and, where reported, downstream outcomes.MethodsWe conducted an integrative literature review of medical informatics research published between 2005 and 2025 on EHR design and use, including EHR-linked decision support and AI-related functionalities when explicitly described. Seventy eligible studies were synthesized using a four-layer socio-technical architecture, Interface, Information, Interaction, and Outcome (4I). For readability, “4I” can be read equivalently as “3I + O” (3I plus an explicit Outcome layer). We developed study-level labels and cross-layer evidence links to derive an evidence-traceable set of 29 variables and to summarize how studies characterized each variable as facilitating, constraining, or mixed for adoption and ongoing use.ResultsThe 29 variables clustered across the 4I layers, with 5 primarily interface-related variables, 5 information-related variables, 10 interaction-related variables, and 9 outcome-related variables. Interface and Information conditions (e.g., access/usability, interoperability, trust, governance, and task–function fit) were most often connected to Interaction experiences, including workflow fit, training and support, trust calibration, and cognitive workload. Across the included studies, adoption was described as more favorable when reliable system performance, interpretable outputs, workflow-aligned training, and organizational support co-occurred. Where studies reported downstream effects, these cross-layer patterns were linked to documentation burden and burnout, patient-safety risks, workflow standardization, and perceived augmentation of clinical decision-making.ConclusionsThe 4I framework provides a structured synthesis that connects established EHR evidence to EHR-linked decision support and AI-related functionalities where documented, while keeping outcome implications explicit. The resulting 29-variable dictionary supports clearer reporting and cross-layer interpretation, and it provides a practical basis for subsequent expert elicitation and empirical assessment as evidence on AI-linked EHR functions continues to develop.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03562-6.

Background

Traditional EHRs have long been criticized for their cumbersome operation, disruption of workflows, and imposition of cognitive burden on clinicians, which in turn undermines their ability to provide effective decision support [1, 2]. Recent advances in AI hold promise for mitigating these limitations by incorporating predictive analytics, real-time recommendations, and adaptive interfaces to better support clinical decision-making [3, 4]. Integrating AI into EHRs introduces additional socio-technical challenges, such as model governance, explanation, accountability, and safe integration into clinical workflows [5–7]. These factors influence how clinicians interpret and use the outputs. In this review, readers will learn how prior studies describe EHR-linked decision support (including explicitly AI-linked modules when reported) as a socio-technical phenomenon, and how this evidence can be organized into a traceable 29-variable dictionary and a 4I structure to support evaluation and governance discussions.

This challenge is not confined to a single national setting. Across the international literature, EHR-linked AI is increasingly discussed alongside broader developments in data sharing, preventive-risk modelling, and digital-twin initiatives, including cross-sector data spaces linking environmental exposures with human health, AI-supported long-term risk evaluation for children and youth, and digital-twin approaches that aim to integrate multimodal patient representations [8, 9, 10]. These examples show that the worldwide case is no longer only about digitizing records; it is about making heterogeneous data, models, and workflows usable and governable across settings.

We focused on the healthcare sector because it is safety-critical, documentation-intensive, and organizationally interdependent. In EHR-mediated care, problems in interface design, information quality, model fit, or workflow integration can affect not only efficiency but also clinical judgment, accountability, and patient safety [1, 2, 4, 11]. This makes healthcare an especially demanding and relevant sector in which to study AI-linked functionality.

Recent adjacent studies show that AI-enabled digital health has developed through several technical and application-oriented routes. These include cross-sector data-space architecture for linking environmental and health data [8], AI-based risk-evaluation frameworks for prevention-oriented behavior change [9], digital-twin architecture for multimodal patient representation [10], multi-layer data-cleaning frameworks for healthcare data quality control [12], and graph data-processing frameworks for modelling large-scale relational data [13]. Together, these studies highlight important functional directions, including interoperability, risk prediction, multimodal representation, data cleaning, verification, and relationship modelling. However, their primary focus is technical architecture or application development rather than a construct-level synthesis of how EHR-linked functionality is experienced across interface, information, interaction, and outcome in clinical work. The present review uses these studies as adjacent reference points while addressing a different aim: to organize EHR-linked socio-technical evidence into a traceable 4I framework.

To support end-to-end traceability, we extend the established 3I socio-technical framework (interface–information–interaction) by adding an explicit Outcome layer (3I+Outcome, or 3I + O), referred to here as 4I [14]. Existing evidence in this review predominantly addresses traditional EHR systems and their well-documented limitations in interface design and information management (e.g., usability, interoperability, and data/knowledge processing), as well as the burdens these constraints impose on clinician-system interactions [1, 15, 16]. We therefore employ the Outcome layer not as a benchmark, but as an organizational tool to track downstream effects when studies report them, particularly operational and governance implications such as support for safety culture and workflow standardization.

We treat AI-linked EHR functionality as an evaluation lens for model-supported functions embedded in routine workflows. We synthesize whether such embedding is reported to support clinical decision-making or to add workload and friction, and we organize these patterns using the 4I structure for traceability. In this review, Interface refers to system access and user-facing design conditions that shape clinical use (e.g., interoperability/access, speed, mobile support, customizability, and interface friendliness). Information is not used as a synonym for raw data; it refers to how system-provided content/functions are presented for clinical use, including operational complexity/ease of use, trust in outputs, information-quality satisfaction, and task–function fit. Interaction captures clinicians’ work practices and experience when using the system, including alerts (effectiveness/fatigue), data-entry efficiency, cognitive load, training, self-efficacy, satisfaction, and collaboration support. Outcome summarizes downstream operational/organizational consequences reported in the literature (e.g., safety culture, workflow standardization, support conditions, error frequency, burnout, and decision-making augmentation).

Simultaneously, we observe that empirical evidence regarding downstream outcome effects directly attributable to AI-integrated EHR functions remains scarce and inconsistent within the existing literature [4, 7, 17]. Furthermore, much of the outcome assessment continues to depend on broader EHR and health-IT studies as proxies, rather than on comprehensive evaluations of mature EHR–AI systems [4, 7, 17]. Accordingly, the main contribution of this study is not a claim about pooled causal effects, but a traceable construct-level map linking upstream design and governance conditions to reported downstream outcomes. A corresponding limitation, discussed further below, is that AI-specific outcome evidence remains uneven across time, applications, and regions.

Across the included literature, the most recurrent findings concern usability, trust, workflow fit, and governance, whereas mature AI-specific downstream outcome measurement remains more limited. Taken together, these patterns indicate that the review is strongest in synthesizing recurrent socio-technical constructs, while AI-specific downstream outcome evidence remains uneven (Table 1).

Based on this rationale, we present three research questions to investigate:

RQ1

How do the included studies describe EHR-related constructs across Interface, Information, and Interaction, and which downstream Outcome constructs are reported using Pre-EHR–AI vs. Post-EHR–AI as descriptive context tags where explicitly stated?

RQ2

Which conditions are most frequently portrayed as facilitating, hindering, or mixed/contingent for adoption and sustained use of EHR-linked decision-support functionality (including explicitly AI-linked modules when reported), as described in the included studies?

RQ3

When studies explicitly link interface/information conditions to interaction experiences and downstream outcomes, what recurring cross-layer linkages are described in the evidence base?

Finally, this study is organized as follows. Section 1 outlines limitations of traditional EHRs and summarizes how emerging AI-enabled functions are being incorporated into EHR workflows, motivating the need for end-to-end traceability. Section 2 describes the integrative review design, PRISMA-guided identification/screening, evidence extraction, and the variable-labelling procedures used to consolidate study-level constructs into the 29-variable dictionary. The results in Sect. 3 report findings for RQ1–RQ3 using the 4I mapping and summarize cross-layer evidence patterns. Section 4 discusses the cross-layer “evidence bridge” and its implications for evaluation, governance, and staged adoption, followed by Conclusions and Limitations.

Methods

This study adopts a qualitative, integrative literature review as its primary research design. The integrative review methodology enables a comprehensive understanding of diverse literature sources (i.e., including empirical findings, conceptual models, and theoretical debates) to develop a novel, multi-layered framework (i.e., how EHR-linked decision-support and AI-related functionality is embedded in EHR workflows; and how studies describe its implications across interface, information, interaction, and outcomes.)

This approach is especially suitable for capturing the complexity of socio-technical systems like EHRs, where structural components (e.g., interface design), cognitive processes (e.g., user trust), and organizational outcomes (e.g., safety and efficiency) interact in non-linear and layered ways [5, 14]. Following final inclusion, all eligible studies were systematically extracted into an evidence extraction matrix (Sect. Data Extraction and Labelling Procedures). Recurring cross-layer relationships were iteratively synthesized and consolidated into a definitive set of 29 variables (Sect. Derivation of the 29 variables).

Research approach and logic

We followed a PRISMA-guided integrative gathering approach with sequential steps for scoping, retrieval, screening, extraction, appraisal, and synthesis. These steps were selected because the target literature spans empirical evaluations, implementation reports, conceptual discussions, and early AI-linked applications, making a strict effect-size aggregation unsuitable. The chosen sequence preserves transparency at the record level while allowing staged handling of heterogeneous evidence: records were first standardized through the extraction legend and study-context tags and were then consolidated through cross-study synthesis into the 4I framework [18–22]. Figure 1 presents this workflow in UML-based form to improve transparency and replicability.

In other words, we followed a PRISMA-guided integrative gathering approach rather than a meta-analytic aggregation strategy, because the aim was to retain heterogeneous study-level detail while still enabling structured cross-study synthesis.

Literature identification and selection

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, and Google Scholar to identify studies on EHR systems, EHR-linked decision support, and AI-related functionalities when explicitly described. The primary search covered 2005–2025. To improve conceptual anchoring, we conducted backward and forward citation tracking and retained a small number of seminal pre-2005 sources addressing foundational constructs and measurement anchors (e.g., trust, communication, and socio-technical governance [23, 24]).

The search terms were combined using Boolean logic and included the following variants: EHRs, CDSSs, artificial intelligence, explainability, human–computer interaction/usability, workflow efficiency, burnout, and digital transformation in healthcare (e.g. [4, 6, 11]). Search strings were adapted for each database. All records were exported to a reference manager and de-duplicated.

Studies qualified if they satisfied three criteria. Initially, they presented peer-reviewed empirical studies or meticulously documented conference proceedings; a limited selection of extensively cited conceptual sources and transparent preprints were preserved solely for construct definition and framework operationalization. Secondly, they analyzed the design, utilization, implementation, or effects of EHRs, specifically including AI-enabled or algorithm-associated functionalities. Third, they provided evidence or frameworks pertinent to usability, clinical decision-making, cognitive workload, or trust and ethics in health information technology.

Screening proceeded in three stages (title/abstract screening, full-text eligibility assessment, and 4I framework thematic mapping). We identified 400 records and 13 duplicates were removed (n = 387 screened). We sought 120 reports for retrieval. After removing 3 duplicate reports at the full-text stage, 117 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility, 47 were excluded, and 70 studies were included. The record-level audit trail (data source, publication type, inclusion/exclusion decisions, and reasons) is documented and in the screening log.

To enhance reporting transparency, the search and study selection procedures were documented with reference to the PRISMA 2020 statement for systematic reviews [18]. The PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 2) summarizes the number of records identified across data sources and the counts of studies screened, assessed for eligibility, and included for the period 2005–2025, together with reasons for exclusion at each stage.

The final set includes foundational studies (e.g. [14, 23, 24]), and recent evidence on EHR-linked decision support and explicitly reported AI-linked implementations (e.g. [5, 17, 25]).

Data extraction and labelling procedures

After evaluating studies against the eligibility criteria, we extracted data using a standardized matrix (Table 2). For each article, the matrix recorded a short APA-style reference, the clinical context or setting, the study methodology (e.g. qualitative, survey, experimental, mixed-methods), and the key variables or focal issues. Because studies use different measures and reporting formats, we do not compare numeric values directly across papers; instead, we synthesize evidence at the construct level. To support consistent synthesis, we also tagged each record using our five-part labelling legend: (L1) medical IT function; (L2) user experience and behavior; (L3) organizational support and governance; (L4-PRE) healthcare operations process evidence; and (L4-POST) healthcare operations outcome evidence. In addition, we applied two study-context tags to indicate reporting context only: Pre-EHR–AI (traditional EHR context) and Post-EHR–AI (explicitly AI-linked EHR context). This staged treatment of the gathered evidence also functioned as a quality-control strategy: duplicate records were cleaned at source level, constructs were normalized at study level, and cross-layer relations were represented in traceability tables. This logic draws on multi-layer data-cleaning and graph-based processing work [12, 13] and is also consistent with implementation/normalization perspectives that distinguish technical procedures from routine embedding in practice [26].

Where reported, we noted the technology timeframe for each study (e.g., deployment period, implementation phase, or platform generation). Because many papers did not provide version-level details, we used publication year and setting only as contextual markers and avoided interpretations that would require unreported technical information. The purpose of this review was not to build a formal timeline of technology generations, but to synthesize how the literature describes EHR-linked functionality across the 4I layers.

We then used the adopted 3I socio-technical view (interface-information-interaction) to organize and compare constructs across studies; during this synthesis, downstream operation and governance consequences repeatedly appeared beyond interaction, motivating an explicit Outcome layer and the resulting 4I structure. Layer assignment reflects the primary locus of discussion and operationalization across the included studies (i.e., where a construct is most consistently defined and evaluated), rather than the only layer in which it may exert influence. We prioritized evidence that explicitly described EHR-linked decision support and, where available, AI-related functions integrated into, or closely associated with, EHR workflows [4, 5, 7, 17].

We then applied a hybrid thematic labelling approach combining deductive and inductive elements. Extracted concepts were first grouped into pragmatic dimensions reflecting how EHR systems (and explicitly AI-linked EHR functions, when reported) are described in practice: (1) system/interface features, (2) content and AI-related information, (3) human-system interaction, and (4) clinical, user, or organizational outcomes. Each candidate variable was assigned a stable ID (S=Interface, C=Information, H=Interaction, O=Outcome) and operationally defined through iterative evidence checking. The IDs provide a simple cross-reference to Table 3 and Appendix without adding narrative length.

Quality assessment

We used the 2018 edition of the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) following the methodological quality of included studies [19]. For each study, we commenced each inquiry by responding to the two MMAT screening queries. Subsequently, we employed the appropriate criteria for the design, which may be qualitative, randomized controlled, non-randomized, quantitative descriptive, or a combination of these. We documented item-level assessments (e.g., Yes/No/Can’t tell) along with succinct reasoning in a structured spreadsheet and did not calculate an overall numeric score [19]. Quality appraisal was conducted by one reviewer; the supervising professor independently checked a subset of studies and reviewed borderline decisions, after which the corresponding notes were refined where needed. This approach aligns with recommendations for single-reviewer screening and targeted verification to reduce errors and improve clarity [20, 21]. We employed the MMAT assessments to contextualize the narrative synthesis and to evaluate the confidence in its findings. This corresponds with mixed evidence synthesis methods that incorporate both relevance and rigor [29, 30].

Derivation of the 29 variables

To aid in the transition from 70 diverse studies to a streamlined set of variables with clarity, we considered the “Key variables/focus” column in the extraction matrix (Table 2) as a compilation of first-order labels. For each study, we identified concise phrases delineating the design features of EHR (and, when available, EHR-linked AI), clinician experiences (e.g., workload, alert fatigue, trust), and reported outcomes (e.g., safety issues, exhaustion, adoption). These initial labels were organized into provisional label groups through iterative comparison. For instance, expressions such as “page-load latency (slow loading/saving),” “system lag,” and “screen freezes” (i.e., performance instability shaped by infrastructure, configuration, and clinical workload) were grouped into a single “system response speed” label category.

Subsequently, each label group was unified into a single multi-study variable and allocated a consistent identifier (S1–S6, H1–H6, C1–C12, O1–O5). For each variable, we documented at least one representative study along with a succinct operational description. The resulting 29-variable dictionary is summarized in Table 3. Supplementary operational definitions and traceability materials are provided in Appendix A (worked examples, extraction crosswalk, layer mapping, adoption roles, and study-level traceability).

These outputs provide a structured view of recurring facilitators and constraints as described in the included studies, ranging from technical configuration and interoperability to information/model governance, clinician workload and trust, and downstream operational consequences. Numerically, the final framework contains 5 interface-related variables, 5 information-related variables, 10 interaction-related variables, and 9 outcome-related variables. This distribution provides a descriptive summary of how the 29 variables were formed through the five-part extraction legend and study-context tagging process, then consolidated and mapped onto the 4I framework. Because the review synthesized evidence at the construct level rather than at the effect-size level, these figures should be interpreted as descriptive counts and layer distributions of the synthesized variables, not as pooled quantitative effects.

The evidence base is not the same for all layers, which is important. Specifically, outcome-oriented evidence in the included studies predominantly derives from extensive EHR and clinical decision-support research rather than from established AI-integrated EHR systems. In this review, we regard outcome variables as reported downstream consequences most linked to EHR change (e.g., safety culture, exhaustion, and workflow standardization), while acknowledging that AI-specific outcome evidence is still scarce [4, 7, 17]. The Discussion section considers the implications of this study for evaluating EHR-AI deployments.

Variable-to-layer assignment followed a primary-layer rule. The ID prefixes (S/H/C/O) reflect consolidation families used during synthesis and traceability and do not determine 4I layer placement. Each variable was assigned to the 4I layer in which it was most consistently defined and discussed across the included studies. For constructs that may span layers (e.g., trust, automation), we designated a primary “home” layer as the main reporting location for consistency, while describing cross-layer roles in the narrative synthesis. Multi-layered variable structure across the 4I layers

RQ1 examines how the included studies describe EHR-related constructs across the four layers (i.e., Interface, Information, Interaction, and Outcome) and how these constructs are reported in traditional vs. explicitly AI-linked context tags (Pre-EHR–AI vs. Post-EHR–AI). Across the 70 included studies, we identified 29 variables that consistently appeared in how EHR systems (and explicitly AI-linked functions, when reported) were described across Interface, Information, Interaction, and Outcome. These include factors such as the effectiveness of the interface and its compatibility with other systems [1, 16, 31]. Information-related issues are frequently the focus of scholarly investigation. Such issues include, but are not limited to, data quality, documentation accuracy, and governance constraints [27, 42].

At the interaction level, the data often reflects the typical clinical experience, including workload, fatigue from vigilance, and usability issues [2, 16, 25]. When AI-related capabilities are clearly defined, research primarily concentrates on AI-assisted decision support and associated implementation factors, with scant and inconsistent documentation of downstream consequences [4, 7]. Compared with traditional EHR studies, explicitly AI-linked papers more often emphasize operational complexity, model fit, explainability, and augmentation of clinical decision-making, whereas earlier EHR studies more often emphasize usability, workflow disruption, and alert burden [4, 7, 14, 15, 16, 17].

Specifically, based on Table 3, we compared descriptions of conventional EHR functionalities with AI-integrated EHR capabilities solely when these were explicitly documented in the included studies. This evidence-based perspective was employed during the transition from the cross-study synthesis phase to the final 29-variable framework, ensuring that AI-associated elements were labelled and preserved solely when explicitly documented (e.g. [4]).

At the interface level, variables such as system interoperability (S1), system response speed (S4), mobile friendliness (S5), customizability (S6), and system interface friendliness (C1) capture foundational access and usability conditions [1, 16, 31, 36, 37, 50]. For example, interoperability and response speed are often described as prerequisites for pulling external labs/imaging and rendering patient summaries quickly during busy clinical sessions, while mobile access supports point-of-care use during rounds. When AI-linked modules are introduced, studies commonly describe additional interface elements (e.g., dashboards, alert panels, or automation prompts) that may improve visibility and access to relevant information in some implementations but may also introduce navigation complexity and additional interaction steps in others. Accordingly, the interface layer is treated here as a set of enabling conditions rather than a guarantee of simplification. This paragraph emphasizes user-experienced performance conditions (e.g., latency/lag) shaped by infrastructure and workload, rather than an AI-specific compute explanation.

Regarding response speed, the literature emphasizes perceived performance in context. Users generally tolerate longer delays for screen transitions or complex queries than for immediate keystroke-level feedback, and interface cues (e.g., progress indicators or asynchronous loading) can reduce impatience and cognitive disruption, especially when tasks involve retrieving or processing larger volumes of information [1, 16]. More broadly, recent work on benchmarking indicators in hospital information systems supports treating system performance as an explicit evaluation dimension rather than as a narrow technical proxy [51].

At the information level, variables capture considerations relating to content quality and governance. These include perceived ease of use (C4) and trust in system outputs (C6), as well as satisfaction with information quality (C7). In studies that explicitly report on AI-linked functions, concerns at the information layer also include how complex AI is to operate (C2) and the type and fit of the model (C12) [40, 41, 49, 52, 53]. For instance, when predictive outputs are presented (e.g., risk flags or ranked recommendations), studies emphasize the importance of knowing whether inputs are current and reliable (data provenance and update timing) and whether the model’s target matches what clinicians need to decide at the bedside (model–task fit). Across the included studies, trust tends to be higher when outputs are described as interpretable/transparent and perceived as suitable for the clinical problem, whereas trust erodes when workflows are opaque or outputs are perceived as misaligned with clinical reasoning or routine workflow.

At the interaction layer, the focus is on the practical application of the EHR–AI system by clinicians in their regular tasks. The variables in this category include alert effectiveness (S2) and Alert fatigue (H3) [15, 32], data entry efficiency (S3) [34], cognitive load (H1) [2, 16], training adequacy (H2) and AI training coverage (C3) [39, 46, 47], user satisfaction (H5) [25], collaboration and communication support (H6) [23], and AI automation (C11) [4, 11, 53]. Across studies, interaction quality was described as more favorable when warnings were precisely targeted, training corresponded with actual workflows, and automated actions aligned with clinical judgment; for example, narrowly targeted alerts tied to actionable thresholds were viewed more positively than high-volume, low specificity alerts that interrupt order entry or documentation. When AI generates poorly targeted alarms, unnecessary screens, or ambiguous automation, clinicians face increased interruptions, heightened cognitive load, and a greater reliance on workarounds. This suggests that improvements at the interaction level are heavily contingent upon the implementation process.

At the outcome layer, the focus moves to the downstream impacts of these structural and behavioral alterations. Variables such as safety culture support (O1) [1, 42], cross-departmental collaboration mechanisms (O2) [43, 44], management support level (O3), technical support quality (O4) [44], and workflow standardization (O5) [34, 45] define organizational conditions that might magnify or reduce the impact of EHR-AI innovations. In several of the reviewed studies, settings reporting visible leadership backing, responsive IT support, and clearly defined processes also described steadier use of EHR functions and, where advanced tools were available, fewer workflow interruptions around decision-support features, for example, timely troubleshooting of alert configuration, clear escalation pathways for system issues, and standardized documentation routines were described as reducing persistent friction around decision-support use [1, 4, 44]. At the clinician and patient-facing levels, technological acceptance (C5) [41], error frequency (C8) [28, 42], occupational burnout (C9) [2, 25], and AI augmentation of decision-making (C10) is reported as perceived efficiency support in decision-support application [4]. In Fig. 3, these outcome variables are positioned at the far end of the 4I structure to emphasize that they are the result of a combination of interface, information, and interaction, rather than just AI modules [1, 4, 44].

Drivers and barriers of EHR–AI adoption across the 4I layers

For RQ2, “adoption” refers to the adoption and sustained use of EHR-linked decision-support functionality, and AI-related modules were considered only when the included studies explicitly described AI-enabled or AI-interfacing features. Evidence from traditional (non-AI) EHR contexts was retained as transferable enabling conditions for decision-support adoption, without implying an AI-specific effect. Within each study, we categorized variables as drivers, barriers, or mixed or contingent based on explicit statements in the text [22]. We then consolidated these study-level labels into a variable-level adoption role categorization for each of the 29 variables [41, 54]. Brief examples are provided to clarify how the included studies described “driver” and “barrier” conditions in practice. Based on the resultant adoption roles (driver/barrier/mixed) (Table A4), the conventional sorts of empirical evidence substantiating each classification are summarized [46, 47].

At the interface level, higher interoperability and faster response times (S1, S4), mobile device access (S5), and some flexibility for local customization (S6, C1) are frequently mentioned in case reports and usability studies as conditions under which AI-related functions are easier to incorporate into daily work [1, 16, 31, 50]. Lower perceived setup complexity, increased ease of use, and a strong fit between the AI model and the clinical problem (C2, C4, C12) are all rated as beneficial when clinicians understand what the AI is doing and how to interpret its outputs [40, 41, 52, 53, 55]. Scenario-based training (H2, C3), targeted and therapeutically significant alerts (S2, H3), low/managed cognitive demands (H1), high user satisfaction (H5), and automation perceived to reduce routine manual work (C11) are examples of interaction facilitators [2, 4, 15, 16, 25, 32, 39, 46, 47, 53]. Similarly, studies distinguish between targeted, clinically meaningful alerts that support timely action versus high-volume, low specificity alerts that interrupt workflow and increase overrides or desensitization (S2 vs. H3) [15, 32]. Organizational elements such as a supportive safety culture, cross-departmental collaboration, visible management support, and responsive IT services (O1-O4) are regarded as reinforcing these circumstances by indicating that utilizing AI technologies is acceptable and that problems will be resolved [1, 42, 43, 44].

Overall, the 70 studies rarely link EHR-AI adoption to a single technical feature. Instead, adoption is typically reported when certain conditions are met, including reliable interfaces, information outputs that clinicians can interpret and trust, interaction demands that align with existing workflows, and organizational support for routine use [31, 39, 44, 52]. The findings in Table 3 and Table A4 bring these conditions together across the 4I layers, providing a structured response to RQ2 by showing which criteria are most often described as promoting or impeding the use of EHR-AI functionalities.

Cross-layer influences on outcomes

RQ3 examines how interface, information, and interaction conditions are described in relation to downstream outcomes in the included studies. These patterns link interface and information conditions with clinicians’ interaction burden and interpretation of outputs, and with downstream outcomes such as satisfaction, safety concerns, and burnout.

At the interface level, variables such as interoperability, response speed, mobile access, customization, and screen clarity (S1, S4-S6, C1 in Table 3) are commonly described as conditions that influence how easily clinicians can obtain, navigate, and act on relevant information during clinical decision-making, and poor usability is repeatedly linked to workarounds and safety concerns [1, 16, 31]. Similarly, at the information level, perceived operational complexity and ease of use of AI (C2, C4), as well as confidence in the credibility and suitability of AI outputs (C6, C12), affect whether clinicians view recommendations as interpretable and reliable enough for action. For example, studies describing slow or unstable systems and confusing layouts often report that clinicians compensate through multitasking, repeated navigation, or workarounds, patterns discussed alongside higher cognitive load and frustration (S4/C1 → H1) [1, 16]. Likewise, when outputs are described as opaque or poorly communicated, clinicians are more likely to question reliability and reduce reliance on decision support (C6/C12), whereas clearer presentation and better fit to clinical needs are described as supporting trust in and use of recommendations [17, 24, 30, 40, 41, 48, 49, 53]. At the interaction level, alert burden and cognitive load (H1, H3) and the adequacy and coverage of training (H2, C3) are described as key mechanisms through which upstream design choices translate into downstream user experience either enabling sustained, confident use or driving interruptions, overrides, and disengagement [15, 32, 39, 43, 56].

Across the included studies, upstream conditions were repeatedly discussed in relation to downstream outcomes documented in the EHR literature, including satisfaction and burnout (H5, C9), perceived error frequency and safety implications (C8), and when explicitly reported, perceived AI augmentation of decision-making (C10) [2, 4, 25, 42].

In the traceability tables (Table A5) and the narrative synthesis, reports of faster response speed (S4), more targeted and clinically meaningful alerts (S2), and training that covers common scenarios (C3) often appear alongside lower cognitive load (H1), reduced alert fatigue (H3), and higher user satisfaction (H5) [1, 2, 15, 16, 25, 32, 46, 47]. In these cases, AI tools are described as auxiliary means integrated into the workflow. Conversely, the evidence related to variables such as high cognitive load (H1), lower satisfaction with information quality (C7) and alert fatigue (H3) typically comes from studies that describe systems as being slow, disorganized, or poorly explained. This pattern is consistent with study narratives in which interaction-level burden is discussed alongside upstream interface and information constraints [1, 15, 16, 33].

Across the reviewed studies, these interaction patterns are associated with downstream outcome indicators in the 4I framework (Table 3). Studies that report higher system interoperability (S1) and higher perceived ease of use (C4) more frequently document positive outcomes such as technology acceptance (C5) [31, 40, 41]. Conversely, persistent issues related to high cognitive load (H1) and low trust in system outputs (C6) are associated with negative results such as occupational burnout (C9) and unwillingness to rely on decision support (C5) [2, 17, 24, 25, 41, 49]. Additionally, as indicated in the comprehensive analysis, organizational variables such as safety culture support (O1) and cross-departmental collaboration mechanisms (O2) are described as background factors that can amplify or mitigate these outcome patterns by legitimizing use and enabling iterative workflow optimization [1, 42, 43, 44].

In short, the 70 included studies do not support the idea that adding AI features to EHRs automatically improves outcomes. What matters is how those functions are built into day-to-day use at the interface (S1, S4, C1), whether the resulting outputs are timely and interpretable for clinicians (C2, C6, C12), and how well these elements fit established workflows and interaction routines (H1, H3, C3). For example, the findings explain RQ3 in a descriptive sense by drawing attention to recurring linkages (i.e., clearer interface presentation (C1) is repeatedly discussed alongside lower cognitive load (H1) and, downstream, less occupational burnout (C9)) and it also points to relationships that are suitable for later causal modelling.

Discussion

Once AI is incorporated into EHRs, the traditional “3I” challenges extend beyond usability and information quality. They also involve governance and downstream outcomes, making it necessary to trace how interface and information conditions translate into interaction burdens and organizational effects.

The 3I perspective articulated by Miller et al. [14] provides a useful starting point for interpreting the literature across the 2005–2025 review period. Earlier studies in this window were largely concerned with interface usability, information presentation, workflow fit, and alert burden in established EHR/CDS settings [1, 14, 15, 16]. In this earlier phase, the main evaluative emphasis remained on how clinicians accessed, interpreted, and interacted with digital decision-support functions in routine care.

A broader framing became more visible in the later part of the review period, particularly from the late 2010s onward. Rather than focusing only on interface and information design, studies increasingly discussed how AI- or machine learning-enabled tools were implemented in care delivery, how they should be governed, and what downstream effects followed from their use. For example, Yu et al. [11] described AI in healthcare not only as a technical development but also as a source of economic, legal, and social implications. Sendak et al. [4] brought this discussion closer to practice by examining how machine-learning products derived from EHR data are translated into clinical care. Related work on the ethical, legal, and social implications of AI-based medical decision-support tools [6] further shows that evaluation increasingly extends beyond system design to questions of accountability, implementation, and consequence.

In this sense, the transition from 3I to 4I in the present review is not intended to replace the earlier framework, but to make explicit a dimension that later literature discusses more directly: outcome. Making Outcome visible helps the review capture not only how EHR-linked tools are designed and used, but also how they are associated with downstream clinical, organizational, and implementation-related consequences.

This shift also helps clarify how the EHR itself is positioned in current practice. In some settings, the EHR functions mainly as a data repository that provides context or data to externally invoked services; in others, it operates as a platform with more directly integrated functionality. In practice, both arrangements are common. Some decision-support and AI-enabled functions are delivered as vendor-integrated modules, whereas others are provided by external services invoked through APIs, such as CDS Hooks or HL7-based integrations, and then returned to clinicians within the EHR workflow. Recent work on integrating AI platforms into clinical IT further illustrates that contemporary deployment often depends on workflow-connected infrastructure rather than on a single self-contained EHR module [57]. For the purposes of this review, we use the term “AI-linked functionality” to cover both arrangements, because the interface, information, and interaction issues identified in the included studies arise at the point where these functions are experienced in EHR-mediated clinical work.

Recent studies on EHR-linked infrastructures further support this broader view. Work on HL7 CDA-based shared EHR reuse shows how standardized shared records can support secondary uses such as trial-feasibility assessment without requiring a wholly separate transformation pipeline [58]. Related systematic reviews on common data models and data standards likewise emphasize the role of harmonized structures in making heterogeneous health data more interoperable and reusable across settings [59]. At the same time, recent work on semantic technologies highlights how data quality can be improved through better semantic alignment, metadata, and explicit representation of meaning [60], while point-of-care NLP tools illustrate how AI can be embedded within routine workflows to turn free text into structured information during clinical use rather than only after the fact [61]. Taken together, these developments support our use of “AI-linked functionality” as a workflow-level term that captures both embedded modules and externally invoked services as they are experienced through the EHR.

From interface modifications to results-driven redesign

The results of this study indicate that AI-linked EHR functionalities should not be seen solely as improvements in usability or interface design. In the reviewed literature, AI components are described as modifying how information is generated, prioritized and presented to clinicians, which in turn was often reported alongside changes in interaction patterns and, in some reports, to organizational performance. Improvements to the interface (e.g. faster response times and contextual prompts) are most effective when aligned with information standards and organizational policies regarding data quality, documentation rules and system usage. The benefits also depend on conditions at the level of interaction, including trust, workload and team coordination.

The value of a hierarchical, traceable representation

The study disaggregates AI-driven EHR transformation into Interface, Information, Interaction, and Outcome layers, facilitating the auditability and comparability of design decisions across institutions. This hierarchical structure supports clarity in variable mapping, conceptual consistency, and empirical traceability, thereby creating a solid foundation for future causal modeling and scenario analysis. For example, a note-drafting feature (interface) can be supported by a regulated LLM service (information). Prior studies link this setup to clinician workload and confidence in recommendations (Interaction) and to downstream process outcomes such as documentation time and care-team coordination (Outcome).

Implications for governance and staged adoption

Finally, building on the adoption profiles in Table A4, the RQ2 drivers and impediments, as well as the RQ3 outcome patterns, have implications for how EHR-AI systems are managed and phased in. The 4I framing raises additional governance concerns. Integrating AI modules into EHRs introduces new risks, including obscure model logic, fluctuating accountability, and limitations in interoperability. Therefore, it is essential to maintain transparency and not restrict governance-related factors solely to implementation specifics, such as explainability, oversight, and data stewardship. By positioning these variables within the Information and Outcome layers, we can facilitate incremental adoption: healthcare organizations can begin with interface-level pilots and limited automation while defining evaluation criteria for these pilots based on workload, safety, or trust outcomes. This staged approach is especially useful in settings where direct implementation evidence remains limited, because it can later be strengthened through expert panels, pilot studies, or Delphi-based methods.

Looking ahead, EHR-linked AI may move beyond clinician-initiated requests toward more event- and data-triggered support, such as proactive risk detection, workflow orchestration, and documentation assistance. Although the included studies do not support firm forecasting, this direction highlights practical design and governance needs, including modular integration, clear accountability boundaries, and periodic evidence updates as EHR-AI architectures continue to evolve.

Recent literature also shows that AI is already being deployed in several ways rather than as a single type of EHR add-on. Reported applications include point-of-care extraction and structuring of clinical text [61], hospital-level AI platforms embedded in clinical IT for model development and deployment [57], human-centered decision-support arrangements intended to strengthen communication, trust, and shared decision-making [62], and mobile tools supporting wound assessment and treatment planning at the point of care [63]. These examples differ in technical architecture, end users, and workflow position. Together, they show that evaluation must consider not only whether AI is present, but also how it is integrated into care, who uses it, and what interaction and downstream effects follow.

Assumptions, lessons learned, and transferable use cases

Our interpretation is based on three assumptions. First, this review is intended as a construct-level synthesis rather than a pooled effect analysis. Second, AI-linked functionality was considered only when it was explicitly reported or clearly connected to EHR-mediated work. Third, interface, information, interaction, and outcome are presented as distinct analytical layers, while acknowledging that they remain closely connected in practice. These assumptions made it possible to compare heterogeneous studies without treating different technologies, settings, and periods as directly equivalent.

Several lessons emerge from the reviewed evidence. Data quality, cleaning, and interoperability should not be treated only as back-end technical matters, because they shape trust, usability, and the interpretability of recommendations in frontline care [12, 13, 26]. In addition, AI features do not create value simply by being added to the system; their contribution depends on task fit, their effect on day-to-day work, and the extent to which they are supported by training and governance [4, 6, 7]. The findings also indicate that organizational conditions, including leadership support, IT-clinical collaboration, and standard workflows, remain important for sustained use [43, 44, 45]. Taken together, these findings suggest several practical priorities before AI functions are scaled, including cleaner and more interoperable data pipelines, clearer alignment between models and clinical tasks, workflow-based training, and visible governance and support mechanisms.

These findings may also inform adjacent digital-health contexts beyond hospital EHR optimization. Similar design principles may inform cross-sector health-environment data spaces, prevention-oriented behavior-change platforms, and digital-twin ecosystems, all of which require interoperable data flows, explainable outputs, and accountable operational integration [8, 9, 10]. These adjacent domains illustrate the broader value of AI-supported health systems, but they also reinforce a shared challenge: aligning data, models, users, and governance over time.

Next-generation GenAI and LLM features are a plausible extension of this trajectory, especially for conversational retrieval, documentation assistance, explanation support, and multimodal summarization. Recent work on LLM-powered conversational agents in XR environments shows how retrieval-grounded dialogue can deliver context-aware expertise at the point of use, while broader GenAI surveys emphasize that model choice must remain aligned with domain-specific data, aims, and constraints [64, 65]. For EHR-linked use, this implies that future GenAI evaluation should cover provenance, escalation boundaries, workload effects, and safety controls rather than only output fluency [52].

Conclusions and limitations

Conclusions

This study builds on and extends the 3I socio-technical view of EHRs proposed by Miller et al. by introducing a fourth Outcome layer and by operationalizing the 4I layers through 29 variables identified in the literature. Across the 70 included studies, selected through a PRISMA-documented screening process, the literature discusses EHR systems (or AI-linked functions) beyond interface design and documentation tasks. Many studies also address patient-safety considerations, clinician workload and well-being, and organizational conditions that shape implementation.

Collectively, the review demonstrates that AI-related modifications to EHRs are portrayed in the literature as layered rather than solitary. First, the 29-variable 4I framework depicts how current studies map these changes at the interface, information, interaction, and outcome levels (RQ1). Second, the adoption profiles show that comparable features can operate as either drivers or barriers depending on how they are built and maintained in practice (RQ2). Third, the cross-layer patterns connect these circumstances to reported outcomes, including trust, technological adoption, perceived safety, and burnout, but they are treated as associations rather than causal effects from a single dataset (RQ3).

The groups that can benefit most directly from this research are clinicians, clinical informatics teams, hospital managers, health-IT vendors, and researchers. Clinicians can use the framework to identify where workflow friction and trust-related issues arise in EHR-supported care. Clinical informatics and IT teams can use it to structure implementation reviews and to align technical configuration with clinical use. Hospital managers can use it to connect governance, support, and standardization decisions with downstream outcomes. Vendors can use the framework to assess design fit beyond narrow usability metrics, and researchers can use the 29-variable dictionary as a basis for further empirical and expert-based work [1, 4, 14, 43, 44].

The framework can be disseminated through scholarly publication, conference presentation, and appendix-based traceability materials, and may support dialogue among clinical, managerial, and technical stakeholders [43, 44].

The immediate goal of this follow-on work is not to declare a final universal model, but to refine and test the 29-variable set prospectively in implementation settings where traceability can be observed in practice.

Future work can proceed in three stages. In the near term, the 29-variable dictionary can be refined through expert elicitation and small-scale implementation review. In the medium term, the framework can support pilot evaluation in one or two hospital contexts, including newer GenAI/LLM-enabled use cases such as documentation assistance or conversational decision support [52, 64, 65]. In the longer term, multi-site and cross-region studies can examine the stability and transferability of the framework and assess whether additional variables are needed for emerging EHR-AI architectures. This staged approach reflects the need for periodic refinement as AI-linked EHR functions continue to evolve.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that are inherent to its design as an integrative review and variable-mapping exercise. First, the 29-variable set was derived from a systematic yet interpretive synthesis of various studies, rather than from a single, consolidated dataset. While predefined extraction templates, iterative reviewer checks, and targeted verification by the supervising professor were used to guide interpretation, the decisions regarding the consolidation of comparable constructs and their placement within the 4I framework inevitably involved subjective judgment. Different research teams or assessments that incorporate additional sources may combine or separate variables in slightly different ways. Given the evolving evidence base on AI-linked EHR functions, future syntheses may identify additional constructs and revisit some of the current layer assignments.

Secondly, the underlying evidence base remains constrained. The majority of the 70 included studies originate from English-language, hospital-focused research conducted within higher-income healthcare systems and from locations that depend on a limited number of significant EHR vendors. This indicates that the existing 4I framework might inadequately capture issues arising in primary care, community, or low-resource environments, or within systems functioning under diverse regulatory, invoicing, or interoperability frameworks. Future research should investigate whether the same set of variables remains applicable or requires modification in those contexts. Geographically, much of the included evidence comes from high-income and English-language settings; therefore, transferability to other regions—particularly settings with different vendor landscapes or API ecosystems—should be interpreted cautiously. A further limitation is the long review window itself. EHR architectures, decision-support functions, and AI-related integration patterns changed substantially across 2005–2025, while many studies did not report version-level technical detail. For this reason, the review should be read as a construct-level synthesis across an evolving field rather than as a time-stable comparison of equivalent technologies. We therefore recommend updating the review on a rolling basis (e.g., every 3–5 years), expanding coverage to additional regions, and explicitly accounting for newer EHR–AI architectures and integration patterns. A more explicit analysis of technology timeframes and architectural evolution was outside the scope of the present review and would be a useful direction for future updates.

Third, the 4I framework and the 29-item instrument are introduced here as conceptual outputs of the review and have not yet undergone prospective testing within real-world EHR–AI implementations. We outline how this variable set could be applied in subsequent causal modeling efforts, such as Delphi-based consensus exercises or related influence-mapping methodologies. However, the current manuscript does not include formal consensus procedures or psychometric validation. Follow-up empirical research, employing expert committees, clinician surveys, and data from actual deployments, will be necessary to assess the stability, utility, and interrelationships of these variables prior to their application as decision-making tools in practice.

Fourth, the AI-related evidence base is heterogeneous in both maturity and application type. As discussed above, recent studies show AI being used in several ways, including point-of-care extraction and structuring tools, integrated hospital AI platforms, human-centered decision-support arrangements, and mobile assessment applications [57, 61, 62, 63]. Our review could capture these developments only at a thematic level. This also reflects the timeframe boundary noted earlier: the review was not designed to compare application-specific trajectories or to identify technology timeframes consistently across studies. As a result, although the review shows why AI-related developments have made outcome-level questions more visible, it does not determine when particular application types became more prominent, nor does it examine their temporal evolution in a systematic way. Future reviews could address this more directly by using shorter update windows, application-specific sampling frames, or separate syntheses for infrastructure, workflow-support, and patient-facing AI uses.

Despite these limitations, this review provides a pragmatic foundation for analyzing EHR–AI as a layered socio-technical system. The 4I framework and the 29-variable set consolidate disparate literature into a coherent set of practical constructs that can be empirically tested, critically examined, and refined in subsequent research. As healthcare organizations deploy AI-linked EHR tools (e.g., ambient documentation, risk stratification, and integrated decision support), the framework can be applied across contexts to clarify adoption conditions and downstream effects. This includes implications for clinical quality, safety, and governance.
