The quest for missing risk factors to predict melanoma
Authors: M Wakkee, L M Hollestein
Published: 2025

Bellinato et al. assessed the association between air pollutants exposure and the risk of melanoma, as known risk factors may not fully explain the increasing global incidence. 1 Air pollutants, also called particulate matter (PM), are a mixture of solid and liquid microscopic substances in the air such as dust, elemental and organic carbon and various chemical compounds. The particulates are categorized by their aerodynamic diameter like PM2.5 (<2.5 μm) and PM10 (2.5–10 μm) determining their capacity to bypass the body's natural barriers. In this case–control study, long‐term exposure to PM was compared between 1473 melanoma patients and 1102 healthy controls residing in North and Central Italy, suggesting a neutral or even protective effect of high PM levels on melanoma risk.

The findings of this study are remarkable as studies generally suggest that PM has a detrimental effect by penetrating the skin and causing oxidative stress and triggering inflammation, which might even lead to exacerbations of conditions like atopic dermatitis, acne and psoriasis. 2 Even skin conditions more closely related to UV exposure, such as skin pigmentation and wrinkles, are generally thought to be increased by PM. 2 In the study by Bellinato et al., the initial univariable logistic regression showed that PM exposure was associated with an increased risk of melanoma. However, after adjusting for covariates like age, sex, smoking, sun exposure and Fitzpatrick skin phototype, the association with PM flipped into a protective effect for melanoma. 1 In epidemiological studies, it may be informative to make a directed acyclic graph (DAG) before performing multivariable analyses, as this helps to identify relationships between variables (e.g. intermediates, confounders and colliders) and potential biases (e.g. selection bias). 3 DAGs contribute to determining which factors need to be adjusted to obtain an unbiased estimate of the potential causal effect. For example, while Fitzpatrick skin phototype is associated with the risk of melanoma, it may not be associated with exposure to PM. In that case, it would not be a confounder and may not need to be included in the multivariable model.

When we compared the cohort of melanoma patients to that of healthy controls, we noticed a remarkable difference. More than three quarters of the melanoma patients (76%) had reported a history of sunburns, while this was less than a quarter (24%) among the healthy controls. Reporting of sunburn history is a notorious variable to be affected by recall bias because of the growing public awareness of this risk factor for melanoma. This has probably also affected the current study. It might be that by adjusting for this variable, the multivariable model has flipped the association between PM and the risk of melanoma. It would therefore be an interesting exercise to repeat the multivariable model without adding the history of sunburns.

Another challenge the authors face within the current study is the imprecision of measuring and modelling PM. Measurement technologies have limitations in their ability to detect all types and sizes of particles per location, and individual exposure is even more complex. While PM concentrations were based on residential addresses, the actual exposure may, for example, vary depending on the amount of time spent outdoors as well as the time spent at a working environment that often differs from the residential address. Perhaps wearable devices may in the future lead to a more precise measurement of environmental factor exposure.

The above‐mentioned limitations make it difficult to point out if PM has a role in the risk of melanoma. Subsequent epidemiological studies should include causal diagrams, address residual confounding and detailed individual modelling of PM exposure to further explore the association between air pollution and melanoma.
