Postpartum diabetes screening for women with gestational diabetes in Japan: an administrative database study
Authors: Toshiki Yoshioka, Masataka Shikata, Sayuri Nakanishi, Kentaro Kurasawa, Shigeru Aoki, Etsuko Miyagi, Atsushi Goto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo estimate uptake of the 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at 6–12 weeks postpartum among women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) in Japan, and to explore the demographic and clinical characteristics associated with screening uptake.DesignRetrospective cohort study using administrative claims data.SettingData from a nationwide employees’ health insurance claims database (Japan Medical Data Center; JMDC) during the fiscal years 2012–2020 were assessed.Participants2282 women with GDM in the JMDC claims database (April 2012–January 2021), ascertained using the International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) codes cross-validated with a high-risk GDM management fee.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcome: completion of a 75 g OGTT at 6–12 weeks postpartum. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is an important public health challenge owing to its associated high risk of morbidity and mortality.1 T2DM leads to a diminished quality of life and substantial healthcare costs, highlighting the importance of preventive strategies, particularly for high-risk groups. The prevalence of and healthcare expenses associated with T2DM are increasing.2 Early detection and intervention, including lifestyle changes and pharmacological therapy such as metformin, have been reported to be useful for delaying T2DM onset in high-risk groups.3

Women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) are recognised as one such high-risk group. In Japan, where roughly 0.76–0.80 million births occur each year, recent studies indicate that GDM complicates about 7–9% of pregnancies, whereas global estimates reach up to 13%.4 5 GDM not only predisposes women to pregnancy complications but also confers substantial long-term risk: those with a history of GDM have a 7–10-fold higher likelihood of developing T2DM—an absolute risk of 50–60% over a 40–50 year follow-up period. Early intervention, including lifestyle modification and metformin therapy, has been reported to delay T2DM onset in women with GDM history.6 7 This is particularly effective for women who are obese before pregnancy or who have impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) postpartum.7

Postpartum screening of women with GDM is essential for early detection and prevention of T2DM. Moreover, monitoring of these screening rates is crucial for developing effective prevention strategies. Approximately 4–5% of women with prior GDM develop T2DM and 25–49% develop IGT by 12–13 weeks after delivery; therefore, early postpartum glucose testing and stratified management are needed.8,10 Most countries—including Japan—recommend postpartum screening with a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) 4–12 weeks after delivery11; however, uptake remains low and highly variable (9–71%), with most evidence stemming from studies in the USA, UK and Canada.812,15 Nonetheless, no detailed study has reported the postpartum screening rate in women with GDM in Japan. Furthermore, the birth settings in Japan differ substantially from that in other countries, with 48% of all deliveries in Japan occurring in clinics with <20 beds and 52% in hospitals16; while >96% of deliveries are reported in hospitals in the countries previously referenced, namely the USA, the UK and Canada.17,19 This variance in birth settings may influence glucose management practices for women with GDM (eg, communication between healthcare providers), highlighting the importance of monitoring screening rates in Japan. Furthermore, the characteristics associated with higher or lower screening rates have not been adequately identified.

Therefore, this study was conducted to address the aforementioned questions, utilising a health insurance claims database encompassing ~10 million individuals from various health insurance associations in Japan. This study aimed to evaluate the screening rates of a 75 g OGTT performed 4–12 weeks postpartum in women whose pregnancies were complicated by GDM and to determine the demographic and clinical characteristics associated with screening uptake.

Materials and Methods

Data sources

We extracted administrative claims data from the Japan Medical Data Center (JMDC) claims database (JMDC Inc., Tokyo, Japan).20 The Japanese health insurance framework ensures coverage for all citizens via a range of programmes, including the Employees’ Health Insurance programme, National Health Insurance programme and Insurance System for Latter-Stage Elderly People for citizens aged 75 years and older. The JMDC database is a large electronic database that collects health insurance claims from various employees’ health insurance programmes. The database primarily includes data from employees working in medium to large-scale corporations and their families, particularly those aged 15–65 years. The database cumulatively has 14 million subscribers, representing ~10% of the Japanese population. All the information in the JMDC database is coded and categorised meticulously. Clinical diagnoses are based on the International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10); medications are coded using the WHO’s Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system; and procedures are based on Japanese original medical claim codes. All direct personal identifiers are removed, and each beneficiary is assigned a randomly generated, encrypted ID; therefore, the dataset is pseudonymised. Since the data are unidentifiable, written informed consent was not required for this study. This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review board of the Yokohama City University (Reference No B200800024).

Study population

In Japan, national guidelines recommend universal screening for GDM. Pregnant women are screened during early and mid-pregnancy, and those with positive results undergo a diagnostic 75 g OGTT; GDM is confirmed according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria. The guideline specifies a two-step screening strategy: (i) a random plasma glucose test in early pregnancy and (ii) either a 50 g glucose challenge test or another random plasma glucose test at 24–28 weeks. We identified pregnant women diagnosed with GDM between April 2012 and January 2021 using ICD-10 codes for GDM (O244 or O249). Recognising the potential inaccuracies of solely relying on GDM diagnosis, we enhanced the validity by incorporating cases with ‘incentive for glucose management for high-risk GDM women’ (Japanese medical claim code: 114021110) to identify true GDM cases. This fee, established by the Japanese government in April 2012, is part of the medical reimbursement system under the public health insurance platform, specifically designated for high-risk patients with GDM requiring glucose self-monitoring for meticulous management, claimable up to once per month until the end of pregnancy. Detailed criteria for claim eligibility are provided in the supplementary file.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) delivery date unidentifiable, (2) diagnoses of type 1 or type 2 diabetes prior to the delivery date, (3) unable to determine whether the delivery and GDM diagnosis occurred during the same pregnancy, (4) exact date of the postpartum 75 g OGTT missing and (5) withdrawal from the database within 12 weeks from the date of delivery.

We identified the date of deliveries using procedures, medications and diagnostic codes, an approach that has been validated previously.21 We used ICD-10 codes for identifying the diagnoses of type 1 or type 2 diabetes (starting with E10–E14). To confirm GDM diagnosis and delivery occurrence within the same pregnancy, women with a time span ≤100 days between the last date of ‘incentive for glucose management for high-risk GDM women’ in the database and the date of delivery were included in the study. The design diagram22 is presented in online supplemental figure 1.

Measured outcomes and variables

The outcome was defined as the rate of 75 g OGTT conducted between 6 and 12 weeks postpartum. The 75 g OGTT was identified by the Japanese medical claim codes (160087210, 160087650, 160087310 and 160087750). We identified the date of births using Japanese medical claim codes (online supplemental table). We considered all cases except cases of caesarean delivery and instrumental vaginal deliveries. We considered the prescription of insulin or psychiatric agents prescribed from 100 days prior to delivery until the date of delivery. Psychiatric agents were identified based on the ATC classification code starting with N05 or N06. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) were included if they were coded with ICD-10 codes starting with O10, O11, O13, O14, O15, O16 or I10 from 100 days prior to the delivery date until 28 days postpartum. The prescription of antihypertensive medications postpartum was defined as those prescribed from day 1 to day 28 postpartum with ATC classification codes starting with C02, C07, C08 or C09. Multiple pregnancies were identified if coded with ICD-10 codes starting with O30 from 280 days prior to delivery until the date of delivery. If the delivery facility and the facility where ‘incentive for glucose management for high-risk GDM women’ was last claimed were different, we assumed that the management of delivery and GDM were conducted at different medical institutions. We also collected the information of ‘financial dependency’ status in alignment with the Japanese taxation system’s classification. A ‘responsible taxpayer’ is defined as the individual primarily responsible for the financial well-being of one or more dependents. A ‘qualifying dependent’ refers to a person—often a spouse, child or parent—who relies substantially on the ‘responsible taxpayer’ for financial support. The study design diagram is presented in the appendix file. We also calculated the claim rate of ‘incentive for postpartum glucose management’ among women giving birth in the fiscal year 2020. This financial incentive was introduced by the Japanese government in the fiscal year 2020 to improve postpartum glucose management and is a once-per-woman fee payable to obstetric or internal-medicine providers when counselling on postpartum blood-glucose management is documented within 12 weeks after delivery. This postpartum incentive is distinct from the antenatal high-risk GDM management fee (claim code 114021110) introduced in April 2012 and is used in the Study population section to improve ascertainment of true GDM cases during pregnancy; the 2012 antenatal fee was not used to calculate postpartum claim rates.

Statistical analysis

We calculated the rate of 75 g OGTT performed between 6 and 12 weeks postpartum for the entire cohort and by the fiscal year (which starts in April and ends in March in Japan). To account for the 75 g OGTT performed outside the 6–12 week postpartum range, we used the Kaplan-Meier estimation to calculate the cumulative rate of 75 g OGTT performed between 4 weeks and 1 year postpartum, treating withdrawal from the database as censoring.

We then calculated the year-standardised screening rates with respect to various demographic characteristics, considering that potential time-related changes in clinical practice could lead to annual variations in the 75 g OGTT screening rate and could therefore obscure the rate differences among the variables under study. Thus, logistic regression models were fitted for each variable to estimate year-standardised marginal means (year-standardised screening rate) and their 95%CIs. Each model included linear and quadratic terms for fiscal years, centred around 2020. The variables analysed in the models were age (categorised in 5-year intervals), mode of delivery, dependency status, insulin use, number of beds in the delivery facility, difference in management facilities, HDP and prescriptions of antihypertensive agents postpartum, multiple pregnancies and prescription of psychiatric agents during pregnancy. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3.23

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the study.

Results

We included 2282 women from the JMDC database in the analysis (online supplemental figure 2). The characteristics of the women included in the analysis are presented in table 1. Of the 2282 eligible women, the median age at delivery was 35 years (IQR (IQR), 31–38) and 706 cases (31%) had caesarean delivery. Medical facilities with >500 beds were the choice for delivery in 1043 cases (46%). Insulin was prescribed for 485 cases (21%) during pregnancy. HDP was diagnosed in 312 cases (14%) and 133 cases (6%) required postpartum antihypertensive therapy via oral medication. Cases where the delivery and GDM management occurred at the same facility totalled 1776 (78%), while other cases were managed at different facilities.

The overall screening rate was 28.7% (654/2282). Figure 1 presents the trend of the 75 g OGTT screening rate 6–12 weeks postpartum. While the trend indicated an increasing screening rate between fiscal years 2012 and 2020, the number of cases was limited until the fiscal year 2015, resulting in imprecise estimates in these years. Even in 2020, the most recent year with the highest screening rate, it remained low at 33.2% (181/546). Figure 2 presents the cumulative rate of 75 g OGTT performed after 4 weeks postpartum, considering the withdrawal from the database beyond 12 weeks postpartum. The screening rate 4–12 weeks postpartum was 35.8% (830/2282). Moreover, 7.7% (176/2282) of the cases underwent screening during the 4–6 week postpartum period. Figure 4 shows that the location of antenatal glucose management varied by facility size. Women who delivered in small clinics (0–19 beds) were often referred to a different facility for GDM care, whereas women delivering in large hospitals (≥500 beds) usually received both obstetric and glucose management in the same institution. Specifically, delivery and GDM were managed within the same facility for 41.4% (133/329) of women delivering in clinics with 0–19 beds, compared with 89.8% (937/1 043) of women delivering in hospitals with ≥500 beds. Discussion

Summary of key findings

Our study using an administrative health database revealed that although the screening rate of the 75 g OGTT between 6 and 12 weeks postpartum for GDM women increased from 2012 to 2020, only ~30% of the cases were tested. Furthermore, on expanding the range to include 4 weeks to ≤1 year postpartum, the data showed that 35% of women still had not undergone screening. Additionally, our analysis of the screening rates across basic demographic characteristics indicated that the 75 g OGTT screening rate during the 6–12 weeks postpartum period was lower when the delivery and GDM were managed at different facilities than when both were managed within the same facility.

Comparisons with other studies and possible explanations of the results

In accordance with reports from other countries, the screening rate obtained in this study was low. In a systematic review on postpartum screening rates, the average screening rate was 33.5%,13 closely aligning with our findings. Barriers inhibiting screening include busyness due to child-rearing, lack of awareness among healthcare professionals and care fragmentation.24 25 In this study, we demonstrated that the claim rate for the government-funded fee (‘incentive for postpartum glucose management’”) was notably low at 13.2%. Although its first-year uptake was modest, such a reimbursement could offset facility costs, signal governmental priority and prompt providers to schedule postpartum testing—mechanisms that may translate into higher screening rates in subsequent years. While some studies show that policy changes can boost postpartum screening—for example, a UK study found higher uptake after the 2015 update of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline recommending routine testing26—raising awareness among healthcare professionals remains crucial for improving uptake. Additionally, our study revealed that screening rates are lower when the delivery and GDM are managed at different facilities. This could be attributed to the challenges new mothers face, including visiting two different medical facilities while handling childcare responsibilities,24 27 28 inadequate communication between healthcare professionals about postpartum glucose management29 and the disruption of the appointments with internists due to the unpredictable and abrupt nature of delivery.

Our study observed heterogeneity in clinical practices across various birth settings. Screening rates may be influenced by structural and systemic differences between facilities, such as the size of the delivery facility, differences in postpartum follow-up methods depending on the presence of pregnancy complications or the mode of delivery and individual patient characteristics, such as age and the presence of mental conditions requiring psychiatric medications. Although Japan has a single national guideline that recommends universal postpartum OGTT after GDM, adherence depends on how each institution operationalises this recommendation. Because the guideline is advisory and does not specify whether obstetric or internal-medicine staff should arrange the test, implementation varies with local resources, referral pathways and electronic reminder systems. In this study, we observed that women giving birth in smaller facilities were more likely to have their GDM managed at different facilities than those at larger facilities. Facilities with fewer beds, typically single-specialty birth centres, might frequently outsource the GDM management to other institutions. In contrast, almost 90% of the women delivering in facilities with ≥200 beds were managed within the same facility, contributing to better screening practices. However, the screening rates at large facilities with ≥200 beds were remarkably lower than those of small facilities with 20–199 beds. Large facilities may experience lower screening rates due to challenges including less personalised patient relationships, more rigid administrative structures and less effective communication with other facilities.

We also found that a considerable proportion of screening tests were conducted outside the 6–12 week postpartum window. In our analysis, 7.7% (176/2282) of cases were tested 4–6 weeks postpartum. In Japan, check-ups are provided for women 1 month postpartum; however, after 6 weeks, patients are required to revisit the medical facilities to schedule the 75 g OGTT. Since barriers to postpartum screening include time constraints due to childcare,27 some facilities could offer 75 g OGTT during the 1-month check-up to reduce maternal burden. Furthermore, in Japan, some women return to their parental home for childbirth and postnatal care. According to a 2017 survey by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, 26.5% of pregnant women gave birth in facilities in different municipalities than their homes, with an average stay of 5.8 weeks (median, 4.0 weeks), potentially necessitating earlier testing before 6 weeks postpartum.30 We also observed a continuous increase in the screening rate beyond 12 weeks postpartum and up to 5–6 months postpartum, reaching 64.5% until 1 year postpartum. This could be attributed to new mothers being preoccupied with childcare and facing unpredictable situations resulting in the sudden need to change their planned screening appointments.

Implications

Considering the context in Japan, it may be beneficial to explore the optimal timeframe to promote screening. Due to the lack of consensus regarding the optimal screening time, current guidelines vary internationally in most countries, including Japan,11 with the recommended timeframe for postpartum 75 g OGTT being between 6 and 12 weeks. The Canadian Diabetes Association (CDA) extends this recommendation to up to 6 months31, and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), along with the American Diabetes Association (ADA), suggests the period of 4–12 weeks postpartum for screening.32 33 To enhance screening compliance, the ACOG and ADA extended the recommended testing window to 4–12 weeks postpartum in 2017.34 Considering that many cases were tested outside the recommended time, along with the specific circumstances in Japan, not strictly adhering to the 6–12 weeks postpartum period may facilitate more supportive care that better aligns with the needs of new mothers. In this context, offering the OGTT during the routine 1 month check-up—and, if that opportunity is missed, at any later visit instead of discontinuing the follow-up once 12 weeks have passed—may be a pragmatic alternative that remains consistent with the broader time frames endorsed in other guidelines (eg, the CDA permits testing up to 6 months, and ACOG/ADA accept 4–12 weeks).31 32

In addition to system-level changes in screening, approaches such as educational interventions and reminders may also be valuable in enhancing screening rates and encouraging subsequent preventive behaviours. Barriers to postpartum screening include a lack of time and feeling overwhelmed due to childcare, as well as the lack of awareness of the long-term negative impacts of GDM or the fear of receiving unfavourable news about their health. To overcome these barriers and promote awareness, educating women, either online or offline, during pregnancy about GDM—including the risks to pregnancy outcomes as well as the increased risk of developing T2DM and the importance of early detection—has been shown to be an effective intervention.35,38 Raising awareness of the risks and the importance of taking care of their own health can help encourage women to not only participate in early postpartum screening but also continue with follow-up care. Reminders are also a commonly used intervention, though their effectiveness has been inconsistent.38 However, by using reminders in combination with a solid foundation of patient education, these interventions can work together to systematically remove the barriers to screening, providing repeated reinforcement and addressing each challenge with care.

Strengths of this study

This study has several strengths. First, this study included diverse institutions, representing the current state and trends of clinical practice so far. Second, by demonstrating that women whose delivery and GDM care were provided at different facilities had substantially lower screening rates than those managed within a single institution, our study points to the potential relevance of effective inter-physician communication and coordination across birth settings.

Limitations of the study

There are some limitations to this study. First, the exact delivery date could not be calculated from the claims data. However, the validity of the algorithm used for detecting the delivery date has been demonstrated previously21; therefore, the analysis is expected to yield plausible results. However, only medical interventions performed at the time of delivery were eligible for insurance coverage. Cases not requiring such interventions were excluded from this study, which may have led to selection bias. Second, selection bias may exist owing to the exclusion of cases that withdrew from the database before 12 weeks postpartum (209/2491=8.4%). One of the features of the JMDC database is that follow-up becomes impossible when patients change their job or health insurance type. These cases could belong to a socio-economic group different from the analysis cohort. However, even if all these cases had undergone testing during the 6–12 week postpartum period, the 75 g OGTT screening rate would still be (209+654)/2491=34.6%, which is unlikely to distort the main study conclusions. Third, the number of cases was particularly low between 2012 and 2015, resulting in less precise estimates for each year. However, since 2016, the latest trends appear to have stabilised with relatively narrow CIs, suggesting reliable estimates. Fourth, the findings may not be equally generalisable to all population segments. The JMDC database mainly consists of employees from medium to large-scale corporations and their families with relatively high socio-economic status. Within socio-economically diverse groups, particularly among those with low socio-economic status, the screening rates could be even lower. Furthermore, the study cohort did not cover the entire population of GDM women and only analysed data from women in the high-risk group requiring intensive blood sugar monitoring during pregnancy. Since delivery itself is not an insured event in Japan, an ICD-10 code for GDM can persist from a prior pregnancy or be entered for counselling alone; using diagnosis codes without the fee would therefore misclassify pregnancies and preclude a reliable sensitivity analysis. Women excluded from our analysis received only nutritional advice and no other medical intervention for GDM and are presumed to have had minimal interaction with the healthcare system, potentially resulting in a lower screening rate. Finally, because claims data do not capture a women’s motivations or providers’ decision-making process, the mechanisms we discuss remain hypothetical and should be confirmed in future qualitative or survey-based studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study presents the first quantified assessment of Japan’s suboptimal national screening rates for women with a history of GDM. This study highlights the need for continuous monitoring, effective strategies and the development of early screening and interventions in this high-risk group. Moreover, this study highlights the importance of enhancing communication between physicians involved in postpartum glycaemic management.
