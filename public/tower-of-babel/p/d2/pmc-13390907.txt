Successful treatment of severe iatrogenic hypercalcemia and acute kidney injury with hemodialysis in a cat
Authors: Lauren A DelMaestro, Rebecca T Bernstein, Peter S Chapman, Nolan V Chalifoux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 4-year-old male castrated domestic shorthair cat was diagnosed with a urethral obstruction after presenting for vomiting, inappetence, and inappropriate urination. The cat developed iatrogenic hypercalcemia (ionized calcium > 4 mmol/L, total calcium 57 mg/dL) and an acute kidney injury (creatinine 9.81 mg/dL) suspected to be a consequence of an overdose of calcium gluconate. Approximately 12 h after admission, the cat became comatose and developed cardiovascular instability. A 6-h hemodialysis treatment was performed using continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration on the Prismaflex platform and hypercalcemia resolved during treatment. The cat’s creatinine normalized (1.01 mg/dL) within 72 h and the cat was discharged after 6 days of hospitalization. The cat subsequently developed signs of upper respiratory and gastrointestinal disease. Diagnostics including upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and bronchoscopy showed evidence of diffuse calcification of the trachea and gastric mucosa. This case demonstrates the successful application of hemodialysis in veterinary medicine to treat an acute kidney injury and iatrogenic hypercalcemia and provides unique documentation of its potential long-term sequelae.Keywords: calcification, electrolyte derangements, extracorporeal, medical error, toxicosis

Introduction

Medical errors are an unavoidable aspect of practicing medicine.1 While some medical errors have limited effects on the animal, others are lethal. In the veterinary field, 42% of medical errors result in harm to the animal, 5% of which cause death.1 Consequently, there is great importance in creating a positive animal safety culture in hospitals and reporting successful interventions to prevent undesirable outcomes.2 In this case report, we describe the clinical consequences of an iatrogenic calcium overdose in a cat that was successfully managed with hemodialysis.

Case description

A 4-year-old, 6.16 kg, male castrated domestic shorthair cat was presented to BluePearl Pet Hospital in Levittown, Pennsylvania, for inappropriate urination. On presentation (day 0), the cat was diagnosed with a urethral obstruction. A venous blood gas (epoc Blood Analysis System, Siemens Healthineers, Malvern, PA) showed marked hyperkalemia (7.9 mmol/L, reference interval [RI]: 3.6-5.6 mmol/L), azotemia (blood urea nitrogen [BUN] 120 mg/dL, RI: 15-34 mg/dL; creatinine 10.32 mg/dL, RI: 0.5-1.9 mg/dL), and ionized hypocalcemia (0.83 mmol/L, RI: 1.21-1.51 mmol/L). To treat the hyperkalemia, the cat received 56 mg/kg of 23% calcium gluconate (Fresenius Kabi, Lake Zurich, IL), 1 unit of Humulin R insulin IV (Eli Lilly and Company, Branchburg, NJ), and a 0.5 g/kg of 50% dextrose (Durvet Inc, Blue Springs, MO) bolus IV at presentation. Subsequently, the cat received 0.2 mg/kg of methadone (Patheon Manufacturing Services LLC, Greenville, NC), 0.2 mg/kg of midazolam (Hikma Pharmaceuticals USA Inc, Berkeley Heights, NJ), and propofol (Zoetis, Parsippany-Troy Hills, NJ) IV titrated to effect for placement of an indwelling 3.5 Fr MILA urinary catheter (Mila International Inc, Hebron, KY). In-house urinalysis showed hematuria and rare struvite crystalluria. Abdominal radiographs showed no evidence of radiopaque cystoliths and therefore, the cat was diagnosed with presumptive idiopathic cystitis. Treatment orders were made for 4 mL/kg/h of lactated Ringer’s solution (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) with a 5% dextrose additive, 0.015 mg/kg q8h buprenorphine PO (Epicur Pharma, Mt Laurel, NJ), and 0.4 mg/kg PO q12h acepromazine (MWI Animal Health, Boise, ID). The fluid rate was matched to the urine output.

Approximately 9 h later (day 1), there was persistent azotemia (BUN > 120 mg/dL, creatinine 9.81 mg/dL), hyperkalemia (7 mmol/L), and ionized hypercalcemia (3.37 mmol/L). The cat received 0.01 mg/kg of terbutaline (West-Ward Pharmaceuticals, Tinton Falls, NJ) IV to treat the hyperkalemia. Throughout the day, the cat became weaker, obtunded, and developed muscle fasciculations. Bloodwork 6 h later showed progressive ionized hypercalcemia (> 4 mmol/L). The total calcium concentration was elevated (57 mg/dL, RI: 8.2-10.8 mg/dL). After further investigation, it was determined that 100 mL of 23% calcium gluconate was added to the lactated Ringer’s solution rather than 50% dextrose, resulting in a final concentration of 23 mg/mL of calcium gluconate (2.1 mg/mL of elemental calcium). The infusion had been administered for 14 h at a varying rate of 4-14 mL/kg/h, resulting in a dose of 93-317 mg/kg/h of calcium gluconate (8.69-29.5 mg/kg/h of elemental calcium). Glucose and ionized calcium (iCa) concentrations were measured from the fluid bag, with the iCa and glucose concentrations being above (iCa > 4 mmol/L) and below (Glu < 20 mg/dL, RI: 63-133 mg/dL) the analyzer’s limits of detection, respectively. The cat’s IV fluids were switched to 0.9% NaCl (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) and care was transferred to the critical care service for hemodialysis.

Blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and potassium were >140 mg/dL, 9.29 mg/dL, and 8.3 mmol/L, respectively, before hemodialysis treatment. A BUN dilution was performed on a separate analyzer and was 124 mg/dL. Anesthesia was induced with 0.2 mg/kg of midazolam, 5 mcg/kg of fentanyl (USP, Hospira, Lake Forest, IL), and 0.5 mg/kg of alfaxalone (Zoetis, Parsippany-Troy Hills, NJ) IV and maintained using 0.4%-0.6% isoflurane (Dechra Veterinary Products, Overland Park, KS). An 8 Fr 16 cm hemodialysis catheter (Mila International Inc, Hebron, KY) was placed in the right jugular vein via fluoroscopic guidance and was confirmed to be terminating in the caudal vena cava. A 14 Fr esophagostomy tube (Mila International Inc, Hebron, KY) was placed on the left side of the neck. Isoflurane inhalation was discontinued after esophagostomy tube and dialysis catheter placement; however, the cat remained intubated without further sedation. The cat received a 10 mL/kg polyionic fluid (Plasma-Lyte A, Baxter Healthcare Corporation, Deerfield, IL) bolus and a continuous rate infusion (CRI) of norepinephrine (0.2-0.6 mcg/kg/min; Pfizer, Lake Forest, IL) due to hypotension (mean arterial pressure [MAP] 55 mmHg). The cat’s blood pressure improved to a MAP of 80 mmHg before initiating hemodialysis. A 6-h hemodialysis treatment was prescribed using continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration on the Prismaflex platform with an HF20 set (Baxter Healthcare Corporation, Deerfield, IL). The arterial and venous catheter lines were reversed to avoid blood recirculation. In total, 25 mL of packed red blood cells, 25 mL of fresh frozen plasma, and 30 mL of 0.9% NaCl were used to prime the extracorporeal circuit due to a large extracorporeal blood volume (17% of total blood volume). A target urea reduction ratio of 10%/h was prescribed over an initial 6 h (Kt/V of 0.9) with 0 net ultrafiltration. To minimize the risk of dialysis disequilibrium syndrome, the prescribed treatment intensity was limited to a urea reduction ratio of 10%/h due to a pretreatment BUN of 100-200 mg/dL.3 The blood flow rate was maintained at 20 mL/min. The total effluent rate was prescribed at 560 mL/h (dialysate flow rate of 500 mL/h and post-filter replacement solution rate of 60 mL/h; target filtration fraction of 7%). The fluid removal rate was prescribed at 0 mL/h. The initial dialysate was BK0/3.5 (potassium concentration, 0 mmol/L; iCa concentration, 3.5 mmol/L; Baxter Healthcare Corporation, Deerfield, IL) due to hyperkalemia with a 110 mg/dL dextrose additive. A 25 U/kg unfractionated heparin (Fresenius Kabi, Lake Zurich, IL) bolus was administered IV followed by a CRI of 25 U/kg/h titrated to effect for a target activated clotting time of 160-200 s. One hour into treatment, the cat was administered a CRI of dopamine (Pfizer, Lake Forest, IL) at 5-10 mcg/kg/min given refractory hypotension (MAP 58 mmHg; norepinephrine CRI at 0.5 mcg/kg/min). The MAP improved to 74 mmHg. However, the dopamine CRI was discontinued after 35 minutes due to multiform ventricular premature contractions and self-resolving ventricular tachycardia. The MAP improved to 106 mmHg after discontinuing the dopamine CRI but the cat remained on a norepinephrine CRI throughout the hemodialysis treatment. Bloodwork 1 h into treatment showed a high normal potassium concentration (5.1 mmol/L), improved azotemia (BUN 124 mg/dL, creatinine 8.8 mg/dL), and persistent ionized hypercalcemia (>2.5 mmol/L). The cat remained comatose and intubated with no gag reflex. No urine production had been noted over 6 h. As no calcium-free dialysate solution was readily available, the BK0/3.5 dialysate was switched to Plasma-Lyte (potassium concentration, 5 mmol/L; iCa concentration, 0 mmol/L) containing an acetate and gluconate buffer and 110 mg/dL of dextrose additive. There was no change in cardiovascular status after this adjustment. Approximately 4 h into treatment, the ionized calcium normalized (1.1 mmol/L), the potassium concentration was stable (5.2 mmol/L), and azotemia improved (BUN 74 mg/dL, creatinine 5.52 mg/dL; 40% urea reduction ratio). The cat’s mentation improved from comatose to stuporous, a gag reflex returned, and the cat was extubated. A small volume of urine was noted to be produced within the indwelling urinary catheter line. The dialysate was subsequently switched to BGK4/2.5 (potassium concentration, 4 mmol/L; iCa concentration, 2.5 mmol/L; Baxter Healthcare Corporation, Deerfield, IL). After 6 h of total treatment, the BUN was 52 mg/dL and creatinine was 5.3 mg/dL, consistent with a 58% urea reduction ratio; iCa and potassium concentrations remained normal (1.18 and 4.8 mmol/L, respectively) and the treatment was discontinued. The cat was administered a norepinephrine CRI of 0.5 mcg/kg/h, 1 mg/kg of maropitant (Zoetis, Parsippany-Troy Hills, NJ) IV q24h, 1 mg/kg of pantoprazole (Eugia US LLC, East Windsor, NJ) IV q12h, 0.3 mg/kg of ondansetron (Fosun Pharma USA, Princeton, NJ) IV q8h, and 3 mL/kg/h of polyionic fluids. Enteral nutrition was started at 25% of the resting energy requirement per day. The MAP upon transfer to the intensive care unit was 73 mmHg. The cat remained obtunded for the first 24 h after treatment.

On day 2, the azotemia was mildly progressive (BUN 81 mg/dL and creatinine 5.96 mg/dL), iCa and potassium remained normal (1.31 and 3.9 mmol/L, respectively), and the cat became polyuric with a urine output of 3-8 mL/kg/h for 72 h. The cat’s kidney values normalized on day 4 of hospitalization (BUN 15 mg/dL, creatinine 1.01 mg/dL). The iCa remained normal and the potassium was low-normal (1.28 and 3.5 mmol/L, respectively). On day 6, the urinary and hemodialysis catheters were removed and the cat urinated. The cat was discharged on day 6 with 0.015 mg/kg of buprenorphine PO q8h, transdermal mirtazapine (Dechra Veterinary Products, Overland Park, KS) q24h, 15 mg/kg of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (Zoetis, Parsippany-Troy Hills, NJ) PO q12h, and 7.5 mg/kg of pradofloxacin (Elanco Animal Health, Indianapolis, IN) PO q24h for 7 days. On day 8, the esophagostomy tube was removed.

On day 17, the cat was presented for coughing and gagging 6-7 times per day and vomiting every other day since discharge. Chest radiographs showed a small volume of gas within the esophagus but were otherwise unremarkable. A respiratory PCR panel (Antech Diagnostics, Fountain Valley, CA) was submitted and the cat was treated with a 10-day course of doxycycline (10 mg/kg PO q24h; Hikma Pharmaceuticals, Berkeley Heights, NJ) and a tapering course of prednisolone (1 mg/kg, PO, q24h for 3 days, then 0.5 mg/kg, PO, q24h for 3 days, then 0.5 mg/kg, PO every other day for 3 doses; Stokes Pharmacy, Mt Laurel, NJ). Prednisolone was prescribed to empirically treat airway inflammation and doxycycline was chosen to provide broad-spectrum coverage for common bacterial respiratory pathogens. The respiratory PCR panel was negative.

On day 49, the cat was presented for ongoing coughing and gagging. The coughing had decreased to 2-3 times per day with an unchanged frequency of vomiting. Chest radiographs showed linear mineral opacities summating with the stomach (Figure 1). The cat underwent bronchoscopy and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy which showed diffuse white, plaque-like lesions along the tracheal rings (Figure 2) and gastric mucosa (Figure 3). Biopsies of the stomach showed moderate to marked multifocal to coalescing mucosal and submucosal mineralization. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid cytology showed low-grade neutrophilic inflammation and an aerobic culture was negative. Bloodwork showed a mild azotemia (BUN 21 mg/dL, creatinine 1.7 mg/dL) with normal potassium and total calcium concentrations (4.4 mmol/L and 9.3 mg/dL, respectively). An ionized calcium level was not checked at this visit and no treatments were prescribed. Four weeks later, the owner reported resolution of the coughing and gagging.

Approximately 1 year later, repeat thoracic and abdominal radiographs showed persistent mineralization of the tracheal rings and gastric mucosa (Figure 4). Bloodwork showed a high normal BUN and creatinine (35 and 1.3 mg/dL, respectively) with adequately concentrated urine (urine specific gravity of 1.064). The potassium, symmetric dimethylarginine, and total calcium were within normal limits (4.3 mmol/L; 12.6 μg/dL, RI: < 15 μg/dL; and 9.2 mg/dL, respectively).

Discussion

This case report describes the successful treatment of iatrogenic hypercalcemia and acute kidney injury (AKI) in a cat using hemodialysis. Despite hemodynamic instability, neurological deterioration, and renal consequences, early extracorporeal clearance of calcium allowed for complete cardiovascular and central nervous system recovery and the prevention of clinically detectable kidney injury.

Considerations to determine whether an intoxicant is amenable to extracorporeal clearance via hemodialysis include the molecular weight (MW), protein binding, volume of distribution, and rate of endogenous clearance. Ideal characteristics include a low MW (< 500 Daltons [D]), low protein binding (< 80%), and a low volume of distribution (<1.5 L/kg).4 Electrolytes are amenable to removal by hemodialysis due to their low MW and existence in a non-protein bound fraction. However, since extracellular calcium consists of 55% ionized (MW 40.08 D), 10% complexed (MW 162.11-498.43 D), and 35% protein-bound (MW > 9000 D) forms, only non-protein-bound calcium can be cleared by the dialyzer due to MW limitations.5,6 Compared to a traditional intoxicant, the volume of distribution of calcium is not well-defined due to its unique total body distribution. Approximately 99% of calcium is stored in the skeleton as hydroxyapatite and is not readily exchangeable in the body, whereas non-skeletal stores exist in both the extracellular space and the intracellular fluid compartment, which limits total body clearance.7 Finally, it is important to consider that the endogenous renal clearance of calcium is expected to be high after discontinuation of the iatrogenic infusion. However, concurrent AKI reduces endogenous calcium clearance, increasing the potential indication for exogenous clearance with hemodialysis.

While hemodialysis is effective at removing both ionized and complexed calcium from the blood, there is no evidence that intervention with extracorporeal therapies is superior to medical management alone. In addition to removing the underlying cause of hypercalcemia, initial treatment considerations should include fluid therapy (0.9% NaCl), and administration of furosemide, and calcitonin, with secondary consideration being administration of glucocorticoids and bisphosphonates. Hemodialysis should only be considered as a potential tertiary intervention in the management of iatrogenic hypercalcemia, particularly in cases with evidence of concurrent AKI where calcium elimination is expected to be impaired.7 Medical management was not attempted after calcium discontinuation in this case; therefore, it remains unknown whether calcium concentrations might have decreased without hemodialysis, or whether the cat’s AKI might have improved with time alone.

While literature exists outlining the clinical sequelae of hypercalcemia in animals,8–10 this report documents an iatrogenic calcium overdose, as well as the long-term consequences of metastatic calcification. In people, a case report has documented iatrogenic hypercalcemia in a woman treated with calcium chloride for a calcium channel blocker overdose.11 The woman developed an anuric AKI and underwent continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), but died in the hospital. Her highest total calcium concentration was 31.2 mg/dL, compared to 57 mg/dL in this veterinary case report.

Comparatively, a vitamin D overdose causes persistent metastatic calcification in dogs.9 Calcification of the gingival mucosa, skin, and rugal folds was documented on abdominal radiography, with gastric mineralization persisting on radiographs 2 months after presentation. Metastatic calcification occurs in normocalcemic cats with chronic kidney disease undergoing renal transplantation, where preoperative metastatic calcification was associated with shorter survival times and a greater risk of death.8

After resolution of the hyperkalemia, the dialysate was switched to Plasma-Lyte, as no calcium-free dialysate with a bicarbonate buffer was readily available. Plasma-Lyte is used as a dialysate in human CRRT without any reported adverse effects.12 However, its use is no longer recommended, as the acetate buffer has the potential to result in arrhythmias and cardiac instability.13 Although the cat in our study had no change in cardiovascular stability, a commercial or custom-made calcium-free dialysate solution with a bicarbonate buffer should be preferentially used when available.

Conclusion

Hypercalcemia can cause life-threatening clinical sequelae. Emergency hemodialysis could be considered for animals with AKI and electrolyte derangements, such as hypercalcemia, as calcium normalization and renal recovery are possible. However, animals should be monitored for future complications such as metastatic calcification.
