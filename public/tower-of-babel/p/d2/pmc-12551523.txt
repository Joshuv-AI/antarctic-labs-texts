Mapping of current resources and models of care for paediatric cancer survivors in Asia: a multinational survey
Authors: Winnie Wan Yee Tso, Melissa M Hudson, Ramandeep Singh Arora, Ronnie E Baticulon, Jiaoyang Cai, Bow-wen Chen, Rashmi Dalvi, Sanjeeva Gunasekera, Hiroki Hori, Muhammad Saghir Khan, Joo-Young Kim, Shawn Hsien Ren Lee, Lok Kan Leung, Mora Mel, Shuichi Ozono, Venkatraman Radhakrishnan, Sudhir Sapkota, Chi Kong Li, Anthony Pak-yin Liu, Yin Ting Cheung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPaediatric cancer survivorship is an emerging priority in the current global health agenda. The models of long-term follow-up (LTFU) in Asia may vary widely based on differences in healthcare systems and resources. This study aims to characterise the models of care and current resources available for paediatric cancer survivors in Asian countries.MethodsThis multinational, cross-sectional study conducted from February to May 2024 recruited clinicians practising in an institution/centre in the United Nations geoscheme, using a combination of purposive and snowball sampling. They were identified through the 18th St Jude-Viva Forum in Singapore and local professional societies. Each invited institution nominated a representative to complete a structured questionnaire focusing on the institution’s characteristics of LTFU care, the availability of risk-based screening tests according to the Children’s Oncology Long-term Follow-up Guideline and the availability of recommended services in an LTFU programme.ResultsThe survey included participants representing 87 paediatric oncology units/institutions from 28 Asian countries/regions (response rate 68%). Out of the 87 units/institutions, the majority of institutions provided LTFU care through specialised LTFU clinics (44%) or oncologist-led clinics (45%). Significantly more institutions with specialised LTFU clinics offered assessments by multidisciplinary professionals, for example, motor (74% vs 44%, p=0.008) and speech/language (66% vs 36%, p=0.008) assessments, than those adopting other models of care. Institutions with LTFU clinics were more likely to provide health risk counselling (p=0.045), fertility preservation (p=0.018), pain clinics (p=0.008) and nutrition programmes (p=0.018). Institutions adhering to a specific LTFU guideline were more likely to provide comprehensive visual assessment (p=0.012) and health risk counselling (p<0.001). There were no differences between the economies in providing basic late-effects screening tests.ConclusionOur findings demonstrated that establishing an LTFU clinic according to evidence-based guidelines can facilitate more comprehensive late-effects screening and support for survivors. Regional collaborations should aim to develop resources-stratified recommendations and policies for coordinated and integrated LTFU care in Asian countries.Keywords: Child health, Cancer

Introduction

Paediatric cancer is an emerging priority in the current global child health agenda. In 2020, the International Agency for Research on Cancer reported that the paediatric cancer burden in Asia accounted for 51.2% of all cases worldwide, which is the largest proportion of the global childhood cancer burden.1 Encouragingly, the development of modern treatment strategies has led to tremendous improvements in the 5-year survival and cure rates of paediatric cancers in Asia, particularly in high-income and upper-middle-income countries where survival rates mostly exceed 80% comparable to those in developed countries in other continents.2 3 However, the survival rates of common paediatric cancers vary from 10% to 60% in low-income countries and middle-income countries in Asia.2

Although further research on paediatric cancer treatment in Asia is unquestionably necessary, current efforts should also target the emerging population of survivors. Survivorship comes at the cost of developing treatment-related late effects and psychosocial challenges that can adversely affect the health-related quality of life of survivors. Studies have shown that paediatric cancer survivors who had early access to structured long-term follow-up (LTFU) care programmes were more aware of the late effects and visited emergency departments less frequently than those who did not have access to such programmes.4 Therefore, international paediatric oncology groups have recommended that a structured LTFU programme should include late effects screening, educational initiatives for promoting protective health behaviours, specialists’ referral networks and psychosocial services for survivors and caregivers.5 6 The CureAll Framework developed by the WHO Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer highlights several core actions in the area of survivorship, which include developing and implementing evidence-based guidelines and establishing adequate multidisciplinary infrastructure. However, the feasibility and actual implementation of such recommendations across global and regional settings have not been adequately evaluated.

The models of survivorship care vary widely based on differences in culture and healthcare systems and resources. To date, most studies describing paediatric LTFU models and practices have been conducted in North America, Europe and Oceania,7,9 while there is a paucity of related studies conducted in Asia. A recent literature review focusing on models and barriers to survivorship care in Asian countries identified the oncology specialist care model (ie, survivorship care provided primarily by an oncology provider) as the most prevalent model, followed by dedicated LTFU programmes that offer services including risk-based surveillance for late effects and psychosocial support.10 However, characterisation of models of survivorship care across Asia was not possible as the systematic review did not identify data for many Asian countries, especially lower income countries. The differences in healthcare systems, dedicated infrastructure and resources, as well as cultural and social issues, may lead to significant differences in LTFU care across Asia.

This study aimed to characterise models of survivorship care and current resources available for LTFU care for paediatric cancer survivors in Asian countries. It is anticipated that the findings of this survey will help to increase professional awareness of survivorship challenges across the multicultural and diverse Asian continent and solicit collaborative efforts to help countries/cities work towards establishing their LTFU services among Asian countries.

Methods

Study design

This multinational, cross-sectional survey study was conducted from February to May 2024. The American Association for Public Opinion Research Survey Disclosure 12-item Checklist was adopted to report the survey findings.11

Study participants

Study participants were identified using a combination of purposive and snowball sampling. To be eligible for this survey, participants had to first identify themselves as: (1) older than 18 years; (2) able to understand written English; (3) a clinician providing direct clinical care to children with cancer; and (4) practising in an institution or centre in the United Nations geoscheme for Asia.12 Each participating institution was invited to nominate a representative to complete the questionnaire. These representatives should preferably be clinicians or any medical care providers who lead or are directly involved in LTFU services at their respective institutions. If an institution did not have established LTFU services, they were asked to nominate clinicians who currently held leadership positions within their institutions.

The sample size was determined based on feasibility, with the goal of reaching at least 80 institutions to enable a meaningful scoping analysis of the current landscape for paediatric oncology survivorship in Asia. It was decided a priori to ensure an approximately similar distribution in each predefined group. The predefined groups were based on country-level income (high-income economies (HICs), upper-middle-income economies (UMICs) and lower-middle-income economies (LMICs), as defined by the World Bank) and geographical regions according to the United Nations geoscheme for Asia (East Asia, South-east Asia, South Asia and West Asia).

The first wave of survey dissemination took place at the 18th St Jude-VIVA Forum—VIVA Asia Learning Exchange (1 March 2024) in Singapore.13 The list of participants and their practising institutions was reviewed, and subsequently, the second wave of recruitment specifically targeted at the under-represented groups (such as institutions from LMICs and institutions from South Asia and West Asia). Potential participants were recruited through the local and regional professional networks of the investigators. Survey instructions were sent to one individual per institution. Investigators were asked to track the number of the surveys distributed, and to send a reminder approximately 2 weeks after the initial invitation.

Survey development

The survey instrument was developed by a core team of investigators, which included paediatric oncologists representing several Asian countries and Asian paediatric oncology networks, as well as a cancer survivorship specialist and a survey methodologist. The survey content and format were based on prior surveys regarding models of care and resources for LTFU care for paediatric cancer survivors.7,914 These prior surveys were designed and tested previously to characterise the models of survivorship care and support in North America, Europe, Oceania and mainland China. To ensure that the items and wordings were relevant to the Asian context, the first draft of the survey was piloted on five oncologists from four Asian countries to refine the questions and administrative procedures. They were asked to comment on the face validity and relevance of each item on the survey. Subsequently, the feedback collected from the pilot phase was used to develop the final version of the survey, which included five sections:

Sections 1 and 2 solicited information on the characteristics of the key respondent and his/her institution, respectively.

Section 3 included items on the availability and description of the characteristics of LTFU care for survivors in the respondent’s institution. To ensure consistency in the conceptual understanding of LTFU, the introductory paragraph included a common definition of LTFU care, that is, ‘care that facilitates strategies for prevention, early detection and timely treatment of late effects, education of the survivor about their health and treatment history and risks, and empowerment in adopting a healthy lifestyle’.15,17 Consequently, a ‘specialized long-term follow-up clinic or service’ was defined as ‘a specialty clinic/service designed to provide ongoing screening and monitoring of late effects for survivors after cessation of active treatment of childhood cancer. It is a separate entity from acute care services’.15 The respondents were asked whether a follow-up programme/clinic was available for paediatric cancer survivors in their practising institutions, and if it was, to describe the characteristics of the LTFU clinic (frequency and location of programme/clinic, and staff and survivors involved).

Section 4 included items on the availability of risk-based basic screening tests for each organ system-based late effect according to the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) Long-term Follow-up Guidelines for Survivors of Childhood, Adolescent and Young Adult Cancers version 6.18 The respondents indicated whether the screening test was ‘readily available’ (defined as available onsite or by referral within a defined time and at nominal cost), ‘somewhat available’ (defined as available but with a long waiting time, and/or travelling time is required, and/or is expensive but still affordable) or ‘not available’ (not accessible onsite or by referral and/or is cost-prohibitive).

Section 5 asked about the availability of recommended services or interventions in an LTFU programme (eg, health risk counselling, fertility counselling, fertility preservation and psychiatric interventions).

Data processing

The participants’ responses were recorded anonymously and retrieved electronically using the online survey software Qualtrics (SAP). The IP addresses were reviewed to confirm that no participant submitted multiple entries. Only questionnaires with completed responses for all sections were analysed. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise responses to each question.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of the responding institutions, including the basic demographics, LTFU models of care and availability of resources (ie, screening tests and services/interventions), were compared based on the income level of the countries/regions. The classification was based on data from the World Bank in the 2025 fiscal year.19 In this analysis, countries/regions were grouped as HICs, UMICs and LMICs, respectively. Our study did not identify any participants from countries/regions with ‘low-income economies’.

To identify any differences in the resources and services provided through the various models of care, we compared the availability of screening tests and services/interventions between institutions that reported having an existing specialised LTFU clinic versus institutions that did not have such a clinic. The analysis was also repeated to compare the availability of resources between institutions that reported adhering to a guideline (ie, an international practice guideline or local/institutional guideline) versus institutions that did not. Continuous variables were compared using independent sample t-tests or non-parametric tests depending on the results of Levene’s test for equality of variances. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or the χ2 test. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.28.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Characteristics of the respondents and participating institutions

During the first wave of recruitment, 32 out of 35 participants of the session at the VIVA Asia Learning Exchange (response rate=91%) completed the survey. Subsequently, another 93 surveys were distributed through the networks of the investigators, of whom 58 responses were received (response rate=62%). After excluding three responses (one from a respondent not based in an Asian country/region and two incomplete responses), data provided by 87 respondents from institutions in 28 Asian countries/regions were analysed (overall response rate=68%; figure 1).

Countries/regions of the responding institutions (n=87). The classification of income level was based on data from the World Bank in the 2025 fiscal year.

Online supplemental table 1 summarises the demographics of the respondents and the characteristics of the participating institutions. Overall, 24 (28%) institutions were from HICs, 26 (30%) from UMICs and 37 (43%) were from LMICs. The majority of the institutions were located in urban areas (n=70, 80%). Institutions from LMICs had significantly more cases per year, with an average of 390 new cases per year compared with 194 new cases per year from LMICs and 57 cases per year from HICs (p<0.001). Most of the respondents were paediatric oncologists (n=78, 90%).

For the 83 institutions that reported providing LTFU care, 38 (46%) institutions delivered LTFU care in the form of a specialised LTFU clinic (table 1). Marginal differences (p=0.051) were found in the proportion of institutions with specialised LTFU clinics among HICs (57%), UMICs (25%) and LMICs (53%). As for institutions that provided LTFU care but did not have a specialised LTFU clinic (n=45), most respondents reported that the LTFU services were provided through the treating oncologist or oncology team (ie, the oncology specialist care model; n=39, 87%), while in the remaining few institutions, non-oncology specialised clinics (n=2, 4%) or primary care practitioners (n=4, 9%) provided the LTFU care for survivors.

In terms of guidance of LTFU survivorship screening, 40% (n=33) reported adhering to an established survivorship guideline, such as guidelines from the COG or the International Late Effects of Childhood Cancer Guideline Harmonisation Group (IGHG), to determine the type and frequency of survivorship-focused late-effects screening practices, while 17% reported using their local institutional guideline (n=14) (table 1). The majority of institutions from HICs and UMICs reported using existing guidelines (either established survivorship or local institutional guidelines) for LTFU care (HICs: n=16, 70%; UMICs: n=14, 58%). Approximately 40% (n=35, 43%) of the institutions reported not using any specific guideline; half of these institutions were from LMICs and reported that their LTFU practices were determined solely at the clinician’s discretion (n=18, 51%).

For financial support, significantly more institutions from HICs and UMICs than from LMICs received financial support from the government for LTFU services (83% vs 79% vs 39%, p=0.003). The other sources of financial support were charity funding (18%) and private insurance (19%).

In terms of the team composition (figure 2), almost all the institutions providing LTFU care involved a paediatric oncologist (n=79, 95%). The involvement of other healthcare providers varied. Significantly more institutions from LMICs than from HICs and UMICs involved nutritionists (44% vs 17% vs 21%, p=0.044), primary care practitioners (22% vs 4% vs 4%, p=0.045) or trainees (42% vs 13% vs 21%, p=0.038) in providing LTFU services.

Characteristics of specialised long-term follow-up clinics

Among the 38 institutions reported to have specialised LTFU clinics, 17 (45%) of the LTFU clinics had been established for over 5 years (table 1). There was descriptively more LTFU clinics in LMICs (53%) that had been established only within the past 2 years compared with HICs (23%) and UMICs (17%), although the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.078).

In terms of the entry criteria, 42% of the institutions reported admitting survivors to the LTFU clinics when less than 2 years had passed since the completion of their treatment. Almost two-thirds of the participating institutions with LTFU clinics (n=25, 66%) offered LTFU for survivors older than 18 years. There were significantly more institutions from LMICs that limited services to paediatric survivors (aged <18 years) only, as compared with HICs (42% vs 8%, p=0.025).

Comparison of resource availability in HICs, UMICs and LMICs

Overall, nearly all institutions had basic blood tests for screening secondary cancers (100%) and late effects affecting the hepatic (100%), renal (98%), endocrine (74%–98%), and cardiovascular (92%) systems (online supplemental table 2). However, less than half of the institutions provided screening for psychosocial problems, with only 45% of the institutions providing healthcare and insurance access and 30% providing educational and/or vocational assessments. More institutions from LMICs (69%) provided assessment for mental disorders compared with 48% in HICs and 35% in UMICs (p=0.027).

For survivorship services, overall, 79% of the institutions had vaccination programmes available as part of their LTFU service (online supplemental table 3). Health risk counselling was available in 56% of the institutions, and less than half of the institutions provided multidisciplinary rehabilitation programmes (43%). Only a minority of the institutions provided a cognitive rehabilitation programme (31%) and vocational rehabilitation services (17%) for cancer survivors approaching adulthood. Support for fertility (25%) and reproductive problems requiring assisted reproduction (27%) was also limited. The support for survivors requiring assisted reproduction (12% vs 38% vs 39%, p=0.030) and hearing impairment (24% vs 43% vs 65%, p=0.009) in institutions from LICs was significantly less than that in institutions from UMICs and HICs.

Comparison of resource availability in institutions with and without specialised long-term follow-up clinics

For screening tests, significantly more institutions with specialised LTFU clinics offered multidisciplinary assessments by allied health professionals (ie, motor skills and hand function assessment, 74% vs 44%, p=0.008; speech and language assessment, 66% vs 36%, p=0.008) than institutions using the oncology specialist care model, non-oncology specialist model or primary practitioner model (table 2A).

Discussion

This study is the first to provide a scoping overview of the availability and landscape of LTFU care for paediatric cancer survivors across 28 Asian countries/regions. The models of LTFU care delivery varied across the countries/regions, with less than half of the participating institutions reporting having access to a specialised LTFU clinic. Institutions with a dedicated LTFU programme had more comprehensive services/interventions addressing the psychosocial and developmental needs of survivors than institutions that adopted other models of care. We did not identify significant differences in the basic late-effects screening tests between LMICs, UMICs and HICs. Overall, our findings identified the need for several high-priority capacity-building initiatives within the Asian paediatric oncology community: (1) boosting the visibility of paediatric cancer survivorship in the policy agenda both locally and regionally, (2) developing and implementing research-stratified guidelines in LMICs, (3) fostering partnerships in establishing a multidisciplinary infrastructure in LTFU care and (4) pooling resources to improve the accessibility to and equity in health education for survivors.

Identifying appropriate and sustainable LTFU models of care delivery for different healthcare systems remains a challenge, particularly in Asia where some countries/regions exhibit vast disparity in income, patient advocacy and resources even within their local population. Almost two thirds of the institutions providing LTFU services in HICs followed the oncology specialist care model. Survivorship studies in adult patients with cancer have reported that shared-care models and specialised multidisciplinary clinics may have more economic benefits and use fewer healthcare resources than oncology specialist-led care.20 21 We also found that institutions with an LTFU clinic endorsed providing more comprehensive screening and multidisciplinary/interdisciplinary rehabilitation services than institutions adopting other delivery models. Our survey did not evaluate specific barriers to the delivery of LTFU care, but other studies have observed that the lack of communication between oncology and non-oncology practitioners, referral systems and a transition infrastructure are common barriers to adopting a shared-care model in most HICs in both Asian and global settings.10 22 23 From an implementation science perspective, healthcare systems may benefit from the inclusion of survivorship and rehabilitation services in national cancer control plans and policies.24 25 However, a recent report noted that among Asian countries, only China, Japan, Malaysia and South Korea had an existing national-level policy for coordinated and integrated cancer survivorship care,26 27 and these policies are heavily focused on survivors of adult-onset rather than paediatric cancers. Our findings underscore the priority need to ensure the visibility and recognition of paediatric cancer survivorship and rehabilitation in the policy agenda across Asia. Extending the successful efforts by regional networks in Europe, Australia and North America, future work should leverage existing networks, such as the International Society of Paediatric Oncology (SIOP)–Asia, the Asian Paediatric Haematology and Oncology Group, St Jude VIVA and various local paediatric oncology networks, to develop partnerships across different professional groups, patient advocates and policymakers.

Half of the institutions from LMICs reported having a dedicated LTFU clinic, many of which had established services within the past 2 years. This is likely due to the heightened awareness of the importance of survivorship care for paediatric patients in recent years.28 29 In many LMICs where long-term survival rates of childhood cancer are still low, much of the resources are allocated to cancer diagnosis and treatment rather than LTFU care. The patient load in LMICs is significantly higher than that in UMICs and HICs, yet 42% of the institutions in LMICs stopped providing LTFU service to survivors when they turned 18 years old. Without a multidisciplinary transition care model, these survivors may not have access to quality survivorship care once they reach adulthood. The improving survival rates due to advances in treatment protocols and radiotherapy also prompted the need for more coordinated survivorship care for childhood cancer survivors at risk of long-term sequelae, particularly brain tumour survivors and recipients of haematopoietic stem cell transplantation.30 31 A more comprehensive analysis of tumour streams and cancer types of survivors treated at the LTFU clinics is essential to map the resources and referral networks required to meet the diverse needs of survivors. Despite these challenges, the nascent state of the paediatric cancer survivorship landscape in LMICs provides an opportunity to explore innovative and cost-effective models of care. For example, our study found that institutions from LMICs also involved trainees/residents and primary care physicians more frequently, which suggests that survivorship care represents an evolving subspecialty in LMICs. There are potential opportunities for regional collaboration, such as developing accredited programmes for interdisciplinary training in cancer survivorship and partnering with larger centres with mature LTFU clinics for staff development and sharing of resources.

Over half of the institutions from LMICs and approximately 30–40% of the institutions from UMICs/HICs reported not adopting any guidelines for LTFU care for paediatric cancer survivors. As a result, there were variations in the availability of screening tests and interventions for survivors. We found that institutions that had adopted a guideline provided more comprehensive screening for visual, hormonal and neurocognitive problems, as well as health risk counselling and nutrition programmes. This is encouraging because studies have shown that delivering supportive care consistent with clinical practice guidelines can potentially improve outcomes in children with cancer. However, we also observed a disparity in the actual accessibility of multidisciplinary services in HICs versus LMICs. This may reflect implementation barriers and the lack of resources for providing such services. Many international organisations are now advocating the development of resource-stratified guidelines to enable the delivery of the best possible care in resource-limited settings by efficiently allocating resources.32 33 Yet, most of the resource-stratified guidelines in paediatric oncology are focused on cancer prevention, diagnosis and treatment and not on supportive and survivorship care. One of the ongoing initiatives aimed at addressing this critical gap is the Adapted Resource and Implementation Application Guide,34 a collaboration among St Jude Global, the SIOP and several leading global organisations to create resource-stratified, evidence-based guidelines for the global paediatric oncology community. Specific to Asia, the regional network should conduct in-depth needs assessments of each country/region and develop a core set of standards for comprehensive LTFU care in various cancer and resource settings in Asia.

The majority of institutions from Asian countries/regions had basic screening tests for late effects affecting major organ systems, as well as screening for secondary cancers. However, despite several guidelines emphasising the importance of screening for psychosocial problems in paediatric cancer survivors,35 36 less than half of the institutions had screening tests for mental health and psychosocial problems, regardless of the income level of the countries/regions. Notably, only 5 of 76 studies cited in the recent IGHG recommendations for the surveillance of mental health problems were conducted in Asia (Hong Kong and South Korea).36 The scarcity of published studies and the lack of Asian representation indicate that mental health and psychosocial problems are an under-addressed topic in paediatric cancer survivorship in Asia.

The provision of health risk counselling is an important component of LTFU care,5 6 yet only half of the participating institutions reported providing such a service, and surprisingly, not much difference was observed in the provision of health risk counselling among HICs, UMICs and LMICs. A major challenge is the development of patient-friendly educational resources in the different major languages spoken in Asia. Future work should identify enablers of this endeavour while aiming to reduce duplication of efforts. This can be done by modelling successful infrastructure in other regions, such as the Passport for Care37 (the USA) and PanCareSurPass38 (Europe), which are clinical decision support systems that generate personalised survivorship care plans and healthcare information for paediatric cancer survivors. The ultimate goal is to develop a one-stop, shared portal of readily accessible patient education materials in different Asian languages that can be disseminated without a large investment of resources in all cancer settings, particularly in LMICs.

While this study provides an unprecedented view of the state of the paediatric cancer survivorship landscape in Asia, there are several limitations to consider. Despite the multinational sampling frame and attempts to reach under-represented countries/regions using purposive sampling, respondents from certain geographical regions were difficult to recruit, most likely due to language barriers as it is methodologically difficult to conduct translation processes for the multiple languages represented in Asia. However, the majority of the participants understood English as they were recruited through St Jude-VIVA programmes (which are mostly conducted in English). We acknowledge that for larger countries within Asia, the responses from the participating institutions may not be reflective of the actual situation at a national level. As the potential participants were identified from local professional societies or networks during the second wave of recruitment, this may have introduced sampling bias as we expect practitioners from lower resource or rural settings to have poorer participation in such professional societies. Moreover, several geopolitical events might have affected the participation rate in some countries, for example, the South Korea doctors’ strike39 and ongoing armed conflicts in the Western Asian region. Lastly, even though we explicitly stated in the email invitation that institutions did not have to have an existing LTFU service to complete this survey, this sampling approach might still have introduced selection bias toward institutions providing such services. Since paediatric cancer survivorship is still in a nascent state in most parts of Asia, we anticipate that the provision of LTFU services is likely even less prevalent and less comprehensive than what is reported in this scoping survey. Future follow-up studies should engage local champions and stakeholders to facilitate the understanding of barriers and facilitators within local contexts.

Although definitions were provided in the survey instructions to ensure consistency in the participants’ understanding of ‘long-term follow-up care’, the perception and operational definition of LTFU programmes may vary between institutions. Notably, the retention of paediatric cancer survivors in LTFU programmes may be different across settings and countries due to socioeconomic and patient characteristics. Hence, the findings on differential care delivery among institutions should be interpreted cautiously. Lastly, we acknowledge concerns with type I errors as a wide range of analyses was conducted to evaluate differences in the accessibility and perceived importance of LTFU resources among the predefined groups without adjustment for multiple testing. Despite these limitations, this is the first study to provide insights into the survivorship landscape in Asia and our findings can inform future research agendas and shape capacity-building efforts towards making LTFU care equitable for all survivors in the region.

Conclusion

Paediatric cancer survivorship in Asia presents many challenges in this large geographical region with diverse needs, cultures and healthcare systems. Our study found that the provision of LTFU care was common among the participating institutions, thus reflecting the growing population of survivors in the region and the need to address their lifelong medical and psychosocial needs as an emerging agenda in Asia. The disparity in the provision of multidisciplinary services in LMICs versus HICs reinforces the need to develop and implement resource-stratified, locally adapted survivorship recommendations. For countries/regions with limited resources, our survey demonstrated that setting up an LTFU service with established guidelines can facilitate more comprehensive screening and support for paediatric cancer survivors. Nevertheless, in-depth needs assessment in each country/region is necessary to identify sustainable and cost-effective models of LTFU care delivery. This may help to identify centres of excellence and care networks within the regions and cultivate partnerships with centres with low-resource economies.
