Network analysis of neuropsychiatric symptoms in mild behavioral impairment among nursing homes: an observational study
Authors: Bingjie Wei, Yuanjiao Yan, Yanhong Shi, Rong Lin, Chenshan Huang, Xiaozhen Fu, Hong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMild behavioral impairment (MBI) is considered a high-risk factor for dementia, characterized by emergent and sustained neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS). This study examined the complex co-occurring symptoms and symptom clusters of NPS in older adults with MBI.MethodsUsing a cross-sectional study design, 328 older adults with MBI, defined as the later-life emergence of sustained neuropsychiatric symptoms lasting at least 6 months, were recruited in 42 nursing homes in China. Participants’ demographic characteristics were collected using a self-designed questionnaire. The Chinese version of the MBI Checklist (MBI-C) was used to assess the degree and severity of NPS. The collected data were used for network analysis to identify the core symptom and bridge symptoms in the MBI network by calculating the strength centrality and bridge centrality. Additionally, symptoms most strongly connected to bridge symptoms were examined to explore potential comorbid mechanisms.ResultsEighteen symptoms forming three clusters were included in this study. Network analysis revealed that the NPS “Not experiencing pleasure” had the highest centrality strength (rs=5.734), with the top three bridge strengths being “Not experiencing pleasure” (rBS=2.644), “Diminished and defeated” (rBS=2.484), and “More stubborn or rigid” (rBS=2.269). “Not experiencing pleasure” and “Diminished and defeated” were the bridge symptoms connecting decreased motivation and affective dysregulation clusters, while “More stubborn or rigid” connected affective dysregulation and impulse dyscontrol symptom clusters.ConclusionsDecreased motivation, affective dysregulation, and impulse dyscontrol in older adults with MBI are highly comorbid and mutually reinforcing. “Not experiencing pleasure” yielded a higher probability of additional NPS. Interventions targeting these symptoms may improve the efficacy of MBI management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06512-5.Keywords: Mild behavioral impairment, Network analysis, Neuropsychiatric, Nursing homes, Older adults

Introduction

With an aging population, dementia has become one of the greatest public health challenges worldwide, and the best care for dementia lies in early identification and intervention [1]. A study has shown that neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) occur in approximately 97% of dementia patients [2]. Common symptoms include apathy, agitation, aggression, anxiety, depression, and delusions, which exacerbate cognitive decline, erode quality of life, and increase mortality and burden on caregivers [3]. While NPS were previously thought to occur in the middle to late stages of dementia, recent studies have found that they can occur in the prodromal stages, even before cognitive impairment, significantly increasing an individual’s risk of developing dementia [4–6]. In order to improve the accuracy of NPS risk estimation, Mild Behavioral Impairment (MBI) was proposed [7].

Mild behavioral impairment (MBI) is a late-life syndrome characterized by significant psychiatric and behavioral abnormalities such as decreased motivation, affective dysregulation, impulse dyscontrol, social inappropriateness, and abnormal perception or thought content, in the absence of severe cognitive impairment [8]. MBI is now recognized as a prodromal symptom of dementia, which significantly increases the risk of transitioning from normal cognition or subjective cognitive decline to mild cognitive impairment, and eventually to various forms of dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease [9–11]. NPS rarely occur in isolation; rather, they interact and cluster together, with one symptom often exacerbating or maintaining others. Such interdependence not only complicates clinical management but may also accelerate disease progression and worsen prognosis in older adults. However, different symptoms have varying impacts on cognitive decline progression. For example, affective symptoms such as depression and anxiety can increase the risk of cognitive decline [12, 13], whereas apathy or impulse control disorders having a greater impact on cognitive decline [14–16]. Therefore, understanding which NPS exert the strongest influence on others is therefore crucial for identifying key intervention targets, as addressing these symptoms may indirectly alleviate broader symptom clusters and improve overall outcomes. Although NPS are highly prevalent among older adults in nursing homes, they are often not detected until dementia has already developed. Therefore, we selected nursing home residents as our study population to identify individuals with MBI, which represents an early stage of dementia. In China, unlike in many Western countries, admission to nursing homes is often driven by frailty, lack of family support, or functional dependence rather than advanced dementia. As a result, nursing home residents often include individuals at early stages of cognitive decline, providing a suitable cohort to examine the early manifestations of MBI. Moreover, the availability of standardized management facilitates more reliable assessment and data collection. Notably, Jiang et al. [17] suggest that early intervention with MBI may have a positive impact on alleviating long-term cognitive decline. Expert consensus [18] emphasizes that person-centered interventions for MBI populations can alleviate NPS symptoms. However, to date, no studies have identified which NPS have the greatest impact on others. Therefore, we used symptom network analysis to identify symptom clusters and bridge symptoms that may serve as potential targets for future interventions.

The symptom network is an emerging research paradigm that employs network analysis to represent symptoms as nodes and their associations as connecting lines. This approach clearly demonstrates the importance of individual symptoms and the complex relationships between them. Visualization methods are often used to understand the mutual reinforcement or causal relationships between symptoms [19]. The core symptoms of the symptom network are analyzed according to the centrality index. When a core symptom is present or exacerbated, it is more likely to affect other symptoms, thereby activating the entire network and producing a series of interrelated symptoms. This provides clinicians with an evaluation index in addition to symptom incidence and severity and helps healthcare professionals more accurately and effectively identify sentinel symptoms that can predict the development or exacerbation of other symptoms [20]. Simultaneously, the identification of bridge symptoms can help cut off the connection between symptom clusters and effectively prevent their connection symptoms, serving as the focus of clinical intervention, making it more accurate and efficient [20]. Nevertheless, the interdependence of NPS in MBI remains insufficiently understood. Most traditional analytic methods examine symptoms separately and overlook their mutual influences. This limits our ability to identify which symptoms may play a central or bridging role in driving overall symptom burden.

Previous studies have mainly described the prevalence and clinical correlates of MBI. However, little is known about how individual neuropsychiatric symptoms interact with and influence one another. To our knowledge, no study has systematically applied network analysis to explore these interdependencies among NPS in older adults with MBI, particularly in nursing home settings. This study aimed to use network analysis to identify the most central and influential symptoms within the MBI network. The findings may inform symptom management strategies in nursing homes, reduce the impact of MBI in older adults, and help lower the risk of dementia.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was an independent cross-sectional observational study conducted to investigate the health status of 5693 elderly people in 42 nursing homes in Fujian Province, China using a multistage stratified cluster sampling method. All data were collected specifically for this study and it was not a secondary analysis of an existing dataset. The study design and procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Fujian Medical University Ethics Committee (2023 − 177). Exemption from registration due to non-clinical trials.

Participants were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) met the diagnostic framework of the International Society for Alzheimer’s Disease Research and Treatment (ISTAART) for mild behavioral impairment (MBI) [21], as assessed with the Chinese version of the MBI Checklist (MBI-C), which was completed either by the participants themselves or by a reliable informant who had known them for at least 6 months; (3) had sufficient cognitive ability to understand and respond to questionnaire items directly, or, in cases of mild cognitive impairment, had an informant available to provide reliable responses. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe language or communication difficulties that prevented completion of the assessment by either the participant or the informant; (2) unconsciousness or severe co-occurring psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or severe major depression) that could confound the assessment of MBI symptoms; (3) serious physical illness in an acute or unstable state (e.g., advanced cancer or severe heart failure) that prevented participation in the study. The participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. According to the sample size estimation method of network analysis [22], the presence of P nodes in a network requires at least P (P-1)/2 samples to ensure statistical efficacy. In this study, P represented 18 symptoms; therefore, at least 153 samples were required.

Data collection

From June 1 to October 31, 2023, with the assistance and cooperation of governmental authorities, nursing homes in nine cities in Fujian Province were selected. A total of 42 nursing homes were included in the study. Facilities were selected using a multi-stage cluster sampling approach. First, nursing homes were stratified by administrative region, covering all nine prefecture-level cities in Fujian Province. Within each city, 2–3 institutions were randomly selected from both urban and rural areas. This procedure ensured that the sample was geographically representative and included a balance of urban and rural nursing homes, thereby reducing the likelihood of selection bias. The study was conducted in nursing homes that were ranked 4–5 stars according to the national standards for elder care facilities in China. The star rating is assessed by independent third-party agencies and officially published by the provincial Department of Civil Affairs. It reflects high standards of infrastructure, staffing, medical and nursing services, and overall quality of care, providing a relatively standardized and reliable environment for data collection. The survey was conducted using questionnaires administered by uniformly trained investigators. Standardized instructions were provided to participants to explain the purpose and significance of the study and obtain their informed consent. The investigators completed the questionnaires on behalf of the participants by asking each question sentence by sentence. After completion, the questionnaire was checked for omissions, errors, and multiple selections. In this study, 1118 paper questionnaires were distributed, and 1106 valid questionnaires were returned, yielding an effective recovery rate of 98.9%.

Assessment of demographic characteristics

Demographic and disease-related information were collected using a self-designed questionnaire. These included age, sex, education (elementary education defined as primary school; secondary education defined as junior high school, high school, or technical secondary school; higher education defined as college or higher), marital status (defined as married [having a spouse], unmarried, widowed or divorced), previous residence, living status (living alone or living with roommates), occupation was classified into two categories: mental work (e.g., teaching, administrative, or office-based jobs) and manual work (e.g., farming, construction, or factory work), body mass index (BMI), hearing, vision acuity, smoking (defined as at least one cigarette per day for 6 months or longer, divided into never, quit, or currently smoking), drinking (defined as at least once a week, with a minimum alcohol consumption of 50 mL for 6 months or longer, and divided into never, quit, or currently drinking) and comorbid medical conditions (e.g., cerebral infarction, cerebral hemorrhage, diabetes, hypertension, and coronary heart disease). The initial version was reviewed by a panel of nursing, geriatric, and neuropsychology experts with more than a decade of practical experience in their respective fields to ensure the appropriateness and relevance of the questionnaire.

Assessment of symptoms of mild behavioral impairment

Following the publication of the ISTAART diagnostic criteria for MBI, the MBI-C was developed in 2016 to assess the symptoms and severity of MBI. The Chinese version of the MBI-C was used in this study [23] (see Additional file 1). The MBI-C contains 34 items answered with “yes” or “no” (“yes” if the patient has neuropsychiatric symptoms for at least 6 months). Symptom severity is rated on a scale from 1 to 3: “1” for mild (detectable but not significant changes), “2” for moderate (significant but not dramatic changes), and “3” for severe (very obvious or dramatic changes), with a total score of 102. Higher scores indicate more severe symptoms. The MBI-C is divided into five symptom clusters [24]: decreased motivation (apathy), affective dysregulation (mood/anxiety symptoms), impulse dyscontrol (agitation and impulsivity), social inappropriateness (impaired social cognition), and abnormal perception and thought content (psychotic symptoms). The Chinese version of the MBI-C has demonstrated good reliability and validity in previous studies, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients reported as 0.895 [23]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.829.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and R 4.3.3 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Quartile quantiles were used to represent variables that were not normally distributed. The MBI-C includes 34 items across five domains. In this study, symptoms with a prevalence below 5% were excluded from the network analysis to improve the stability and accuracy of the model [20]. After this step, 18 symptoms met the criteria and were retained for analysis. In addition, to control for the presence of latent variables in the network model, we assessed the redundancy of the remaining items using a “goldbricker” function with a threshold of 0.25 [25]. An item is considered redundant if there is a statistical difference of less than 25% in the polychoroidal correlation between two specific variables [25].

The R package “qgraph” was employed to use the “EBICglasso” function, and the symptom network graph was constructed based on Spearman correlation analysis, the “ggplot2” package, and spring distribution (vsize = 6, maximum = 0.45, minimum = 0.16, esize = 15, border width = 1.5). The construction of a regularized causal correlation network relied on the application of the Gaussian graphical model algorithm, utilizing the graph minimal absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Glasso) process [22]. The most suitable model was selected using the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) [26]. Each node in the network represents a symptom. The connecting line between the nodes is the edge, and the edge of the network is the partial correlation coefficient; the thicker the edge, the stronger the correlation between the two symptoms [22].

Graphs were plotted using the centrality plot function, including strength, closeness, and betweenness, which were used to assess the importance and proximity of nodes in the network [27]. Using the “network tools” package, bridge symptoms were analyzed using the “bridge” function, and the symptom with the largest value of bridge strength centrality was identified as the bridge symptom [27]. Using the “Bootnet” package, the “corStability” function was employed, the 95% confidence interval was calculated by non-parametric bootstrapping to determine the correctness of the edge weights, and the correlation stability coefficient (CS-C) was calculated for the centrality indices to measure stability. The stability of the strength, closeness, and betweenness of network connections was assessed; the CS-C coefficient is usually considered to be at least 0.25, with ≥ 0.5 indicating sufficient stability [22].

S8 had the highest bridge strength (rBS=2.644), followed by S9 (rBS=2.484) and S16 (rBS=2.269). Therefore, S8 and S9 are considered bridge symptoms that connect the symptom clusters of decreased motivation and affective dysregulation. S16 is a bridge symptom that connects the affective dysregulation and impulse dyscontrol symptom clusters (see Additional file 2).

The stability test results revealed that the CS-Cs of strength, closeness centrality, and betweenness centrality were 0.671, 0.128, and 0.049, respectively, indicating that the stability of strength was good, while the stability of closeness centrality and betweenness centrality were poor. Therefore, strength was selected as the main criterion for core symptoms (see Additional file 3).

Discussion

This study is the first to employ network analysis to explore the relationships among 18 common symptoms in older adults with MBI, using ratings of occurrence to understand their symptom experience. Network analysis has enhanced our knowledge of the structural relationships among co-occurring symptoms and symptom clusters of MBI, providing insights into how symptoms and clusters interact and change within the network.

The most prevalent and severe symptom identified in older adults with MBI in this study was “Not experiencing pleasure (S8)” (40.9%) within the affective dysregulation symptom cluster, consistent with prior research [28–31]. This symptom emerged as the core symptom within the entire network, highlighting its central role in the experience of MBI. Affective dysregulation symptoms are commonly reported in older adults with MBI, with incidence rates between 34 and 69% [30, 32], possibly linked to decreased cortical thickness in the right supramarginal gyrus [33] and associated with the progression from MBI to AD due to hippocampal atrophy [34].

Bridge symptoms such as “Not experiencing pleasure” (S8), “Diminished and defeated” (S9), and “More stubborn or rigid” (S16) were found to connect multiple symptom clusters, highlighting their potential as targets for clinical interventions. Notably, previous studies have shown that interventions targeting bridge symptoms are more effective than those targeting other symptoms [35, 36]. Therefore, these bridge symptoms illustrate the interconnectedness within the symptom network, suggesting that interventions targeting these could block the progression of related symptom clusters.

The identification of bridge symptoms facilitates a deeper understanding of the relationships and patterns within symptom cluster interactions, allowing for the development of more precise intervention programs. Given the dual role of S8 as both a core symptom and a bridge symptom, regular assessments and timely interventions targeting emotional states could enhance symptom management precision in older adults with MBI.

We found that “Not experiencing pleasure” (S8) and “Diminished and defeated” (S9) were the bridge symptoms connecting the clusters of decreased motivation and affective dysregulation. Notably, both S8 and S9 belong to the affective dysregulation dimension of MBI. This may be related to the long-term inability of older adults in nursing homes to receive care and support from their families, reduced social networks, and restricted social participation, leading to feelings of sadness, loneliness, indifference, and depression [37].

A meta-analysis showed that the prevalence rates of moderate and severe loneliness in older adults in nursing homes were 61% and 35%, respectively [38]. There is a strong association between loneliness and depression, whereby individuals with higher loneliness scores tend to have more severe depression symptoms [18, 39]. Indeed, the incidence of depression in older adults is as high as 25.55% [40], and the prevalence of depression in nursing homes is significantly higher, being to 3–4 times greater than at home [41]. These episodes mainly manifest as diminished interest, depressed mood, and low energy. As such, it is evident that loneliness and depression can reduce the social motivation of older adults. Consequently, symptoms S8 and S9 can significantly impact other symptoms associated with MBI, such as affective dysregulation and decreased motivation, affecting the entire symptom network structure.

Improving symptoms of affective dysregulation through effective management and psychological interventions, group interventions, and other measures [42–45], along with increasing social interactions among older adults, may prevent the development of an unfavorable symptom network. This approach can alleviate related symptoms, ultimately enhancing the effectiveness of interventions, improving symptom management efficiency, and safeguarding the physical and mental health of older adults.

Additionally, our results showed a strong correlation between S8 and S9, suggesting a potential for mutual transformation between symptoms of affective disorders. This opens new directions for future research to explore the patterns of changes between these symptoms and their intrinsic mechanisms.

“More stubborn or rigid” (S16) was also identified as a bridge symptom that connects the affective dysregulation and impulse dyscontrol symptom clusters. Furthermore, it is the core symptom of the impulse dyscontrol cluster, consistent with previous studies [46]. S16 falls under the impulse dyscontrol dimension of the MBI, and the symptom of feeling more stubborn or rigid may be closely related to impulse control deficits in older adults. In fact, studies have shown that impulse dyscontrol is associated with gray matter atrophy and reduced cortical thickness in the parahippocampal gyrus, an early manifestation of AD [47]. The identification of bridge symptoms such as S16 is clinically significant because these symptoms may act as pathways linking different domains of neuropsychiatric disturbance, thereby accelerating the spread of dysfunction across clusters. Qualitative studies have shown that individuals with MCI or early dementia often describe experiences of rigidity, frustration, or reduced flexibility in daily life. These difficulties may further increase emotional distress and create interpersonal conflicts [48–50]. Such real-world accounts highlight how symptoms are interconnected and emphasize the value of early detection and intervention. Targeting bridge symptoms may help to interrupt the progression of multiple symptom clusters at once, thereby improving prognosis and quality of life.

Stubbornness or rigidity often manifests as an uncharacteristic insistence on one’s own way or an unwillingness to listen to others. This may be closely related to paranoia, as studies have shown that paranoid states are most strongly associated with race, lower income and education, less exercise, and more depressive symptoms [51]. Older adults in nursing homes may experience negative emotions such as insomnia, worry, anxiety, and depression due to decreased social and family relationships, which can exacerbate paranoia [52, 53]. This may constitute an objective basis for the connection between the clusters of affective dysregulation and impulse dyscontrol symptoms. Although the incidence of S16 in the symptom cluster or whole symptom set is not high, as a bridge symptom, it can significantly affect the physical health and quality of life of older adults [54]. Additionally, emotional problems and paranoid symptoms have been reported to increase the risk of suicide [55]. Therefore, timely identification and intervention of stubbornness or rigidity in older adults, along with attention to their emotions, especially depression, is crucial.

In conclusion, identifying the core and bridge symptoms of MBI through symptom network analysis is conducive to the early identification of NPS symptoms by caregivers. This allows for precise management and intervention to reduce the risk of developing AD and ensures the physical and mental health of older adults in nursing homes.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in the network analysis to provide an objective explanation of the targets of symptom intervention. Additionally, our research location, Fujian Province, with its diverse economic and geographical characteristics, serves as a microcosm of China. Older adults in nursing homes from various regions of Fujian Province were selected as participants, ensuring good representation of the sample.

However, this study has some limitations. First, the dynamic pattern of change between symptom clusters and bridge symptoms was not considered, and the sample size was small. Therefore, future large-sample longitudinal investigations should explore the changing laws of symptom clusters, the potential causal relationships, and the mechanisms of mutual influence between bridge symptoms. Second, this study evaluated the symptom network structure at the group level, which may differ from the individual level. In the future, personalized individual symptom networks can be created for precise symptom management. Third, the external validity of our findings may be limited. As shown in Fig. 1, we excluded 1462 individuals with serious medical conditions and 2086 individuals with communication difficulties. Because comorbidities and communication problems are common in nursing home residents, our sample may not fully reflect this broader population. Caution is therefore needed when generalizing the results to all nursing home residents.
