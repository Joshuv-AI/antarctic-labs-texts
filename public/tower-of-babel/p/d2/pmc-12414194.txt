Identification of robust associations between admission microbiome profiles and complications of acute pancreatitis
Authors: Hannah S Pauw, Hester C Timmerhuis, Marc G Besselink, Yama Issa, Marco Bruno, Pieter Jan Floris de Jonge, Harry van Goor, Erwin-Jan M van Geenen, Rutger Quispel, Wim van de Vrie, Adriaan Tan, Muhammed Hadithi, Niels G Venneman, J M Jansen, Ben J Witteman, Matthijs P Schwartz, Roy L J van Wanrooij, Rogier P Voermans, Alexander C Poen, Peter van Duijvendijk, Marie Paule G F Anten, Tessa Römkens, Elske Sieswerda, Merel M Tielemans, Jeanin E van Hooft, Marja A Boermeester, Robert C Verdonk, Hjalmar C van Santvoort, Fons F van den Berg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePatients with acute pancreatitis show reduced gut microbiome diversity and high abundance of pathogenic bacteria compared with healthy subjects. Admission microbiome profiles are increasingly linked to severity, but methodology and study quality hamper interpretation. Our aim was to investigate whether admission microbiome analysis provides robust and reproducible associations with severity and complications of acute pancreatitis.MethodsPatients with acute pancreatitis were prospectively enrolled from 20 Dutch hospitals (2019–2022). Admission saliva and rectal samples from 276 patients underwent 16S rDNA sequencing for microbiome profiling. Subgroups were defined based on a literature search. The microbiota endpoints (alpha- and beta-diversity, and genus abundance) were compared across subgroups and with previous studies. Robustness of the significant associations was classified as ‘moderate’ or ‘high’ in case of statistical significance in, respectively, 2 or ≥3 differential abundance models.ResultsRectal alpha diversity (Shannon Index 3.55 vs 3.63, p=0.026) was decreased in necrotising (n=49) versus oedematous pancreatitis (n=218). Microbiota communities of either saliva or rectal samples differed in all the subgroups. In total, 270 (rectal) and 138 (saliva) genera were associated with severity or complications, of which 35 and 3 (Anaeroglobus and Finegoldia in saliva; Lachnospiraceae_FE2018_group in rectal) were classified as, respectively, moderately and highly robust. Fourteen associations were previously reported, of which 10 were in the opposite direction compared with this study.ConclusionThree admission microbiome taxa associated with severity and complications were highly robust, although their biological relevance remains unclear. This study also shows the lack of replicable findings of admission microbiome associations, highlighting the need for longitudinal studies to establish temporal relationships between microbiome changes and disease progression.

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis is one of the most common gastrointestinal diseases requiring hospitalisation. In approximately 20% of patients, it has a severe disease course characterised by necrosis of the pancreas and peripancreatic tissues, infections, early (multiple) organ failure and relatively high mortality.1 Among other things, intestinal bacteria may drive disease progression in patients with acute pancreatitis. Early severity is mainly characterised by systemic hyperinflammation and gut injury. An increase in potential pathogens such as Escherichia coli MG1655 may aggravate intestinal barrier injury, thereby accelerating systemic inflammation leading to organ failure, pancreatic necrosis and intensive care admission.2 3 Immunosuppression and infectious complications are hallmarks of late severity such as infected (peri)pancreatic necrosis. Intestinal inhabitants such as E. coli and Enterococci are often recovered from infected collection, suggesting bacterial translocation from the gastrointestinal tract as the primary source.4

Observational studies linking gastrointestinal (mostly faecal) microbiome composition to complications of acute pancreatitis are mounting.58 Based on these studies, one might hypothesise that patients who progress to severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) possess a less diverse, pathogen-harbouring intestinal microbiome. Similarly, some studies have reported microbiome compositional differences between subgroups based on pancreatitis severity (beta-diversity).5 7 A higher alpha diversity, meaning the diversity of a microbiome sample, is generally associated with health and has not been previously associated with pancreatitis severity outcomes.57 Alternatively, a health-promoting microbiome, which includes, among others, butyrate-producing bacteria that contribute to the maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity, may protect against severe complications.9 Nevertheless, findings are often conflicting, and causal evidence remains lacking due to the lack of appropriately designed and adequately powered studies. The aim was to define (1) The association between admission microbiome profiles and subgroups of patients with acute pancreatitis (based on severity and development of complications) in our study, and (2) To compare these associations with previous studies to assess replicability.

Methods

Study design, population and clinical data collection

The POEMA (Prospective Multicenter Microbiota Study for Monitoring and Evaluation of Severe Acute Pancreatitis) Study was a prospective, observational multicentre cohort study (NL-OMON29684) conducted in 20 Dutch hospitals.10 The present manuscript presents the main, prespecified analysis of the POEMA Study. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient or the patient’s legal representative. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Between October 2019 and January 2022, all consecutive patients that were hospitalised with a first episode of acute pancreatitis were eligible for inclusion. Acute pancreatitis was diagnosed according to the revised Atlanta Criteria.11 Exclusion criteria were: an age younger than 18 years, pregnancy, disease onset more than 72 hours, a history of (recurrent) acute or chronic pancreatitis (according to the M-ANNHEIM criteria12) and antibiotic use within the last 2 months. Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records and a patient questionnaire, and entered into an Electronic Data Capture database (Castor EDC). The questionnaire, collected at inclusion, included self-reported medical and lifestyle information, such as alcohol use, smoking, antibiotic treatment within 6 months prior to admission and proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use (see online supplemental Methods). The follow-up of clinical data was at least 90 days following the initial admission. The revised Atlanta classification was used for severity stratification.11 The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist and all definitions used are provided in the online supplemental methods.

Patient subgroups and endpoints

A systematic literature search was performed (see box 1) to define acute pancreatitis subgroups with previously reported associations with admission microbiome taxa. Based on the included studies, the following subgroups were defined: SAP versus mild acute pancreatitis/moderate severe acute pancreatitis (MAP/MSAP), MSAP/SAP versus MAP, acute necrotising pancreatitis (ANP) versus acute oedematous pancreatitis (AOP), and patients with persistent organ failure and/or endoscopic/radiological drainage for infected necrotising pancreatitis (POF/dIPN) versus the remaining cohort. MAP, MSAP and SAP in this study were defined according to the Revised Atlanta Classification.11 ANP was defined by the presence of extra-pancreatic or pancreatic parenchymal necrosis observed on contrast-enhanced CT. The POF/dIPN subgroup comprised patients who experienced organ failure of one or more organ systems for more than 48 hours and/or those who underwent dIPN. All subgroups were classified at the end of follow-up. The endpoint is the bacterial microbiome composition at the genus level and is composed of alpha-diversity, beta-diversity and differential abundance (DA).

Systemic literature search and subgroup selection

PubMed was searched on 1 April 2024 using the following search: (“pancreatitis”(tiab) OR “pancreatitis”(Mesh)) AND (((“Gastrointestinal Microbiome”(Mesh) OR 'intestinal microbiota'(mesh) OR gut microbiota(mesh) OR dysbiosis(mesh)) OR (“gut flora”(tiab) OR “faecal bacteria”(tiab) OR “commensal bacteria”(tiab) OR “faecal bacterial flora”(tiab) OR “enteric bacterial flora”(tiab) OR microbiome(tiab) OR “commensal microbes”(tiab) OR “gut microflora”(tiab) OR “intestinal microbial communities”(tiab) OR “intestinal bacteria”(tiab) OR dysbiosis(tiab) OR resistomes(tiab) OR metagenome*(tiab)) OR “16S rRNA”(tiab) OR “16S rDNA”(tiab)). In total, 293 hits were found and these were screened for title and abstract. Finally, five articles were included for comparative review (see online supplemental table 1) for details.5835

Sample collection

Rectal swabs were used as a representative sample of the lower gastrointestinal tract because they provide comparable profiles and the difficulty of consistent and timely collection of stool in this ill patient population.1315 A rectal swab and saliva sample was collected directly following enrolment, using commercial kits (respectively, DNA Genotek Omnigene P-117 and OM-501) according to the product instructions. The kits are designed to stabilise samples for, respectively, 30 days and a year at room temperature. Samples were sent by mail (ambient temperature) to a central laboratory and stored at −80°C as soon as possible (within 72 hours from collecting) until further processing.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing

DNA was extracted from rectal swabs and saliva using a modification of a previously described protocol (method 5).9 16 The storage medium was centrifuged, and the pellet resuspended in 300 µl Stool Transport and Recovery (STAR) Buffer and 30 µl proteinase K. The suspension was added to a bead beating tube together with the cotton tip in case of a rectal swab, followed by the bead beating protocol. DNA was purified using the Maxwell RSC whole blood DNA kit with the Maxwell RSC instrument. 16S rRNA gene amplicons were generated using a single-step PCR protocol targeting the V3-V4 region.17 PCR products were purified using Ampure XP beads and purified products were equimolar pooled. The libraries were sequenced using a MiSeq platform using V3 chemistry with 2×251 cycles.

Bioinformatic processing and taxonomic assignment

Forward and reverse reads were truncated to 240 and 210 bases, respectively, and merged using USEARCH.18 Merged reads that did not pass the Illumina chastity filter, had an expected error rate higher than 2, or were shorter than 380 bases, were filtered. Amplified sequence variants (ASVs) were inferred for each sample individually with a minimum abundance of 4 reads.19 Unfiltered reads were then mapped against the collective ASV set to determine the abundances. Taxonomy was assigned using the Ribosomal Database Project classifier and SILVA 16S ribosomal database V.132.20 21 Samples with less than 10 000 sequences were removed to filter out low quality samples (online supplemental figure 1). ASVs with a relative abundance <0.1% per sample were removed to filter out spurious taxa.

Statistical analyses

Alpha diversity, beta diversity and DA were compared between the defined patient subgroups and subsequently with the findings reported in the included studies from the systematic literature search.

The Inverse Simpson index and the Shannon Index were used as a measure of alpha diversity (the diversity within a sample). Rarefaction was applied to balance the differences in reads, and rarefaction depth was based on visual inspection of the rarefaction curve. Differences between subgroups were calculated using a Mann-Whitney U test. Principal coordinate analysis was used to calculate Bray-Curtis distances as a measure of beta diversity (the between-group diversity) between the subgroups. Differences in beta diversity were calculated using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA).

For DA analyses, Microbiome Multivariable Association with Linear Models (MaAslin) 2, Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) and Empirical Analysis of Digital Gene Expression Data in R (EdgeR) were used. Rare taxa were removed by applying a relative abundance filter of 0.25%. MaAsLin2 is based on generalised linear and mixed models and allows multivariate analysis.22 In multivariate analyses, the variables age, sex, biliary aetiology and PPI use on admission were included to account for confounders in the association between DA and patient subgroups. LEfSe identifies genomic features distinguishing different biological conditions.23 It uses non-parametrical tests and linear discriminant analysis to estimate the effect size of each differentially abundant feature. EdgeR uses an overdispersed Poisson model and empirical Bayes methods to estimate variability and adjust for multiple testing, controlling the false discovery rate.24 For DA analysis, only associations observed in two or more models were reported. Unless stated otherwise, statistical significance was defined as a value of p<0.05.

Patient and public involvement

A patient panel was involved in the grant application process. The Dutch patient organisation Alvleeskliervereniging formally supported this study and was involved in planning and execution.

Results

Patient characteristics and clinical outcomes

A total of 319 consecutive patients with a first episode of acute pancreatitis were included in the cohort (see online supplemental figure 2 for the flow chart and online supplemental table 2 for inclusions per centre). In 22 patients no samples were collected because participants withdrew their consent, were transferred to a different hospital or were discharged before samples were collected. In 21 patients no baseline samples were collected, leaving 276 patients that provided samples for 16S rDNA sequencing.

A total of 267 rectal swabs and 255 saliva samples from 275 patients were sequenced that had sufficient depth (≥10 000 reads), resulting in a respective median number of sequences of 53 181 (IQR 48 549–58 293) and 65 676 (IQR 48 997–76 338) (online supplemental figure 2 and online supplemental table 3 show the number of reads per sample).

Clinical characteristics of the 275 patients are listed in table 1. The most common aetiologies were biliary pancreatitis and alcohol-induced pancreatitis, respectively, 163 (59.3%) and 41 (14.9%). In total, 22 (8.0%) patients developed organ failure and 11 (4.0%) patients were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) during the initial admission. At the end of follow-up, 218 (79.3%) patients were classified as MAP, 52 (19.9%) patients as MSAP, and 5 (1.8%) patients as SAP. Because of the low number of patients in the SAP subgroup, this subgroup comparison was not included in the analyses. The ANP subgroup consists of 49 (17.8%) patients and the POF/dIPN subgroup 17 (6.2%) patients. The sizes of the subgroups per sample type are presented in the flow chart (online supplemental figure 2) and the distribution of possible health and lifestyle confounders between the subgroups in online supplemental table 4.

MSAP/SAP versus MAP

In the rectal and salivary microbiome, respectively, eight and one genera were moderately robustly associated with both EdgeR and LEfSe. In rectal swabs, Agathobacter, Blautia, Dorea, Faecalibacterium, Phascolarctobacterium and Ruminococcaceae_NK4A214_group were decreased, and Olsenella, Peptoclostridium increased. In saliva, Staphylococcus was increased. Online supplemental table 6 shows the comparison of this subgroup with the literature and one similarity was defined. Wang et al reported a decrease in Roseburia_inulinivorans, while this study identified a decrease in Roseburia (LEfSe).6

ANP versus AOP

In the ANP versus AOP group, eight and three significant differences (moderately robust) in abundance using EdgeR and LEfSe in, respectively, the rectal and saliva microbiome were observed. Agathobacter, Blautia, Dorea, Faecalibacterium, Lachnospira, Pharscolarctobacterium Ruminococcaceae_NK4A214_group were decreased, and Olsenella increased in the rectal microbiota of the ANP group. Online supplemental table 7 shows the comparison with literature of general abundant species in this subgroup of ANP versus AOP. The decrease of Ruminococcaceae_NK4A214_group and Ruminococcaceae_UCG-004 was consistent with findings by Zou et al, who reported a decrease in Ruminococcus.5

For saliva, there were three increased genera (Finegoldia, Prevotella and Staphylococcus) in the ANP versus AOP group.

POF/dIPN versus the remaining cohort

In the rectal microbiota, abundance was higher for Lachnospiraceae_FE2018_group in three DA models (robust association). Additionally, Lawsonella, Mogibacterium, Peptoclostridium and Veillonella were increased in two DA models, and Phascolarctobacterium was decreased. Online supplemental table 8 shows the results of analyses of DA of species for this subgroup from this cohort compared with Ammer-Herrmenau et al.7 Nine taxa were (at species level) reported as increased (Acidaminococcus intestine, Alistipes dispar, Flavonifractor plautii, Lachnospira GAM79, Lachnospira eligens, Oscillibacter, Parabacteroides species, Parabacteroides distasonis, Roseburia hominis), while they were decreased (at genus level) in this analysis. Three taxa were reported as decreased (Anaerococcus mediterraneensis, Anaerococcus prevotii, Peptoniphilus harei, Lawsonella clevelandensis), while they were increased in this analysis. None of the associations were replicable in terms of significance and direction.

For saliva, Finegoldia and Anaeroglobus were increased in EdgeR, and univariate and multivariate MaAsLin2 (robust association). Alloscardovia and Staphylococcus were increased in EdgeR and univariate MaAsLin2 (moderately robust).

Discussion

This multicentre prospective cohort study of patients with acute pancreatitis identified three robust associations (meaning in at least three DA models) of admission microbiome samples (Finegoldia and Anaeroglobus in saliva, Lachnospiraceae_FE2018_group in rectal) with subgroups based on the Atlanta severity classification or severe complications (POF/dIPN). When comparing our findings to previously reported associations, only Roseburia and Ruminococcus were replicable, while 10 other reported associations were not.

Finegoldia is an anaerobic Gram-positive coccus found in the normal flora of the oral, gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts, as well as the skin. It is associated with various opportunistic anaerobic infections.2527 Its virulence is particularly associated with biofilm formation and occasionally found in polymicrobial prosthetic joint infections.28 Positive and negative microbiome associations with disease were previously described, precluding a clear conclusion about its role in health and disease. Rectal Finegoldia abundance at admission predicted infected necrosis in patients with acute pancreatitis, although with limited accuracy (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.66).5 In contrast, one comparative study found a decrease in Finegoldia magna abundance in the rectal swab microbiome in patients with acute pancreatitis with POF/dIPN, which further hampers the credibility of the association.7 Anaeroglobusus is an anaerobic Gram-negative coccus and commonly resides in the human gastrointestinal microbiome. Infections with Anaeroglobus are rare and this genus was not previously associated with acute pancreatitis.29 Negativicoccus succinicivorans, also a member of the Veillonellaceae family, was isolated from blood from a patient with pancreatitis.30 Lachnospiraceae_FE2018_group is a poorly characterised taxonomic group within the Lachnospiraceae family that was not associated with disease including acute pancreatitis.

Alpha diversity was not associated with severity or outcome in the current or previous studies, except for the subgroups with necrotising pancreatitis and MSAP/SAP in this study. One may conclude from this that a more diverse bacterial microbiome does not protect against the most severe complications such as POF. Beta diversity, the distance between groups of microbiome samples, differed among others in the subgroup POF/dIPN versus the remaining cohort. This is in line with a previous study indicating a robust association.7

Although some overlapping associations between previous studies and the current analysis were found, there were far more discrepancies, especially with one study.7 This may be caused by many factors. First, differences in sample collection, storage and preprocessing analyses might cause significant variance in the microbiome composition.31 Second, different reference databases are used for alignment leading. Benchmarking of DA analysis methods indicated that methods produce different results using the same data set.32 Therefore, it is advisable to combine multiple methods to find robust associations. Lastly, definitions of subgroups and the follow-up time might differ between studies.

The limited information that a single microbiome sample at admission provides in the context of acute pancreatitis needs consideration. Longitudinal studies are useful to find temporal relations between dynamic changes in the microbiome and occurrence of clinical events. This is crucial for understanding disease progression and to identify potential biomarkers or druggable targets.33 Serial sampling and state-of-the-art multiomics methods are needed to investigate both composition and function at high resolution. A longitudinal microbiome study by our research group is ongoing.

Besides this, there are some specific limitations to this study. Important general (age, gender, PPI use) and study-specific confounders (pancreatitis aetiology) were used in the multivariate model; however, there may be additional unknown confounders. These results were compared with other studies in the field, but three out of four were conducted in China. Due to global differences in aetiology of pancreatitis and microbiome composition, our results are specific to a Western-European population and cannot be generalised beyond this population without validation in other geographical regions. Also, variation of sample type (rectal swab vs stool) and differences in quality of care may impact generalisability of the results. Late ICU mortality in the Netherlands has indeed decreased following the implementation of the step-up approach for infected necrotising pancreatitis.34 However, there are still no effective treatments to prevent complications; therefore, the prevalence of outcomes that are the subject of interest in our study, such as pancreatic necrosis and secondary infection, is most likely comparable to other populations. Although the total sample size within this study was relatively large considering the patient population, the majority of patients developed a mild disease course, leaving relatively few patients with severe disease and complications. Additionally, 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing was used, which is a highly standardised method but limits identification to the genus level and lacks functional information, such as antimicrobial resistance, virulence and metabolic activity. Another limitation was the use of rectal and saliva samples as proxies of the intestinal microbiota, while recognising that biologically meaningful mechanisms such as bacterial translocation occur at the mucosal level of the small and/or large intestine. Endoscopically obtained samples pose logistical and ethical issues, especially in a multicentre study, and the future implementation of a microbiome-based test requires non-invasively obtained samples. Finally, CT imaging was not routinely done, but guided by clinical judgement, so there might be underreporting of patients with (probably limited) (peri-) pancreatic necrosis.

To conclude, robust associations were found between disease severity and outcome with Finegoldia, Anaeroglobus and Lachnospiraceae_FE2018_group. If these taxa are causally related to disease progression, usefulness to improve diagnosis and/or treatment of patients largely depends on the underlying mechanism(s). Targeted therapies—stand-alone or as a cocktail—might involve virulence, inhibitors, metabolites and/or live bacterial products. The failure to replicate previously published results is striking, which may be overcome by combining and re-analysing the raw data using a standardised bioinformatics pipeline and conducting an individual patient data meta-analysis. These discrepancies in microbiome findings within subgroups of acute pancreatitis between the current and previous studies may be attributed to differences in the study populations, as well as variations in sample collection protocols, DNA extraction methods and analysis techniques. This study highlights the potential biases and resulting variability in findings among different admission microbiome studies. Harmonised microbiome analysis protocols are needed to ensure reliable and reproducible results, in order to ultimately integrate microbiome diagnostics in clinical settings. The precise biological role of identified associated bacteria in the pathogenesis of SAP remains unclear.
