Comment on “Biomechanical Comparison of Plate Versus Intramedullary Screw Fixation for Extra-Articular Metacarpal Base Fractures”
Authors: Kishankumar Mahida, Snehal Rajendra Jagtap
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the study by Mzeihem et al,1 which offers a timely biomechanical data comparing dorsal plate and intramedullary screw fixation for extra-articular metacarpal base fractures, a clinical subset that remains underrepresented in fixation literature. The use of fresh-frozen cadaveric specimens and standardized osteotomy placement strengthens internal validity, and the decision to isolate both thumb and small finger metacarpals permits digit-specific subgroup interpretation.

One interpretive concern pertains to the real-world translation of load to failure metrics. Although dorsal plates exhibited higher ultimate strength, the loads sustained by intramedullary screw constructs notably exceeded physiologic demands in both thumb and small finger specimens.2 Published normative data show that maximal thumb forces during daily activity rarely surpass 15 N, whereas small finger outputs remain under 3 N.3 Therefore, the statistically lower load to failure of screw fixation may not equate to clinically meaningful inferiority, especially when considered against its higher stiffness and potential for earlier mobilization.

Additionally, the absence of statistical significance in stiffness comparisons should not preclude clinical attention to this parameter. The consistent trend toward higher stiffness in intramedullary constructs suggests favorable resistance to early micromotion, which may mitigate loss of reduction in the early postoperative phase. This feature, especially in the context of minimally invasive screw insertion, may support broader consideration of intramedullary screws in fracture patterns amenable to canal fixation.

We commend the authors for addressing an uncommonly studied fracture location with a well-controlled biomechanical design. As intramedullary approaches gain popularity in shaft fixation, their application to proximal fractures warrants further in vivo validation. Future investigations integrating soft-tissue dynamics, postoperative rehabilitation protocols, and patient-centered outcomes will be essential to refine implant selection and optimize functional recovery.
