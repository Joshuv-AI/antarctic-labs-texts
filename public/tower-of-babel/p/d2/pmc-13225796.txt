Diagnostic Utility of The International System for Reporting Serous Fluid Cytopathology
Authors: Akhil Kocherlakota, Chaithra Venkataramana, Deepa Sowkur Anandarama Adiga, Ranjitha Rao, Dinesh Kumar Ambati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSerous effusions are common specimens in cytopathology, yet their reporting has historically lacked uniformity. The International System for Reporting Serous Fluid Cytopathology (TIS) provides a standardized five-tiered classification system to improve diagnostic clarity and clinical communication. This study was conducted to assess the diagnostic utility and applicability of TIS by determining the risk of malignancy (ROM) and diagnostic accuracy metrics for each category.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on all serous effusions (pleural, pericardial, ascitic fluids, and peritoneal washes) reported from January 2021 to December 2023. Cases were re-categorized according to the TIS framework. The ROM for each category was calculated with diagnostic accuracy metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV).ResultsA total of 1,447 cases were included in the analysis. Distribution across TIS categories was as follows: nondiagnostic (ND) (8 cases; 0.6%), negative for malignancy (NFM) (1,170 cases; 80.9%), atypia of undetermined significance (AUS) (40 cases; 2.8%), suspicious for malignancy (SFM) (35 cases; 2.4%), and malignant (MAL) (194 cases; 13.4%). The calculated ROM for each category was 62.5% for ND, 28.8% for NFM, 66.7% for AUS, 93.8% for SFM, and 98.7% for MAL. The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV are 33.8%, 99.5%, 97.9%, and 69.7%, when AUS and NFM are considered negative. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV are 38.1%, 98.1%, 92.9%, and 70.8%, when AUS, SFM, and MAL cases are considered positive.ConclusionThe implementation of TIS provides a standardized framework for reporting serous effusions with definite categorization. The ROM for each category helps in risk stratification of cases reported under TIS. The system demonstrates high specificity and PPV, making it a robust tool for the detection of malignancy.

Introduction

Serous effusions, involving the pleural, pericardial, and peritoneal cavities, are frequently encountered in cytopathology practice, representing a pathological accumulation of fluid often resulting from circulatory, inflammatory, or neoplastic etiologies. These specimens provide a critical window for screening, diagnosis, and staging [1, 2]. The cytological evaluation of these fluids reveals a heterogenous population of cells, including mesothelial cells, inflammatory infiltrates, and potentially metastatic or primary malignant cells. While acute effusions are often dominated by neutrophils, chronic effusions from tuberculosis or neoplasms typically comprise lymphocytes [1, 3].

Historically, the cytological reporting of serous effusions has been challenging. The morphological overlap between reactive mesothelial and malignant cells creates a significant diagnostic dilemma, especially in the absence of ancillary studies [4, 5]. Serous effusions reporting was largely descriptive and lacked uniformity. Terms like “atypical,” “inconclusive,” and “suspicious” were used without strict criteria, sometimes interchangeably. The lack of standardization often led to confusion among clinicians, inconsistent management, and the inability to compare data across institutions. The International System for Reporting Serous Fluid Cytopathology (TIS) was introduced in 2019 and classifies effusions into five categories: nondiagnostic (ND), negative for malignancy (NFM), atypia of undetermined significance (AUS), suspicious for malignancy (SFM), and malignant (MAL) [6, 7]. Unlike previous reporting methods, TIS links the categories with a specific risk of malignancy (ROM) and suggests a management algorithm. The system aims to enhance diagnostic clarity, reduce interobserver variability, and provide clear communication between pathologists and clinicians.

The widespread applicability and adoption of TIS rely on robust validation of the system across diverse geographical and clinical settings. There has been a lack of evidence with a large dataset in southern India, and this study aims to determine the risk of malignancy across the different categories of TIS with the calculation of diagnostic accuracy metrics in the study population.

Methods

This retrospective study included all pleural, pericardial, and peritoneal/ascitic fluids, as well as peritoneal/gutter washes, reported based on morphology in the Department of Pathology. Following approval by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC: KMC MLR 05/2023/225), cases processed between January 2021 and December 2023 were retrieved for analysis. Clinical information and relevant follow-up data for 2 years from the date of receiving the sample were collected from departmental records, while corresponding cytology slides and paraffin blocks were collected from departmental archives.

Specimen processing followed the standard operating procedure of the laboratory. Fresh fluid samples were aliquoted into 15-mL screw-capped tubes and centrifuged at 1,500 RPM for 10 min. Following the decantation of the supernatant, three conventional smears were prepared from the sediment and all of them were immediately wet-fixed in 100% methanol for 20 min to ensure optimal nuclear and cytoplasmic detail preservation. Two of these smears were stained with Papanicolaou stain, and the other smear with hematoxylin and eosin.

Cell blocks (CBs) were prepared when clinically indicated or cytology yielded equivocal results. For CB preparation, the residual fluid was centrifuged for an additional 10 min, and the resulting cell button was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h, followed by routine tissue processing, paraffin embedding, and sectioning for hematoxylin and eosin staining. CB evaluation was only used to confirm or recategorize the fluid category in equivocal cases. It was not considered as histological evidence in the calculation of ROM.

The ROM was calculated using histological follow-up data (if available), followed by radiological suspicion and evidence of malignancy, followed by clinical evidence of malignancy, in that order of priority, to comprehensively evaluate the ROM and to avoid falsely high or low ROM in any category. The follow-up data of 2 years are strictly considered to maintain uniformity. Given the equivocal nature of the AUS category, tests of diagnostic accuracy were assessed under two scenarios: In the first scenario, cases reported as ND, NFM, and AUS were grouped as “negative,” and cases reported as SFM and MAL were grouped as “positive.” In the other scenario, only cases reported as ND and NFM were considered “negative,” and cases reported as AUS, SFM, and MAL were considered “positive.”

Results

During the study period, we received 1,601 cases of serous effusions. Of these, 1,447 (90.4%) cases were included in the analysis, while 154 (9.6%) cases were excluded. The cohort comprised 799 (55.2%) males and 648 (44.8%) females (M:F ratio of 1:0.81) with patient ages ranging from 2 months to 98 years (mean of 56.7 years). The cases included consisted of 710 pleural effusions (49.1%), 576 ascitic effusions (39.8%), 117 peritoneal washes (8.1%), and 44 pericardial effusions (3%). The minimum and maximum quantities of effusion samples received were 0.2 mL and 2,500 mL of pleural and ascitic fluids, respectively, with a median volume of 57.7 mL.

Out of all the included cases, 799 (55.2%) were male and 648 (44.8%) were female, with an M: F ratio of 1:0.81. The ages of the patients ranged from 2 months to 98 years, with a mean of 56.7 years. Pleural fluids constituted the majority of the samples, with 710 cases (49.1%), followed by ascitic fluids (576 cases; 39.8%), peritoneal washes (117 cases; 8.1%), and pericardial fluids (44 cases; 3%). On re-categorization into the diagnostic categories of TIS, 8 cases (0.6%) were categorized as ND, 1,170 cases (80.9%) as NFM, 40 cases (2.8%) as AUS, 35 cases (2.4%) as SFM, 1 case (0.1%) as MAL-P, and 193 cases (13.3%) as MAL-S.

Negative for Malignancy

Most of the sample size pertains to the NFM category, comprising 1,170 cases (80.9%). Pleural fluids constituted the majority, with 593 cases (50.7%), followed by ascitic fluids (456 cases; 39%), peritoneal washes (92 cases; 7.9%), and pericardial fluids (29 cases; 2.5%). Four cases (0.3%) were bilious, 540 cases (46.2%) were hemorrhagic, 41 cases (3.6%) were purulent, and 584 cases (49.9%) were serous on gross appearance, while we received only the slides for a second opinion in 1 case. The mean fluid volume received in these cases was 53 mL. These fluids contained predominantly benign, reactive mesothelial cells, followed by lymphocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, and macrophages, as shown in Figure 1. The most common clinical diagnosis in these cases was a suspicion of TB with pleural effusion, followed by decompensated CLD with portal hypertension and ascites, as well as benign ovarian neoplasms undergoing surgery. A benign cystic teratoma with peritoneal wash was reported to exhibit mature squamous cells and well-formed granulomas composed of epithelioid histiocytes. Follow-up histological, radiological, or clinical data for a period of 2 years were collected in all 1,170 cases, and the ROM for this category was calculated to be 28.8%.

Atypia of Undetermined Significance

Numerous cases were reported as “atypical” sometimes in combination with the term “suspicious,” with comments such as reactive changes and favoring atypical features originally added to descriptive notes. On re-categorization, out of 1,447 cases, 40 (2.8%) cases were deemed to be of the AUS category. One case (2.5%) was bilious, 18 (45%) cases were hemorrhagic, 1 case (2.5%) was purulent, and 20 cases (50%) were serous on gross appearance. The mean fluid volume received in these cases was 45 mL. They featured atypical cytological features such as mild nucleomegaly, mildly irregular nuclear borders, slightly increased nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, or other equivocal features not qualifying for NFM, SFM, or MAL categories, as shown in Figure 1. Out of the 40 cases, 17 (42.5%) cases were ascitic, 16 (40%) cases were pleural, 6 (15%) cases were pericardial fluids, and 1 (2.5%) case was peritoneal wash. CB was done for academic purposes in 13 (32.5%) of these cases, which rendered a similar AUS diagnosis due to equivocal cytological features. Follow-up data were available in 36 (90%) out of 40 cases with a ROM of 66.7%. The 36 cases reported as AUS with the available follow-up diagnoses are summarized in Table 1.

Malignant-Primary

One case of malignant mesothelioma (MM) was diagnosed via cytology and categorized as MAL-P, according to TIS. It involved an elderly patient with complaints of cough and dyspnea for 2 months, exhibiting multiple enhancing lesions extending to the intercostal spaces and the left dome of the diaphragm on the CECT of the chest. There was radiological and clinical suspicion of MM. We received 50 mL of pleural fluid, which appeared hemorrhagic on gross examination. Microscopy revealed sheets of malignant mesothelial cells set against a dense hemorrhagic background. These cells were arranged in 2D groups and sheets, with cytoplasmic blebbing and intercellular windowing. There was a 2-tone cytoplasm with a few cells showing cytoplasmic vacuoles. Multinucleation and prominent nucleoli were seen, as shown in Figure 2. The ROM for this category was calculated to be 100%.

Malignant-Secondary

A total of 193 cases (13.3%) were classified as MAL-S according to TIS. Eighty-seven cases (45%) were ascitic, 81 (42%) cases were pleural, 17 (8.8%) cases were peritoneal washes, and 8 (4.2%) cases were pericardial fluids. Among these, 124 cases (64.1%) were hemorrhagic, 66 (34.1%) cases were serous, and there was 1 case each of bilious (0.6%) and purulent (0.6%) fluid. One case (0.6%) was also sent for a second opinion. The mean volume of fluid received in these cases was 77 mL. Follow-up histological, radiological, or clinical data were available in 159 cases (82.3%). The most predominant primary site of neoplasms is of ovarian origin, with 64 cases (40.3%), followed by adenocarcinomas of the lung (26 cases; 16.5%), squamous cell carcinoma of the lung (1 case; 0.6%), gastric carcinoma (19 cases; 12.1%), breast adenocarcinoma (14 cases; 9%), colorectal adenocarcinoma (11 cases; 7.1%), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (3 cases; 1.8%), cholangiocarcinoma (3 cases; 1.8%), metastatic carcinoma of unknown origin (3 cases; 1.8%), T-cell lymphoma in the mediastinum (2 cases; 1.2%), NHL (2 cases; 1.2%), hepatocellular carcinoma (2 cases; 1.2%), parotid adenocarcinoma (2 cases; 1.2%), Wilms’ tumor (2 cases; 1.2%), esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (2 cases; 1.2%), multiple myeloma (1 case; 0.6%), chronic lymphocytic leukemia/lymphoma (1 case; 0.6%), and clear cell renal cell carcinoma (1 case; 0.6%). Of the 64 ovarian origin tumors, 60 were malignant, confirmed on histopathology, one serous cystadenoma, one mucinous cystadenoma, and two borderline mucinous tumors. The borderline tumors are considered to be malignant for the calculation of ROM. The 2 cases of T-cell lymphoma were hemorrhagic pleural effusions from the same patient, received separately. Similarly, three samples from the same patient with pancreatic adenocarcinoma and metastases to the liver were received: two serous ascitic and one hemorrhagic pleural fluid. Two hemorrhagic ascitic fluids from a 2-year-old girl were received and reported as MAL-S, accompanied by a descriptive note suggestive of a small round blue cell tumor, which was confirmed to be a Wilms’ tumor on histopathology and IHC. These cases are shown in Figure 3. The ROM was calculated to be 98.7% for the MAL-S category. The ROM of all categories is summarized in Table 3.

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of TIS when AUS is considered a “negative” diagnosis

The category AUS has been a topic of diagnostic dilemma and controversy in the estimation of the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of TIS. Hence, we have calculated them considering ND, NFM, and AUS as “negative” and SFM and MAL categories as “positive” in one scenario and considering ND and NFM as “negative” and AUS, SFM, and MAL categories as “positive” in another scenario. This method showed that the specificity and PPV improved by considering AUS as a “positive” diagnosis. Discussion

Our study was compared with similar studies conducted by other Indian and international researchers, including Rakheja et al. [8] in 2022, Singh et al. [9] in 2023; two systematic literature reviews by Farahani and Baloch [10] in 2019 and Ahuja et al. [11] in 2024; and a detailed analysis by Shen. et al. [12] in 2024 regarding the impact of retrospective reclassification of serous effusions with equivocal features into AUS and SFM categories according to TIS. Our data confirm that TIS provides a robust, clinically actionable framework with high specificity for malignancy.

Patient Demographics

Our cohort demonstrated a slight male predominance (1:0.81), contrasting with the female-exclusive gynecologic study by Rakheja et al. [8] and the balanced ratio reported by Singh et al. [9]. This male predominance likely reflects the high volume of pleural effusions secondary to smoking-related pathologies and occupational exposures in the locality. Our study encompassed the widest age range (2 months–98 years), validating the applicability of TIS across pediatric and geriatric populations.

Nondiagnostic

We reported a low ND rate of 0.6%, which aligns with the lower end of the spectrum (0.2%–1.56%) reported in systematic reviews [10, 11]. This rate is significantly lower than the 5–15% observed in some Indian institutional audits [8, 9], suggesting a high-quality pre-analytical processing and adherence to the TIS recommendation of processing a minimum of 75 mL of fluid [13–15]. While the global average ROM for ND is approximately 17%, our study yielded 62.5%. This elevation is attributable to the small sample size (8 cases) and the availability of follow-up in all cases. This underscores the importance of the recommendation for this category by TIS that an ND diagnosis should not be dismissed, but rather warrants immediate resampling if clinically indicated.

Negative for Malignancy

Constituting 80.9% of our caseload, the NFM category aligns with global distributions [10, 11]. Our calculated ROM of 28.8% is higher than the 12–20% reported in literature. This deviation may stem from follow-up bias, where patients with benign clinical courses are lost to follow-up. Furthermore, this highlights a potential limitation in detecting low-grade malignancies in fluid cytology without ancillary testing, which our study lacked. To mitigate the risk, we advocate for increased utilization of CB and immunocytochemistry even in macroscopically benign fluids if a clinical suspicion persists.

Atypia of Undetermined Significance

The AUS category represents a critical “grey area” in cytopathology. We categorized 2.8% of cases as AUS, adhering strictly to the TIS recommendation of maintaining this category below 3% of the total workload. Our ROM of 66.7% is remarkably consistent with the 65.9% reported in the systematic review [10]. Clinically, a diagnosis of AUS necessitates a repeat tap or concurrent biopsy as per TIS recommendations.

Suspicious for Malignancy

We identified 35 cases (2.4%) as SFM with an ROM of 93.8%. This is higher than the TIS benchmark of 80–88% and the 81.8% reported by Farahani et al. [10], reflecting our strict adherence to the diagnostic criteria validating SFM as a near-definitive marker of malignancy. In practice, an SFM result should prompt a similar response as a MAL diagnosis while awaiting histological confirmation.

Malignant-Primary

We reported 1 case of MM (1/1,447 or 0.07%) with a ROM of 100%, which was in correlation with the clinical suspicion supporting the diagnosis. However, no follow-up or ancillary tests were available. Only Singh and Gupta’s study had 1 case of MM out of 1,115 cases (0.09%). Farahani, Baloch, and TIS observed MM to be <1% of the total workload of any cytopathology laboratory. All the literature reviewed suggests ancillary testing with IHC for loss of BAP1 to confirm a diagnosis of MM [9, 10, 13, 16, 17].

Malignant-Secondary

The MAL-S category (13.3%) with a ROM of 98.7% confirms the high PPV of fluid cytology for metastatic disease. While lung and ovarian malignancies predominated, our study identified a diverse spectrum of metastases. The high ROM confirms that a MAL-S diagnosis is sufficient for therapeutic decision making in an advanced metastatic setting, especially when tissue biopsy is not feasible.

A malignant pleural and pericardial effusion is predominantly associated with primary malignancies originating in the lung (adenocarcinoma more than squamous cell carcinoma), followed by breast carcinomas in women and hematolymphoid malignancies. Malignant ascites is almost always linked to ovarian malignancies in women and gastrointestinal malignancies (gastric, followed by colorectal) in men. Hematolymphoid malignancies are frequently observed in pleural, ascitic, and pericardial fluids. Our study reported rarer malignancies such as pancreatic adenocarcinomas, cholangiocarcinomas, Wilms’ tumor, mucoepidermoid carcinoma of the parotid, HCCs, esophageal squamous cell carcinomas, and clear cell renal cell carcinoma metastasizing to the serous effusions.

Diagnostic Accuracy and Clinical Utility

The diagnostic performance of TIS varies based on how AUS is treated when calculating the diagnostic accuracy parameters. Considering AUS as negative improved the specificity (99.1%) and PPV (97.9%) but lowered sensitivity (31.7%). When AUS was considered positive, the sensitivity marginally improved, but at the cost of specificity (96.5%) and PPV (92.9%). This aligns with international consensus confirming that TIS is designed to maximize specificity. Hence, we recommend considering AUS as negative in definitive treatment planning and positive in screening programs. The limitations of this study include the retrospective nature, with the inability to perform ancillary testing (immunocytochemistry/molecular) on cases retrospectively categorized as ND or AUS.

Conclusion

This study validates TIS as a standardized framework for reporting serous effusions with definite categorization. The stepwise progression of ROM from NFM to MAL categories provides clinicians with clear risk stratification for fluids reported under TIS. The system demonstrates high specificity and PPV, making it a robust tool for the detection of malignancy. The high ROM of SFM and MAL categories supports the use of serous fluid cytology as a definitive diagnostic modality in advanced malignancies. The aberrancy in ROM of ND and NFM emphasizes the importance of ancillary testing in the fluid sample.
