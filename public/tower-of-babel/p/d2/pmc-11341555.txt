A model for the hydrothermal tremor source of the Mefite d’Ansanto (Italy) CO2 non-volcanic emissions in the intermediate frequency band (1–15 Hz)
Authors: Simona Morabito, Paola Cusano, Lucia Nardone, Simona Petrosino
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In the Summer 2021 a seismic passive survey was carried out at Mefite d’Ansanto (Italy), well-known for the cold non-volcanic and lethal CO2 emissions. Mefite is located close to that part of Irpinia region hosting the large historical earthquakes’ faults, that generated the destructive magnitude 6.8 earthquake of 1980 and have been related to the CO2 leakage by the scientific community. The survey was conducted by installing a small short-period seismic array with the purpose of determining the wavefield features and detecting the possible signature of the regional stress variations. By analyzing the acquired data, we have determined the properties of the seismic wavefield associated with the emission vents, in the intermediate frequency band, 1–15 Hz, which was found to be composed of a stationary background component and an intermittent higher energy one. Basing on our results, considerations on the medium properties and the deep source available information, we have depicted a schematic model of the shallow seismic source: the background components are linked to the shallower activity of the hydrothermal system, e.g. the bubbling, while the intermittent ones are likely generated by the passage of the overpressured gas, trapped in the first-tens-meters’ layers, after overcoming the internal cohesion charge. The characteristics of the wavefield that we have defined, refer to a condition in which no regional earthquakes occurred and could be considered as a basis to identify possible significant variations linked to CO2 emissions and to the regional stress changes.

Introduction

The Mefite d’Ansanto is the largest area of non-volcanic low temperature CO2 emissions measured on the Earth1. It is set in the axial part of the southern Apennine chain (Fig. 1), characterized by an extreme structural complexity of the thrust belt2–4, between the Sannio and Irpinia seismogenic regions (Fig. 1). The activity of fault systems that affect these regions, some of which determined large historical earthquakes1,5,6, has shown a link with the presence of fluids7. The gasses emitted at Mefite are probably sourced from the mantle wedge beneath the Apennines along the Tyrrhenian side; they ascend through an interconnected network of fractures and faults and then they may be trapped in crustal pockets which feed the gas emissions at the surface1. Mefite is likely fed by the CO2 reservoir found at Mt. In the last decade the interest in the interplay between the background seismicity and the CO2 emissions at Mefite has grown and several seismological studies have been carried out. By using the polarization technique, Pischiutta et al.6 recognized that the seismic noise was significantly polarized in the horizontal plane with a N115° predominant trend in the gas emission area. To understand the link between fluid circulation and seismogenesis, in 2021 Morabito et al.9 performed a passive seismic experiment by deploying a seismic array that recorded the seismic noise. The results showed wavefield features very stable in time, with the components at frequencies > 5 Hz likely linked to the emission source. At the frequencies < 5 Hz, the seismic noise was made up by different contributions, of both endogenous and exogenous nature. The frequencies below 1 Hz were clearly correlated to meteorological parameters. The array data were also analyzed by La Rocca et al.10, which attributed the signal at frequency < 3 Hz mainly to a stationary source and the 3–20 Hz frequency band to an intermittent one, supposed to cause significant variations in the signal amplitude. In both the bands, those authors indicated a dominance of surface waves generated by very shallow sources. Panebianco et al.11 tested an automated machine learning technique in the 6–20 Hz frequency band to retrieve tremor signals.

The studies to date published have revealed interesting characteristics of the noise wavefield at Mefite d’Ansanto that motivate further investigations. At frequencies higher than 15 Hz the noise wavefield in proximity of the lake is clearly determined by Mefite emission dynamics9,10, but it attenuates very fast with the distance from the vents. Both, Morabito et al.9 and La Rocca et al.10 observed that this component is visible at about 50 m from the vents, but it is no longer observable at about 100 m. The noise wavefield below 1 Hz has shown a strong correlation with rainfall9. The intermediate frequency band, 1–15 Hz, that contains the most of the seismic energy in the area covered by the array9,10, has not yet been studied in details. The observations obtained so far indicate possible contributions from multiple sources, endogenous and exogenous. In this paper we investigate in detail these last aspects in order to track the main seismic sources in the 1–15 Hz frequency band and to delineate the underlying mechanisms. We estimate the Root Mean Square (RMS) of the signal amplitude, apply the Independent Component Analysis (ICA), the polarization technique and Single Station Spectral Ratios, and compare the results’ temporal patterns with those of meteorological parameters, to unravel the different contributions to the seismic signal.

Results

We carried out a series of analyses in both time and frequency domain, mainly using the seismic data continuously recorded by AME (Array of MEfite, Fig. 1), installed close to the Mefite d’Ansanto CO2 emission vents in the framework of FURTHER project (https://progetti.ingv.it/en/further), in the period 8 June–28 September 2021. Two array configurations, each composed of 7 Short Period three-component stations, were used (see Fig. 1 and Methods section for more details). The acquired dataset consists mainly of seismic noise and no regional earthquakes, linked to Irpinia or Sannio fault systems, were recorded.

In the 1–15 Hz band a stable amplitude level of the signal is recognized over the whole acquisition time, with several and persistent spectral components (PSD, Power Spectral Density, Fig. 2a–d). Often a higher amplitude signal overlaps, with a fast starting increase (on the order of a few tens of seconds), a main PSD content in the 7–15 Hz band (Fig. 2e), random inter-times and a rapid attenuation spatial pattern starting from the stations closer to the vents; due to this peculiar pattern we will refer to it as ‘intermittent wavefield’.Figure 2Examples of spectrograms (PSD) in the 0.7–15 Hz band for signals recorded by two AME stations’ vertical components: background wavefield at AME4 (a) and AME2 (b) in day-time; background wavefield at AME4 (c) and AME2 (d) in night-time; (e) AME4 in 0.7–25 Hz band in day-time. We analyzed the temporal pattern of the extracted ICs’ daily number, by performing a Changing Point analysis14 in the band 1–5.5 Hz. We found a general decrease with time, with a sharp decrease in mid-June, a smoother one in the first week of August and then it stabilizes (Fig. S1c). Such an observation can reflect an evolution of the dynamical system towards a less chaotic state15. It is noteworthy that the sharp decrease is coeval with meteorological variations (Fig. S1b-c and Table S1).

On the basis of PSD and ICA results, for the next analyses we considered the signals in following frequency bands: 1–2 Hz, the band associated with the wind turbines; 2–5 Hz, which is dominated by IC3 (see Fig. 3a); 5–7 Hz, which is the band of transition towards the peculiar spectral components of the intermittent wavefield.

The spectral components above 7 Hz will be investigated in detail in the ‘Intermittent wavefield’ section.

RMS amplitude and energy release

The seismic signal squared amplitude is proportional to the energy release, and, hence, the variations in the energy release can be revealed by the temporal pattern of RMS. The analysis of the seismic noise RMS, estimated over 1 h-long samples of raw signals, was calculated for each station of AME. Moreover, we estimated the RMS in the 2–5 Hz and 5–7 Hz frequency bands.

In general, the RMS values are higher at AME4, the station closest to the CO2 vents, and decrease with the station-vent distance (Fig. S2a), thus suggesting that the main wavefield components originate close to the CO2 emissions. A 24-h periodicity is also observed (Fig. S2b). Finally, considering all the array stations, we observed a decrease of the mean RMS values with station-vent distances (Fig. S2c), confirming an overall attenuative pattern of the wavefield starting from the lake.

The time evolution of the RMS amplitude is compared with the meteorological parameters’ pattern (Figs. S1b–c and S2b) evidencing a clear correlation, especially in the 2–5 Hz band. E.g., in this band the correlation with the wind speed is significant also at AME4 (75%), despite it is the station closer to the vents, and at AME0 (66%), even if it is located close to a road, which is likely the more contaminated site by anthropogenic factors in this band. For the other stations the correlation is: AME2 78%, AME3 85%, AME5 83%, AME6 80%, AM19 87%, and AM20 89%.

An overall increasing trend of RMS is also present, statistically evaluated by performing a Changing Point analysis at AME414. The results indicate four main significant changes (around mid-June, 5–7 and 16 July, and 8 September) that coincide with the main meteorological (temperature, pressure and rainfall) variations of the investigated period (Fig. S1b and Table S1), in agreement with the already observed correlation.

In particular, we observe:

(1) In 1–2 Hz band, most of the stations show more dispersed azimuthal distributions during day-time. In addition, at some sites, the average azimuth direction appears to change from day to night. Overall, this pattern could be related to the presence of anthropogenic noise, stronger during the day and coming from different directions. The two stations closer to the CO2 emissions (especially AME5) show almost the same prevailing azimuth directions pointing towards the CO2 field.

(2) In the 2–5 Hz band, the azimuth values are in general well-collimated, particularly during night-time, with a pretty common orientation towards the CO2 field. This behavior suggests that the main contribution to the noise wavefield is produced by the activity of the Mefite.

(3) In the 5–7 Hz band, the day/night variability is almost absent, suggesting a practically absent anthropogenic component.

Regarding the incidence angle, the obtained values are in the range 60–90° in the 1–2 Hz, 2–5 Hz and 5–7 Hz bands for all the sites, indicating sub-horizontal polarization of the noise wavefield (Fig. S5).

In order to investigate the eventual temporal variations of the Mefite activity, we focused on the time evolution of the hourly-averaged polarization parameters in the 2–5 Hz frequency band, considering the stations (AME0, AME2, AME3, AME4 and AME6) which operated almost continuously. Overall, the azimuth, the resultant length, the incidence angle and the rectilinearity time series show few fluctuations (Fig. 5 and Fig. S6). We performed a Changing Point analysis, considering the changing points common to the five selected stations. The results provide evidence of resultant length variations around mid- and late June as well as mid-September 2021 (Fig. 5 and Table S2). Past studies have provided evidences that the seismic signal is very sensitive to the input of meteoric water, which often induce a delayed hydrological response in terms of seismic velocity variations16, increase of the seismic activity17,18, noise depolarization19. In particular, the variations of the resultant length indicating the polarization/depolarization of the seismic noise have proved to be a powerful tool to detect fluid migration in the shallow crustal structure, providing a technique to monitor fluid-driven processes19,20. This motivated us to compare the observed changes of the resultant length with the rainfall amount and the soil moisture. As shown in Fig. 5, the depolarization of seismic noise (resultant length decrease) follows the major rainfalls and the consequent increase of ground water content of June and late August/first decade of September 2021. A correlation analysis21 of the daily sampled time series of the soil moisture and resultant length shows that the two variables are anticorrelated above the 95% confidence level (Fig. S7). The corresponding p-value results < 0.05, indicating that there is less than 5% of probability that the correlation occurs by chance. The maximum of the cross-correlation function occurs at lags (5–8), which corresponds to a delay of 5–8 days. Such observations lead us to hypothesize that the depolarization of the noise represents the delayed response of the ground to the increase of meteoric water in the soil.Figure 5Temporal pattern of the polarization parameters (from top to bottom, azimuth, incidence angle, rectilinearity, resultant length in the 2–5 Hz frequency band for AME4 station. Intermittent wavefield

The intermittent wavefield sometimes appears as continuous signals lasting several tens of minutes, sometimes as brief transients. In general, the inter-times between two consecutive intermittent wavetrains are not regular. To better characterize these signals, we selected, by visual inspection, some seismic traces in which the intermittent amplitude peculiarities unequivocally dominate or are completely absent (19 and 16 samples, respectively). Hereinafter we will refer to the samples that do not contain intermittent signal as ‘background’. The selection criteria are based on the intermittent wavefield peculiarities. We chose seismic traces characterized by more than 30 minutes of intermittent or background wavefield. The amplitude of the intermittent wavefield is much higher (typically almost twice) than that of the background and the transition background-intermittent wavefield is easily detectable by observing this evident and fast amplitude increase (see Fig. 2e), as well as the activation of the main PSD components of the intermittent wavefield in the 7–15 Hz band.

Independent component analysis

The ICA was separately applied to the low energy background and to the higher energy intermittent selected traces to ensure the stationarity in the analyzed time-windows. All the seismic traces were filtered between 0.7 and 15 Hz. The ICA shows significant differences between the background wavefield and the intermittent one. In the case of the background wavefield samples, ICA extracts several independent signals whose spectral content spans the whole 0.7–15 Hz band, with IC3 always well identified (Fig. 3e). For the intermittent wavefield the ICs’ spectral content tends to be less dispersed inside three sub-bands (Fig. 3f): 2–5 Hz (IC3), 7–9 Hz (ICa) and 11–14 Hz (ICb). In this latter case the dynamics of the system is defined by a lower number of ICs, with a few eigenvalues of the seismic signal covariance matrix that determine the 60–80% of the total dynamics of the system12,22, in comparison to the former. We deduced that when the signal amplitude is higher the system is less chaotic15, with the dominance of a few well defined sources.

In both the cases, we observe a decrease over time of maximum frequency extracted values (Fig. 3e–f).

The amplitude increase of the intermittent seismic signals, observed through the visual inspection, finds quantitative support in the pattern of the energy release at AME4 station. In fact, such signals correspond to the outliers exceeding the 90th percentile level23 of the RMS distribution in the 10–15 Hz band, which is dominated by ICb (see Figs. 2d and 3f) and, hence, the most part of the intermittent wavefield energy.

Polarization analysis

Considering the rapid attenuation with distance of the intermittent signal, we performed the polarization analysis of intermittent signal samples recorded at AME4 and AME5. The 10–15 Hz frequency band at AME4 shows (Fig. 6a) that in correspondence with the amplitude increase, the azimuth is less scattered and switches from about 60° (63+/−40) to about 20° (18+/−7), pointing towards the CO2 emissions. AME5 shows a persistent azimuth value, around 80°, compatible with Mefite main vents’ direction, and a superficial incidence angle, for both the wavefields. The 7–10 Hz band, dominated by the ICa, has characteristics similar to the 10–15 Hz one, but it is less energetic and affected by higher uncertainties.

Single station spectral ratios

To highlight possible amplification effects and to extract the Rayleigh wave ellipticity from an ambient wavefield of unknown composition, we applied to the selected signals two spectral ratio methods, HVSR and HVTFA, implemented as modules in the GEOPSY software (www.geopsy.org, last access 20 May 2024).

In Fig. 7 we compared the results of the standard HVSR and the ellipticity HVTFA processing for the background (blue line) and intermittent (red line) noise. In general, both HVSR and ellipticity curves have similar shapes at frequency lower than 1 Hz, showing a frequency peak centered at 0.7–0.8 Hz. When the ellipticity curve is very close to the HVSR, there is predominance of Rayleigh waves in the measured wavefield24. No variation between background and intermittent noise is observed for this peak. In the 1–10 Hz frequency band a greater difference, particularly for the stations far from the main vents, in the shape of the curves is observed. On the other hand, if the ellipticity amplitude is much lower than the HVSR curve, then Love waves or body waves have high influence. Based on this assumption, and taking into account that the noise sources are located near the surface, our results highlight that the wavefield is dominantly composed of Rayleigh waves. The relative proportion between surface and body waves on the measured wavefield is controlled by noise source distribution but also by soil properties24. Thus, in presence of strong velocity contrasts and with nearby sources, the noise wavefield is dominated by surface waves.Figure 7HVSR and ellipticity curves with relative standard deviation (dashed lines for H/V and vertical error bars for ellipticity).

A different pattern of the spectral ratios of intermittent and background noise is observed in the 1–8 Hz frequency band. In some cases, i.e. AME5 and AME2, the shape of the ratios is the same, except for the amplitude: the higher the noise level, the higher the amplitude spectral ratios (Fig. 7). A possible explanation could be related to the activation of a second superficial source25, with an intermittent behavior. In other case, instead, i.e. AME3, the spectral ratios exhibit two distinct peaks between the background and intermittent noise. In this case the energy jumps to the higher mode peak, centered at 4.5 Hz (red lines in Figure 7, that correspond to the intermittent noise ratio). This is in agreement with the IC4 drumming pattern observed in the ICA, and links this component to propagation effects in high energy noise conditions. The 4.5 Hz peak becomes dominant compared to the fundamental mode peak, centered at 2 Hz (IC2) (blue lines in Figure 7, that correspond to the background noise ratios). Possibly this substantial variation is not related to a structural change in the subsoil layers, but to a local heterogeneity and to the presence of a high velocity contrast that make the first higher mode ellipticity peak more evident26. Therefore, the activation of a shallow energetic source, combined with a high velocity contrast and a local heterogeneity, can significantly affect the shape of the spectral ratios (HVSR and HVTFA).

Discussion

The seismic noise wavefield emitted by the Mefite d’Ansanto hydrothermal system in the intermediate frequency band (1–15 Hz) shows two well distinct behaviors, one stationary and one intermittent.

The stationary, or background, wavefield, characterized by a general stable low amplitude/energy level and an overall attenuation starting from the CO2 vents, manifests a wide range of spectral components with properties stable in time.

The ICA has returned a main persistent signal, IC3, with a spectral content in 2.4–3.3 Hz. Even if this signal is not the most energetic among that sourced by the CO2 vents9,10, it is always extracted, regardless of the operating stations and the environmental conditions. IC3 shows a temporal distribution over the 24h which is compatible with a main stationary source and the superposition of secondary sources, slightly more intense in day-time. These observations are in agreement with the results of La Rocca et al.10, which found this signal more coherent in night-time, when the wavefield clearly travels from the vents, and attributed the day-time incoherence to anthropogenic sources. We do not exclude also the influence of meteorological factors, such as the wind27,28. E.g., the signal could be influenced by the direct action of the wind on the ground27, or the array could have detected the indirect effect of the wind which acts on the CO2 emissions at the surface causing the gas to flow along a narrow valley and producing a persistent gas river1,29. Other phenomena linked to meteorological factors are possible. E.g., the surficial water temperature of the lake could respond to air temperature and solar radiation, favoring the evaporation in the hotter hours of the day, reducing streamflow so that the daily changes in water surface temperature modulate the bubble collapse30.

Instead, in this band we exclude a link with the wind parks present in the zone9. Panebianco et al.11 associated the wind turbine's noise with a mono-frequency peak around 1.5 Hz, which fits the characteristics of IC1 signal (1.3–1.5 Hz band). Anyway, the existence of a weak contribution of the emission source in the IC1 frequency band is indicated by IC1 polarization at the stations closer to the lake, that points towards the vents (Fig. S3). The IC3 dominates over IC2 (2–2.3 Hz) even if the area explored by the array presents a general site amplification around 2 Hz (Fig. 7). Summarizing these considerations, the 2–5 Hz band is dominated by IC3. The azimuth estimated with polarization analysis in 2–5 Hz band clearly indicates a well-collimated orientation towards the CO2 emission field, especially at night-time, confirming that the main noise wavefield in this band is sourced underneath Mefite lake. Inside the uncertainties’ limits, most of the azimuth direction points stably towards the lake and the incident angle of the polarization vector shows values in the range 60–90° for all the stations, indicating a sub-horizontal polarization.

The polarization results for the whole continuous wavefield show a wide variability that, jointly with the complexity of its spectral content, likely reflects a wide range of possible source processes. As claimed by La Rocca et al.10, the source of the seismic noise generated by CO2 emissions can be linked to the fluid discharge throughout many conduits, evidenced by the presence of several vents in the emission area. Some of these vents show bubbling, some others emit a gas flux accompanied by a characteristic sound, some others seem inactive. Therefore, several different processes, such as different flow velocities between the gas and liquid components near the surface10 or breaking or resonance of the gas bubbles28, can contribute to the wavefield.

The intermittent wavefield superimposes on the background, with an amplitude level consistently higher (see Figs. 2e and upper panel of 6a), a fast onset and a main spectral content above 7 Hz. The occurrence of the switches between the background and the intermittent behaviors, appears random and can occur more times within 1-hour intervals, as well as it could not take place within longer intervals. To better highlight the differences between the two behaviors, we analyzed long-noise intervals in which one of the two behaviors is dominant. ICA mainly extracts three distinct independent signals: IC3, ICa, with a spectrum peaked in 7–9 Hz, and ICb, peaked in 11–14 Hz. As peculiar frequency bands for Mefite intermediate hydrothermal noise, we focused on 2–5 Hz, which is dominated by IC3 (see Fig. 3a), and 10–15 Hz, which is dominated by ICb (see Figs. 3f and 2d). ICa is less energetic than ICb (see Fig. 2d) and, even if it has characteristics similar to ICb, it is affected by higher uncertainties. At almost all the stations, in correspondence of the intermittent tremor insurgence, the incidence angle and the azimuth, even if widely scattered, show a net switch between two distinct propagating patterns. In particular, for the 10–15 Hz band the azimuth at AME4 switches from about 60° to about 20° (pointing towards the emissions) and the incidence angle varies from about 80° for the background signal to about 70° for the intermittent wavefield (Fig. 6a). AME5 shows a persistent polarization roughly compatible with Mefite main vents.

The switches occur simultaneously in the two bands. The background signal indicates a very shallow propagation, while the intermittent one seems to originate from a deeper source. For the background, in the 2–5 Hz band almost all the stations show an azimuth distribution compatible with the lake surface, but for each individual station the mean propagation direction points toward different sectors of the lake and, in some cases, with a wide uncertainty (Fig. 4). Differently, the intermittent wavefield in this band seems to show a better defined propagation direction. We tried to carry out a rough estimation of the depth variation between the two sources, by considering the background signal generated at the lake surface (about 660 m a.s.l.). The 2–5 Hz band is suitable for this calculation since it is the least affected by attenuation. Moreover, only below 10 Hz the signal is coherent across AME stations10. We excluded the central array stations from the calculation. Indeed, at these stations the Spectral Ratio analyses indicate an energy jump to a higher mode peak around 4.5 Hz in correspondence of the high energy intermittent noise (Fig. 7), possibly linked to a local heterogeneity and a high velocity contrast, that can cause distortions in the polarization estimates. Moreover, AME7, AM19 and AME0 seem strongly affected by some local heterogeneities/local sources (i.e. an artificial well located between AM19 and AME0). For AME4, AME5 and AME6 we calculated the straight line with the associated incidence angle and azimuth estimated for the intermittent wavefield and passing through the station. Then, we estimated the source position as the intersection among the three estimated straight lines (Fig. 4). We found a source depth on the order of 15–25 m, below the lake surface.

Also in 10–15 Hz the polarization estimates indicate a source better constrained for the intermittent wavefield. For AME4 station the azimuth passes from 63°+/−40° for the background to about 18°+/−7° (towards the CO2 emissions), and from the incidence angle value of 77°+/−8° to 69°+/−4°. The agreement between the incident angle estimated at 10–15 Hz and 2–5 Hz (70°+/−9°) for the intermittent wavefield, lets us suppose a source depth compatible for the two frequency bands.

The switches between the two well distinct patterns of incidence angle likely reflect the existence of two key depths at which the dynamics of the Mefite shallow hydrothermal system works: the shallow one is very surficial; the deeper one is located at about 15–25 m below the lake surface.

To have a rough idea of the time evolution of the intermittent source depth, we look at the incidence angle at AME4 in 2–5 Hz distribution, over 15-day intervals. The distribution shows two well distinct peaks, relative to the surficial and deep source. The difference between the two incidence angle peaks is more pronounced in June (87° and 65°, respectively) and in the first half of July. Subsequently, the deeper angle values gradually tend to be more surficial (maximum values of 88° and 74° respectively), and then deepen again in the second half of September. This temporal pattern strongly reflects the long-term pattern of the resultant length (Fig. 5), which, in turn, exhibits a clear link with the rainfall. To shed light on the link between the estimated seismic long-term patterns and the rainfall, we carried out an investigation of the possible local groundwater indicators. During the three years of investigations performed at Mefite9,31, we observed that the quantity of water inside the lake presents a seasonal variation. Several interviewed eyewitnesses confirmed a marked seasonality of the water amount within the lake, with an evident higher water quantity in the wet season (October-April/May) with respect to the dry season (May-September). According to Ortolani et al.32 and Martinelli et al.33 the Mefite d’Ansanto area is interested by two main groundwater circuits, a deep one, with a maximum depth of about 100 m from the surface (Fig. 8b), and a shallow one, with a depth of a few meters below the surface. This second circuit includes very shallow lenticular aquifers. We carried out some measures of the water level inside some private wells in a restricted area around the emissions (Fig. 1), by using a freatimeter (error +/−0.05 cm), in two distinct periods of the year, as an indicator on which of the two groundwater circuits could feed the Mefite lake. We performed a measure on 11 May 2023 (maximum of water table charge) and another on 11 October 2023. As shown in Table 1, the depth of water table inside the sampled wells is very shallow and shows a water table depth higher in October, at the end of the dry season, with respect to May, when the theoretical maximum of aquifer recharge is reached, at the end of the wet season. Moreover, the geochemical measurements performed at the lake34,35 show a water pH typical of surficial aquifers (pH < 3, unlike the pH measured inside the water of the artificial well near AME0 which shows value around 7 32). This evidence lets us suppose that the Mefite lake is fed by one or more shallow aquifers, which are heavily influenced by meteoric waters, and it is reasonable to suppose that the rising gasses do not drag a crucial amount of deep aquifer water. The investigations carried out so far (started at least from the early 80s) on the CO2 emissions at Mefite d’Ansanto show no significant change over time of the gas emission features1,35–38. In 2005, Chiodini et al.1 measured a total gas flux of ∼2000 tons per day with a fraction of 98 vol% of CO2, and the following measure gave compatible values35. So far, reports of exceptional gas emissions have only been found in historical documents39. Moreover, the measured CO2 flux is on the same order of the fluxes recorded at the Campania volcanoes1 with which Mefite emission shares the same large scale (tens of kilometers) of the high depth dynamics7,40,41. On the basis of all these clues, it is reasonable to suppose that in recent years the high depth CO2 source behaves in a stationary state, with the possibility of generating strong emissions only in exceptional cases. It is reasonable to hypothesize that the temporal variations in seismic noise that we have observed in the summer of 2021 are mostly caused by the interplay among the CO2 emissions, the dynamics of the shallow groundwater circuit, which in turn is strongly influenced by rainfall, and the local shallower ground layers’ features.

In the area surrounding Mefite d’Ansanto, flysch deposits belonging to Lagonegrese Unit crop out. For their depositional and tectonic history, flysch units are characterized by lithological heterogeneity, consisting of alternating rock (highly deformed or completely disarranged) and silty-clayey or clayey layers, and affected by a network of discontinuities. According to a deep well, logged about 500 m far from Mefite emission site42, the uppermost 80 m of the encountered stratigraphic succession mainly consist of clayey deposits (the presence of water is reported between 80 and 85 m depth within the underlying fractured carbonates) (Fig. 8b). Clayey flysch deposits are marked by sharp spatial changes in hydraulic conductivity, which are determined by a network of discontinuities and by changes in material properties even within short distances43. The behavior of the clayey flysch deposits is strongly affected by the water content, with a permeability that widely varies, also expressible in terms of the plasticity index, which in turn is strongly conditioned by the water content (dry and wet conditions). The scenario is similar to mud volcano systems, in which the gas produced at high depth can find high permeability areas serving as preferential pathways for gas to emerge at surface, otherwise it remains trapped in subsurface strata formed by low density clays combined with lithic fragments. In this last case, the pore fluid pressure increases, due to the continuous gas refill, until it overcomes the lithostatic confining charge and the gas can escape at the surface along fractures or conduits44–46. We hypothesize a similar mechanism for the gas route, which generates the seismicity at Mefite d’Ansanto.

Based on our analyses results, the considerations on the ground chemical/physical properties and the stationarity of the high depth source, we depicted a schematic model (Fig. 8a) to describe the hydrothermal seismic tremor behavior in the intermediate frequency band (1–15 Hz). The gas sourced at high depth rises through fractures and faults1, up to shallow strata underneath the lake area. In its pathways, if the gas encounters one or more surficial aquifers, it can drag some water at the surface. When the gas encounters layers of lesser strength, open conduct emissions verify and generate the background seismic wavefield, in correspondence of the various vents. If the gas has encountered a shallow aquifer, the emission manifests as bubbling, otherwise the emissions are mainly dry. When the gas encounters layers of higher strength, it accumulates, and gradually undergoes overpressure. When the internal pressure overcomes the lithostatic confining charge, the gas suddenly permeates through the flysch, creating ephemeral pathways, and emerges at the surface causing the noise amplitude to rapidly increase, generating the intermittent wavefield. The creation of the ephemeral conduits could be favored by the presence of weak layers. Progressively the gas pressure decreases, the ephemeral conduits tend to close, and the cycle restarts.

The variation of the seismic noise properties in response to meteorological changes could reasonably be linked to the variation of shallow strata chemical/physical properties varying dry/wet conditions. Roberts et al.47 highlighted that seasonal increase in the water saturation of outcropping rocks will decrease the relative permeability of rocks in the vadose zone to CO2 gas and increases the interaction of gas with groundwaters (giving rise to bubbling water seeps) when groundwater depth becomes shallower due to high rainfall. Likely, the zones of higher strength are roughly located around 15–25 m of depth. Dry conditions can increase the rock permeability, favoring the passage of the gas toward the surface. In this situation the gas would rise more easily and could be trapped at shallower depths, where the buildup of the overpressure would start. As a consequence, the source of the intermittent wavefield would be shallower, as it happens in August. On the other side, in wetter conditions, the gas encounters rocks with reduced permeability, and could be trapped at deeper depths, as we observe in June.

Conclusions

Currently, the Mefite d’Ansanto hydrothermal system behaves in a stationary state, whose variations, reflected in seismicity (noise) changes, are determined by the surficial groundwater patterns, which in turn are strongly influenced by meteoric waters, and by the interplay between the high depth source of CO2 (indicatively stationary) and the chemical/physical properties of surficial layers. The knowledge of this stationary mechanism could be an essential key for the identification of possible variations of CO2 emissions linked to critical regional stress changes7. Within this scenario, the monitoring of geophysical, geochemical and environmental parameters plays an essential role in the hazard evaluation linked to the occurrence of strong earthquakes that hit the Irpinia region.

A limitation of our analysis lies in the use of only some samples of high energy noise intermittent wavefield, derived from a visual catalog. An improvement to this method could derive from the use of machine learning approaches as done in Falanga et al.48 or Panebianco et al.11. However, our results claim the importance of the link between seismic noise and meteorological parameters and highlight the importance of monitoring these factors.

Methods

Dataset: Seismic array and meteorological data

The dataset consists of seismic noise continuous signals recorded by AME (Array of MEfite) that was employed close to the Mefite d’Ansanto emission field (Fig. 1). We mainly used two configurations of 7 three-component stations, with a sampling rate of 100 sps and equipped with 1 Hz Lennartz 3D-Lite sensors. The first configuration was designed to exploit the available space and operated from 8 June to 24 August 2021 (red and purple triangles in Figure 1). On 24 August three stations were moved to form the second configuration (blue and purple triangles in Figure 1) which was able to better explore a higher frequency band (2.8–8 Hz). AM21 station acquired only a few data and, hence, was not used for this study. The array was uninstalled on 28 September 2021. A complete description of the acquisition survey is reported in Morabito et al.9.

Meteorological time series of air temperature, atmospheric pressure, wind speed and rainfall are recorded by Montemarano station, located about 13 km from Mefite emissions. Soil moisture time series are available for a station located in Pietradefusi, at a distance of about 23 km. Data are accessible at www.agricoltura.regione.campania.it/meteo/agrometeo.htm (Last access 25 June 2024).

Independent component analysis

ICA is an entropy-based decorrelation technique working in the time domain and aimed to identify underlying statistical independent sources from multivariate statistical data12. It has been successfully applied to a multiplicity of experimental areas such as acoustics, volcano seismology and thermometry ambit14,49,50. The sources can be detected by using fourth-order statistics, based on the Central Limit Theorem, according to which “given two independent random variables, the distribution of their sum is close to a Gaussian, more than the distribution of either of the variables”. Hence, one independent component (IC) of the signal can be found maximizing its non-Gaussianity. The ICA model presumes to have m recorded signals, x, as a linear superposition of n mutually independent unknown sources s. The superposition is expressed as a matrix (A) due to path, noise, instrumental transfer-functions, etc., such as x = As. Under these hypotheses, ICA provides a separating matrix W = A−1, in such a way that the vector IC = Wx is an estimation of the original independent sources, IC ∼ s. In this framework, seismic recorded signals are seen as linear representation of non-Gaussian sources and the extracted components are as independent as possible. In our analysis we applied the fixed-point FastICA algorithm12 for the estimation of the independent source signals (ICs). The algorithm evaluates the independence of the sources by approximate the negentropy matrix (JG) through the equation JG(w) = E{G(wTx)}-E{G(υ)}, where G is a suitable non-quadratic contrast function (i.e. a cubic power law), w is an m-dimensional vector constituting one column of the weight matrix W, E is the expectation value so that E{wTx} = 1, υ is a Gaussian random variable. The ICs are obtained from the estimation of weight vectors w1, . . ., wn, by maximizing JG.

We applied the ICA technique according to the procedure described in Cusano et al.22. The mixture matrices are composed of noise signal time-windows recorded simultaneously by all the operating stations. The analyzed time-windows are sampled over the entire acquisition interval, from 9 June to 28 September 2021, so that a dependence on the specific hour of the day or on the geometrical configuration of the stations are avoided. We fixed two starting times a day. The first one slides along the 24 hours of each day, according to a uniform distribution. The second one starts 12 h after the first. The time-windows duration is fixed at 15 s of contemporaneous seismic traces at all the three-components of stations.

We ensured that the signals inside the sampled time-windows were stationary so the corresponding sources’ statistical properties do not vary inside the time-windows themselves. We obtained about 230 time-windows of noise recordings.

Root mean square (RMS)

We computed the RMS over a 1-h-long time window with no overlap and averaged over the three components of motion for each station of the array22 for the considered time interval. To highlight the temporal characteristics of the noise we calculated the RMS of the unfiltered (raw) and the filtered signal in selected frequency bands.

Spectral ratios

To extract the Rayleigh wave ellipticity from an ambient wavefield of unknown composition and for site effects evaluation, several techniques have been developed over the last few decades54–56. The Horizontal to Vertical Spectral Ratio technique, known as HVSR method, was proposed by Nakamura55 and is applied to ambient noise recorded by a three component seismic station. Nakamura55,57 states that the spectral ratio between the horizontal and vertical components reflects the S-wave resonance in the surface sediments, and hence that the HVSR curve provides an estimate of the resonance frequency and amplification of ground motions influenced by a surface layer. Although this method is based on little theoretical foundation, it has found widespread use due to its ease of use.

The HVTFA method (H/V using time frequency analysis57) was originally proposed by Kristekova58 and uses a Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) based on modified Morlet wavelets57 to transform the three signal component into the time-frequency domain. To extract mostly Rayleigh waves, the absolute value of the CWT for the vertical component is scanned for all maxima59. Love or SH waves that contain horizontal energy only are thus effectively excluded from further consideration. For each maximum on the vertical component, the corresponding maximum value on the horizontal components with a phase shift of ± π/2 is identified and used to calculate an ellipticity value. All values derived for a given frequency are analyzed statistically via filtering of histograms54.

HVSR curves in the 0.5–15 Hz frequency range have been obtained using a 60-s sliding window with 5% overlap, and smoothed with Konno and Ohmachi smoothing algorithm60. HVTFA method requires two input parameters, the Morlet wavelet parameter, that controls the wavelet’s width in the spectral domain, and the number of maxima on the vertical component selected per minute (nppm). The parameter nppm controls the energy maxima distributed over the signal. Indeed, specific time windows with higher amplitudes can be affected by temporary near sources with particular spectral properties and nppm ensures that the obtained statistics better represents the average characteristics of all data54. Based on the study reported by Fäh et al.54, we selected a value of 8 for the Morlet wavelet parameter and chose 2 maxima per minute.
