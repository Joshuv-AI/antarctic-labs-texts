Robot-assisted versus conventional unicompartmental knee arthroplasty: a retrospective comparative study of postoperative complications
Authors: Waldo Gonzalez, Juan Pablo Riquelme, Josefina Arellano, Daniela Landea, Francisco Figueroa, Rafael Calvo, David Figueroa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) is an established procedure for selected patients with unicompartmental osteoarthritis. Robotic-assisted UKA (RA-UKA) has been introduced to improve accuracy of implant positioning. However, its clinical advantage over conventional UKA remains uncertain. This study aimed to compare postoperative complications between robotic-assisted and conventional techniques.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients who underwent primary UKA between 2012 and 2022, with ≥ 2 years of follow-up. Group allocation was determined by calendar period and robotic platform availability: conventional UKA was performed before adoption of the robotic system, whereas robotic-assisted UKA (RA-UKA) was performed after its introduction in 2018. Demographic, surgical, and complication data were analyzed. Due to the small number of events, statistical comparisons are exploratory and underpowered.ResultsA total of 208 patients underwent UKA, of whom 190 (91.3%) were available at follow-up. The mean age was 65.7 years (SD 10.7), and 52.6% were women. Eighty-four procedures (44.3%) were RA-UKA. Ten complications (5.2%) occurred; arthrofibrosis was the most frequent complication (n = 2), followed by deep vein thrombosis (n = 1). One intraoperative periprosthetic fracture was observed in the conventional group. Complication rates were 1.2% in RA-UKA versus 8.5% in conventional UKA (p = 0.29), but this difference did not reach statistical significance. Notably, comorbidities were more prevalent in the RA-UKA group (50% vs. 7.6%).ConclusionUKA demonstrated a low complication rate at two-year follow-up. While fewer events were observed after RA-UKA, this study is underpowered and no statistically significant differences were found. Background

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) is an effective and safe procedure. Lower rates of complications and mortality, better preservation of the knee’s natural motion, and faster recovery have been reported with UKA compared to total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [1–4]. However, registry studies show that TKA has lower revision rates than UKA [5–7]. The main causes of revision in UKA include aseptic loosening, malalignment, progression of osteoarthritis, instability, infection, and periprosthetic fractures [5–7]. In addition to revision-related endpoints, clinically relevant postoperative complications include arthrofibrosis, deep vein thrombosis, wound and soft-tissue complications, and infection, all of which may affect postoperative recovery even when implant survival is preserved.

Recent studies have reported favorable mid- to long-term survivorship for both conventional and robotic-assisted fixed-bearing unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, with some robotic-assisted series reporting survivorship above 90% at long-term follow-up [8–10]. Recent literature has consistently shown that robotic-assisted UKA may improve implant positioning accuracy, reduce alignment outliers, and optimize intraoperative balancing compared with conventional techniques [11–15]. In addition, recent studies have reported favorable mid- to long-term survivorship after robotic-assisted UKA, with some series demonstrating lower revision and aseptic loosening rates compared with conventional techniques [16–18].

However, whether these technical advantages translate into fewer postoperative complications remains controversial. While systematic reviews and meta-analyses have suggested potential benefits of robotic-assisted UKA in alignment, revision rates, and selected functional outcomes, they have not consistently demonstrated differences in short-term complication profiles between techniques [17, 20].

In addition, most available studies primarily focus on implant positioning, revision rates, survivorship, or functional outcomes, while comparatively limited evidence specifically evaluates routine postoperative complications such as arthrofibrosis, thromboembolic events, infection, wound complications, and early reinterventions. This distinction is clinically relevant because these complications may significantly affect postoperative recovery even when implant survival is preserved.

Therefore, despite the increasing adoption of robotic-assisted UKA, important uncertainty remains regarding its real-world impact on postoperative complications. This gap is particularly relevant in transition settings where institutions adopt robotic platforms after years of conventional UKA experience, which reflects common real-world practice patterns.

Although robotic-assisted UKA has been increasingly adopted, its clinical impact on routine postoperative complications remains incompletely defined, particularly in real-world transition settings. Our institutional cohort is relevant because it reflects adoption of robotic-assisted UKA within a single center using experienced surgeons, a consistent implant platform, and standardized perioperative care, allowing a clinically grounded comparison of complication patterns between conventional and robotic-assisted techniques.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the incidence and nature of postoperative complications in patients who underwent unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, comparing those treated with robotic-assisted and conventional techniques. We hypothesized that robotic-assisted UKA would demonstrate a similar overall safety profile compared with conventional UKA, while potentially reducing selected technical complications.

Materials and methods

This was a retrospective cohort study including all patients who underwent primary unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) at our institution between January 2012 and December 2022. UKA was indicated in patients with symptomatic unicompartmental osteoarthritis refractory to nonoperative treatment, with clinical findings and weight-bearing radiographs consistent with isolated disease in a single tibiofemoral compartment. Eligibility required preservation of the contralateral tibiofemoral compartment and absence of advanced patellofemoral degeneration considered clinically relevant by the treating surgeon.

Patients were considered candidates for UKA when they had correctable coronal deformity, preserved knee range of motion, and functionally competent ligamentous stabilizers. Exclusion criteria were: (1) less than two years of follow-up (2), incomplete clinical or radiographic data (3), flexion contracture > 10° (4), diagnosis of inflammatory arthritis, and (5) multicompartmental osteoarthritis or other findings considered incompatible with UKA indication, such as severe patellofemoral compromise, fixed deformity, or major ligament insufficiency.

Conventional UKA procedures were performed between 2012 and 2018, prior to the availability of a robotic platform at our institution. From 2018 onward, robotic-assisted UKA (RA-UKA) was systematically performed using the MAKO® system. Therefore, treatment allocation was determined by calendar period and equipment availability rather than randomization, introducing an inherent temporal/adoption bias related to the non-concurrent cohort design.

Surgeries were performed by experienced knee surgeons (10 + years of experience). Patients underwent general anesthesia with inhaled and intravenous anesthetic agents, and a tourniquet was used to facilitate a bloodless surgical field. All unicompartmental knee prostheses included in the study were cemented, fixed-bearing implants. The same implant system was used throughout the study period: the RESTORIS MCK Medial or Lateral Onlay Partial Knee Arthroplasty system (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA). This implant represented the standard UKA platform used at our institution during the study period, thereby reducing variability related to implant-related factors across both conventional and robotic-assisted procedures.

All patients were rehabilitated by physical therapists at our institution according to the same standardized protocol. Rehabilitation was initiated immediately after surgery with early mobilization, assisted ambulation, pain control, and progressive recovery of knee range of motion. Crutch-assisted weight-bearing was allowed immediately as tolerated, and walking aids were generally maintained during the first two postoperative weeks according to patient tolerance and clinical progress.

The rehabilitation program focused on edema control, gait training, progressive restoration of extension and flexion, quadriceps activation, and recovery of functional independence for daily activities. Progression to more advanced strengthening and functional exercises was supervised during outpatient follow-up according to individual recovery. Clinical follow-up was scheduled at 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 2 months, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years.

Postoperative thromboprophylaxis consisted of Rivaroxaban 10 mg once daily for 3 weeks according to our institutional protocol.

Complications

Postoperative complications were defined as any adverse event occurring within 2 years after surgery that required additional evaluation, medical treatment, procedural intervention, hospital readmission, or modification of the standard postoperative course. Both major and minor complications were included, including events managed on an outpatient basis when clearly documented in the medical record. For descriptive purposes, complications were also stratified according to time of occurrence as early complications when they occurred within 90 days after surgery and mid-term complications when they occurred after 90 days and within the 2-year follow-up period.

Complications were identified through retrospective review of outpatient and inpatient clinical records, operative reports, postoperative follow-up notes, imaging reports, and documented reinterventions or readmissions within the institutional medical record system. Postoperative radiographs obtained at routine follow-up visits were reviewed retrospectively by the treating team as part of the institutional record. Radiographic findings of interest included progressive radiolucent lines, component migration or loosening, periprosthetic fracture, and evident changes in implant alignment. These findings were not interpreted in isolation but in conjunction with clinical symptoms, follow-up notes, and any subsequent intervention. Formal blinded independent review and interobserver agreement testing were not performed.

For descriptive purposes, complications were categorized as major when they required reoperation, invasive treatment, hospital admission, or had the potential to compromise implant survival, and as minor when they were managed nonoperatively without major deviation from the intended recovery pathway. Infection-related complications were classified retrospectively based on the treating team’s documentation, clinical signs, laboratory and microbiological findings when available, and the need for antibiotic and/or surgical treatment. Deep vein thrombosis was recorded when clinically suspected and confirmed by vascular imaging; routine screening was not performed.

RA-UKA operative technique

The robotic-assisted procedures were performed using the MAKO® system (Stryker, MI, USA) following a standardized workflow. Preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans of the hip, knee, and ankle were obtained using the UKA application platform to create a patient-specific three-dimensional model. This model was used for preoperative planning of component size, implant position, and alignment.

After surgical exposure, anatomical landmarks were registered intraoperatively to match the patient’s anatomy with the preoperative CT-based model. The robotic system was then calibrated, and real-time assessment of limb alignment, implant position, and gap balance was performed throughout the range of motion. The preoperative plan could be adjusted intraoperatively according to ligament balance and implant tracking.

Bone preparation was performed using the robotic arm-assisted haptic guidance system, which constrained resection within the planned boundaries. Trial components were inserted to confirm stability, alignment, and range of motion before definitive implantation. Final components were cemented fixed-bearing implants, inserted according to the finalized plan after bone preparation and lavage. Standard wound closure and postoperative management were then performed as described above.

Conventional UKA operative technique

Conventional unicompartmental knee arthroplasty was performed using standard manual instrumentation through an approach to the affected compartment. After surgical exposure, femoral and tibial bone preparation was performed according to the implant manufacturer’s technique, using conventional alignment guides and intraoperative anatomical references. Implant size and position were determined intraoperatively based on bone cuts, compartment anatomy, and soft-tissue balance.

Trial components were inserted to assess stability, alignment, tracking, and range of motion before definitive implantation. Final components were cemented fixed-bearing implants, inserted after final preparation of the bone surfaces and lavage. Standard wound closure and the same postoperative rehabilitation and thromboprophylaxis protocols were used in both groups.

The operative principles described above applied to both medial and lateral UKA cases; however, the surgical approach and soft-tissue management were adapted according to the compartment involved. Medial UKA was performed through the standard compartment-oriented exposure used at our institution. In lateral UKA cases, exposure and balancing were modified according to the lateral compartment anatomy and surgeon preference, with specific attention to patellar tracking, lateral soft-tissue tension, and implant positioning. Because this was a retrospective study, a fully standardized compartment-specific technical description was not available for all cases; however, all procedures were performed by experienced knee surgeons using the same implant platform and the same general perioperative protocol.

Data analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, as appropriate, and categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Given the low number of complication events, the analysis was planned and reported primarily as descriptive. Comparisons between robotic-assisted and conventional UKA were considered exploratory only, and no multivariable modeling was used for inferential purposes. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as descriptive patterns rather than evidence of association or causality. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 19.0.

Because this was a retrospective non-randomized cohort study, the results are potentially affected by several sources of bias. In particular, we considered possible bias due to confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, and outcome assessment. Relevant confounding factors included age, comorbidities, compartment involved, implant-related factors, and possible variation in surgical technique. Other potentially relevant variables, such as body mass index, alignment correction, and implant positioning accuracy, were not consistently available and could not be fully accounted for.

Results

Between 2012 and 2022, 208 patients underwent UKA at our institution. At the time of review, 190 patients (91.3%) had completed at least two years of follow-up and were included in the analysis. The mean age was 65.7 years (SD 10.7), and 52.6% were women. Overall, 134 procedures (70.5%) were medial UKA. Robotic-assisted UKA (RA-UKA) was performed in 84 patients (44.3%), and conventional UKA in 106 (55.7%) (Table 1).

The 2-year follow-up (n = 190) revealed 10 complications (5.2%), and their onset occurred a median of 59 days (IQR: 5–127) before presentation. Of the 10 complications, 7 were classified as early complications occurring within 90 days after surgery, whereas 3 were classified as mid-term complications occurring after 90 days and within the two-year follow-up period. Due to the low number of events in each subgroup, no meaningful comparative analysis according to surgical technique and complication timing was performed.

The most frequent complications were arthrofibrosis (2/10). Because only 10 complications were observed, multivariable modeling was considered unreliable for inferential purposes and was not used to support causal or comparative conclusions. Therefore, the present study is reported primarily as a descriptive analysis of postoperative complications.

Types of complications

Ten complications (5.2%) were recorded within two years of surgery. The most frequent complication was arthrofibrosis (n = 2), followed by deep vein thrombosis (n = 1). Other complications included prosthetic loosening (n = 1), cellulitis (n = 1), intraarticular loose body (n = 1), bleeding with hematocrit drop (n = 1), infected seroma (n = 1), and one periprosthetic fracture (n = 1). The single periprosthetic fracture occurred intraoperatively in a patient undergoing conventional UKA and was successfully managed with internal fixation (Table 2).

Periprosthetic fracture

One case of periprosthetic fracture was recorded in a female patient in her 70s undergoing medial conventional UKA for symptomatic medial osteoarthritis secondary to avascular necrosis. The fracture occurred intraoperatively during impaction of the cemented femoral component, resulting in a minimally displaced coronal plane (Hoffa) fracture of the medial femoral condyle. Immediate internal fixation was performed using two compressive screws, achieving anatomical reduction and stable fixation. The patient followed a non–weight-bearing protocol for six weeks with unrestricted range of motion. Radiographic and clinical fracture healing was achieved by 12 weeks. At that time, she was walking unaided with 125° of knee flexion and full extension. At ten-year follow-up, clinical outcome was excellent, with a Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) of 92.7 (Fig. 1) [19].

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 190 patients undergoing unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA), we observed a low overall complication rate of 5.2% at two-year follow-up. The most frequent complications were arthrofibrosis and deep vein thrombosis, while periprosthetic fracture was uncommon, with only one case identified in the conventional group. These findings are in line with previous reports showing that UKA is a safe procedure with complication rates comparable to those described after total knee arthroplasty [20, 21].

When comparing robotic-assisted UKA (RA-UKA) with conventional procedures, complication rates appeared numerically lower in the robotic group (1.2% v/s 8.5%). However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.29). Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have suggested that RA-UKA may improve component positioning and potentially reduce revision rates [13, 22–25], but our data do not provide evidence of a significant difference in short-term complications. Importantly, complications after UKA should not be interpreted as a homogeneous endpoint. Some complications may theoretically be influenced by technical factors such as implant positioning, alignment correction, or fixation; however, these variables were not systematically measured in our cohort. Therefore, no mechanistic conclusions regarding the potential technical advantages of robotic assistance can be drawn from the present study.

Our overall complication rate of 5.2% should be interpreted in the context of prior comparative studies on robotic-assisted and conventional UKA. Although direct comparison is limited by differences in event definitions and follow-up windows, recent studies have generally reported low complication rates for both techniques, with some series suggesting lower overall complication or revision rates with robotic assistance [20]. In this context, the numerically lower complication rate observed in our RA-UKA group is directionally consistent with some previous reports. However, differences between our findings and those of prior studies may be explained by variation in study design, complication definitions, sample size, follow-up duration, patient selection, surgeon experience, and the non-concurrent nature of our cohorts. In addition, many published studies focus primarily on implant positioning, revision, or aseptic loosening rather than on common postoperative complications such as arthrofibrosis or deep vein thrombosis, which further limits direct comparison [13].

This study has several important limitations. First, the retrospective non-randomized design makes the results vulnerable to confounding and selection bias, as has also been emphasized in prior registry-based and observational studies on UKA and robotic-assisted arthroplasty [5–7, 18]. A major source of confounding in our cohort is the marked imbalance in baseline comorbidities between groups, with comorbid conditions being substantially more frequent in the RA-UKA group, which may have influenced complication risk independently of surgical technique. Second, although robotic-assisted UKA is often promoted for improving technical accuracy, this study did not systematically measure robotics-specific parameters such as implant positioning accuracy, coronal or sagittal alignment restoration, joint-line reconstruction, soft-tissue balance, or implant outliers. Therefore, we cannot determine whether the observed differences in complication rates were related to improved technical execution or to other factors unrelated to the robotic platform. This limitation is particularly important because the proposed mechanistic advantages of robotic assistance remain speculative in our cohort. Third, the low number of complications (n = 10) greatly limited statistical power and precluded robust multivariable adjustment; therefore, the regression analysis should be considered exploratory only, and the findings should be interpreted primarily as descriptive rather than inferential. Another important limitation is the non-concurrent nature of the groups: all conventional procedures were performed between 2012 and 2018, whereas RA-UKA cases were conducted after adoption of the robotic system in 2018. This introduces potential bias related to temporal changes in institutional practice, surgical expertise, and perioperative care protocols, in addition to the learning curve associated with a new technology (26,27). Importantly, this learning-curve effect may be bidirectional. Early adoption of robotic-assisted UKA could theoretically increase complications during the initial phase of platform use; however, the opposite pattern was observed in our cohort. This suggests that other factors may also have contributed, including more selective allocation of favorable cases to RA-UKA during the adoption period and broader improvements over time in perioperative management, patient selection, and institutional workflows. An additional paradoxical finding was the higher prevalence of baseline comorbidities in the RA-UKA group despite the lower observed complication rate. This finding should be interpreted with substantial caution. Because only one complication occurred in the robotic cohort, this pattern may reflect random variation related to the low event count. Additionally, the retrospective non-randomized design introduces potential confounding by indication, temporal bias, and selection bias. It is also possible that improvements in perioperative protocols, patient optimization, complication prevention strategies, and institutional workflows during later years contributed to lower complication rates independently of robotic assistance. Therefore, this apparent paradox should not be interpreted as evidence that robotic assistance mitigates the effect of medical comorbidities.

Finally, the relatively short follow-up is insufficient to assess later complications such as aseptic loosening, polyethylene wear, or revision for long-term mechanical failure, which remain critical endpoints in UKA survivorship studies [5–7, 13, 22, 23]. Accordingly, the present study should be interpreted as a descriptive comparison of complication patterns rather than an inferential analysis of independent predictors of postoperative complications.

Some events may be more closely related to technical or implant-related factors, such as component positioning, bone preparation, or fixation, whereas others may be more strongly influenced by patient comorbidities or perioperative management. Given the small number of events in our cohort, the present study cannot reliably disentangle these mechanisms, but it highlights the importance of considering them when interpreting comparative results [22–25].

Despite these limitations, our findings contribute to the growing body of literature describing short-term outcomes of RA-UKA and conventional UKA. While RA-UKA has been associated with improved implant alignment and lower revision rates in some series [13, 22, 23], the clinical impact of these technical advantages remains debated [24, 25]. Based on our results, both techniques appear safe, but the potential benefits of robotic assistance in reducing complications cannot be confirmed. Larger, prospective, and methodologically rigorous studies are needed to determine whether RA-UKA translates into improved long-term outcomes and survivorship compared with conventional techniques. In addition, because the cohorts were non-concurrent, differences in follow-up duration, annual case volume, perioperative management, and the learning curve associated with adoption of robotic-assisted UKA may also have influenced the findings. Although institutional perioperative protocols were generally standardized, retrospective reconstruction cannot completely exclude temporal changes over the 2012–2022 study period.

Conclusion

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty demonstrated a low overall complication rate at two-year follow-up. Although the RA-UKA group showed a numerically lower proportion of complications than the conventional UKA group, the present study does not support RA-UKA as being superior to conventional UKA with respect to postoperative complications. At most, these findings suggest a descriptive, non-causal trend that must be interpreted with substantial caution because of the retrospective design, the non-concurrent cohort structure, the low number of events, and the marked imbalance in baseline comorbidities between groups. Larger prospective and adequately powered studies are required to determine whether robotic assistance provides clinically meaningful advantages beyond conventional techniques.
