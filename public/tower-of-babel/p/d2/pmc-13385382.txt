Amino acid-based biological age clock and its implications for human health and aging
Authors: Keke Ding, Ruimin Xu, Xiaowen Chao, Mengci Li, Tao Sun, Tianbiao Yang, Min Zhu, Xufei Peng, Ruofan Liu, Peiyang Luo, Guoli Tian, Yun Lin, Guoxiang Xie, Xiaojiao Zheng, Lu Zhang, Wei Jia, Tianlu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amino acids are fundamental to human physiology, yet their impact on growth, development, and aging remains elusive. Here, we introduce AmiAge, a biological age predictor constructed using a Random Forest model trained on the concentrations of 18 amino acids across individuals aged 1 to 89 years. Leveraging data from 9 studies encompassing over 11,000 in-house and more than 270,000 publicly available samples with diverse demographic and genetic backgrounds, AmiAge demonstrates robust accuracy. The deviation between AmiAge and chronological age (AmiAge Gap) correlates strongly with established aging biomarkers, disease risk, and clinical outcomes. Individuals with higher gaps exhibit increased frailty, telomere attrition, and elevated incidence of age-related diseases. To enhance clinical utility, we distilled AmiAge into an 8-amino acid model (including alanine, glutamine, glycine, histidine, leucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and valine). Our findings suggest that this simple, scalable amino acid clock offers a valuable complement to existing biological aging metrics, with potential applications in personalized health management and aging research.

Introduction

Chronological age is a key measure of time passing and is closely linked to health outcomes. However, individuals of the same chronological age often exhibit substantial heterogeneity in physiological function and disease risk1. To better capture this variability, the concept of biological age has been proposed as a more informative indicator of overall health status2,3. Biological age reflects the cumulative effects of genetic factors, environmental exposures, and lifestyle influences on the functional integrity of multiple biological systems, thereby providing a more comprehensive representation of the aging process4.

Across the life course, biological age offers valuable insights into health and disease risk5–8. In childhood, deviations in biological age may indicate abnormal developmental trajectories. During adolescence, biological age reflects the dynamic adaptation of physiological systems, potentially influencing long-term health trajectories. In adulthood, biological age serves as an important indicator of resilience to environmental stress, enabling more precise risk stratification and intervention. In older populations, biological age is strongly associated with the risk of age-related diseases and mortality, supporting its role in guiding personalized prevention and healthcare strategies9.

There are various biological age clocks, each with distinct objectives. Some clocks aim to create sophisticated age models by integrating data from physiology, hematology, and omics, such as the hematology-based clock from the Study of Longitudinal Age in Mice10, MetaboAge based on 1H-NMR metabolomics11, proteomic clocks1,12, and DNA methylation (DNAm) clocks13,14, and the Explainable Biological Age (ENABL Age) model, which leverages diverse demographic and phenotypic data and has been validated across large datasets such as the UK Biobank (UKB) and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)15. Others focus on exploring age-related biological aspects, including clocks associated with mortality risk and health outcomes9,16,17, aging patterns, and age related molecules18,19. However, the majority of clocks target adult populations, overlooking children and infants. Furthermore, validating biomarkers across diverse populations is increasingly recognized as essential to ensure their reliability. Additionally, while numerous age clocks are available, many of those based on DNAm20, proteomics1,12, microbiome21, or metabolome5 are complex or costly. Prioritizing simple and easily measurable markers is essential to enhance the practicality and applicability of age clock models.

Amino acids are essential for various bodily functions, such as protein synthesis, metabolism, and cellular signaling, all of which play a critical role in growth, development, and aging processes22–24. They are taken as essential components of biological age modeling and as metabolic signatures associated with health and disease. For example, the MetaboAge clock based on ¹H-NMR plasma metabolomics incorporated alanine, glutamine, histidine, isoleucine, leucine, valine, phenylalanine, and tyrosine as core features11. Another biological model reported that elevated l-methionine was associated with accelerated biological aging25. Glutamic acid, tyrosine, citrulline, arginine, histidine, isoleucine, leucine, methionine, serine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine were significantly associated with aging and mortality risk in Long Life Family Study26. Alanine, glutamine, glycine, histidine, leucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and valine were associated with all-cause mortality, with their hazard ratios (HR) per SD ranging from 0.854 to 1.118 in UKB27.

Given the well-recognized associations between amino acids and growth, metabolism, and disease, their established use in biological age modeling, and the relatively low cost and high reliability of amino acid measurements, we focused on 18 commonly measured amino acids as the biochemical basis of the AmiAge clock. To capture both absolute amino acid levels and their relative balance, we derived a total of 36 features, including 18 individual amino acids and 18 amino acid ratios normalized to their total concentration. These features were used as input for a classical Random Forest (RF) model. A total of nine datasets comprising 283,561 individuals across a broad age range (1–89 years) and diverse demographic backgrounds were used for model training, internal testing, external validation, and biomedical relevance evaluation. Results

Training and testing of AmiAge clock

The healthy individuals sourced from the metabolomics Atlas database (see Methods) was randomly divided into a training set (80%, n = 4529, including 2580 females and 1,949 males, aged 1 to 89 years, mean age = 48.87 years) and a test set (20%, n = 1130, including 599 females and 531 males, aged 1 to 89 years, mean age = 49.04 years) (Table S1). Selection of healthy participants was based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, detailed in the Supplementary Information. A feature set comprising 36 variables was constructed, including the absolute concentrations of 18 amino acids and 18 derived ratios, each defined as the concentration of an individual amino acid divided by the total concentration of all 18 amino acids (Table S2). Subsequently, ten machine learning methods were trained on the training set and feature set using a five-fold CV approach. The ten methods include three linear models: Linear Regression (LR), Lasso Regression, and Elastic Net; one tree-based model: Decision Tree (DT); three ensemble models: RF, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM); one proximity-based model: k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN); a Support Vector Machine with a radial kernel (SVM radial); and a Neural Network (NNet). The performance of the models was evaluated on the test set, where RF exhibited the best predictive ability in both five-fold CV and the test set (Fig. 2a, Table S3). The detailed parameter tuning process for different models is provided in Table S4. In the test set, the predicted age showed a strong correlation with chronological age (Fig. 2c). The RF model achieved high performance (correlation r = 0.90, coefficient of determination (R²) = 0.80, root mean square error (RMSE) = 10.30, mean absolute error (MAE) = 7.53). However, consistent with previous findings28, it systematically overestimated the age of younger individuals and underestimated the age of older individuals. To address this, we applied a statistical correction to the predicted age values28–31 (see Methods) and obtained a slightly improved performance (Fig. 2d) (r = 0.94, R² = 0.86, RMSE = 8.66, MAE = 6.73). The correlation coefficients between chronological age and the original and corrected AmiAge in the test set and across age strata are presented in Table S5, which demonstrates the effectiveness of correction.

Additionally, two separate RF models were developed: one using the concentrations of the 18 amino acids alone, and another using the ratios of each amino acid to the total concentration. Both models showed comparable performances to the 36-feature model (Table S3). We selected the model incorporating all 36 features for subsequent analyses.

Validation of the AmiAge Clock in 2 external datasets

To confirm the reliability and applicability of the AmiAge model, we evaluated it on two independent healthy datasets: the HC1 dataset, comprising 798 females and 613 males (mean age = 48.77 years); and the HC2 dataset, consisting of 263 females and 333 males (mean age = 41.33 years) (Table S1). The predicted age exhibited strong correlations with chronological age in the external validation set HC1, with r = 0.88, R² = 0.73, RMSE = 12.10, and MAE = 9.64 (Fig. 2e). After adjustment, performance improved further, with r = 0.95, R² = 0.89, RMSE = 6.09, and MAE = 5.11 (Fig. 2f). Similarly, in the external validation set HC2, the predicted age showed strong correlations with chronological age (r = 0.83, R² = 0.68, RMSE = 7.57, MAE = 5.94; Fig. 2g), which further improved after adjustment (r = 0.89, R² = 0.74, RMSE = 5.51, MAE = 4.57; Fig. 2h). Together, the AmiAge clock delivers robust performance across both external datasets, and the adjustment further enhances accuracy.

We further visualized age-related trends of total and individual amino acids using five-year age increments. The levels of total amino acids and those of most individual amino acids followed an inverted U-shaped pattern, peaking at 30–40 years of age (Figs. 3b–t, n = 5659). Notably, the trends of most amino acids are similar in males and females, except for tyrosine. When focusing on younger (≤ 20 years) and older (≥ 60 years) individuals, we found that 9 of the 18 amino acids showed opposite trends between the two age groups (Fig. S1). To elucidate the contributions of individual amino acid features to the AmiAge clock, we conducted SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)32 value analysis and sensitivity analysis. The SHAP values on the internal test, external validation HC1, and external validation HC2 datasets consistently highlighted glutamic acid, citrulline, glycine, methionine, and their corresponding ratios as the most influential features for the AmiAge clock (Fig. 3u). The clustering patterns suggested that amino acids with similar biochemical properties or biological functions tend to exert comparable impacts on the model. In the sensitivity analysis, we simulated a twofold increase in the input values of each feature and quantified the percentage of resultant change in the predicted age. We found that arginine, phenylalanine, glutamic acid, methionine, and their ratios had the greatest influence, with up to 68.7% changes in predicted age (Fig. 3v). Notably, glutamic acid and methionine consistently emerged as influential features across SHAP and sensitivity analyses, underscoring their key roles in age prediction.

Clinical relevance of the AmiAge clock in disease datasets

To assess the clinical relevance of the AmiAge clock, we calculated the adjusted AmiAge Gap (adjusted AmiAge − chronological age), a value reflecting the degree of deviation of an individual’s biological status from the average level of healthy peers of the same age (see Methods). We next investigated its associations with established disease biomarkers across five distinct disease cohorts. These included cross-sectional datasets from individuals with Depression (Dep, n = 673), Cardiovascular Disease (CVD, n = 1002), Hepatic Fibrosis (HepF, n = 768), and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM, n = 803). Additionally, we included a longitudinal dataset of patients with NAFL who completed a 6-month exercise intervention (NAFL-6m-ExerciseIntervention, n = 133 × 2), with assessments at baseline and 6-month follow-up (Fig. 4a). We segmented the adjusted AmiAge Gap into three quartile groups (Q1, Q2, and Q3) and observed that as AmiAge Gap (adj.) increased, disease-related biomarkers showed a heavier burden, consistent with accelerated aging (Fig. 4b–q). Specifically, in the Dep dataset, AmiAge Gap (adj.) was positively correlated with higher Hamilton depression scores (Fig. 4b). In CVD patients, a higher AmiAge Gap (adj.) was associated with elevated blood lipid levels, including low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (TG) (Fig. 4c–e). In the T2DM dataset, higher AmiAge Gap (adj.) corresponded to increased TG, TC, and D-glucose levels (Fig. 4f–h). In the HepF dataset, AmiAge Gap (adj.) was positively associated with liver function markers, including aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (Fig. 4i–k). In the NAFL-6m-ExerciseIntervention dataset, AmiAge Gap (adj.) was positively associated with liver function markers (Fig. 4l–n). After the 6-month physical exercise program, both AmiAge Gap (adj.) and liver function markers decreased compared with those of baseline (Fig. 4o–q), underscoring the beneficial effects of exercise on liver health. In addition, we evaluated whether AmiAge Gap (adj.) provides incremental predictive value beyond established clinical risk models. When added to widely used risk scores, including Systematic Coronary Risk Estimation 2 (SCORE2) for cardiovascular disease, the clinical Cambridge Diabetes Risk Score (CDRS) for T2DM, and the Cardiovascular Risk Factors, Aging, and Dementia (CAIDE) for dementia, AmiAge Gap (adj.) resulted in modest improvements in risk prediction (Tables S6 and S7). These findings suggest that AmiAge captures additional aging information beyond conventional clinical risk factors.

Toward application: a simplified amino acid-based biological age clock

To maximize practical utility, we used the 8 most routinely measured amino acids that were available in both our Atlas (n = 5659; measured by mass spectrum platform) and the UKB (n = 272,383; measured by NMR platform) (Table S8) to develop a simplified AmiAge clock.

We first assessed the associations of 8 amino acids with age and diseases. As Fig. 5a shown, the total concentration of 8 amino acids was associated with chronological age in the overall UKB population and in sex- and race-stratified subgroups. Figures 5b and S2 further demonstrate strong associations between the eight amino acids and risks of diseases, cancer, and mortality, highlighting their potential clinical relevance. To further assess the robustness of these associations and reduce potential reverse causation, we conducted sensitivity analyses by excluding participants who developed disease within the first two years of follow-up, restricting the analysis to individuals with fasting duration over 6 hours, and removing extreme values of CRP and eGFR. After these exclusions, the final analytic sample consisted of 25,214 participants. The results are consistent in direction and magnitude (Fig. S3). The adjusted AmiAge Gap derived from our simplified clock was associated with nearly all of the aging and physical-performance measures examined, even after adjustment for chronological age, sex, and major sociodemographic and lifestyle confounders (Fig. 5c, d). Specifically, a higher AmiAge Gap (adj.) was associated with elevated levels of kidney function biomarkers (cystatin C and creatinine), liver enzymes (ALT, AST, and GGT), slower walking pace, daily fatigue, frequent insomnia, a higher frailty index, increased arterial stiffness, and greater body-mass index (BMI). Conversely, it was linked to lower levels of albumin, Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (IGF-1), total bilirubin, and telomere length, as well as reduced grip strength and lower Fev1. Notably, we observed some unexpected associations (i.e., shorter reaction time and lower systolic blood pressure), which should be interpreted with caution.

The adjusted AmiAge Gap was also associated with disease incidence. We categorized participants into the Bottom 25% and Top 25% groups based on their adjusted AmiAge Gap values and found that those with a higher gap had a significantly greater cumulative incidence of type 2 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, ovarian cancer, osteoarthritis, macular degeneration, chronic kidney disease, and breast cancer (Fig. 5e–k). Consistently, Cox regression analyses revealed increased risks for these outcomes, including type 2 diabetes (HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.10–1.20), chronic kidney disease (HR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.12–1.24), rheumatoid arthritis (HR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.03–1.25), macular degeneration (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.05–1.21), osteoarthritis (HR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04–1.11), breast cancer (HR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.70–1.98), and ovarian cancer (HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.25–1.89). Notably, after adjustment for covariates, the associations remained robust for type 2 diabetes (HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.12–1.23) and chronic kidney disease (HR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.08–1.20), supporting the clinical relevance of the simplified AmiAge clock.

Discussion

Leveraging amino acid profiles of multiple studies comprising 283,561 samples with diverse lifestyle and genetic backgrounds, we constructed the AmiAge clock model and evaluated its performance and clinical significance. The model achieves accuracy comparable to many biological age clocks, particularly those derived from metabolomic profiles, and the resulting age gap demonstrates high clinical relevance. It serves as a simple and stable metabolic representation of biological age.

Compared with existing biological age clocks, our AmiAge clock has several distinctive advantages. One key strength is its simplicity, as the model relies on a streamlined set of 18 or 8 amino acid-related variables, in contrast to many omics models that require hundreds of features1,6,33,34, thereby reducing complexity for practical applications. Second, it is capable of resisting the impact of short-term fluctuations caused by factors such as diet or exercise rhythms, as it relies on both concentration and ratio variables and captures fundamental homeostatic patterns and intercorrelations among amino acids. Third, it showed reasonably consistent performance across different detection platforms, including mass spectrum and NMR, suggesting a cross-platform stability. Fourth, the AmiAge clock was trained on a dataset covering a broad age span, from infancy to late adulthood, whereas many existing clocks are developed using only individuals over 40 years of age19,27,35,36. While our AmiAge clock demonstrates notable advantages, it should be viewed as a complementary tool rather than a replacement for existing clinical markers or biological age clocks. Considering its relative simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and robustness, it may be particularly suitable for repeated measurements and long-term monitoring in large-scale populations. Given this, we have launched a free, public website (https://www.metaboage.com), where users can instantly obtain their predicted age and personalized guidance for health management.

The AmiAge clock provides a quantitative estimate of biological age across a broad age spectrum (r = 0.94–0.89, R² = 0.89–0.74, RMSE = 5.51–8.66, MAE = 4.57–6.73), derived from circulating amino acid profiles. Comparatively, epigenetic13,37 and proteomic1 clocks report lower MAEs, while within the context of metabolomics-based aging models4,6,11,25,28,33,36,38–40, AmiAge exhibits broadly comparable accuracy. As such comparisons are constrained by differences in cohort composition, age distribution, and feature sets, we further conducted a direct head-to-head evaluation of existing clocks using the same UKB dataset (n = 27,428, see method). Consistent to that of literatures, AmiAge (RMSE = 7.99, MAE = 6.76) yielded higher RMSE and MAE values than KDM-derived phenotypic age41 (RMSE = 5.75, MAE = 4.45) and the proteomic age clock ProtAge42 (RMSE = 3.10, MAE = 2.43), while achieving comparable performance to other metabolic clocks28 (RMSE = 6.83-8.58, MAE = 5.59-7.36).

Our analyses identified a set of core amino acids involved in age prediction, including glutamic acid, arginine, phenylalanine, and methionine, all of which have been implicated in aging biology. Previous studies link glutamic acid to aging-related diseases, including diabetic retinopathy43 and neurobiological aging transitions44. Phenylalanine differs in centenarians compared with typical older adults45, while methionine shows consistent associations with aging across plasma, serum, and cerebrospinal fluid25,38,46,47. Arginine and its downstream metabolite ornithine exhibit age-related alterations and are involved in the polyamine synthesis pathway48,49. Together, these findings support the biological relevance of these amino acids as potential aging biomarkers. Building on this, the distilled AmiAge model incorporates alanine, glutamine, glycine, histidine, leucine, tyrosine, and valine, together with phenylalanine. These amino acids capture complementary biological processes underlying aging. Alanine reflects liver–muscle metabolic crosstalk and metabolic dysregulation50. Glutamine regulates immune–muscle interaction, autophagy, and senescence via mTOR signaling51,52. Glycine mimics methionine restriction and modulates insulin/IGF-1 signaling22. Histidine supports mitochondrial function through ergothioneine53,54. In contrast, dysregulated leucine and valine metabolism promotes senescence via mTORC1 activation55 while tyrosine reflects oxidative stress–related functional decline56. Phenylalanine, as noted above, further links the model to longevity-associated metabolic profiles. Overall, the simplified model retains key biological information despite reduced feature complexity.

Although both SHAP analysis and sensitivity analysis provide insights into feature importance, they capture different facets of model behavior and biological system alteration. SHAP is a model-performance-driven metric that reflects the average contribution of each feature to predictive ability across all individuals. In contrast, sensitivity analysis lies closer to the biological system, which quantifies how experimentally doubling a single input feature alters the model output, thereby affording greater biological significance. Consequently, amino acids like arginine, which may show relatively low SHAP values due to limited natural variation in the dataset, exert large effects on predicted age when their levels are experimentally doubled. The complementary results of the two approaches reflect diverse levels of impact of amino acids on biological age.

Despite the valuable insights provided by AmiAge, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, amino acid levels are highly sensitive to short-term metabolic states. Factors such as recent dietary intake, fasting duration, physical activity, and circadian timing can cause transient spikes in individual amino acid levels57,58. Existing disease processes, particularly liver disease, diabetes, and inflammatory conditions, also raise the possibility of reverse causation in the associations between AmiAge Gap and incident diseases. To mitigate these effects, we performed sensitivity analyses and adjusted for various available covariates, which yielded broadly consistent patterns and supported the robustness of our findings. Nevertheless, short-term variation and reverse causation cannot be fully controlled. Future studies with well-characterized samples are needed to further elucidate the biological value of AmiAge. Second, some cross-sectional associations with physiological measures appeared counterintuitive, for example, shorter reaction time or lower systolic blood pressure in individuals with higher AmiAge Gap. These patterns likely reflect the multidimensional nature of biological aging, cohort selection effects (e.g., healthy volunteer bias in UK Biobank), and differences between systemic dysregulation and organ-specific functional measures. Importantly, longitudinal analyses of disease incidence and mortality support the relevance of AmiAge Gap despite these cross-sectional nuances. Third, AmiAge was trained across the full age spectrum, including children and adolescents. In younger individuals, amino acid metabolism is strongly shaped by growth and developmental processes. Therefore, the AmiAge Gap in children and adolescents should not be interpreted strictly as accelerated aging, but rather as reflecting variation in developmental trajectories, whereas in adults it captures aging-related systemic remodeling. Finally, although longitudinal follow-up data from the UK Biobank were used for disease outcomes, most other datasets and clinical indicators in this study were cross-sectional. Further validation in longitudinal cohorts and in diverse populations is essential to establish the robustness and predictive value of the AmiAge clock.

In conclusion, our AmiAge clock represents a significant advancement in biological clock research, providing a simple yet effective means to predict biological age and support personalized health management. Further validation is needed across diverse populations and settings.

Methods

Ethics approval

Three in-house datasets were used in this study: Metabolomics Atlas, HC1, and HC2, all of which were conducted in accordance with the relevant ethics committee approvals, with written informed consent obtained from all participants. The HC1 dataset was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (approval number: 2024-KY-053(K)), and the HC2 dataset was obtained from the multimodal database of the Department of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, China (project number: KY-2025-369(K)). UKB data were obtained under approved application number 171860 (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk). Both studies complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participant data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

Datasets

The training and test datasets

Amino acid concentrations of healthy individuals (n = 5659) for model training (n = 4529, including 2580 females and 1949 males, mean age = 48.87 years) and internal test (n = 1130, including 599 females and 531 males, mean age = 49.04 years) were obtained from the Metabolomics Atlas database (http://www.metabolomicsatlas.com/, authorized by Human Metabolomics Institute, China, project ID A00230). The introduction of the Metabolomics Atlas database and the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria of the training and test datasets are provided in the SI. All studies of Atlas complied with the criteria of respective ethics committees, and all participants provided informed consent.

The external validation HC1 dataset

This dataset was sourced from the metabolomics database of 20,000 physical examination cohort of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, China (project No. 2024-KY-053(K)). Individuals who came to Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital for medical examinations between July and September 2024 were involved. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were the same as those of the training and test datasets. This study was conducted in accordance with the criteria of the ethics committees of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, and all the participants or their guardians signed the informed consent agreement.

The external validation HC2 dataset

This dataset was sourced from the multimodal database of the Department of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, China (project No. KY-2025-369(K)). The project was initiated in January 2025 and enrolled both outpatients and inpatients from the Department of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were the same as those of the training and test datasets. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, and all participants or their guardians provided written informed consent.

Disease datasets

The disease datasets (n = 3512) were also obtained from the Metabolomics Atlas database and consisted exclusively of patient data59–65. Five disease cohorts (Dep, CVD, HepF, T2DM, NAFL-6m-ExerciseIntervention) were involved. Descriptions and detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria of each cohort and dataset are provided in the SI. All studies of Atlas complied with the criteria of respective ethics committees, and all participants provided informed consent.

The UKB dataset

UKB is a large prospective cohort study, which includes comprehensive omics and phenotypic data from 502,505 individuals residing in the United Kingdom, recruited between 2006 and 201066. The full UKB protocol is available online (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/media/gnkeyh2q/study-rationale.pdf). In this analysis, all participants with available amino acid data at baseline were involved (Applicant Number 171,860). For amino acid data with more than 20% missing values, imputation was performed using the R package missForest67, which combines random forest imputation with predictive mean matching.

Model selection and AmiAge clock construction

For model development, we conducted fivefold cross-validation within the training set. At each iteration, the training data were randomly divided into five equal subsets, with four subsets used for training and the remaining subset for validation. This procedure was repeated five times so that each fold served once as the validation set. For each algorithm, we selected the hyperparameter configuration that minimized the MAE across the five folds.

We benchmarked a range of algorithms, including Lasso regression, LR, DT, KNN, LightGBM, RF, XGBoost, SVM radial, NNet, and Elastic net. All models were trained on normalized data (centered and scaled) using their respective R packages: glmnet (v4.1.10) for LASSO and elastic net, lm (base R) for LR, rpart (v4.1.24) for DT, kknn (v1.4.1) for KNN, lightgbm (v4.6.0) for LightGBM, ranger (v0.17.0) for RF, xgboost (v3.1.2.1) for XGBoost, kernlab (v0.9.33) for SVM radial, and nnet (v7.3.20) for NNet. Fixed and tuned hyperparameters were defined for each algorithm according to prior knowledge and computational feasibility. For example, λ was tuned for Lasso and elastic net, the complexity parameter (cp) for DT, kernel parameters for SVM, and a combination of learning rate, tree depth, and subsampling strategies for LightGBM and XGBoost. Grid sizes varied depending on the number and type of hyperparameters. Details of preprocessing, fixed settings, and tuning grids are summarized in Table S4.

The best hyperparameter configuration for each model was refitted on the entire training set, and performance was evaluated in the internal test and external validation sets using Pearson’s r, MAE, RMSE, and R². The RF model based on 36 features and the best hyperparameter was selected as the AmiAge clock.

Calculation of AmiAge gap

AmiAge Gap was defined as the difference between predicted and chronological age. Positive values indicated an older predicted age than chronological age, and negative values indicated a younger predicted age.

AmiAge adjustment

As reported, we also found that age clocks tend to overestimate age in younger individuals and underestimate age in older individuals. Therefore, we applied a statistical correction to the age prediction28. Briefly, we regressed AmiAge on chronological age and used the resulting intercept (β) and slope coefficient (α) estimates to adjust the age:

adjusted age = AmiAge + (chronological age−α×chronological age +β)

Feature importance analysis by SHAP and sensitivity analysis

Feature importance was assessed using SHAP, which provides consistent and locally accurate attributions of each feature’s contribution to model predictions. To evaluate the stability and robustness of the feature rankings, SHAP values were computed across the test set, HC1, and HC2 datasets, respectively, allowing comparison of variable contributions across different cohorts.

The sensitivity of AmiAge to variations in amino acid levels was assessed by independently increasing each amino acid concentration by 100% in the dataset. For each amino acid, the resulting change in predicted age was calculated as a percentage difference from the baseline prediction. It was derived to quantify the impact of each amino acid on model predictions. An ANOVA test was performed on changes of these predicted ages to determine whether sensitivity differed significantly across amino acids, and post hoc Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference analysis identified amino acids with distinct sensitivity effects.

Outcomes

The construction of aging-related, frailty, and physical function variables followed the procedures described in a previous UKB study1. Field IDs for all biomarkers and measures of physical and cognitive function are shown in Supplementary Table S9. Binary variables included poor self-rated health, slow walking pace, self-rated facial aging, frequent tiredness/lethargy, insomnia, and long sleep duration (>10 h/day), while continuous variables (e.g., blood pressure, lung function, grip strength) were derived according to established protocols. Frailty index was calculated using the UKB algorithm by Williams et al.68 (Table S10). Leukocyte telomere length was assessed as the T:S ratio, adjusted for technical variation, log-transformed, and z-standardized.

Prevalence and incidence of diseases and clinical risk factors defined using linked prescription records (details in Table S11) and ATC codes. Hospital admissions, cancer registries, and mortality data were obtained through national registry linkages with censoring dates as specified by UKB (https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/refer.cgi?id=115559).

Detailed information about outcomes is provided in the SI

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.4.1, Graphpad Prism version 10, and Python version 3.10. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were made using t-tests, ANOVA, or chi-squared tests, as appropriate. Correlations between amino acids and other variables were assessed using Pearson correlation or linear regression analyses. Age-associated trends of total and individual amino acids were visualized using five-year increments and LOESS smoothing. To investigate the clinical relevance of AmiAge Gap (adj.), we stratified participants into quartiles and compared disease-related biomarkers across groups using ANOVA and post-hoc tests.

For analyses in the UKB dataset, associations between amino acids and chronological age were assessed using linear regression models, with p-values adjusted for multiple testing by the FDR method. The relationships between amino acids and incident diseases were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models, and the results were visualized as bubble plots showing hazard ratios (HR) and -log10(FDR) values. Associations between the adjusted AmiAge Gap and aging-related, frailty, and physical function indicators were examined using linear regression models for continuous outcomes and logistic regression models for binary outcomes, with adjustments for age, sex, ethnicity, nation, IPAQ activity group (low, moderate, high), education (less than colledge degree, colledge degree), income (<31000, > 31,000), smoking status (never, former/ever or current smoker) and drinking status (never, former/ever or current drinker). For disease risk stratification, participants were divided into the top 25% and bottom 25% of AmiAge Gap (adj.), and cumulative incidence was compared using Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank tests.
