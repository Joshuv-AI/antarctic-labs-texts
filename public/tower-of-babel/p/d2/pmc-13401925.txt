Construction of Smart Hydrogel–Exosome Drug Delivery Platforms and Their Applications in Skin Rehabilitation
Authors: Wenyue Deng, Yuting Liu, Yixiu Zhong, Jingwei Wang, Jianglin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkin rehabilitation is a highly coordinated process involving sequential cellular and molecular events that ultimately restore the integrity and function of damaged tissue, yet this cascade is frequently disrupted by infection, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Precise and controllable drug delivery is the key to enhance the efficacy of skin rehabilitation and reduce the systemic side effects. Although exosomes, as natural drug carriers, possess low immunogenicity and excellent biocompatibility, they still face challenges such as rapid in vivo clearance and insufficient targeting ability. The smart hydrogels, with their three-dimensional polymeric network structure and stimulus-responsive properties (such as pH, reactive oxygen system and glucose), can not only serve as a “reservoir” for exosomes but also act as their “intelligent switch”. Therefore, the smart hydrogel-exosome platforms can enhance the efficacy of skin rehabilitation by enabling local, continuous, targeted and controllable drug release. Excitingly, by reducing secondary infection risk, enabling responsive inflammatory regulation, and preventing the tissue damage associated with frequent dressing changes, this system addresses the key limitations of conventional therapies, including topical agents, wound dressings, intralesional injections, and skin grafting. This article reviews the construction of these smart hydrogel-exosome drug delivery platforms, focusing on the sources, isolation and drug-loading strategies of exosomes, as well as the response mechanisms and fabrication strategies of the smart hydrogels. It also summarizes the mechanism and latest progress of this system in skin rehabilitation. Finally, this article looks forward to the challenges and future directions in this field, with the aim of providing new strategies for precise treatment of skin.

Introduction

The skin is the largest organ of the human body and is highly susceptible to damage from various internal and external factors such as ultraviolet radiation, pathogen infections, and diabetes. Such damages easily result in skin infection, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Effective skin rehabilitation thus represents a pressing clinical priority, calling for therapeutic strategies that can accelerate wound closure, reduce scarring, and alleviate the long-term economic burden on patients and healthcare systems.

In current skin rehabilitation therapy, conventional drug delivery approaches suffer from issues such as slow healing rates, secondary damage caused by frequent dressing changes, a high incidence of scar hyperplasia, and high long-term care costs. As naturally occurring nanoscale extracellular vesicles (30–200 nm) derived from cells,1 exosomes offer intrinsic advantages for drug delivery. Exosome biogenesis begins with inward budding of the early endosomal membrane to form intraluminal vesicles (ILVs), which subsequently develop into multivesicular bodies (MVBs);2 when MVBs fuse with the plasma membrane, exosomes are released. Exosomes possess biocompatibility and the structural capacity to carry both hydrophilic agents (in the aqueous core) and poorly soluble small-molecule drugs (within the lipid bilayer).3 Nevertheless, exosomes are limited by short half-lives in vivo, rapid clearance, and difficulty accumulating at target sites,4 and insufficient to overcome the pharmacokinetic barriers encountered in skin rehabilitation.

Hydrogels are three-dimensional network structures formed by crosslinking natural or synthetic polymers. They can absorb large amounts of water or bodily fluids while retaining a semi-solid form.5,6 Hydrogels can be fabricated into microspheres, nanoparticles, films, and other forms to meet diverse drug-delivery needs.7–9 When engineered to respond to inflammatory signals—such as pH, temperature, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and enzymes—they are termed “smart hydrogels” and enable targeted, stimuli-triggered release.10,11 Owing to their highly tunable network architecture, inherent bio-responsiveness, and modular multifunctional integration, smart hydrogels have emerged as intelligent drug-delivery platforms. Importantly, incorporating exosomes into hydrogels has been shown to preserve, or even enhance, the stability of encapsulated proteins and microRNAs.12,13 As the hydrogel network gradually degrades, exosomes are released via long-term diffusion, which minimizes potential adverse reactions caused by fluctuations in drug concentration following rapid bolus administration.14 Furthermore, the retention time of exosomes can be prolonged by adjusting the hydrogel structure and degradation behavior. Another advantage is that exosome-loaded hydrogels can function both as injectable therapeutics and as biocompatible scaffold-based wound dressings. Collectively, this smart hydrogel–exosome system combines the inherent benefits of hydrogel materials with spatiotemporally controlled release, thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy and surpassing conventional delivery systems in terms of release precision and timeliness.

Nevertheless, systematic reviews in this area remain limited, and the advantages and limitations of smart hydrogel–exosome systems require deeper exploration. By integrating principles of structural design with therapeutic mechanisms, this review offers a new perspective on smart hydrogel–exosome research. We focus particularly on exosome sources, isolation and loading strategies, the fundamental composition of smart hydrogels, the principles of intelligent responsiveness, and the research progress and translational applications of smart hydrogel–exosome systems in skin rehabilitation, with the goal of providing a comprehensive framework to guide future development and clinical adoption.

Exosomes and the Fundamentals of Their Use as Drug Carriers

Secreted by cells as nanoscale vesicles, exosomes contain a variety of biomolecules in their cargo, including proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, and nucleic acids. Their precise composition depends on the source of the exosomes, the physiological or pathological state, and even the specific site of cellular release. As mediators of intercellular communication, exosomes deliver chemically active payloads to target cells.15 When exosomes fuse with the target-cell membrane layer, the vesicular structure ruptures and transfers its contents and surface proteins to recipient cells. With growing understanding of their functions, researchers are exploring the potential of exosomes as drug-delivery platforms. Although carriers such as liposomes,16 carbon nanotubes,17 and nonionic surfactant vesicles18 offer abundant possibilities for drug delivery, the phospholipid bilayer structure of exosomes resembles that of cell membranes and therefore elicits relatively low immune responses.19 In particular, exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) or immature dendritic cells are almost non-immunogenic.20 Their lipid bilayer membrane also protects internal drugs from enzymatic degradation in the bloodstream, thereby prolonging circulation half-life and helping intact drugs reach the target site.20 Moreover, the nanoscale size and specific membrane components of exosomes allow them to cross tightly regulated biological barriers, especially the blood–brain barrier. This property provides a new non-invasive route for treating central nervous system diseases such as glioma, Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease.21 Notably, the exosomes can simultaneously carry and protect multiple therapeutic agents, which include hydrophilic and hydrophobic small-molecule drugs, proteins/peptides, nucleic acids (siRNA, miRNA, mRNA), and gene-editing tools such as CRISPR/Cas9. Therefore, they have unique advantages in delivering diverse drugs and therapeutic molecules.

Sources of Exosomes for Drug Delivery

Exosomes used for drug delivery come from a broad range of sources. The main sources currently investigated and applied can be divided into the following categories:

Plant-derived Vesicles/exosome-Like Nanovesicles

In recent years, research on small vesicles secreted by plant cells—plant extracellular vesicle-like nanovesicles (PELNVs)—has attracted broad attention.22–24 PELNVs are increasingly valued because of their high biocompatibility, immunomodulatory effects, promotion of angiogenesis, enhancement of cell proliferation and migration, scalability of production, and strong safety profile.24–27 PELNVs extracted from aloe vera can promote skin regeneration and accelerate wound healing by activating antioxidant defense mechanisms, whereas ginseng-derived PELNVs may exhibit anti-aging and skin-brightening effects.28

Source of Cell Culture in vitro

Large-scale in vitro cell culture followed by collection of secreted exosomes is currently the most mainstream and scalable strategy. (1) Stem cell-derived exosomes regulate intercellular communication by transferring mRNA, miRNA, proteins, and other components to recipient cells, thereby stimulating skin-cell growth and proliferation during wound healing.29 They are mainly derived from bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord,30 dental pulp, and other tissues. Their advantages include extremely low immunogenicity, innate homing ability, and endogenous therapeutic molecules with anti-inflammatory and pro-repair effects, making them widely used in drug delivery systems, regenerative medicine, and tissue engineering. MSCs, for example, are a heterogeneous class of stromal regenerative cells that can be isolated from various adult tissues.31 Because MSC-derived exosomes are rich in physiologically active molecules, including proteins and RNAs, they can better respond to external stimuli and maintain tissue homeostasis. Exosomes isolated from gingival MSC supernatants and loaded into chitosan/silk fibroin hydrogels have been shown to improve re-epithelialization, collagen deposition, micro-vessel density, and nerve ingrowth.32

(2) Immune-cell-derived exosomes mainly originate from dendritic cells, macrophages, natural killer cells, T cells, and other immune cells. These exosomes may inherit the immunomodulatory functions of their parent cells. For example, dendritic-cell-derived exosomes can present antigens and are therefore useful in tumor immunotherapy,33 while macrophage-derived exosomes may possess inflammation-targeting properties.

(3) Exosomes derived from tissue functional cells (eg, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, neural cells) For instance, exosomes derived from human dermal fibroblasts and loaded with the antifibrotic drug pirfenidone can serve as carriers for antifibrotic delivery in skin rehabilitation to prevent scar formation.34 Endothelial-cell-derived exosomes are a typical representative of sources from in vitro cultured cells and can be used to target vascular diseases, particularly those associated with angiogenesis. Li et al induced the formation of exosomes from hypoxia-preconditioned human umbilical vein endothelial cells and combined them with GelMA hydrogels to achieve sustained exosome release. This significantly enhanced endothelial-cell migration, proliferation, and angiogenesis.35 Neural-cell-derived exosomes can transmit neural signals and are often used in drug delivery for neurological disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease. Neuron-derived exosomes have been shown to carry synaptic proteins, such as AMPA receptors, which are crucial for synaptic plasticity and neuroprotection.36

Source of Engineered/biomimetic

To overcome the limitations of natural exosomes, such as low drug-loading efficiency, low yield, and insufficient targeting, researchers have developed engineering strategies. ① Parent cells can be genetically engineered so that the exosomes they secrete display specific targeting peptides or therapeutic proteins on their surfaces, thereby acquiring “customized” functionality.37 Yet it entails the highest operational complexity and regulatory hurdles. ②Physical extrusion,38 ultrasonication,39 and related methods can reorganize cellular plasma membranes into nanoscale vesicles. These cell-membrane-biomimetic nanovesicles can be produced at scale, retain some parent-cell membrane proteins and functions, and offer greater flexibility in drug loading. This approach aims to achieve a balance between large-scale production and the retention of biological functionality. ③Hybrid exosomes/biomimetic vesicles are formed by fusing exosomes with synthetic liposomes or nanoparticle membranes through extrusion, ultrasonication, or freeze–thaw methods. These strategies can combine the strengths of both systems and improve exosome yield, stability, targeting ability, and drug-loading capacity40 (Figure 1). Isolation Methods for Exosomes

Currently, the main isolation methods for exosomes used as drug delivery systems include: ultracentrifugation (differential centrifugation and density gradient centrifugation), size-based methods (ultrafiltration, size-exclusion chromatography, and microfluidic filtration), and immunoaffinity capture41,42 (Figure 2). Among these, ultracentrifugation is a well-established technique with broad applicability, suitable for large-scale preliminary isolation, while density gradient centrifugation can yield exosomes of high purity. Size-exclusion chromatography is simple to operate and can preserve the integrity and functionality of exosomes relatively well, but it has low processing capacity. Immunoaffinity capture, due to its high specificity and purity, can isolate specific subpopulations of exosomes from complex samples; however, it is costly, and the elution process may lead to loss or inactivation of exosomes. Drug-Loading Methods for Exosomes

The strategies for loading drugs into exosomes are generally categorized into pre-isolation and post-isolation based on the chronological order of drug loading and vesicle isolation. The pre-isolation strategies are to pre-treat the donor cells with the drug and then secrete exosomes containing the drug.43 These strategies primarily involve two methods. In the co-incubation method, the drug is co-cultured with donor cells, taken up by them, and subsequently packaged into exosomes.44 This method is suitable for certain hydrophobic small molecules. Furthermore, the genetic engineering/transfection method is to treat the donor cells with plasmids, viral vectors or transfection reagents to induce the overexpression of specific proteins or nucleic acids, and then these products are encapsulated into exosomes.45 This method is suitable for loading nucleic acid substances such as mRNA, siRNA, and specific proteins, and enabling endogenous expression of targeted molecules.46 In contrast, the post-isolation strategies are to directly load the drug into the pre-isolated exosomes. These strategies include co-incubation/passive loading, electroporation, ultrasonication, and extrusion.47,48 Overall, the choice of strategy mainly depends on the physicochemical properties of the drug and the therapeutic requirements.

Exosome drug-loading strategies have been reviewed above. The following section addresses the internalization of exosomes by recipient cells, an essential process for drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy.

Exosome Internalization

Target cells internalize exosomes through multiple pathways, including clathrin-mediated endocytosis, lipid-raft-associated internalization, macro-pinocytosis, phagocytosis, caveolin-mediated endocytosis, and membrane fusion49,50 (Figure 3). Endocytic pathways, particularly those mediated by clathrin and caveolin, represent the predominant mechanism for exosome uptake in most cell types. In contrast, membrane fusion enables direct release of exosomal cargo into the cytosol, thereby enhancing the intracellular delivery efficiency of bioactive molecules. Fundamentals of Smart Hydrogel Design

Exosome-mediated drug delivery offers many advantages, but exosomes can be readily lost with body fluids or displaced from the application site due to movement and friction, causing a rapid decline in local drug concentration and reduced therapeutic efficacy. Owing to their unique properties and therapeutic potential in inflammation-related diseases, hydrogels have emerged as highly promising drug-delivery carriers. The porous three-dimensional hydrogel network provides a matrix for exosome adsorption. Encapsulating exosomes in hydrogels effectively restricts their Brownian motion, thereby ensuring their retention.51 In addition, when exosome-loaded hydrogels are applied locally, such as in skin rehabilitation, they can form a film on the skin surface, enhance retention time, and promote sustained release across the skin.52

On the basis of conventional hydrogels, smart hydrogels can be created by integrating chemical functional groups and bioactive recognition components, such as stimulus-responsive molecules and growth factors.53 Drug release from smart hydrogels is no longer constant; rather, it responds to specific signals in the lesion microenvironment, namely “stimulus signals”, and manifests substantial, reversible macroscopic changes in physical or chemical properties. This enables “on-demand” release, thereby improving efficacy and reducing side effects.

Compositional Elements

The material system of smart hydrogels includes the polymer backbone, responsive elements, and functional additives. For smart drug delivery hydrogels, material selection centers on molecular engineering. This allows polymers to accurately and reversibly respond to stimuli like acidity or high blood sugar, governing drug loading, retention, and release.

The polymer backbone provides the principal structural framework and mechanical properties of the hydrogel. Drugs (including exosomes) can enter the hydrogel pores via physical diffusion. The drug adsorption capacity can then be controlled by modulating the specific surface area of the polymer.54 Furthermore, the graft density of reactive functional groups (eg, carboxyl, amino, and sulfhydryl groups) on the polymer chains also influences the drug-loading capacity of the hydrogel.55 Consequently, controlling the loading capacity depends on the drug type. For small-molecule hydrophilic drugs, it primarily relies on chemical grafting or a porous structure with a high specific surface area.54 For macromolecules (eg, exosomes), it is highly dependent on pore architecture compatibility and affinity anchoring (eg, via integrin-ligand binding).55 Drug release can be controlled by altering the polymer crosslinking density to modulate drug diffusion; alternatively, switching from physical adsorption to chemical anchoring can transform the release profile from first-order to zero-order or near-zero-order. Similarly, tuning the degradation rate (eg, by controlling the oxidation degree of alginate) can precisely match first-order or linear release profiles.56 Some polymers (eg, alginate, agarose) are inherently anti-adhesive (preventing non-specific protein adsorption). The extracellular matrix (eg, collagen, fibronectin) contains specific amino acid sequences (eg, RGD, YIGSR, IKVAV), which can be specifically recognized and bound by integrin receptors on the cell surface. Therefore, short peptides containing these bioactive sequences can be covalently grafted onto the natural polymer backbone via chemical methods (eg, EDC/NHS coupling) to enhance cell adhesion.57 Thus, meticulous regulation of polymer molecular structure and crosslinking mechanisms allows for precise control over key biological behaviors. These include drug loading capacity, release kinetics, cell adhesion, and proliferation.

Polymer materials may be divided into four main categories based on the origin and molecular characteristics. ①Natural polymers, such as sodium alginate, hyaluronic acid, chitosan, cellulose, starch, dextran, agarose, natural gums, and plant polysaccharides,58 are naturally sourced, biodegradable, and highly biocompatible. Numerous studies have shown that, through precise regulation of molecular structure and crosslinking mechanisms, natural-polymer hydrogels can accurately control drug loading, release profiles, cell adhesion, proliferation, and other key biological behaviors.12 ② Synthetic polymers, including poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA), poly (acrylic acid) (PAA) and its derivatives, polyurethane (PU), polylactic acid (PLA), and poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), offer strong designability and stable, controllable properties.59 ③ Protein-based materials, such as gelatin and collagen—for example, collagen hydrogels derived from porcine skin have shown controllable drug-release properties because of their transparency and rigidity60 —and silk fibroin,61 which can be structurally modified to prepare functional hydrogels with specific properties for controlled drug release and tissue regeneration. In situ programmable hydrogels based on real-time biomarker fluctuations can achieve ligand-responsive hydrogel remodeling through protein communication networks embedding CRISPR-Cas systems, thereby autonomously adjusting material stiffness or drug-release profiles in response to cellular signals.62 Integrating such innovations with patient-specific omics data may enable personalized hydrogel therapies and bridge the gap between laboratory design and clinical need. ④ DNA hydrogels. The programmability and predictability of DNA make it an ideal functional building block for synthesizing responsive DNA hydrogels.63 One type is pure DNA hydrogel,64 prepared through DNA polymerization enabled by biochemical reactions such as branched-DNA self-assembly,65 enzymatic ligation of branched DNA, and crosslinking of macromolecular DNA chains.66 Pure DNA hydrogels possess the programmable intelligence of DNA and other unique properties, but they also suffer from highly unfavorable overall charge, limited durability, and a lack of functional clusters for subsequent modification. Heterogeneous DNA hydrogels67 are prepared by attaching short hairpin-forming DNA sequences or predesigned DNA strands68 to hydrophilic polymer backbones. Both types combine self-assembly, programmability, stimulus responsiveness, and the physicochemical properties of hydrogels themselves. Ye et al, for example, designed a multifunctional drug-loaded DNA hydrogel using linear DNA chains containing self-complementary palindromic sequences and disulfide bonds as linkers through an amidation reaction.69

Intelligent Response Mechanism

Smart hydrogels are developing toward enhanced functionality, such as tunable physicochemical properties, precise conformability, and advanced functions triggered by microenvironmental cues such as temperature, pH, and ROS, these systems can achieve controlled and precise delivery of exosomes and other bioactive factors.70

(1) Thermo-responsiveness exploits the temperature-dependent interaction forces between the polymer network and the solvent, usually water. These changes induce reversible phase transitions, manifesting macroscopically as swelling, shrinkage, or a switch between solution and gel states.71 For example, a PLGA-PEG-PLGA triblock copolymer thermosensitive hydrogel undergoes a rapid sol–gel transition at approximately 32 °C, and exhibits favorable mechanical stability as well as self-healing properties.72 Another research showed poly(N-isopropylacrylamide), in water below its lower critical solution temperature (LCST, approximately 32°C), water molecules form strong hydrogen bonds with the amide groups (-CONH-) on the polymer chains. The polymer chains are enveloped by a hydration layer, remaining extended and soluble. When the temperature rises, the thermal motion of water molecules intensifies. As the temperature approaches the LCST, these hydrogen bonds are disrupted, leading to the destruction of the hydration layer. Concurrently, the hydrophobic interactions among isopropyl groups and other hydrophobic moieties are enhanced, driving the polymer chains to collapse, aggregate, and precipitate.73

(2) pH responsiveness takes advantage of substantial pH differences in biological microenvironments, thereby enabling targeted delivery.74 Two main strategies are used to construct pH-responsive platforms. The first introduces ionizable groups, such as carboxyl or amino groups, whose charge density changes with pH, thereby regulating polymer swelling or contraction and controlling drug diffusion and release.75 The second strategy links drugs are that Schiff base bonds can serve as pH-sensitive dynamic bonds that promote network cleavage and drug release in an acidic environment.76 Li et al developed a pH-responsive hydrogel formed through dynamic crosslinking of amphoteric chitosan and multi-arm polyethylene glycol.77 Niknejad et al constructed a novel pH-responsive platform by electrospinning a blend of hydroxypropyl methylcellulose and poly (vinyl alcohol), followed by thermal crosslinking with citric acid. This system exhibited the lowest release at pH 4.5 (healthy skin), moderate release at pH 6.0, and a cumulative release rate exceeding 87% at pH 7.4 (diseased skin).78

(3) Light responsiveness: A variety of light sources, including near-infrared light,79–81 visible light,82–84 and ultraviolet light,85–87 can be used to modulate the properties of photo-responsive hydrogels by controlling the wavelength, intensity, and irradiation duration. One representative mechanism is photoisomerization. For example, azobenzene molecules undergo a trans-to-cis conformational transition upon ultraviolet irradiation, typically at 365 nm. And then it can reversibly return to the trans configuration under visible light or thermal stimulation. This conformational change alters the dipole moment and spatial configuration of azobenzene molecules. When azobenzene is incorporated into a hydrogel network as a crosslinking point, these molecular-level changes can be macroscopically manifested as hydrogel shrinkage or swelling.88 In addition, photodegradation can be used to achieve light-triggered release. For instance, photosensitive chemical groups, such as o-nitrobenzyl derivatives, can be introduced into the hydrogel backbone. Upon ultraviolet irradiation, these groups undergo cleavage, leading to the gradual disintegration of the hydrogel network and the release of encapsulated exosomes. Kim et al prepared monodisperse photodegradable hydrogel microparticles using microfluidic technology. The study showed that, in the absence of light irradiation, the exosomes were stably retained inside the hydrogel microparticles. However, after only 2 minutes of ultraviolet irradiation, the microparticles degraded rapidly, enabling the prompt release of exosomes.89

(4) Glucose responsiveness is particularly relevant to diabetic wounds, where local hyperglycemia aggravates inflammation and impairs healing. Hydrogels that respond to high glucose—such as those using glucose oxidase, phenylboronic acid (PBA), and its derivatives—and release pro-healing agents (for example exosomes and growth factors) or hypoglycemic drugs represent a precise therapeutic strategy. Wen et al encapsulated mangiferin-loaded gelatin microspheres (MGF@GMs) within a hydrogel network formed by PBA-grafted chitosan and dopamine-modified oxidized sodium alginate. In response to elevated glucose and ROS levels, the hydrogel underwent intelligent dissociation mediated by Schiff-base and borate-ester bonds, achieving targeted release of the payload.90

(5) ROS responsiveness: Excessive ROS in wound microenvironments are a key driver of oxidative stress and chronic inflammation. ROS-responsive hydrogels usually contain ROS-sensitive chemical groups, such as thioether bonds, phenylboronic ester bonds, or catechol groups, which degrade at high ROS levels and release loaded therapeutic factors. For instance, under high ROS concentrations, the thioketal bonds were cleaved, causing network disintegration and exosome release while also consuming ROS.91 Jing et al designed a hydrogel based on dynamic phenylboronic ester bonds formed from phenylboronic-acid-modified flaxseed gum and PVA; under high concentrations of ROS, the PBA groups undergo oxidation, triggering cleavage of the carbon–boron bonds and subsequent disintegration of the crosslinked network.92

(6) Magnetic/electric-field responsiveness. Because magnetic fields cause minimal physical harm to the human body, they are considered ideal external stimuli.93 For example, a magnetic-field-responsive dual-chamber hydrogel was prepared using gelatin and diphasic iron gel.94 Its porous outer gel carried pro-inflammatory cytokines that recruit macrophages, while the inner diphasic iron-gel core was divided into two parts according to whether it contained Fe3O4; under a gradient external magnetic field, deformation of the core released anti-inflammatory factors. Wang et al developed a multifunctional conductive hydrogel that integrated adipose-derived MSC exosomes into a network formed by quaternized chitosan–polyaniline crosslinked with oxidized dextran. When combined with external electrical stimulation, the hydrogel significantly enhanced proliferation, migration, and differentiation of fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and macrophages.95

(7) Antigen–antibody responsiveness. Some smart hydrogels’ responsiveness relies on highly specific, structurally complementary molecular recognition,96 such as antigen–antibody binding, biotin–avidin complexes, host–guest assembly (eg, cyclodextrin–adamantane), and Watson–Crick DNA base pairing.97 Bai et al developed a chitosan/β- glycerophosphate hydrogel sustained-release system loaded with TNFα-specific IgY antibodies. The IgY antibodies specifically bound and neutralized the key inflammatory factor TNFα, thereby precisely regulating inflammatory pathways.98

(8) Enzyme responsiveness. Meng et al constructed an enzyme-responsive smart hydrogel that dynamically regulated the release kinetics of M2-Exos under stimulation by high concentrations of MMP-9 in inflammatory microenvironments.99 Sun et al prepared an enzyme-responsive cryo-self-assembled silk fibroin sponge for controlled exosome release and found that the exosome release rate was similar to the in vitro enzymatic degradation rate of the hydrogel.100

With continuing research, more dual-responsive hydrogels have been developed, such as glucose/ROS dual-responsive hydrogels101 and pH/glucose dual-responsive hydrogels,102 enabling on-demand release, improved drug utilization, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, these studies show that intelligent responsiveness allows smart hydrogels to shift from passive carriers to active, context-dependent delivery platforms.

Fabrication Strategies

The purpose of hydrogel fabrication is to construct the above materials into stable three-dimensional hydrogel networks while integrating responsive elements. The synthesis method determines the hydrogel’s mechanical properties, swelling behavior, degradation rate, and ability to encapsulate and release bioactive compounds.

Physical Crosslinking Methods

Physical crosslinking forms stable three-dimensional polymer networks through non-covalent interactions. The gelation process is mild and usually reversible; therefore, it causes relatively little damage to the bioactivity of exosomes and other bioactive substances.103,104 ①Ionic crosslinking uses divalent cations, such as Ca2⁺ and Zn2⁺, or polyelectrolytes as bridging agents to connect anionic biopolymers, such as alginate and gellan gum.105,106 ②Hydrogen-bonding or crystalline-domain crosslinking relies on non-covalent interactions within the polymer network. Although hydrogen bonds are weaker than covalent bonds, strategically arranged hydrogen-bonding interactions can endow materials with self-healing or shear-thinning properties, especially when they are incorporated into supramolecular assemblies.107 ③The freeze–thaw method is a crosslinking technique based on the reconstruction of polymer chains during repeated freezing and thawing cycles.108 For example, repeated freeze–thaw treatment of poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA) produces highly elastic gels through the formation of ice crystals and PVA crystalline domains as physical crosslinking points, together with topological entanglements induced by chain folding, such as β-sheet crystallization in silk fibroin.109,110 This method can significantly increase the specific surface area and pore size of hydrogels. As a result, exosomes can be adsorbed onto the pore walls rather than being confined only within the gel matrix, thereby greatly improving loading efficiency. ④Thermally induced phase separation occurs when hydrated polymer chains, remain in a solvated state below the critical solution temperature. Upon heating, dehydration and the assembly of hydrophobic domains are induced, leading to the formation of physically water-trapping compartments.111

In physically crosslinked hydrogels, the network structure is mainly maintained by non-covalent bonds. Therefore, the network is relatively loose, and the crosslinking points are prone to dynamic rearrangement. These characteristics usually result in limited exosome loading capacity and relatively low loading efficiency. Moreover, under physiological conditions, such hydrogels may swell or dissociate, leading to rapid release of the encapsulated exosomes.112

Chemical Crosslinking Methods

Chemical crosslinking involves reactions between chemical crosslinkers, such as glutaraldehyde and isocyanates, and specific functional groups on polymer chains to form three-dimensional network structures. By adjusting the concentration of the crosslinker, light intensity, or irradiation time, the crosslinking density, pore size, and gelation kinetics of hydrogels can be precisely regulated. Therefore, chemical crosslinking allows better control over exosome loading capacity and release duration.

①Free-radical polymerization involves mixing monomers, crosslinkers, and initiators in solution, followed by chain-growth polymerization initiated by heating or by the addition of catalysts, thereby forming a covalent network. For example, Liu et al introduced a crosslinker to initiate free-radical polymerization and form a polyacrylamide network.113 ②The Schiff base reaction occurs when aldehyde-containing polymers and primary amine-containing polymers, such as oxidized sodium alginate and chitosan, are mixed to rapidly form dynamic covalent networks.114 These networks often exhibit self-healing properties. For example, biopolymer hydrogels synthesized through Schiff base reactions between polyaldehydes and the amino groups of chitosan have shown pH responsiveness, reversibility, injectability, and self-healing capability.115,116 ③The amidation reaction refers to the condensation between carboxyl groups (-COOH) and amino groups (-NH2) under specific conditions, during which one water molecule is removed and a highly stable amide bond (-CO-NH-) is formed. A stable chemically crosslinked network can effectively “lock” exosomes within the hydrogel during gelation, thereby reducing burst release and exosome loss during storage and the early stage after implantation.117 In addition, by introducing specific chemical groups, such as hydrolysable ester bonds or oxidative modifications, the degradation period of the hydrogel can be precisely regulated.118 ④Photo-crosslinking uses ultraviolet or visible light to activate crosslinkers, which then react with polymer chains to form hydrogels. This method enables rapid curing under low light intensity, requires only a short exposure time, generates minimal heat, and offers potential spatiotemporal control.119 For example, platelet-derived exosome-loaded hydrogels based on methacrylated decellularized dermal matrix and formed by ultraviolet photo-crosslinking have been shown to accelerate diabetic wound healing.120

Chemical crosslinking procedures are usually more complex and place higher demands on reaction conditions and purification processes. They often involve catalysts or solvents with potential cytotoxicity. Therefore, modification strategies with high reaction efficiency and improved safety are increasingly preferred. ⑤Click chemistry can rapidly form covalent bonds under mild reaction conditions. This approach reduces the need for harsh chemical reagents. For example, the bio-orthogonal Diels–Alder click reaction between tetrazine and trans-cyclooctene can proceed efficiently under physiological conditions without external chemical catalysts.121 In addition, chitosan-based films synthesized using naturally derived dicarboxylic and tricarboxylic acid solvent systems combined with thiol–ene/yne Michael addition click chemistry have demonstrated enhanced mechanical strength and improved barrier properties, showing considerable potential for transdermal drug delivery applications.122 ⑥Enzyme-catalyzed crosslinking uses enzymes such as horseradish peroxidase and transglutaminase to catalyze the crosslinking of specific functional groups. This method is not only biologically friendly but also highly controllable by adjusting the concentrations of the biopolymer and enzyme. It offers several advantages, including mild reaction conditions and low toxicity. For example, ferulic acid-rich arabinoxylan extracted from corn fiber can be crosslinked by horseradish peroxidase, producing hydrogels with a highly porous structure and large heterogeneous aggregates.123

Exosome-Loaded Hydrogel

Current strategies for exosome-loaded hydrogel have evolved from simple physical encapsulation to the active anchoring of exosomes within hydrogel networks through chemical or physical approaches. As a result, the release duration has been extended from only a few days to more than one month.

Physical Encapsulation Methods

Physical encapsulation refers to the simple, non-covalent “trapping” of exosomes within the three-dimensional porous network of hydrogels through mechanical or physical means. This approach is the simplest to perform, involves mild conditions, and is highly compatible with fragile exosomes. However, because the pore size of hydrogels is usually much larger than the size of exosomes, physically encapsulated exosomes can be released in large quantities within several hours, making long-term sustained release difficult to achieve.124

① Electrostatic interaction: This method utilizes the naturally negative surface charge of exosomal membranes to bind them to positively charged hydrogel carriers. For example, MSC-derived exosomes exhibit a markedly negative surface charge, reflected by a negative zeta potential, and this property can be exploited to design electrostatic capture systems.125 Wang et al covalently modified a hydrogel backbone with the zwitterionic choline phosphate (CP) group. Through electrostatic interactions, CP specifically bound to the naturally negatively charged membrane components of stem cell-derived exosomes, such as phosphatidylserine and sialic acid.126

② Self-assembly: In this approach, three-dimensional nanofibrous networks are spontaneously formed by the self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules or short peptides under physiological conditions, whereby exosomes are encapsulated in situ within the resulting hydrogel.127 Self-assembly does not rely on the surface charge or specific functional groups of exosomes. Instead, it achieves spatial confinement of exosomes through the physical barrier effect generated during hydrogel network formation. Because this method does not involve chemical modification or charge regulation of the exosomal surface, it maximally preserves the native state and biological activity of exosomes.128 However, the gelation kinetics and mechanical properties of self-assembled systems, such as the storage modulus G′, must be precisely regulated to avoid low encapsulation efficiency or burst release. Luo et al demonstrated this using the chiral self-assembling peptide R-LIFE-1. Upon ionic triggering, the peptide forms a nanofibrous network via hydrogen bonding and intermolecular forces. This physical scaffold then entraps exosomes. Consequently, it preserves the native state and biological activity of exosomes while achieving sustained release over 10 days.129

③ Hydrophobic interaction is an entropy-driven non-covalent interaction. In aqueous environments, non-polar groups, such as the hydrophobic core of exosomal lipid membranes and alkyl side chains on hydrogel polymers, spontaneously aggregate to reduce disruption of the hydrogen-bonding network of water molecules. Patel et al incorporated MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) into supramolecular hydrogels based on dodecyl (C12)- or octadecyl (C18)-modified hydroxypropyl methylcellulose. The study confirmed that hydrophobically modified polymer chains could insert into exosomal membranes through hydrophobic interactions, thereby forming stable hydrogel networks and enabling sustained exosome release.130 A recent study provided further detail: alkyl-modified HPMC polymers, such as those modified with C12, C14, C16, or C18 chains, were mixed with extracellular vesicles (EVs) to form supramolecular networks through hydrophobic interactions. Meanwhile, the mechanical properties of the gel, such as stiffness and self-healing behavior, could be precisely regulated by adjusting either the exosome concentration or the alkyl chain length, thereby establishing a “structure-as-function” system.128

④ Hydrogen-bond interaction: Hydrogen-bond-mediated incorporation commonly involves non-covalent interactions between functional groups on polymer chains, such as hydroxyl, amino, and carboxyl groups, and polar head groups or glycosylation sites on membrane proteins at the exosomal surface. It represents an important driving force for constructing supramolecular hydrogel–exosome hybrid systems. Guan et al used polar groups, such as hydroxyl and amino groups, on conductive hydrogel chains to reversibly bind glycoproteins or phospholipid polar head groups on the membrane surface of M2 microglia-derived exosomes (M2-Exos) through hydrogen bonding, thereby achieving in situ immobilisation of exosomes.131 In addition, hydrogen bonding may also play an auxiliary role in hydrogel–exosome hybrid systems. For example, alkyl-modified hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC-Cx) can insert into EV lipid membranes through hydrophobic interactions to form a dynamic crosslinked network. However, the abundant hydroxyl groups on the polymer chains may also form hydrogen bonds with the EV membrane surface, thereby further stabilizing the hybrid structure128 (Figure 4). Chemical Methods

Chemical methods use covalent bonds, affinity interactions, or related mechanisms to firmly anchor exosomes onto the hydrogel framework. These methods provide strong linkage and can achieve ultra-long and stable release.132

① Surface functionalization: Surface functionalization involves introducing specific functional groups, such as antibodies or affinity ligands, onto the hydrogel surface to promote specific binding between exosomes and hydrogels. Deng et al grafted NHS-PEG12-biotin onto the amino groups of gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA). They also modified exosomal membranes with biotin to obtain biotinylated exosomes (Bio-EXs). Using streptavidin as a bridge, Bio-EXs were immobilized within Bio-GelMA. This strategy combines high binding strength with good biocompatibility and represents a precise and robust anchoring approach.133

② Phenylboronic ester crosslinking: PBA and its derivatives can form reversible boronic ester bonds with molecules containing cis-diol groups, such as glucose, glycoproteins, and dopamine. These dynamic covalent bonds are responsive to glucose and ROS. Yuan et al developed a glucose-responsive multifunctional hydrogel composed of methacrylated hyaluronic acid, methacrylated chitosan, and 3-methacrylamidophenylboronic acid. The PBA groups formed boronic ester bonds with glycosyl groups on the surfaces of polymers and exosomes. Under high-glucose conditions, competitive binding to PBA triggered exosome release.134 Xiao et al synthesised aldehyde/phenylboronic acid dual-functionalised dextran (di-Dex) to construct a hydrogel framework with self-adhesive properties and ROS/glucose-responsive regulatory capability. Dopamine methacrylamide (DMA) was conjugated to exosomes to produce modified exosomes (mExo), thereby introducing catechol groups. The catechol groups on the surface of mExo formed reversible boronic ester bonds with the PBA groups of di-Dex. In hyperglycaemic and ROS-enriched microenvironments, cleavage of the boronic ester bonds was triggered, resulting in the release of mExo.135

③ Derivative peptide binding: This method exploits the binding between integrin receptors on the exosomal surface and specific peptide sequences immobilized within the hydrogel, such as collagen-derived RGD or DGEA motifs, to achieve exosome loading in hydrogels. For example, fibronectin-derived RGD mimetic peptides were introduced into alginate hydrogels to bind MSC-derived, functionally engineered exosomes with osteo-inductive activity. The hydrogel retained the exosomes for seven days while maintaining their biological activity.136 Wu et al encapsulated kartogenin (KGN) and halofuginone (HF) separately into human umbilical cord MSC-derived exosomes, producing EXO-KGN and EXO-HF. The specific peptide CP05 was used to bind selectively to the highly expressed CD63 protein on the exosomal surface, thereby specifically immobilizing EXO-HF within GelMA hydrogel, whereas EXO-KGN was incorporated in a non-binding manner. This design enabled rapid release of EXO-KGN and sustained retention of EXO-HF, thereby achieving sequential therapeutic effects.137

④ Covalent anchoring: Covalent anchoring refers to the formation of stable chemical bonds between the hydrogel matrix and exosomes through chemical reactions. For example, using DNA grafting and atom transfer radical polymerisation (ATRP), Yerneni et al developed an ATRP-based exosome-anchored hydrogel. Cholesterol-DNA initiators were modified onto exosomal membranes, and polymer grafting was then performed via photoinduced ATRP, allowing the exosomes to participate in the hydrogel polymerisation process. This approach not only covalently anchors exosomes within the hydrogel network and enables release for up to one month, but also allows the exosome release profile to be regulated by altering the crosslinking density of the polymer network. Therapeutic Mechanisms and Applications of Smart Hydrogel-Exosome Systems in Skin Rehabilitation

The skin is the largest and outermost organ of the human body. It is crucial for the regulation of water balance and body temperature and for the production of neuropeptides, hormonal signals, cytokines, and other mediators.139 It also plays essential roles in protection, excretion, and sensory perception. However, when it is damaged, the healing process often faces multiple challenges such as infection, inflammatory imbalance, and regenerative disorders. Meanwhile, the traditional treatments often fail to achieve the precise and dynamic regulation required for sequential and dynamic processes. The smart hydrogel-exosome system is precisely an advanced strategy developed to address this issue. It can release therapeutic components on demand and in stages in response to external or physiological signals, thereby synergistically promoting high-quality skin recovery. This system demonstrates dynamic regulatory capabilities at different stages of wound healing. In the early stage of wound infection, hydrogels can rapidly release antibacterial agents to eliminate or suppress invading pathogens. Once infection is controlled, they can continuously release anti-inflammatory factors, such as exosomes, IL-4, and IL-10, or modulate macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 state to the reparative M2 state. In later stages, hydrogel degradation releases pro-growth factors that promote cell proliferation, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis, ultimately facilitating high-quality functional healing.140

Therapeutic Principles

① Maintenance of a moist healing environment and provision of a physical barrier. The three-dimensional network structure of hydrogels enables them to absorb and retain large amounts of water, thereby maintaining a moist environment for wounds and preventing the formation of dry, hard scabs after wound exudate evaporation. Hydrogels are soft and elastic and can therefore buffer irritation. In addition, the physical barrier formed by hydrogels effectively blocks bacteria, dust, and foreign matter, while anti-inflammatory factors carried by exosomes can reduce activation of nociceptive nerves by local inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins and bradykinin. Wei et al designed a PLGA-PEG-PLGA thermosensitive hydrogel for loading MSC exosomes. This hydrogel formed an in situ semi-solid physical barrier on irregular wound surfaces, effectively isolating the wound from the external environment and preventing dry scab formation. Meanwhile, its three-dimensional network structure displayed excellent water-retention capacity, thereby providing an ideal moist healing environment.72 These findings indicate that smart hydrogels serve not only as passive wound coverings, but also as functional barriers that stabilize the local healing environment.

② Modulation of the wound microenvironment: oxidative stress, inflammation, and immune regulation. When skin—especially aged skin—suffers injury, ROS production induces oxidative stress, which damages intracellular lipids, proteins, and DNA.141 In the microenvironment of skin wounds, bacterial infection, inflammation, oxidative stress, and other factors are intertwined and jointly determine whether the wound proceeds toward normal healing or becomes trapped in chronic inflammation or excessive scar formation. Antioxidant components in hydrogels can reduce free-radical-induced skin damage and promote repair. Polysaccharide hydrogels provide a dual antioxidant strategy: they can directly scavenge ROS and serve as carriers for antioxidant molecules,142,143 such as vitamins, polyphenols, flavonoids, and enzymatic antioxidants like SOD,144 thereby enabling controllable local release. A recently reported smart 3D-printed chitosan/collagen hydrogel loaded with exosomes and epidermal growth factor in the core and adsorbed glucose oxidase (GOx), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) in the outer layer uses polydopamine (PDA) as an acid-responsive shell. In an acidic inflammatory microenvironment, the PDA shell degrades and releases GOx/SOD/AMPs at an early stage for antibacterial and antioxidant action, followed by release of exosomes and epidermal growth factor to promote tissue regeneration.145 Xin et al found that puerarin-loaded gelatin microspheres embedded in a hydrogel formed by chitosan grafted with gastrodin and adsorbed oxidized gastrodia polysaccharides could effectively reduce oxidative stress in wound tissue cells, inhibit bacterial growth, and exert anti-inflammatory effects.146 In addition, let-7b contained in human umbilical cord MSCs alleviates inflammation and promotes wound healing by reducing M1 macrophage markers.147 Taken together, these studies suggest that smart hydrogels can reshape the wound microenvironment by integrating antioxidant, antibacterial, and immunomodulatory functions.

③ Promotion of cell migration, proliferation, and tissue regeneration. A major advantage of exosome-loaded smart hydrogels is their ability to promote cell migration, proliferation, and tissue regeneration through sustained delivery of bioactive signals. For example, exosomes derived from human umbilical cord MSCs contain Wnt proteins, particularly Wnt4, which can activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in skin cells, a pathway closely associated with cell proliferation and migration.148 Wu et al showed that a hydrogel containing exosomes extracted from human umbilical cord MSCs effectively promoted fibroblast proliferation and angiogenesis.149 Li et al incorporated exosomes derived from synovial MSC-126 exosomes into a hydroxyapatite/chitosan composite hydrogel.150 In addition to the pro-healing effects of the exosomes, chitosan promoted proliferation of skin fibroblasts and accelerated wound healing, whereas Ca2+ ions released by biodegradation of hydroxyapatite nanoparticles promoted keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation and could also induce angiogenesis.151 These findings demonstrate that smart hydrogels support tissue repair not only by preserving a favorable wound environment, but also by actively enhancing cellular behaviors essential for regeneration.

④ Regulation of collagen deposition and wound remodeling. Beyond the early phases of wound closure, smart hydrogels also contribute to tissue remodeling by regulating collagen deposition and scar-related pathways. Exosomes—especially those derived from various types of MSC—can more effectively promote collagen synthesis because of the biological information carried in their cargo.148 For example, Guo et al demonstrated that platelet-rich-plasma-derived exosomes promoted fibroblast proliferation and migration by activating the Rho/YAP signaling pathway, thereby enhancing collagen synthesis and deposition, improving re-epithelialization, and accelerating closure of chronic wounds.152 Wang et al developed an Exos-GelMA hydrogel, this hydrogel contained various non-coding RNAs, including miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs, as well as proteins, which enable them to regulate key signaling pathways such as TGF-β/Smad to inhibit fibrosis. Additionally, they significantly downregulated the overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), thereby suppressing pathological angiogenesis.153 Therefore, the role of smart hydrogels in skin rehabilitation extends beyond simple wound closure to the regulation of matrix remodeling and scar prevention.

Applications of Smart Hydrogels in Skin Rehabilitation

The therapeutic mechanisms described above show that smart hydrogels can not only maintain a moist local environment and regulate inflammation and oxidative stress, but also promote cell migration, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling through the controlled release bioactive components. Owing to these integrated advantages, smart hydrogels show broad application potential in diverse forms of skin injury and rehabilitation. Nevertheless, the therapeutic priorities are not entirely identical: burn care places particular emphasis on rapid cooling, pain relief, infection control, and re-epithelialization, whereas radiation-induced injury requires more sustained intervention against apoptosis, oxidative damage, and delayed tissue repair.

① Wound dressings. By maintaining a moist microenvironment, controlling infection, and providing nutritional support, this approach facilitates the sequential progression of skin wound healing through the three phases of hemostasis/inflammation, cellular proliferation, and tissue remodeling. As wound dressings, smart hydrogels can conform to irregular wound surfaces, maintain a moist healing environment, and enable the sustained local release of bioactive agents, including drugs, growth factors, or exosomes, thereby providing continuous and stable microenvironmental support for wound repair. Compared with conventional dressings, smart hydrogels not only offer good moisturizing and barrier- protective effects but can also further promote keratinocyte migration, fibroblast proliferation, and re-epithelialization by regulating local temperature, pH, and inflammatory status. A hydrogel based on recombinant human collagen I and carboxymethyl chitosan and loaded with exosomes derived from human umbilical cord MSCs (hUCMSCs) significantly promoted skin wound healing and regeneration.149 Another studies confirmed that stem cell-derived apoptotic vesicles can transfer intact mitochondria to damaged cutaneous MSCs and significantly accelerate the healing of skin wounds induced by 5-FU.154 These findings suggest that, in acute wound management, smart hydrogels function not merely as covering materials, but as bioactive platforms that integrate protection, local regulation, and regenerative support.

② Treatment of burns and radiation-induced skin injuries. Burns and radiation-induced skin injuries share several pathological features, including severe local tissue destruction, persistent inflammatory responses, enhanced oxidative stress, and aggravated apoptosis. Therefore, the core therapeutic mechanism lies in achieving protection, anti-inflammation, antioxidant effects, anti-apoptosis, and pro-regeneration. In burn treatment, smart hydrogels can form a covering layer on the wound surface to rapidly cool the site, relieve pain, reduce infection, and isolate external stimuli. Meanwhile, the sustained release of active components can improve the damaged tissue microenvironment and promote re-epithelialization.155 Researchers have developed a GelMA-MXene composite hydrogel loaded with engineered exosomes. By continuously releasing engineered exosomes that suppress miR-192-5p, the hydrogel upregulated expression of the cell-proliferation-related gene OLFM4 and effectively promoted re-epithelialization of burn wounds.156 Yeh et al developed a novel thermosensitive hydrogel system for burn-wound treatment in which Poloxamer F-127 remains liquid at low temperature and forms a protective gel at body temperature, enabling sustained local delivery. Oxygen supply mediated by perfluoronaphthalene created a favorable microenvironment that may enhance cellular endocytosis and thereby improve exosome uptake and bioavailability.63 Once internalized, the exosomes delivered therapeutic factors, neutralized ROS, suppressed inflammation, and promoted tissue regeneration.157 By contrast, radiation-induced skin injury is often characterized by persistent apoptosis and oxidative stress. Ren Boyuan et al developed a smart hydrogel in which a thermosensitive poloxamer hydrogel was used to encapsulate stem-cell-derived exosomes genetically modified with hepatocyte growth factor (HGF). In animal models, this system significantly prolonged exosome retention at wound sites and promoted healing by inhibiting the P53/P21 pathway, thereby reducing radiation-induced apoptosis and oxidative stress.158

③ Treatment of diabetic foot ulcers. Chronic refractory wounds such as diabetic foot ulcers are usually accompanied by persistent inflammation, increased risk of infection, impaired local microcirculation, and diminished tissue-regeneration capacity. Therefore, the therapeutic challenge lies not merely in wound coverage alone, but in achieving antibacterial action, immunomodulation, antioxidation, and tissue regeneration simultaneously. Owing to their tunable network structures and responsive release characteristics, smart hydrogels are well suited for long-term, precise, and local treatment of chronic wounds. He et al developed a gelatin methacryloyl–methacryloyl choline phosphate bioactive hydrogel loaded with adipose MSC exosomes (AE), while physically blending into the hydrogel matrix an antibacterial metal–organic framework activated by blue light to improve curcumin bioavailability.159 This bioactive hydrogel exhibited potent antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities and significantly promoted fibroblast proliferation and tissue regeneration. Zhang et al utilized four-arm PEG-SH and Ag⁺ to construct a self-healing conductive hydrogel via Ag-S coordination, and co-loaded ADSC exosomes with metformin. By interfering with mitochondrial fission, reducing ROS levels, protecting F-actin homeostasis, and alleviating microvascular dysfunction, this system promotes the healing of chronic diabetic wounds.160 These findings suggest that smart hydrogels are particularly valuable for diabetic foot ulcers and other chronic nonhealing wounds because they can simultaneously address infection, persistent inflammation, and impaired regenerative potential.

④ Applications in skin aging and photoaging. Aged skin is commonly characterized by collagen loss, reduced elasticity, accumulated oxidative damage, and weakened repair capacity, whereas photoaged skin is often accompanied by ultraviolet-induced inflammation and cellular damage. Therefore, the therapeutic core centers on antioxidant activity, hydration maintenance, collagen remodeling promotion, and skin barrier enhancement. Rather than primarily promoting wound closure, the key value of smart hydrogels in this setting is antioxidation, moisturization, promotion of collagen remodeling, and improvement of skin-barrier function. Smart hydrogels can provide a more stable local platform for anti-aging interventions through their good adhesiveness, water-retention ability, and active-component-delivery capability. Wang et al incorporated exosomes derived from olive leaves into a crosslinked hyaluronic acid/tannic acid hydrogel system with strong UV-absorption capacity and found that it effectively alleviated UV-induced photoaging and promoted skin repair and regeneration.28 Lee et al developed an injectable hyaluronic-acid hydrogel modified with gallol groups, which underwent in situ gelation via gallol-mediated auto-oxidation without requiring external crosslinkers. This hydrogel maintained mechanical strength (600–700 Pa) and showed effective wrinkle correction and volumizing effects lasting up to 12 months after application.161 These studies highlight that, in anti-aging applications, smart hydrogels are valued not only for biological repair, but also for their ability to improve structural support and visible skin quality.

⑤ Local treatment of inflammatory skin diseases. Inflammatory skin diseases, including eczema and psoriasis, essentially arise from a vicious cycle of immune dysregulation and aberrant epidermal structure. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies must address immunosuppression and stratum corneum restoration. In the treatment of inflammatory dermatoses smart hydrogels offer important advantages in local drug administration, reduction of systemic side effects, improved transdermal efficiency, and alleviation of local inflammation. Chen et al developed a near-infrared-light-responsive thermosensitive hydrogel loaded with methotrexate. Under near-infrared irradiation, the hydrogel underwent a gel–sol transition and synergistically induced apoptosis in abnormally proliferating keratinocytes while promoting drug penetration through the stratum corneum, thereby enabling local treatment of psoriasis.162 Additionally, hydrogel microspheres encapsulating Cutibacterium acnes-derived extracellular vesicles (CA-EVs) have been reported for psoriasis therapy. CA-EVs@GHM exerted immunosuppression by regulating innate lymphoid cell (ILC) subpopulations, suppressing the differentiation of pathogenic ILC3 cells and decreasing cutaneous levels of inflammatory cytokines including IL-17 and IL-22. Furthermore, this hydrogel system restored skin barrier function in animal models.163 These examples illustrate that, in inflammatory dermatoses, the principal advantage of smart hydrogels lies not merely in acting as drug carriers, but in improving local therapeutic precision and efficacy through stimulus-responsive delivery (Figure 6). Challenges and Future Prospects

The smart hydrogel–exosome system enables a paradigm shift in skin rehabilitation toward intelligent responsiveness, multi-target synergy, and prolonged controlled release. But its transition to clinical application still faces a series of scientific and technical challenges. Several technical obstacles remain to be overcome for the successful practical application of exosomes. First, the yield of cell-secreted exosomes is limited, and bioreactor and cell-membrane-biomimetic technologies have not yet fully matured. Second, exosome recovery suffers from low yield, insufficient purity, time-consuming processing, and poor scalability; contamination by impurities such as protein aggregates and lipoproteins can affect both efficacy and safety evaluation. Third, differences in size, cargo, and function among exosomes from different batches and sources affect consistency of therapeutic outcomes.88 Fourth, drug-loading efficiency remains low. Endogenous loading requires transfection of parent cells during cell culture, which is operationally complex and may impair cell and exosome function; exogenous loading can cause exosome aggregation or membrane damage, and the overall loading efficiency is generally unsatisfactory. Moreover, after drug loading, additional purification is usually required to remove unencapsulated drugs, which results in further losses. Fifth, the in vivo distribution, metabolism, clearance pathways, and drug-release kinetics of exosomes from different sources and loading methods have not yet been fully elucidated, which complicates the design of precise delivery regimens. Finally, certain safety risks persist. Although exosomes show low immunogenicity, they may still elicit mild immune or toxic reactions, especially under repeated administration. In addition, although exosomes have good targeting ability, they may still be taken up by non-target organs such as the liver and spleen, potentially causing unknown toxicity.

In the process of constructing the smart hydrogel–exosome systems, multiple factors must be comprehensively considered to balance the delivery efficiency and biological activity. When exosomes are loaded into hydrogels, physical adsorption is simple but results in low loading efficiency, whereas chemical crosslinking is stable but may affect exosome bioactivity. During hydrogel gelation, processes such as photo-crosslinking and thermal crosslinking may adversely affect exosome activity. Some hydrogel crosslinkers or by-products also pose cytotoxicity risks. Furthermore, a balance must be achieved between the mechanical strength of hydrogels, which should match the tissue, and their degradation rate, which should be synchronized with exosome release, while the biocompatibility of degradation products must be ensured. In addition, single-responsive systems are insufficient for complex pathological microenvironments such as diabetic wounds, yet the design of multi-responsive systems is more complicated and the precision of in vivo responsiveness remains difficult to guarantee.

The research is aiming to break through the current bottlenecks and ultimately achieve the clinical transformation and precise application of this system. Future directions include developing large-scale exosome-production technologies, such as 3D bioreactors, microcarriers, and cell-culture bags; engineering exosomes to display targeting ligands such as RGD peptides and aptamers on their surfaces; and loading therapeutic miRNA, mRNA, or proteins into their interiors. Furthermore, there is also a need to establish internationally accepted systems for exosome characterization and potency evaluation; to develop multifunctional responsive hydrogel systems suitable for complex therapeutic settings; to create self-healing hydrogels that can spontaneously repair their gel networks after fracture and thereby extend service life; and to implement 3D-printing customization for personalized tissue repair. Moreover, the field is also moving from single-function treatment toward theragnostic integration, forming a self-regulating “detection–response–therapy” closed loop. With continued advances in materials science and biomedicine, smart hydrogels will play an increasingly important role in skin rehabilitation and regenerative medicine.
