Expression and experience domains of negative symptoms in relation to social functioning in patients with schizophrenia: Results from a commonality analysis
Authors: Ayumu Wada, Andrew Stickley, Haeeun Song, Sawako Sumiyoshi, Kazuki Sueyoshi, Risa Yamada, Tomiki Sumiyoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNegative symptoms in schizophrenia are often conceptualized as comprising two domains, i.e., expression and experience, which have been associated with social functioning. However, whether these two negative symptom domains independently or jointly contribute to social functioning in this population remains unclear. Therefore, we investigated this issue by utilizing commonality analysis (CA).MethodsData were analyzed from 45 outpatients diagnosed with schizophrenia. Negative symptoms were assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale, and social functioning was measured with the Specific Levels of Functioning Scale, which includes three subdomains: interpersonal relationships, activities, and work skills. Correlation analyses were conducted to examine the association between the expression and experience domains. Regression analyses were performed to predict the three subdomains of social functioning from the two negative symptom domains. Finally, CA was used to quantify their unique and shared contributions to social functioning.ResultsThere was a significant positive correlation between expression and experience scores (r = 0.52, p < .01). Regression analyses showed that expression scores were more strongly associated with the activities subdomain, and experience scores with the interpersonal relationships and work skills subdomains. CA revealed that a large proportion (≥ 0.48) of the explained variance in social functioning across subdomains was attributable to shared variance across the expression and experience domains.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the importance of assessing both aspects of negative symptoms when planning interventions for patients with schizophrenia. In particular, treatment targets may need to be tailored according to deficits in specific subdomains of social functioning.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a psychiatric disorder characterized by positive symptoms (e.g., hallucinations and delusions), negative symptoms (e.g., avolition and alogia) (Crow, 1982; Schultz and Andreasen, 1999) and cognitive dysfunction (e.g., impairments in verbal memory, working memory and fluency) (Bora et al., 2017; Bowie et al., 2010; Keefe et al., 2004; Sumiyoshi et al., 2016). In particular, negative symptoms lead to poorer outcomes, including reduced social functioning (Bowie et al., 2010; Ebrahimi et al., 2021; Fujino et al., 2016; Galderisi et al., 2018; Handest et al., 2023; Sumiyoshi et al., 2025) and a lower quality of life (Fervaha et al., 2013; Fervaha et al., 2014; Fujino et al., 2016). Emerging evidence indicates that the subdomains of negative symptoms comprise two distinct domains; an expression domain involving blunted affect and alogia, and an experience domain involving anhedonia, asociality, and avolition (Correll and Schooler, 2020; Foussias and Remington, 2010; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006; Marder and Galderisi, 2017). However, whether these two negative symptom domains independently or jointly contribute to social functioning in this population remains unclear. To address this issue, in this study, we investigated the associations of these negative symptom domains with key facets of social functioning in patients with schizophrenia.

Clarifying the definition of negative symptoms in schizophrenia is crucial, given their frequent role as an important treatment target (Kirkpatrick et al., 2006). If negative symptoms are unidimensional, comprehensive assessment of individual symptoms might not be required. Conversely, if they comprise multiple domains, evaluating each domain in detail becomes essential for clinical trials. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) - Measurement and Treatment Research to Improve Cognition in Schizophrenia (MATRICS) project states that negative symptoms consist of two domains, namely, an expression domain and an experience domain (Kirkpatrick et al., 2006). Negative symptom rating scales such as the Brief Negative Symptom Scale (BNSS) (Kirkpatrick et al., 2011; Weigel et al., 2023) and the Clinical Assessment Interview for Negative Symptoms (CAINS) (Forbes et al., 2010; Kring et al., 2013) have also identified two underlying domains: expression and experience. Additionally, similar domains have also been identified in the Scale for Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS) (Andreasen, 1982; Schlosser et al., 2015; Strauss et al., 2013) and the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) (Fervaha et al., 2014; Jang et al., 2016; Kay et al., 1987; Kay et al., 1988; Khan et al., 2017). It is worth noting that this structure appears to be immune to the effects of medication (Kelley et al., 1999; Kimhy et al., 2006).

Several studies have shown that both negative symptom domains are associated with social functioning. These findings appear to be robust across different assessment tools. Overall, it appears that the experience domain has a greater influence on social functioning than the expression domain in patients with schizophrenia (Foussias and Remington, 2010; Strauss et al., 2021). In particular, research using the SANS (Andreasen, 1982), PANSS (Kay et al., 1987; Kay et al., 1988), BNSS (Kirkpatrick et al., 2011; Weigel et al., 2023), and CAINS (Forbes et al., 2010; Kring et al., 2013) has linked both the expression and experience domains to social functioning (Blanchard et al., 2017; Galderisi et al., 2018; García-Fernández et al., 2022; Kalisova et al., 2023; Kring et al., 2013; Strauss et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2023), whereas other studies have reported associations only with the experience domain (Harvey et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2017; Llerena et al., 2018; Rocca et al., 2014; Saperia et al., 2025). Even when both domains are associated with social functioning, the experience domain has often shown a stronger association.

However, further refinement of these findings may benefit from two additional perspectives. First, it is possible that each negative symptoms domain may be associated with distinct aspects of social functioning. Indeed, a network analysis (Galderisi et al., 2018) found that the experience domain was linked to interpersonal relationships and occupational functioning, whereas the expression domain was associated with everyday life skills. Similarly, a longitudinal study (Galderisi et al., 2013) reported that the experience domain was associated with social outcomes, while the expressive domain was related to participation in family life and behavior in emergency situations.

Second, there has been little reserch on the shared contribution of these negative symptom domains to social functioning. This is an important omission given that in terms of neural mechanisms, it is unlikely that the expression and experience domains are entirely distinct (Millan et al., 2014). Also, these aspects may be positively correlated in psychological rating scales (Blanchard et al., 2017; Galderisi et al., 2018; Jang et al., 2016; Kalisova et al., 2023; Kring et al., 2013; Okada et al., 2020; Rocca et al., 2014; Schlosser et al., 2015). In instances where predictors are correlated, the importance of each predictor cannot be determined solely by ranking their coefficients. Given that most previous studies have not explicitly examined shared contributions to social functioning, the individual effects of each symptom domain may not have been accurately estimated.

Based on previous studies (Galderisi et al., 2013, Galderisi et al., 2018), we hypothesized that both the expression and experience negative symptom domains would be associated with social functioning, but that their relative contributions would differ across functional domains. Specifically, we expected the expression domain to be more strongly associated with activities of daily living, whereas the experience domain would show stronger associations with interpersonal relationships and work functioning. Although the experience domain includes asociality and may therefore be conceptually related to interpersonal functioning, these constructs are not identical. Asociality primarily reflects reduced motivation and interest in social engagement, whereas social functioning reflects actual performance in real-world settings. Therefore, the experience domain of negative symptoms and social functioning may represent two separate constructs.

The aim of the present study was therefore to assess the shared and unique variance of the expression and experience negative symptom domains in relation to social functioning in patients with schizophrenia. To address this issue, we applied commonality analysis (CA; Nimon and Oswald, 2013), which decomposes the explained variance into unique and shared domains. Additionally, to better capture the potential differential impact of the expression and experience domains, this study investigated their contributions to area-specific aspects of social functioning.

Methods

Participants

The study design was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry (NCNP) (Approval No. A 2023-082), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. When the participant was a minor (i.e., < 18 years of age), written consent was obtained from a parent or guardian, with additional written assent obtained from patients. The recruitment phase took place between November 2023 and September 2025, targeting outpatients with schizophrenia who visited the NCNP in Tokyo, Japan. All patients had received a diagnosis of schizophrenia based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Patients were excluded if their physician determined that participation could be detrimental to them. Forty-eight outpatients with schizophrenia were initially recruited. After the exclusion of two individuals with missing data and one individual with an updated diagnosis, 45 outpatients were included in the main analysis. Information on years of education, age at illness onset, duration of untreated psychosis, duration of illness, and medication use was obtained from both patient interviews and medical records.

Assessment procedures

Premorbid IQ (intellectual quotient)

Premorbid IQ was assessed using the 25-item short version of the Japanese Adult Reading Test (JART25) that evaluates the ability to read phonologically irregular kanji words (Matsuoka et al., 2006).

Psychiatric symptoms

Psychiatric symptom severity was assessed using the PANSS (Kay et al., 1987; Kay et al., 1988). This measure is used to assess positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and general psychopathology among patients with schizophrenia. The instrument consists of 30 items, each rated on a 7-point scale (1–7). Scores range from 7 to 49 for the positive and negative subscales and 16–112 for the general psychopathology subscale, with higher scores reflecting greater symptom severity. In line with previous studies (Harvey et al., 2017; Khan et al., 2017), scores for the expression and experience negative symptom domains, were constructed using the following PANSS items:

Expression: Blunted Affect (N1), Poor Rapport (N3), Lack of Spontaneity (N6), and Motor Retardation (G7),

Experience: Emotional Withdrawal (N2), Passive Social Withdrawal (N4) and Active Social Avoidance (G16).

Utilizing the two-factor expression (N1, N3, N6, and G7) and experience (N2, N4, and G16) structure identified by Khan et al. (2017), we computed scores using the same procedure described in a previous study (Harvey et al., 2017). Specifically, for each domain, the relevant items were subjected to a principal components analysis, and the first principal component was extracted and used as each domain's score. The correlations between the PCA-derived domain scores and the corresponding summed scores were high for both domains in the current study (r > 0.99, p < .001).

Cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed using the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS) Japanese version (Kaneda et al., 2007; Keefe et al., 2004). This instrument assesses six cognitive domains: verbal memory, working memory, motor speed, attention and speed of information processing, verbal fluency, and executive function. The primary BACS measure used in this study was the composite score calculated from its six domains, with higher scores indicating better cognitive function.

Social functioning

Social functioning was evaluated using the Japanese version of the Specific Levels of Functioning Scale (SLOF), which was employed in accordance with well-established methodological practices from prior studies (Sabbag et al., 2011; Sumiyoshi et al., 2016). This scale includes three subdomains: interpersonal relationships, activities, and work skills. Each item is scored on a five-point Likert scale, and scores range from 7 to 35 for the interpersonal relationships subscale, 11–55 for the activities subscale, and 6–30 for the work skills subscale. Higher scores reflect better social functioning. Ratings were obtained by trained clinicians during interviews, incorporating information from both patients and caregivers (Sabbag et al., 2011; Sumiyoshi et al., 2016).

Statistical analysis

Pearson's correlation coefficients were used to assess the associations between the two negative symptom domains in relation to the SLOF and PANSS scores. Given that these negative symptoms have a common basis, a positive correlation was expected. This provided the rationale for conducting a CA to partition their unique and shared contributions to social functioning.

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine domains associated with social functioning (as measured by the SLOF scale). The three SLOF subdomains (interpersonal relationships, activities, and work skills) were used as dependent variables, and the two negative symptom domains were included as independent variables. Analyses were first performed without adjustment and subsequently adjusted for sociodemographic variables (age and sex). The unique contribution of each negative symptom domain was quantified using squared semi-partial correlations (sr2), which represent the amount of variance in a dependent variable that is independently explained by each predictor. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values of 1.39 and 1.42 for the expression and experience domains, respectively, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. A regression model without demographic covariates (Model 1) was used as the primary model for interpretation and for the subsequent CA, while a model including demographic covariates (Model 2) was also examined.

To further decompose the explained variance (R2) in social functioning, CA was conducted following Nimon and Oswald (2013). In this approach, R2 is partitioned into the unique contribution of each domain and the variance shared between domains (Fig. 1). Discussion

This study examined the relationships between two negative symptom domains (expression and experience) and social functioning in patients with schizophrenia, with the aim of decomposing their shared and unique contributions. The proportions of unique variance differed across the social functioning subdomains, whereas approximately half of the explained variance in social functioning reflected variance shared by these two domains.

A positive correlation was observed between the PANSS expression and experience scores, consistent with previous studies (Blanchard et al., 2017; Galderisi et al., 2018; Jang et al., 2016; Kalisova et al., 2023; Kring et al., 2013; Okada et al., 2020; Rocca et al., 2014; Schlosser et al., 2015). This provides a rationale for partitioning the explained variance in social functioning into unique and shared domains. When predictors are correlated, conventional regression coefficients may not clearly distinguish unique from shared effects, even in the absence of problematic multicollinearity.

The expression and experience domains were associated with distinct aspects of social functioning. Specifically, the expression domain was more closely related to the activities subdomain, while the experience domain was more closely related to the interpersonal relationships and work skills subdomains. These results are consistent with previous research. Specifically, a previous study using the SLOF has reported a pattern similar to the present findings; namely, the negative symptoms expression domain is associated with the activities subdomain, while the negative symptoms experience domain is related to the interpersonal relationships and work skills subdomains (Galderisi et al., 2018). Given that the SLOF assesses social functioning in the context of available support, the expression domain of negative symptoms may show a particularly strong association with the SLOF activities subdomain. In particular, expressive abilities, such as facial expression and verbal output, may be associated with more effective interaction with others, which may in turn relate to greater engagement in daily activities, and link to higher SLOF activities scores. Meanwhile, CA showed that a large proportion (≥ 0.48) of the explained variance in social functioning was attributable to variance shared by the expression and experience domains. These findings indicate that expression and experience had differential, domain-specific associations with distinct aspects of social functioning despite a substantial portion of overlap in the associations between the two negative symptom domains and each outcome.

To date, the experience domain has often been emphasized as a treatment target, given its strong contribution to social functioning (Foussias and Remington, 2010; Strauss et al., 2021). Nevertheless, understanding how each negative symptom domain differentially contributes to specific aspects of social functioning may help guide which domains should be targeted in interventions. Regarding medications, the European Psychiatric Association guidance on the assessment of negative symptoms emphasizes the importance of minimizing secondary negative symptoms, such as those arising from medication side effects, as well as those attributable to positive symptoms and/or depression (Galderisi et al., 2021). As regards specific treatments, roluperidone, which is a serotoninin-5-HT2A and sigma2 receptor antagonist, has recently attracted attention as it may ameliorate negative symptoms. For example, Harvey et al. (2020) reported that roluperidone improved both the expression and experience domains, and that the treatment effects on both the expression and experience domains remained significant even after controlling for changes in the other domain. These findings suggest that the two domains are related but may also capture partially distinct aspects of negative symptoms. Furthermore, a network analysis by James et al. (2024) suggested that improvements in experiential deficits, particularly avolition, may play a central role in broader improvements across the negative symptom domains. While these findings highlight the potential importance of experiential deficits, our results further suggest that expression deficits may also make unique contributions to specific aspects of social functioning. Taken together, these findings support the value of considering both expression and experience domains when examining the functional impact of negative symptoms. In addition, an earlier systematic review and meta-analytic study reported that some antipsychotics may be effective for negative symptoms. For example, amisulpride demonstrated superiority over the use of a placebo, and cariprazine was superior to risperidone in a large randomized trial of patients with predominantly negative symptoms (Krause et al., 2018).

Noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS), including transcranial direct current stimulation and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, may also have potentially beneficial effects on both the expression and experience domains (Chen et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024). Also, transcranial alternating current stimulation has been shown to alleviate negative symptoms (Wei et al., 2024), with some protocols preferentially improving the experience domain (Chang et al., 2021). In addition, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and cognitive remediation (CR) may have complementary effects on negative symptoms. Thus, CBT appears to be more strongly associated with improvements in the experience domain (Grant et al., 2012; Granholm et al., 2014; Riehle et al., 2020), whereas CR may preferentially affect expression-related aspects (Riehle et al., 2020; Melville et al., 2025).

Based on the above considerations, treatment strategies for negative symptoms may benefit from being tailored to specific functional subdomains. After adequately addressing secondary negative symptoms, such as those related to pharmacological effects and/or affective symptoms, subdomain-specific interventions could be selected. For deficits in daily activities, approaches including NIBS and CR may be appropriate, whereas deficits in interpersonal functioning and work skills may be more effectively targeted by interventions such as those using NIBS and CBT.

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged in relation to the current study. First, patient data were collected from a single site, which may introduce sample bias. As the NCNP hospital is an advanced care center, patients may have already received substantial treatment or may present with more severe symptoms. Second, the present study employed a cross-sectional design. Therefore, causal relationships cannot be inferred. Third, given that treatment approaches may differ between primary negative symptoms and secondary negative symptoms, it remains unclear whether the relative contributions of the shared and unique domains observed in the present study apply similarly to these two types of negative symptoms. Finally, the sample size in our study was smaller than those in previous studies (Khan et al., 2017; Harvey et al., 2017). This limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings. On the other hand, it should be noted that the aim of the present study was not to re-establish the factor structure of negative symptoms, but rather, to apply the previously established two-factor model to an independent sample of patients with schizophrenia.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings highlight the importance of addressing the interrelationship between the expression and experience domains of negative symptoms in patients with schizophrenia. The results of this study also suggest interventions targeting domain-specific aspects of negative symptoms may be more effective for improving specific subdomains of social functioning.
