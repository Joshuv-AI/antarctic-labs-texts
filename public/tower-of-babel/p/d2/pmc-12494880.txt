Enhancing sustainability in RC beams with magnetically treated mixing water for improved flexural performance
Authors: Malathy Ramalingam, Karuppasamy Narayanan, G K Arunvivek, Pramod Kumar, M K Diptikanta Rout, Regasa Yadeta Sembeta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Civil Engineering network continues to produce high-performance materials and environmentally sustainable construction for society. A new technology has been developed to enhance the properties of concrete through water magnetization. The Magnetized treated water (MW) has received increasing attention from the scientific community due to its potential to enhance the hydration process. Due to the effect of magnetic field on normal water (NW) while mixing with concrete, the molecular structure is oriented into a more uniform shape and enhances the hydration process, which directly influences the strength properties of concrete. This research article investigates the effectiveness of MW in enhancing the properties of concrete through a comprehensive study involving fresh property tests, mechanical strength evaluations, and durability assessments. The structural behaviour of reinforced concrete (RC) beams prepared using NW and MW with an M40 grade concrete mix was experimentally investigated to evaluate the influence of MW on flexural performance. The workability of MW mixed concrete improved by up to 42.7% to the NW mix. The compressive strength and split tensile strength of concrete prepared with MW increased by up to 12.29% and 19.8%, respectively, indicating a significant enhancement in mechanical performance. The influence of magnetically treated water (MW) on reinforced concrete beams was found to enhance flexural strength by up to 12.8% compared to the control beams prepared with normal water, demonstrating its effectiveness in improving structural performance. Moreover, the scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis revealed that concrete prepared with MW exhibited a significantly greater formation of calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel compared to the mix made with NW. The micrographs showed a denser and more uniform matrix in the MW-based concrete with fewer microcracks and voids, indicating improved hydration and better bonding between cement particles.Keywords: Magnetic field, Reinforced concrete beams, Flexural performance, Magnetized water, Statistical analysisSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Concrete is essentially a composite material containing aggregates, cement, and water, in which water plays a critical role in determining its fresh and hardened properties1. The quality of water available for construction purposes is increasingly being compromised due to factors such as overexploitation of groundwater resources, industrial effluents, and environmental pollution2,3. Variations in water chemistry can significantly influence cement hydration, setting time, workability, and the long-term durability of concrete4. The presence of dissolved salts, particularly calcium and magnesium ions, often leads to the formation of hardness scale, which not only affects water quality but can also create technical and economic challenges in construction applications. Furthermore, the natural molecular structure of water (H₂O) can be modified through various physical or chemical treatments, potentially altering its interaction with cementitious materials5,6. Therefore, potable water has been mixed with the concrete for improved the quality as a novel approach in magnetic field. When this water is allowed to pass through the magnetic field with specific intensity, it is referred as magnetic field treated water (MFTW)7,8. A thin hydration layer formed on the external section of cement, preventing further entry of other water molecules, as a result of the hydration process between cement and water on the surface of the cement particles. Hydration increases due to H–O-H structure aligned in one direction and change in bond angle after magnetization9–11. Pang Xiao-Feng examined that externally applied magnetic field altered the distribution of molecules and structure of molecules in water and it is optimized by FTIR graph, it is observed by some new peaks (2000–3000 cm−1) occurs in MW12,13. Magnetic field treated water on concreting provides better dispersion effect on fine cement particles, to which lumps of concrete particles are broken down and provide maximum surface area, which results in good workability and provides comfort zone for hydration of cement. The usage of MW will increase the workability and strength properties14. The fresh and hardened concrete prepared using MW improved the strength by 45% and 18% than NW usage and reduced the cement consumption by 28%15,16. Abdilrida S.Hasaani observed the strength of cement mortar increased by 20% with the addition of MW8. Ductility is enhanced up to 15% in reinforced concrete beam casted with MW17. MW can decline water absorption of self-compacting concrete up to 50% with 20% replacement of Silica fume18. Moosa Mazloom observed that the use of magnetized water led to reduced superplasticizer demand, which was attributed to the increased dispersion and scattering of water molecules so as to enhancing the workability of concrete without additional chemical admixtures19–21. While previous studies have largely focused on the influence of MW on improving water quality parameters such as hardness reduction and molecular dispersion, the transition of these benefits into the structural behavior of concrete has not been thoroughly investigated. Conventional concrete continues to face challenges including reduced workability at lower water–cement ratios, delayed hydration, higher porosity, and susceptibility to deterioration in aggressive environments. Conventional concrete suffers from several inherent limitations such as reduced workability at lower water–cement ratios, delayed hydration reactions, increased porosity, and reduced durability when exposed to aggressive environments. These drawbacks often lead to premature cracking, loss of strength, and shortened service life of reinforced concrete structures, thereby increasing maintenance costs and resource consumption. Recent research has suggested that MW may provide a sustainable alternative by altering the molecular structure of water, dispersing water clusters, and promoting enhanced cement hydration, which collectively improve workability, strength, and durability characteristics of concrete. However, systematic investigations addressing the combined mechanical, durability, and microstructural performance of MW concrete remain limited, leaving a critical research gap that the present study aims to address.

Gaps identified and contribution

Despite extensive research on concrete technology, a significant number of gaps remain were identified on the use of MFTW in structural applications. Most existing studies focus on the chemical and physical properties of treated water and its effects on cement paste and mortar, with very few addressing its influence on full-scale reinforced concrete (RC) elements. Furthermore, limited research has systematically evaluated how MFTW affects mechanical properties such as compressive, tensile, and flexural strength, or durability indicators including water absorption, porosity, and microstructural characteristics. There is also a scarcity of work connecting these performance aspects with sustainability and cost-effectiveness in practical pavement and structural applications. Taking into consideration the literature survey, the present study investigated the mechanical, durability performance and microstructural behaviour of the effect of MFTW on RC beams. Additionally, the study provides a comprehensive assessment of statistical analysis for the experimental validation through Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), ensuring the reliability and significance of the observed effects of MW on reinforced concrete performance. By bridging the gap between water treatment techniques and structural performance, this research contributes valuable insights for the design and optimization of eco-friendly, high-performance concrete.

Research significance

The significance of this research lies in the application of magnetically treated mixing water as a sustainable and non-conventional method to enhance the flexural performance of RC beams. Although numerous studies have explored the effects of magnetic water treatment in various concrete applications, its impact on RC beams subjected to flexural loading remains largely unexamined. Prior research has reported that magnetically treated water can enhance cement hydration and microstructural densification. However, most investigations have been limited to mortar or paste specimens, with flexural and durability performance of RC members receiving minimal attention. For instance, Kharde et al. (2020) observed up to a 12% improvement in compressive strength in mortar after 28 days24, whereas Safy et al. (2024) recorded a 15% reduction in water absorption; however, neither study evaluated structural members under bending loads or aggressive environments25. Acid attack test

The cubic samples of size 100 × 100 × 100 mm were casted with NW and MW to determine the behaviour of concrete samples exposed to acid medium. The acid resistance of concrete samples against Sulphuric acid (H2SO4) attack was determined. NW concrete and MW concrete samples were immersed in 5% H2SO4 as shown in Fig. 5. This test measures the resistance of concrete to acidic environments by calculating the percentage loss in weight after immersion. The weight loss of concrete specimens due to H₂SO₄ attack was evaluated after 30, 60, 90, and 120 days of exposure, allowing the assessment of acid resistance over extended curing durations. During the acid resistance tests, the solution was periodically refreshed every 7 days to maintain a consistent concentration and acidity. Additionally, the pH of the solution was monitored on a weekly basis to ensure that the specimens were continuously exposed to a stable acidic environment, as concrete gradually neutralizes acids over time through chemical reactions. This procedure ensured uniform exposure conditions for all specimens over the entire test duration (30–120 days), thereby allowing accurate assessment of mass loss, surface degradation, and durability performance under sustained acidic conditions.

Microstructural observations

The microstructural development of hydration products in concrete prepared with NW and MW was examined using SEM on 28-day cured samples. Small fragments were extracted from the inner core of the hardened concrete to avoid surface contamination, then finely ground into powder form. The powdered samples were oven-dried to eliminate residual moisture and subsequently mounted on aluminum stubs using carbon adhesive. To prevent charging under the electron beam, the specimens were coated with a thin conductive layer of gold using a sputter coater. The samples were then placed in the SEM chamber and examined under a high-energy electron beam at an accelerating voltage of 10–20 kV. Images were captured at various magnifications to observe hydration products. Through comparative analysis of the SEM images, distinct morphological variations were identified between NW and MW-based concrete.

Result and discussions

Quality of MW

When water molecules (H–O–H) are exposed to a magnetic field, the molecular structure undergoes physical and chemical changes. The interaction of the magnetic field causes many hydroxide ions (OH⁻) to react with existing minerals, forming calcium bicarbonate and other alkaline compounds. This process leads to an increase in pH and a reduction in acidity.

Experimental results revealed that after magnetization, the Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) and residual chloride content were significantly reduced. Furthermore, the turbidity of the water decreased, which in turn lowers the hardness. The reduction in molecular clustering also decreases surface tension, allowing water to penetrate more effectively into cement particles. This improved penetration facilitates a more uniform and efficient hydration process, enhancing the workability and reactivity of the mix. As a result, concrete prepared with magnetically treated water exhibits improved performance characteristics in both fresh and hardened states.

Fresh properties of concrete

Measurement of workability

The workability of concrete was measured using the slump cone test as per IS: 1199–1959 guidelines. This test was widely adopted for evaluating the consistency and flow characteristics of fresh concrete. The addition of MW to the concrete increases the flowability of the concrete. Concrete prepared with MW with a high-intensity magnetic field of 0.9 T shows an increase in the slump value (137 mm) compared to NWC (96 mm), as shown in Fig. 9. The increase in slump is attributed to water in its nano state, where molecules exist in clusters whose size depends on the dominant intermolecular forces. When exposed to the magnetic field, these cluster breaks apart due to the macroscopic properties of water, causing hydrogen atoms to decrease from 104.5° to 103 °C35. This was due to the higher magnetic intensity disperses the more water molecule clusters. Hence, the highly active molecules bind with the cement particles and roll on while mixing. With only the use of MW, a 42.7% increase in flow can be achieved compared to NW concrete. This result suggested that concrete prepared with MW exhibited higher slump values, indicating improved flowability and workability, which can be attributed to enhanced dispersion and hydration characteristics influenced by magnetic treatment. Hardened properties of concrete

Compressive strength of Concrete

Figure 10 shows that the compressive strength of concrete using MW in 7 Days is increased by 16.63%, when compared to the concrete prepared with NW. It was observed that the strength increment at the early age (7 days) of concrete was more than the strength increase between 14 and 28 days. This was due to the active water molecules in the MW reacting quickly with the cement particles and producing the hydration products than the NW concrete42–44. At 28 28-day curing period, the strength of MW concrete is increased by up to 12.29% to the control concrete. The 28-day strength achieved in NW can be attained in MW in 21 days itself. As a result, the formwork can be removed sooner and the expense is decreased. Hence, the findings suggest that the use of magnetized water in concrete can reduce cement and mineral admixture demand, thereby lower material consumption and contributing to cost savings in building construction.

Compressive strength of NW and MW based concrete.

Split tensile strength of concrete

Figure 11 demonstrates the beneficial impact of MWC on the split tensile strength of concrete over different curing periods. Similar observations were also noted at all curing ages (7, 14, and 28 days) that the MWC concrete exhibited consistently higher strength compared to the NWC. Specifically, at 7 days, the MWC mix showed a strength increase of approximately 21.9% over NWC. This trend continued at 14 days with an increase of about 17.9%, and by 28 days, the MWC concrete achieved a 20% higher split tensile strength than the control mix. These results highlight the positive significant of magnetic water in enhancing cement hydration and improving the internal bonding of the concrete matrix, thereby leading to better tensile performance of concrete45–47.

Durability properties

To evaluate the corrosion resistance of steel reinforcement in concrete, a half-cell potential test was conducted on specimens prepared with both NWC and MWC. The test followed the guidelines specified in ASTM C876 to determine the probability of corrosion based on measured half-cell potential values over time. The half-cell potential measurements were taken with respect to a copper–copper sulfate reference electrode, enabled the assessment of the likelihood of corrosion activity in the embedded steel.

Variations in Half-cell potential values of NWC and MWC are depicted in Fig. 12. When compared with the values of Half-cell potential of NWC, lower Half-cell potential values were obtained for the MWC mix. It can be observed that the half-cell potential results reveal that both mixes initially exhibited similar values (~ –200 mV), but NWC showed a steeper decline compared to MWC. Throughout the exposure period, MWC consistently maintained fewer negative potentials, indicating delayed initiation and slower progression of corrosion. By 60 days, NWC reached −680 mV, while MWC stabilized near −600 mV, confirming the superior corrosion resistance and durability of concrete prepared with magnetized water. The significant reddish-brown corrosion stains indicate active steel deterioration and loss of protective passivation. The concrete surfaces also show localized discoloration and deposits, suggesting chloride penetration and corrosion-induced distress.

Structural behavior of RC beam

First crack and ultimate load carrying capacity

Figure 16 shows the initial crack loads and ultimate failure loads for NWC and MWC specimens. The initial crack load amplified for MWC specimen by 23.08% compared to NWC. It is evident that the beam made with MWC exhibited improved performance in both parameters. The first crack load for the NWC beam was 13 kN, while for the MWC beam it increased to 16 kN, indicating enhanced crack resistance. Similarly, the ultimate load capacity of the MWC beam reached 64.8 kN, which is approximately 12.8% higher than that of the NWC beam. This improvement demonstrates the beneficial effect of magnetized water on the structural capacity of concrete, likely due to better hydration, denser microstructure, and stronger interfacial bonding within the composite material. These results affirm that the use of MW contributes to both early-age cracking resistance and better flexural strength of RC beams.

First crack and ultimate load.

Load Vs deflection behaviour

The RC beams were tested under a two-point loading setup, and deflection values were recorded at each 1 kN increment. The resulting load–deflection behaviour of the beams is illustrated in Fig. 17. The beam prepared with NW sustained an ultimate load of 56.80 kN with a corresponding deflection of 6.2 mm. In contrast, the beam prepared with MWC demonstrated an increased load-carrying capacity by 12.11%, reaching a higher ultimate load with a slightly larger deflection of 8.69 mm. This increase in load capacity indicates the improved energy absorption and ductility of MWC beams compared to NW beams. Henceforth, the use of MW significantly enhances the flexural strength, ductility, and post-peak behaviour of RC beams, making it a viable approach for improving the structural performance and energy dissipation capacity of concrete elements.

Mechanism

Figure 21 explains the mechanism of MW in concrete. MW in concrete revolves around the physical and chemical alterations that occur when normal untreated water is subjected to a magnetic field. Typically, this water contains clustered water molecules and a high concentration of dissolved minerals such as calcium, magnesium, chlorides, and TDS. When this water passes through a magnetic field such as from a magnet with 0.9 Tesla intensity, it undergoes several transformations that enhance its interaction with cementitious materials.

Mechanism of magnetic water in concrete.

Firstly, the magnetic exposure alters the molecular structure of water, breaking the large clusters of H₂O molecules into smaller, more discrete units38,39. These smaller water molecules exhibit lower surface tension and increased mobility, allowing them to penetrate deeper into the voids and surfaces of the cement particles. This results in improved dispersion of water throughout the concrete matrix, facilitating more uniform hydration reactions37,44. However, the magnetic field reduces the concentrations of certain unwanted ions in the water, including calcium, magnesium, chloride, and TDS, and modifies the pH slightly towards a more neutral or alkaline range. These chemical changes enhance the compatibility of water with cement, reducing the potential for interference in the hydration process and improving the formation of C–S–H gel, which is responsible for strength development41–43. The enhancement in concrete performance when mixed with MW can be attributed to changes in the physicochemical behaviour of water molecules under magnetic exposure. Magnetic treatment is known to reduce the size of water molecule clusters, increase the proportion of free water molecules, and alter hydrogen bond angles, which in turn improves the dissolution of cement minerals and accelerates hydration reactions44,48,49. As a result, cement particles are more efficiently hydrated, leading to a denser microstructure with fewer capillary pores. Several studies have reported that concrete prepared with MW achieves comparable or higher mechanical strength at reduced cement content by approximately 5 to 10% without compromising durability53–55. This improvement is attributed to the enhanced early-age hydration and refined pore structure, which together increase the interfacial bond between the cement paste and aggregate, thereby improving load transfer and reducing crack propagation. The findings of the present study agree with these mechanisms, as evidenced by the higher flexural strength and durability indicators observed in MW concrete compared to the control mix.

Conclusion

In this study, the tests show that the magnetic field intensity induced in water improves the water quality and that production of MW has the ability to reduce the hardness in water, which softens the water molecules quickly, helping the water to percolate into the cement particles easily, which enhances the binding property. The use of MW significantly enhanced the workability of concrete, with a 42.7% increase in slump compared to NW concrete. This improvement was attributed to the dispersion of water molecule clusters under magnetic treatment, which facilitated better interaction with cement particles and led to improved flowability and hydration. The use of MW markedly improved the mechanical performance of concrete compared to NW concrete. The compressive strength increased by 12.29%, while the split tensile strength improved by 19.8%, demonstrating the beneficial effect of MW on both load-bearing capacity and resistance to cracking. Similarly, the flexural strength of beams prepared with MW was enhanced by 12.9% compared to NW beams, indicating better resistance to bending and improved structural performance. These improvements could be attributed to the enhanced hydration process, denser microstructure, and better bonding between cement paste and aggregates under the influence of magnetically treated water. The beams casted with magnetized water offers fewer cracking during external loading compared to NW concrete beams. The probability of corrosion with respect to time in steel embedded in the mix MWC is lesser than NWC. It was observed that the concrete prepared with MWC demonstrated improved resistance against acid attack compared to the control mix. It was also observed that concrete prepared with MWC demonstrated improved resistance against acid attack compared to the control mix. The MWC specimens showed minimal weight losses of 1.57%, 2.35%, 3.68%, and 4.46% after 30, 60, 90, and 120 days of exposure to sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), respectively, indicating enhanced durability and reduced deterioration over time. Moreover, microstructural analysis using SEM revealed that concrete prepared with MW exhibited a denser matrix and a higher volume C–S–H gel, especially at early ages of curing as compared to the NW mix. The enhanced formation of C–S–H gel in MW concrete can be attributed to improved dispersion and accelerated hydration due to the altered molecular structure of water under magnetic influence. Statistical analysis (ANOVA) validated that all improvements in mechanical and durability parameters were significant at α = 0.05, while lack of fit and residual errors were found to be non-significant that confirmed model adequacy and reliability. These findings affirmed that the use of MW promoted better internal bonding and reduced porosity, thereby playing a significant role in enhancing the performance and sustainability of reinforced concrete structures.

Practical implementation

Advancements in the civil engineering construction field are marvelous. Innovative developments are brought forth in the design, layout, and implementation of structural and non-structural elements of infrastructures. Locally available water can be magnetized and used in the making of concrete. MW treatment is increasingly explored for enhancing concrete properties, reducing cement usage up to 10–20%, and improving hydration64. However, scaling it up for large construction sites requires higher flow rates (10–100 m3/h), with high intensity ranges from 1 to 1.4 T of non-electric (permanent magnet) devices, so that consume zero or minimal energy with device durability up to 10–15 years. Limitations

The present experimental program employed a fixed magnetic field intensity of 0.9 T, with water used immediately after magnetization and a specific set of concrete mix proportions. While this controlled framework enabled a focused evaluation of the mechanical, durability, and microstructural performance of MW concrete, it inevitably narrows the scope of interpretation. Parameters such as varying field intensities, exposure durations, storage periods after treatment, and different water-to-cement ratios may significantly influence hydration kinetics and, consequently, the resulting concrete properties. Future research should adopt a broader parametric framework to systematically capture these influences and to develop standardized protocols for the practical application of MW in concrete production.

In addition, several practical challenges must be considered for field implementation. The magnetic effect on water is transient, with optimal reactivity typically sustained for only a few hours, necessitating immediate utilization at construction sites. The presence of impurities such as dissolved salts and suspended particles can reduce treatment efficiency by interfering with hydrogen bonding or promoting scale buildup. Furthermore, while permanent magnet devices are energy-independent, high-capacity or electromagnet-based systems may require a stable power supply, which could be challenging in remote areas. Finally, the scale-up from laboratory conditions to field applications demands adjustments in flow rates, exposure durations, and equipment configurations to ensure consistent and reliable performance under diverse operational constraints.

Recommendation for future research

Based on the findings of this study, several recommendations can be made for future research. While the use of MW has shown promising improvements in the mechanical and durability performance of reinforced concrete, further investigations are required to understand the long-term effects under various environmental exposures such as freeze–thaw cycles, carbonation, and sulfate attack. Additionally, exploring the optimal magnetic field intensity and exposure duration for different grades of concrete could help standardize the treatment process. Advanced analytical techniques such as XRD, TGA, and FTIR can also be employed to better understand the phase composition and chemical changes induced by magnetic water treatment. Furthermore, the influence of MW on the bond strength between steel and concrete, and its effect on large-scale structural elements should be studied to validate its application in real-life structures. Additionally, the effects of MW on rheological behavior, setting time, and shrinkage characteristics should be explored in detail. A life cycle assessment (LCA) comparing MW and conventional methods could offer a sustainability perspective. Finally, field-scale trials and real-time structural monitoring would be valuable to validate laboratory findings and encourage the adoption of MW technology in practical engineering applications.
