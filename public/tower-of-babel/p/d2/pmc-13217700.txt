Cannabinoid CB1 receptors drive oligodendrocyte maturation during myelin regeneration
Authors: Sara Ezquerro-Herce, Aníbal Sánchez-de la Torre, Krisztina Monory, Beat Lutz, Manuel Guzmán, Tania Aguado, Javier Palazuelos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOligodendrocytes, the myelin-producing cells in the central nervous system, derive from oligodendrocyte progenitor cells during both development and remyelination. The intricate process of oligodendrocyte differentiation, which involves several transitional stages before generating fully mature myelin-producing cells, is tightly regulated by extracellular signals. Despite advances in identifying key regulators, the comprehensive network of signaling molecules governing oligodendrocyte maturation in the healthy, and more importantly, the demyelinated brain remains incompletely understood. The endocannabinoid system has been recognized as a key regulator of oligodendroglial lineage progression, contributing to myelin formation and regeneration. In particular, CB1 cannabinoid receptors are prominent modulators of the initial steps of oligodendrocyte progenitor cell differentiation. However, the potential role of CB1 receptors in the terminal stages of oligodendrocyte differentiation remains poorly explored. Here, using a new conditional genetic mouse model in which the CB1 receptor-encoding gene was selectively ablated from differentiated oligodendrocytes, together with the cuprizone diet-induced demyelination paradigm, we demonstrate that CB1 receptors are essential for the terminal stages of oligodendroglial maturation during myelin regeneration. Specifically, we found that CB1 receptor gene inactivation in newly regenerated oligodendrocytes hinders oligodendroglial cell maturation and brain remyelination, exacerbates glial activation and axonal damage, and abolishes motor function recovery. Taken together, our findings demonstrate a crucial role for CB1 receptors in modulating oligodendrocyte maturation during myelin regeneration and supports the notion that appropriate endocannabinoid signaling in the oligodendroglial lineage is essential for achieving efficient remyelination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02852-w.Keywords: CB1 Cannabinoid receptor, Oligodendrocyte maturation, Remyelination, RhoA, mTORC1

Introduction

Oligodendrocyte (OL) lineage cells progress through a continuum from proliferative, migratory oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs; aka NG2 glia) to pre-OLs, newly formed OLs, and ultimately myelinating/mature OLs [1]. During this maturation process, OLs extend and stabilize processes, wrap axons, and compact myelin while simultaneously shifting metabolism to provide trophic and metabolic support to axons, which are essential functions for saltatory conduction and long-term axonal integrity [2]. This maturation process is regulated by multiple intrinsic and extrinsic factors, with emerging evidence showing that myelin growth and remodeling in adulthood contribute to neural plasticity [3–5]. Moreover, the relevance of this step is underscored in neurological disorders, where dysregulation of OL maturation and remyelination defects have been documented. When this remyelination process stalls, axons become vulnerable and disability accrues [6, 7]. Altogether, delineating the checkpoints of NG2 cell differentiation and, most critically, the mechanisms that drive and maintain OL maturation, offers a framework for potential therapies that enhance myelination and, even more significantly, restore myelin after demyelination [8].

Cannabinoids have been shown to modulate oligodendrogenesis and myelination during development and remyelination through activation of CB1 cannabinoid receptors [9]. Pharmacological approaches have reported that CB1 receptor pharmacological activation, by administration of the paradigmatic phytocannabinoid Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), promotes NG2 cell differentiation and OL maturation both during postnatal myelination [10] and during myelin regeneration [11]. Moreover, elevation of the endocannabinoid (eCB) tone, by pharmacological inhibition of the 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG)-degrading enzyme monoacylglycerol lipase, promotes OPC differentiation and OL maturation in cultured OPCs [12, 13], at mouse embryonic stages [14], and in Theiler’s murine encephalomyelitis virus (TMEV), a progressive mouse model of multiple sclerosis [15–17]. In addition, genetic mouse models selectively targeting NG2 cells showed that CB1 receptor deficiency in NG2 cells, by dysregulating Ras homolog family member A (RhoA)/Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK)/cofilin and mTORC1 signaling pathways, impairs cell differentiation during postnatal myelination [18] and during remyelination following cuprizone-induced demyelination [19]. As such, CB1 receptors are essential modulators of the initial steps of OPC differentiation, both during development and during remyelination [20–22]. By contrast, the possible role of CB1 receptors in the terminal stages of OL maturation remains inadequately defined. Here, employing a new OL-specific mouse line and a toxin-induced demyelination model, we unveil the indispensable function of CB1 receptors in controlling OL maturation during remyelination. This finding underscores the therapeutic potential of enhancing CB1-mediated signaling in promoting myelin regeneration in neurological disorders.

Materials and methods

Animal procedures

All animals used were male and were bred in the C57BL/6J background. Proteolipid protein (Plp)-CreERtm (B6.Cg-Tg(Plp1-cre/ERT)3Pop/J, RRID: IMSR_JAX:005975) mice from Jackson Laboratories [23] were crossed with Cnr1ff aka CB1ff (floxed/floxed) (Cnr1tm1.2Ltz, RRID: IMSR_JAX:036107) mice [24], to generate the Plp-CB1KO and Plp-CB1WT mouse lines. Both mouse lines were crossed with 2′,3′-cyclic nucleotide 3′-phosphodiesterase (Cnp)-mEGFP (Tg(Cnp-EGFP*)1Qrlu/J, RRID: IMSR_JAX:026105) [25] to generate the Cnp-EGFP-Plp-CB1KO and Cnp-EGFP-Plp-CB1WT mouse lines. Mice received tamoxifen (MedChem, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA, at 75 mg/kg, prepared in ethanol and sunflower oil (1:9) administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) injections at a concentration of 15 mg/ml starting on the day before Cpz diet removal, once a day for 7 consecutive days.

Cuprizone-induced demyelination

Male mice aged 6–8 weeks were maintained on a pelleted diet containing 0.2% cuprizone (Cpz) (bis(cyclohexanone) oxaldihydrazone; TD.140804, Envigo) [11] for 6 weeks to induce demyelination. Then, mice were fed with a regular chow for allowing remyelination. Control diet animals included both Plp-CB1KO and Plp-CB1WT mice distributed randomly. They were fed only with RD or a Cpz diet for 6 weeks (6w Cpz), and received ethanol-sunflower oil administrations without Tam. We observed similar levels of demyelination (Suppl. Fig S1) and OL cell densities (not shown) in Plp-CB1KO and Plp-CB1WT mice in both control groups. We used male mice to reduce intersex and estrous cycle-related variability [26].

Immunofluorescence

For immunohistochemistry, brain samples were prepared following previously established procedures. Animals were first transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), after which brains were collected, fixed overnight in the same solution, and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose before freezing. Free-floating coronal cryosections (30 μm) were then obtained, rinsed in PBS, and incubated in a blocking buffer consisting of 10% goat serum and 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS. Sections were subsequently exposed to the corresponding primary antibodies, diluted in blocking buffer, and left at 4 °C overnight. When required, antigen retrieval was carried out either with citric acid (10 mM, pH 6.0, 65 °C for 30 min) or with the Retrieve-All Antigen Unmasking System 3: Acidic (BioLegend) at 92 °C for 10 min. After washing the next day, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied. The primary antibodies employed were as follows: anti-Caspr (1:300, Millipore, Cat#MABN69, RRID: AB_10806491), anti-CC1 (1:400, Millipore, Cat#OP80, RRID: AB_2057371), anti-GFAP (1:500, Sigma Aldrich, Cat#G3893, RRID: AB_477010), anti-GSTπ (1:200, MBL International, Cat#312, RRID: AB_591792), anti-Iba1 (1:500, Synaptic Systems, Cat#234004, RRID: AB_2493179), anti-Iba1 (1:1000, Wako Chemicals, Cat#019-19741, RRID: AB_839504); anti-iNOS (1:250, BD Transduction, Cat#610329, RRID: AB_1009559), anti-MBP (1:200, BioLegend, Cat#808402, RRID: AB_2564742), anti-NF-200 (1:200, Sigma Aldrich, Cat#N4142, RRID: AB_477272), anti-NaV1.6 (1:300, Alomone Labs, Jerusalem, Israel, Cat#ASC-009, RRID: AB_2040202), anti-Olig2 (1:250, Millipore, Cat#AB9610, RRID: AB_570666), anti-pCofilin (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#3313, RRID: AB_2080597), anti-pS6 (Ser240/244, 1:800, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#5364, RRID: AB_10694233), anti-SMI-32 (1:250, BioLegend, Cat#801701, RRID: AB_2564642). Highly cross-adsorbed Alexa Fluor 488, 594, and 647 secondary antibodies raised against mouse, rabbit, or guinea pig (1:1000; Invitrogen) were applied as appropriate. Nuclear staining was performed by incubating sections with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min, followed by PBS washes, and samples were finally mounted using Mowiol.

Confocal microscopy

Confocal epifluorescence images were obtained as sequential optical stacks (z-step = 0.5 μm) using a TCS-SP8 laser-scanning confocal microscope (Leica DMI6000 B) equipped with 20×, 40×, and 63× objectives and operated with Leica Application Suite X (LAS X) software. For each animal, digital images were collected from the corpus callosum (CC). For quantitative analyses of the rostral corpus callosum (CC), at least six sections from a 1-in-10 series spanning + 0.7 to − 0.5 mm relative to bregma were evaluated, whereas caudal CC regions were assessed using sections located between − 1.2 and − 2.2 mm from bregma. Cell quantification was carried out in a blinded manner using ImageJ software (NIH), and results are reported either as mean cell density (cells/mm²) or as the percentage of positive cells. Image acquisition was performed under identical exposure settings for all samples. To assess remyelination, confocal microscopy was used to generate z-stack images of MBP and NF-200 immunostaining or Cnp-EGFP fluorescence in white matter areas from Plp-CB1KO-Cnp-mEGFP and Plp-CB1WT-Cnp-mEGFP mice. Maximum-intensity projections were generated from each stack using LAS X software. Fluorescence levels of Cnp-EGFP were quantified as integrated signal density and expressed in arbitrary units (a.u.). Myelination indices were determined by calculating Mander’s coefficients with Costes’ automatic thresholding through the JACOP plugin in ImageJ.

Myelin staining. Sudan black

Free-floating sections were placed onto TESPA-coated glass slides, dehydrated in 70% ethanol, and stained with 0.5% Sudan Black prepared in 70% ethanol for 20 min. After staining, excess dye was removed by rinsing the slides in 70% ethanol, followed by a final wash in water. Samples were subsequently examined under bright-field microscopy using a Nikon SMZ25 microscope equipped with a Nikon DS-Ri2 camera.

Behavioral assessments

All tests were conducted during the light cycle, with uniform lighting conditions and white noise in an isolated room. Animals were acclimated to the room for 45–60 min before testing. Actimeter: Spontaneous motor activity was evaluated using an automated actimeter (Acti-Track; Panlab, Barcelona, Spain). This consisted of a 22.5 × 22.5 cm area with 16 surrounding infrared beams coupled to a computerized control unit. After 1 min of habituation, activity was recorded for a period of 5 min and data were collected with Acti-Track v2.7 software (Panlab, Barcelona, Spain). Total distance traveled (cm), mean velocity (cm/s) and resting time (%) were used as a measure of locomotor activity. Beam walking test: To evaluate fine motor coordination, mice were trained to cross a narrow wood beam (100 cm length, 7 mm width) to reach a safety box. The number of foot slips were measured manually during the test and reanalyzed following video recording [27].

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using independent data points, corresponding to the number of animals per group or experimental replicates, as indicated in each experiment. Group sizes were planned to be equivalent whenever possible, with random allocation of animals and blinded data analysis. The reported sample numbers reflect the final datasets after applying exclusion criteria or accounting for unexpected data loss. Occasional experimental losses resulted from factors such as incorrect treatment administration, unrelated infections, sampling or acquisition errors (e.g., equipment miscalibration or software failure), or other inadvertent human mistakes. Statistical power was estimated using IBM SPSS software (IBM France, Bois-Colombes, France), and sample sizes were selected based on previous studies in which comparable group sizes provided sufficient power to detect significant effects. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality (p > 0.05), while homogeneity of variance was evaluated with the Brown–Forsythe test (p > 0.05). Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed t-tests when normality assumptions were satisfied. For analyses involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied when both normality and variance homogeneity criteria were met. Post hoc analyses were conducted only when ANOVA results reached statistical significance (p < 0.05) and variance assumptions were not violated. When datasets did not follow a normal distribution, a Kruskal–Wallis one-way ANOVA with Dunn’s uncorrected post hoc test was used instead. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied throughout. Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism (version 8.00).

Results

CB1 receptor gene inactivation in regenerating oligodendrocytes impairs cell maturation during remyelination

To study a specific function for CB1 receptors in OL maturation during myelin regeneration we generated a new mouse line by crossing Cnr1-floxed mice [24] with Plp-CreERT2 [23] mice, thus conceivably depleting CB1 receptor expression selectively in differentiated OLs upon tamoxifen (Tam) administration. In addition, to evaluate the role of CB1 receptors in oligodendrocyte maturation and myelin repair, we employed the cuprizone (Cpz)-induced model of demyelination and remyelination. Specifically, mice were fed with a Cpz-containing diet for 6 weeks, followed by 2 weeks with a regular diet (RD) to allow spontaneous remyelination. Mice received Tam injections starting one day before Cpz diet removal, once per day for 7 consecutive days (Fig. 1A), to selectively deplete CB1 receptors in newly regenerated OLs across the process of OL maturation during remyelination. First, we monitored OL loss and regeneration in the corpus callosum (CC) at the demyelination and remyelination stages. Immunofluorescence analysis in the CC revealed that the Cpz diet efficiently depleted the great majority of CC1+ OLs (Fig. 1B, D, F) and glutathione S-transferase P (GSTπ)+ mature myelinating OLs (Fig. 1C, E). The analysis at 2 weeks during remyelination revealed a reduced density of GSTπ+CC1+ mature myelinating OLs (Fig. 1B, C, E), together with a reduced fraction of myelinating GSTπ+ OLs within CC1+ OLs cells in Plp-CB1KO mice compared to their Plp-CB1WT control littermates (Fig. 1B, C, E). We did not find differences in CC1+ OL cell densities between both genotypes (Fig. 1D, F), thus supporting the selective depletion of CB1 receptors in maturing OLs but not in OPCs, and suggesting that CB1 receptors modulate OL maturation without affecting OL cell survival during myelin regeneration. Altogether, these findings indicate that CB1 receptor ablation in differentiating OLs prevents cell maturation and disrupts efficient oligodendrogenesis during remyelination.

CB1 receptor gene inactivation in regenerating oligodendrocytes following cuprizone-induced demyelination leads to impaired myelin regeneration

To address whether the observed defects in OL maturation shown in Plp-CB1KO mice following Cpz-induced demyelination impact the remyelination process, we analyzed myelin loss and regeneration in the CC. Sudan black staining confirmed the efficient demyelination achieved in rostral and caudal regions of the CC by the Cpz diet compared to RD-fed mice (Fig. 2A). The analysis at 2 weeks of the remyelination period unveiled the defects in myelin regeneration in the CC of Plp-CB1KO mice compared to Plp-CB1WT mice (Fig. 2A). To further confirm these defects in remyelination of Plp-CB1KO mice, we generated another new mouse line (herein termed Plp-CB1KO-Cnp-mEGFP) by crossing Plp-CB1KO and Cnp-mEGFP mice, to directly visualize myelin and avoid false-positive antibody-mediated signals. Confocal analysis of Cnp-mEGFP fluorescence confirmed the proficient myelin loss in the CC achieved by the Cpz diet compared to RD, as well the defects in myelin regeneration of Plp-CB1KO-Cnp-mEGFP mice compared to Plp-CB1WT-Cnp-mEGFP control littermates after 2 weeks of remyelination (Fig. 2B). Then, we analyzed whether the hypomyelinated phenotype observed upon OL-CB1 receptor depletion during remyelination impacts glia-axonal interaction and node/paranode regeneration. Immunofluorescence analysis of nodal (Nav1.6) and paranodal (Caspr) markers in the CC after 2 weeks of remyelination revealed an altered regeneration of both node density and the percentage of complete nodes in Plp-CB1KO mice compared to control animals (Fig. 2C). Moreover, immunofluorescence analysis of myelin basic protein (MBP) and neurofilament 200 (NF-200) and quantification of the myelination index, confirmed a defect in remyelination in the CC and in the cingulum areas of the subcortical white matter of Plp-CB1KO mice compared to control littermates (Fig. 2D). Overall, these results indicate that CB1 gene inactivation in newly regenerated OLs leads to a block in the maturation process that is translated into strong defects in myelin regeneration.

CB1 receptor gene inactivation in regenerating oligodendrocytes following demyelination impairs motor function recovery following remyelination

To address whether the observed defects in myelin regeneration observed in Plp-CB1KO mice were translated into alterations in motor function recovery, we analyzed motor performance and coordination during demyelination and during remyelination in Plp-CB1KO and control mice. First, we confirmed the motor defects achieved in animals treated with the Cpz diet compared to RD in the beam walking (Fig. 3A) and the actimeter (Fig. 3B) test. Moreover, while Plp-CB1WT mice progressively recovered motor function to the levels of RD control mice along remyelination, Plp-CB1KO mice showed impaired motor function recovery assessed in the beam walking (Fig. 3A) and actimeter (Fig. 3B) tests at 2 weeks during remyelination. Taken together, these data indicate that CB1 receptor gene inactivation in differentiating OLs following demyelination impairs mature OL regeneration, remyelination and motor function recovery.

CB1 receptor gene inactivation in regenerating oligodendrocytes during remyelination exacerbates gliosis

Failure in the remyelination process leads to a chronic and exacerbated glial activation, characterized by persistent proliferation and reactivity of microglia and astrocytes, which perpetuates neuroinflammation and contributes to progressive tissue damage and demyelination [28]. Thus, we asked whether the defects in myelin regeneration observed in Plp-CB1KO mice were associated with exacerbated glial activation in the CC during remyelination. Immunofluorescence analysis of the microglial ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba-1) marker and the astroglial glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) marker revealed a higher number of Iba-1+ microglial cells and an increased GFAP immunoreactivity in the CC of Plp-CB1KO mice compared to Plp-CB1WT controls at 2 weeks during remyelination (Fig. 4A-C). Moreover, we found an increased percentage of reactive microglial Iba-1+ cells expressing inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (Fig. 4C), which points to an activated and pro-inflammatory phenotype that contributes to neuroinflammation and demyelination [29]. Altogether, these results indicate that CB1 receptor gene inactivation in newly generated OLs following demyelination leads to impaired functional remyelination and sustained glial activation.

CB1 receptor gene inactivation in regenerating oligodendrocytes during remyelination aggravates axonal damage

In demyelinating disorders, axonal injury represents a critical contributor to long-term clinical disability, largely associated with deficient remyelination, glial activation, and ongoing neuroinflammatory responses [7, 28, 30]. Thus, we next analyzed whether the defects in myelin regeneration and sustained gliosis observed in Plp-CB1KO mice lead to enhanced axonal damage in the CC during remyelination. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed an increased immunolabelling of the axonal damage marker SMI-32 (aka non-phosphorylated neurofilament H) in the CC of Cpz diet-fed mice at 6 weeks compared to RD animals (Fig. 4D, E). We also found that following 2 weeks of recovery, while SMI-32 immunoreactivity was attenuated in Plp-CB1WT mice, it remained elevated in Plp-CB1KO animals (Fig. 4D, E), thus indicating that CB1 receptor gene deletion in OLs following demyelination impairs myelin regeneration and leads to enhanced axonal damage in the CC.

CB1 receptor gene inactivation dysregulates mTORC1 and RhoA/ROCK/cofilin signaling pathways in newly regenerating oligodendrocytes during remyelination

CB1 receptors have been shown to control NG2 cell differentiation, at least in part, by modulating the mTORC1 and RhoA/ROCK/cofilin signaling pathways, during both postnatal myelination and myelin regeneration [18, 19]. As those two signaling pathways are also essential in modulating the terminal stages of OL maturation [31–34], we analyzed their activation levels in Plp-CB1KO OLs during remyelination. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a reduced percentage of pS6+ cells among CC1+ OLs in the CC of Plp-CB1KO mice compared to Plp-CB1WT controls at 2 weeks during the remyelination period (Fig. 5A), indicating reduced activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway in Plp-CB1KO OLs. In addition, we found an increased percentage of pCofilin+ cells among CC1+ OLs in the CC of Plp-CB1KO mice compared to Plp-CB1WT controls during remyelination (Fig. 5B), suggesting a deregulation of the RhoA/ROCK/cofilin axis in Plp-CB1KO OLs. Altogether, these results suggest that CB1 receptor deficiency in newly regenerated OLs during remyelination impairs OL maturation and myelin regeneration, possibly, and at least in part, by dysregulating the mTORC1 and RhoA/ROCK/cofilin signaling pathways.

Discussion

Our study highlights the critical role of the eCB system in controlling the terminal stages of OL maturation during myelin regeneration. It also complements and advances previous pharmacological approaches that have shown that CB1 receptor pharmacological activation promotes NG2 cell differentiation and OL maturation during myelin regeneration [11, 15, 16, 19]. Unlike pharmacological approaches, which act systemically on CB1 receptors located in neuronal and multiple glial cell types, the inducible Plp-CB1KO specifically ablates the CB1 receptor-encoding gene in mature OL lineage cells post-demyelination. This reveals a cell-autonomous requirement for OL-CB1 in terminal differentiation, independent of neuronal, astrocyte, microglial, or NG2 cell CB1 effects. Specifically, eCB signaling through CB1 receptors expressed in the OL lineage, not only regulates the initial stages of NG2 cell differentiation during remyelination [19], but possibly also orchestrates the final steps of OL maturation (present study). As such, the comparative use of the OPC (Ng2-CB1KO mice) [19] and OL (Plp-CB1KO mice, present study) cellular compartments provides a framework to distinguish independent roles of CB1 receptors in NG2 cell differentiation vs. OL maturation. This dual regulatory function significantly influences the efficiency and dynamics of CNS remyelination and, consequently, motor function recovery. Hence, CB1 receptors are essential for the transition from undifferentiated NG2 glial cells to fully mature myelinating OLs in the demyelinated brain. Because the mechanism of cell death in OLs is maturation stage-dependent [35], this may conceivably suggest that impaired OL maturation resulting from CB1 receptor dysfunction or downregulation would increase the vulnerability of these cells to apoptosis-mediated death. This is particularly relevant in neurological disorders, as accumulating evidence indicates that defective OL maturation constitutes a key pathogenic mechanism across a range of neurological and neurodevelopmental conditions [35–37]. Rather than representing a secondary response to neuronal injury, impaired differentiation from OPCs to mature myelinating OLs actively contributes to neuronal dysfunction and circuit instability. Recent multi-omics analyses have confirmed that dysregulation of OL maturation is a shared hallmark of numerous demyelinating, neurodegenerative, neurodevelopmental, and neuropsychiatric diseases [35–37]. This so-called “maturation block” has been documented in multiple sclerosis [36], Parkinson’s disease [38], Alzheimer’s disease [39], Huntington’s disease [37], and autism spectrum disorders, among others [40]. Collectively, these findings support the notion that defective OL maturation and abnormal myelin biogenesis represent core cellular substrates underlying disrupted connectivity and dysfunctional neural circuits in neurological disorders. This convergence also suggests that restoring the transcriptional and metabolic programs that drive OL differentiation may provide an effective strategy to ameliorate network dysfunction across a broad spectrum of brain diseases [41]. Consistent with this view, pharmacological agents with remyelinating potential are under active investigation as therapeutic candidates for numerous neurological conditions [41]. In this context, our findings complement previous studies demonstrating that pharmacological activation of CB1 receptors, through THC administration, promotes NG2 cell differentiation, stimulates OL maturation, and enhances both myelin formation [10] and regeneration [11]. Taken together, our findings reinforce the therapeutic potential of targeting the eCB system to modulate OL lineage progression and promote remyelination. Given the pervasive involvement of myelin dysfunction in neurological disease, cannabinoid-based compounds emerge as particularly attractive candidates to restore cellular homeostasis and improve neural connectivity. Their combined ability to regulate OL differentiation, attenuate neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, and support neuronal survival highlights their multifaceted potential for intervention in neurological disorders.

In this study, we also show that the defects in remyelination observed in Plp-CB1KO mice are concomitant with a sustained and reactive phenotype of astroglial and microglial cells. Detailed neuropathological characterization of this mouse model indicates that prolonged myelin loss and insufficient restoration are accompanied by robust and persistent microgliosis and astrogliosis, supporting the idea that failure of remyelination is tightly associated with chronic glial reactivity [42]. Under these conditions, microglia tend to maintain a reactive, pro-inflammatory phenotype and enhanced phagocytic/activated profiles, instead of shifting toward a more pro-regenerative program [43, 44]. In parallel, astrocytes remain strongly reactive, contributing to a chronic injury-like microenvironment that can reinforce inflammation and limit effective tissue repair [45, 46]. Moreover, oligodendrocyte lineage cells are now recognized to produce immunomodulatory factors that modulate glial activation and CNS inflammation. Oligodendrocyte lineage cells produce many different chemokines, cytokines, antigen presentation complexes, and complement proteins [47, 48], thereby regulating microglial and astroglial activation, survival, and migration to the site of injury [42]. As such, we cannot exclude the possibility of a specific function for OL CB1 receptors in modulating astro/microglial cell activation during remyelination. Further research aimed at elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms and optimizing cannabinoid-based therapeutic formulations may pave the way for innovative treatment strategies that harness the reparative capacity of the eCB system in the diseased brain.
