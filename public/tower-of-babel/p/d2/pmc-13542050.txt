Assessing the Work Relative Value Unit System in Orthopaedic Oncology: A Matched Cohort Analysis
Authors: James O. Gaston, Sydney Horenstein, Pietro M. Gentile, Vineeth Romiyo, John Epoh Dibato, Richard D. Lackman, Christina J. Gutowski, Tae Won B. Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Benign and malignant bone and soft-tissue tumors have a broad range of clinical presentations. Operative management ranges from limited excision to complex resection and reconstruction. The work relative value unit (wRVU) is designed to quantify physician time, technical skill, and effort, but it is unclear whether current assignments adequately reflect the overall procedural demands of musculoskeletal orthopaedic oncology. The aim of this study was to compare wRVU generation between orthopaedic oncology procedures and matched nononcologic orthopaedic procedures after controlling for measures of physician time and intensity.Methods:A retrospective observational study was conducted using the American College of Surgeons' National Surgical Quality Improvement Program. Musculoskeletal orthopaedic oncology cases were stratified by Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code according to tumor type. Mahalanobis distance matching was used to match orthopaedic oncology cases with nononcology orthopaedic controls. Total case wRVU generation was compared between groups.Results:Among 2,138,294 orthopaedic cases, 6,700 were musculoskeletal orthopaedic oncology cases (mean age 48 years, 45% female, 61% White, 9.6% Black, 5.0% Asian, 1.4% Other, and 23% unknown/not reported). After matching, oncology cases had 19.0% lower average wRVU (16.2 vs. 20.0) than matched controls despite similar operative times and case characteristics. The mean total case wRVUs were lower for benign soft-tissue tumors (9.7 vs. 15.8, −38.6%), benign bone tumors (11.0 vs. 15.8, −30.6%), and malignant soft-tissue tumors (18.1 vs. 26.3, −31.1%) compared with matched orthopaedic controls. By contrast, malignant bone tumors generated higher mean total case wRVUs than matched controls (43.9 vs. 37.0, +18.9%). Using a standardized workload of 675 operative hours per year, orthopaedic oncology procedures were estimated to generate 6,883 annual wRVUs vs. 8,570 for matched controls, corresponding to $56,167 lower annualized Medicare-based procedural value.Conclusions:Musculoskeletal orthopaedic oncology procedures generated fewer wRVUs than matched nononcologic orthopaedic procedures. We advocate for hospital and department leaders to consider this discrepancy within the wRVU model and account for it when designing compensation models for orthopaedic oncologists.Level of Evidence:Economic and Decision Analysis Level IV. Introduction

The work relative value unit (wRVU) captures physician work as a function of time and intensity. Time includes preservice, intraservice, and postservice work, while intensity reflects mental effort, technical skill, and stress related to procedural risk. Physician work comprises approximately 51% of total relative value units, with the remaining components attributable to practice expense and professional liability expense.1-3

Debate persists regarding whether the RVU system accurately reflects physician time and value, particularly for specialists managing high-risk populations and complex surgical cases such as orthopaedic oncologists4-6. Currently, the way the RVU system evaluates the effort of orthopaedic oncologists, particularly its relationship to procedure type, service-related physician work, patient complexity, and reimbursement, remains unclear7,8. If the current wRVU framework does not accurately reflect the work required for orthopaedic oncology procedures, this may have important implications for surgeon compensation, workforce recruitment, and institutional support for orthopaedic oncology practice. Therefore, further research on the relationship among these variables and the influence of the current wRVU system on orthopaedic oncology practice is warranted.

Within the data elements available in the American College of Surgeons' National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP), the aim of this study was to evaluate total case wRVU generation between orthopaedic oncology procedures and matched nononcologic orthopaedic procedures after accounting for available NSQIP measures representing preservice work, intraservice work, postservice work, and case intensity. We hypothesized that orthopaedic oncology procedures would generate lower wRVUs than matched nononcologic orthopaedic procedures after adjustment for these measured factors.

Methods

Study Design

A retrospective review of the NSQIP Participant Use Data File was performed using data from 2016 to 20249. NSQIP is a national surgical registry with standardized preoperative, intraoperative, and 30-day postoperative data abstracted by trained reviewers. The study population was generated by querying all cases tagged with the orthopaedic surgical specialty. This study was deemed exempt from Institutional Review Board review because the data are deidentified and accessed under the NSQIP data use agreement.

Cohort and Subgroup Identification

Three main cohorts were identified: (1) all orthopaedic cases, (2) all musculoskeletal orthopaedic oncology cases, and (3) a matched cohort of nononcology orthopaedic controls. The orthopaedic oncology cohort was based on the primary Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code and subdivided into 4 subgroups based on tumor type: benign soft-tissue, benign bone, malignant soft-tissue, and malignant bone (Supplemental Table 1).

Exclusion criteria included cases with wRVU or operative time of 0, or identified as extreme outliers in operative time (defined as values more than 3 interquartile ranges above the third quartile or below the first quartile). Cases involving concurrent procedures performed by separate surgical teams were excluded to avoid inflating wRVU values or operative times (Supplemental Figure 1).

Variables of Interest

The primary outcome was the mean total case wRVUs. Sixteen NSQIP variables were used as proxies for shared domains of physician work. Preservice work was approximated using demographic and comorbidity variables, including age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, diabetes status, and hypertension requiring medication. Intraservice work was represented by operative time, measured from incision to closure. Postservice work was approximated using readmission, reoperation, and hospital length of stay. Case intensity was represented by American Society of Anesthesiologists classification, functional status, inpatient vs. outpatient status, and NSQIP-estimated morbidity and mortality probability. Total case wRVUs were defined as the wRVU for the primary CPT code plus the summed wRVUs of any NSQIP “other” CPT codes recorded for the same operation (Supplemental Tables 2 and 3).

Statistical Techniques

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 19 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) with the PSMATCH2 package. Mahalanobis distance matching was used to minimize differences across the 16 physician-work proxy variables and achieve the closest 1:1 patient-to-patient matches possible between orthopaedic oncology cases and nononcology orthopaedic controls. Matching was performed in the overall cohort and within each tumor-type category (Appendix A. Supplemental Methods). After matching, covariate balance was assessed to verify that the matching process minimized systematic differences between groups. wRVUs per operative minute, defined as total case wRVUs divided by operative time, were calculated as a secondary standardized rate for annualized modeling and comparison with prior literature. For cohort comparisons, continuous variables were compared between the case and control groups using two-sample t-tests. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson χ2 test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Continuous variables are summarized as mean (SD) and categorical variables as counts (percentages). To express differences in wRVU as annualized dollar equivalents, annual wRVUs were estimated by multiplying the observed wRVU-per-minute by a standardized annual operative workload. Medicare-based procedural value was then estimated by multiplying annual wRVUs by the 2024 Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Medicare conversion factor.

Following cohort matching, the 6,700 orthopaedic oncology cases were matched with an equal number of nononcology orthopaedic control cases (Supplemental Table 2). Orthopaedic oncology procedures demonstrated 19.0% lower mean wRVUs compared with matched orthopaedic cases (16.2 ± 14.7 vs. 20.0 ± 18.3; p < 0.001), despite similar mean operative times (95.2 ± 102.0 vs. 94.4 ± 96.5 minutes; p = 0.649) and similar measures across the measured NSQIP matching variables. When standardized by operative time, this corresponded to a 19.7% lower wRVU-per-minute for orthopaedic oncology cases (0.170 vs. 0.212).

Among tumor types, benign soft-tissue, benign bone, and malignant soft-tissue cases demonstrated significantly lower mean wRVUs than matched nononcology orthopaedic controls. Benign soft-tissue cases had 38.6% lower wRVUs (9.7 ± 4.7 vs. 15.8 ± 11.3; p < 0.001), benign bone cases had 30.6% lower wRVUs (11.0 ± 6.1 vs. 15.8 ± 12.1; p < 0.001), and malignant soft-tissue cases had 31.1% lower wRVUs (18.1 ± 7.2 vs. 26.3 ± 21.9; p < 0.001). By contrast, malignant bone cases had 18.9% higher wRVUs than matched controls (43.9 ± 17.1 vs. 37.0 ± 28.8; p < 0.001). Operative time did not differ significantly between orthopaedic oncology cases and matched controls in any tumor-type subgroup (Table II).

Overall, orthopaedic oncology cases generated lower wRVUs than matched orthopaedic cases across each study year, with the difference widening from 2016 to 2024. Across the soft-tissue and benign bone subgroups, orthopaedic oncology cases consistently demonstrated lower wRVUs compared with matched orthopaedic cases. Over the past 9 years, the gaps across these cohorts have also widened. Although malignant bone cases generated higher wRVUs than the matched cohort in aggregate, this difference fluctuated during the study period and slightly widened in recent years (Fig. 2).

To express differences in wRVUs as an annualized dollar equivalent, we modeled a standardized operative workload of 15 operating hours per week (incision-to-closure) for 45 weeks annually (totaling 675 operative hours per year). Under this model, orthopaedic oncology cases would generate approximately 6,883 annual wRVUs at an average rate of 0.170 wRVU-per-minute. In comparison, matched orthopaedic cases would generate 8,570 wRVUs annually at a rate of 0.212 wRVU-per-minute. Using a CMS conversion factor of $33.29 per wRVU, this corresponds to $229,110 vs. $285,277 in annual Medicare-based procedural value, a difference of $56,167 per year (Table III).

Discussion

The wRVU is intended to represent the total professional effort required to perform a medical or surgical procedure10,11. RVU assignments are informed by the American Medical Association Relative Value Scale Update Committee recommendations, which rely heavily on physician survey data to estimate procedure time and complexity12-14. Despite the intent to standardize reimbursement according to physician effort, orthopaedic oncology procedures, characterized by long technically demanding operations and extensive perioperative management, may not be appropriately valued within this framework. This study provides an exploratory comparison of wRVU generation for orthopaedic oncology procedures relative to matched nononcologic orthopaedic procedures.

Orthopaedic oncology procedures yielded 19.0% fewer wRVUs than matched orthopaedic controls, which translated to $56,167 less annualized value at the CMS Medicare conversion rate. These findings suggest that current wRVU assignments may not fully capture the work associated with orthopaedic oncology procedures, and that the overall wRVU gap between orthopaedic oncology and matched orthopaedic procedures widened during the study period. This finding was heterogeneous across orthopaedic oncology categories. The largest relative differences were observed in the benign bone, benign soft-tissue, and malignant soft-tissue cohorts, where orthopaedic oncology cases generated more than 30% fewer wRVUs than matched controls. By contrast, malignant bone procedures generated 18.9% more wRVUs than matched controls. Prior orthopaedic oncology literature has similarly demonstrated reimbursement pressure within this subspecialty, with inflation-adjusted Medicare payment for common orthopaedic oncology procedures declining by 14% from 2000 to 20207. The real-world reimbursement implications of these findings may be amplified in multicomponent oncologic procedures. For example, intralesional curettage of a giant cell tumor in the proximal tibia may include curettage, adjuvant treatment, and fixation during the same operation. Although each additional procedure adds operative time, payment for secondary procedures may be reduced under Medicare’s Multiple Procedure Payment Reduction.15

The lower wRVU generation observed in this study adds to prior literature showing that discrepancies in wRVU assignment have been reported in other orthopaedic subspecialties. Sodhi et al. found that revision total hip arthroplasty (THA) required significantly longer operative time compared with primary THA (152 vs. 94 minutes, p < 0.001) but yielded fewer wRVU-per-minute (0.249 vs. 0.260, p < 0.001)16. Simcox et al. demonstrated in both orthopaedic trauma and sports surgery that procedures with extended operative durations received significantly lower wRVUs per hour (0.153 vs. 0.187, p = 0.02), even after adjusting for complication rates and case complexity17. Likewise, studies in the hand and shoulder literature have documented substantial discrepancies between operative time and wRVU allocation (R2 ≈ 0.6), with more complex reconstructive procedures being disproportionately undercompensated18-20. Beyond orthopaedics, Childers et al. found that across surgical specialties, operative time explained only 74% of the variation in wRVU assignment (R2 = 0.74), underscoring a systemic limitation in how physician work is quantified.21

The authors acknowledge that negotiating change to the RVU system is challenging. These data are not intended to directly advocate for modification of the RVU framework. Instead, our findings recognize that all subspecialties of orthopaedic surgery are valuable to patients and healthcare systems. We hope to provide hypothesis-generating, policy-relevant data showing that orthopaedic oncology procedures generate lower wRVUs than matched nononcologic orthopaedic procedures within the limits of available NSQIP measures. We encourage department chairs and hospital executives to consider the nonmonetary value that an orthopaedic oncologist provides, including resident education, allowing the institution to take on medically and surgically complex patients, downstream revenue recognized by other departments through multidisciplinary cancer care, and the development of breadth and depth of a well-rounded orthopaedic department. Alternative compensation models for orthopaedic oncologists, unbound from a strict volume-based framework, may account for the complexity of care delivered and the broader institutional value contributed by this subspecialty. Future efforts should focus on generating objective data to more accurately quantify the workload associated with orthopaedic oncology surgery, through large national and society-wide databases. These data-driven updates would provide a more accurate and consistent structure for valuing surgical work in orthopaedic oncology.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the NSQIP database cannot fully capture all aspects of surgical intensity, surgeon judgment, or perioperative decision making. For example, NSQIP also does not capture surgeon-specific or institutional factors, including surgeon volume, trainee involvement, instrumentation, intraoperative imaging, or multidisciplinary planning. Accordingly, the matched cohorts should be interpreted as relevant comparisons from the perspective of evaluating wRVU assignment, rather than as attempts to match clinically identical procedures across orthopaedic subspecialties. Nevertheless, this approach still allowed standardization across multiple measurable domains relevant to physician work and provided a more rigorous framework than unmatched analyses based on operative time alone. Second, NSQIP provides only short-term (30-day) follow-up, limiting assessment of long-term complications, reoperations, and resource use. Third, NSQIP participation is voluntary and may skew toward larger academic or high-volume centers. Therefore, the data set may not fully reflect all US practice environments and may introduce selection bias. Fourth, NSQIP does not capture billing modifiers, which may influence reimbursement. Last, the annualized dollar estimate should not be interpreted as surgeon salary or actual compensation. It represents Medicare-based procedural value under a standardized operative workload and does not account for employment structure, payer mix, collections, salary guarantees, wRVU conversion rates, call pay, institutional subsidies, or contract negotiation. Despite these limitations, the national scope of the database provided sufficient orthopaedic oncology case volume for analysis, which may be difficult to achieve in single-institution studies.

Conclusion

Orthopaedic oncology procedures generated lower wRVUs than matched nononcologic orthopaedic procedures after adjustment for available measures of physician time and intensity. The current wRVU system may not fully reflect the procedural work of orthopaedic oncologists. These findings suggest that department chairs and hospital executives may consider procedure-level wRVU discrepancies when developing compensation models and institutional support structures for orthopaedic oncology.
