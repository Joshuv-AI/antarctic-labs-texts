Optimizing Polymyxin B Therapy in Critical Care: Pharmacokinetic Insights and Clinical Outcomes in a Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Qingyun Peng, Qing Li, Mengru Xiong, Mingze Bi, Linlin Hu, Jie He, Shijia Zhong, Shuai Liu, Haofei Wang, Chen Zhang, Jianfeng Xie, Yingzi Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCarbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria pose significant global health threats due to high infection rates and limited treatment options. Polymyxin B (PMB) has reemerged as a last-line therapy against these pathogens, despite nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity concerns. However, the precise correlation between PMB exposure and response/toxicity has not been well established. Introduction

Multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria (MDR-GNB) infections are a major cause of morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients [1, 2], especially carbapenem-resistant GNB (CR-GNB) pose significant global health threats due to high infection rates and limited treatment options [3]. Polymyxin B (PMB) has reemerged as a last-line therapy against these pathogens, despite nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity concerns [4].

PMB works by binding to lipopolysaccharides on GNB outer membranes, disrupting integrity and causing cell death [5]. However, its clinical use is limited by a narrow therapeutic window [6], significant interpatient pharmacokinetic (PK) variability, and high incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI), which occurs in 20–60% of patients [7, 8]. Current guidelines recommend an area under the concentration–time curve across 24 h at steady state (AUCss, 24 h) target of 50–100 mg·h/l for PMB against CR-GNB with MIC ≤ 2 mg/l [4, 9]. Recent population pharmacokinetic (Pop-PK) studies highlight PMB’s complex PK profile, especially in critically ill patients with varying renal function, obesity, or those receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) [10, 11]. PMB clearance is influenced by creatinine clearance, body weight, and albumin levels, but there is no consensus on dose adjustments for these factors [10, 12, 13].

The precise correlation between PMB exposure and response/toxicity has not been well demonstrated [14, 15]. Achieving the recommended AUCss, 24-h target is challenging in clinical practice. Studies show that exposure below the target range is associated with suboptimal outcomes [7, 8], while exposure above 100 mg·h/l increases AKI risk [16]. The relationship between PMB exposure and clinical response is also controversial. For example, Liu et al. found no link between AUC compliance and mortality but observed a significant association with AKI (P = 0.019) [17], while another study showed favorable outcomes in pneumonia patients with AUCss, 24 h > 66.9 mg·h/l [18]. These findings highlight the need for individualized dosing strategies based on patient-specific factors and therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM).

This study aims to investigate the impact of PMB pharmacokinetics and dose adjustments on clinical outcomes in critically ill patients, identify AKI risk factors, and evaluate PMB’s clinical efficacy.

Methods

Patient Enrollment and Data Collection

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary teaching hospital (Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University, Nanjing, China). Between January 2020 and July 2023, all patients who received intravenous PMB (Polymyxin B sulfate for injection, Shanghai First Biochemical Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) for the treatment of CR-GNB infections were included. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) under 18 years of age; (b) received intravenous PMB treatment for less than 48 h; (c) lacked pathogenic microorganism results; (d) lacked TDM results; (e) pregnant women. Carbapenem susceptibility of carbapenem-resistant organisms (CRO) was determined by EUCAST criteria [9, 19]. PMB susceptibility testing was performed using the standard broth microdilution method. PMB breakpoints were defined as sensitivity (S ≤ 2 mg/l) and resistance (R > 2 mg/l). Polymyxin-sensitive bacteria were identified using the VITEK-2 Compact system (BioMerieux, France).

Data were extracted from electronic medical records (EMR), including demographic information, comorbidities, clinical and microbiological features of the infection, antimicrobial treatment regimens (including duration and dosage of PMB therapy and concomitant medications), laboratory test parameters on the day of PMB initiation and TDM data. The daily serum creatinine levels and daily urine output, organ support therapies (including CRRT, mechanical ventilation, and ECMO), and severity of illness scores (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation [APACHE II] score and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA] score) were obtained from medical records. Two physicians independently extracted data and conducted cross-verification to ensure accuracy. This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (2023ZDSYLL296-P01). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature.

PMB Administration and the TDM

For PMB, a loading dose of 2.0–2.5 mg/kg followed by a maintenance dose of 1.25–1.5 mg/kg every 12 h is recommended, administered intravenously with each infusion lasting at least 1 h [4, 20, 21]. The treatment regimens, including medication dosage, duration, selection of combination antibiotics, TDM, and therapeutic modifications, were collaboratively developed by attending physicians and clinical pharmacists based on individual patient conditions.

The TDM of PMB follows the established protocol of the Department of Pharmacy [22]. TDM was initially assessed after the completion of the fourth dose of the medication. Blood samples were collected immediately before the fifth dose infusion of PMB (Cmin), 1 h (Cmax) and 6 h (C6h) after the start of infusion. Prior to analysis, blood samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 1500 × g and 4 °C. The analytical method, designed and validated according to the standard operating procedure, demonstrated linearity in the range of 0.25–15 μg/ml for plasma. Quality control samples, comprising low, middle, and high concentrations, accounted for over 6% of the total sample volume. The precision and accuracy of this analytical method conformed to the standards for biological sample analysis. The inter-run precision ranged from − 2.8 to 6.2%. The corresponding values for intra-run precision were 2.6% to 5.8%. Using Phoenix WinNonlin 6.4 software for the non-compartmental model analysis (NCA) of PMB, pharmacokinetic parameters were computed by considering patient dosing, dosing intervals, and blood sample collection times, resulting in values for AUCss, 24 h. TDM was routinely performed throughout the treatment course. Clinical pharmacists provided dosage adjustment recommendations. PMB dose adjustments were made based on TDM results, clinical response, and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values. If the AUCss, 24 h reached the target range of 50–100 mg·h/l [4], the current dosage was maintained. For values outside this range, the maintenance dose was adjusted by 25–50% per titration step until the target AUCss, 24-h attainment. For patients with TDM data, the AUCss, 24 h, Cmin, Cmax, and C6h values were collected for each instance during the treatment course.

Endpoints and Study Variables Definitions

The primary outcome is the 28-day mortality, documented from the initiation of PMB treatment. Secondary outcomes encompass clinical efficacy, length of hospital and ICU stay, and the occurrence of newly developed AKI.

The infection site was defined as the primary anatomical origin of the PMB-treated infection, classified according to the criteria of the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) [23–25]. Diagnostic confirmation required: (1) microbiological documentation (positive culture from sterile site or quantitative BAL ≥ 10 CFU/ml); and (2) compatible clinical/radiological findings. Sites included: pulmonary (including ventilator-associated pneumonia), bloodstream (primary bacteremia or catheter-related), intra-abdominal, urinary tract, skin/soft tissue, and central nervous system infections. Site-of-infection assignment process: two attending physicians independently determined the infection site by integrating clinical findings, imaging results, and microbiological evidence; discrepancies were resolved by the research team cross-validation. To ensure diagnostic accuracy, we implemented a rigorous two-stage validation process: (1) Initial Diagnostic Extraction: Potential infection categories were first identified using structured clinical data within the EMR. This included relevant ICD-10 codes corresponding to specific infection types and explicit "infection category" diagnoses documented in admission or discharge summaries. (2) Investigator Validation: All infection diagnoses underwent independent review by two senior intensive care specialists, each possessing over 15 years of clinical experience. Validation was based on a comprehensive assessment of clinical presentation, serial changes in infection biomarkers, imaging findings, and microbiological evidence. Any discrepancies in diagnosis between the two reviewers were resolved through consensus-based discussion within the research team.

Clinical efficacy was assessed according to clinical and microbiological criteria after the last dose of PMB and evaluated by two physicians at the end of treatment. The clinical response to PMB treatment was determined by comparing the patient’s baseline signs and symptoms of infection with those after treatment. Favorable clinical response was defined as disappearance or improvement of clinical symptoms (body temperature < 38.0 °C, hemodynamic stability with mean blood pressure ≥ 65 mmHg without vasopressor support, improved or stable PaO2/FiO2 ratio and SOFA score), radiological resolution of infection-related signs, and biochemical improvement (≥ 30% decrease in total peripheral white blood cell count or C-reactive protein [CRP] level) [8, 17]. Microbiological eradication was defined as negative cultures for the causative microbiota during the course of PMB treatment [8, 26].

New-onset AKI was defined as an increase in serum creatinine by ≥ 0.3 mg/dL (26.5 μmol/l) or ≥ 50% from baseline within 48 h during PMB treatment. AKI severity was further categorized according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria [27].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R software version 4.3. Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as frequencies (%). For comparisons, Student’s t test was used for normally distributed variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratio (HR) for 28-day mortality. Variables that were clinically relevant or showed a univariate relationship with the outcome were included in the multivariate models. Variables were carefully selected to ensure model parsimony and to avoid overfitting, considering the number of events. Collinearity was assessed, with a tolerance > 0.2 or a variance inflation factor (VIF) < 10 deemed acceptable. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between risk factors and PMB-associated AKI, using clinically relevant and statistically significant variables as covariates. Time-to-event analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier curves. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

From January 2020 to July 2023, 178 patients treated with PMB were screened. Of these, 32 patients were excluded: six were under 18 years old, eight did not receive intravenous PMB, ten lacked TDM data, six received treatment less than 48 h, and two were pregnant. Ultimately, 146 patients were included in the analysis (Fig. S1).

The key demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table S1. The median age was 68 years (IQR 58–75), and 81.5% (119/146) were male. The most prevalent comorbidity was hypertension (54.1%, 79/146). The median APACHE II score was 24, and the median SOFA score was 9. Regarding resistance profiles, 132 patients (90.4%) had MDR infections, and 144 (98.6%) had CRO, 89% of CRO isolates demonstrated polymyxin B MICs ≤ 1 mg/l. The most common pathogens were Acinetobacter baumannii (58.9%, n = 86). Based on the AUCss, 24 h, patients were categorized into three groups: high AUCss, 24 h (> 100 mg·h/l, n = 47), moderate AUCss, 24 h (50–100 mg·h/l, n = 68), and low AUCss, 24 h (< 50 mg·h/l, n = 31). Table 1 presents the characteristics of patients stratified by initial AUCss, 24 h values. Among all infection sites, pulmonary infections represented the predominant site, accounting for 85.6% (125/146) of cases (Table S1). Within the pulmonary infection cohort, the sources of bacterial isolates were as follows: bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) specimens yielded isolates in 63.2% (79/125) of patients, sputum samples in 29.6% (37/125), and pleural drainage fluid or pulmonary pus specimens in 7.2% (9/125). The proportion of lung infections was significantly higher in the high AUCss, 24 h group (87.2%) and moderate AUCss, 24 h group (91.2%), compared to 71.0% in the low AUCss, 24 h group (P = 0.027). Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections were more frequent in the high AUCss, 24 h group (23.4%) than in the moderate (7.4%) and low (6.5%) AUCss, 24 h groups (P = 0.019). We observed that patients in the low AUCss, 24 h group had higher baseline albumin concentrations compared to those in the high and moderate AUCss, 24 h groups [33.8 g/l (IQR 31.6–38.4) vs. 32.1 g/l (IQR 29.1–36.3) and 32.0 g/l (IQR 29.5–33.9), P = 0.009). The use of CRRT also differed significantly among the groups, with 61.3% (19/31) in the low AUCss, 24 h group, 41.2% (28/68) in the moderate AUCss, 24 h group, and 27.7% (13/47) in the high AUCss, 24 h group (P = 0.013) (Table 1).

PMB Treatment and Pharmacokinetics

The median daily treatment dose of PMB was 2.1 mg/kg/day. Approximately 20.6% of patients received an initial loading dose. The median duration of PMB treatment was 11 days, with 84.9% of patients receiving treatment for 7 days or more (Table S1). Specifically, the median duration of PMB use was 13 days in the high AUCss, 24 h group, 11 days in the moderate AUCss, 24 h group, and 8 days in the low AUCss, 24 h group (P = 0.104) (Table 1).

The median AUCss, 24 h for PMB was 78.3 mg·h/l (IQR 52.0–110.0). The median AUCss, 24 h values for the high, moderate, and low groups were 133.2 mg·h/l (IQR 110.0–169.0), 73.4 mg·h/l (IQR 63.2–83.7), and 41.8 mg·h/l (IQR 31.6–46.0), respectively (P < 0.001). The median Cmax was 6.6 μg/ml (IQR 4.9–9.9), the C6h was 2.7 μg/ml (IQR 1.4–4.0), and the Cmin was 1.4 μg/ml (IQR 0.7–2.4) (Table S1).

Ultimately, 43.2% (63/146) of patients achieved a favorable clinical response, and 48.6% (71/146) had microbiological eradication (Table S1). However, there were no statistically significant differences among the three groups in these outcomes. Dosage adjustments of PMB were more common in the high AUCss, 24 h group (34.0%) and low AUCss, 24 h group (45.2%) compared to the moderate AUCss, 24 h group (7.4%, P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Risk Factors for 28-day Mortality

Of these patients, the 28-day mortality rate was 43.2% (63/146). Notably, the mortality rates in the high, moderate, and low AUCss, 24 h groups were 27.7%, 45.6%, and 61.3%, respectively (P = 0.012), suggesting higher survival in the high AUCss, 24 h group (Tables 1, S1, Fig. 1). Comparisons between the survival and non-survival groups revealed several significant differences (Table S1). The median duration of PMB treatment was longer in survivors (13 days [IQR 9–18] vs. 9 days [IQR 7–13]; P < 0.001). The median length of hospital stay was 27 days (IQR 18–36) among non-survivors versus 50 days (IQR 34–87) in survivors (P < 0.001). The AUCss, 24 h was higher in the survival group (87.2 [IQR 65–123.2] vs. 68.2 [IQR 46–97.4]; P = 0.002).

Regarding antimicrobial combination therapy, tigecycline was most commonly used with PMB (44.5%, 65/146 cases). The rate of combination therapy was significantly higher in the non-survivor group (58.7%, 37/63) compared to the survivor group (33.7%, 28/83; P = 0.003). Conversely, carbapenem-containing combination therapy was associated with improved survival outcomes. The survival group exhibited a significantly higher utilization rate of carbapenem combination therapy (71.1% [59/83]) compared to the non-survival group (28.9% [18/63]; P = 0.04). Among organ support measures, CRRT use was more frequent in the non-survival group (55.6%, 35/63) than in the survival group (30.1%, 25/83; P = 0.002). (Table S1). Cox regression analysis identified independent risk factors for 28-day mortality: male sex [HR 0.503, 95% CI 0.292–0.865, P = 0.013], presence of comorbidities [HR 2.556, 95% CI 1.155–5.654, P = 0.021], higher SOFA score at admission [HR 1.097, 95% CI 1.035–1.161, P = 0.002], lower AUCss, 24 h [HR 0.993, 95% CI 0.987–0.999, P = 0.023], and shorter duration of PMB treatment [HR 0.896, 95% CI 0.850–0.945, P < 0.001] (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Impact of PMB Pharmacokinetic Parameters on the Risk of 28-Day Mortality

To investigate the impact of PMB pharmacokinetic parameters on 28-day mortality, we constructed two multivariable Cox regression models, each adjusted for different confounding factors. Model 1 was adjusted for comorbidities, SOFA score at admission, and CRRT treatment, while Model 2 included additional adjustments for baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), APACHE II score at admission, PMB treatment duration, and baseline total bilirubin. In Model 1, the hazard ratios for AUCss, 24 h and C6h were 0.992 (95% CI 0.985–0.998, P = 0.008) and 0.791 (95% CI 0.672–0.931, P = 0.005) respectively. The HR for Cmin was 0.767 (95% CI 0.606–0.971, P = 0.027). In Model 2, the HRs for AUCss, 24 h and C6h were 0.992 (95% CI 0.986–0.998, P = 0.011) and 0.8 (95% CI 0.684–0.935, P = 0.005) respectively. The HR for Cmin was 0.75 (95% CI 0.598–0.941, P = 0.013). These results indicate that AUCss, 24 h, C6h, and Cmin were inversely correlated with the 28-day mortality in patients (Table S2).

PMB pharmacokinetic parameters can serve as predictors of 28-day mortality in patients. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis indicated that the cutoff values for predicting 28-day mortality were 81.6 mg·h/l for AUCss, 24 h, 1.4 μg/ml for Cmin, 7.7 μg/ml for Cmax, and 2.5 μg/ml for the C6h (Table S3, Fig. 3).

Risk Factors of AKI

To assess the impact of PMB treatment on AKI, we analyzed the occurrence of AKI in patients, excluding those who had received CRRT before PMB initiation or had pre-existing AKI. A total of 45 patients were excluded, leaving 101 patients for analysis. AKI was observed in 40.6% of patients, with 34.1% patients classified as stage 1, 24.4% patients as stage 2, and 41.5% patients as stage 3. AKI incidence rates were 31.3% in the low AUCss, 24 h group, 39.1% in the moderate group, and 46.2% in the high group (P = 0.571) (Table S4, Fig. S2).

Baseline characteristics revealed that the AKI group had higher creatinine levels [median 85 µmol/l (IQR 60.5–120.5) vs. 60 µmol/l (IQR 41.7–88.2); P = 0.002] and lower eGFR [median 83 ml/min/1.73 m2 (IQR 43–100) vs. 101 ml/min/1.73m2 (IQR 81–112); P = 0.007] compared to the non-AKI group. The duration of PMB treatment was longer in the AKI group [median 14 days (IQR 9–18) vs. 11 days (IQR 7–14); P = 0.01]. However, no significant differences were observed in median AUCss, 24 h (87.2 mg·h/l vs. 84.5 mg·h/l; P = 0.338) or 28-day mortality (36.6 vs. 33.3%; P = 0.736) between the two groups (Table S5).Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified baseline eGFR as a protective factor against AKI (adjusted OR 0.979, 95% CI 0.963–0.994, P = 0.007) and PMB treatment duration as a risk factor (adjusted OR 1.101, 95% CI 1.007–1.204, P = 0.034) (Table S6). ROC curve analysis determined cutoff values for predicting AKI: 12.5 days for PMB treatment duration (P = 0.004) and 89.5 ml/min/1.73m2 for baseline eGFR (P = 0.002) (Table S7, Fig. S3).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between PMB pharmacokinetics and clinical outcomes in critically ill patients with CR-GNB infections. The favorable clinical response rate was 43.2%, the bacteriological eradication rate was 48.6%, and the 28-day mortality was 43.2%. These findings are consistent with previous studies [28, 29]. A meta-analysis of 27 studies involving 2, 910 patients with MDR-GNB infections reported an all-cause mortality rate of 41.2%, further supporting the comparability of our results [7].

We found a high proportion of males (81.5%). This predominance primarily reflects the higher baseline incidence of PMB-targeted MDR-GNB (e.g., P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii) in males, consistent with numerous studies reporting 60–75% male representation among recipients of this antibiotic [17, 30–32]. Moreover, as a tertiary referral ICU managing transferred critical cases, our center frequently encounters advanced infections where delayed healthcare-seeking behavior in males contributes to disease progression, necessitating last-resort therapies. This distribution thus reflects the real-world epidemiological context of this high-alert antibiotic [33, 34].

Our TDM protocol demonstrated key strengths including an analytically robust LC–MS/MS methodology with rigorous validation addressing critical care-specific challenges. This encompassed comprehensive specificity, accuracy, and precision assessments, with dedicated evaluation of matrix effects in hyperlipidemic plasma. The method achieved a wide linear range (0.25 to 15 μg/ml), maintained precision within acceptance criteria (inter-run: − 2.8% to 6.2%; intra-run: 2.6–5.8%), and incorporated quality control samples exceeding Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requirements (> 6% of analytical runs). Post-fourth-dose sampling avoided initial distribution phase interference while enabling dose adjustments within 48–72 h.

In our study, the Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated that patients with AUCss, 24 h > 100 mg·h/l exhibited a higher survival rate compared to those with AUCss, 24 h of 50–100 mg·h/l or those with AUCss, 24 h < 50 mg·h/l (P = 0.0081). Furthermore, we analyzed the relationship between the PK parameters of PMB and the 28-day mortality. The results indicated that AUCss, 24 h ≥ 81.6 mg·h/l, Cmin ≥ 1.4 µg/ml, and C6h ≥ 2.5 µg/ml were associated with a reduction in 28-day mortality. Previous studies have shown that achieving the AUCss, 24 h target of 50–100 mg·h/l is associated with favorable clinical outcomes for PMB [8]. Liu et al. reported that the target AUC compliance was not correlated with clinical outcomes but with AKI (P = 0.019) in their prospective study [17]. Another prospective observational multicenter study has indicated that the AUC was an independent risk factor for 14-day mortality in intra-abdominal infection patients, and the cutoff value was 76 mg∙h/l [35].

Our findings exhibit minor discrepancies compared to previous studies, which may be attributable to the following two reasons. Firstly, patients in this study had a median APACHE II score of 24 and a median SOFA score of 9 upon enrollment, the severity of illness among patients in this study was higher compared to previous clinical studies on PMB pharmacokinetics [8, 17, 29, 36]. Secondly, 85.6% of the patients in our study had pulmonary infections. According to the results of mouse lung infection models, the present recommended AUCss, 24 h target (50–100 mg·h/l) may be suboptimal for the systemic treatment of pneumonia [4]. Another real-world prospective study indicated that PMB can lead to favorable clinical outcomes in pneumonia patients with AUCss, 24 h > 66.9 mg∙h/l [18]. Using the ROC curve, we identified a clinical cutoff value for AUCss, 24 h of 81.6 mg·h/l. Given that 89% of carbapenem-resistant isolates exhibited PMB MIC ≤ 1 mg/l, the clinical cutoff value of AUCss, 24 h/MIC of 81.6 may represent a promising PK/PD target for optimizing PMB efficacy in patients. These results suggest that in critically ill patients with severe pulmonary infections; higher AUC levels may be associated with improved survival.

However, it is noteworthy that this ROC analysis demonstrated a sensitivity of only 60% and a specificity of only 66%, indicating that the risks of both false-negative and false-positive results are not negligible. Therefore, this parameter should be used in conjunction with other variables to enhance predictive accuracy. Importantly, this identified threshold falls within the currently recommended exposure target range (50–100 mg·h/l) and aligns with previously reported mortality-associated thresholds [18, 35]. Despite its limited discriminative power, it may serve as a preliminary screening tool to identify high-risk patients, rather than as a definitive basis for clinical decisions. Given that this was a single-center, retrospective study with a limited sample size, the derived threshold requires further validation in prospective, multicenter cohorts. Future research should explore the development of dynamic predictive models integrating PK/PD parameters with clinical variables to improve the identification of patients at high risk of PMB-associated mortality.

Our study observed pulmonary infections as the predominant presentation, with ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) constituting the majority of cases. Methodologically, while VAP diagnosis employed rigorous IDSA/ATS criteria [23] with independent expert adjudication, we acknowledge inherent challenges in definitively confirming pneumonia in mechanically ventilated patients [37]. These include: (1) non-specificity of classic VAP symptoms that may manifest in systemic inflammatory responses; (2) overlapping radiological features with alternative diagnoses (e.g., Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome [ARDS], pulmonary edema); (3) limited specificity of biomarkers (e.g., procalcitonin, CRP) in critical illness; and (4) potential contamination in non-bronchoscopy sampling techniques. Such diagnostic uncertainties could introduce bias into our findings. However, several protocol safeguards mitigated diagnostic uncertainty. As PMB is a restricted antimicrobial agent, its administration followed stringent institutional protocols requiring multidisciplinary consensus confirming: (a) VAP caused by MDR-GNB pathogens; (b) microbiological confirmation via quantitative thresholds (BAL ≥ 104 CFU/ml, endotracheal aspirate ≥ 105 CFU/ml); and (c) serial radiological/ultrasonographic verification of infiltrate progression while excluding volume overload or cardiogenic causes. The high VAP proportion reflects our ICU’s role as a tertiary referral center managing complex transfers, predominantly involving treatment-refractory severe pneumonia or ventilator-dependent patients. Thus, our cohort represents clinically confirmed VAP in real-world practice, with incidence rates consistent with multicenter studies [8, 17, 38]. Future investigations incorporating metagenomic sequencing could enhance diagnostic precision by discriminating colonization from true infection.

In our study, 38 patients (26%) received combination therapy with PMB and carbapenem antimicrobial agents. The clinical utility of polymyxin-carbapenem combination therapy for CR-GNB remains debated, though recent guidelines provide conditional recommendations. Both international guidelines [3, 39] and a consensus guidance document [40] specify distinct indications: (1) For carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), combination therapy is recommended when meropenem MIC is ≤ 8 mg/l with high-dose extended-infusion administration; (2) For carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), polymyxin-meropenem combinations are recommended generally [40], and specifically for pneumonia and bloodstream infections demonstrating in vitro synergy regardless of carbapenem MIC (≤ 32 mg/l) [39]. Consequently, carbapenems were co-administered with PMB in selected patients to leverage therapeutic advantages for CRAB respiratory/hematogenous infections. Furthermore, combination therapy was clinically warranted in cases of concomitant carbapenem-susceptible and CR-GNB infections within individual patients.

The prevalence of AKI in our study was 40.6%, aligning with a recent meta-analysis that reported an overall incidence of 40.7% [7]. Multivariable logistic regression did not identify AUCss, 24 h as a risk factor for AKI. However, Wang et al. found that an AUCss, 24 h > 100 mg·h/l was a significant predictor of nephrotoxicity (P = 0.001) [16]. Similarly, a retrospective study by Yang et al. demonstrated that an AUCss, 24 h > 100 mg·h/l was a strong predictor of AKI (HR 16.29, 95% CI 8.16–30.25, P < 0.001) [8]. Our retrospective study design and limited sample size may have influenced these results. Excluding 45 patients due to uncertainty regarding AKI’s relation to PMB treatment could have affected the assessment of AUCss, 24 h and AKI. Additionally, AUCss, 24 h values were measured after the fifth dose, potentially influenced by dose adjustments and CRRT. A higher proportion of patients receiving CRRT was observed in the AKI group compared to the non-AKI group (26.8 vs. 6.7%, P = 0.005).

In our study, a higher baseline eGFR had a protective effect against AKI (adjusted OR 0.979, 95% CI 0.963–0.994, P = 0.007), patients with an eGFR < 89.5 ml/min/1.73 m2 had a significantly higher incidence of AKI. These findings are consistent with a prospective multicenter study by Chang et al., which also reported that eGFR had a protective effect on AKI occurrence (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–0.99, P = 0.0006) [36]. Multivariable logistic regression analysis in our study showed that the duration of PMB treatment was significantly associated with an increased risk of AKI (adjusted OR 1.101, 95% CI 1.007–1.204, P = 0.034), and ROC curve analysis indicated that the cutoff values for predicting AKI were 12.5 days (P = 0.004). Similar findings were reported by Huseyin et al., who identified the duration of PMB use as an independent risk factor for AKI development (HR 0.845, 95% CI 0.728–0.98, P = 0.026) [41]. However, Chang et al.’s study suggested that treatment duration did not affect AKI occurrence (HR 1.0, 95% CI 0.98–1.03, P = 0.9619) [36]. In our study, the median duration of PMB treatment was 14 days in the AKI group and 11 days in the non-AKI group. Differently, Chang et al.’s study reported an average treatment duration of 13 days in both the AKI and non-AKI groups [36]. This discrepancy may suggest that 14 days of PMB treatment may represent a critical threshold for AKI occurrence.

This study has several limitations: (1) potential restricted generalizability as a single-center retrospective investigation of adult patients; (2) a sample size that precluded comprehensive covariate analysis of population pharmacokinetic parameters; (3) potential confounding in the treatment duration-mortality association due to the “death during therapy” phenomenon, wherein shorter therapy in non-survivors may reflect disease severity rather than causal effects; (4) inability to define the upper AUCss, 24 h threshold balancing efficacy against nephrotoxicity despite observed survival benefits with higher exposure; (5) exclusion of 45 patients with ambiguous polymyxin B-attributable nephrotoxicity, potentially biasing assessment of AUCss, 24 h-AKI relationships; and (6) use of non-isotopically labeled roxithromycin as the internal standard, which increases susceptibility to matrix effects.

Conclusions

The pharmacokinetic parameters of PMB are closely related to patient outcomes. An AUCss, 24 h ≥ 81.6 mg·h/l may reduce 28-day mortality. Additionally, baseline eGFR and PMB treatment duration were independent risk factors for AKI.
