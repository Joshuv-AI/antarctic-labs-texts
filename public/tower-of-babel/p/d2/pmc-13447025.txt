Longitudinal Evolution and Cellular Plasticity in Meningioma: Insights From Single-Cell Transcriptomics
Authors: Ji Yoon Lee, Jason K. Sa, Harim Koo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Meningiomas are the most common primary intracranial tumors and exhibit a broad spectrum of clinical behavior, ranging from indolent growth to repeated recurrence and aggressive progression. Over the past decade, genomic, epigenomic, bulk transcriptomic, and single-cell studies have refined meningioma classification and identified molecular features associated with recurrence risk. However, recurrence is increasingly recognized as a dynamic process shaped by tumor-intrinsic plasticity, subtype redistribution, treatment-related selection, and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Recent longitudinal single-nucleus RNA sequencing of matched primary and recurrent meningiomas has extended this framework by demonstrating enrichment of proliferative, DNA repair, and extracellular matrix programs in recurrent disease, together with multidirectional transcriptional trajectories rather than simple linear progression. These findings complement earlier methylation- and transcriptome-based risk models, as well as cross-sectional single-cell studies that have highlighted neoplastic heterogeneity, macrophage interactions, and immune-state diversity across meningioma grades. Among recurrence-associated genes, COL6A3 has emerged as a clinically relevant extracellular matrix marker linked to aggressive tumor states and poor relapse-free survival. Nevertheless, the proposed COL6A3-macrophage interaction should be interpreted cautiously because current evidence remains largely correlative and may be influenced by intervening treatment between primary and recurrent sampling. This review synthesizes current evidence on longitudinal evolution, cellular plasticity, molecular subtype shifts, and microenvironmental remodeling in recurrent meningioma, while outlining key limitations and future directions for clinically actionable longitudinal profiling.

INTRODUCTION

Meningiomas arise from arachnoid cap cells and account for approximately one-third of all primary central nervous system tumors. Although most are classified as World Health Organization grade 1 and generally follow a benign clinical course, a clinically important subset, particularly World Health Organization grade 2 and 3 tumors, demonstrates aggressive behavior, treatment resistance, and frequent recurrence [1]. Surgical resection, with or without radiotherapy, remains the standard treatment. However, effective systemic therapeutic options for recurrent meningiomas remain limited.

Over the past decade, integrative genomic, epigenomic, and transcriptomic studies have substantially advanced our understanding of meningioma biology and led to molecular subclassification systems with prognostic value [234567891011121314]. These include DNA methylation classes, integrated molecular-morphologic grades, hypermitotic and proliferative subgroups, immunogenic states, NF2 wild-type groups, and hypermetabolic programs, each associated with distinct biological pathways and clinical outcomes. These classification systems have been particularly useful for risk stratification, but most were developed from cross-sectional cohorts and therefore provide a relatively static view of a biologically dynamic disease.

Longitudinal studies are especially important in neuro-oncology because recurrent tumors are often biologically distinct from their primary counterparts. Bulk sequencing approaches have identified clonal selection, copy-number alterations, CDKN2A alterations, TERT promoter mutations, and transcriptomic programs associated with aggressive behavior. However, bulk profiling has limited ability to resolve functional cellular states, rare subpopulations, and changes in the immune or stromal microenvironment. Single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic approaches have begun to address this gap by mapping neoplastic heterogeneity, meningeal cellular composition, macrophage states, and immune interactions at cellular resolution [1516].

Within this broader literature, longitudinal single-nucleus analyses of matched primary and recurrent meningiomas provide an important opportunity to connect molecular classification with temporal evolution. Rather than treating recurrence as simple regrowth of the original tumor, these studies support a model in which recurrent meningiomas can acquire new transcriptional states, shift subtype composition, and remodel their surrounding microenvironment. The objective of this review is to synthesize current evidence for longitudinal evolution and cellular plasticity in recurrent meningioma, integrate these observations with existing molecular and single-cell literature, and critically discuss the limitations that must be addressed before these findings can guide clinical practice.

LONGITUDINAL TRANSCRIPTIONAL REPROGRAMMING IN RECURRENT MENINGIOMA

A central theme emerging from longitudinal profiling is that recurrent meningiomas are not always simple replicas of the primary lesion. Matched primary-recurrent analyses show that recurrence can be accompanied by increased expression of genes involved in cell-cycle progression, DNA repair, extracellular matrix organization, and stress-adaptation programs. These observations are consistent with broader molecular studies linking proliferative and hypermitotic signatures, loss of cell-cycle control, and high-risk methylation groups with recurrence and aggressive clinical behavior.

Single-cell and single-nucleus approaches add an additional layer by showing that these programs are distributed across heterogeneous tumor-cell states rather than appearing as a uniform bulk signal. Cross-sectional single-cell studies have demonstrated marked heterogeneity across pathological grades and identified interactions between neoplastic cells and macrophage populations. Profiling of human dura and meningioma has also provided a reference for meningeal cell types and immune states, helping distinguish tumor-associated programs from the broader cellular landscape of the meninges. Longitudinal single-nucleus data extend these observations by demonstrating how cellular states can be redistributed between primary and recurrent disease.

Trajectory-based analyses, including RNA velocity and latent-time inference, suggest that recurrent meningioma cells may follow divergent transcriptional paths rather than a single linear evolutionary route. This interpretation supports a model of multidirectional evolution, in which cellular plasticity allows tumor cells to adapt to selective pressures imposed by growth, surgery, radiotherapy, and the altered post-treatment niche. Because these computational trajectories infer directionality from transcriptional states rather than directly observing every intermediate state, they should be interpreted as models of plausible evolution rather than definitive proof of a fixed causal sequence.

MOLECULAR SUBTYPE DYNAMICS AND CELLULAR PLASTICITY

Molecular subtype identity in meningioma appears to be partly dynamic over time. Prior studies have defined major molecular groups, including immunogenic, NF2 wild-type, hypermetabolic, and proliferative subtypes. In longitudinal single-nucleus datasets, primary tumors were relatively enriched for MG1 immunogenic states, whereas recurrent tumors showed increased proportions of MG2 cells and, in some cases, expansion toward hypermetabolic or proliferative MG3/MG4 states. These patterns suggest that subtype composition can change during disease progression rather than remaining permanently fixed at initial diagnosis.

The directionality of these subtype shifts is important. Available evidence does not establish that transition from MG1 toward MG2, MG3, or MG4 is itself the initiating cause of recurrence. A more cautious interpretation is that recurrence, intervening treatment, and microenvironmental selection may favor the expansion or emergence of tumor-cell states with less immune-reactive and more proliferative or metabolically active features. Thus, subtype redistribution is best viewed at present as a marker and potential mediator of recurrent biology rather than as a proven primary cause of recurrence.

This distinction has practical implications. Molecular classification at initial diagnosis remains valuable for risk stratification, but a single baseline profile may not fully capture the biology of later recurrent disease. Re-biopsy and repeat molecular profiling at recurrence may therefore improve risk assessment and therapeutic planning, particularly for high-grade or multiply recurrent meningiomas. Longitudinal classification frameworks will need to integrate stable genomic alterations, dynamic transcriptional states, and treatment history to distinguish durable risk markers from acquired recurrence-associated programs.

COL6A3 AS A RECURRENCE ASSOCIATED DRIVER AND THERAPEUTIC CANDIDATE

Among recurrence-associated genes, COL6A3 has emerged as a notable extracellular matrix-related candidate. Longitudinal single-nucleus profiling showed that COL6A3 expression increased along later latent transcriptional states and was associated with high-risk cellular programs. Recurrent tumors showed higher COL6A3 expression than primary tumors, and elevated COL6A3 levels were associated with worse relapse-free survival in an independent validation cohort. These findings position COL6A3 as a potentially useful biomarker of aggressive tumor biology.

Functional data also support a possible tumor-intrinsic role for COL6A3. Silencing of COL6A3 reduced tumor cell proliferation and altered cell-cycle distribution in meningioma cell lines, suggesting that COL6A3 may contribute to proliferative fitness rather than serving only as a passive marker. Immunohistochemical validation further confirmed increased COL6A3 protein expression in recurrent meningioma tissues. These observations are biologically plausible because extracellular matrix remodeling can influence cell adhesion, mechanotransduction, growth-factor signaling, and the architecture of the tumor niche.

At the same time, COL6A3 should be framed as a recurrence-associated marker and candidate vulnerability rather than a fully established therapeutic target. The available functional evidence primarily addresses tumor-cell proliferation, whereas the mechanisms linking COL6A3 to macrophage recruitment, C1Q-positive macrophage enrichment, or M2-like polarization remain incompletely defined. Future studies should test whether COL6A3 directly regulates macrophage behavior, acts indirectly through extracellular matrix remodeling, or marks a broader stromal program associated with recurrent disease.

REMODELING OF THE TUMOR MICROENVIRONMENT

The tumor microenvironment is increasingly recognized as an active component of meningioma biology. Cross-sectional single-cell studies have shown that meningiomas contain diverse immune and stromal populations and that macrophage-neoplastic cell interactions vary across pathological grades. Single-cell profiling of human dura and meningioma further established that immune-response programs in meningioma should be interpreted against the background of the normal meningeal cellular landscape. These studies provide broader context for interpreting microenvironmental changes observed in recurrent tumors.

Longitudinal single-nucleus data indicate that recurrent meningiomas can exhibit increased infiltration or enrichment of immunosuppressive macrophage populations, including C1Q-positive macrophages, along with reduced inflammatory or immune-reactive features. These myeloid states are associated with extracellular matrix organization and pro-tumorigenic signaling, suggesting that recurrent tumors may evolve within a niche that supports growth, immune evasion, and persistence. Changes in lymphocyte states, including features suggestive of dysfunction or exhaustion, further support the concept of an immunologically remodeled recurrent microenvironment.

The proposed interaction between COL6A3-expressing tumor cells and C1Q-positive macrophages is an important hypothesis, but current evidence should be interpreted with restraint. Ligand-receptor analyses suggested enrichment of COL6A3-related crosstalk, including interactions involving CD44 and integrin complexes, and spatial or protein-level validation supported colocalization of COL6A3-expressing tumor cells with C1Q-positive macrophages. However, ligand-receptor inference and immunohistochemical correlation do not prove that COL6A3 directly recruits macrophages or induces an immunosuppressive phenotype. Experimental coculture systems, perturbation of COL6A3 in vivo or ex vivo, spatial transcriptomics, and treatment-annotated longitudinal cohorts will be required to establish mechanism (Fig. 1).

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Current evidence has several clinical implications. First, recurrent meningioma should be regarded as a potentially evolved disease state rather than a simple continuation of the primary lesion. Treatment strategies based only on the molecular features of the initial tumor may not adequately reflect the biology of recurrence, especially in high-grade or multiply recurrent disease.

Second, longitudinal molecular profiling may improve patient stratification. Recurrent tumors may acquire distinct proliferative, extracellular matrix, and immune programs, and these acquired states may influence prognosis or therapeutic vulnerability. Combining DNA methylation classification, copy-number and mutation analysis, bulk transcriptomics, single-cell or single-nucleus profiling, and spatial validation may provide a more complete picture than any single platform alone.

Third, COL6A3 and related extracellular matrix pathways represent promising areas for translational investigation, but therapeutic development should proceed from mechanistic validation. Candidate strategies may include targeting tumor-cell proliferative programs, matrix-remodeling pathways, macrophage-tumor interactions, or immune-suppressive niches. The most effective approaches for recurrent disease may ultimately require combined targeting of tumor-intrinsic programs and the microenvironment that supports them.

Future studies should prioritize prospective longitudinal sampling, treatment-annotated cohorts, standardized single-cell and spatial workflows, and functional perturbation experiments. Integration with clinical endpoints such as recurrence-free survival, radiographic progression, and response to radiotherapy or systemic treatment will be essential for moving from descriptive evolutionary maps to clinically actionable biomarkers.

CONCLUSION

The study of recurrent meningioma is moving from static molecular subclassification toward dynamic models of longitudinal evolution. Genomic, methylation, bulk transcriptomic, and single-cell studies collectively show that recurrence can involve proliferative activation, DNA repair programs, extracellular matrix remodeling, subtype redistribution, and immune microenvironmental change. Longitudinal single-nucleus profiling strengthens this view by demonstrating that recurrent tumors may follow multidirectional transcriptional trajectories and acquire distinct cellular states over time.

COL6A3 is a particularly notable recurrence-associated marker because it links extracellular matrix remodeling, high-risk tumor-cell programs, proliferative potential, and macrophage-rich microenvironments. However, the COL6A3-macrophage axis remains a hypothesis that requires direct mechanistic testing, and treatment-induced remodeling must be carefully considered. A balanced review of the field therefore supports longitudinal profiling as a powerful framework for understanding recurrent meningioma while emphasizing the need for larger, treatment-aware, mechanistically validated studies before these insights can be translated into routine clinical practice.
