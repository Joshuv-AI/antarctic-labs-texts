Detection of sex‐specific glutamate changes in subregions of hippocampus in an early‐stage Alzheimer's disease mouse model using GluCEST MRI
Authors: Narayan Datt Soni, Anshuman Swain, Halvor Juul, Quy Cao, Mohammad Haris, David A Wolk, Virginia M‐Y Lee, John A Detre, Ravi Prakash Reddy Nanga, Ravinder Reddy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONRegional glucose hypometabolism resulting in glutamate loss has been shown as one of the characteristics of Alzheimer's disease (AD). Because the impact of AD varies between the sexes, we utilized glutamate‐weighted chemical exchange saturation transfer (GluCEST) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for high‐resolution spatial mapping of cerebral glutamate and investigated subregional changes in a sex‐specific manner.METHODSEight‐month‐old male and female AD mice harboring mutant amyloid precursor protein (APP NL‐F/NL‐F : n = 36) and wild‐type (WT: n = 39) mice underwent GluCEST MRI, followed by proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H‐MRS) in hippocampus and thalamus/hypothalamus using 9.4T preclinical MR scanner.RESULTSGluCEST measurements revealed significant (p ≤ 0.02) glutamate loss in the entorhinal cortex (% change ± standard error: 8.73 ± 2.12%), hippocampus (11.29 ± 2.41%), and hippocampal fimbriae (19.15 ± 2.95%) of male AD mice. A similar loss of hippocampal glutamate in male AD mice (11.22 ± 2.33%; p = 0.01) was also observed in 1H‐MRS.DISCUSSIONSGluCEST MRI detected glutamate reductions in the fimbria and entorhinal cortex of male AD mice, which was not reported previously. Resilience in female AD mice against these changes indicates an intact status of cerebral energy metabolism.HighlightsGlutamate levels were monitored in different brain regions of early‐stage Alzheimer's disease (AD) and wild‐type male and female mice using glutamate‐weighted chemical exchange saturation transfer (GluCEST) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).Male AD mice exhibited significant glutamate loss in the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and the fimbriae of the hippocampus.Interestingly, female AD mice did not have any glutamate loss in any brain region and should be investigated further to find the probable cause.These findings demonstrate previously unreported sex‐specific glutamate changes in hippocampal sub‐regions using high‐resolution GluCEST MRI.Keywords: 1H‐MRS, Alzheimer's disease, APPNL‐F/NL‐F mice, GluCEST MRI, glutamate, sex‐specific changes

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder and comprises 60%–70% of all cases of dementia, 1 with at least 55 million people worldwide and 6.7 million Americans living with AD. 1 , 2 Glutamate is the major excitatory neurotransmitter in the mature mammalian central nervous system 3 and regulates various cognitive functions 4 commonly impaired in AD including learning and memory. 5 , 6 Numerous proton (1H) and carbon‐13 (13C)‐magnetic resonance spectroscopic (MRS) studies have reported perturbations in the levels of cerebral glutamate and rate of its biosynthesis in human patients and animal models of AD. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Glucose hypometabolism has been shown to be a key characteristic of AD, 13 , 14 , 15 resulting in reduced availability of a tri‐carboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediate, α‐ketoglutarate, which serves as the key precursor for the biosynthesis of glutamate and thus leads to its decreased availability. 16 Multiple studies conducted using 13C‐MRS have convincingly demonstrated a reduction in the labeling of glutamate from the metabolism of 13C‐labeled glucose in mouse models of AD, which is very often attributed to reduced glutamatergic neuronal TCA flux due to glucose hypometabolism leading to reduced glutamate synthesis. 8 , 14 , 15 Consequently, spatial mapping of glutamate could be useful in understanding regional glutamate changes in AD.

Glutamate‐weighted chemical exchange saturation transfer (GluCEST) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a technique that utilizes the phenomenon of bulk water saturation by saturation of exchangeable glutamate amine protons at 3.0 ppm with respect to water at 0 ppm. 17 , 18 , 19 This enables the indirect measurement of glutamate through bulk water using conventional MRI sequences and enables high‐resolution spatial mapping of glutamate. Preliminary studies using GluCEST suggest the presence of regional glutamate alterations in AD models. 17

The prevalence, progression, and severity of AD 20 , 21 and other neurological disorders 22 differ across males and females. A recent demographic study concerning the prevalence of AD in the United States shows that almost two‐thirds of total patients are female, which may be attributed to their longevity, as age is the biggest risk factor for the disease. 21 However, sex‐specific structural and functional differences 22 , 23 in the brain may also contribute to sex differences in AD prevalence and clinical course. Furthermore, it supports the consideration of sex as a biological variable in AD research, 24 which has relevance for the diagnosis and management of AD. 25 Altogether, the inclusion of both male and female subjects in AD studies, and sex‐specific evaluation of findings, could be more informative. Unfortunately, such inclusive studies in various neuroscience and biomedical research investigations are limited 26 , 27 , 28 and longstanding neuroscience research has been predominantly male biased. 29 Although changes in regional cerebral glutamate levels have been studied extensively in AD, there is limited information available about the sex‐specific impact of AD on cerebral glutamate metabolism. In the current study, our goal was to monitor regional alterations in the level of cerebral glutamate in the APPNL ‐F/NL‐F (mutant amyloid precursor protein gene) mouse model of AD using GluCEST MRI in vivo in a sex‐specific manner. To corroborate the findings from GluCEST MRI, we performed localized in vivo 1H‐MRS to monitor the levels of glutamate in the hippocampus and thalamus–hypothalamus regions. Furthermore, immunohistochemical studies were performed to evaluate the levels of amyloid beta (Aβ), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neuronal nuclei (NeuN) in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus as markers for amyloidosis, gliosis, and neuronal loss, which have been often reported in AD. 30 , 31

METHODS

The protocol used for animal experiments was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Pennsylvania. The study was performed using 6‐ to 9‐month‐old wild‐type C57BL6/J (WT) and APP NL‐F/NL‐F (AD) mice. These mice were divided into four groups: WT males (age [mean ± SD] = 7.33 ± 1.30 months; n = 21), WT females (age = 7.64 ± 1.42 months; n = 18), AD males (age = 7.46 ± 0.84 months; n = 24), and AD females (age = 7.85 ± 1.88 months; n = 18). The APP NL‐F/NL‐F is a knock‐in mouse model of AD, 32 harboring Swedish (APP KM670 / 671NL ) and Iberian (APP I716F ) mutations in the amyloid precursor protein (APP) gene. These AD mice recapitulate several AD‐associated pathologies, including Aβ1‐42 plaques, synaptic loss, and microgliosis.

Animal preparation

For MRI/MRS experiments, mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (1.5%) in a mixture of air and O2 (70:30), with the head placed in a conical restrainer equipped with tubing for a continuous supply of isoflurane and air. A pressure pillow underneath the chest and a rectal thermal probe were placed for the continuous monitoring of respiration and temperature. This setup was placed inside a 20‐mm diameter dual channel volume 1H transceiver coil (m2m imaging, USA) in a way that our brain slice of interest falls on the center of the coil. This complete assembly of the coil with the mouse was finally placed inside a 30‐cm horizontal‐bore 9.4 T magnet interfaced with an Avance III HD console and Paravision 6.0.1 (Bruker Biospin, Germany). The coil center was aligned with the isocenter of the magnet and respiration and temperature of mice were maintained at 60–80 beats per minute and 37⁰C, respectively, throughout the course of the experiment.

MR imaging and spectroscopy

A localizer was acquired for the proper positioning of the animal. After achieving the proper position, the frequency of both channels was tuned to the 1H frequency and global frequency was corrected. Following that, automated global shimming for first‐ and second‐order shims were performed to attain a smooth and symmetric water line. Radiofrequency power for all subsequent experiments was optimized for a 5‐mm thick slice around the isocenter and a B0 map was acquired. To select our slice of interest, longitudinal relaxation time (T1)‐weighted fast low angle shot (FLASH) (Echo time (TE)/repetition time (TR) = 4/498 ms, four averages, sixteen 1 mm thick axial slices, field of view (FOV) 20 × 20 mm2, matrix size 128 × 128) and transverse relaxation time (T2)‐weighted rapid acquisition with relaxation enhancement (RARE) (TE1/TE2 = 33/121 ms, TR = 3082 ms, 2 averages, 1 mm thick 16 axial slices, rare factor = 6, FOV 20 × 20 mm2, matrix size 128 × 128) images were acquired.

Using these anatomic images, a 1‐mm thick axial slice (3.3 mm anterior to lambda) covering hippocampal regions, cerebral cortex, and the thalamus/hypothalamus was selected for our GluCEST MRI data acquisition. At first, the local frequency was adjusted, and first‐order shimming was performed to achieve a symmetric water linewidth (less than 30 Hz) using the Bruker MAPSHIM protocol. A water saturation shift referencing (WASSR) image (TE/TR = 4/410 ms, 22 frequency offsets from 0 to ±1.0 ppm with a step‐size of 0.1 ppm, B1 = 0.1 µT, one average) was acquired for correcting B0 inhomogeneities. GluCEST images were acquired using the given parameters: B1rms = 5.87 µT; saturation duration = 1000 ms (five block pulses of 200 ms length); offsets = ±2.4 to ±3.6 ppm; step‐size = 0.2 ppm; averages = 4; FOV 20 × 20 mm2, matrix size 128 × 128); in‐plane resolution: 0.156 mm × 0.156 mm; total acquisition time for the WASSR and GluCEST scans was ≈10 min. 17 , 18

Following the GluCEST acquisition, localized water‐suppressed 1H MR spectra were acquired from the hippocampus and thalamus of WT (n = 28; 16 male and 12 female) and AD (n = 27; 17 male and 12 female) mice. Voxels were placed in hippocampal (size: 6 × 1 × 2 mm3) and thalamus (size: 2 × 3 × 2 mm3) regions of the slice of interest, and local frequency and shims were adjusted to achieve a linewidth of ≤18 Hz. A Point RESolved Spectroscopy (PRESS) sequence (TE = 16 ms; TR = 3000 ms, reference scans 8, average 256) was used to acquire 1H spectra with a simultaneous VAriable Power and Optimized Relaxation delays (VAPOR) water suppression scheme.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was performed in a small set of mice (WT males, n = 3; WT females, n = 4; AD males, n = 3; AD females, n = 3) following GluCEST MRI. In brief, mice were first deeply anesthetized using isoflurane until a surgical plane of anesthesia was attained. Their hearts were exposed by opening the thoracic cavity. Mice were transcardially infused with 20 mL of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution each for live perfusion fixation. Brains of mice were collected and stored in 4% PFA for another 24 h for postmortem fixation. Following that, brain samples were washed and treated with a series of ethanol solutions (70%–90%) to dehydrate. These dehydrated brains were embedded in a paraffin block and 5‐µM thick axial sections were collected from the region utilized for GluCEST MRI.

Sections were placed on charged slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and deparaffinized. Chromogenic IHC was performed as described elsewhere 33 using a Leica BOND RXm automated platform combined with the Bond Polymer Refine Detection kit (Leica #DS9800). Briefly, after dewaxing and rehydration, sections were pretreated with the epitope retrieval BOND ER2 high pH buffer (Leica #AR9640) for 20 min at 98°C. Endogenous peroxidase was inactivated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at RT. Nonspecific tissue–antibody interactions were blocked by incubating the sections for 30 min at room temperature (RT) with Leica PowerVision IHC/ISH Super Blocking solution (PV6122). The same blocking solution also served as a diluent for the primary antibodies. Primary antibodies were incubated on the sections for 45 min at RT. A biotin‐free polymeric detection system consisting of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated anti‐rabbit or anti‐mouse IgG was then applied for 25 min at RT. For IHC, immunoreactivity was then revealed with the diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen reaction. Tissue sections were finally counterstained in hematoxylin, dehydrated in an ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and permanently mounted with a resinous mounting medium (Thermo Scientific ClearVue coverslipper). Negative controls were obtained by replacement of the primary antibodies with irrelevant isotype‐matched rabbit antibodies. The Aperio Versa 200 instrument was used for image acquisition. GFAP‐ and NeuN‐stained brain slices were processed using Aperio ImageScope (Leica Biosystems, USA) to obtain fractional positive areas in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex. The fractional area of Aβ plaques in cerebral cortex and hippocampus was calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health [NIH], USA).

GluCEST image processing and spectral analysis

All image‐processing steps were performed in MATLAB 2022a (MathWorks, CA) with custom‐written codes. The following registration and segmentation steps follow the methodology described previously. 34 Briefly, automated skull‐stripping was performed on the T2‐weighted image using fuzzy c‐means clustering. The brain was histogram matched to a corresponding slice of a C57BL/6J mouse brain atlas from Dorr et al., 35 with the selected atlas slice determined to be 3.3 mm anterior from the center of lambda. Iterations of affine transformations were performed with reduced blurring of the atlas and experimental brains in each subsequent iteration to allow for the registration of finer features. The final transformation was a non‐linear registration using the Demons algorithm. 36 The corresponding segmentations from the atlas were registered using nearest‐neighbor interpolation by applying the transforms determined from the anatomic image registration. The gray and white matter regions of interest that were evaluated in this study were as follows: gray (cerebral cortex, entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, and hypothalamus) and white (corpus callosum and fimbria).

1H MR spectra were analyzed using LCModel for the quantification of different metabolites. 37 Absolute concentrations of metabolites with reference to an unsuppressed water signal were obtained. Metabolite concentrations that had a Cramer Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) value <15%—glutamate, glutamine, N‐acetyl aspartate (NAA), taurine, myo‐inositol, total choline (phosphocholine + glycerophosphocholine), and total creatine were considered reliable for the analysis. Typically, creatine and phosphocreatine are not resolved properly at 9.4 T, and a reliable and established method for quantification of these metabolites does not exist. In this study, LC model fitting with our simulated basis set quantified creatine and phosphocreatine levels with a mean CRLB value <15%, and so we included these metabolites in our analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.0 (GraphPad, USA). The normality of data sets was tested using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Following this, we performed group wise Rout's outlier test (Q = 1%) for all the parameters to find that no value qualified to be an outlier. To analyze the effects of sex (male and female), genotype (WT and AD), and interaction (combined effect of sex and genotype) on GluCEST contrast and neurometabolite levels, a two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. ANOVA findings are reported as “least square (LS) mean difference; FDFn, DFd value; p value,” where DFn and DFd are degrees of freedom for the numerator and denominator, respectively. An effect with p ≤ 0.05 was considered significant. For post hoc multiple pairwise comparisons (six pairs) across the four groups, the Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used to find a significance of difference. Tukey HSD findings are reported as (95% confidence interval [CI] for the difference of means; LS mean difference; adjusted p value). Any change between two groups with an adjusted pvalue ≤ 0.05 (following correction for multiple comparison) was considered significant. Relative changes in the Discussion section are presented as %change ± standard error of the mean.

RESULTS

GluCEST measurements

Global and regional GluCEST maps from the brain slice of interest reflecting glutamate levels in the corpus callosum, entorhinal cortex, thalamus‐hypothalamus, hippocampus, cerebral cortex, dentate gyrus, and fimbriae of the hippocampus of representative WT and AD mice are shown in Figure 1. Mean GluCEST (%) was quantified from these brain regions of all male and female WT and AD mice. ANOVA for comparison of regional GluCEST (%) across mice in four groups indicated that the effects of sex and interaction between sex and genotype were insignificant on GluCEST contrast in these regions. We observed a significant effect of genotype on reduced GluCEST contrast from corpus callosum (LS mean diff. = 1.74 ± 0.49%; F(1, 77) = 12.74; p = 0.001), entorhinal cortex (LS mean diff. = 1.60 ± 0.56%; F(1, 77) = 8.12; p = 0.006), cerebral cortex (LS mean diff. = 1.13 ± 0.46%; F(1, 77) = 6.09; p = 0.016), hippocampus (LS mean diff. = 2.11 ± 0.74%; F(1, 77) = 8.18; p = 0.006), and fimbriae (LS mean diff. = 3.16 ± 0.85%; F(1, 77) = 13.81; p = 0.0004) of AD mice, whereas this effect remained insignificant in dentate gyrus (p = 0.36) and thalamus (p = 0.11) (Table S1). A Tukey HSD post hoc test revealed significant reductions in the hippocampus (LS means diff. = 3.15 ± 0.75%; 95% CI: 0.56 to 5.73; p = 0.011) (Figure 2A), fimbriae of the hippocampus (LS means diff. = 4.30 ±1.14%; 95% CI: 1.32 to 7.28; p = 0.002) (Figure 2B) and entorhinal cortex (LS means diff. = 2.24 ± 0.75%; 95% CI: 0.27 to 4.21; p = 0.019) (Figure 2C) of male AD mice compared to male WT mice. GluCEST contrast in the corpus callosum (LS means diff. = 1.69 ± 0.65%; 95% CI: −0.01 to 3.40; p = 0.053) showed a trend toward reduction, whereas changes were insignificant in the cerebral cortex (LS means diff. = 0.66 ± 0.61%; 95% CI: −0.95 to 2.27; p = 0.705) of male AD mice compared to WT males (Figure 2D). Interestingly, changes in GluCEST contrast in all assessed brain regions of female AD mice compared to female WT were insignificant (p ≥ 0.075) (Table 1).

1H‐MRS measurements

Representative 1H MR spectra, relatively scaled, from male WT and AD mice shown in Figure 3 were acquired from voxels that encompass the majority of the hippocampus, as presented in the image, show relatively lower intensities of glutamate and NAA signal from the AD mouse. Spectra were quantified to obtain concentrations of metabolites in the hippocampus and thalamus‐hypothalamus of mice. Following a two‐way ANOVA, we observed a significant effect of genotype resulting in reduced levels of glutamate (LS mean diff. = 0.99 ± 0.46 mM; F(1, 53) = 4.64; p = 0.036), taurine (LS mean diff. = 2.02 ± 0.47 mM; F(1, 53) = 18.18; p = <0.001), total creatine (LS mean diff. = 1.19 ± 0.43 mM; F(1, 53) = 7.71; p = 0.008), and phosphocreatine (LS mean diff. = 1.02 ± 0.39 mM; F(1, 53) = 6.93; p = 0.011) in the hippocampus of AD mice, whereas the level of NAA (LS mean diff. = 0.65 ± 0.39 mM; F(1, 53) = 2.86; p = 0.097) showed a trend toward reduction. On the other hand, the effect of sex was observed only by reduced levels of level of taurine (LS mean diff. = 1.38 ± 0.47 mM; F(1, 53) = 8.48; p = 0.005) and creatine (LS mean diff. = 0.83 ± 0.30 mM; F(1, 53) = 7.39; p = 0.009), whereas a trend toward reduction was observed for total creatine (LS mean diff. = 0.82 ± 0.43 mM; F(1, 53) = 3.68; p = 0.060) in female mice regardless of genotype. In addition, a significant interaction effect between sex and genotype on glutamate level (F(1, 53) = 4.27; p = 0.044) was observed in the hippocampus, whereas NAA (F(1, 53) = 3.36; p = 0.072) showed a trend toward a significant interaction (Table S2). Post hoc Tukey HSD multiple comparison test revealed a significant reduction in the level of hippocampal glutamate (LS means diff. = 1.93 ± 0.59 mM; 95% CI: 0.36 to 3.51; p = 0.01) in male AD mice compared to male WT mice, whereas levels remained unchanged (p > 0.99) between AD and WT female mice (Figure 4A). These results are in consonant with GluCEST measurements. Moreover, the levels of NAA (LS means diff. = 1.36 ± 0.50 mM; 95% CI: 0.03 to 2.69; p = 0.043) (Figure 4B), total creatine (LS means diff. = 1.75 ± 0.56 mM; 95% CI: 0.27 to 3.22; p = 0.014) (Figure 4D), and taurine (LS means diff. = 2.75 ± 0.62 mM; 95% CI: 1.12 to 4.38; p = 0.0002) (Figure 4C) were also found to be significantly reduced in male AD mice compared to WT mice, whereas they remained unaffected in female AD mice compared to WT females. The observed reduction in the levels of total creatine in male AD mice may be directly associated with a significant drop in phosphocreatine (LS means diff. = 1.57 ± 0.50 mM; 95% CI: 0.24 to 2.90; p = 0.014) levels in these mice because creatine (LS means diff. = 0.17 ± 0.39 mM; 95% CI: −0.87 to 1.22; p = 0.97) levels were unaltered. Furthermore, we observed a significant reduction in the levels of taurine (LS means diff. = 2.11 ± 0.67 mM; 95% CI: 0.32 to 3.90; p = 0.015) in the hippocampus of WT females compared to WT male mice (Table 2). In the thalamus‐hypothalamus region, a significant effect of sex was observed with reduced levels of taurine (LS mean diff. = 0.62 ± 0.23 mM; F(1, 52) = 7.22; p = 0.01) and total creatine (LS mean diff. = 0.52 ± 0.24 mM; F(1, 52) = 4.62; p = 0.036) in female mice (Table S3). We did not observe any significant effect of genotype or interaction between sex and genotype on neurometabolite levels in the thalamus‐hypothalamus region of these mice. Tukey HSD multiple comparison revealed no significant change across four groups in pairwise comparisons (Table S4).

Immunohistochemical measurements

Representative IHC images presented in Figure 5 show Aβ plaque, GFAP, and NeuN levels in AD and WT mice. We observed a significant interaction effect in the cerebral cortex (F(1, 9) = 9.20; p = 0.014) and hippocampus (F(1, 9) = 7.72; p = 0.022). However, we did not observe any significant effect of sex on GFAP expression in the cerebral cortex (LS mean diff. = 0.05 ± 0.02%; F(1, 9) = 4.43; p = 0.065) and hippocampus (LS mean diff. = 0.05 ± 0.02%; F(1, 9) = 4.14; p = 0.073). Similarly, the effect of genotype was also not significant in the cerebral cortex (LS mean diff. = −0.04 ± 0.02%; F(1, 9) = 2.84; p = 0.126), whereas the hippocampus (LS mean diff. = −0.05 ± 0.02%; F(1, 9) = 5.08; p = 0.051) showed a trend toward significance (Table S5). Post hoc Tukey HSD analysis showed significantly higher GFAP positive fractional areas in the cerebral cortex (LS means diff. = −0.11 ± 0.03%; 95% CI: −0.217 to −0.004; p = 0.043) (Figure 6A,B) and hippocampus (LS means diff. = −0.12 ± 0.03%; 95% CI:–0.22 to −0.01; p = 0.031) (Figure 6A,E) of male AD mice compared to male WT mice, whereas changes across female AD and WT mice remained insignificant for both regions (Table S6). We observed a significant effect of genotype on the presence of Aβ plaques in the hippocampus (LS mean diff. = −0.26 ± 0.11%; F1, 9 = 5.66; p = 0.041), whereas the effect in the cerebral cortex (LS mean diff. = −0.41% ± 0.19%; F(1, 9) = 4.44; p = 0.064) was not significant (Table S5). Effects of sex and interaction between sex and genotype on the presence of Aβ were not significant in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus regions of these mice. Multiple comparison analysis using Tukey HSD test did not show any significant change in cerebral cortex (Figure 6A,C) or hippocampus (Figure 6A,F) regions of male or female AD mice compared to respective WT controls (Table S6). No significant effects of sex, genotype, or interaction were observed in NeuN expression in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus (Figure 6A,D,G) of these mice.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we utilized high‐resolution GluCEST MRI for sex‐specific spatial assessment of regional glutamate changes in 6‐ to 9‐month‐old APP NL‐F/NL‐F mice. Herein we report significant reductions in GluCEST contrast in the hippocampus (11.29 ± 2.41%), hippocampal fimbria (19.15 ± 2.95%), and entorhinal cortex (8.73 ± 2.12%) of male AD mice, whereas changes in female AD mice were insignificant (Figure 2). Consistent with GluCEST findings, 1H MRS in the hippocampus also showed a similar decrease in the level of glutamate (11.22 ± 2.33%) exclusively in male AD mice. Reductions in hippocampal glutamate levels have been reported previously 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 and could be associated with compromised cognitive functions 38 including memory formation in AD due to impaired glutamate signaling. 5 , 6 Fimbriae surrounding the hippocampus comprise its afferent and efferent white matter pathways and relay information to and from the hippocampus. 39 Similarly, entorhinal cortex is the primary mediator in the exchange of information between the cerebral cortex and hippocampus proper 40 and intrinsic entorhinal cortex connectivity has been identified to be largely excitatory. 40 Based on in vivo electrophysiological recordings in mouse models of AD and brain imaging of AD patients, it has been argued that entorhinal cortex dysfunction may present itself in early stages of AD. 41 Reduced glutamate levels may affect the normal functioning of these brain regions, potentially leading to compromised cognition and memory in AD, 42 as reported previously in ≈8‐month‐old APP NL‐F / NL‐F mice. 43

Despite any significant sex‐specific changes (Table 1), GluCEST contrast from the corpus callosum and cerebral cortex of AD mice exhibited a significant genotype effect (p ≤ 0.016), with a reduction of 6.9 ± 1.4% and 3.9 ± 1.1%, respectively. Glutamate signaling plays a crucial role in the axonal remyelination of electrically active neurons in the corpus callosum by stimulating oligodendrocytes. 44 , 45 , 46 In fact, a recent GluCEST study demonstrated a direct relation between glutamate and myelin levels in the corpus callosum of a cuprizone‐administered rat model of demyelination and remyelination. 47 Reduction in cortical glutamate levels has been reported consistently in AD. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Because the cerebral cortex regulates a variety of cognitive and memory functions, including learning, spatial recognition, speech, emotions, decision making, and so forth 48 via glutamate signaling, 4 a reduction in glutamate levels could result in impairment of these functions in AD.

In addition to glutamate, 1H MRS results also suggested significant reductions in the levels of NAA (10.14% ± 2.47%), total creatine (10.06 ± 1.93%), and taurine (13.47 ± 1.82%) in the hippocampus of male AD mice, whereas female AD mice remained unaffected (Figure 4). Because NAA is mostly localized in neurons and reflects the status of neuronal integrity and viability, 49 a concomitant reduction in the level of NAA and glutamate may indicate poor integrity of glutamatergic neurons. Reduction in total creatine could be attributed to a significant decrease in phosphocreatine levels (17.33 ± 3.82%), which in turn indicates an impaired status of energy metabolism in male AD mice. Although unaltered creatine status suggests a disproportionate change in levels of creatine and phosphocreatine, the difficulty in resolving their signals in 1H MR spectra makes it a challenge to determine accurate contributions. Creatine phosphokinase, an extremely active enzyme, catalyzes a reversible reaction between phosphocreatine and creatine to synthesize adenosine triphosphate (ATP) from adenosine diphosphate (ADP). Hence, phosphocreatine serves as an energy buffer in the brain and helps in the regulation of mitochondrial activity and countering oxidative stress–mediated neurodegeneration. 50 , 51 , 52 Reports suggesting reduced creatine kinase activity (86%) and protein expression (14%) and lower phosphocreatine levels in the beginning stages of AD 53 , 54 , 55 support our observations of reduced hippocampal phosphocreatine levels in early‐stage male AD mice. Furthermore, the creatine/phosphocreatine system in the hippocampus has been shown to directly correlate with spatial memory. 56 , 57

Taurine acts as a γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor agonist and enhances chloride influx into postsynaptic neurons, causing hyperpolarization and consequently inhibiting hyperexcitability and mitigating neuroinflammation. 58 Given its role in regulating cellular energy metabolism, gene expression, osmosis, and quality control of proteins, 59 its therapeutic potential against AD and other neurodegenerative disorders has also been studied. 60 A postmortem study reported much lower levels of taurine in the temporal cortex of patients with AD (7.9 nM/mg) compared to healthy controls (15.01 nM/mg), 61 and is consistent with our observations in male AD mice.

Although IHC studies were performed in a limited number of mice due to ongoing longitudinal studies, observations of increased GFAP (glial activation) and Aβ plaque expressions without any neuronal loss in AD mice (Figures 5 and 6) agree with the reported phenotypes of these mice at this age. 32 Of interest, significantly higher GFAP levels, suggesting glial activation, were observed in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus of only male AD mice (Figure 6). We did not observe any sex‐specific changes in the levels of Aβ plaques, although higher Aβ levels have been reported in female mice in other mouse models of AD. 62 , 63

A granulovacuolar degeneration (GVD) study in patients with AD categorizes the progression of AD into five stages based on temporal degeneration of brain regions. 64 Beginning with hippocampal subfields cornu ammonis (CA) 1, CA2, and the subiculum in the first stage, entorhinal cortex and CA4 neurons are affected in the second stage. Further progression affects temporal neocortex in the third stage; amygdala and/or the hypothalamus in the fourth stage; and cingulate cortex, parietal cortices, and other regions in the fifth stage. Findings of the current study with male AD mice exhibiting significant perturbations in the hippocampus, fimbriae of the hippocampus, and entorhinal cortex suggest that these mice recapitulate early‐stage AD pathology.

Overall findings of the study indicate that the observed effects of AD on regional GluCEST contrast and hippocampal neurometabolites were driven mostly by prominent sex differences, with significantly higher metabolite reductions in male AD mice. In fact, loss of glutamate and NAA in the hippocampus of AD mice, with a significant genotype effect, appears to be driven by significant reductions (11.22 ± 2.33% and 10.14% ± 2.47%, respectively) in male AD mice, as female AD mice exhibited a negligible loss of 0.25 ± 4.40% and −0.44 ± 4.02%, respectively (Figure 4). Existing reports explain the role of estrogen in the protection against AD. It has been reported that Aβ induces the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by disrupting the electron transport chain. 65 Of interest, it was found that the mitochondria from younger female mice (5‐ to 6 months of age) produce much less ROS under Aβ(1‐42) toxicity compared to older female mice (24–26 months). 66 , 67 As female mice age, they become reproductively senescent due to a reduction in the levels of estrogen as well as reduced tissue sensitivity to estrogen. 68 , 69 The decrease in the levels of estrogen has been shown to affect cerebral glucose transport, metabolism, mitochondrial respiration, and ATP production, resulting in impaired brain energy metabolism in 3xTg‐AD mice. 70 , 71 The presence of estrogen receptors in the pre‐frontal cortex, subregions of the hippocampus, and the cingulate cortex 72 , 73 further validates the connection between estrogen loss–mediated glucose hypometabolism in these regions for female mice presenting AD after reproductive senescence.

An association between estrogen levels and energy metabolism is further supported by the finding of a 15–25% decline in metabolic function in the brain following ovariectomy and reproductive endocrine aging. 70 , 74 , 75 , 76 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose–positron emission tomography (18F‐FDG‐PET) studies in post‐menopausal women have suggested glucose hypometabolism in the hippocampus, para‐hippocampal gyrus and temporal lobe, 77 medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, 70 , 78 , 79 and frontal and temporal cortices. 70 Furthermore, estrogen replacement therapy has been shown to preserve regional glucose metabolism and memory function, 78 , 79 as these regions are associated with memory. The findings from our investigation support the observations from these previous studies and warrant a detailed investigation of the role of female reproductive hormones in AD.

A limitation of this study was an imbalanced sample size between the male and female cohorts. However, the inclusion of the mice in this study was based purely on availability, as the colonies of mice are bred and maintained in‐house. In addition, the magnitude of changes in regional GluCEST and neurometabolites in female mice were relatively small, with a power analysis suggesting that the minimum number of mice required in female AD and WT groups to achieve the anticipated effect size for significant changes was ≥29 animals for each group (i.e., female WT and female AD).

In summary, this study demonstrates the utility of GluCEST MRI for the measurement of subregional glutamate levels. In early stages of AD, loss of glutamate in the hippocampus has been reported in multiple 1H MRS studies. A previous GluCEST study has also reported glutamate loss in the CA region of the hippocampus proper in a mouse model of tauopathy. 17 Here, use of GluCEST MRI combined with an efficient segmentation pipeline enabled us to observe glutamate loss in entorhinal cortex and fimbriae of hippocampus. Furthermore, we observed sex differences in the severity and progression of AD in terms of glutamate and other neurometabolites, with male AD mice presenting significant reductions in metabolite levels, and no significant changes inAD female mice compared to their WT counterparts. To the best of our knowledge, such findings are being reported for the first time in APPNL‐F/NL‐F mice and could be utilized to further investigate the underlying cause for this disparity at preclinical stages of AD. Future investigations will study younger and older mice to understand the age‐dependent effect of AD in a sex‐specific manner, as the phenotypic effects observed at this age of mice in the current study may present themselves differently in male and female cohorts.
