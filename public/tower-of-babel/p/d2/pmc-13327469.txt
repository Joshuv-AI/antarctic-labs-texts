UGT1A1 Fragment Analysis: Genotyping the (TA)n Variable Repeat Polymorphism for Clinical Applications

Background

UGT1A1 is a gene involved in glucuronidation of many substances and plays a large role in the metabolism of drugs [1]. Variants within the gene are associated with reported variability in enzymatic activity; therefore, impacting pharmacokinetics and therapeutics, such as anticancer and antiretroviral agents [1, 2]. Genotyping variants of differing dinucleotide TA repeat length within the TATA box of the UGT1A1 region (dbSNP ID rs3064744, A(TA)nTAA) is utilized in dosing of irinotecan for pharmacogenomics purposes [2]. PharmacoScan, a widely used pharmacogenomics assay, fails to elucidate all possible UGT1A1 variants at this loci, including inability to detect UGT1A1*37 with the probeset for the gene due to absence of the *37 allele altogether [3] which can negatively impact UGT1A1 glucuronidation capacity [2, 3]. Several commercial solutions such as Generi Biotech's gb PHARM UGT1A1 kit and Diatech Pharmacogenetics' EasyPGX kit are currently available to address rs3064744 allele determination using quantitative PCR (qPCR) with melt curve analysis. Although these IVD qPCR methods are simple and quick, the UGT1A1 dinucleotide repeat frequently produces PCR stutter, an occurrence in vitro that is not well elucidated by qPCR, as the technique does not deploy accurate base pair sizing and area under the curve (AUC) analysis to correlate probe signal with probe size to prevent false positives [4]. Additionally, neither kit utilizes SuperFi polymerase, which has been shown to produce the lowest stutter rates among most commercially available poymerases [5]. Here, we describe a validated PCR and fragment analysis assay utilizing SuperFi polymerase that distinguishes between four possible star alleles at this position: UGT1A1*36 (TA)5, UGT1A1*1 (TA)6, UGT1A1*28 (TA)7, and UGT1A1*37 (TA)8, as well as potential heterozygous combinations. The development of this new fragment analysis target as a validated CLIA platform offers a determinant for genotype, which when interpreted in conjunction with PharmacoScan can more accurately inform patient treatment [2, 3, 6, 7]. This work was performed in our CLIA (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments)‐certified laboratory (CLIA ID 21D0947274).

Methods

Primers (Invitrogen) and MiniGene plasmids (IDT) used for controls were designed targeting UGT1A1 rs3064744 as shown in Table 1. MiniGene sequence was confirmed by next‐generation sequencing by the manufacturer prior to shipment. A master mix was made consisting of: 1.25 μL 10 μM forward primer, 1.25 μL 10 μM reverse primer, 12.5 μL 2X Platinum SuperFi PCR Master Mix (Invitrogen), 5 μL 5X SuperFi GC Enhancer (Invitrogen), and 5 μL 0.5 ng/μL‐20 ng/μL DNA template (sample or 2 ng/μL respective MiniGene for control wells). Reactions were amplified using an Applied Biosystems ProFlex thermal cycler at 98°C for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 60°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 30 s, then a cycle at 72°C for 7 min followed by a 4°C hold. QC was performed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 DNA 1000 Kit or TapeStation 4200 D1000 Kit.

Fragment analysis was performed with a master mix of 8.5 μL HiDi Formamide (Applied Biosystems) and 0.5 μL LIZ 500 size standard (Applied Biosystems). To this, 1 μL of PCR product, appropriately diluted with molecular biology‐grade water (Lonza) to prevent signal saturation, was added. Denaturation occurred at 95°C for 5 min, then rapid‐cooled on a cold block for 2 min. Reactions were then loaded onto either 3730XL or SeqStudio 24 Flex Genomic Analyzers (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for capillary electrophoresis on a 50 cm array using POP‐7 polymer (Applied Biosystems) and the preset FragmentAnalysis50_POP7 run modules on each respective instrument.

Analysis was performed using the GeneMapper version 5 software (Applied Biosystems). Fragment analysis plots and sizing tables were established from instrument‐generated fsa and ser files and peaks with the largest AUC were identified and associated bp size compared with known bp sizes of the various MiniGene controls. Genotyped fragment sizes were then corresponded to various star allele calls and associated CPIC guidelines on functionality status as shown in Table 2 [4]. Example plot images and corresponding QC display from Bioanalyzer and TapeStation are shown in Figure 1.

Validation and Implementation

Per CLIA regulations, the UGT1A1 fragment analysis assay went through a rigorous and blinded validation experiment. Twenty‐one DNA samples extracted from whole blood were tested for accuracy, precision, reportable range, reference range, analytical specificity, and analytical sensitivity. Accuracy involved running samples with previously known results. Precision testing included within‐run repeatability and inter‐operator reproducibility using replicate samples on the same and different days, respectively. Reportable range was tested for the 5–8 (TA) nucleotide repeats. Reference range was addressed by using genomic DNA extracted from whole blood. Analytical specificity for the assay interrogated only the UGT1A1*1, *28, *36, and *37 alleles. No other genes, alleles, or markers were assessed in this experiment. Regarding analytical sensitivity, the assay displayed a limit of detection down to 0.5 ng/μL. All results matched and achieved 100% concordance.

Following validation, the UGT1A1 fragment analysis assay was implemented for supplemental use with current CLIA PharmacoScan samples within our laboratory to ensure accuracy in UGT1A1 genotype and phenotype call representation for patients within the NIH Clinical Center. Over 900 samples have been tested since clinical implementation (n = 940) with disagreement between UGT1A1 dbSNP ID rs3064744 calls from PharmacoScan and fragment analysis observed in 74 samples (Table 3). The UGT1A1*37 allele that is absent from the PharmacoScan probeset, but detected by fragment analysis, accounted for 26 of the call disagreements, where PharmacoScan yielded “unknown” genotype for 2 cases and a definitive genotype containing probes for *1, *28, and *36 within the calls for 24 cases. This indicates the need for genotype correction due to absence of *37 testing in the PharmacoScan assay which occurs at a frequency of 2.77% within our dataset.

Conclusions

Utilizing evidence supported by our collaborators in Sissung et al. [2], we sought to implement an assay that would result in higher fidelity calling for UGT1A1 variants at the dbSNP ID rs3064744 loci. For this reason, we based our fragment analysis assay around the fluorescence PCR assay previously outlined [2] to implement our design using a custom design and fluorescent forward primer to target dbSNP ID rs3064744. We have also validated this assay by CLIA guidelines, enabling this accurate, rapid, and inexpensive test to be utilized for generating data that can be used as a supplement to other pharmacogenomics testing, such as PharmacoScan, to determine the number of (TA) repeats. Patients can then be dosed/treated according to their specific genotype [6]. Through a dataset of 940 samples processed for both PharmacoScan and the supplemental fragment analysis, 74 samples within the cohort had disagreement between the assays and required use of the supplemental fragment analysis data to supplant or supplement the assigned genotype provided by PharmacoScan, indicating a 7.8% correction rate to PharmacoScan probeset data. This correction rate suggests PharmacoScan alone is insufficient for assigning fully comprehensive UGT1A1 genotypes due to the absence of the *37 allele and should be processed with supplementary testing to provide clarified genotypes at the rs3064744 locus specifically. Through this assay implementation, physicians have become able to more accurately determine dosing and drug response that are pharmacokinetically linked to UGT1A1, allowing for safer application of anticancer and antiviral therapeutics in the clinic.
