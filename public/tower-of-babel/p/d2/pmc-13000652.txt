Thermal Decomposition Mechanism of β‑HMX under Irradiation Damage Based on Molecular Dynamics Simulations
Authors: Rong Liu, Wei-Yi Li, Wan-Xiao Guo, Xi-Yao Yun, Yu-Ling Wang, Wei-Wei Qin, Tao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Energetic materials undergo chemical bond cleavage and structural reorganization under external stimuli such as heat and irradiation, leading to performance degradation or change. This study employs molecular dynamics simulations to investigate the atomic-scale mechanisms of irradiation-induced damage in β-HMX and its subsequent thermal decomposition mechanism. Results revealed that irradiation defects are primarily localized within collision cascade regions. Irradiation-generated nitrogen oxides and HNO x radicals provide new reaction sites during HMX thermal decomposition, significantly altering pyrolysis pathways and increasing the activation energy. Concurrently, irradiation-induced defects restructure the reaction network, modifying the propagation efficiency of radical chain reactions. This work provides atomic-level insights into the microscopic mechanism through which irradiation influences the thermal decomposition of HMX, offering a theoretical foundation for understanding the chemical evolution and stability of energetic materials under extreme conditions.

Introduction

1,3,5,7-Tetranitro-1,3,5,7-tetraazacyclooctane (HMX) is a typical monomolecular energetic material (EM). Owing to its superior detonation performance, it has been widely applied in both military and civilian fields, such as solid propellants and high-power missile warheads. HMX exists in four polymorphs, i.e., α-, β-, γ-, and δ-phases. Among these, β-HMX is the most stable under ambient temperature and pressure, making it the polymorph commonly used in practical engineering. In specialized environments such as the exoatmospheric space and nuclear-related settings, energetic materials (EMs) are subjected to the action of high-energy ionizing radiation. High-energy particles transfer their energy to target atoms within EMs. If the energy acquired by a target atom exceeds a specific threshold (referred to as the displacement threshold energy), the atom will break its chemical bonds, depart from its original lattice site, and become a self-interstitial atom (SIA). Molecules that dissociate from the main structure due to bond cleavage are defined as interstitial fragment molecule (IFM), while a vacancy (Vac) is formed at the original lattice position of the displaced atom. The atom initially displaced is termed the primary knock-on atom (PKA). During the subsequent movement of the PKA, if it collides with other atoms, the recoiled atoms generated from these collisions will further interact with surrounding atoms, thereby triggering a collision cascade. This cascade process ultimately results in extensive molecular bond cleavage events within the EMs. The damage to energetic materials (EMs) induced by such radiation is far more severe than that caused by conventional thermal effects. Particularly under high-dose irradiation conditions, EMs undergo discoloration, accelerated decomposition, and the formation of structural defects such as internal pores and voids. , Concurrently, this process triggers chemical decomposition and free radical generation, leading to significant changes in explosive properties. These alterations ultimately jeopardize the safety and reliability of EMs in practical applications.

Accordingly, an in-depth investigation into the radiation effects of energetic materials (EMs) in irradiated environments is of paramount importance. A variety of typical energetic materials (EMs) have been experimentally tested under different irradiation conditions. For instance, the Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) conducted γ-irradiation experiments on HMX, 1,3,5-trinitro-1,3,5-triazinane (RDX), 2,4,6-trinitrotoluene (TNT), and pentaerythritol tetranitrate (PETN). These studies revealed that HMX and RDX release small amounts of gas under low-dose γ-irradiation. As the irradiation dose increases, HMX, TNT, RDX, and PETN all exhibit gas release, color changes, and increased impact sensitivity. , Urizar irradiated HMX, RDX, TNT, and PETN with fast neutrons, which resulted in the formation of numerous small voids within these explosives and the release of gases such as H2, N2, N2O, NO, CO, CO2, CH2O, and H2O during the irradiation process. Property tests on the irradiated explosives indicated an increase in gas release rate; however, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Miles et al. detected NO2 radicals in γ-irradiated HMX, RDX, and PETN using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), hypothesizing that N–N bond cleavage had occurred. Furthermore, at least two types of radicals (NO2 and NO) accumulate under irradiation, leading to the generation of internal defects, which may account for the changes in the reaction mechanism of explosives after irradiation. Luo conducted irradiation experiments, which showed that pentaerythritol tetranitrate (PETN) exhibited discoloration, released a large amount of gas, and had a delayed decomposition temperature after irradiation. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results confirmed the occurrence of denitration reactions, verifying that the NO2 radicals observed by Miles were a product of these denitration reactions. Furthermore, Zhang et al. directly irradiated 1,3,5-trinitro-1,3,5-triazinane (RDX) using high-flux synchrotron radiation X-rays. Evaluations via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and XPS revealed that the regulatory mechanism of radiation on the explosive primarily involved denitration reactionsthese reactions induced internal defects in the explosive, leading to reductions in detonation velocity, detonation pressure, and electrostatic sensitivity. Huestis et al. replaced dodecane with energetic functional groups to investigate the patterns of irradiation-induced molecular damage. The results indicated that high-energy particles primarily caused damage to molecules at the energetic functional groups, which is consistent with previous experimental results. ,, This further confirms the irradiation sensitivity of nitro groups in explosives.

Although denitration reactions in irradiated explosives are now widely recognized, experimental limitations at small scales hinder a clear understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving performance changes in EMs after irradiation. For example, the causes of gas release and variations in decomposition temperature remain unclear, which restricts the application of explosives in irradiated environments. Computer simulation techniques, however, can provide detailed and accurate information at the atomic and molecular scales, offering an effective approach to explore the properties of materials under extreme conditions. − Feng et al. investigated the neutron irradiation effects on RDX using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, revealing that neutron irradiation has little impact on the types of RDX thermal decomposition products but affects their quantities and reaction rates. Li et al. simulated the displacement cascade process of PKA in HMX via MD methods. The results indicated that bond cleavage, radical generation, and cluster defects occur after displacement cascades, and the mechanism underlying the degradation of mechanical properties in irradiated explosives was successfully explained through stress distribution analysis. Yun et al. explored the influence of irradiation on RDX decomposition products using MD simulations, finding that the formation of internal pores is attributed to the aggregation of small molecules.

Despite progress in understanding irradiation-induced microstructural damage in explosives, the radicals generated by irradiation trigger complex chemical processes. These radicals react with the surrounding system and ultimately affect the decomposition temperature. Currently, the impact of irradiation on the thermal decomposition mechanism of explosives remains unclear, particularly the influencing mechanism on decomposition reaction pathways. Therefore, this study employs reactive molecular dynamics (RMD) simulations to simulate the damage process of HMX under irradiation. Additionally, thermal decomposition simulations of irradiated HMX crystal structures are performed to investigate the effects of irradiation-induced microstructural damage on the thermal decomposition mechanism of HMX.

Methodology and Computational Details

In this work, β-HMX (space group: P21/c; lattice parameters: a = 6.54 Å, b = 11.05 Å, c = 8.7 Å, α = 90°, β = 124.4°, γ = 90°) was selected for simulations. An 8 × 6 × 8 supercell was constructed, containing a total of 768 molecules, as shown in Figure . MD irradiation simulations were carried out using the LAMMPS package, and the ReaxFF-lg/ZBL potential was utilized to model the irradiation reaction process. ReaxFF refers to the Reactive Force Field. On the basis of ReaxFF, ReaxFF-lg/ZBL incorporates long-range London dispersion forces and the Ziegler-Biersack-Littmark (ZBL) short-range repulsive potential. This force field has been validated for simulating radiation damage and thermal decomposition processes in energetic materials, including HMX. after modification for short-range interactions, this potential can effectively describe the reaction kinetics of EMs under high-energy particle impact. Initially, the Nose-Hoover method was used to relax the HMX supercell in the NVT ensemble at 300 K for 40 ps with a time step of 0.1 fs. Subsequently, the P-R pressure control method was applied for 60 ps of relaxation in the NPT ensemble at 1 atm and 300 K, until the system reached thermodynamic equilibrium. Previous work has shown that under 0.1 MeV neutron irradiation, nitrogen (N) atoms account for the largest proportion of primary knock-on atoms (PKAs) that induce effective damage inside HMX, with their equivalent energy ranging from 0 to 5.18 keV. Therefore, as shown in Figure , a nitrogen (N) atom was randomly selected at the boundary of the simulation cell and designated as the primary knock-on atom (PKA), which was assigned an initial kinetic energy (E PKA) along the body diagonal direction of the model. The simulations were performed with E PKA values of 0.43, 1.13, 2.75, 3.36, and 4.88 keV to initiate irradiation-induced cascades in the HMX matrix. The cascade region was time-integrated under the NVE ensemble, with a 2 Å-thick dissipative layer surrounding the model to allow energy dissipation, thereby eliminating spurious stress waves reflected from periodic boundaries. Due to the extremely high velocity of high-energy PKAs, the time step during the collision cascade process was adaptively adjusted between 10–5 and 0.1 fs. After the completion of cascade annealing, the time step was restored to 0.1 fs for a 20 ps NPT relaxation at 300 K. Following irradiation, thermal decomposition simulations of the structure models containing irradiation-induced defects were performed at 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 2500, and 3000 K, respectively. A heating rate of 500 K/ps was employed during the temperature ramp, and each target temperature was maintained for 30 ps. This temperature range is widely adopted to reasonably accelerate the reaction process, thereby enabling the observation of decomposition events within the time scale of MD simulations. − These simulations were conducted under the NVT ensemble, with products sampled every 50 fs based on the bond orders specified in Table . To reduce random errors, five parallel simulations were performed for the thermal decomposition process at each temperature, and the results were statistically averaged. Finally, the visualization tool OVITO and HlightReaxMD analysis software were used to analyze the irradiation decomposition mechanism, reaction kinetic parameters, and reaction pathways of HMX.

Results and Discussion

Decomposition Process Induced by Irradiation in the β-HMX

Simulation results show that after the primary knock-on atom (PKA) enters the HMX model system, it initiates collision cascades inside HMX and transfers energy. Due to the limitation of the simulation box volume, PKAs with energy higher than 1.13 keV may move beyond the boundaries of the simulation box during motion; thus, PKA atoms escaping the box were removed to prevent secondary incidence caused by periodic boundaries. Since the entire system was under the NVE ensemble, the energy difference between the PKA atom at incidence and at rest (or escape) was calculated to characterize the energy deposited in the system. The deposited energy represents the amount of energy transferred by the PKA to the molecules in the entire box. When the PKA energies were 0.43, 1.13, 2.75, 3.36, and 4.88 keV, the deposited energies were 429, 634, 1167, 1028, and 1915 eV, respectively. Figure presents the changes in the number of HMX molecules and major products during the irradiation cascade process. Figure a–e shows the quantities of corresponding products during the irradiation cascade at deposited energies of 429, 634, 1167, 1028, and 1915 eV, respectively, while Figure f illustrates the variation in the number of HMX molecules under different deposited energies. Under PKA impact, HMX decomposed and generated various small molecules, including NO2, NO, HNO2, HNO3, HCN, H2CN, CO, CH2O, O2, CH2N2O2, N2, and H2O. After 20 ps of NPT relaxation, the main stable products were NO2, O2, CH2N2O2, N2, and H2O. Notably, due to the different penetration effects of PKAs with varying energies, the damage degree of HMX molecules decreases with the increase in PKA implantation depth. No further HMX decomposition occurs once the collision cascade stops. The decomposition rate was analyzed by calculating the ratio of decomposed HMX molecules to the initial total number of HMX molecules in a limited volume (200 nm3) of the simulation. The HMX decomposition rates corresponding to different deposited energies were 3.78, 6.64, 18.46, 14.10, and 20.80%, respectively, indicating a positive correlation between the HMX decomposition rate and deposited energy. The quantities of small-molecule products (NO2, CH2N2O2, N2, and H2O) also show a positive correlation with deposited energy, as higher deposited energy increases the probability of HMX decomposition. For stable products such as N2 and H2O, they were only generated when the PKA energy exceeded 0.43 keV. This suggests that low-energy irradiation only causes the cleavage of HMX functional groups (e.g., −NO2) rather than complete decomposition of HMX, which is consistent with the conclusions of experimental studies.

Taking the simulation results of PKA with 2.75 keV (deposited energy: 1167 eV) as an example, the further analysis results of chemical bond evolution are shown in Figure . In terms of the degree of chemical bond cleavage, fractures occurred in N–N (2.24%), N–O (1.07%), C–N (1.33%), and C–H (0.85%) bonds. Among these, the N–N bond exhibited the most severe cleavage with a degree of 2.24%, indicating that the irradiation-induced damage to chemical bonds is mainly concentrated on the N–N bond, i.e., the N–NO2 functional group. Meanwhile, the number of N–O bonds first decreased and then increased, whereas the numbers of other bonds did not increase after initial reduction. Additionally, free radicals generated by N–O bond cleavage evolved into product molecules such as NO and NO2, which confirms that the irradiation-induced cleavage of HMX is irreversible. Approximately 95% of the displaced atoms have a kinetic energy less than 5% of the PKA energy. Oxygen (O) atoms are the most abundant displaced atoms, followed by hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N) and carbon (C) atoms, with the kinetic energy of all displaced C atoms being less than 200 eV. Among the displaced atoms with high energy (>5% E PKA), only H and N atoms are observed, while no C atoms are detected. The volume of the irradiation-damaged region was calculated by measuring the polyhedral volume enclosed by the mean free paths of displaced atoms, resulting in a damaged volume of 78.882 nm3, which accounts for 39.24% of the total volume of the simulation system (201.02 nm3). Both displaced atoms and defects are distributed near the PKA’s trajectory, indicating that the damage region induced by a single PKA in HMX is on the nanoscale, and the damage is confined to the irradiated area of the material.

The decomposition process of HMX under irradiation follows a three-stage mechanism: from bond cleavage to free radical initiation, and finally to product formation. As shown in the product distribution in Figure , the number of NO2 radicals exhibits the most significant variation. This is because the N–NO2 bond is the weakest chemical bond in HMX, making it more prone to cleavage during displacement cascades, thereby generating primary nitrogen oxide fragments such as NO2 and NO. Analysis of the reaction pathways during irradiation reveals that the primary pathway for NO2 radical generation is C4H8N8O8 → NO2 + C4H8N7O6. In secondary reactions, NO2 is largely consumed to form secondary products like NO and HO, while the amount of O2 decreases rapidly after reaching its maximum. The cleavage of N–O bonds releases O radicals, which are gradually consumed with a concurrent increase in O2. Subsequently, NO2 and O2 further react (NO2 + O2 → NO4), leading to additional consumption of NO2. The number of HO radicals during irradiation first increases and then decreases. The main pathway for HO radical generation involves H radicals (after kinetic energy attenuation) attacking oxygen atoms in the nitro groups of HMX, forming the unstable intermediate C4H9N8O8. This intermediate then undergoes deoxygenation to produce HO, following the reactions: H + C4H8N8O8 → C4H9N8O8 and C4H9N8O8 → C4H8N8O7 + HO. Finally, HO radicals participate in chain termination reactions with NO and NO2: NO + HO → HNO2 and NO2 + HO → HNO3. A small amount of CO2 is produced during irradiation, with its reaction pathway as follows: irradiation induces the ring-opening of HMX to form the linear intermediate CH2N2O2, which then further reacts to generate CO2. Effect of Irradiation Damage on Thermal Decomposition of β-HMX

Both the perfect supercell model and the models containing irradiation-induced defects were subjected to temperature-ramped decomposition to investigate the effect of irradiation defects on the thermal decomposition behavior of HMX. The variation in the number of HMX molecules with time during thermal decomposition follows the relationship shown as N(t)=N0·exp[−k(t−t0)] 1 where N 0 denotes the initial number of HMX molecules, t 0 is the time at which HMX molecules start to decompose, and k represents the decomposition rate constant. The time-dependent variation in the quantity of products follows an exponential relationship: N(t)=N∞·{1−exp[−kf(t−tf)]} 2 where N ∞ is the final equilibrium quantity of product molecules, t f is the time at which product molecules start to form, and k f is the formation rate constant of products. The kinetic process of HMX decomposition was analyzed using the Arrhenius equation, which correlates the reaction rate constant with the activation energy: lnk=lnA−(EaR)1T 3 where A is the pre-exponential factor, R is the molar gas constant, and E a is the activation energy. The time point at which the ratio of the changed quantity of products to their initial quantity exceeds 5% was defined as the reaction onset time, while the reaction termination time was defined when this ratio drops below 5% or the product quantity reaches zero (No decomposition occurred at 500 K, and the reaction rate was considered to be 0). For the perfect HMX lattice model (prior to irradiation), the calculated initial thermal decomposition activation energy of C4H8N8O8 was 109.257 kJ/mol. Compared with the experimental isothermal decomposition value of 114.150 kJ/mol, the deviation is 4.29%. This experimental method allows for a more accurate assessment of the intrinsic decomposition kinetics of HMX than traditional Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) and Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA). After irradiation, the activation energy of HMX increased to 133.944 kJ/mol (a 22.60% increment), accompanied by a 4.38% decrease in reaction rate and a 12.07% extension in decomposition duration. Among the products, CO2 and N2 were most significantly affected: their formation energy barriers increased by 61.91 and 11.05%, respectively, while their reaction rates decreased by 48.19 and 9.37%, leading to a delay in the appearance of these products. In contrast, H2O was least impacted, with only a 2.52% increase in activation energy and a 5% reduction in reaction rate. The elevation of HMX’s initial decomposition activation energy resulted in increases in both decomposition temperature and decomposition duration. This finding explains the results of our previous experimental study, where the thermal decomposition temperature of irradiated explosives slightly increased and the decomposition peak shifted toward the higher temperature region. It is noteworthy that the difference in reaction rates gradually diminishes with increasing temperature, indicating that irradiation-induced defects raise the energy barrier for HMX thermal decomposition, but this inhibitory effect can be gradually mitigated by temperature elevation.

To further unravel the mechanism by which irradiation-induced defects affect the decomposition activation energy and reaction rate of HMX, an in-depth analysis of reaction products and pathways was conducted. As illustrated in Figure , distinct from conventional thermal decomposition behavior, irradiation first induces the generation of numerous fragmented products in HMX, such as NO2, HNO x , CH2N2O2, CH2N, CHN, O2, CHN, CH2O, NO3, and H2. These fragments exist as free radicals within the system and participate in secondary reactions during the subsequent thermal decomposition process.

Initial species distribution of the perfect HMX model and irradiated model.

In this work, we define the difference in product quantity between the irradiated model and the perfect supercell model as Q = N irradiated – N perfect. By analyzing the variation trend of Q, the sensitive species in the model during the pyrolysis reaction can be identified. Figure presents three distinct variation trends of Q: Figure a shows a trend of gradual increase in Q; Figure b exhibits a trend of gradual decrease in Q; and Figure c displays an oscillating trend of Q. The species corresponding to these three trends are as follows: (a) H2, N2, CH2O, H2O; (b) CH2N2O2, CH2N; (c) NO, NO2, NO3, HNO3, CHN, HO. This indicates that in the same thermal decomposition environment, compared with the perfect crystal lattice, irradiation-induced defects promote the formation of products such as H2, N2, CH2O, and H2O, while inhibiting the generation of CH2N2O2 and CH2N. Radicals including NO, NO2, NO3, HNO3, CHN, and HO participate in intermediate reactions. Figure d shows the oscillation amplitude and cycle lifetime of the radicals in Figure c. It reveals that NO2 exhibits the largest quantity difference among radicals after irradiation, while HNO3 shows the most significant difference in reaction cycleundergoing oscillation approximately every 3.3 ps. This suggests that the key intermediates with differences between the irradiated and nonirradiated models during thermal decomposition are primarily NO2 and HNO3.

The reaction pathways of sensitive species were further analyzed using the “Reaction Analysis” function of the HlightReaxMD software. The reaction pathway networks for each sensitive species are provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S1–S12). The results indicate that irradiation-induced defects reconstruct the pyrolysis reaction network of HMX. Below are the results for species most significantly affected, which will be presented in the main text: Figure a,b illustrates the N2 generation pathways in the perfect model and the irradiated model, respectively. In the perfect model, HMX undergoes initial decomposition to produce NO2, followed by a pathway dominated by HNO3/HNO2, which ultimately leads to N2 formation. In contrast, irradiation pregenerates NO2 radicals, thereby opening a new pathway in the irradiated modeldistinct from the perfect onethat proceeds via N2O4 and NO intermediates. This new pathway not only provides additional routes for N2 generation but also increases the number of reaction steps required for N2 formation. Figure c,d shows the H2 generation pathways in the perfect and irradiated models. The irradiated model contains preformed HNO3 (a species that typically requires multiple reaction steps to generate in the perfect model), which enables a new H2 formation channel. This channel proceeds through a chain reaction: NO2 → HNO3 → HNO2 → NO → HNO → H → H2. Notably, this pathway is absent in the perfect model, indicating that the pregenerated HNO x radicals (from irradiation) provide new reaction channels for H2 formation. For the CH2O product, the increase in NO2 radicals induced by irradiation similarly promotes its formation via the reaction sequence: NO2 + CH2NO2 → NO + CH2NO3 → NO + NO2 + CH2O.

Figure e,f depicts the CH2N2O2 generation pathways in the perfect model and the irradiated model, respectively. In the perfect model, CH2N2O2 is primarily generated through two pathways: active ring-opening cleavage and passive ring-opening cleavage following attack by NO x radicals. However, in the irradiated model, active ring-opening cleavage leading to CH2N2O2 is nearly eliminated. Further analysis of its consumption pathways revealed that NO2, NO3, and HNO3 radicalsacting as key intermediatesparticipate in the consumption of CH2N2O2 and CH2N via the following reactions: (a) CH2N2O2 → CH2N + NO2; (b) NO2 + NO3 + HNO3 + 3CH2N → NO2 + HNO3 + 3CHN + HNO2 + H2O. These reactions constitute the primary consumption channels for CH2N2O2 and CH2N in the early stage of the reaction. In the perfect model, CH2N2O2 is mainly produced by the ring-opening decomposition of C4H8N7O6 (or C4H8N6O4) via C–N bond cleavage after nitro group removal. In contrast, the irradiated model contains extensive NO2 and HNO x radicals generated internally. These radicals preferentially undergo the reaction NO2 + HNO x → NO + HNO(x+1), which consumes NO2 radicals. Subsequent denitration reactions occur, and the released NO2 radicals further react with adjacent NO2, N2O3, and N2O4 before ring-opening reactions take place. To determine the minimum energy pathways (MEPs) of these two reactions, the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method implemented in LAMMPS was employed. The classical potential energy barrier (ΔE‡) was calculated as the difference in force field potential energy between the transition state and the initial state. The potential energy surfaces (PES) of the two reaction pathways are shown in Figure . All kinetics and NEB-based barriers presented herein are estimates at the force field level. Results indicate that the reaction NO2 + HNO x → NO + HNO(x+1) has an extremely low energy barrier of only 17.91 kJ/mol, meaning HMX preferentially undergoes this reaction. Consequently, the energy barrier for further cleavage to generate CH2N2O2 increases from 168.36 to 207.02 kJ/mol. It is worth noting that due to accuracy limitations associated with empirical potentials, the activation energy barriers calculated via ReaxFF-MD cannot quantitatively represent the true intrinsic barriers. Nevertheless, ReaxFF is capable of qualitatively revealing reaction directions and has been extensively employed in numerous reaction mechanism studies. − On the other hand, the accurate prediction of the initial decomposition activation energy for a perfect HMX crystal by ReaxFF suggests that the trends identified based on these calculations remain reliable. Furthermore, the kinetic calculations based on ReaxFF are sensitive to the initial number of free radicals, as shown in Figure S13 of the Supporting Information. A lower radical count results in a less pronounced shift in activation energy, resembling the response of perfect HMX, whereas a higher radical count leads to significant changes in activation energy, all of which are higher than that of perfect HMX. Importantly, the alteration in reaction propensity caused by pre-existing NO x /HNO x species is corroborated by the NEB calculations in this work; although the absolute barrier values may not represent the true energies, they qualitatively reflect the observed trends.

Statistical analysis shows that the probability of C–N bond cleavage (for CH2N2O2 generation) in the irradiated model is only 6.25% of that in the perfect model. The inhibited formation of CH2N2O2 further affects the subsequent generation of CO2. This finding demonstrates that the increase in activation energy is not caused by irradiation-induced defects directly elevating the energy barrier for HMX decomposition, but rather by the presence of radicals altering reaction pathway selection. This alteration hinders the normal C–N bond cleavage of HMX, thereby impeding the second-step ring-opening reaction in HMX thermal decomposition and prolonging the decomposition duration. Macroscopically, this manifests as a shift of the HMX decomposition peak toward the higher temperature region. It should be noted that the pathways for generating H2O, CH2N, NO, NO x , HNO x , and HO during HMX pyrolysis do not change significantly before and after irradiation. The initial increase in H2O quantity is attributed to variations in the probability of other side reactions.

In conclusion, the nitrogen oxide and HNO x radicals generated by irradiation provide new reaction sites during the thermal decomposition of HMX. These species induce a low-barrier redox reaction that significantly influences pyrolysis pathway selection, thereby delaying the ring-opening reaction. This atomic-scale mechanism ex plains the experimentally observed shift of the decomposition peak toward higher temperatures. It is noteworthy, however, that the increased decomposition temperature does not stem from the intrinsic strengthening of chemical bonds, but rather from the modulation of reaction pathways by defects. Furthermore, the decomposition caused by irradiation itself reduces the number of HMX molecules compared to the perfect model, and the hindered CO2 formation suggests potential effects on detonation velocity and pressure. Our pathway analysis further reveals that while irradiation defects delay initial decomposition, they also restructure the reaction network, opening new pathways for H2 generation and altering the propagation efficiency of free radical chain reactions. This implies that irradiation damage may affect not only the decomposition temperature but also the explosive performance.

Conclusions

To explore the thermal decomposition mechanism and irradiation decomposition mechanism of perfect HMX and irradiated HMX, β-HMX was selected as the research object. Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to conduct irradiation cascade collision simulations on HMX, and the irradiation decomposition behavior of HMX as well as its thermal decomposition properties before and after irradiation were analyzed. First, after irradiation, the main free radicals of HMX are NO x and HNO x . During thermal decomposition, NO x is consumed rapidly, while HNO x has a longer lifespan. Second, irradiation leads to the generation of several important intermediates inside HMX. These intermediates provide new reaction sites during thermal decomposition, induce a redox reaction with a lower energy barrier to consume NO2, significantly affect the pathway selection of HMX pyrolysis, and increase the decomposition activation energy of HMX. Finally, while delaying the initial decomposition, irradiation-induced defects also reconstruct the reaction network and change the transfer efficiency of free radical chain reactions. The study shows that, on the one hand, irradiation defects raise the threshold for thermal initiation of HMX; on the other hand, they may alter the subsequent reaction process of HMX detonation. This paper conducts an in-depth study on the properties and reaction mechanisms of HMX before and after irradiation, which can provide strong theoretical support for the design and optimization of energetic materials.
