Automated biventricular quantification in patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot using a three-dimensional deep learning segmentation model
Authors: Sofie Tilborghs, Tiffany Liang, Stavroula Raptis, Ayako Ishikita, Werner Budts, Tom Dresselaers, Jan Bogaert, Frederik Maes, Rachel M. Wald, Alexander Van De Bruaene
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeep learning is the state-of-the-art approach for automated segmentation of the left ventricle (LV) and right ventricle (RV) in cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) images. However, these models have been mostly trained and validated using CMR datasets of structurally normal hearts or cases with acquired cardiac disease, and are therefore not well-suited to handle cases with congenital cardiac disease such as tetralogy of Fallot (TOF). We aimed to develop and validate a dedicated model with improved performance for LV and RV cavity and myocardium quantification in patients with repaired TOF.MethodsWe trained a three-dimensional (3D) convolutional neural network (CNN) with 5-fold cross-validation using manually delineated end-diastolic (ED) and end-systolic (ES) short-axis image stacks obtained from either a public dataset containing patients with no or acquired cardiac pathology (n = 100), an institutional dataset of TOF patients (n = 96), or both datasets mixed. Our method allows for missing labels in the training images to accommodate for different ED and ES phases for LV and RV as is commonly the case in TOF. The best performing model was applied to all frames of a separate test set of TOF cases (n = 36) and ED and ES phases were automatically determined for LV and RV separately. The model was evaluated against the performance of a commercial software (suiteHEART®, NeoSoft, Pewaukee, Wisconsin, US).ResultsTraining on the mixture of both datasets yielded the best agreement with the manual ground truth for the TOF cases, achieving a median Dice similarity coefficient of (93.8%, 89.8%) for LV cavity and of (92.9%, 90.9%) for RV cavity at (ED, ES) respectively, and of 80.9% and 61.8% for LV and RV myocardium at ED. The offset in automated ED and ES frame selection was 0.56 and 0.89 frames on average for LV and RV respectively. No statistically significant differences were found between our model and the commercial software for LV quantification (two-sided Wilcoxon signed rank test, p<5%), while RV quantification was significantly improved with our model achieving a mean absolute error of 12 ml for RV cavity compared to 36 ml for the commercial software.ConclusionWe developed and validated a fully automatic segmentation and quantification approach for LV and RV, including RV mass, in patients with repaired TOF. Compared to a commercial software, our approach is superior for RV quantification indicating its potential in clinical practice.

Background

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is the most common cyanotic congenital heart defect. Although long-term outcome is good, adult patients with repaired TOF may still have residual right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) obstruction and dysfunction along with pulmonic valve regurgitation (PR) or stenosis (PS). Serial assessment using cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) of right ventricle (RV) end-diastolic volume (EDV), RV end-systolic volume (ESV) and RV ejection fraction (EF) is important to guide management and is therefore implemented in current guidelines [1], [2], [3]. Since manual delineation of the endo- and epicardium of both the left ventricle (LV) and RV in multiple image slices at multiple cardiac phases is time-consuming and prone to inter- and intra-observer variability, automated segmentation using deep learning based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) has become the state-of-the-art approach for LV and RV segmentation [4]. However, currently such approaches have been mainly developed for normally structured hearts with no or acquired cardiac disease (e.g., dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy) and are usually less accurate for cases with congenital cardiac disease such as TOF [5].

While several works dedicated to automated segmentation of CMR images of TOF patients use a two-dimensional (2D) CNN to segment each slice individually [6], [7], [8] or only segment the RV endocardium and not epicardium [6], [7], [9], our approach uses a 3D CNN that performs multiclass segmentation of LV and RV cavity and myocardium on all slices simultaneously, thereby exploiting 3D spatial relationships between different slices and anatomical structures. To quantify EDV, ESV and EF for LV and RV, the segmentation is performed consecutively for all time frames and ED and ES phases are subsequently automatically detected for LV and RV separately since their timing may differ in TOF patients due to differences in afterload and conduction (i.e., the presence of a right bundle branch block). Our approach for training of the CNN takes this into account by allowing that the ED and ES contours of LV and RV used for training can be in different frames, in contrast to Tandon et al. [8] for instance. The benefit thereof is that our method can be trained directly on TOF and non-TOF cases in which the clinically relevant ED and ES contours for LV and RV have been delineated in the same or different frames, without the need for additional contouring. In this paper, we investigate the performance of our method on repaired TOF cases in terms of segmentation quality and derived functional parameters for different training setups of the CNN, training it either on a standard CMR dataset of non-TOF cases, a dataset with only TOF cases, or a mixture of both. In addition, we compare the performance of our approach with a commercial software tool.

Methods

Datasets

Two CMR datasets were used in our experiments for training and evaluation of the CNN: a public dataset of non-TOF cases and an institutional dataset of repaired TOF cases.

The non-TOF dataset was the ACDC (Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge) training dataset [10] containing CMR scans of 100 subjects, acquired with two Siemens MRI scanners with field strengths of either 1.5 T or a 3 T, of which 20 normal cases, 20 cases with dilated cardiomyopathy, 20 cases with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, 20 cases with previous myocardial infarction, and 20 cases with RV dysfunction. The cine short axis (SA) image stacks consist of 6 to 18 slices (median = 9 slices) and 12 to 35 frames (median = 30 frames), with pixel size between 0.72 to 1.92 mm and slice spacing between 5 to 10 mm. Manually delineated contours of the LV and RV cavity and the LV myocardium are provided with this dataset for the selected ED and ES frames, which for all cases were identical for LV and RV.

The TOF dataset was retrieved from the CORRELATE (Comprehensive Outcomes Registry Late After Tetralogy of Fallot Repair) registry, an international prospective multi-center cohort of children and adults after TOF repair [11], [12]. A subset of 150 baseline CMR scans of adult subjects followed at the Peter Munk Cardiac Center (Toronto, Canada) was randomly selected from this registry, all acquired with the same 1.5 T Siemens MRI scanner. The cine SA image stacks consisted of 10 to 18 slices (median = 13 slices) and 25 frames, with pixel size between 0.94 to 1.88 mm and slice spacing between 7.2 to 10 mm. The LV and RV cavity were manually delineated in the ED and ES frames by a single expert using Medis Suite MR (Medis Medical Imaging Systems, Leiden, Netherlands), while the LV and RV myocardium were delineated in ED only [12], [13]. The selected ED and ES frames were not always identical for LV and RV: LV and RV contours were drawn in a different frame for 25 subjects in ED and for 104 subjects in ES.

In addition, a separate dataset consisting of 8 CMR scans of adult TOF cases was used for external validation. These cases were also retrieved from the CORRELATE database, but were acquired in a different center (UZ Leuven, Leuven, Belgium) and with a different type of scanner (Ingenia, Philips Healthcare, The Netherlands), but analyzed by the same observer.

Preprocessing

To serve as input for the CNN, the cine SA image stacks were resampled using linear interpolation to the same voxel size of 1.5 × 1.5 × 3 mm3 and image size of 192 × 192 × 48, with the original number of frames. The resampled image intensities were cropped at 1% and 99% before linearly rescaling them to zero mean and unit variance (i.e., z-normalization). To create the corresponding ground truth segmentation maps, the contours for LV cavity, LV myocardium, RV cavity and RV myocardium as delineated in the original SA image stack were first converted into signed distance maps for each structure separately and for the background as well. These maps were subsequently resampled to the same voxel grid as the resampled SA image stack and processed by a sigmoid function to convert them into probability maps. A 5-class segmentation map was obtained by assigning each voxel the label of the structure with the highest probability in that voxel. In this way, struc tures that were not delineated in the images (i.e., missing labels) were assigned the background label. CNN architecture and training

We used a 3D CNN with input size 192 × 192 × 48 to segment all slices in the 3D SA image stack at once for each frame in turn. The architecture of the CNN was the same for all experiments and is based upon U-Net [14]. The network consists of 4 consecutive max pooling layers, each decreasing the image resolution by half, followed by 4 upsampling layers to recover the original resolution. Due to the lower size of the input images in the third dimension, this size is not altered in the first max pooling layer. Before, after and between every max pooling and upsampling layer are 2 convolutional layers using same padding with (3,3,3) and (3,3,1) kernels, respectively. Initially, 16 feature maps are created and this number is doubled every time the resolution is halved, resulting in 256 feature maps at the lowest resolution. Every convolutional layer is followed by batch normalization and a parametric rectified linear unit (PReLU) activation function. Feature maps of the max pooling network branch are connected to feature maps of the upsampling branch with the same resolution through skip connections. Finally, a convolutional layer with a (1,1,1) kernel, 5 feature maps and a softmax activation function is used to obtain the output, which are 5 probability maps of size 192 × 192 × 48 for background, LV cavity, LV myocardium, RV cavity and RV myocardium respectively.

The marginal Dice loss [15] was used as loss function for training the 3D CNN, as this was specifically developed to deal with missing labels in only partially labeled training images. The Adam optimizer was used with a batch size of 2 and a learning rate scheduler which gradually decreased the initial learning rate from 2 × 10−3 to 1 × 10−4 over 500 epochs. During training, online data augmentation was applied, which consisted of adding random Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.1 and affine transformation by a random rotation around the z-axis and a random scaling, uniformly sampled from the range [−2,+ 2] radians and [0.95,1.05] respectively. The images were resampled using linear interpolation, while for the label maps nearest neighbor interpolation was used.

Postprocessing

The probability maps for each of the 5 structures as generated by the 3D CNN were first resampled using linear interpolation to the original resolution of the SA image stack and a 5-class segmentation was subsequently obtained by assigning each voxel the label of the structure with the highest probability. The segmentation was performed for each frame in turn to obtain a fully segmented cine SA stack. The volume of each structure for a given frame was computed by multiplying its number of voxels with the voxel size of the image. To obtain the myocardial mass (MM), the myocardial volume was multiplied with 1.05 g/ml, the assumed density of heart muscle [16]. The ED and ES frames were either determined based on the information provided with each dataset, or automatically selected for LV and RV separately based on their maximal and minimal cavity volumes respectively. EF of LV and RV was obtained as EF = (EDV − ESV)/EDV ∗ 100.

Validation

The 3D CNN was implemented, trained and tested using DeepVoxNet2 [17] which is a high-level CNN framework build on top of Tensorflow2 (Python). Pre- and post- processing, validation and statistical analysis were performed in Matlab® (MathWorks, Natick, Massachusetts).

We used a 5-fold cross validation approach, whereby the training dataset was split into 5 equally sized folds and 5 different models were trained, using each fold in turn for validation and the remaining 4 folds for training. When evaluating on a separate test set, an ensembled model was used, i.e., each of the 5 individually trained models was applied on the same test case and the final output for this case was obtained by averaging the 5 outputs (i.e., their probability maps).

We validated the segmentation performance using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) and the mean absolute surface distance (MASD) [18]. The DSC calculates the percent of volume overlap between the ground truth and the automated segmentations, while the MASD calculates the average distance between their surfaces. DSC and MASD were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). In addition, we evaluated the differences between the quantified and ground truth values for EDV, ESV, EF and MM of both LV and RV. Mean absolute error and Pearson’s correlation coefficient between quantified and ground truth values were reported. The error in the automated selection of the ED and ES frames was quantified by the absolute difference in frame numbers with the manually selected ground truth, taking the periodicity of the cardiac cycle into account. The statistical significance of the results was assessed using the two-sided Wilcoxon signed rank test and the level of statistical significance was set at 0.05.

Experiments

Benefit of using TOF versus non-TOF training cases

In a first experiment, we assessed the impact of the nature of the training data on the performance of the 3D CNN for LV and RV segmentation in TOF patients. To this end, 3 different models with identical architecture were trained using the same 5-fold cross validation procedure but using different training datasets consisting of either non-TOF cases, TOF cases or a mixture of both (see Fig. 2, Exp1).

The non-TOF dataset (n = 100 subjects) was split into 5 equal folds (n = 20), with each fold containing an equal number of subjects from each of the 5 disease types. The TOF dataset (n = 150) was randomly split into a training set (n = 100) and a separate test set (n = 50). Due to missing data or an unreadable data format, 4 and 14 cases had to be removed from the TOF training and test set, respectively. The TOF training set (n = 96) was further randomly split into 5 equal folds (n = 19 or 20). For both non-TOF and TOF training sets, all images of the same subject, i.e. the different 3D SA image stacks of the LV and RV ED and ES frames, were assigned to the same fold. The TOF and non-TOF folds were concatenated and 3 different training configurations were used in which models were trained using either the non-TOF images only of each fold (40 images per fold), the TOF images only (∼54 images per fold) or the mixture of TOF and non-TOF images (∼94 images per fold). Validation of each configuration (denoted as nTOF, TOF, and Mixed in the results) was performed for each of the trained models on the ED and ES TOF images of the left-out fold. The ED and ES frames were not automatically selected in this experiment, but identified based on the ground truth provided with each dataset.

Evaluation against the performance of a commercial software tool

In a second experiment, we applied the ensemble model of the best performing training configuration from the first experiment on the TOF test set (n = 36 subjects) for validation of automated quantification of LV and RV based on the generated segmentation maps. We further refer to these results as TOF-C.

We evaluated the performance of our model by comparison with the commercially available suiteHeart® software (NeoSoft, Pewaukee, Wisconsin). A fully automated segmentation and quantification of LV and RV cavity and LV myocardium was performed for all cases in the TOF test set using suiteHeart® with the automatic tricuspid valve (TV) and mitral valve (MV) detection option enabled. The software detects TV and MV in the 2 chamber and/or 4 chamber LA views and segments the LV endo- and epicardial contours and the RV endocardial contours in all SA slices and frames, but not the RV epicardial contours. The software also automatically detects the ED and ES frames and quantifies the LV and RV volumes for each frame and the LV mass for the selected ED frame. The contours and the values as quantified by the software were exported for further evaluation with Matlab®. The evaluation was performed in two different ways (see Fig. 2, Exp2). First, for specified LV and RV ED and ES frames, we used the contours generated by suiteHeart® for these frames to evaluate agreement with the ground truth segmentation and to calculate and evaluate LV and RV EDV, ESV and EF and LV myocardial mass (MM) in the same way as for our own approach. We further refer to these results as NS-C. Second, we evaluated the quantification performed by suiteHeart® directly, which we further refer to as NS-Q. Evaluation was performed using either the ground truth ED and ES frames as provided with each dataset or the frames as automatically selected by the software, in order to evaluate the impact of differences in the selection of the LV and RV ED and ES frames between the commercial software, our own approach, and the manual ground truth. As suiteHeart® did not provide LV MM for all frames but for the automatically selected ED frame only, LV MM was calculated for any other frame from its LV myocardial volume as quantified by the software using the same assumed myocardial density as mentioned previously, which was verified to be consistent with the values provided by the software for its selected ED frame.

Validation on the external test set

In a third experiment, we validated the proposed fully automated segmentation and quantification approach on the external TOF test set (n = 8, see Fig. 2, Exp3), acquired in a different center using a different scanner as the TOF training cases.

Benefit of using TOF versus non-TOF training cases

Suppl. Fig. 1 shows the DSC and MASD on the TOF validation images for the different training configurations (nTOF, TOF, Mixed), pooled over all 5 folds (n = 96). Training on the more specific TOF dataset resulted in significantly more accurate segmentations compared to the more general non-TOF dataset covering different pathologies. Adding TOF and non-TOF cases improved the DSC for RV and the MASD for both LV and RV compared to training on TOF cases only. Interestingly, although the non-TOF dataset did not contain labels of the RV myocardium, the RV myocardial DSC and MASD improved significantly by adding this dataset to the TOF dataset for training. The myocardial DSC and endo- and epicardial MASD were significantly worse for RV compared to LV for all training configurations (p<0.05).

Suppl. Fig. 2 shows the errors in EDV, ESV, EF and MM on the TOF validation images (pooled over all 5 folds, n = 96) for the different training configurations. A negative bias can be observed for all predicted volumes. The mean absolute volume errors are larger for RV compared to LV for all training configurations (nTOF, TOF, Mixed) and improve significantly when TOF cases are used during training. The improvement in EDV and ESV between the nTOF and Mixed training configurations is higher for RV (77% improvement for ED and 61% improvement for ES) than for LV (55% improvement for ED and 32% improvement for ES). LV MM is significantly better estimated with the TOF or Mixed training configuration than with the nTOF training configuration. For RV MM, no statistically significant difference was found between the TOF or Mixed training configurations (p>0.05). Discussion

In this study, we trained and validated a 3D CNN for fully automated LV and RV cavity and myocardium segmentation from a stack of 2D SA CMR slices in patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot. The 3D CNN is applied sequentially to all such stacks for different SA frames, allowing for automated quantification of LV and RV volumes and mass and automated selection of the ED and ES phases for LV and RV separately. Since these patients can have abnormally large RV volumes compared to healthy subjects as a result of chronic pulmonary regurgitation, existing deep learning approaches trained on non-TOF CMR cases with no or acquired cardiac disease are not well suited. This was confirmed by investigating the impact of using different training configurations on the performance of our method and by the comparison of our approach with the NeoSoft suiteHeart® software, for which we found a large negative volume offset for RV.

Benefit of using TOF versus non-TOF training data

The first experiment showed that training on TOF cases or a combining TOF and non-TOF cases achieved much better results than training on non-TOF cases only, especially for RV segmentation. This can be attributed to (i) the large difference in RV volume between the non-TOF and TOF cases used in this study, as can be observed in Fig. 3, (ii) the presence of a ventricular septal defect patch between LV and RV or a transannular patch in the RVOT (see Suppl. Fig. 3 (B)), and (iii) the absence of a clear pulmonary valve in some TOF patients. As the non-TOF dataset and the TOF training and external test datasets used in this study originated from different sources, potential differences in CMR acquisition should also be considered.

First, the TOF dataset had a larger number of partially labeled images for training, although the total number of LV and RV cavity labels remained the same. Second, due to the clinical nature of the TOF dataset, the myocardium was only delineated in ED and not in ES. Consequently, our training approach had to be robust to the use of partially labeled instead of only fully labeled images. To this end, we used the marginal Dice loss [15], which was specifically developed to deal with missing label problems and which simplifies to the standard Dice loss for fully labeled cases. Other loss functions for missing labels have been proposed in literature. In [19] for example, it has been shown that the gradient of the standard Dice loss ignores missing labels by default, achieving similar results for CMR segmentation as the marginal Dice loss. Interestingly, we observed that adding the non-TOF dataset for training improved the segmentation of RV myocardium in the TOF cases, although this dataset contains labels for RV cavity only and not for RV myocardium. We hypothesize that the model may have learned that the RV myocardium is adjacent to the RV cavity, such that an improved RV cavity segmentation resulting from the use of a larger training set may induce improved RV myocardium segmentation as well.

When comparing the results for LV and RV myocardium obtained with the 3D CNN with the mixed training configuration, a much lower DSC was found for RV (median/IQR = 61.7/8.3%) than for LV (median/IQR = 82.1/5.0%). This can be partially explained by the susceptibility of the DSC to small or thin structures, as an error of a single pixel has more effect on the DSC of a small structure than on the DSC of a larger structure. However, the MASD is also higher for RV compared to LV, indicating that the segmentation of RV myocardium is, similarly as for RV cavity, a more difficult task. In standard clinical practice, the RV myocardium is not always delineated since its thickness in healthy patients is close to the voxel size commonly used. We included it in our automatic segmentation approach because the RV myocardium is usually thicker in TOF patients, and RV mass/volume ratio is an established indicator for prognosis in TOF [20]. However, the segmentation accuracy of 61.7% may be insufficient to allow implementation in clinical practice and requires improvement.

Although our 3D CNN was trained on the ground truth ED and ES frames only, we applied it for automatic 3D segmentation of all other frames as well to allow for automatic determination of the ED and ES frames for LV and RV separately. As ground truth was available only for the ED and ES frames, we could not perform a quantitative validation of segmentation performance for the intermediate frames. However, based on visual inspection of the segmentations and the volume curves as shown in Suppl. Fig. 4, we conclude that the network seems to generalize well to the intermediate frames.

Evaluation against the performance of a commercial software tool and external validation

We found that our TOF-C results based on a 3D CNN trained on a combination of TOF and non-TOF cases were superior for RV quantification compared to the NS-C and NS-Q results based on the commercial suiteHeart® software tool. This is consistent with prior studies [5] indicating a high correlation with the manual analysis, but that this correlation is lower for TOF subjects, hence requiring a quality check and manual recontouring for this population.

LV volume estimation with NS-Q was slightly better compared to TOF-C. In contrast to our 3D CNN, NS-Q includes LA information by indicating the MV and TV planes in the two chamber and/or four chamber views for volume calculation. These LA views better depict MV and TV compared to SA views and thereby allow a more accurate indication of the basal ventricular end. Since there is a discrepancy between the shown contours and the reported volumes in suiteHeart®, visual verification of the NS-Q results by inspecting the contours is difficult. When investigating this discrepancy in more detail, we found that it arose from indicating the MV and TV planes (either manually or automatically). If these planes are not indicated, the NS-Q results become identical to NS-C and the estimated volumes in general decreased. Manual or automatic MV/TV indication appears to be crucial for suiteHeart® to avoid underestimation of the LV and RV volumes.

Comparison with previously published CNN-based approaches

Giannakidis et al. [9] and Tran et al. [7] trained and validated CNNs to segment the RV cavity in CMR images of TOF patients. While the patch-based segmentation CNN [21] of Giannakidis et al. was trained to segment the entire 3D stack of SA slices, the CNN of Tran et al. [7] considered only the 2D SA slices that contained the RV cavity. Diller et al. [6] proposed a deep learning approach for the prediction of prognosis in patients with TOF using multiple 2D segmentation CNNs which each segment a single structure (LV cavity, RV cavity or right atrium) in mid-cavity SA or LA images. Whether augmenting the training data with synthetic images could improve the performance of segmentation networks was investigated by Diller et al. [22] and Karimi-Bidhendi et al. [23] with contrasting results.

The study most similar to ours is the work of Tandon et al. [8] who developed a CNN to segment LV cavity, RV cavity and LV myocardium (but not the RV myocardium) simultaneously in CMR images of TOF patients. Such multi-class segmentation approach instead of only segmenting the RV as in [6], [7], [9] allows the CNN to learn spatial relationships between cardiac structures. Similar to our results, they found that the algorithm trained on both datasets simultaneously (structurally normal hearts augmented with TOF hearts), performed better for RV cavity segmentation compared to training on the structurally normal hearts only.

Quantitative comparison of the performance of our method with the methods found in literature is difficult, since different datasets were used for evaluation. Tandon et al. [8] reported median DSCs of 90.3% and 84.0% for the LV cavity, and of 89.4% and 82.5% for the RV cavity in ED and ES, respectively, when training on TOF and non-TOF cases simultaneously. These values are lower than the results we obtained for the TOF test set in this study: 93.8% and 89.8% for LV and 92.9% and 90.9% for RV (see Table 2). While their training dataset of non-TOF cases (n∼5000) was larger, their dataset of TOF cases (n = 57) was smaller than ours (n = 96). Moreover, while our non-TOF and TOF datasets were balanced in size, their training set was largely dominated by non-TOF cases, although the authors stated that they oversampled the TOF cases to overcome this problem. Furthermore, they used only fully labeled images for training and excluded cases for which the manual LV and RV ED or ES delineations were not in the same frame. Due to the frequent difference in ES phase for LV and RV in TOF patients, this resulted in an imbalance between ED (n = 57) and ES (n = 31) frames in their TOF dataset. Our approach did not require the LV and RV manual delineations for training to be in the same frame, imposing less constraints on the training dataset and avoiding a significant decrease of the effective dataset size available for training.

All previously described approaches, except for the RV cavity segmentation of Giannakidis et al. [9], used a 2D CNN while our approach was fully 3D. nnU-Net [24], a state-of-the-art approach for medical image segmentation, combines both 2D and 3D CNNs. However, applying the nnU-Net automatic training frame- work for our study was not possible since it cannot handle missing labels which are abundantly present in our training data as explained above.

Similar to the other approaches described above, our 3D CNN used only SA images. Manual segmentation is easier to perform when also the complementary long axis images are available, as the valves, indicating the basal end of LV and RV, are better shown on long axis than on SA slices. Incorporating both SA and long axis images in a CNN for LV and RV segmentation is not straightforward since many options exist on how to fuse SA and long axis information. The problem of integrating SA and long axis information in a CNN is not specific for TOF subjects, but is an issue for all CMR segmentation approaches. Therefore, although relevant, we considered the integration of LA information out of scope for the current paper. Existing work in this area can be found in [25], [26], [27], [28], [29].

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to perform automatic RV myocardium segmentation using a CNN. (Semi-)automatic RV myocardium segmentation using older approaches was treated in Petitjean et al. [30]. However, this paper did not validate the myocardium segmentation separately but in conjunction with the RV cavity. Hence, comparison of performance of our RV mass estimation with other automated approaches was not possible. As mentioned before, the segmentation accuracy of 61.7% may be insufficient to allow implementation in clinical practice and requires improvement.

Limitations

This study indicates that training a 3D CNN on TOF cases or a combining TOF and non-TOF cases achieved better results than training on non-TOF cases only. The study has some limitations. The impact of adding additional types of congenital heart defects as well as the impact on performance of the variation in pathology requires further study. The current study setup cannot fully distinguish the beneficial effect of increasing the amount of data from increasing the variability of the data by adding TOF cases. Since the ground truth was only available for ED and ES frames for LV and RV, we could not perform a quantitative validation of segmentation performance in the intermediate frames. We did not see a negative impact of using partially labeled images when comparing the different training configurations, but this should be further investigated in a more dedicated study. Because of difficulties to integrate SA and LA information, this was not performed in the current study. Further improvement in performance could be achieved by careful tuning of the architectural and training parameters of our 3D CNN. Due to the missing labels in our training data, it was not possible to investigate this with the automated nnU-Net training framework. We did not compare speed performance between methods. Further expansion of the external test set is required.

Future perspectives

The current paper presents a retrospective validation of our 3D CNN based approach for biventricular segmentation in TOF patients. Future work should include the validation of the method in clinical practice. Besides accuracy, also the time efficiency of the automated approach with possibly manual correction, compared to fully manual analysis, should be considered, as well as the impact on inter- observer variability. Finally, a similar approach, involving a pathology-specific CNN as developed here for RV quantification of patients with repaired TOF, could also be conceived for other congenital cardiac diseases such as univentricular hearts or transposition of the great arteries.

Conclusion

We developed and validated a fully automatic segmentation and quantification approach for LV and RV cavity and myocardium in patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot. We found that training on images of TOF patients is crucial for accurate RV quantification. Compared to a commercially available software tool, our approach is superior for RV quantification, indicating its potential value in clinical practice.
