Characteristics and influencing factors of spontaneous imbibition in deep tight conglomerate reservoirs in the Mahu Sag
Authors: Panpan Zhang, Rui Wang, Sai Liu, Zongjie Mu, Jixing Chen, Quanzhen Xiu, Qilong Wei, Shouceng Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Research on spontaneous imbibition in deep tight conglomerate reservoirs of the Mahu Sag is vital for enhancing oil recovery and optimizing development. This study employs porosity-permeability tests, CT scanning, contact angle measurements, volumetric imbibition experiments, and NMR T₂ spectrum/imaging to investigate impacts of core properties, size, temperature-pressure conditions, and contact modes. Findings reveal strong heterogeneity in porosity (5.4%-15.8%) and permeability (0.11–8.99 mD), poor porosity-permeability correlation, and weak connectivity with low coordination numbers (≤ 5 predominant); macroscopic connected porosity strongly correlates with permeability (r = 0.92). Reservoirs show strong hydrophilicity: interstitial clays < 10° contact angle, detrital grains averaging 30°. Imbibition recovery factors range 29.6%-50.9%, influenced by higher porosity/permeability and shorter cores. Reservoir conditions (95 °C, 56 MPa) increase recovery by ~ 8%, with temperature reducing oil viscosity for faster rates and pressure enhancing drive for longer duration. Microscopically, imbibition favors sub-micropores/micropores (up to 50% recovery), while macropores exhibit counter-imbibition and oil redistribution. Results provide insights into imbibition mechanisms, refine experimental methods, guide shut-in timing, and support efficient reservoir development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21543-z.

Introduction

Conglomerate reservoirs are typically composed of cemented mixtures of gravel, sand, and mud, emerging as an important unconventional oil and gas resource with increasing prominence in the global energy structure1–3. China possesses abundant conglomerate reservoir resources, with recoverable reserves exceeding 3 billion tons, accounting for approximately 25% of unconventional petroleum resources4–6. Chinese conglomerate reservoirs are mainly distributed in the Junggar Basin, Ordos Basin, and Songliao Basin. Among them, the Mahu Sag on the northwestern margin of the Junggar Basin is the world’s largest conglomerate reservoir enrichment area, with proven reserves exceeding 1 billion tons, harboring enormous potential for large-scale production7,8.

The conglomerate reservoirs in the Mahu Sag are primarily developed using “horizontal wells + fracturing” as the core approach, integrated with geology-engineering concepts9. This involves optimizing well trajectories and multi-stage fracturing to form complex fracture networks, thereby enhancing development efficiency10,11. Due to the prevalent strong heterogeneity, low permeability, and complex pore structures in these reservoirs, along with underdeveloped natural fractures, challenges such as rapid production decline and low recovery rates are encountered during development12,13. Microscopic fracture extension induced by spontaneous imbibition can improve reservoir seepage capacity, and optimizing shut-in time based on imbibition characteristics can maximize its effects, providing support for efficient development of such reservoirs14. Therefore, under the current development mode, the role of imbibition in matrix oil displacement becomes prominent, serving as a key factor determining well productivity15.

Imbibition effects are primarily influenced by reservoir physical properties, including rock wettability, pore structure, and permeability, which collectively determine fluid flow behavior and recovery efficiency in the reservoir16–18. Wettability is a major factor affecting imbibition oil displacement efficiency; in hydrophilic rocks, water spontaneously imbibes into the matrix to displace crude oil, with capillary force as the driving mechanism, whereas in oleophilic rocks, capillary force acts as resistance, though crude oil may be expelled via buoyancy or other forces19,20. Pore structure is another key parameter influencing imbibition oil displacement, where micro-fractures and high-permeability zones are considered areas of strong imbibition activity21,22. Additionally, actual reservoir conditions such as temperature and pressure also affect imbibition efficiency; elevated temperatures reduce crude oil viscosity and alter rock thermal expansion coefficients, synergistically enhancing imbibition displacement efficiency, while pressure variations influence capillary force intensity by adjusting oil-water interfacial tension23.

Existing studies on imbibition effects are mainly conducted through experiments and numerical simulations. Due to the fixed mechanistic models in current numerical simulation methods, they are challenging for investigating imbibition influencing factors and are primarily suitable for production prediction and guiding development strategies24,25. Experimental research methods mainly include conventional laboratory experiments (saturated oil cores placed in formation water imbibition bottles for oil displacement measurement), micro-nano chips (imbibition experiments in chip grooves to reveal microscopic mechanisms), and CT and NMR techniques to observe fluid distribution and mobilization characteristics26–28. Key insights include: ① Wettability alteration is the core mechanism of imbibition oil displacement, with low-salinity water or nanofluids optimizing capillary force and wettability; ② Imbibition volume exhibits a linear relationship with the square root of time, consistent with the classic Handy model; ③ Imbibition efficiency is significantly constrained by reservoir heterogeneity and stress sensitivity24,29–31.

Previous studies have employed volumetric measurement, mass measurement, nuclear magnetic resonance, CT, and other methods to investigate spontaneous imbibition behavior in depth, but the research objects are mostly sandstone and shale reservoirs, with few reports on imbibition in deep tight conglomerate reservoirs of the Mahu Sag. The conglomerate reservoirs in the Mahu Sag are influenced by complex sedimentation and diagenesis environments, resulting in strong reservoir heterogeneity and highly complex spontaneous imbibition patterns. Therefore, there is an urgent need to refine the understanding of crude oil occurrence states and imbibition mobilization characteristics in this block, providing theoretical support for evaluating imbibition recovery factors in tight conglomerates of the Mahu Sag and designing shut-in times.

This paper analyzes the influence of factors such as core physical properties, core size, temperature and pressure conditions, and contact modes on imbibition efficiency through porosity and permeability testing, CT analysis, contact angle analysis, volumetric imbibition experiments, and NMR imaging combined with T₂ spectrum analysis. The findings provide theoretical support for further understanding the spontaneous imbibition characteristics of deep tight conglomerate reservoirs in the Mahu block. The research outcomes contribute to refining experimental methods for tight conglomerate imbibition, guiding shut-in time design at the mining scale for tight conglomerates, and promoting efficient development of tight conglomerate reservoirs in the Mahu Sag.

Results and discussion

Porosity and permeability

A total of 25 core samples were collected from wells M2 and J5, with burial depths ranging from 3900 m to 4100 m. Sample information and porosity-permeability test data are shown in Table 1. Figure 1 illustrates the porosity-permeability distribution and correlation for cores from wells M2 and J5. For well M2, porosity ranges from 5.4% to 15.8% (average 8.7%), and permeability from 0.11 mD to 2.05 mD (average 0.83 mD). For well J5, porosity ranges from 7.0% to 9.0% (average 7.8%), and permeability from 0.27 mD to 8.99 mD (average 4.24 mD), with average permeability higher than that of well M2. Comparing data from full-diameter (68 mm) and 25 mm samples reveals minimal correlation between porosity-permeability and core size. Porosity and permeability data vary significantly with depth, showing poor regularity, indicating strong vertical heterogeneity. Samples #12 from well M2 and #19 from well J5 exhibit anomalously high permeability, deviating from the overall trend, possibly due to high-permeability channels or special rock structures. These samples have significantly higher permeability than other samples in the same reservoir and deviate from the overall “low porosity and low permeability” trend. Based on the regional geological background and macroscopic core observations, their abnormally high permeability is presumably related to natural fractures or local high-permeability channels developed inside the reservoir—natural fractures can form preferential paths for rapid fluid migration, while high-permeability channels reduce fluid flow resistance; both factors break the conventional correlation between porosity and permeability, leading to an abnormal increase in permeability32,33. Additionally, it can be inferred that the correlation between porosity and permeability in this area is poor. Therefore, porosity and permeability in wells M2 and J5 are controlled by multiple complex factors, characterized by strong heterogeneity and poor correlation, making it impossible to predict reservoir porosity-permeability performance based on a single factor.

Pore connectivity

Pore characteristics directly influence oil storage space and seepage paths. Figure 2 shows the three-dimensional pore network model and pore characteristic analysis of conglomerates based on CT scanning. Taking sample #6 as an example, the total porosity curve fluctuates between 1.1% and 9.8% (average 5.8%), with connected porosity generally lower than total porosity, ranging from 0.8% to 4.9% (average 2.2%). Comparing two-dimensional slices of total pores (upper red scatter points) and connected pores (lower red scatter points) reveals that the tight conglomerates in the Mahu Sag are characterized by “low total porosity and weak effective connectivity,” indicating that many pores are isolated or weakly connected and do not participate in effective seepage network formation.

The ball-and-stick model (Fig. 3a) further visualizes the spatial connection morphology of pores and throats, where spheres and sticks correspond to pore and throat units, respectively, displaying a complex and dispersed pore-throat network. The pore-throat coordination number statistics (Fig. 3b) show that coordination numbers are concentrated between 1 and 5, dominated by low coordination connections. Among them, coordination number 1 accounts for 35%, 2–3 for 35%, and 4–5 for 28%, with low coordination numbers (≤ 5) totaling nearly 90%. Low coordination numbers imply simple pore-throat connections and few fluid flow paths, naturally limiting seepage efficiency, which is the microscopic mechanism underlying the “difficult seepage” in tight conglomerates. Due to the CT resolution of approximately 10 μm, the identified pores are macroscopic pores. Figure 3c shows the correlation between macroscopic connected porosity and permeability, indicating that “connected porosity is a key influencing factor for permeability.” For samples #6, #13, and #24, connected porosity increases from 2.22% to 7.03%, corresponding to permeability growth from 0.80 mD to 7.03 mD. Correlation analysis yields a Pearson coefficient of 0.92, showing a strong positive correlation.

Wettability

Conglomerates are mainly composed of detrital particles and interstitial materials (sand, silt, clay); experiments reveal that interstitial materials generally have contact angles less than 10°, exhibiting extreme hydrophilicity. In contrast, detrital particles show distinct differences, with contact angles ranging from 15° to 47° (average approximately 30°), still hydrophilic overall. This difference may be related to mineral composition—interstitial materials are rich in clay minerals (e.g., montmorillonite, illite), whose surface polar groups readily adsorb water molecules, leading to ultra-low contact angles; detrital particles, dominated by quartz and feldspar, have relatively stable surface chemical properties, resulting in slightly higher contact angles. Wettability experimental data indicate strong hydrophilicity in the conglomerates of this well area, favoring enhanced crude oil recovery through “imbibition displacement.” During imbibition, the aqueous phase preferentially enters small pores to displace crude oil, while the higher contact angles of detrital particles may delay aqueous phase breakthrough, prolonging imbibition duration.

Imbibition characteristic analysis (based on volumetric method)

Figure 4 shows the imbibition characteristics of tight conglomerates under ambient temperature and pressure conditions. Combining reservoir parameters such as permeability, porosity, and core length (Table 1) with imbibition experimental data, the imbibition characteristics of tight conglomerates in the Mahu block are analyzed as follows: Imbibition recovery factors (29.6%–44.2%) exhibit significant dispersion, synergistically controlled by porosity, permeability, and core length; porosity shows a moderate positive correlation with recovery rate (correlation coefficient 0.65), permeability a moderate correlation (0.58), and length a weak negative correlation (− 0.51), indicating that high porosity, relatively high permeability, and short cores favor enhanced recovery rates; imbibition equilibrium time is negatively correlated with permeability, with core length acting as a modulating factor that interferes with this relationship; short cores can mitigate the negative impact of low permeability on equilibrium time.

The tight conglomerates in the Mahu block are deeply buried, with reservoirs mostly in high-temperature and high-pressure environments. Figure 5 further analyzes the influence of temperature and pressure on spontaneous imbibition characteristics. Figure 5a presents results for full-diameter cores. Comparing curves for samples #1 and #2 shows that open imbibition has a faster rate and reaches equilibrium earlier, but its recovery rate (50.9%) differs little from end-face imbibition (49.2%). Comparing samples #2 and #3 reveals that under high temperature and high pressure (reservoir conditions), both “imbibition rate” and “imbibition recovery rate” are enhanced, with final recovery increasing by approximately 8%. Due to the larger imbibition area in full-diameter cores, their equilibrium time is significantly shorter than standard cores, at about 50 h.

“End-face imbibition” simulates the imbibition scenario where a single main fracture contacts the end face of the reservoir matrix after fracturing. The main fractures formed by on-site fracturing have a limited extension range, and the contact between the fracture wall and the matrix is mainly “single-sided or local end-face”. At this time, the imbibition process is restricted by factors such as the contact area and distance between the fracture and the matrix, which is consistent with the characteristic that only one end face of the core contacts the imbibition fluid in the experiment.

“Open imbibition” corresponds to the imbibition conditions of the complex fracture network formed by volume fracturing. Efficient volume fracturing can generate complex fracture systems such as multi-directional branch fractures and activated natural fractures, enabling the matrix to contact the fracturing fluid on multiple sides and significantly increasing the imbibition area. This is consistent with the “open” characteristic where the core is exposed to the imbibition fluid on multiple sides in the experiment.

Figure 5b shows results for standard cores. For sample #13, imbibition temperature was ambient, and after the rate slowed, experimental pressure was increased from 0.1 MPa to formation pressure (56 MPa). The curve for sample #13 indicates that high pressure alters oil-water interfacial tension, enhances driving potential, slightly increases recovery rate (about 2–3%), and extends imbibition duration. For sample #15, imbibition was at ambient pressure, and after the rate slowed, temperature was raised from 25 °C to formation temperature (95 °C). The curve for sample #15 shows that high temperature accelerates imbibition displacement by reducing crude oil viscosity and enhancing final recovery by approximately 5%.

The imbibition recovery efficiency of tight conglomerates exhibits strong sensitivity to temperature and pressure conditions. Under ambient temperature and pressure, the recovery efficiency fluctuates between 29.6% and 44.2%, and shows a variation characteristic of “rapid increase in the early stage and stabilization in the later stage” with the imbibition process, reflecting the imbibition kinetics dominated by capillary driving force. Under high-temperature and high-pressure reservoir conditions, the synergistic effect of temperature and pressure significantly improves the imbibition performance: high temperature can effectively reduce crude oil viscosity and eliminate fluid migration resistance; pressure can regulate oil-water interfacial tension and enhance imbibition driving capacity. The combined action of the two increases the final recovery efficiency by approximately 8% compared with that under ambient temperature and pressure, highlighting the optimization value of in-situ reservoir conditions for imbibition efficiency.

Imbibition characteristic analysis (based on NMR T₂ spectrum and imaging technology)

Figure 6 shows the imbibition characteristic analysis results based on nuclear magnetic resonance technology, providing insights into imbibition features in pores of different sizes and oil distribution before and after imbibition. First, based on T₂ relaxation time, pores are classified into sub-micropores (< 1.0 μm), micropores (1.0–10 μm), and macropores (≥ 10 μm). From Fig. 6a, sub-micropores are the primary component of tight conglomerate pores, dominating reservoir storage and imbibition characteristics, with quantified data showing sub-micropores accounting for 72%, micropores 22%, and macropores ≤ 6%. After imbibition begins, the relaxation time distribution gradually shifts from short relaxation (sub-micropores) dominance to medium and long relaxation (micropores, macropores), indicating a strong association between conglomerate imbibition and pore scale, with imbibition priority as sub-micropores > micropores > macropores; additionally, spontaneous imbibition causes partial oil phase migration from small to large pores. Figure 6b quantitatively analyzes imbibition recovery factors for different pore sizes: sub-micropores and micropores show rapid increases in recovery with time, ultimately reaching about 50%; macropores, due to weaker capillary forces, exhibit unstable recovery changes, and under high permeability, macropores are prone to “counter-imbibition” phenomena, leading to oil phase redistribution, as evidenced in Fig. 6c. Comparing imbibition characteristics of samples #17 and #21 in Fig. 6c also reveals that higher permeability results in greater imbibition depth and more pronounced oil phase redistribution.

Discussion

In terms of reservoir physical properties, the conglomerates in the study area exhibit strong heterogeneity in porosity and permeability with poor correlation (Fig. 1), closely related to the complex sedimentation and diagenesis on the northwestern margin of the Junggar Basin. CT scanning results further indicate that reservoir pores are characterized by “low total porosity and weak effective connectivity,” with most pore-throat coordination numbers ≤ 5 (Fig. 3b). This low-coordination pore-throat network directly limits fluid seepage efficiency, providing a microscopic explanation for “difficult seepage.” Notably, macroscopic connected porosity shows a strong positive correlation with permeability (Pearson coefficient 0.92), suggesting that improving pore connectivity is key to enhancing reservoir seepage capacity, offering theoretical basis for “expanding effective stimulation volume” in volume fracturing design.

Wettability experiments show that the conglomerates in the study area are generally strongly hydrophilic (interstitial materials with contact angles < 10°, detrital particles averaging 30°), related to the strong water absorption of clay minerals (e.g., montmorillonite) in conglomerates and the stable surface properties of quartz and feldspar. This strong hydrophilicity provides favorable conditions for spontaneous imbibition, confirming the feasibility of “aqueous phase spontaneous imbibition displacing crude oil.”

Imbibition experimental results further reveal the synergistic influence of multiple factors on imbibition efficiency: under ambient temperature and pressure, high porosity, relatively high permeability, and short cores favor enhanced imbibition recovery factors, possibly due to more unobstructed fluid migration paths in high-porosity high-permeability reservoirs and shorter imbibition distances in short cores. High temperature and high pressure conditions can simultaneously enhance imbibition rate and recovery, with temperature effects being more pronounced, related to reduced crude oil viscosity and enhanced molecular diffusion at elevated temperatures34,35; pressure, by altering oil-water interfacial tension, strengthens driving potential and extends imbibition duration. NMR analysis from a microscopic perspective indicates imbibition priority as sub-micropores > micropores > macropores36, with “counter-imbibition” phenomena in high-permeability samples, possibly related to weaker capillary forces in macropores and crude oil redistribution under buoyancy and capillary forces. The experimental insights provide new perspectives for understanding mobilization differences in crude oil across pore sizes. However, this study has certain limitations: although the core samples cover major depth intervals, they do not fully encompass all lithological combinations in the reservoir; the interaction between fractures and matrix in actual reservoirs on imbibition has not been incorporated into the experimental system, requiring further validation with dynamic monitoring data in the future.

Conclusion

This study reveals the imbibition characteristics and controlling factors of deep tight conglomerate reservoirs in the Mahu Sag through porosity-permeability testing, CT scanning, wettability analysis, and imbibition experiments, with results holding significant implications for understanding the development mechanisms of such reservoirs. Materials and methods

Porosity and permeability measurements in this study adhered to the Chinese national standard GB/T 29,172 − 2012, “Practices for Core Analysis,” which outlines procedures for core sample processing, storage, preparation, and testing to ensure accurate petrophysical characterization. Porosity was determined using Boyle’s law via the double-chamber (gas expansion) method37, a widely adopted technique that leverages the Boyle-Mariotte gas law to measure pore volume. In this approach, helium gas is expanded into the core’s pore space within a calibrated chamber, and the pressure change is used to calculate porosity as the ratio of pore volume to bulk volume. This method is particularly effective for tight formations, providing reliable results with minimal sample alteration, as supported by standard petrophysical protocols. Air was flowed through the core at controlled pressure differentials, and permeability (in millidarcies, mD) was derived from the flow rate, fluid viscosity, core dimensions, and pressure drop, following Darcy’s law38.

For imbibition experiments, core samples were sourced from reservoir formations and prepared to simulate end-face spontaneous imbibition. The saturating oil was aviation kerosene with a density of 0.794 g/cm³, chosen for its similarity to subsurface crude oil in terms of viscosity and density. In NMR-based imbibition tests, heavy water (D₂O, density 1.106 g/cm³) was used as the imbibing fluid to shield hydrogen signals from water, enabling selective detection of oil displacement via nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) relaxometry, which quantifies fluid distribution in pores39. For conventional volumetric imbibition, the fluid was a 100,000 ppm KCl solution fortified with 1‰ hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPAM) as a viscosifier, yielding a density of 1.054 g/cm³; This formulation can inhibit clay swelling to improve experimental success rate, and it is similar in properties to fracturing fluids, making it more consistent with engineering practice.

“End-face imbibition” simulates the imbibition scenario where a single main fracture contacts the end face of the reservoir matrix after fracturing. The main fractures formed by on-site fracturing have a limited extension range, and the contact between the fracture wall and the matrix is mainly “single-sided or local end-face”. At this time, the imbibition process is restricted by factors such as the contact area and distance between the fracture and the matrix, which is consistent with the characteristic that only one end face of the core contacts the imbibition fluid in the experiment.

“Open imbibition” corresponds to the imbibition conditions of the complex fracture network formed by volume fracturing. Efficient volume fracturing can generate complex fracture systems such as multi-directional branch fractures and activated natural fractures, enabling the matrix to contact the fracturing fluid on multiple sides and significantly increasing the imbibition area. This is consistent with the “open” characteristic where the core is exposed to the imbibition fluid on multiple sides in the experiment.

Core sealing for single-end-face imbibition involved coating samples with epoxy resin mixed with a curing agent, followed by encapsulation in heat-shrinkable tubing to expose only one face, preventing multi-directional fluid entry and ensuring controlled capillary action. Key equipment included: (1) a vacuum saturation apparatus comprising a vacuum pump, desiccator, and filtration flask, capable of evacuating cores to approximately − 0.1 MPa for fluid saturation; and (2) an imbibition setup with a graduated beaker and funnel, where displaced kerosene floats to the funnel’s narrow neck for precise volumetric measurement of recovered oil.

The experimental workflow proceeded as follows: (1) Measure core porosity and permeability as described; (2) Evacuate the core under vacuum and saturate it with pressurized aviation kerosene, then weigh to calculate initial oil saturation; (3) Prepare and load the imbibing fluid into the apparatus; (4) Seal the saturated core to expose a single end-face; (5) Immerse the sealed core in the fluid to initiate spontaneous imbibition, periodically recording displaced oil volume and computing recovery factor as the ratio of recovered oil to original oil in place. Actual photographs of the experimental process are shown in Fig. 7. All procedures followed safety protocols and were conducted at ambient conditions unless specified. Other routine analyses employed standard laboratory equipment, aligning with petrophysical best practices from literature on tight reservoir characterization.
