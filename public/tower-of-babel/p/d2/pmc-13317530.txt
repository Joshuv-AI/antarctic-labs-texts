Delayed identification and treatment of a cervicomedullary spinal vascular lesion following angiogram-negative, high-grade subarachnoid hemorrhage: illustrative case
Authors: Christopher Chang, Michael Motoc, Anil Can, Mitchell Lachs, Mitali Bose, Joshua D Bernstock, Nirav J Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDAn angiogram-negative subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) with a high Hunt-Hess grade and atypical radiographic features warrants evaluation for occult lesions. Cervicomedullary spinal arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are rare causes frequently missed on initial imaging.OBSERVATIONSA 54-year-old male presented with a Hunt-Hess grade V SAH, and initial multimodal angiography findings were negative. In approximately 15%–20% of subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) cases, no definitive bleeding source is identified on initial vascular imaging, leading to a diagnosis of angiogram-negative SAH.1,2 Angiogram-negative SAHs are clinically heterogeneous, and the distribution of blood on initial noncontrast CT serves as the primary prognostic indicator. The perimesencephalic SAH pattern, characterized by blood localized to the prepontine or interpeduncular cisterns, is associated with a benign clinical course and a low risk of rebleeding.2,3 The non-perimesencephalic SAH pattern (diffuse or atypical distribution) presents with a higher risk of harboring an occult vascular lesion.2 In cases of high-grade SAH (Hunt-Hess grade IV or V) in which the initial diagnostic workup is negative, the persistent search for occult sources, including arterial dissections, dural arteriovenous fistulas, or spinal vascular malformations, becomes paramount.1

Spinal vascular lesions, particularly those involving the anterior spinal artery (ASA) and the cervicomedullary junction, are rare, frequently underrecognized, and technically difficult to diagnose on initial imaging.4,5 The small caliber of the feeding vessels and the complex bony anatomy of the skull base often obscure these lesions on standard CT angiography (CTA) and MR angiography (MRA). Furthermore, the acute hemodynamic shifts and vasospasm that follow a high-grade rupture can cause transient thrombosis or constriction of the lesion, rendering it invisible on early angiography.5–7

Illustrative Case

Clinical Presentation and Initial Management

A 54-year-old male with no significant prior medical history was at home when he experienced “the worst headache of his life,” localized occipitally during a bowel movement, followed by a generalized tonic-clonic seizure and rapid neurological decline. On arrival at the emergency department, the patient was hypertensive (systolic blood pressure 190 mm Hg) and obtunded with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 3 (E1V1M1). Given the clinical grade (Hunt-Hess grade V), the patient was immediately intubated and stabilized with 4.5 g of levetiracetam and 250 mL of 3% hypertonic saline. Noncontrast CT demonstrated a diffuse SAH involving basal cisterns and the prepontine space with intraventricular extension (modified Fisher grade 4) (Fig. 1A, B, and E).8 The patient was transferred to a tertiary neurosurgical center in Boston, where an external ventricular drain (EVD) was placed via stereotactic navigation (technically complex due to the patient’s incidental congenital colpocephaly and agenesis of the corpus callosum). Following CSF diversion, the patient’s neurological status improved by the next morning as he localized painful stimuli in the upper extremities.

Initial CTA and four-vessel DSA findings were negative for an intracranial aneurysm or malformation. Brain and cervical spine MRI and MRA confirmed blood products anterior to the medulla without a discrete vascular source (Fig. 1F and G). Two days later, a separate, complete multisequence, multiplanar spine MRA study was also negative.

Imaging Clues Suggesting Spinal Etiology

Despite these negative angiographies, several features raised suspicion for a spinal source. First, a focal hyperdensity at the ventral cervicomedullary junction represented a spot sign on CTA, indicating the hemorrhage source (Fig. 1C). Second, the high-volume hemorrhage on CT and MRI showed disproportionate prepontine cistern and ventral cervicomedullary junction accumulation with inferior tracking along the spinal cord—a ventrally predominant distribution pattern (Fig. 1D).9–11 Third, intraventricular hemorrhage involving the third and fourth ventricles (Fig. 1E) is atypical for classic perimesencephalic SAH, in which ventricular blood is typically minimal.12,13 The patient’s Hunt-Hess grade V presentation contrasted sharply with perimesencephalic SAH, which almost universally presents as Hunt-Hess grades I and II and is associated with good outcomes.14,15 This SAH pattern and presentation carries a 10%–15% likelihood of harboring occult sources requiring delayed imaging.7,16,17

Diagnostic Persistence and Delayed Identification

Given high suspicion, the team pursued repeat DSA despite initially negative studies. This repeat DSA at day 10 included dedicated spinal runs with vertebral artery injections. The resolution of the initial vasospasm likely enabled better visualization of the fine feeding vessels,18 or there was more filling of the lesion as thrombus evolved. The study revealed a small, 3-mm vascular nidus supplied by the meningeal branches from the V2–V4 segments of the right vertebral artery and a branch from the ASA (Fig. 2A and B). The most cranial artery supply shared a common origin with the ASA and had early venous drainage, confirming the diagnosis of a cervicomedullary AVM. A 3-mm ASA aneurysm was identified at the malformation’s inferior aspect (Fig. 2B). In retrospect, the prior MRA study also had a small focal intensity at the same location.

Treatment Planning: Endovascular Versus Microsurgical

The neurovascular surgeon evaluated treatment options, and a third DSA study with selective microcatheterization and neuromonitoring modalities was completed. Endovascular treatment was deemed prohibitive for three reasons. First, the cranial feeder’s shared origin with the ASA created an unavoidable reflux risk into the vessel. Furthermore, despite attempts, safe distal microcatheter positioning could not be achieved given the proximity to the ASA trunk and the small caliber of the feeding vessel. These technical limitations, combined with unfavorable morphology for selective catheterization, led to the decision to pursue microsurgical management via a far-lateral transcondylar approach.

Surgical Technique

In a prone position, head turned, vertex down, a far-lateral transcondylar approach with a C1 and C2 laminectomy was performed, including early exposure and control of the right vertebral artery. Neuronavigation loaded with fused MRI and CTA images was registered and utilized through the heads-up display of the microscope. This technique can provide orientation and localization of the arterial feeders to the surgeon, even at high magnification, without having to use the navigation pointer. After dural opening and subarachnoid clot removal (Fig. 3A and B), careful dissection revealed a right-biased ventral spinal AVM at the C1–2 level with three arterial pedicles from vertebral artery segments (one from the distal V2 segment, a second from the mid V3 segment, and a third from the V3–V4 junction) (Fig. 3C and D). The dentate ligament was sectioned, and the right C2 dorsal root was sacrificed, allowing controlled rotation of the spinal cord to improve anterior exposure (Fig. 3E–H). The ASA aneurysm and AVM nidus were identified and further characterized using intraoperative indocyanine green video angiography (ICG-VA). The feeding arteries of the malformation were carefully dissected free. During resection, a permanent clip was applied to what was initially believed to be an isolated AVM feeding branch but was later recognized to be the ASA itself. Findings From Intraoperative Neuromonitoring

Multimodal intraoperative neuromonitoring—somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs), transcranial motor evoked potentials (TcMEPs), brainstem auditory evoked responses (BAERs), corticobulbar motor evoked potentials (CBMEPs) of cranial nerves (CNs) IX–XII, laryngeal adductor reflexes (LARs), and median-hypoglossal reflexes—was used throughout. All SSEPs, BAERs, LARs, and reflexes and CBMEPs of CNs were present and reliable during the procedure. Bilateral upper and lower extremity motor evoked potentials (MEPs) demonstrated robust baseline responses. The baseline D-wave amplitude was established after the dura was opened.

During AVM resection, an AVM clip intended for a feeding branch was applied. One hour 15 minutes after clip placement, the neurophysiology team reported a 50% decline in bilateral upper extremity MEPs. Approximately 20 minutes later, bilateral upper extremity MEPs declined further, while D-waves continued to show preservation > 50% of baseline (Fig. 4A–C). The discordant pattern–bilateral progressive MEP decline with preservation of D-wave amplitude indicated reversible ischemia affecting non-corticospinal motor pathways rather than direct mechanical injury to the corticospinal tracts (CSTs).

The signals started recovering at 2:54 pm and returned to baselines at 4:55 pm without changing the stimulation intensity throughout the procedure. The progressive bilateral upper extremity involvement raised concern for major arterial supply compromise rather than a temporary ischemia from surgical manipulation. Careful reinspection of the surgical field revealed that the AVM clip had been inadvertently placed on the main ASA trunk rather than the intended isolated AVM branch vessel. After clip removal, we followed the feeder to the nidus, where it was coagulated and cut, disconnecting from the nidus.

The AVM clip was removed to restore ASA flow. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was increased to 85–90 mm Hg using vasopressor support to optimize spinal cord perfusion. Additionally, 1 unit of packed red blood cells was transfused. Within 30 minutes, there was improvement in MEPs, and approximately 2 hours after these interventions, both upper extremity MEPs returned to baseline amplitudes, confirming the reversibility of ischemic changes and validating that D-wave preservation indicated intact CST function. This neuromonitoring approach allowed safe completion of the AVM resection with confidence that the CSTs remained intact.

The final ICG-VA study confirmed complete exclusion of the AVM lesion while maintaining robust flow through the intact ASA.

Postoperative Course

Consistent with monitoring predictions of D-wave amplitude preservation throughout the procedure, the patient had no motor deficits postoperatively. He was admitted to the ICU. There was expected numbness in the C2 distribution given the dorsal root sacrifice. Postoperative DSA confirmed the complete exclusion of both lesions (Fig. 5). The patient was weaned from the EVD, and he was discharged to acute rehabilitation. At his 3-month follow-up, he walked independently with near-complete cognitive recovery.

Discussion

Angiogram-Negative SAH: Diagnostic Challenges and the Role of Delayed Imaging

The incidence of spinal vascular malformations as a cause of angiogram-negative SAH is low, accounting for only 1% of cases according to a case series of 47 patients with angiogram-negative SAHs.19 Our case warns against assuming “benign” etiologies in patients with high-grade, angiogram-negative SAH. While 15%–20% of all SAHs are angiogram-negative and generally have better outcomes, this only applies to the perimesencephalic subgroup.17,20,21 Hunt-Hess grade V presentation further distinguishes non-perimesencephalic SAHs from the benign perimesencephalic subgroup, which presents almost universally as Hunt-Hess grades I and II with excellent outcomes.14,15 Multiple mechanisms were theoretically responsible for false-negative initial angiography: vasospasm-obscuring lesions, aneurysm thrombosis, small vessel caliber, and local hematoma compression.22–24 Critically, when hemorrhage involves the prepontine/ventral medullary spaces, dedicated spinal angiography with selective vertebral injections is essential. The timing of repeat angiography balances the risk of rebleeding against the resolution of obscuring factors. Delgado Almandoz et al. demonstrated an overall yield of 4.2% on the 7-day repeat study, with non-perimesencephalic SAH showing a detection rate of 5.1%.6 Furthermore, Nguyen et al. detected lesions at 7 days, with secondary DSA yielding a 11.3% rate of non-perimesencephalic patterns of SAH.7 The 7- to 14-day window allows vasospasm resolution, hematoma clearance, and thrombus recanalization. However, there remains a lack of clinical guidelines for the specific timing of repeat angiography after initial negative studies.25,26

Endovascular Versus Microsurgical Management of ASA-Associated Lesions

Endovascular treatment of ASA aneurysms and associated malformations presents unique technical and safety challenges. Multiple published reports have demonstrated that the spinal vasculature tolerates substantially less embolic material than cerebral vessels before catastrophic reflux occurs.27,28 Furthermore, the small caliber of spinal vessels often leads to rapid nidus packing, making reflux patterns difficult to predict. A case report by Kim et al. notes this complication, where incidental ASA occlusion during Onyx embolization of a spinal type II AVM led to quadriplegia in a patient.29 Additionally, lower complete obliteration rates were found in nidus-type lesions compared to fistulous-type AVMs, with technical limitations frequently cited.30

The far-lateral transcondylar approach has evolved as a standard for ventral foramen magnum and upper cervical pathology. Wen et al. described the anatomical foundations, demonstrating direct access to structures anterior to the medulla without brainstem retraction.31 The benefit of controlled spinal cord rotation through multilevel dentate ligament sectioning allows for exposure of the ventral root entry zone, the ipsilateral ventral cord surface, and the ASA with up to 30° of rotation.32,33 While C2 dorsal root sacrifice may cause posterior scalp numbness,34 the maneuver enables rotation without extensive neural tension.

Comparative outcomes support microsurgical approaches for complex cervicomedullary lesions. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed a higher initial success rate for surgically treated spinal vascular malformations and lower recurrence rates in lesions treated surgically.35 These data reaffirm our decision to operate in this case and support primary microsurgical management when there is restricted endovascular access or a high risk of ASA compromise.

Intraoperative Neuromonitoring: D-Wave as a Nuanced Predictor of Motor Outcomes

D-wave monitoring represents the gold standard for assessing CST integrity during spinal cord surgery, as D-waves result from direct activation of fast-conducting, thickly myelinated CST fibers and remain stable under total intravenous anesthesia.36 The critical prognostic threshold with multiple series validation is > 50% amplitude decline from baseline.37,38 Sala et al. demonstrated that a decline in D-wave amplitude > 50% was the primary indication to stop surgery, whereas preservation of > 50% predicted motor recovery even with transient postoperative weakness.38 These predictive thresholds were reconfirmed in other studies demonstrating successful D-wave monitoring.39

Discordance of significant MEP decline with preserved D-wave (> 50% baseline) indicates a specific pathophysiology. Costa et al. clarified that MEP loss or a significant reduction with D-wave preservation suggests a transient paraplegia induced by surgery from reversible inactivation of non-corticospinal descending tracts, while fast-conducting CST fibers remain intact.40,41 This pattern permits continued safe resection with an expectation of motor recovery.

Progressive bilateral upper extremity involvement in our case, with continuously intact D-waves, provides evidence of progressive ischemia affecting non-corticospinal tract pathways. The loss of MEPs bilaterally after permanent clip application raised concern for compromise of a major arterial supply rather than a temporary clip ischemia. The preservation of D-waves throughout the episodes proved critical for surgical decision-making.

Hemodynamic optimization in this setting represented a critical intervention for ischemic changes. A multidisciplinary guideline development group suggested that MAP should be augmented to at least 75–80 mm Hg as the lower limit, but not actively augmented beyond an upper limit of 90–95 mm Hg to optimize spinal cord perfusion in acute traumatic spinal cord injury (SCI). Although these guidelines were developed for traumatic SCI, similar principles of spinal cord perfusion optimization may apply to intraoperative ischemic events.42 The gradual reversibility of MEP changes over 2 hours with clip removal, MAP elevation, and improved oxygen delivery through blood transfusion confirmed the ischemic mechanism, distinguishing it from an irreversible mechanical injury. Furthermore, unlike mechanical injuries, where D-wave decline generally occurs in an abrupt fashion, ischemic changes demonstrate reversibility when perfusion is restored, as seen in this case. A single-center study demonstrated that combining D-wave monitoring with TcMEPs had a significantly lower false-positive rate when compared to monitoring with MEPs alone.43

Nevertheless, important limitations exist. D-wave monitoring should not be used for scoliosis surgery due to high false-positive rates from spinal cord rotation within the canal,44 affecting electrode position. However, for intramedullary tumors, vascular malformations, and other pathologies in which there is cord manipulation, D-wave remains a reliable predictor of motor outcomes.45

Observations

Initial CT clues that suggested a spinal rather than intracranial source despite negative angiography findings included a focal spot sign of hyperdensity at the ventral cervicomedullary junction, disproportionate prepontine blood accumulation with inferior tracking, and intraventricular hemorrhage, which is atypical for classic perimesencephalic SAH. This prompted a discussion among a multidisciplinary team of neurointensivists, radiologists, and neurosurgeons, leading to repeat angiography at day 10 and revealing a 3-mm ASA aneurysm associated with a complex cervicomedullary AVM supplied by V2–V4 vertebral artery segments.

Endovascular treatment was contraindicated due to the shared origin of an AVM feeder with the ASA, creating an unavoidable liquid embolic reflux risk. Microsurgical resection via the far-lateral transcondylar approach with C1–2 laminectomies and controlled cord rotation provided safe access to this ventral lesion.

Multidisciplinary collaboration between the surgical and neurophysiology teams proved critical as progressive bilateral upper extremity MEP declines were identified following permanent clip placement. Careful surgical inspection revealed the clip had been inadvertently placed on the main ASA trunk rather than the intended AVM branch feeder. However, D-waves remained intact throughout (> 50% baseline), indicating reversible ischemia rather than mechanical CST injury. This real-time communication between the monitoring and surgical teams enabled clip removal, MAP optimization to > 85 mm Hg, and blood transfusion, which produced complete MEP recovery within 2 hours. A monitoring-guided, team-based approach enabled safe lesion resection. The patient experienced no permanent neurological deficit.

Lessons

This case provides several critical lessons for neurosurgeons managing complex angiogram-negative SAHs and cervicomedullary lesions.

Surgeons must recognize imaging clues that distinguish high-risk from benign angiogram-negative SAHs, as not all angiogram-negative SAHs carry a benign prognosis. Three specific CT features should prompt aggressive investigation: a focal spot sign hyperdensity localizing the hemorrhage epicenter, a ventrally predominant distribution with disproportionate prepontine accumulation and inferior spinal tracking, and an intraventricular hemorrhage, distinguishing benign perimesencephalic presentations from those with atypical features that warrant further investigation. Furthermore, Hunt-Hess grade V presentation should also exclude benign perimesencephalic SAH. These patients require targeted spinal angiography with selective vertebral artery injections at 7–14 days to detect occult spinal sources.

Surgeons should understand when endovascular intervention of ASA-associated lesions is prohibitive. We have identified three factors that preclude safe endovascular treatment: ASA shares a common origin with AVM feeders, creating a high reflux risk; the inability to achieve safe distal microcatheter positioning in small-caliber spinal feeders; and aneurysm and AVM morphology unsuitable for selective catheterization without compromising the ASA trunk. Spinal vessels tolerate minimal embolic material before catastrophic reflux occurs. In addition, microsurgical approaches allow more precise and reversible control of feeding arteries, and superior obliteration rates for complex cervicomedullary lesions when feasible.

The far-lateral transcondylar approach with cord rotation can be an acceptable surgical plan for ventral cervicomedullary pathologies when endovascular options are limited. This approach provides direct visualization without brainstem retraction, controlled 30° rotation after dentate sectioning, and access to the ventral cord surface and ASA.

D-wave monitoring should be applied and carefully interpreted to distinguish reversible ischemia from irreversible mechanical injury and to detect surgical errors in real time. The pattern of MEP decline with preserved D-wave (> 50% baseline) indicates reversible ischemia affecting non-corticospinal pathways. In this case, progressive bilateral upper extremity involvement following permanent clip placement indicated major arterial compromise, leading to the discovery of inadvertent ASA trunk clipping rather than isolated AVM branch occlusion. Immediate interventions, such as careful surgical field inspection to identify the cause, removal of clips to restore flow, MAP elevation to > 85–90 mm Hg, optimization of oxygen delivery (consider transfusion if anemic), and observation of MEPs and D-waves for recovery, should be considered. Preservation of D-wave amplitude > 50% of baseline predicts motor recovery even with transient postoperative weakness. However, a loss of D-wave amplitude > 50% of baseline strongly indicates irreversible CST injury and mandates immediate cessation of manipulation.

Finally, surgeons must recognize the temporal characteristics of ischemia from mechanical injury. Ischemic changes often demonstrate progressive bilateral involvement over minutes and reversibility of motor function within a couple of hours after intervention. D-waves should be preserved throughout. In contrast, mechanical injury typically presents with immediate changes following manipulation, a gradual decline in D-wave, and irreversibility despite intervention. Surgeons should understand these patterns to enable real-time surgical decision-making.
