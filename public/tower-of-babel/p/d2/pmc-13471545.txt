Ultrasound characteristics vs. outdoor activities impacts on obese adolescents: a cross-sectional cardiopulmonary study
Authors: Yongqian Han, Qiong-e Wei, Juchuan Dong, Wenyao Cheng, Lihua Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity among adolescents is rising, raising concerns about future cardiopulmonary disease. It’s unclear if this group has impaired cardiopulmonary function and whether this stems from muscle issues or sedentary behavior.MethodsThis cross-sectional study compared obese/overweight (n = 29) and non-obese (n = 29) adolescents (age ~ 21 yrs). It assessed cardiopulmonary function, axial rotation, muscle quality / quantity (respiratory, abdominal, gluteal), and outdoor activity.ResultsNon-obese females had significantly more weekly outdoor activities. Cardiopulmonary results showed mixed findings: the obese group had higher maximum voluntary ventilation (MVV) and peak heart rate (HRpeak), while the non-obese group had better resting heart rate (HRrest) and oxygen consumption per kilogram (VO2/kg). The obese group had thicker sternocleidomastoid, rectus abdominis (RA), and gluteal muscles, but poorer muscle quality (ultrasound grayscale) in the upper trapezius and RA compared to the non-obese group. In obese females, higher VO2/kg correlated with better RA quality, while higher peak VO2 correlated with less outdoor activity time. Muscle imbalance was only seen in non-obese females and was positively associated with activity levels.ConclusionIn comparison to non-obese peers, obese/overweight adolescents show inferior cardiopulmonary function and reduced muscle performance. While some muscles are thicker in the obese group, lower grayscale values indicate poor quality/function. Muscle assessment should not rely solely on thickness. Although outdoor activity shows a positive correlation with some cardiopulmonary parameters, muscle metabolic issues related to sedentary behavior likely represent a significant correlate of cardiopulmonary dysfunction.

Introduction

In recent decades, the prevalence of obesity among adolescents has increased to the level of a global epidemic, with more than 13% of this population worldwide classified as obese or overweight [1]. While the prevalence of obesity is influenced by a variety of factors, including genetic, environmental, and lifestyle components [2], research has documented a correlation between the global increase in obesity rates and a simultaneous decline in cardiorespiratory fitness observed over the same timeframe [3, 4]. This increase in obesity rates has been implicated in the observed decline in cardiopulmonary function during the same period. Although diminished cardiopulmonary fitness is associated with an elevated risk of cardiometabolic diseases and adverse mental health outcomes [5], the precise relationship between obesity and cardiopulmonary function remains inadequately defined. Some studies indicate that the association between obesity and cardiometabolic risk may be mitigated by enhanced physical fitness levels [6]. Physical fitness is closely linked to exercise habits; however, our preliminary research indicates that adolescents often have limited or no time for outdoor activities [7]. This lack of engagement in outdoor exercise results in extended periods of indoor inactivity, particularly increased sitting time, which may further exacerbate abnormalities in respiratory-related muscles as well as the muscles of the abdomen and buttocks [8].

In our prior research, we established that respiratory function is substantially affected by the respiratory muscles [9]. Individuals with obesity are predisposed to heightened sedentary behavior and diminished physical activity [10]. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether the decreased outdoor activity observed in individuals with obesity contributes to sedentariness and potentially muscle-damaging behaviors, thereby leading to impaired cardiopulmonary function, or if the decline in cardiopulmonary function is simply a result of respiratory muscle abnormalities linked to cardiopulmonary fitness within this demographic.

An investigation into the characteristics of obese adolescents, in comparison to their normal-weight counterparts, focusing on the quality and quantity of respiratory, abdominal, and gluteal muscles, as well as their relationship with outdoor activity and cardiopulmonary function, could provide valuable theoretical insights to elucidate these associations. We hypothesize that obese individuals will demonstrate a higher prevalence of cardiopulmonary dysfunction relative to their normal-weight peers. This is expected to be accompanied by a decreased frequency and total duration of outdoor activities, reduced thickness of respiratory, abdominal, and gluteal muscles, and a greater degree of muscle imbalance. These muscle and activity characteristics may have a direct correlation with cardiopulmonary function.

Methods

Study design

This study is a cross-sectional observational investigation, with data collection conducted between June and October 2025. Prior to participant enrollment, all participants were thoroughly informed about the objectives and procedures of the study and provided written informed consent. Ethical review board approval was obtained for this research. The study adheres to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size

Based on previous studies comparing obese and non-obese individuals [11], we utilized G*Power to calculate the effect size for Cohen’s d and to determine the sample size required. The calculations indicated that a sample size of 29 participants per group was necessary.

Participations

Participant recruitment was facilitated through health education initiatives emphasizing cardiopulmonary and obesity awareness. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Due to the significant correlation between muscle quality and quantity, cardiopulmonary function, and age, participants were limited to those aged 18 to 25 years; (2) To ensure measurements were predominantly reflective of muscle assessment, all participants were required to be right-handed (Given the potential influence of lateral dominance on muscle characteristics, we have elected to include the majority right-handed participants in our study in order to minimize bias); (3) Participants were categorized based on body mass index (BMI) into either the normal weight group (BMI < 24) or the overweight/obese group(obese) (BMI > 24), with the stipulation that they had no history of severe pulmonary diseases. Exclusion criteria encompassed any history of upper limb injuries, particularly rib fractures, that could potentially influence muscle ultrasound outcomes, as well as any cardiac disease or contraindications to exercise testing.

Outcome measurement

ATR

Axial Trunk Rotation (ATR) angles were assessed at the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar regions of the participants used a scoliometer, with only the highest ATR value among these vertebrae being documented. Our prior research has established that spinal ATR associated with lifestyle habits can serve as an indicator for the presence or absence of physical functional deficits at a threshold of 5° [7]. Consequently, the ATR values were analyzed with reference to a threshold exceeding 5°, as opposed to 7°.

Cardiorespiratory exercise testing

Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing (CPET) was performed using a cardiopulmonary function measurement system (Pulspire® CPX-1000 Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing System, Zhixinjian, Nanjing, China), with the entire procedure supervised by a specialized physician to ensure participant safety and accurate determination of peak values. The protocol adhered to the guidelines established by Jacquinot et al. [12]. Throughout the CPET, physicians provided encouragement to participants and monitored their heart rate and blood pressure until maximum power output was achieved. Participants engaged in cycle ergometry while wearing a CPET mask and undergoing continuous 12-lead ECG monitoring. Following a 30-watt, 3-minute warm-up, the power output on the cycle ergometer was incrementally increased by 10 watts per minute until the participant reached exhaustion, chose to discontinue due to fatigue, or exhibited cardiac symptoms. Data collected during the final 60 s of the highest achieved values were designated as peak values. The mask system automatically recorded both resting and peak values for heart rate (HR rest, HR peak), ventilatory equivalent for carbon dioxide (VE/VCO2 rest, VE/VCO2 peak), oxygen consumption (VO2 rest [ml/min], VO2 peak [ml/min]), oxygen consumption per kilogram (VO2/kg rest [ml/min/kg], VO2/kg peak [ml/min/kg]), as well as forced vital capacity percentage predicted (FVC %pred), forced expiratory volume in one second percentage predicted (FEV1%pred), FEV1/FVC percentage predicted, peak expiratory flow (PEF [L/s]), and maximum voluntary ventilation (MVV [L/min]).

Weekly engagement in physical activity and frequency

To address potential biases in the collection of weekly physical activity data, researchers utilized a face-to-face interview methodology. This approach emphasized the primary focus on dynamic activities, as opposed to static activities like walking. Participants were requested to specify the frequency of their engagement in these dynamic activities, which was documented as weekly activity frequency. Data were systematically recorded in terms of both the total duration and frequency of activities performed each week.

Measurement of muscle quality and quantity

Taking into account the advantages of ultrasound devices, such as their non-invasive nature, capability for real-time imaging, and user-friendliness, we have chosen ultrasonography as the preferred method for evaluating muscle characteristics [13]. Muscle quality and quantity, which are represented by the grayscale and thickness of muscle, were simultaneously collected. Participants were instructed to assume specific postures for muscle measurements: standing for the upper and lower trapezius (UTm and LTm) and sternocleidomastoid muscles (SCm), supine for the rectus abdominis (RA), and prone for the gluteus maximus (GA). A 6-MHz linear-array transducer, utilizing B-mode ultrasonography (Sonimage HIS; Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan), was employed for these assessments. The UTm and LTm measurement methodology, noted for its high reliability by O’Sullivan et al. [14], was adopted for our evaluations. For the SCm, the ultrasound transducer was positioned parallel to the clavicle, with measurements taken at the midpoint between the jawline and the clavicle. During the RA assessment, participants were positioned supine with a pillow under their knees. The transducer was aligned parallel to the superior border of the pubic symphysis, positioned 3 cm above the umbilicus and 3 cm lateral to the midline. Measurements were conducted at the end of expiration. For the evaluation of the GA, participants were positioned in a prone orientation. The transducer was employed to identify the gluteus maximus at the level of the superior aspect of the ischial tuberosities and along the horizontal line of the gluteal fold. Grayscale measurements were performed in accordance with our previously established protocols [15]. Specifically, ultrasound images were stored in JPEG format and subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/index.html; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland) to ascertain the grayscale values of the muscles.

Statistical analysis

Although the sample size was determined a priori to ensure sufficient statistical power before the commencement of the trial, an unexpected and considerable gender disparity arose during participant recruitment. Notably, obese males exhibited a significantly greater propensity to enroll in the study compared to females, especially concerning assessments of muscular thickness. As a result, this imbalance led to an overrepresentation of females among non-overweight or non-obese participants, while males predominated within the overweight or obese group.

To address potential confounding effects associated with gender-related differences in cardiorespiratory function and muscular quantity and quality, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by gender. Within each gender group, we systematically compared non-overweight/non-obese participants with their overweight/obese counterparts in terms of outdoor physical activity levels, cardiorespiratory performance, and muscular characteristics. Additionally, we investigated the correlations among clinically significant indicators of muscular morphology, cardiorespiratory function, and outdoor activity patterns to elucidate the interactions between anthropometric traits, behavioral factors, and physiological outcomes.

Before conducting statistical analyses, all datasets were subjected to normality testing using the Shapiro-Wilk test. As the majority of variables demonstrated non-normal distributions, non-parametric group comparisons were carried out using the Mann-Whitney U test. To evaluate the strength and direction of associations between key indicators—such as muscular and cardiorespiratory metrics and levels of outdoor activity—within gender-specific subgroups further stratified by weight classification (overweight/obese versus normal weight), Spearman’s rank-order correlation was employed. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the large number of variables analyzed, we acknowledge the increased potential for Type I errors due to multiple comparisons. Consequently, p-values were interpreted as nominal indicators of significance, and findings were assessed based on their clinical relevance and consistency across indicators. All statistical analyses were executed using SPSS software version 30.0 (IBM SPSS, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), the graphical abstract was conducted by Figdraw (www.figdraw.com), and data visualization was performed with GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 58 young adults (19 males; age = 21.16 ± 1.26 years) participated in this study, consisting of 29 non-obese individuals (BMI = 20.49 ± 2.31 kg/m²) and 29 individuals classified as overweight or obese (BMI = 30.20 ± 4.06 kg/m²). As detailed in the Methods section, the non-overweight cohort had a disproportionately higher number of females, leading to a statistically significant gender imbalance between the two groups (p < 0.001) (Table 1). To mitigate potential interpretive bias in the study’s results, gender-stratified subgroup analyses were subsequently conducted.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Subgroup analysis

Comparative analyses were performed to assess baseline characteristics and cardiorespiratory function across non-overweight and overweight/obese subgroups, stratified by gender. The results indicated no significant differences in height between BMI categories for either males or females. However, significant differences were observed in body weight and BMI (p < 0.05). Notably, variations in activity patterns by weight status were evident solely among females: those with normal weight participated in significantly longer durations of physical activity (p < 0.05) and engaged in exercise with greater weekly frequency compared to their overweight/obese counterparts (p < 0.05). This trend was not observed among males.

In terms of pulmonary function, the FVC %pred exhibited only a marginal statistical difference among males (p = 0.05–0.10). Regarding cardiorespiratory metrics, significant differences were observed in females across nearly all parameters, except for VO₂ rest and VO₂/kg peak. In contrast, among males, only the VO₂/kg at rest and VO₂/kg peak showed significant differences between non-overweight and overweight/obese groups (Table 2).

To elucidate the influence of muscular thickness and echogenicity on cardiorespiratory function, we initially investigated the variations in muscle characteristics among the stratified subgroups. All participants included in this analysis were right-hand dominant. In male participants, significant intergroup differences in muscle thickness were observed exclusively on the right side for the SCm, RA, and GA muscles. In contrast, female participants exhibited bilateral differences in SCm RA, and GA thickness between the non-obese and obese subgroups. Regarding muscle echogenicity, as assessed through grayscale analysis, significant differences between non-obese and obese participants were identified solely in females, specifically in the right-sided UTm and bilateral RA muscles (Table 3).

In order to explore the relationship between outdoor physical activity and postural balance, we conducted correlational analyses to examine the associations between the weekly duration and frequency of outdoor activity and lateral asymmetry in muscle characteristics (delta values= right side minus left side) across three distinct subgroups: men with obesity, women with obesity, and women with normal weight. Due to an inadequate sample size, participants with normal weight in the male subgroup (n = 3) were excluded from the analysis (Supplemental File 1).

In individuals with obesity, no significant associations were identified between outdoor activity metrics (either duration or frequency) and indices of muscle asymmetry, regardless of sex (p > 0.05). In contrast, among women of normal weight, the duration of weekly outdoor activity showed a moderate positive correlation with lateral asymmetry in the thickness of the lumbar multifidus (LTm delta value, r = 0.447, p = 0.022) and a moderate negative correlation with lateral asymmetry in the echogenicity of the upper trapezius (UTm grayscale delta value, r = -0.404, p = 0.040). Furthermore, the frequency of weekly outdoor activity was weakly negatively correlated with lateral asymmetry in the echogenicity of the GA grayscale delta value (r = -0.394, p = 0.046).

Discussions

We performed a comparative analysis of cardiorespiratory fitness, outdoor activity levels, and the mass and quantity of respiratory-related muscles between normal-weight and overweight or obese young adults. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, overweight or obese individuals did not demonstrate abnormal ATR compared to their normal-weight peers. Additionally, their muscle thickness was greater than that of normal-weight individuals, although the majority of differential grayscale values suggested more favorable outcomes in the normal-weight group. As expected, normal-weight young adults participated in outdoor activities more frequently and with higher activity frequencies; however, these findings did not reveal a significant correlation with muscle imbalance. Interestingly, in normal-weight females, the frequency and total duration of weekly activities were associated with the mass or quantity of specific muscles.

The concept of metabolically healthy obesity suggests that not all individuals with obesity are at risk of diminished cardiorespiratory fitness. It is possible for cardiorespiratory and metabolic indicators to remain within healthy ranges despite excess body weight [16]. Nonetheless, health-related physical fitness comprises both cardiorespiratory and muscular components [17], with the latter including functional indicators such as muscle strength and endurance. In our study, although individuals with obesity demonstrated higher VO2 peak values, indicating ostensibly superior cardiorespiratory performance, the peak VO2/kg values, when adjusted for body weight, were significantly lower than those observed in individuals of normal weight. As noted by AlGhatrif et al. [18], VO2 peak reflects not only cardiac output but also other contributing factors need to considered.

In our study, specific indicators of cardiorespiratory fitness, such as MVV and HRpeak, exhibited enhanced performance within the obese cohort. In contrast, other parameters, including the percentage of FVC%pred and HRrest, were more favorable among normal-weight young adults. The VE/VCO2 is employed to evaluate cardiorespiratory fitness [19] and is instrumental in assessing cardiopulmonary capacity and prognosis in patients with heart failure [20]. Similar to HRrest, an elevated VE/VCO2 slope is indicative of diminished cardiorespiratory fitness. Contrary to our expectations, in obese women, increased total weekly physical activity was associated with higher resting and peak VE/VCO2 values and lower VO2 peak. These paradoxical findings corroborate previous literature suggesting that obesity does not necessarily equate to reduced cardiorespiratory fitness.

Nonetheless, these discrepancies highlight the critical need to examine muscle health, particularly the respiratory and abdominal/hip muscles, alongside cardiopulmonary function in obese populations. Our study, however, reveals conflicting outcomes between muscle and cardiopulmonary functions. The VO2/kg peak is strongly linked to enhanced cardiorespiratory fitness and cardiovascular health [21]. Additionally, higher VO2 peak values have been associated with increased bone strength in elderly men, indicating that improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness may be positively linked to skeletal health [22]. Contrarily, our study found that VO2 peak values exhibited an inverse relationship with muscle function, specifically in the RA of obese women. Correlation analyses indicated that a higher VO2 peak was associated with diminished muscle function, particularly in the RA. We hypothesize that this finding may be related to the RA’s limited role in cardiopulmonary function. In obese populations, lower-quality RA muscle may be more closely linked to sedentary behavior, which is often accompanied by fat infiltration and a reduction in muscle metabolic capacity. This interpretation is supported by our observation that obese women with higher VO2 peak values participated in less total outdoor activity, implying increased indoor sedentary time. Given the abnormalities in muscle metabolism observed in obese individuals, a comprehensive, multidimensional assessment of these correlations is necessary [23].

The body composition characteristics associated with obesity differ between males and females. Females tend to accumulate more adipose tissue in the abdominal and gluteal regions, which can mechanically stimulate muscle tissue, potentially contributing to muscle fiber hyperplasia and hypertrophy [24]. In our study, both obese males and females demonstrated variations in muscle thickness, particularly in the rectus abdominis (RA) and gluteus maximus (GM), when compared to their non-obese counterparts. Despite the apparent increase in muscle thickness, grayscale assessments indicated that non-obese females exhibited lower grayscale values in the RA, suggesting superior functional performance of the muscle.

Existing research has demonstrated that calorie restriction, when combined with either high-intensity or moderate-intensity exercise, is beneficial for weight loss and fat reduction in obese individuals [25]. However, our discussion section consistently emphasizes that both the weekly volume and frequency of physical activity are markedly lower in overweight or obese populations compared to individuals of normal weight. In a previous study, we observed that young individuals who engage in fewer weekly exercise sessions and have reduced total exercise time are more likely to experience physical functional discomfort [7]. Therefore, we also examined Axial Trunk Rotation (ATR) and muscle balance in these populations, as abnormalities in ATR and imbalances in respiratory muscles may be adversely associated with respiratory function [9, 26]. Contrary to our initial hypotheses, the study found no significant differences in ATR between obese and non-obese young adults. Among non-obese young women, increased outdoor activity was associated with greater muscle imbalance. This observation aligns with previous research indicating that overweight or obese individuals have a significantly lower risk of spinal abnormalities compared to their underweight counterparts [27], a phenomenon thought to be linked to disruptions in muscle balance. Consistent with these findings, our study did not identify significant muscle imbalance within the obese cohort.

While we have consistently highlighted that muscle thickness in obese individuals does not necessarily reflect muscle function, our study found that in obese females, there was a positive correlation between the thickness of the SCm and both FVC and MVV. The SCm function as accessory respiratory muscles, enhancing blood flow during respiration to meet increased ventilatory demands [28]. For example, in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, the SCm can contribute to respiratory muscle training irrespective of diaphragmatic condition. In our study, the SCm thickness tended to be greater in obese individuals compared to their non-obese counterparts, with this difference observed on the right side in males and bilaterally in females. However, grayscale analysis revealed no significant difference in the SCm grayscale between obese and non-obese participants, indicating that muscle thickness, rather than grayscale, may be a more relevant indicator of respiratory function for the SCm.

The trapezius muscle functions as an accessory respiratory muscle, exhibiting electrical activity that can exceed that of the SCM under conditions of increased inspiratory resistance [29]. In our study, the grayscale measurement of the UTm, as opposed to the LTm, demonstrated a negative correlation with HR rest. The UTm plays a significantly greater role in thoracic activity and, consequently, in respiratory function compared to the LTm [30]. Unlike the SCM, whose function is assessed based on thickness, the higher grayscale values of the UTm observed in our study were indicative of enhanced cardiorespiratory fitness.

Limitation

Our study is subject to several limitations, the foremost being the inadequate sample size of non-obese males and obese females. Future investigations should aim to collect samples stratified by gender. Moreover, it is important to acknowledge that physical activity was assessed through self-report, which may introduce potential recall bias and limit the comparability of our results with those obtained via objective measurements. It is also important to consider that obese females who are less inclined to participate may inherently partake in fewer outdoor activities due to their predisposition. Furthermore, the associations observed between outdoor activity and certain cardiopulmonary parameters were somewhat unexpected. Given the complexity of these physiological interactions, the mechanisms we have proposed remain speculative and should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted with significant caution until further research can elucidate the underlying causal pathways. The findings related to muscle characteristics and cardiorespiratory fitness in obese adolescents remain inconclusive, highlighting the need for larger sample sizes to enable a definitive analysis of these conflicting outcomes.

Conclusion

We performed an exploratory comparative analysis of cardiorespiratory fitness, outdoor activity levels, and muscular characteristics between obese or overweight adolescents and their non-obese counterparts. The findings suggest that obese adolescents tend to demonstrate inferior muscle performance and cardiorespiratory function relative to the non-obese group. Notably, certain muscles, particularly the RA, appeared to exhibit a trend towards increased thickness in obese adolescents, yet concurrently presented with elevated grayscale values, potentially suggesting diminished functional performance. In assessing cardiorespiratory function, these initial observations imply that the evaluation of muscle thickness, rather than grayscale values, may be more pertinent for the SCm, similar to the UTm. Additionally, the non-obese cohort seemed to experience a higher prevalence of muscle imbalance, which might be further exacerbated by increased levels of outdoor activity. However, the relationships between outdoor activity and cardiopulmonary parameters were complex and somewhat counterintuitive. We offer potential interpretations for these associations, yet these remain preliminary and warrant further investigation. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, these conclusions should be interpreted with caution, and future longitudinal studies are required to fully understand these relationships and substantiate our initial observations.
