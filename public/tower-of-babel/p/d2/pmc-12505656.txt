A multi-level exploration of the genetic basis between lung cancer and schizophrenia
Authors: LanE Wu, Yunxiang Wu, Yanhua Gan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between schizophrenia (SCZ) and lung cancer (LC) remains inadequately understood, necessitating further exploration through a genetic perspective.MethodsUtilizing extensive genome-wide association study databases, a comprehensive multi-level genetic analytical framework was implemented to examine the genetic association patterns between SCZ and distinct LC subtypes. Initially, genome-wide genetic correlations were assessed through linkage disequilibrium score regression and high-definition likelihood approaches. Subsequently, local variance association analysis (LAVA) was employed to identify key genomic regions exhibiting significant genetic associations. Mendelian randomization (MR) was employed to assess causal effects. Finally, the degree of genetic overlap and shared genetic loci between these diseases were quantitatively evaluated by integrating the conditional/conjunctional false discovery rate methodology.ResultsGenetic correlation analyses demonstrated significant positive associations between SCZ and LC, particularly its squamous cell subtype, at the genome-wide level, whereas no statistically significant associations were detected concerning lung adenocarcinoma or small cell lung cancer. Moreover, LAVA identified distinct genetic association patterns within multiple chromosomal segments. A systematic evaluation incorporating a joint false discovery rate confirmed the existence of genetic overlap phenomena between these diseases and successfully pinpointed multiple shared pathogenic risk loci.ConclusionThis study furnishes novel theoretical evidence regarding the comorbidity mechanisms linking various LC subtypes with SCZ from a genetic perspective, thereby enhancing the comprehension of their intrinsic interconnections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-025-00601-w.

Introduction

In the global malignant tumor spectrum, lung malignancies rank as the second most prevalent, following breast cancer. On a worldwide scale, this disease contributes to approximately 2.2 million newly diagnosed cases annually, with mortality figures reaching 1.8 million [1]. Due to the lack of distinct early symptoms, the majority of patients receive diagnoses at advanced disease stages, leading to a notably poor prognosis, with an overall five-year survival rate of merely 20% [2]. From a histopathological perspective, lung cancer (LC) is categorized into multiple subtypes, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) being the most common subtypes of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which accounts for over 80% of all LC cases, while small cell lung cancer (SCLC) represents a distinct and less prevalent subtype [3]. Although smoking has been widely acknowledged as the primary etiological factor, supporting evidence for additional oncogenic mechanisms remains limited [4]. Recent research has suggested that schizophrenia (SCZ) may share intrinsic pathological links with lung malignancies [5]. As a severe mental disorder marked by profound psychopathological abnormalities, SCZ exhibits a global lifetime prevalence of approximately 1%. Notably, around 5% of affected individuals tend to encounter more complex physical health complications and an elevated risk of comorbid conditions [6]. The substantial morbidity and disability attributed to this disorder contribute to a reduced life expectancy of 10 to 25 years compared to the general population, not only exerting immense psychological and financial strain on patients and their families but also presenting significant challenges to public health systems [7, 8]. However, conflicting research findings have cast doubt on the association between SCZ and LC, highlighting the need for further investigation [9]. The rapid advancement of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) has introduced a novel approach for an in-depth examination of genetic correlations between SCZ and various LC subtypes. By effectively controlling for potential confounding variables, this methodology is anticipated to yield more robust scientific evidence in clarifying the relationship between these two conditions.

Investigating genetic associations between diseases necessitates adherence to rigorous theoretical frameworks within statistical genetics. With the continuous advancement of statistical analysis methodologies, genetic correlations among various complex traits have been effectively deciphered. These methodological improvements have facilitated the identification of intrinsic genetic links between epilepsy and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder [10], provided insights into genetic consistency across diverse diagnostic criteria for depression [11], and revealed shared genetic variants between SCZ and metabolic syndrome [12]. Building upon these analytical advancements, the present study seeks to conduct a comprehensive examination of the potential genetic correlation network structure linking SCZ with various LC subtypes.

This study implements a structured algorithmic framework to investigate two fundamental aspects: first, a comprehensive evaluation of genetic association patterns across diseases, and second, the precise determination of shared genetic susceptibility loci. Within this analytical approach, multiple advanced statistical methodologies have been incorporated. Specifically, linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) [13] and high-dimensional likelihood (HDL) modeling [14] were employed to quantitatively assess genetic correlations on a genome-wide scale, whereas local analysis of variant associations (LAVA) [15] was utilized to conduct an in-depth examination of specific chromosomal regions. Mendelian randomization (MR) was employed to assess causal effects [16]. Furthermore, the conditional/conjunctional false discovery rate (cond/conjFDR) method [17] was applied to reinforce the validation of genetic overlap phenomena and identify comorbidity-associated loci.

Methods and materials

GWAS data

The genetic variation data for LC analyzed in this study were obtained from a large-scale meta-analysis conducted by McKay et al., which integrated datasets from the International Lung Cancer Consortium and OncoArray-TRICL, encompassing genetic information across three primary histological subtypes. Specifically, the analysis incorporated data from LC (29,266 cases, 56,450 controls), LUAD (11,273 cases, 55,483 controls), LUSC (7,426 cases, 55,627 controls), and SCLC (2,664 cases, 21,444 controls) [18] (Table 1). The lung cancer-related data can be downloaded from the GWAS Catalog: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/home. The GWAS data related to SCZ were sourced from an extensive research initiative led by the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC), which aggregated genetic profiles from 52,017 affected individuals and 75,889 healthy controls [19] (Table 1). The SCZ-related data can be downloaded from the IEU database: https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/. To ensure consistency in genetic background, the study populations were limited to individuals of European ancestry. To control the false-positive rate, we applied the Bonferroni correction for the genome-wide genetic correlation analyses conducted using LDSC, MR and HDL. For the LAVA analysis, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR) and identify significant associations. In the condFDR/conjFDR analyses, a significance threshold of conjFDR < 0.05 was adopted, in line with commonly used practices in previous studies [20–23].The research workflow is depicted in Fig. 1.

Global genetic correlation analyses

The specific steps for estimating overall genetic correlation using LDSC (LD Score Regression) are as follows: First, the LDSC software package developed by Bulik-Sullivan et al. is used for analysis [13]. The required input data consists of GWAS summary statistics, including the effect sizes, standard errors, and sample sizes for each SNP, which are used for the analysis of both exposures and outcomes. The LD reference panel typically consists of European population data from the 1000 Genomes Project, which provides linkage disequilibrium (LD) information for SNPs and aids in the estimation of genetic correlations. During data processing, several filtering thresholds are applied, such as a minimum allele frequency (MAF) of greater than 1%, an INFO score greater than 0.9 to ensure data quality, and a P-value threshold (P < 5e-8) to exclude false-positive results. The window size for LD Score calculation is adjusted based on the SNP distance to ensure sufficient LD information is captured. Through these steps, LDSC enables the estimation of genetic correlations between different traits or diseases without relying on individual genotype data, providing a computationally efficient method suitable for large-scale GWAS data in genetic studies.

The HDL method for estimating overall genetic correlation involves the following steps [14]: First, the HDL software package (version 1.0) is used, which estimates genetic correlation based on GWAS summary statistics. The input data includes effect sizes, standard errors, and sample sizes for each SNP, used for both exposure and outcome traits. The LD reference panel is typically derived from large cohorts, such as the 1000 Genomes Project. The filtering thresholds (MAF, INFO score, and P-value) are consistent with those used in LDSC. LD scores are calculated using a 500 SNP window. HDL utilizes full LD information for more precise genetic correlation estimation compared to methods like LDSC, offering computational efficiency suitable for large-scale GWAS analysis.

Local genetic correlation analyses

The LAVA method is designed to estimate local genetic correlations through the following procedures [15]. The analysis is conducted using the LAVA software package, which is specifically developed for local genetic correlation analysis and supports both binary and continuous phenotypes, as well as overlapping samples. The input consists of GWAS summary statistics, including effect sizes, standard errors, and sample sizes for each SNP, with phenotype data being either binary or continuous. LAVA utilizes the European-ancestry reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project to provide LD information between SNPs. During data preprocessing, quality control thresholds for MAF, INFO score, and P-values are set consistently with those used in LDSC to ensure data validity and robustness. In the analytical process, LAVA applies partial and multiple regression models to assess conditional genetic relationships between traits. It enables researchers to identify shared genetic signals and local genetic correlations among multiple phenotypes within user-defined genomic regions. With its flexibility and efficiency, LAVA is particularly well-suited for dissecting complex genetic architectures and local heterogeneity, offering refined insights beyond what can be captured by global genetic correlation analyses, especially in the context of multivariate models.

Mendelian-randomization analysis

This study employed a bidirectional two-sample MR strategy to investigate the potential causal relationships between SCZ and LC, including its histological subtypes. The study design strictly adhered to the three core assumptions of Mendelian Randomization analysis [16]. We performed a genome-wide significance and LD check with the conditions set as “P < 5 × 10–8,” “r2 = 0.001,” and “kbp = 10,000”. To enhance the credibility and statistical robustness of the findings, a series of rigorous sensitivity analyses were conducted, including assessments of horizontal pleiotropy [24, 25], heterogeneity tests [26], and leave-one-out validation [27].

All MR analyses were performed in the R programming environment, primarily utilizing the TwoSampleMR package (available at https://mrcieu.github.io/TwoSampleMR/) and the MR-PRESSO package (accessible at https://github.com/rondolab/MR-PRESSO) for data exploration and causal inference.

CondFDR/ConjFDR analysis

CondFDR and ConjFDR are Bayesian-based statistical methods that enhance the detection of multi-phenotype GWAS signals at the genome-wide level, particularly for identifying loci with shared genetic bases [28]. Researchers start by collecting GWAS summary statistics for the primary and secondary traits, including SNP P-values and effect sizes. The analysis involves matching and quality control of the GWAS data, applying filters such as removing SNPs with MAF < 1%, INFO < 0.9, or missing/zero P-values. SNPs from chromosomes M, Y, and non-autosomal regions are excluded, and the P-value distribution is checked for uniformity. Conditional FDR re-ranking is applied to the primary trait based on the significance of SNPs in the secondary trait. The core of this process is constructing conditional cumulative distribution functions for the primary trait at different levels of the secondary trait. CondFDR outputs conditional FDR values for each SNP, with a threshold of CondFDR < 0.05. ConjFDR integrates the CondFDR values from both traits, with the maximum value defining significance. ConjFDR < 0.05 indicates SNPs that are conditionally associated with both traits, identifying shared genetic variants. This process can be implemented in R, using the “pleioFDR” framework and visualized through quantile-QQ plots. The method is valuable for detecting shared genetic signals even when individual traits do not meet traditional GWAS thresholds, improving biological interpretation and discovery power.

Functional annotation

The genes associated with SNPs identified by the ConjFDR method are functionally annotated using the SNP2Gene module of the FUMA platform (https://fuma.ctglab.nl/) [29]. The analysis is based on linkage disequilibrium data from the European population of the 1000 Genomes Project, with independent significant SNPs selected using the criteria of condFDR < 0.05 and LD r² ≤ 0.6. SNPs with LD r² ≤ 0.1 are defined as independent lead SNPs. Adjacent independent significant SNPs with a physical distance of less than 250 kb are merged into a single genomic risk locus, with the SNP having the smallest P-value designated as the lead SNP. Specifically, GWAS summary statistics are uploaded to the FUMA platform, and the MHC region is excluded in the “5. MHC region” settings within the SNP2GENE module to ensure the reliability of the analysis. Additionally, functional enrichment analysis of shared genes is performed using the GeneMania platform (https://genemania.org/) [30].

Results

Global genetic correlation

The genetic architectural relationship between SCZ and LC spectrum diseases was identified through bivariate LDSC analysis. The findings indicated a significant degree of genetic overlap between SCZ and overall LC (Rg = 0.1461, P = 5.9517e-06). In the analysis of LC subtypes, a genetic correlation between LUSC and SCZ was observed (Rg = −0.1288, P = 0.0084), whereas the associations for LUAD and SCLC did not attain statistical significance (Rg = 0.079, P = 0.0534; Rg = 0.024, P = 0.6259, respectively) (Table 2). The genetic association characteristics of SCZ-LC (Rg = 0.156, P = 4.70e-06) and SCZ-LUSC (Rg = 0.1895, P = 4.14e-03) were further validated through cross-verification using the HDL method (Table 2). These findings furnish empirical support for elucidating the shared genetic foundation of psychiatric disorders and lung malignancies.

Among these, 10 segments displayed positive correlations, predominantly situated on chromosomes 6, 7, 8, 9, 15, 19, and 20, whereas the remaining 21 segments demonstrated negative correlations and were distributed across chromosomes 1, 6, 12, 14, 16, and 18 (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 1).

The association study involving SCZ and LUAD led to the identification of 15 significant segments, comprising 7 positively correlated segments mapped to chromosomes 2, 5, 8, 15, 18, and 20, while 8 negatively correlated segments were located on chromosomes 1, 4, 6, 13, and 14 (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 2).

In the SCZ and LUSC analysis, a total of 32 significant chromosomal segments were detected. Sixteen segments exhibiting positive correlations were found on chromosomes 1, 3, 6, 11, 15, 17, 18, and 19, whereas the remaining 16 negatively correlated segments were concentrated on chromosomes 3, 6, and 13 (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 3).

The exploration of genetic associations between SCZ and SCLC revealed 28 statistically significant segments. Of these, 9 segments displaying positive correlations were distributed across chromosomes 1, 6, 11, 15, 17, and 18, while 17 negatively correlated segments were primarily localized on chromosomes 3, 6, 13, and 16 (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table 4).

Mendelian randomization

The bidirectional MR analyses conducted between SCZ and LC, including its histological subtypes, did not provide evidence supporting a significant causal relationship. Across all seven MR analyses performed (MR analysis was not conducted with SCLC as the exposure due to an insufficient number of instrumental variables), no significant horizontal pleiotropy was detected, indicating the validity of the selected instrumental variables. Moreover, the strength of these instruments was confirmed by F-statistics consistently exceeding the conventional threshold of 10, thereby minimizing the risk of weak instrument bias and reinforcing the robustness of the results (see Supplementary Table 5). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses further demonstrated a relatively concentrated distribution of SNP effects, with no outlier variants identified. Collectively, these findings suggest that the comorbidity observed between SCZ and LC is more likely attributable to shared genetic architecture rather than a direct causal link.

ConjFDR analysis identifies shared genomic loci between two traits

The Q-Q plot analysis identified significant genetic correlations between SCZ, LC, and its subtypes (Fig. 3). The findings suggested that as the association significance of one phenotype increases, the statistical distribution of the other phenotype demonstrates a characteristic leftward deviation. This progressive shift substantiates the presence of shared pathogenic loci between SCZ and LC, offering substantial evidence for unraveling their common molecular genetic mechanisms.

In the subtype analysis, distinct genetic association patterns between SCZ and various LC subtypes were identified. Specifically, SCZ was found to share four genetic loci with LUAD, namely GULOP, MMP16, CHRNA5, and FUT2 (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Table 7). Only a single shared locus, CHRNA5, was detected between SCZ and SCLC (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Table 8). Additionally, nine overlapping genes were observed in the association between SCZ and LUSC, encompassing ST3GAL3, BARHL2, DCAF8, PTK2B, GULOP, FRMD5, MAP2K5, CHRNA5, and ZNRF3 (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Table 9). These findings offer novel insights into the molecular mechanisms linking SCZ and LC.

Discussion

In this study, multiple statistical genetic approaches, including LDSC, HDL, LAVA, MR, and cond/conjFDR, were integrated to investigate the genetic associations between SCZ and LC, along with its subtypes, at both genome-wide and local chromosomal levels. While previous studies have reported general genetic correlations between SCZ and various cancer types, few have systematically combined complementary analytic frameworks to dissect both global and region-specific signals, assess potential causality, and identify pleiotropic loci. Through functional annotation analysis, key shared genes were successfully identified, some of which are involved in neurotransmitter receptor activity, thereby offering novel biological insight into the common genetic mechanisms underlying these two disease categories. This integrative framework not only reinforces prior findings but also contributes new evidence regarding the spatial architecture and potential functional relevance of SCZ–LC genetic overlap.

The research findings indicate that both LC and LUSC exhibit significant genetic correlations with SCZ at both overall and local levels, whereas LUAD and SCLC do not demonstrate substantial overall genetic associations with SCZ. The molecular mechanisms responsible for these differential associations may involve multiple factors. First, GWAS identified several shared susceptibility loci between SCZ and LUSC, particularly in the chromosomal regions 3p21 and 15q25, which are closely linked to neurotransmitter signal transduction and apoptosis regulatory pathways [31]. Transcriptomic studies conducted by Li et al. further confirmed that genes associated with squamous epithelial differentiation, such as TP63 and KRT5/14, were significantly upregulated in the peripheral blood of SCZ patients. These genes also play fundamental roles in the development and progression of LUSC [32]. Second, SCZ-related genetic variants may regulate squamous epithelial differentiation by influencing the NOTCH signaling pathway. Functional genomic analyses by Yotsumoto et al. demonstrated that this pathway undergoes significant alterations in both SCZ and LUSC [33]. In contrast, LUAD and SCLC primarily rely on alternative driver genes and signaling pathways, such as EGFR mutations and ALK rearrangements, which exhibit minimal overlap with the genetic background of SCZ [34]. Additionally, through multi-omics integration analysis, Zuber et al. discovered that gene expression profiles associated with inflammation and immune regulation in SCZ patients more closely resemble those of LUSC rather than other LC subtypes [35].

Large-scale cohort investigations have substantiated that individuals diagnosed with SCZ are at a significantly elevated risk of developing LC compared to the general population. A retrospective cohort study carried out by Bradford et al., encompassing a total of 34,664 LC patients, identified that SCZ patients exhibited notably inferior survival rates following an LC diagnosis. The risk ratio associated with all-cause mortality in SCZ patients was determined to be 1.33 (95% confidence interval [95% CI], 1.22–1.44) [36]. This observation has been further corroborated by subsequent studies. A meta-analysis incorporating data from 16 independent studies indicated that the risk of LC was increased by approximately 70% among SCZ patients (risk ratio = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.51–1.83) [37]. Earlier investigations proposed a lower LC incidence in SCZ patients [38]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis of epidemiological studies conducted by Zhuo et al. suggested that uncertainty remains regarding the precise association between SCZ diagnosis and LC incidence [9]. The characteristics of LUSC in individuals with SCZ have been observed to be distinct. A large-scale retrospective cohort analysis (n = 28,859), utilizing data from the Danish national registry database, established that the incidence of LUSC among SCZ patients was significantly greater than that in the general population (standardized incidence ratio = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.72–2.38), with this difference being particularly pronounced in male patients [39]. Clinical observations have also indicated that LUSC in SCZ patients is frequently diagnosed at more advanced stages and is linked to poorer prognostic outcomes. A multicenter retrospective study demonstrated that these individuals had markedly lower 5-year overall survival rates in comparison to non-SCZ LUSC patients (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.32–1.89) [40]. However, findings from an extensive systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Catalá-López et al., which included 65 studies with a combined sample size exceeding 450,000, revealed that after adjusting for confounding factors such as smoking, SCZ patients exhibited a lower risk of LUSC than the control group (adjusted HR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.71–0.96) [41]. This conclusion was further reinforced by a cohort study conducted by Hodgson et al. (n = 26,996) based on the Finnish national registry database, which reported a standardized incidence ratio of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.76–1.04) for LUSC in SCZ patients, suggesting no statistically significant correlation between these conditions [42]. Given these discrepancies, an early and precise elucidation of the relationship among LC, LUSC, and SCZ remains essential. The present study, utilizing genetic analysis while minimizing the impact of environmental confounders, confirms a substantial genetic correlation and gene overlap among these conditions.

Several shared disease-associated genes identified in this study merit further investigation. KDM4A, a pivotal component of the lysine-specific demethylase family, is implicated in multiple essential processes contributing to LC development and progression. Research conducted by Wang et al. demonstrated that KDM4A exerts an influence on the proliferative and metastatic properties of LC cells through the modulation of histone demethylation [43]. Moreover, KDM4A has been recognized as a potential therapeutic target in LC, participating in DNA damage repair mechanisms, which may be linked to tumor responsiveness to chemotherapeutic interventions [44]. With regard to SCZ, multiple variant loci within the KDM4A gene have exhibited significant associations with SCZ susceptibility [45]. Alterations in its methylation levels are considered one of the fundamental mechanisms by which antipsychotic medications exert their effects [46]. CHRNA5 has been identified as a key factor in LC initiation and progression. A multitude of studies suggest that genetic variations at the CHRNA5 locus are strongly correlated with LC susceptibility. The rs1051730 polymorphism within the CHRNA5-CHRNA3-CHRNB4 gene cluster was first identified by Hung et al. through large-scale GWAS research as being significantly associated with LC risk [47]. This association was subsequently validated by Amos et al. in a study involving 5,739 cases and 5,848 controls, which further established a significant link between this risk allele and both smoking behavior and nicotine dependence [48]. At the molecular level, findings by Wang et al. indicate that CHRNA5 facilitates LC cell proliferation and survival by modulating calcium influx and activating the MAPK signaling pathway [49]. Additionally, research by Krais et al. has revealed that CHRNA5 plays a role in the regulation of nicotine-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition, thereby influencing tumor cell invasiveness and metastatic potential [50]. Studies utilizing animal models have also demonstrated that CHRNA5 affects cognitive functions such as working memory and attention by modulating dopaminergic neuron activity within the prefrontal cortex [51]. The rs16969968 locus of CHRNA5 has been reported to be associated not only with nicotine dependence but also with the severity of cognitive deficits observed in SCZ patients [52]. ADAMTS7, a protease involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, has been shown to enhance tumor cell invasion and metastasis by facilitating the degradation of extracellular matrix components [53]. Recent findings indicate that ADAMTS7 contributes to vascular remodeling, suggesting a potential role in tumor angiogenesis in LC [54]. A systematic review summarizing the diverse roles of ADAMTS family members in tumor progression has provided new insights into the function of ADAMTS7 in LC pathophysiology [55]. Further validation in PGC research has suggested that ADAMTS7 could serve as a potential susceptibility gene for SCZ [37]. Genetic variations in ADAMTS7 may also be implicated in the pathological processes of SCZ by modulating neuroinflammatory responses [32]. MMP16 expression levels have been proposed as potential biomarkers for predicting LUAD patient responses to targeted therapy [56]. Additionally, alterations in MMP16 expression patterns within the prefrontal cortex of SCZ patients suggest a possible involvement in the pathogenesis of the disorder [57]. PTK2B, a critical non-receptor tyrosine kinase, is believed to modulate the immune microenvironment of LUSC by regulating calcium ion signaling pathways [58]. Moreover, PTK2B has been shown to play a role in the regulation of glutamatergic neurotransmission within the prefrontal cortex, where its dysregulated expression may contribute to synaptic dysfunction [59]. Transcriptome analysis has identified a close association between PTK2B expression changes and cognitive deficits in SCZ patients [60]. The activation of the MAP2K5-ERK5 signaling cascade has been demonstrated to enhance the proliferative and invasive capacities of LUSC cells [61]. MAP2K5 has also been implicated in LUSC progression through its regulatory effects on the tumor microenvironment [62]. In the context of SCZ, dysregulation of the MAP2K5 signaling pathway is believed to influence synaptic plasticity and neural circuit development [63], and this gene has additionally been recognized as a susceptibility factor for SCZ in a separate GWAS study [64]. Functional enrichment analysis of the shared genes highlighted the involvement of neurotransmitter receptor activity. These receptors are not only central to the pathophysiology of schizophrenia (SCZ), particularly through disruptions in glutamate, GABA, and dopamine systems [65], but are also implicated in tumor development. In lung cancer (LC), especially small cell lung cancer (SCLC), aberrant expression of neurotransmitter receptors has been reported, suggesting their role in regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, and the tumor microenvironment [66].

This study has several important limitations. First, residual LD between genetic loci may compromise the accuracy of genetic correlation estimates. Second, potential sample overlap across multiple GWAS datasets may introduce statistical bias. Third, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the population specificity of the included cohorts, which were primarily derived from individuals of a particular ancestry. In addition, the mechanistic pathways linking SCZ and LC remain largely unclear. Although our integrative analyses revealed shared genetic architecture and pleiotropic loci between the two disorders, the biological functions and causal mechanisms underlying these overlaps are not yet fully understood. It also remains uncertain to what extent these genetic associations are independent of environmental influences—especially smoking, which is a major risk factor for LC. The potential confounding effect of smoking warrants further investigation and careful adjustment in future studies.Moreover, the clinical translatability of our findings presents additional challenges. While the identification of shared loci may offer promising insights for early screening, risk stratification, or disease subtyping, the practical application of these findings in precision medicine remains preliminary and exploratory. Future research should aim to integrate large-scale GWAS data from diverse ethnic populations to conduct more representative meta-analyses. Establishing independent replication cohorts and further dissecting the genetic interactions between SCZ and LC will also be essential to enhance the robustness and biological interpretation of the findings. Finally, functional validation studies—including in vitro and in vivo experiments targeting prioritized loci, eQTL colocalization analyses, and tissue-specific expression profiling—will be critical for elucidating the biological relevance and potential pathogenic mechanisms involved.

Conclusion

Gene association analysis identified a significant genetic correlation between SCZ and LC, as well as LUSC. Localized genetic association patterns shared by these diseases were detected across multiple chromosomal regions, and key overlapping genetic loci were validated. These genetic findings have laid an initial foundation for further elucidation of the comorbidity mechanisms between these conditions and the advancement of precision treatment strategies.
