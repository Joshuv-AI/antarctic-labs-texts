Barriers to integrating evidence-based practices into intrapartum care during vaginal births: a descriptive qualitative study in Sri Lanka
Authors: Thilini Kumari Weerasingha, Chathura Ratnayake, Abey Rathnayake, Sampath U B Tennakoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study intended to investigate barriers to implementing evidence-based intrapartum care during vaginal births, from maternity care providers’ point of view.DesignA descriptive qualitative study was conducted using in-depth interviews, with data analysed through thematic analysis.SettingThe labour room of a major tertiary care hospital in Central Sri Lanka.ParticipantsPurposively selected 17 maternity care providers including doctors, nurse managers, nurse-midwives and midwives.ResultsThree major themes and twelve sub-themes were generated: (1) barriers related to care providers (lack of human resources, negative attitudes of care providers, poor relationship among care providers, poor relationship between women and care providers, lack of knowledge on evidence-based practice in childbirth care); (2) barriers related to organisational environment (gaps in management, heavy workload, inadequate physical resources, insufficient in-service training and lack of availability/use of updated guidelines) and (3) barriers related to women’s birth preparedness (women’s limited knowledge on childbirth and intrapartum practices and women’s limited engagement during labour and childbirth). Many maternity care providers perceived that prevailing challenges to implement evidence-based childbirth care were one of the major reasons that impacted the quality of current childbirth care in the labour room.ConclusionsThe findings showed that an integrative approach may be essential to address the diverse barriers to the implementation of evidence-based intrapartum care. It is necessary to engage healthcare administrators, healthcare professionals and care recipients to enhance the quality of current childbirth care in the setting through the successful implementation of evidence-based care.

Introduction

Improving the health of mothers and newborns continues to be a global challenge. Evidence-based practice (EBP) stands as a vital tool for improving the quality of maternal and newborn care.13 EBPs are informed by the best available research evidence and are used to guide healthcare decisions and clinical practice.2 4 In recent years, the WHO has advocated for the systematic adoption of evidence-based intrapartum practices and the replacement of ineffective or harmful practices with those supported by the best available evidence.2 5 6 Two entities that reinforce the application of evidence-based intrapartum care are the provision of ‘recommended practices’ and the prevention of ‘not-recommended practices’. Recommended practices have evidence of effectiveness and usually promote physiological birth. Not-recommended practices are often invasive medical interventions which proved to be harmful or ineffective, if provided in a routine manner.2 7

The key WHO-recommended intrapartum practices include labour companionship, continuity of care, effective information sharing and communication, ensuring privacy, encouraging mobility and upright positions, epidural analgesia, correct pushing techniques, skin-to-skin contact, early initiation of breastfeeding and interventions to prevent perineal trauma and postpartum haemorrhage. Equally important to evidence-based intrapartum care is the avoidance of practices not recommended by the WHO, such as continuous cardiotocography (CTG) monitoring, routine amniotomy, fundal pressure and routine episiotomy.2 5 Timely and satisfactory use of evidence-based intrapartum practices can improve maternal, foetal and neonatal outcomes, encourage respectful and effective care and aid health professionals’ and women’s decisions.4 However, a strong body of literature demonstrates that perceived barriers to employing EBP in intrapartum care have been debated from diverse perspectives, including the institutional rules and policies,1818 the knowledge and attitude of care providers1813 17 18 and different maternity care models.12 19 20

Many healthcare providers perceived institutional policies, human and physical resources and leadership style as the organisational factors influencing providers’ attitudes towards adopting evidence-based childbirth care.14 16 17 19 21 Poor working/staffing conditions such as unsatisfactory job descriptions, low remunerations, heavy workload, shift work and staff turnover appeared to influence the implementation of evidence-based care in Turkish,1 Australian,19 Ethiopian,10 Gambian8 and Palestinian15 maternity settings. Lack of physical resources, guidelines and funding has been reported to act as barriers to adopting evidence-based intrapartum care in healthcare settings in Iran,9 Latin America,17 Australia,19 Ethiopia10 and Gambia.8 Poor communication and attitudes have been identified as key obstacles impacting the effective implementation of EBP in Iranian,9 Australian19 and Gambian8 maternity settings. Gaps in providers’ knowledge and skills have been identified as barriers in maternity settings in Iran,9 Latin America17 and Ethiopia,10 while lack of supervision and professional support has been reported in Palestinian settings.15 Lack of autonomy and indignities in the working place were reported as barriers in Iranian9 and Palestinian15 maternity settings.

The evidence on the barriers and challenges for adopting EBP in intrapartum care is scant in the Sri Lankan context. However, a study by Senanayake et al22 found that 58.8% consultant obstetricians opposed labour companionship due to lack of privacy, inadequate space, lack of resources, heavy workload and possible disruptions to patient care by the companion. Identifying barriers to evidence-based intrapartum care is crucial to inform policymakers, develop targeted strategies and enhance maternity care quality through a participatory, evidence-informed approach. However, the execution of evidence-based intrapartum care is a multifaceted and intricate process. Identifying the potential barriers is crucial as the initial step to devise and implement effective strategies to foster evidence-based obstetric care.9 Therefore, this study aimed to explore the barriers to implementing EBP in intrapartum care at the labour room of Teaching Hospital Peradeniya, in Central Sri Lanka, from the perspective of maternity care providers. Even though published statistics pertaining to the number of deliveries in Teaching Hospital Peradeniya are not available, hospital statistics indicate approximately 600 births per month and 6000–7000 births annually. This reflects the substantial volume of intrapartum services in the setting and emphasises the importance of understanding context-specific barriers to the implementation of EBPs in this high-volume setting. In this study, WHO recommendations for high-quality, evidence-based intrapartum care were used as the benchmark for interviewing care providers.5

Materials and methods

Study design and study setting

A descriptive qualitative study was conducted during August 2021–January 2022 at the labour room in Teaching Hospital Peradeniya. Teaching Hospital Peradeniya is a major tertiary care hospital located in urban Central Sri Lanka that provides specialised services in a wide range of medical disciplines including Obstetrics and Gynaecology. The hospital’s obstetric unit, staffed by at least seven obstetricians at a given time, manages approximately 6000–7000 births annually. The obstetric unit comprises antenatal wards, a labour room, postnatal wards, operating theatre facilities and neonatal intensive care services. The labour room is a physician-led unit in which the care decisions for both low- and high-risk mothers are made by the obstetricians, whereas the deliveries, the immediate newborn care and the postpartum care of women are mainly provided by the midwives and the nurse-midwives.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Participant recruitment

Seventeen maternity care providers (one nurse-manager, two obstetric physicians, six midwives and eight nurse-midwives) with different educational qualifications, professional roles and years of experience were recruited using purposive sampling. Consultant obstetricians (also referred to as ‘consultants’ in the quotations) were not included in the sample due to competing clinical commitments and time constraints that precluded scheduling interviews. However, two obstetric physicians who were involved in intrapartum clinical decision-making participated. Additionally, the nurse manager was included to obtain supervisory and decision-making perspectives. Consequently, the final sample predominantly comprised nursing and midwifery care providers, the main workforce delivering day-to-day, direct labour and birth care in Sri Lankan maternity settings.

The initial four interviews with midwives and nurse-midwives guided further sampling to recruit obstetric physicians and nurse managers to address emerging questions beyond direct care providers. Care providers (nurse managers, nurse-midwives, midwives and obstetric physicians) in the labour room with >1 year experience in delivery care were included and care providers on leave or recently returned (within 1 week) from extended leave (eg, maternity leave) were excluded from the study. The sample size was guided by data saturation, which occurred when no new codes or categories were identified from the final two interviews.23

Data collection tools

Published literature related to exploring care providers’ perceptions of the barriers/challenges to implementing EBP in intrapartum care in Sri Lankan healthcare settings was not available. Therefore, in-depth interviews were used as they reveal valuable insights and allow the researcher to discover ‘the true story’ from those with firsthand knowledge.24 A semi-structured interview guide was used which comprised open-ended questions along with supplementary probing questions to explore access to guidelines, care providers’ knowledge, challenges to adopting EBP and their suggestions for improvement (online supplemental file 1). All the interviews were conducted in ‘Sinhala’, the native language of the participants. The tool was checked by the qualitative and subject experts in the research team for content validity and modifications were made accordingly.

Two pilot interviews were conducted with two nurse-midwives in a different healthcare setting to assess the clarity of the questions, interview length and feasibility.25 The recordings were listened to and reviewed by the first author to refine the interviewing technique. While the main structure of the interview guide remains unchanged, several decisions were made to select quieter venues and improve probing and direction during interviews to enhance data quality.

Data collection procedure

Interviews were conducted in a quiet area in the labour room by the first author when participants were free to talk. The written informed consent was obtained from participants after stating the purpose and the steps of the interview process. Each interview commenced with an informal introductory talk, followed by topic-related questions and probing to explore key issues in depth. The interview duration lasted between 40 minutes and 105 minutes.

Seventeen face-to-face interviews were conducted on an individual basis until the data saturation point. Data saturation was reached at the 15th interview, and two further interviews were conducted to confirm that no new codes or concepts were generated from them. A number was given to each participant to pseudonymise both individual interviews and transcripts. Recorded data were kept confidential, with access limited to the research team. Hard copies were securely stored, and electronic data were password-protected.

Data analysis

Recordings were transcribed verbatim, cross-checked for accuracy and de-identified to remove all personal information. Two researchers (including the first author) independently coded each transcript and identified themes and sub-themes through an inductive approach following the framework outlined by Braun and Clarke.26 Themes and sub-themes were assessed for similarities and differences between two researchers, which then were discussed and confirmed by other authors. Data was analysed in the local dialect, minimising data loss during translation. Selected quotations were translated into English by the first author (a native bilingual speaker) and then reviewed for accuracy by another native bilingual speaker. Although formal back-translation was not conducted for all quotations, a randomly selected subset was back-translated by the first author to verify the accuracy and consistency of the translations.

Quality assurance

Two researchers independently analysed data and resolved disagreements by consensus, enhancing credibility. In addition, member checking with three participants validated the findings. A qualitative expert reviewed the coded transcripts and data analysis process for accuracy, affirming the dependability and confirmability. Further, the first author documented detailed information on data collection and analysis steps, including theme development. Transferability was ensured through detailed accounts of the study design and analysis, referring to relevant literature. Moreover, purposive sampling captured diverse participant backgrounds, opinions and perceptions to enrich data.

Major themes and sub-themes generated during the thematic analysis

EBP, evidence-based practice.

Barriers related to care providers

This theme captures the various challenges related to the quantity and quality of maternity care providers that affect the implementation of best intrapartum practices in the study setting. Barriers related to care providers are discussed under five discrete sub-themes highlighting the diversity and complexity of obstacles that influence care delivery.

Lack of human resources

Staff shortage, especially of midwives, was the main barrier noted. Inadequate staffing led to heavy workloads for single midwives, affecting safe, effective and timely childbirth care.

There is a huge staff shortage. For example, five mothers are allocated for one midwife, and she must look after all of them at the same time. And, if an emergency cesarean section happens, she needs to attend to that as well. Sometimes, things get delayed. Sometimes, two deliveries happen at the same time. So, she fails to attend to both deliveries at once. (HCW 04, midwife)

Not only midwives, but there is also a shortage of medical professionals as well. Care providers also reported the difficulties they face due to the lack of a dedicated labour room doctor.

Oftentimes, we fail to find a doctor for episiotomy suturing. Sometimes, episiotomies are left unsutured for long periods due to the absence of a doctor in the labour room. Sometimes, medical students suture episiotomies which lead to bleeding as they lack enough experiences. (HCW 10, nurse-midwife)

As the care providers perceive, there was a significant staff shortage of midwives and doctors that negatively influenced the effective and safe delivery of intrapartum care to the labouring women.

Negative attitudes of care providers

The maternity care providers should generally have good and positive attitudes towards peer workers, labouring women and the care they render to these women. However, some participants noted poor attitudes and resistance to change as key barriers to implementing EBPs.

Once, we received training from a foreign team on best practices for PPH (postpartum haemorrhage) management. When we tried to share that knowledge among staff, the majority resisted saying “We can’t do additional things, we already have enough work to do”. That was their attitude towards implementing good practices. (HCW 08, nurse-midwife)

Similarly, the nurse manager emphasised the care providers’ inattentive attitude towards protecting the mother’s privacy throughout the labour and delivery.

Sometimes I have to remind them to draw curtains. If I do feel the need to protect women’s privacy, why don’t they? That’s why I believe their attitude is the issue. (HCW 14, nurse manager)

Poor relationship among care providers

Poor relationships and lack of mutual support among care providers were identified as negatively influencing the implementation of EBP in the maternity setting.

Sometimes, no minor staff member comes to support us with basic tasks like providing a dustbin or a spotlight during deliveries, leaving us to manage everything alone. (HCW 15, midwife)

This experience was mirrored in another account by a midwife, who emphasised that lack of peer support compromises patient care, as no one takes responsibility during their absence, which badly affects newborn care.

Only two midwives work during the night shifts. If one rests, and the other attends caesarean surgery in the operation theater, no one gives the baby to the mother for breastfeeding, until that midwife comes back from the theatre, or the other returns after resting. So, the baby is not breastfed for two or three hours until a midwife comes to do so. (HCW 09, midwife)

Further highlighting this issue, some midwives expressed feeling neglected as some care provider groups do not acknowledge their opinions and feedback related to patient care.

Sometimes, we feel neglected. When we are trying to express our opinion or discuss something related to patient care, they don’t even listen to us. So, I keep myself quiet, even if I want to discuss an issue related to patient care (HCW 09, midwife)

Collectively, these accounts demonstrated how dysfunctional relationships between care provider groups have adversely affected the quality of care provided to women and the overall atmosphere of the labour room.

Poor relationship between women and care providers

The relationship between care providers and women was ineffective in terms of adequate and proper communication and interaction patterns. Many care providers appeared to deliver care interventions without engaging in direct verbal communication with the women.

Sometimes, we haven’t talked to some mothers until the delivery, either we were busy, or we missed talking. But I think we need to frequently communicate with mothers to identify their needs. (HCW 07, midwife)

Supporting the above statement, another participant stated how language has become a major barrier to the relationship between mothers and care providers. Language barriers, especially with Tamil and Muslim mothers whose mother tongue is not Sinhala, hindered effective communication and made relationships with primarily Sinhala-speaking care providers more challenging.

We often face difficulties when communicating with Tamil and Muslim mothers, who don’t understand Sinhala or English. Some mothers can’t understand a single Sinhala word. Sometimes we use gestures and signs to communicate. It’s our weakness for not learning Tamil, and it is unfair to mothers as we fail to understand their needs during labour. (HCW 15, nurse-midwife)

These narratives emphasised the importance of fostering mutual relationships between mothers and care providers through effective and culturally sensitive communication to provide better care.

Lack of knowledge on EBP in childbirth care

Many participants were unfamiliar with evidence-based intrapartum care and unclear about routine practices or updated clinical guidelines, which appeared to be a major barrier to adopting EBPs.

I think evidence-based care is like what we practice here. I don’t know about the updates, but as far as I know, the partograph we use is based on WHO guidelines. If any update comes in partograph, we adapt it accordingly. (HCW 02, nurse-midwife)

This subtheme was further strengthened by another account highlighting care providers’ limited knowledge and unfamiliarity with best intrapartum practices regarding the use of CTG for assessing foetal well-being.

Midwives constantly contact us when a baby’s FHS (foetal heart sound) drops or variability decreases. So, usually, we order continuous CTG monitoring for every mother as it helps quickly identify foetal distress. (HCW 16, obstetric physician)

Further emphasising this barrier, care providers demonstrated that they were not up to date with the latest WHO recommendations on intrapartum care.

According to our ward policy, we perform an ARM (artificial rupture of membranes) on every mother, including those on spontaneous labour, to speed up the delivery. (HCW 16, obstetric physician)

We give episiotomies to all primi mothers and as needed for multiparas. My opinion is to give an episiotomy to everyone. So, we can reduce the number of cervical and perineal tears. (HCW 09, midwife)

Taken together, these accounts highlighted that limited knowledge has restricted the real-world application of EBP in certain procedures like assessing foetal well-being, amniotomy and episiotomy, which stress the need for care providers to be updated with contemporary knowledge.

Barriers related to the organisational environment

This theme reflects the structural and systemic factors within the healthcare setting that impede the delivery of best intrapartum practices. It highlights how aspects of the organisational environment such as administration, workload, training opportunities, infrastructure, availability of equipment, guidelines, policies and institutional support have influenced the quality of care provided to labouring women.

Gaps in management

‘Gaps in management’ arose from poor supervision, inadequate administrative support and lack of appreciation, with many noting inadequate supervision in labour-room care.

Consultants do not often visit the labour room. If someone supervises us, we will be motivated to work and will not practice wrongly. But no one supervises our work here or tells whether we are doing wrong. (HCW 11, nurse-midwife)

Another participant expressed a similar view, stating that the labour room lacked organisational support, with an inadequate supply of linen and delayed replacement of old equipment.

We don’t receive enough linen from the hospital. These linens are very old and torn. Replacing old equipment also doesn’t happen regularly. (HCW 03, nurse-midwife)

Some participants felt unappreciated, which demotivated them from providing better care for women.

We handle around 600 deliveries per month with no major complications. Some days, we’re so busy we can’t even drink a sip of water, and despite our hard work, we receive no appreciation, leaving us unmotivated, and feeling undervalued. (HCW 03, nurse-midwife)

Further emphasising the pitfalls of the healthcare system, the nurse manager highlighted the deficit of an effective feedback system in the maternity section.

There is no feedback system to guide our practice or to discuss adverse events. At least we should discuss what happened and how to avoid adverse events in future. It’s a big drawback of our system. (HCW 14, nurse manager)

Adding depth to the theme, a nurse-midwife stressed that they lacked authority to make care decisions, which hindered their ability to change existing practices as it was a doctor-led labour room.

Though we need to change the practice, we do not have the authority. If a change is needed, that should be made by a consultant. Even to use an updated guideline, a consultant should order us to follow that in practice. (HCW 10, nurse-midwife)

These accounts consistently demonstrated how institutional and health system drawbacks have influenced the effective provision of evidence-based care to labouring women.

Heavy workload

Some care providers highlighted that the quality of intrapartum care is affected by the increased workload in the labour room.

When the patient load increases, our workload also increases. So, we rush and do the work without considering much about quality. For example, even though catheterisation is a sterile procedure, we do it without considering much of the sterility. (HCW 09, midwife)

Consistent with the above statement, another participant described that incomplete and inadequate intrapartum care due to high workload can cause injustice to women.

Sometimes, we run to the next delivery without completing the postpartum care and the immediate newborn care of the previous delivery. We have nothing to do with it. When we are busy, it is truly unfair to mothers. (HCW 01, midwife)

This view was echoed by several participants, including one who remarked that the unbearable workload was a cause of stress and anger. Also, care providers failed to pay individual courtesy to women, despite the maternal pleas due to increased workload.

I feel so stressed and sometimes angry when the workload is unbearable. Sometimes mothers plead, “please, miss, come to me”. With the huge workload, we can't be around so often. So, I say, “mom, you are not the only one, there are many moms to be taken care of. I want to see them too”. (HCW 12, midwife)

The above narrations suggested that the quality of care is immensely affected by the high workload in the labour room which prevents the care providers from adopting best practices even if they want to do so.

Inadequate physical resources

Lack of physical resources and infrastructure was perceived by many participants as a barrier to delivering proper intrapartum care. Few midwives described the challenges faced by postpartum women and themselves due to the insufficient availability and poor conditions of delivery beds in the labour room.

We don’t have enough beds. Sometimes, we have to transfer postpartum mothers to trolleys to give a bed to a new mother. It’s very uncomfortable and stressful for them. (HCW 01, midwife)

There’s a small bed in the labour room. Even a normal person can’t lie on that bed. Then, how a pregnant woman can? Sometimes, perineal tears occur during deliveries even if we provide adequate perineal support, because of the inadequate space and poor conditions of that bed. (HCW 09, midwife)

This perspective was mirrored in another account, in which a participant identified the poor condition of available delivery beds as a daily challenge, noting that many of the beds did not meet required standards.

Side rails and locks are broken in some beds. So, the beds move back and forth during deliveries. Some beds can’t be adjusted as their control panels are broken. So, we can’t prop up the beds or adjust their height when a mother requests. (HCW 15, nurse-midwife)

Further strengthening this theme, many care providers affirmed the inadequacy and malfunctioning of machines and equipment in the labour room as a barrier to providing evidence-based care to women.

We lack cardiac monitors and CTG machines. We move them from bed to bed whenever we measure maternal and foetal wellbeing. Some of these machines are already exhausted as they are continuously working for 24 hours. Sometimes, they give false readings. (HCW 05, nurse-midwife)

Episiotomy scissors and cord scissors do not cut properly. Most of the scissors are blunt so it causes severe pain and tears to mothers. The artery forceps also do not work properly and fail to clamp effectively. (HCW 02, nurse-midwife)

Moreover, participants perceived that inadequate space in the labour room hindered the presence of labour companions, as women’s privacy could not be ensured without separate or partitioned bed units.

We don’t have adequate space and separate or partitioned bed units in our labour room to keep labour companions. Even if we put the privacy curtains, labour companions can see what’s happening in the next bed. (HCW 13, nurse-midwife)

Insufficient in-service training

Most care providers emphasised the need for regular in-service training, noting its absence as a barrier to applying evidence-based intrapartum care in labour rooms.

Currently, no in-service training is happening. Maternity care practices change and getting updated over time with research findings. But we do not receive new knowledge through training and still practice what we learnt a long time ago (HCW 14, nurse manager)

Confirming the above testimonial, another participant highlighted the less frequent nature of such training and inconsistencies of those trainings.

The last training I attended was about two years ago and it was on emergency obstetric care. That is also 18 years after I came to the labour room…means I hadn’t attended any training on emergency obstetric care for 18 years. (HCW 03, nurse-midwife)

Building on this point, another participant elaborated the lack of opportunity to attend in-service training as another limitation, stressing the need to increase the frequency of such training, enabling all care providers to effectively participate.

I have attended three or four in-service training courses in the past, but only one or two got the opportunity each time as the others had to work here. So, the best thing is to increase the frequency of training so that all can participate several times within a year (HCW 13, nurse-midwife)

Collectively, the above narrations demonstrated the importance of and need for regular and frequent in-service training programmes for all labour room care provider groups, to align practice with the best available evidence.

Lack of availability/use of updated guidelines

It was evident that the lack of availability or use of updated guidelines was a major barrier to improving existing practice. Current intrapartum care largely followed consultant obstetricians’ plans, rather than a common protocol.

Patient management in our labour room is merely based on the consultant’s plan. Besides that, we haven’t received any guidelines recently to follow. There are few guidelines in the labour room which were received around 2002, but I don’t know whether the Ministry of Health issued any labour care guidelines after that. (HCW 14, nurse manager)

Mostly I learn from practice and from my seniors. I follow them during patient management. Other than that, I don’t really refer to guidelines when managing patients. (HCW 16, obstetric physician)

Echoing this perception, care providers emphasised the need for a common intrapartum care guideline, noting difficulties faced due to inconsistent practices and lack of awareness of evidence-based care.

Doctors change practices from time to time without explanations and we follow without knowing why. When a certain care practice is questioned by someone, we have no answers. But, if we have a common guideline, it will ensure consistency of practice, reduce errors, and make our work easier. (HCW 12, midwife)

Barriers related to women’s birth preparedness

This theme reflects maternity care providers’ perceptions of how women’s level of birth preparedness, as observed in clinical practice, influenced intrapartum care practices. The findings describe providers’ experiences of challenges that arose from women’s limited awareness regarding intrapartum practices and varying levels of engagement during labour, and how these barriers were perceived to constrain the delivery of optimal care.

Women’s limited knowledge on childbirth and intrapartum practices

Care providers perceived that some labouring women had limited awareness of labour pain during vaginal birth and of care interventions in the labour room. An obstetric physician perceived that this inadequate awareness influenced women to request unnecessary obstetric interventions.

Even though many women including primi mothers are prepared and preferred for vaginal birth, they don’t seem to have a prior knowledge on the severity and duration of pain during vaginal birth. That’s why they are crying and begging for a caesarean section when they are in labour room for the delivery. (HCW 17, obstetric physician)

Another narration by a nurse-midwife described challenges encountered due to limited maternal awareness of delivery positions and correct pushing techniques.

Mothers are not aware of the delivery position. After coming to the labour room only, they get to know how to grab and hold the legs. And they don’t know when to push and how to push. Sometimes, mothers start pushing early regardless of our instructions which causes tears at the end (HCW 08, nurse-midwife)

Supporting the above observations, another participant highlighted that limited participation in antenatal education sessions was perceived to be associated with reduced understanding of intrapartum practices, which, in their view, hindered the implementation of certain best practices during labour.

Some mothers know nothing about labour room procedures. Probably they might not have participated in antenatal education sessions in the field. Many women don’t know about epidural analgesia and refuse to have it despite our explanations of its efficacy in relieving labour pain. (HCW 10, nurse-midwife)

Overall, these accounts highlighted the importance of women’s prior awareness in supporting best intrapartum care. Care providers perceived that women’s limited understanding of intrapartum care interventions was associated with inadequate participation in antenatal awareness sessions. They believed this, in turn, influenced women’s acceptance of certain intrapartum practices and affected the delivery of optimal care.

Women’s limited engagement during labour and childbirth

Some providers described challenges in implementing intrapartum interventions when women were perceived as reluctant or resistant to care interventions. Limited engagement of women was viewed as complicating the delivery of care and, in some instances, causing both maternal and neonatal complications.

Some women are very uncooperative and never listen to what we say. Some mothers get tears during episiotomy when they resist the procedure. (HCW 06, nurse-midwife)

Supporting the above statement, another participant explained how women’s non-adherence to effective pushing instructions from care providers contributed to adverse delivery outcomes.

Some women don’t push when the time comes despite our instructions and encouragement, so the baby doesn’t cry when born. And these babies may have complications following birth due to the lack of maternal cooperation during delivery. (HCW 12, midwife)

Some care providers perceived that certain women lacked psychological preparedness for vaginal birth, which in turn limited their engagement with and support for a physiological birthing process.

Even though many women are physically prepared for childbirth, they lack mental preparation. They haven’t prepared their mind for a vaginal birth. So, they don’t support any care intervention repeatedly requesting caesarean sections saying they can’t go through it. (HCW 08, nurse-midwife)

Collectively, the above narrations suggested that, from the care providers’ perspective, limitations of women’s engagement in care interventions during labour and childbirth affected the delivery of optimal care.

Discussion

The present study identified several important barriers that hindered the provision of evidence-based intrapartum care. Those barriers were complex and existed at various levels.

Barriers related to the care providers

Staff shortage, negative attitudes, poor professional relationships, poor relationship with women and lack of knowledge hindered care providers from adopting EBP. The staff shortage was raised by many participants in line with the previous studies where the adoption of evidence-based childbirth care guidelines was impeded by the inadequacy of staff.8 14 27 Care providers need to be encouraged and trained to adapt the latest evidence into their routine practice.9 Nevertheless, the current study revealed care providers’ resistance and poor attitudes towards changing the practice as a major barrier, in agreement with studies by Turan et al,1 Sambou,8 Iravani et al,9 Belizan et al17 and Raghunathan et al.27

A collaborative rapport between maternity practitioners at every tier is crucial to the effective application of EBP in maternity care.9 Some study participants reported negligence, discrimination and lack of mutual support, which have led to poor professional relationships, which in turn affected the quality of care. Similar findings were reported by Hassan-Bitar and Narrainen,15 where the Palestinian midwives were humiliated and shouted at by other care provider groups whenever they failed to comply with the ward policies at work. Furthermore, a poor relationship between care providers and women was found to be another significant challenge to adopting EBPs in line with previous studies in Gambia,8 Iran,9 Ethiopia10 and Malawi.28 Effective communication between care providers and women makes childbirth a positive experience for women, creating trustworthy relationships.9 Recent research studies indicate that some women do not access available intrapartum services due to unfavourable behaviours and negative attitudes of staff.29 Many participants identified the shortfall of receiving up-to-date knowledge as a major barrier to implementing EBP, parallel to the findings of previous studies.9 10 17 27 29 Skilled birth attendance and essential evidence-based intrapartum care are important strategies to decrease maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality.9

Barriers related to the organisational environment

Gaps in administration, heavy workload, lack of physical resources and training opportunities and lack of updated guidelines were identified as organisational barriers which impede the adoption of evidence-based care. Care providers highlighted inadequate supervision, poor administrative support and lack of appreciation as the gaps in administration. Similarly, lack of incentive schemes and poor retention rates were identified as barriers to the embracing of evidence-based maternity care in Latin American healthcare settings.17 Poor compensation, suboptimal supervision and inadequate professional support were the major barriers in Palestinian maternity care settings.15 Iravani et al9 also described the administrators’ resistance and insufficient clinical supervision as major challenges to implementing evidence-based childbirth care in Iran.

Appropriate management and effective feedback are fundamental to providing high-quality and safe care in the health setup.9 Care providers reported the lack of a feedback system to audit, guide or provide feedback on care provision and adverse events. Correspondingly, a Palestinian study reported a lack of official meetings to audit work practices.15 Another shortfall identified was the lack of power to make care decisions by nurses and midwives. Their ability to adopt EBP is unwaveringly related to the authority of obstetricians. Congruently, the Iranian midwives’ capacity to use EBP was influenced by the authority of obstetricians.9

Furthermore, participants in our study viewed their workload as a source of stress and anger when it became overwhelming. Also, increased workload in the study setting has affected the quality of patient care as some improper and harmful practices happen with increased workload. These findings align with the findings of a Malawian study that reported that women are sometimes unavoidably left alone due to heavy workload which, in turn, results in stress, fatigue and frustration among healthcare providers.28 Similarly, high workload and shortage of staff were considered as restrictive factors to apply evidence-based care in Iranian maternity care settings.9 Recruiting more midwives would ensure continuous care for all labouring women and enhance client safety by reducing unnecessary obstetric interventions. Furthermore, few studies identified work-related burnout10 15 among maternity care providers parallel to our study.

The lack of physical resources was mentioned by many care providers to affect the delivery of high-quality care. They mentioned the shortage of standard delivery beds as some beds were in poor condition with broken control panels, side rails and locks. They also mentioned the scarcity and poor conditions of cardiac monitors, CTG machines, episiotomy/cord scissors and artery forceps in the labour room. An Ethiopian study identified the unavailability of essential medicine and supplies as organisational barriers in line with our study findings.10 Similarly, Iravani et al9 reported that using old or broken instruments in the labour room is problematic in care provision in Iranian maternity care settings. A shortage of beds to accommodate all expectant women was reported in a Malawi study by Machira and Palamuleni.30 Moreover, barriers such as limited access to information, lack of medical supplies and clear guidelines were reported by Belizan et al17 strengthening the current study findings.

The most noteworthy barriers to the implementation of the EBP reported in other studies appeared to be lack of knowledge of the EBPs and the inadequacy of training.1 The participants in the current study also highlighted the lack of in-service training and the unavailability of latest guidelines to update their knowledge in line with the findings of Oliveira et al.29 Similarly, an Ethiopian study also reported that the care providers did not adopt EBP because of lack of updated books, protocols, guidelines, research articles and proper training.31 Turan et al1 also emphasised the need to update the Turkish maternity care workers about the up-to-date evidence regarding childbirth practices. They recommended incorporating the literature and principles of EBP into medical education, in-service training and professional meetings, in addition to improving access to EBP literature online and professional journals. It is essential that such information be made available in the current setting also, to be reached by all levels of care providers.

Barriers related to women’s birth preparedness

This theme comprises provider-perceived barriers related to women’s limited knowledge of childbirth and limited engagement during labour and childbirth, reflecting how inadequate birth preparation was perceived to challenge the delivery of evidence-based care. Many care providers believed pregnant women’s low participation in the antenatal classes may have contributed to their lack of awareness about childbirth and care which, in turn, affected the quality of care they deliver. However, these women’s limited awareness may also have been influenced by structural and systemic factors within antenatal services, including inefficiencies in the delivery of information and a lack of culturally appropriate antenatal education. A review by Oliveira et al29 also reported that the level of awareness and beliefs of pregnant women can be obstacles that impede the quality care provision during labour and childbirth.

Participants in this study noted that women’s limited awareness of epidural analgesia, which is an evidence-based intrapartum intervention, often led to reluctance in accepting it during labour. Similar findings in an Ethiopian study indicate that pregnant women were often reluctant to receive this procedure, despite its effectiveness in providing pain relief and promoting relaxation during labour.32 Further, care providers in this study identified women’s requests for caesarean sections, often due to lack of psychological readiness for vaginal birth, as a barrier to promoting physiological birth. Other studies have similarly reported that such requests, often made without awareness of potential complications like urinary incontinence or sexual dysfunction, hindered care providers from delivering evidence-based care.33

Care providers also perceived women’s limited engagement with care interventions as a barrier to providing optimal care, as it was sometimes associated with maternal and neonatal complications. Care providers in the study attributed perineal tears that occurred during episiotomy to women’s resistance during the procedure. However, women’s resistance to the procedure may stem from multiple factors, including lack of informed consent, lack of privacy, poorly managed labour pain and inadequate analgesia prior to the procedure. These findings raise important questions about potential systemic shortcomings in the provision of respectful maternity care for these women. In addition, previous literature has also demonstrated a significant association between disrespectful maternity care and the occurrence of maternal and neonatal complications at birth.34 35 Care providers further claimed that women’s lack of adherence to instructions during pushing contributed to neonatal complications. However, several other factors that extend beyond women’s control may make effective pushing challenging for women. Pushing can be influenced by the duration of labour, the type and timing of analgesia used for pain management36 and foetal position. Additionally, effective pushing requires appropriate maternal positioning as well as adequate, timely and supportive guidance from the birthing team.37 38 Therefore, care providers must pay close attention to labouring women as they develop the urge to push during the second stage of labour. At this stage, supportive and timely assistance is vital to prevent both maternal and neonatal complications.37

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of the current study is that it is believed to be the first study in Sri Lanka to investigate and unveil maternity care providers’ perspectives on challenges to the implementation of evidence-based intrapartum care. Moreover, the study included participants with diverse characteristics which led to the exploration of diverse perspectives in maternity care. Regarding limitations, the present study findings are restricted only to a single tertiary care hospital in Central Sri Lanka, which hinders them from being extrapolated to other maternity care settings in Sri Lanka. Furthermore, it was hoped to conduct an interview with an obstetrician to increase the richness of information. However, it was not possible to obtain an appointment to get their viewpoints, due to their busy schedules.

Recommendations for practice

We recommend resource-constrained settings, such as this study context, prioritise low-cost, system-level strategies that can be immediately implemented to overcome barriers to the adoption of evidence-based intrapartum care. Strengthening in-service education through short, regular, training sessions and bedside mentorship can keep care providers updated on current evidence. The use of simplified, context-adapted clinical guidelines can support consistent practice during labour and delivery. Improving teamwork and communication among maternity care providers may help mitigate workforce shortages and enhance care coordination. Furthermore, supportive supervision, regular multidisciplinary team meetings, audit and feedback mechanisms, and peer review of intrapartum practices can further promote adherence to evidence-based care. In addition, implementing structured maternal education sessions on evidence-based intrapartum care interventions within the care setting may enhance women’s engagement and facilitate the delivery of optimal intrapartum care.

Conclusion

Evidence-based intrapartum care has been proven to significantly lower the maternal and neonatal mortality and morbidity. However, the implementation of these EBPs during labour and delivery is complex and faces daily challenges.1 Therefore, effective strategies should be developed through a participatory approach to motivate maternity care providers to embrace them. The present study findings revealed that care providers continue to use non-EBPs mainly due to their lack of up-to-date knowledge and resistance to change, which suggests the continuous improvement of care providers’ knowledge, attitudes and skills. Adequate supervision, training opportunities and adequate human and physical resources may encourage maternity care providers to implement EBPs. Additionally, increasing women’s awareness of evidence-based care is crucial. Strong professional relationships among maternity care providers are also vital for successfully applying evidence-based intrapartum care. Finally, developing national policies and quality assurance systems based on evidence-based intrapartum care will help reduce unnecessary obstetric interventions and better address women’s needs and requests during childbirth.
