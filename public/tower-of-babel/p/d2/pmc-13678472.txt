Associations of exposure to cotinine, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon, and metal(loid) mixtures during pregnancy with birth weight: the MIEHR study
Authors: Wei-Jen Chen, Elaine Symanski, Iman Moussa, Patrick J. Parsons, Kurunthachalam Kannan, Peter M. Chutcharavan, Morgan Reynolds, Jasmin Chovatiya, Ravikumar Jagani, Syam S. Andra, Kristina W. Whitworth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Prenatal exposure to chemical mixtures may have greater fetal health impacts than single chemicals due to complex, interacting biological mechanisms. Hydroxylated polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon metabolites (OH-PAHs) and metals/metalloids share exposure sources, yet evidence of their combined effects on perinatal outcomes is limited.OBJECTIVE:To examine associations between individual cotinine, OH-PAHs, and metals/metalloids and birth weight, and to investigate their overall and interactive effects.METHODS:We included 1120 mothers from the Maternal and Infant Environmental Health Riskscape (MIEHR) study in Houston, Texas. A large suite of chemical constituents, including cotinine, six OH-PAHs, and 18 metals/metalloids, was measured in spot urine samples collected at delivery. Birth weight, abstracted from medical records, was converted into z-scores standardized by gestational age and sex. Linear regression was employed to examine associations between each constituent and birth weight, adjusted for covariates. Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) was used to evaluate individual, joint, and interactive effects of multiclass chemical mixture on birth weight.RESULTS:Based on the BMKR analysis, no overall mixture effect on birth weight was observed. The exposure-response functions showed no clear patterns between OH-PAHs and birth weight. After accounting for the other constituents in the mixture, molybdenum, thallium, and strontium were negatively associated with birth weight, whereas barium and tungsten were positively associated. In comparing the 75th versus 25th percentiles of the distribution with other constituents fixed at their median levels, the strongest associations were observed for molybdenum and barium with a change in birth weight z-scores of −0.12 (95% credible interval: −0.23, −0.01) and 0.14 (0.02, 0.27), respectively. No interactions between individual mixture constituents were identified.SIGNIFICANCE:In the MIEHR study, with relatively lower urinary OH-PAH and metals/metalloids concentrations compared with women from other U.S. pregnancy cohorts, molybdenum was associated with decreased birth weight, and barium with increased birth weight. Given that both metals are relatively understudied, additional investigation is warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Birth weight, which reflects the culmination of fetal growth in utero, serves as an indicator of fetal growth restriction or prematurity and is a recognized predictor of neonatal and infant mortality and morbidity [1, 2]. Infants born with suboptimal weight are at greater risk of delays in mental and psychomotor development during childhood and metabolic and cardiovascular diseases in adolescence [3, 4]. Established risk factors contributing to reduced birth weight include maternal socioeconomic conditions, smoking, alcohol, and drug use [5]. Further, exposure to environmental pollutants from traffic and industrial emissions, as well as synthetic chemicals commonly found in consumer products, may also play a role in the etiology of reduced birth weight [6].

Two chemical classes of particular concern with respect to human health are polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and metals and metalloids (collectively referred to as “metals” hereafter). These chemical constituents often occur as mixtures due to shared exposure sources such as incomplete fuel combustion, vehicle exhaust, e-waste processing, and tobacco smoke, and are ubiquitous in the environment [7–9]. PAHs and metals can accumulate in soil and water, potentially enter the food chain, and be ingested [10, 11]. In addition, PAHs and metals present in fine particulate matter can be inhaled, pass through the respiratory barrier, and adversely impact the fetus by altering placental function [12]. Consequently, exposure to both classes of chemicals is widespread. Fetal development is a particularly vulnerable period and highly sensitive to environmental exposures due to the physiological immaturity, limited detoxification capacity, and an underdeveloped immune system of the fetus [13]. PAHs and metals may share potential biological mechanisms of toxicity, and even essential elements can be harmful at excessive levels. These chemical constituents can induce oxidative stress or act as endocrine-disrupting chemicals, both of which may affect fetal growth [14, 15]. Evidence of the effects of exposure to the mixture of PAHs and metals is emerging. Several studies using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey have evaluated their joint effects on inflammatory biomarkers and sex hormones [16, 17], and a Chinese study reported a synergistic effect between pyrene and molybdenum (Mo) that increased neural tube defect risk [18]. Together, these findings suggest that jointly assessing health effects of both chemical classes may clarify shared exposure sources and biological pathways and better capture the overall effects of prenatal exposure on infant health within the broader context of the chemical exposome [19].

Numerous epidemiological studies have reported the impacts of prenatal exposure to individual metals on birth weight. For example, increased prenatal exposure to heavy metals such as arsenic (As), lead (Pb), and cadmium (Cd) has been associated with reduced birth weight [20], whereas exposure to essential trace elements like zinc (Zn) and selenium (Se), which play critical roles during pregnancy, has been associated with increased birth weight [21]. However, few studies have evaluated associations with potentially beneficial trace elements such as chromium (Cr) and Mo [22] or with naturally occurring radioactive elements such as uranium (U) [23]. Although PAHs have demonstrated developmental toxicity in experimental studies [24], findings from epidemiological studies on prenatal PAH exposure and birth outcomes remain equivocal. In a meta-analysis, Latifi et al. [25] indicated that prenatal PAH exposure, as assessed using varied metrics (e.g., dietary intake assessments and measurements from ambient air, cord blood, or urine), was associated with reduced birth weight. Another meta-analysis that included only studies measuring maternal urinary concentrations of 1-hydroxypyrene (1-PYR), a hydroxylated PAH metabolite (OH-PAH), reported no association between PAH exposure and birth weight [26]. Research examining OH-PAHs other than 1-PYR remains inconclusive as to their effects on birth weight [27–31].

A growing number of studies have assessed multiple metals as a mixture and their impacts on birth weight. A recent systematic review, including studies published through May 2023 [32], reported that commonly studied metals, such as Pb and mercury, were associated with adverse birth outcomes, including decreased birth weight, when controlling for co-exposure to other metals. In this review, equivocal findings were noted for cobalt (Co) and nickel (Ni), with some studies reporting positive associations with birth weight [33–36] and others inverse associations [34, 35]. Additionally, though less frequently examined, some metal mixture studies observed that antimony (Sb), thallium (Tl), and tin (Sn) were associated with reduced birth weight, whereas strontium (Sr) and barium (Ba) were linked to increased birth weight [33–37]. In addition, evidence regarding the associations between the OH-PAH mixture and birth weight remains limited and inconclusive. Li et al. [38] found no overall association between the mixture of 11 OH-PAHs and birth weight. However, Hu et al. [39] evaluated associations of a mixture of three OH-PAHs and a phthalate metabolite and reported that increasing concentrations of all compounds in the mixture were associated with reduced birth weight.

Given that few studies have evaluated associations between prenatal exposure to PAH and metals as a mixture and birth weight, we conducted a study utilizing data from the Maternal and Infant Environmental Health Riskscape (MIEHR) study, which included non-Hispanic Black and white mother-child pairs recruited at birth from several large obstetric hospitals serving the greater Houston area [40]. Importantly, in Houston, PAHs and heavy metals from industrial areas and waste sites have been redistributed across neighborhoods due to frequent flooding, thereby increasing the risk of exposure to these chemicals [41, 42]. This study aimed to: (1) characterize maternal urinary concentrations of OH-PAHs and metals among MIEHR participants; (2) examine associations between a mixture of individual chemical constituents and birth weight; and (3) investigate the overall and interactive effects of OH-PAH and the metal mixture on birth weight. We also included cotinine in the mixture to account for co-exposure to active or passive tobacco smoke, which is a source of exposure to both PAHs and heavy metals [43, 44].

METHODS

Study design and population

This study used data from women recruited in the MIEHR study [40]. Women were enrolled at the time they delivered their infant from three major obstetric hospitals in Houston, Texas: Memorial Herman Hospital, Ben Taub Hospital, and Texas Children’s Hospital Pavilion for Women, from April 2021 through December 2023. Inclusion criteria included: ages 18 years or older, self-identification as either non-Hispanic Black or non-Hispanic white, English speaker, delivery of a live singleton birth with no major congenital anomaly, and residence in the greater Houston area (i.e., Harris, Brazoria, Chambers, Fort Bend, Galveston, Harris, Liberty, Montgomery, and Waller counties). At enrollment, written informed consent was obtained. Following consent, study staff administered a questionnaire, obtained a urine sample, and abstracted information from a participant’s electronic health record (EHR). A total of 1847 women were enrolled in the MIEHR study (Fig. S1). The current study was restricted to a subset of 1218 women with available chemical profile data. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Baylor College of Medicine and the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston.

Urinary cotinine, OH-PAHs, and metals analysis

Spot urine specimens were collected in sterile containers at the time of recruitment, stored at −80 °C freezer, and shipped frozen to two human biomonitoring laboratories: Wadsworth Center (Albany, NY, USA) and the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (New York City, NY, USA). These laboratories were a part of the U.S. National Institutes of Health-funded Human Health Exposure Analysis Resource (HHEAR) and the Children’s Environmental Health Exposure Analysis Resource (CHEAR) [45, 46].

At the Wadsworth laboratory, concentrations of cotinine in urine, a marker of tobacco smoke exposure, were quantified using hybrid solid-phase extraction coupled with high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS; ABSCIEX 5500, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) [47, 48]. Urinary concentrations of eight OH-PAHs derived from four parent PAHs (i.e., naphthalene, phenanthrene, fluorene, and pyrene) were quantified by liquid-liquid extraction coupled with HPLC-MS/MS, as the detailed methods have been previously described by Guo et al. [49]. At the Mount Sinai laboratory, concentrations of cotinine and OH-PAHs were analyzed using a multiclass, low-volume assay (0.2 mL), ultra-sensitive assay with detection limits ranging from 0.005 to 0.05 ng/mL. The method involved automated solid-phase extraction for sample cleanup and isotope-dilution HPLC-MS/MS for detection and quantification, employing a Sciex Exion ultra-HPLC coupled with a Sciex 6500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA), as detailed by Jagani et al. [50]. Both laboratories participating in CHEAR/HHEAR have engaged in efforts to harmonize measurements, with detailed methodology and quality assurance (QA) and quality control (QC) procedures as outlined in Kannan et al. [51]. Satisfactory agreement between the lab measurements was observed from the analysis of 45 blinded duplicates, yielding a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.93 and an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.95 for urinary cotinine. Similarly, the OH-PAHs exhibited values in the range of 0.29–0.98 and 0.22–0.96 for the respective coefficients (Table S1).

Urinary concentrations of 36 metals (including 15 rare-earth elements) were measured in all samples by inductively coupled plasma–tandem mass spectrometry (ICP-MS/MS; Agilent Model 8900, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) at the Wadsworth laboratory. Urine samples were thawed to room temperature and mixed on a laboratory rocker for at least 45 minutes, after which a 0.3-mL aliquot was removed for trace element analysis. Samples were then diluted to 5 mL with a reagent solution containing 2% (v/v) double distilled HNO3, 0.5% (v/v) Veritas ultimate grade HCl (GFS Chemicals, Powell, OH, USA), 1000 μg/L Au (High Purity Standards, Charleston, SC, USA), and 0.005% (v/v) Triton X-100™ (Sigma Aldrich Co., St. Louis, MO, USA). A summary of method performance and average method limit of detection (LOD) for the metals during the analysis period is provided in Tables S2–S6. Quality control procedures are also summarized in the Supplementary Material, Appendix A.

In the downstream statistical analyses, urinary OH-PAHs for which more than 50% of values were below the analyte-specific method LOD were excluded. Following this exclusion, six OH-PAHs remained for analysis: 1-hydroxynaphthalene (1-NAP), 2-hydroxynaphthalene (2-NAP), 2-hydroxyphenanthrene (2-PHEN), combined 1/9-hydroxyphenanthrene (1/9-PHEN), combined 2/3/9-hydroxyfluorene (2/3/9-FLUO), and 1-PYR. For each of these six compounds, concentrations below the LOD were substituted with LOD/√2 [52]. For urinary metals, the laboratory provided all values generated by the instrument software, including values below the LOD, which were retained to maximize data availability and preserve a more realistic exposure distribution [53]. However, in some cases, the instrument software generated zero or negative values for measurements below the LOD, depending on the calibration model selected, and after the reagent blank was subtracted. Because downstream statistical analyses were conducted using log-transformed concentrations, metals with a significant proportion (5.4–79.4%) of values ≤ 0 were excluded. After exclusion, 18 metals remained: As, Cd, Sb, Tl, Pb, U, Co, Cu, Zn, Se, Mo, Cr, Ni, Sn, tungsten (W), rubidium (Rb), Sr, and Ba. We additionally excluded 63 participants (5.2%) with measured concentrations ≤ 0 for at least one of these 18 metals (Fig. S1). To adjust the concentrations of cotinine, OH-PAHs, and metals for urinary dilution, urinary creatinine was measured using HPLC-MS/MS at the Wadsworth laboratory [54] and a proficiency-validated colorimetric assay at the Mount Sinai laboratory [55]. Both methods demonstrated good reliability (Table S1). As a result, all chemical concentrations are expressed in units of μg/g creatinine.

Birth weight assessment

We abstracted the following data related to the index pregnancy from each participant’s EHR: birth weight (grams), clinical estimate of completed weeks of gestation, and infant sex. To facilitate comparisons, we converted birth weights into z-scores by standardizing for gestational age and infant sex based on an updated U.S. reference of birth weight-for-gestational age [56].

Covariates

The interviewer-administered questionnaire completed by MIEHR participants collected data on maternal sociodemographic factors, residential history during pregnancy, prenatal care, and pregnancy history, as well as lifestyle and behavior factors [40]. The following covariates were included in the present study a priori based on previous literature [32]: maternal age (years), race (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic Black), education level (high school or less, some college, and college or beyond), marital status (married/living with partner, unmarried), and initiation of prenatal care (prenatal care initiated < 13 weeks, no prenatal care or prenatal care initiated ≥ 13 weeks). We also included parity, which was abstracted from EHRs and categorized into three levels (1, 2, 3 + ). Given that the birth weight z-scores were standardized according to infant sex, we did not include infant sex as a covariate in our analysis. We also did not adjust for smoking or environmental tobacco smoke exposure, as cotinine was one of the chemical constituents that was measured in urine samples. We evaluated missingness in all covariates and excluded 35 (3.0%) participants with missing data (one participant was missing education level, and 34 were missing information about when prenatal care began). Thus, 1120 births were retained for analysis (Fig. S1).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to summarize maternal and infant characteristics. Summary statistics, including detection frequency, geometric means (GM) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), and selected percentiles (i.e., 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th), were calculated for urinary concentrations of chemical constituents. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between pairs of the natural log-transformed chemical concentrations, and the logged values were used in subsequent analyses. As a preliminary analysis, we first conducted a series of multiple linear regression models that adjusted for covariates to estimate regression coefficients (β^) and 95% CIs representing the change in birth weight z-scores per interquartile range (IQR) increase in the log-transformed creatinine-adjusted concentrations of each chemical constituent. In this analysis, the Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons to retain an overall significance level of 5%.

Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) [57] was employed to simultaneously examine associations of cotinine, OH-PAHs, and metals on birth weight z-scores. BKMR can simultaneously incorporate variable selection across the potentially large number of exposures while controlling for multiple testing, enabling a parsimonious representation of the exposure-response function [57]. BKMR was implemented to examine both linear and nonlinear dose-response effects of single chemical constituents within the multiclass chemical mixture, evaluate the overall effect of exposure to the mixture of chemical constituents, and explore interactions between chemical constituents in the mixture. The Gaussian kernel was specified when fitting the BKMR models [57]. We implemented the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm with 100,000 iterations (with half of the iterations as a burn-in) [58]. Log-transformed creatinine-adjusted concentrations of each OH-PAH or metal constituent were centered with a mean of 0 and scaled to have a standard deviation of 1 (i.e., transformed into z-scores) to reduce the influence of extreme values and facilitate comparisons across different chemical constituents within the mixture [59]. All covariates previously discussed were included in the BKMR models. The convergence of the MCMC algorithm was visually inspected using trace plots. In the fitted BKMR model, we estimated a single multivariable exposure-response function to describe the association between OH-PAH–metal mixture and birth weight. To evaluate how each chemical constituent was associated with birth weight z-scores and visualize whether the associations were linear or non-linear and positive or negative, we obtained univariate cross-sections of the exposure-response function with 95% credible intervals (95% CrIs) for each constituent, holding all other constituents fixed at their respective median concentrations. Second, we estimated the change in birth weight z-scores associated with exposure to a given chemical constituent at the concentration equal to the 75th percentile of its distribution, compared with the concentration equal to the 25th percentile of its distribution, and with all other chemical constituents fixed at concentrations equal to the 25th, 50th, or 75th percentiles of their respective distributions. Third, the overall mixture effect was calculated by comparing the birth weight z-scores when concentrations of all chemical constituents were equal to selected percentiles of their respective distributions (i.e., 25th, 30th, 35th, …75th), compared with their respective median concentrations. Last, we obtained the bivariate exposure-response functions to investigate potential interactions between exposure to selected pairs of chemical constituents when the concentrations of all other chemical constituents were fixed at their respective median concentrations.

As a secondary analysis, we applied quantile g-computation [60], a parametric modeling approach that estimates the overall effect of an exposure mixture with appropriate confidence interval coverage, to evaluate the association between the OH-PAH–metal mixture and birth weight. In quantile g-computation, weights are estimated for each chemical constituent to indicate the direction and relative magnitude of its contribution to the overall mixture effect. We reported the estimated change in birth weight z-scores per one-decile increase in the overall exposure mixture (95% CIs were obtained from 500 bootstrap resamples), adjusting for the same set of covariates as in the BKMR analysis. Given that several studies observed sex differences in the effects of metal mixtures [35, 37, 61, 62] and OH-PAHs [28, 31] on birth weight, we conducted infant sex-specific BKMR models to investigate potential sex differences in the associations.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.0.4 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

The present study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Baylor College of Medicine and the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston. All pregnant participants provided informed consent before the study staff administered a questionnaire and collected specimens.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

A summary of maternal and infant characteristics of the study sample and all participants in the MIEHR study is shown in Table 1. Compared to all MIEHR participants (n = 1847), the 1120 mother-child pairs included in the present analysis were similar with respect to these sociodemographic characteristics. Among the analytic sample, over half of the mothers were less than 30 years of age, with 23.8% being less than 25 years and 28.4% being 25–29 years. Most women identified as non-Hispanic Black (61.0%), while 39.0% identified as non-Hispanic white. Most women reported at least a college education (45.5%), were married or living with their partner (58.7%), initiated prenatal care early (i.e., before 13 weeks of gestation) (82.5%), did not smoke during pregnancy (89.4%) and were not exposed to environmental tobacco smoke (81.2%).

Maternal urinary chemical constituents

The distribution of urinary concentrations of cotinine, OH-PAHs, and metals is shown in Table 2. Cotinine concentrations were >LOD in 91.0% of samples. Except for 2-PHEN, the remaining five OH-PAHs had concentrations >LOD in over 80% of samples. Eight metals (As, Cd, Tl, Co, Cu, Zn, Se, and Rb) had concentrations >LOD in nearly 100% of the urine samples. As shown in Fig. S2, there was a weak correlation between cotinine and each chemical constituent (r = −0.01 to 0.35). Strong correlations (r = 0.60–0.64) were observed between 1/9-PHEN, 2/3/9-FLUO, and 1-PYR, while correlations between metals were weak or moderate, except for Tl and Rb (r = 0.79) and Sr and Ba (r = 0.82).

Linear regression analysis of each chemical constituent

The associations between urinary concentrations of each chemical constituent in the mixture and birth weight z-scores are presented in Table S7. We observed a reduction in birth weight z-scores (β^=−0.11, 95% CI = −0.18, −0.04) associated with an IQR increase in cotinine concentration. Negative associations were observed for several OH-PAHs and birth weight z-scores, including 1/9-PHEN (β^=−0.09, 95% CI = −0.17, −0.004), 2/3/9-FLUO (β^=−0.12, 95% CI = −0.20, −0.05), and 1-PYR (β^=−0.08, 95% CI = −0.16, −0.01). There was less consistency in the direction of associations between metals and birth weight z-scores. The negative associations were observed for Tl (β^=−0.10, 95% CI = −0.17, −0.02) and Mo (β^=−0.12, 95% CI = −0.20, −0.03), while a positive association was found for Cr (β^=0.07, 95% CI = 0.01, 0.14). However, among these chemical constituents, only cotinine and 2/3/9-FLUO remained statistically significantly associated with birth weight after Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (p-value < 0.002 for 25 comparisons).

BKMR analysis of the OH-PAH–metal mixture

Figure 1 presents the univariate exposure-response functions and 95% CrIs for associations between individual chemical constituents and birth weight z-scores, fixing concentrations of all other chemical constituents at their respective median concentrations. No evidence of associations was observed for cotinine or OH-PAHs with birth weight z-scores. However, Se and Ba showed positive linear patterns, whereas Tl, Cu, Mo, and Sr showed negative linear patterns; the 95% CrIs excluded the null value at the highest concentrations only for Mo, Sr, and Ba.

For individual chemical constituents, the change in birth weight z-scores associated with exposure to concentrations at the 75th percentile of the distribution compared with the 25th percentile is displayed in Fig. 2 (see corresponding effect estimates and 95% CrIs in Table S8). Although exposure to higher concentrations of cotinine, 2-NAP, and 2/3/9-FLUO was associated with reductions in birth weight z-scores, the 95% CrIs included the null value when other chemical constituents were fixed at the 25th, 50th, or 75th percentiles of their respective distributions. For metals, we observed a reduction in birth weight z-scores associated with high Mo concentrations, fixing the concentrations of other chemical constituents at their 25th (β = −0.10, 95% CrI = −0.22, 0.03), 50th (β = −0.12, 95% CrI = −0.23, −0.01), or 75th (β = −0.15, 95% CrI = −0.27, −0.02) percentiles. Similarly, Tl and Sr showed negative associations with birth weight z-scores, though these were marginal. In contrast, a positive association was observed between higher Ba exposure and birth weight z-scores, with the largest magnitude observed when the concentrations of other chemical constituents were fixed at the 75th percentile of their respective distributions (β = 0.16, 95% CrI = 0.03, 0.30). A positive association was also observed for W, although the 95% CrIs included the null value.

No overall effect of exposure to the chemical constituent mixture on birth weight z-scores was observed (Fig. S3). In addition, there was limited evidence of interaction between any pairs of OH-PAHs and metals, as indicated by the bivariate exposure-response functions (Fig. S4).

Secondary and sensitivity analyses

The overall effect of OH-PAH–metal mixture on birth weight z-scores examined using quantile g-computation was consistent with the BKMR results. The overall effect was not statistically significant, as the 95% CIs for the effect estimates across mixture deciles included the null value (Fig. S5a). Additionally, Ba contributed the largest positive weight, followed by W and Zn, whereas Sr, 2/3/9-FLUO, cotinine, Tl, and Mo contributed the largest negative weights (Fig. S5b).

In the sex-stratified mixture analysis (Fig. S6), the negative effects of Tl and the positive effects of Ba on birth weight z-scores were observed only in male infants. In contrast, the negative effects of Mo and the positive effects of W were observed only in female infants. Furthermore, although not observed in the overall model, the marginally negative association with 2/3/9-FLUO and the marginally positive association with Sn and Rb were observed only among male infants.

DISCUSSION

In this study, investigating associations between exposure to a multiclass chemical mixture and birth weight among mother-child pairs in the greater Houston area, urinary concentrations of OH-PAH and metals among pregnant women were relatively low compared to those reported in other prior U.S. pregnancy cohorts recruited over 10 years ago [35, 63]. After accounting for co-exposure effects within the mixture, higher urinary concentrations of Mo, Tl and Sr were associated with decreased birth weight, while Ba and W were associated with increased birth weight. Further, evidence of interactions between constituents from different chemical classes was not observed. Moreover, consistent with previous studies [33, 35, 37, 38], no overall effect of the OH-PAH–metals mixture on birth weight was found using either BKMR or quantile g-computation.

Our study found suggestive negative associations between urinary concentrations of 2-NAP and 2/3/9-FLUO and birth weight, accounting for other chemical constituents in the mixture. To our knowledge, two previous studies have employed BKMR to examine OH-PAH mixtures in relation to birth weight [38, 39]. While Li et al. [38] evaluated a broad set of OH-PAHs (including the metabolites assessed in our study), they did not find evidence of an association between the OH-PAH mixture and birth weight, nor did they report results for individual OH-PAHs within the mixture. In contrast, Hu et al. [39] observed a negative association between exposure to the OH-PAH mixture and birth weight, but their evaluation of a chemical mixture included only a limited number of OH-PAHs (i.e., 2-PHEN, 4-PHEN, and 1-PYR) as well as a single phthalate metabolite (i.e., mono-iso-butyl phthalate). Human exposure to PAHs occurs through water, diet, dust, and air [64], although the specific sources and exposure routes vary for different PAHs. In our mixture analysis, 2-NAP and 2/3/9-FLUO were inversely associated with birth weight, but neither association reached statistical significance. 2-NAP is related to naphthalene exposure, which can occur from indoor (e.g., mothballs and tobacco smoke) as well as outdoor (e.g., vehicle and industrial emissions) sources [65, 66]. 2/3/9-FLUO is derived from fluorene and is related to petroleum and industrial pollution sources [65, 67]. While the biological mechanisms by which exposure to specific PAHs affects birth outcomes remain unclear, there is evidence that PAHs can act as endocrine disruptors [15], potentially altering reproductive and thyroid hormone levels and thereby compromising maternal health and placental function [68, 69]. In addition, PAHs may influence placental and fetal development through modulation of DNA methylation [6, 69, 70].

Although the individual effects of heavy metals such as Pb and Cd on reduced birth weight have been well-studied [20], our findings are consistent with other mixture-based studies of metals [33–37], which found null associations for both metals on birth weight. Additionally, urinary concentrations of Pb may not accurately reflect internal exposure, particularly among low-exposed populations [71]. In the case of As, no association with birth weight was observed. Similar to many previous studies [33, 34, 37], we did not differentiate between toxic and non-toxic forms of As, which may mask a true negative association. In contrast to these toxic metals, we observed a marginal inverse association between urinary concentrations of Tl and birth weight. Tl is a potassium-mimicking metal that can disrupt cellular functions [72], and exposure to Tl has been previously associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes [73–75]. Although the inverse association observed for Tl is consistent with a study conducted in Israel [33], other mixture-based studies did not find this association [34, 36, 76].

In addition to heavy metals, we observed positive associations with Zn and Se and birth weight, although the magnitude of these associations was close to the null when other chemical constituents were fixed at the 75th percentiles of their respective distributions. The prior literature related to exposure to these compounds (as part of chemical mixtures) has been limited. Although toxicity of trace elements can arise from both excessive levels and deficiencies [72], our study did not provide strong evidence of nonlinear associations. Zn and Se are well-known antioxidants essential for regulating excessive inflammation, maintaining oxidative balance, and modulating immune responses and are crucial for placental and fetal development [71, 77]. While Hu et al. [34] reported a positive association between urinary concentrations of Zn and birth weight, Rahman et al. [62], who relied on blood concentrations to assess exposure, found no association. Some previous mixture-based studies also reported null associations between Se exposure and birth weight [33, 34, 37]. Exposure to Co, a key component of vitamin B12 that is critical for fetal development [78], was positively associated with birth weight in a study in China [34]. However, we did not observe an association for Co in our study, and a pooled analysis of three U.S.-based pregnancy cohorts reported a U-shaped association [35], with decreased birth weight associated with exposure to mid-range levels.

We observed a negative association between exposure to Mo and birth weight. Previously reported associations between urinary Mo concentrations (as part of a chemical mixture) and birth weight have been equivocal. For example, in a study of low-income Hispanic women in Los Angeles, Mo was positively associated with ultrasound-measured estimated fetal weight [79] but not with birth weight [36]. On the other hand, two other mixture-based studies found no evidence of an association between Mo and birth weight [35, 37]. The urinary concentrations of Mo in our study were relatively lower than those reported in U.S.-based studies [35, 36], but similar to levels observed in a China-based study [76]. Although Mo can cross the placenta [80] and the elevated level of Mo in placenta tissue has been reported to be associated with neural tube defects [18], the mechanisms linking exposure to adverse pregnancy outcomes remain unknown. Nonetheless, exposure to elevated levels of Mo has been associated with increased oxidative stress among pregnant women [81, 82].

Suggestive associations were observed between exposure to several metals (i.e., Ba, Sr, and W) and birth weight. Sr and Ba are alkaline earth metals that occur naturally in the Earth’s crust and in rocks. Although they are not considered essential elements, dietary intake from vegetables, fruits, and dairy products is the primary source of exposure [71, 83]. There is a paucity of research investigating the associations of these metals with birth weight. In the present study, urinary concentrations of Sr and Ba were highly correlated, and they were negatively and positively associated with birth weight, respectively. We are aware that only one prior mixture-based study included Ba and Sr in their analysis [34], and their results differed from ours; they reported a positive association between Sr and birth weight and a null association for Ba. Although one epidemiological study reported that Sr was associated with oxidative damage during pregnancy [84], and animal studies suggest high levels of Ba may cause adverse reproductive and developmental effects [85], the underlying mechanisms by which these metals affect fetal health remain unclear. Additionally, our study observed a positive association between W and birth weight. To our knowledge, no prior research has examined this association. Although certain W species serve as essential cofactors in enzymes involved in carboxylic acid reduction in some microorganisms [86], there is no established developmental effect of prenatal W exposure in humans. Given the limited number of studies examining the perinatal health effects of exposure to earth metals, further investigation is warranted.

To our knowledge, no studies have reported sex-stratified associations between the OH-PAH mixture and birth weight. In a study of OH-PAHs in separate models, Cathey et al. [31] reported associations with decreased birth weight only among male infants, especially for 2/3-PHEN. Our study observed a marginal negative association between 2/3/9-FLUO and birth weight among male infants only. Sex-specific effects of exposure to metal mixtures and birth weight were explored in several epidemiologic studies, although the findings are inconsistent, with some reporting effects only among female [35, 37] or male infants [62], and one study reported mixed results [61]. In our study, findings were also mixed in evaluating sex-specific differences in the impact of metals on birth weight. Among male infants, we observed negative associations with Tl and positive associations with Ba and birth weight, whereas Mo was negatively associated and W were positively associated with birth weight among female infants. In addition, marginal positive associations of Sn and Rb on birth weight were only observed in the sex-stratified results. There are biologically plausible mechanisms for sex-specific differences, including sexually dimorphic variations in the efficiency of placental transfer of chemical constituents [87] and the impact of environmental exposures on placental structure and function [88]. Given the inconsistencies in sex-specific associations between the metal mixture and birth weight, future studies may provide insights into sexually dimorphic effects of OH-PAH–metal mixture on birth weight.

Our study has several strengths, including assessing a large suite of chemical constituents, which included several essential elements that are known to play roles in detoxifying heavy metals and PAHs [89, 90]. Additionally, inclusion of cotinine in the models provided a more precise measure of exposure to smoking compared to previous studies that primarily relied on self-reported data. Nonetheless, several limitations need to be considered in our study. First, we assessed concentrations of chemical constituents in urine because this was the available matrix for the largest number of women in the present study. Although urine is a common and reliable biomarker for OH-PAHs [91] and certain metals, such as Cd [71], it is not the ideal biomarker for all metals. For example, urinary measurements of Pb and Se may not accurately reflect internal exposure, particularly among low-exposed populations [71]. Additionally, we collected only a single spot urine sample around the time of delivery. Given that infant birth weight reflects the culmination of fetal growth throughout pregnancy, reverse causality is unlikely to occur and affect prenatal exposure. Furthermore, urinary concentrations of PAH metabolites and metals may only reflect exposure in the days before the specimen collection; however, a single measurement may be sufficient to characterize pregnancy exposure under stable conditions [92]. Although urinary measurements of OH-PAHs and metals reflect integrated internal exposure from all routes, we could not identify specific exposure sources or pathways. In addition, assessments of urinary cotinine and OH-PAHs were conducted in two laboratories that participated in CHEAR/HHEAR harmonization efforts. Duplicate measurements from both laboratories showed good reliability for cotinine and most OH-PAHs (ICC > 0.8) (Table S1), whereas poor reliability was observed for 1-NAP (ICC = 0.22). Thus, we cannot rule out the possibility that measurement error reduced the precision of the estimate and led to the null association observed for 1-NAP. Second, our study used gestational age- and sex-standardized birth weight z-scores to facilitate comparisons with other U.S.-based studies. We also log-transformed and centered chemical constituent concentrations before fitting the mixture models to ensure stability of the BKMR algorithm and to reduce the influence of outliers [59]. Although the resulting effect estimates were relatively small and cannot be directly comparable to studies that did not apply these preprocessing steps, our effect sizes are similar to those reported in prior studies that used similar methods [33, 35, 37]. Third, residual confounding is possible in our study if we categorized some covariates that did not appropriately capture confounding effects. Also, we were unable to account for unmeasured factors, including diet and supplement use, which are associated with pregnancy health and may also be sources of exposure to certain PAHs and metals [93, 94]. Lastly, the MIEHR study was originally designed to evaluate drivers of perinatal health disparities between non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic white women [40]. Therefore, women of other races and ethnicities were not included, and the results cannot be generalized to these other groups.

The urinary levels of OH-PAHs and metals in our study were lower than those reported in other early pregnancy cohorts in the U.S. Although null associations were observed for OH-PAHs and birth weight overall, two metabolites of low-molecular-weight PAHs, naphthalene and fluorene, were marginally associated with decreased birth weight within the mixture framework. Several essential trace elements, such as Mo, Zn, and Se, may potentially play a role in birth weight, although the direction of these associations varied. Relatively understudied metals, including W, Sr, and Ba, were also marginally associated with birth weight and further investigation of these associations, including potential mechanisms, is warranted. Given the growing number of studies considering multiple classes of chemical stressors [19], future research may also incorporate non-chemical neighborhood stressors, which could better our understanding of the multifactorial sources of exposures and contribute to an exposome perspective for addressing environmental and perinatal health disparities.
