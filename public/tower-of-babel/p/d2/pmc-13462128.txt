A cold manipulation of rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) at eyed-stage has few impacts on hepatic lipid metabolism in juveniles
Authors: Stephane Panserat, Hélène Braga, Elodie Harte, Michael Marchand, Tina Terrenne, Lionel Goardon, Marianne Houssier, Delphine Lallias, Lucie Marandel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic programming through thermal manipulation in the early stages of life has been observed in animals such as birds and fish. Here, we analysed two lines of rainbow trout selected for their lipid content, which have been shown to exhibit divergent regulation of lipid metabolism. The eyed eggs of the fat and lean lines, which were produced in the seventh generation of selection, were subjected to cold manipulation for 15 days at 3 °C. The effect of this stimulus on lipid and cholesterol metabolism in the livers of juvenile fish was then investigated later in life. Although the differences between the two lines were confirmed with regard to lipid metabolism (beta-oxidation, lipogenesis, and cholesterol metabolism), no major differences were observed in relation to the early cold manipulation with respect to metabolic and growth levels. Indeed, our data showed that early cold manipulation at the eyed stage has no adverse effects on fish performance or metabolism, confirming that this common aquaculture practice has no drastic consequences for fish rearing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55144-1.Keywords: Thermal manipulation, Fat and lean lines, Lipid metabolism, Rainbow trout

Introduction

Metabolic programming is based on the idea that early environmental stimuli can have long-term effects on metabolic processes, especially lipid metabolism, in later life, primarily through molecular mechanisms1,2. In fish, the possibility of adapting metabolism to improve the utilization of new ingredient-based diets was investigated through early nutritional programming3–8. Other environmental factors, such as oxygen levels, have already been successfully tested, showing permanent metabolic modifications later in life9–11. Temperature has also been shown to impact intermediary metabolism in juveniles, for example in European sea bass (Dicentrarchus labrax)12,13. However, to date, no studies have examined the long-term effects of early cold thermal manipulation on rainbow trout, despite the common practice of cold manipulating trout eggs in aquaculture for shipping fertilised eggs at the eyed stage14. Therefore, it is of the utmost importance to study the long-term effects of cold manipulation on trout eggs and juveniles.

Another interesting point is that programming may depend on genetic background, as was previously observed15, and is probably partly due to epigenetic-genetic interactions16. To test the interaction between programming and genetics in trout, we analysed the effect of thermal manipulation on two trout lines: fat (F) and lean (L)17. Such lines constitute a relevant model for better understanding the determinants and consequences of lipid deposition, as previously observed in generations 3 and 4 of selection. Indeed, differences in lipid metabolism, such as fatty acid oxidation and lipogenesis, have clearly been observed between the two lines19. It is important to determine whether these differences in lipid metabolism are still present in the current seventh generation of selection. Furthermore, these lines are particularly relevant for studying the effects of cold thermal exposure, given that the relationship between decreased temperature and altered lipid metabolism and deposition has been well documented17. Lipid and cholesterol metabolism are thus good indicators with which to evaluate the programming effect of early exposure to a low temperature18,19.

For all these reasons, we compared the two trout lines after seven generations (G7) of selection, in order to: (i) analyse lipid metabolism at a molecular level in a genomic context; (ii) describe cholesterol metabolism for the first time; and (iii) test the effect of early thermal manipulation on lipid and cholesterol metabolism in these two lines.

Metabolic programming refers to the long-term modulation of physiological functions induced by environmental stimuli experienced during critical early-life windows1,2. In fish, this concept has been mainly explored through nutritional programming, with the objective of improving the utilisation of alternative feed ingredients later in life6,7. In rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), several studies have shown that early-life stimuli can durably affect intermediary metabolism. For instance, hypoxic exposure during early development altered the expression of glucose metabolism-related genes later in life10,11,20. Similarly, short-term exposure to a high-carbohydrate diet after first feeding induced long-term changes in carbohydrate utilisation and glucose metabolism21,22, whereas early exposure to a plant-based diet improved its later acceptance and utilisation3. Altogether, these studies support the idea that early environmental conditions can durably modify metabolic trajectories in fish.

Among environmental factors, temperature is of particular interest because fish are poikilothermic and therefore highly sensitive to thermal conditions during development. Early temperature manipulations have been shown to affect growth, muscle development and metabolic traits in several fish species including Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar)23,24, gilthead sea bream (Sparus aurata)25 and European sea bass (Dicentrarchus labrax)13, as more generally reviewed by Johnston (2006)26. In Atlantic salmon in particular, temperature until the eyed stage of embryogenesis was shown to program the growth trajectory and muscle phenotype later in life23, and early thermal experience was also reported to affect muscle fibre recruitment and growth patterns in salmon populations24. These studies provide a strong rationale for investigating whether early cold exposure can also induce long-term metabolic effects in rainbow trout, especially since cold storage of fertilised eggs at the eyed stage is a common aquaculture practice.

Another important point is that programming responses may depend on the genetic background15,16. To address this question, we used two rainbow trout lines divergently selected for muscle lipid content, namely a fat line (F) and a lean line (L)27. These lines constitute a relevant model because they were specifically developed to differ in muscle lipid deposition and were later shown to differ in nutrient utilisation, lipid retention, hepatic and muscle metabolic regulation, and flesh quality-related traits as previously observed in generations 3 and 4 of selection28–33. It is important to determine whether these differences in lipid metabolism are still present in the current seventh generation of selection. In this context, particular attention was paid to genes involved in lipid metabolism, including genes related to fatty-acid oxidation, cholesterol metabolism and LC-PUFA biosynthesis. In particular, fads and elovl genes were analysed because desaturases and elongases are key components of fatty-acid metabolism and endogenous LC-PUFA biosynthesis, including EPA and DHA, which are major contributors to fish flesh nutritional quality. This point is also relevant in the present rainbow trout model because previous work on these divergent lines suggested differences in fatty-acid metabolism and biosynthetic capacity34. More broadly, the complement and regulation of Fads- and Elovl-dependent pathways are known to differ among fish species, including salmonids, gilthead sea bream, Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua) and Japanese sea bass (Lateolabrax japonicus), which further supports the relevance of briefly comparing these pathways across teleost models35–38.

For all these reasons, we compared the two trout lines after seven generations (G7) of selection, in order to: (i) analyse lipid metabolism at a molecular level in a genomic context; (ii) describe cholesterol metabolism for the first time; and (iii) test the effect of early thermal manipulation on lipid and cholesterol metabolism in these two lines.

Material and methods

Ethical statement and accordance with ARRIVE guidelines

The rainbow trout used in this study came from the INRAE experimental facilities (reproduction and rearing) in France (PEIMA, INRAE, 2021, Fish Farming systems Experimental Facility, 10.15454/1.5572329612068406E12, Sizun, France).

Investigations were carried out in strict accordance with the guiding principles for the use and care of laboratory animals and in compliance with French and European regulations on animal welfare (Decree 2001–464, 29 May 2001 and Directive 2010/63/EU, respectively). This protocol and the project as a whole were approved by the French National Consultative Ethics Committee CEFEA No 74 (APAFIS #31861-2021060117041206 v4). This confirms that our study is in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Fish rearing and samplings

The experimental design is described in Fig. 1 and was performed at the INRAE experimental facilities (PEIMA, INRAE, 2021, Fish Farming systems Experimental Facility, 10.15454/1.5572329612068406E12, Sizun, France). Details on the fertilisation, cold manipulation and fish rearing conditions have been published previously39. Briefly, 2 years-old G7 breeders of Lean and Fat lines were used to produce the two experimental groups. For each of the two lines, clutches from 20 females among those available on the same day were selected. Approximately 500 eggs per female of a given line (lean or fat) have been pooled and coelomic liquid was added to homogenise the pool of eggs. The eggs were then drained and redistributed into 20 cups for fertilisation with sperm from 20 males of the same line. All fertilisations were carried out on the same day. Fertilised eggs from each line were then incubated in hatching trays supplied with spring water at 12.0 °C. At 17 days post fertilisation (dpf), a 15-day cold manipulation (3 °C, the temperature at which eggs are kept for shipping in farms) was conducted at eyed-stage eggs in both fat and lean lines, whereas control fish were kept at 12 °C, the rearing temperature at our facilities. Small incubators were installed in two separate 200-L tanks supplied with natural spring water at either 12.0 °C (control) or chilled at 3.0 °C using a water chiller (Cooling Plus Energy system, Hitema). For each line, three incubators containing 500 eyed eggs were used for each incubation temperature condition (2 lines × 2 incubation temperatures × 3 incubators). Eyed-stage eggs were sampled at the end of the 15-day cold exposure (n = 15 per condition, called 15D in the following manuscript) and at an equivalent number of degree-days (°D) for the control fish (210°D). Eggs were directly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80 °C until analyses.

Experimental design.

After the early thermal manipulation, fry were kept in small incubators for hatching. Water temperature was increased at a rate of 3.0 °C per hour, by allowing a controlled stream of water at the targeted temperature to flow, reaching a final temperature of 12.0 °C. Before the first feeding, fish (4 experimental groups: 2 early temperatures * 2 lines) were transferred into 0.3 m3 indoor tanks supplied with natural spring water at 12.0 °C (n = 3 tanks per experimental group and ~ 270 fish per tank). At 78 dpf (average weight: 0.6–1.0 g), fish were transferred into covered 0.3 m3 tanks under a greenhouse and then, at 155 dpf, into covered 1.8-m3 outdoor fiberglass tanks, supplied with lake dam water. Fish were fed daily to satiation using automatic feeders with a commercial diet from Le Gouessant’s company for 20–21 weeks (Fig. 1). The growth was followed by weighing a random subsample of 50 fish from each tank every 2–3 weeks. At 189 dpf, 120 juveniles (n = 10 fish per tank; 12 tanks in total: 2 early temperatures * 2 lines * 3 replicate tanks) were anaesthetised and killed with terminal anaesthesia by bathing in Tricaïne MS-222 (150 mg/L) prior to sampling. Death was confirmed by the absence of body and opercular movements. Fish were individually measured (total length) and weighed. Blood was collected from the caudal vein using heparinized syringes and then centrifuged (3000 g, 5 min). The recovered plasma was immediately frozen and stored at −20 °V until analysis. The liver of fish was sampled in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Fish were a mix of males and females. As programming effects are known to be dependent on sex, fish were sexed by PCR using the sdY male-specific gene40 after sampling. Based on sex and plasma parameters, 48 fish out of 120 were selected for gene expression analysis (2 males and 2 females per triplicate tank, i.e. 12 fish per experimental group). For each measured parameter, we tested the sex-effect by a Kruskal Wallis test. As no sex-effect was found regardless the considered parameter (plasma metabolite, mRNA level,…), we pooled all data in the following paper.

Biochemical analysis: chemical composition and plasma parameters.

Whole-body composition of fish (n = 12 per condition, same fish as for plasma and gene analysis) was analysed as follows: dry matter was determined by oven drying to constant weight at 105 °C; ash was determined via combustion in a muffle furnace at 600 °C to a constant weight; crude protein (N × 6.25) was determined by the Kjeldahl method41 after acid digestion. Crude lipid in the diets and whole-body were determined by petroleum ether extraction (Soxtherm), while total lipid content in the liver were performed using the dichloromethane/methanol (2:1, v/v) as the extraction liquid, according to Folch et al. (1957).

Commercial kits were used to determine the level of several plasma metabolites. Plasma glucose was quantitatively determined using the GOD (glucose oxidase)-POD (peroxidase) method (Sobioda, Montbonnot, France). Triglyceride content was analyzed using the GPO (glycerol phosphate dehydrogenase)-POD (peroxidase) method (Sobioda, Montbonnot, France). Free fatty acids were quantified using ACS (acyl-CoA synthase)-ACOD (acyl-CoA oxidase) method (Wako Chemicals GmbH, Neuss, Germany). Cholesterol was quantitatively determined using the cholesterol esterase, cholesterol oxidase and chromogens (Sobioda, Montbonnot, France). The kits were adapted to 96-well plates formats according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA were extracted from the frozen eyed eggs or frozen livers using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, United States) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luciferase control RNA (Promega), 10 pg per 1.9 mg of embryo, was added to each eyed-egg sample after homogenisation to allow data normalization as previously described42,43. Then, 1 µg of the total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA in duplicate using the SuperScript III RNase H-Reverse Transcriptase kit (Invitrogen) with random primers (Promega, Charbonnières-les-Bains, France). All quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assays were carried out in the Roche Lightcycler 480 system (Roche Diagnostics, Neuilly-sur-Seine, France). Primers used in the present work were listed in supplementary material (Tables S1 and S2). Analysed genes’ symbols and names were listed in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as means ± s.d. The effects of line, temperature and line × temperature on different parameters were tested using R software (v3.1.0)/R Commander by two-way ANOVA with line and temperature as independent variables after having tested the normality and homoscedasticity. When line × temperature interaction was significant, means of all treatments were compared by a Tukey’s post hoc analysis.

Results

An efficient thermal stimulus

Rainbow trout at eyed-stage embryos from lean and fat lines were maintained at 12 °C (control) or at 3 °C during 15 days (15D) as a thermal manipulation. Survival was tested after this stimulus and no significant differences were found between control and 15D animals31. To be sure that the animals received efficiently the thermal manipulation, the mRNA levels of genes previously shown to be sensitive to temperature manipulation in fish, i.e. dnmt3aa, dnmt3ab2, dnmt3bba116,46 and cirbpb44, were analysed at 210°D corresponding to the developmental stage of 15D embryos at the end of the cold exposure. As shown in Fig. 2, mRNA levels of the 4 genes cited above were found significantly higher in 15D embryos compared to control ones, regardless of the considered line. These data demonstrated that the cold thermal manipulation was efficient.

Zootechnical performance and whole body composition impacted by the genetic line but not the thermal exposure history

After the early thermal manipulation of eggs, all the fish were reared under the same environmental conditions (fed with the same diets at the same water temperature) during 20–21 weeks in order to test for the existence or not of a thermal programming. At 189 dpf, 120 juveniles (10 fish per tank) were individually weighed; mean fish weight ranged between 32.4 ± 6.7 g (15D fat line) and 38.5 ± 7.4 g (control lean line).

After the 20–21 weeks, the zootechnical performances of the two trout lines subjected or not to the early thermal manipulation are shown in Table 2. For the calculation of these performances, the entire bulk weighed divided per the number of fish were considered (measure done on 3 tanks per condition). None of the studied parameters were impacted by an interaction between the line and the early thermal manipulation. However, for SGR (specific growth rate), feed intake and feed efficiencies (calculated with formulas previously described in33), there was a global line effect, whereas the early thermal manipulation only decreased the SGR.

As expected, higher levels of lipids (and concomitantly lower levels of proteins) were found in the fat line compared to the lean line (Table 2), but body composition was not affected by the early thermal manipulation.

Lipid and cholesterol metabolism in juvenile fish slightly affected by the thermal exposure at eyed-stage

At juvenile stage, plasma and liver were sampled to measure metabolites’ levels and expression of lipid/cholesterol metabolism-related genes, respectively.

In plasma, a statistically significant interaction was found between lines and temperature (early thermal manipulation) for free fatty acids (FFA) and cholesterol. FFA levels were found to be the same in the fat line independently of the temperature history, whereas in the lean line they were higher in 15D fish (1.13 nmol/l) compared to control fish (0.79 nmol/l). (Table 3). Cholesterol levels were similar in the lean line whatever the temperature history, whereas in the fat line cholesterol level was lower in 15D fish (5.44 g/l) compared to control fish (6.76 g/l). No significant differences were found for glucose and triglycerides regardless of the condition.

For lipid metabolism-related genes (Table 4), only cpt1a mRNA level exhibited a significant statistical interaction between line and temperature (early thermal manipulation), displaying a higher level in 15D fat fish compared 15D lean fish. The early thermal manipulation had no significant effect on studied mRNA levels, whereas the line had a significant impact. Indeed, for genes related to fatty acid β-oxidation, the lean line tended to display higher mRNA levels of cpt1ba, cpt1bb, acox1 and acox3 than the fat line. In contrast, for genes involved in PUFA biosynthesis, the lean line tended to show lower mRNA levels than the fat line for genes such as scdba and scdbe, fads6a and fads6b. For the de novo lipogenesis metabolic pathway, only aca-β-a and acsf2c were significantly different, showing higher levels in the lean line compared to the fat line.

We then studied for the first time the cholesterol metabolism-related genes in the two trout lines (Table 5). No effect of the early thermal manipulation nor of the interaction between line and temperature were observed for the cholesterol metabolism-related genes. Two genes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis (screbp2a and idi1), and two genes involved in the cholesterol elimination (lxrαb and abca-1a) showed significant differences in mRNA levels between lines, with a tendency for higher mRNA levels in the lean line.

Discussion

In this study, we used two lines of rainbow trout selected for their muscle lipid content: the fat and lean lines27. The fish used in this study were produced through seven cycles of the same selection pressure (fat content measurement in muscle). This final generation had never previously been studied at a molecular level. Furthermore, a systematic analysis of all paralogous and orthologous genes involved in lipid and cholesterol metabolism, which are specific to the recent whole genome duplication in salmonids47, was performed for the first time using these rainbow trout lines. The first objective of the present study was therefore to identify differences between the lines at this 7th generation of selection, based on their zootechnical performance and lipid/cholesterol metabolism.

The fat and lean lines from the 7th generation of selection process present divergent growth performances. Indeed, regarding the zootechnical parameters, fat line (the one selected for a higher muscle lipid content) showed a higher specific growth rate associated with a higher feed intake but lower feed efficiencies, as previously observed after 3 or 4 generations of selection in different studies29–32. On the other hand, the fat and lean lines were previously described to have divergent intermediary metabolism as well as different nutritional responses to diets, irrespective of the dietary formulation.

In agreement with previous studies, the present data confirm that the two lines still differ in the hepatic regulation of lipid metabolism at the 7th generation of selection. Regarding fatty-acid β-oxidation, the overall pattern observed here suggests a lower oxidative signature in the fat line, since cpt1ba, cpt1bb, acox1 and acox3 were globally lower in this line, whereas hadh was unchanged. The opposite pattern observed for cpt1a likely reflects a gene-specific regulation rather than a globally enhanced β-oxidation capacity in the fat line. A similar context- and genotype-dependent regulation of hepatic fatty-acid oxidation pathways has been reported in other fish species, including atlantic salmon and gilthead sea bream, and the diversification of cpt1 paralogs/orthologs in teleosts further supports the possibility of gene-specific regulation among β-oxidation markers48–50. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies on these divergent lines, which showed higher lipid retention and suggested a greater propensity for lipid deposition in the fat line28, in line with the initial selection history of these lines for divergent muscle lipid content27. Moreover, the line × temperature interaction was detected only for cpt1a and was not supported by similar changes in the other β-oxidation-related genes, suggesting a limited response to early cold exposure. Because only transcript levels were measured, this result should be interpreted cautiously and does not demonstrate higher mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation per se.

Particular attention was paid to fads and elovl genes because desaturases and elongases are key components of the endogenous LC-PUFA biosynthesis pathway, including the synthesis of EPA and DHA. This point is especially relevant here because EPA and DHA are major contributors to the nutritional quality of fish flesh and, consequently, to product quality. It is also particularly relevant in the present model, since previous work in these divergent rainbow trout lines showed differences in fatty acid metabolism and flesh fatty acid profile, suggesting differences in LC-PUFA biosynthetic capacity34. In salmonids, Elovl2- and Elovl5-related pathways play central roles in endogenous LC-PUFA biosynthesis35, and functional evidence in Atlantic salmon further supports the importance of this pathway for DHA synthesis and tissue LC-PUFA balance51. In addition, differences in EPA and DHA content have been associated with the expression of key metabolic genes in Atlantic salmon52. Similar Fads- and Elovl-dependent differences in endogenous LC-PUFA biosynthetic capacity have also been described across teleost fish species, including gilthead sea bream in which fads2 expression has been linked to LC-PUFA bioconversion capacity and DHA-related traits36,53, Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua), where hepatic elovl and fadsd6 expression responded to dietary replacement of fish oil and reflected the species’ limited LC-PUFA biosynthetic ability37, Japanese sea bass (Lateolabrax japonicus), in which increased n-3 LC-PUFA levels were associated with Elovl5 upregulation38, and rabbitfish (Siganus canaliculatus), a marine teleost model in which LC-PUFA biosynthesis is strongly regulated through Fads2- and Elovl5-dependent mechanisms54. However, our results do not support a generalized up-regulation of the whole LC-PUFA biosynthetic pathway in the fat line. Rather, they suggest a selective and paralog-specific modulation of this pathway. Therefore, these transcriptional differences may indicate differences in LC-PUFA biosynthetic potential, but they cannot be interpreted as direct evidence of higher EPA or DHA levels in the product, because fatty-acid composition was not measured in the present study.

Interestingly, the differences in lipid metabolism observed between the two lines are associated with variations in energy utilisation after feeding and resistance to food deprivation through distinct lipid mobilisation strategies, the fat line being more impacted than the lean line40,41. We also studied for the first time in livers of fat and lean lines genes involved in cholesterol metabolism (biosynthesis, elimination, esterification). mRNA levels of only 2 genes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis (idi1 and screbp2a) and 2 involved in cholesterol elimination (lxrαb and abca1a) differed between lean and fat lines with a trend to be lower in the latter one. Taking caution into account, we can hypothesise that lower cholesterol metabolism in the fat line could be linked to its lower resistance to food deprivation, given the importance of cholesterol in stress resistance42. Overall, our data confirmed that some genes related to lipid/cholesterol metabolism in the liver were modified in relation to divergent selection of trout based on muscle lipid content. These findings also confirmed that selection based on lipid content, involving differentially expressed genes in lipid metabolism, has persisted for seven generations. In addition, by evaluating paralogs of lipid-related genes for the first time here in the two lines, our data showed that some studied paralogs displayed an identical the expression pattern between lines (for example cpt1ba/cpt1bb, fads6a/fads6b, aca-αa/aca-αb, hmgcra/hmgcrb, mvdaa/mvdab…). In contrast, for other paralogs one was differentially regulated regarding the line whereas the expression pattern of the second one remained identical regardless of the line (for example scdba/scdbb, elovl5a/elovl5b, aca-β-a/aca-β-b, screbp2a/screbp2b, lxrαa/lxrαb, abca-1a/abca-1b). Such results may suggest a genetic impact on regulation of paralogs’ expression.

Beyond the characterization of the 7th generation of selected fat and lean lines, the second objective of the present study was to analyse the effect of an early thermal cold stimulus mimicking the well-used farmed practice undergone by eyed-stage embryos for shipping purpose. For the last decades, studies in mammals, birds and fish have shown that early environmental stimuli can permanently change the metabolism later in life, which is called metabolic programming1,2. This is particularly true for poikilothermic and oviparous animals such as trout. Especially, several studies in fish have shown that temperature experienced during early development can impact phenotypes later in life43–45 and more particularly growth and intermediary metabolism32,46–49. While previous studies have focused on the effects of increased temperature in the context of global warming or exposure to low temperatures during the juvenile or adult stages43–45, to our knowledge, none have reported on the impact of early cold exposure during development and its consequences for trout in later life. Thus, our study aimed to analyse how early cold exposure at the eyed stage impacts hepatic metabolism in fat and lean trout selected lines in the long term (at a molecular level). First, we confirmed that the animals had efficiently received the thermal manipulation immediately after the stimulus by analysing temperature-sensitive genes. Our data then showed that the specific growth rate was lower overall in the 15D fish compared to the control group. Previous studies have shown that SGR is positively correlated with water temperature in salmonids50. This difference may also reflect developmental retardation induced by early cold exposure, which is not compensated for later in life. Additionally, we demonstrated that plasma FFA levels were higher in 15D fish in the lean line. Similar variations were previously observed in juvenile pacu (Piaractus brachypomus) exposed to low culture temperatures51. However, the opposite trend was observed in the fat line, which is consistent with their divergent metabolism. Interestingly, no effect of early cold manipulation on the mRNA levels of genes related to lipid and cholesterol metabolism was found in juveniles, suggesting that there was no metabolic programming at the eyed stage due to the early decrease in water temperature. Overall, our findings demonstrated that cold exposure at the eyed stage has few and tenuous effects on fish intermediary metabolism later in life, which is positive for the salmon farming sector.

In conclusion, the 7th cycle of selection revealed that the fat and lean lines exhibited the same differences in zootechnical parameters and hepatic lipid metabolism as observed in earlier generations. Conversely, no metabolic programming associated with early cold manipulation was observed in this study, indicating that thermal manipulation at the eyed stage does not have a long-term detrimental impact on rainbow trout. Fish breeders can therefore use this practice without fear of negative consequences. However, further studies are needed to confirm that parameters such as fish welfare, health and robustness are not affected by the cold manipulation of eggs.
