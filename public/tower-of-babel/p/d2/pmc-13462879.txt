Ultra-high tumor mutation burden breast cancer: genomic profiling and real-world immunotherapy results
Authors: Kristina Fanucci, Mariya Rozenblit, Julia C. F. Quintanilha, Ryon P. Graf, Neal Fischbach, Maureen Pelletier, Abirami Sivapiragasam, Prashanth Ashok Kumar, Mansi Kallem, Ethan S. Sokol, Smruthy Sivakumar, Dean C. Pavlick, Jeffrey S. Ross, Maryam Lustberg, Lajos Pusztai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with metastatic breast cancer and tumor mutational burden (TMB) ≥10 mutations/megabase are eligible for immunotherapy, but clinical and genomic characteristics and outcomes of patients with ultra-high TMB (UHTMB) ≥20 mutations/megabase remain under-characterized. In this retrospective cohort and comparative effectiveness study we examined comprehensive genomic profiling assessed on tumors as part of routine care. Real-world time to next treatment (TTNT), and overall survival (rwOS) data were collected from the Flatiron Database. 45/2049 (2.2%) patients had UHTMB; patients with UHTMB were more likely to have estrogen receptor-positive disease (86.6% vs. 70.0%), lobular histology (40.0% vs. 14.5%), and PIK3CA mutation (81.8% vs. 37.9%) vs. patients with TMB ≤ 20. Patients with UHTMB who received immunotherapy had longer TTNT (4.9 vs. 2.6 months, hazard ratio (HR) = 0.49, 95% confidence interval (CI):0.26–0.91,p = 0.025) and longer rwOS (14.1 vs. 3.2 months, HR = 0.37, 95%CI:0.18–0.76,p = 0.007) than patients with TMB <10. Single agent immunotherapy is an important treatment option for patients with UHTMB.

Introduction

Metastatic breast cancer (MBC) is the most common metastatic disease in women, with more than 150,000 women in the United States living with MBC1. The current standard of care first-line treatments vary by tumor subtype. For patients with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) with programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1)-positive disease (i.e. combined positivity score (CPS) ≥ 10) pembrolizumab (anti-programmed cell death protein 1 [PD-1] antibody) is the recommended first-line therapy in combination with either paclitaxel, nab-paclitaxel, or gemcitabine plus carboplatin2. In other breast cancers (BC) subtypes, treatment with pembrolizumab is limited to cancers with high tumor mutational burden (TMB) defined as ≥10 mutations per megabase (mut/Mb) by a US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved test3.

However, the efficacy of single agent pembrolizumab in MBC remains uncertain outside of biomarker-defined groups. Pembrolizumab has revolutionized treatment in many cancer types4–7, and the FDA issued a tumor-agnostic approval for this agent in 2020 for use in solid tumors with TMB ≥ 10 that have progressed on prior treatment without additional, more promising treatment approaches available8. This approval was based on results of the single arm Keynote-158 trial which enrolled patients with different solid tumor types with progression on or intolerance to one or more lines of standard therapy to receive pembrolizumab 200 mg IV every 3 weeks3 together with additional data supporting TMB as a clinically valid marker which was reviewed as supplemental evidence by the FDA8. An exploratory analysis of the data from this trial used a cutoff point of TMB ≥ 10 to define TMB-high and non-TMB-high tumors. This threshold was selected based on prior data that showed it optimized for clinical utility9,10. TMB-high status was found to result in higher objective response rate (ORR) and had evidence of progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) benefit in later portions of the survival curves. The Keynote-158 trial did not include any patients with MBC, and therefore it remains uncertain how accurately the high TMB threshold of ≥10 applies to BC. The subsequent TAPUR trial enrolled 28 patients with BC with TMB ≥ 10 and tested the single agent efficacy of pembrolizumab. It found a disease control rate of 37%, overall response rate (ORR) of 21%, median PFS (mPFS) of 2.5 months, and median OS (mOS) of 7.1 months. In this small study with limited range of TMB values, there was no relationship between TMB value and survival11. The NIMBUS trial of ipilimumab and nivolumab enrolled 31 patients with TMB ≥ 9 HER2-negative MBC and found that tumors with TMB ≥ 14 (n = 5) had a response rate of 60% compared to 4% in tumors with TMB 9–14, opening the question of whether setting a TMB cutoff of 10 or above is the optimal threshold to predict benefit from immunotherapy across cancer types12. A recent evaluation of >8000 patients treated with single-agent immunotherapy in standard of care settings observed consistent enrichment for more favorable OS across tumor types for patients with TMB ≥ 10 as measured by an FDA-approved clinical diagnostic for pembrolizumab, BC included13. The analysis also observed greater enrichment of benefit in BC as part of an exploratory analysis with higher TMB cutoffs; however, the study did not separate out this association by BC subtype, and it was unclear how much of this was associated with hormone receptor-positive (HR + ) BC.

In general, BC has a low TMB (median 2.63 mut/Mb) and only 8.4% of MBCs have TMB ≥ 10 mut/Mb14. Several studies showed that high TMB tumors are more likely to have higher expression of CD8-positive T-cells and are commonly PD-L1 positive, features that are also independently associated with benefit from immunotherapy14. However, in other studies high TMB was not shown to correlate with CD8 + T-cell infiltration across several cancer types including BC15. Metastatic invasive lobular cancers are more likely to have TMB ≥ 10 than invasive ductal carcinomas, (17% vs.8%, respectively)14. Apolipoprotein B mRNA editing catalytic polypeptide (APOBEC) activity was the most common mutation signature in TMB ≥ 10 cancers followed by mismatch repair (MMR)-deficiency signature14. Higher TMB is associated with improved OS with immunotherapy16 but to our knowledge genomic analysis and survival outcomes for a larger cohort of patients with ultra-high TMB (UHTMB), defined as TMB ≥ 20 mut/Mb have not been described. In this paper, we report the results of analyses describing the genomic profile of patients with UHTMB as well as real world outcomes for patients with UHTMB HR + /human epidermal growth factor-negative (HR + /HER2-) MBC.

Results

Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) results of patients with clinically advanced BC (CABC) (cohort A)

Cohort A included 2049 patients: 165 (8.1%) had high TMB ( ≥ 10 mut/Mb) and 45 (2.2%) had UHTMB ( ≥ 20 mut/Mb). In the UHTMB group, the mean TMB was 32.4 and the median was 26.3. In the TMB < 20 group, the mean TMB was 3.5 and the median was 2.5. Patients with UHTMB ≥ 20 were older, with a mean age of 64.6 (range 34–88) compared to 58.2 (24-89 + ), p = 0.0001. Estrogen receptor-positive (ER + ) BC was more common in the UHTMB group (86.6%) compared to TMB < 20 group (68.2%), p = 0.013, as was lobular histology (40% vs. 14.5%, p < 0.0001). APOBEC signature was more common in tumors with UHTMB (82.5%) compared to TMB < 20 (60.3%), p = 0.009. Genomic alteration in ARID1A (25.0% vs. 5.0%, p < 0.0001), MAP3K1 (15.9% vs. 6.2%, p = 0.023), NF1 (20.5% vs. 5.9%, p = 0.0014), CDKN2A (11.4% vs. 3.2% p = 0.017), PIK3CA (81.8% vs. 37.9%, p < 0.0001), and CDH1 (45.5% vs. 12.3%, p < 0.0001) were more common in UHTMB compared to TMB < 20. Microsatellite instability (MSI)-high status was found in 11.6% of patients with UHTMB vs. 0.4% of patients with TMB < 20, p < 0.0001. PDL1 (CD274) gene amplification (2.3% vs 1.3%) or protein expression measured by Ventana PS142 assay (57.1% vs. 51.1%) were not significantly different between the two TMB groups. In 35/45 patients with UHTMB, HER2 immunohistochemistry (IHC) data were available. Eleven (31.4%) were HER2 0+ by IHC, 21 (60%) were HER2-low (9 [25.7%] with IHC 1 + , 12 [34.3%] IHC 2 + / in situ hybridization [ISH] negative), and 3 (8.6%) were IHC 3+ (Table 1). Survival outcomes in cohort B of patients with ER + /HER2- CABC

1924 patients with ER + /HER2- disease had treatment data available. Eighty-nine received single agent immunotherapy, and 1,835 received chemotherapy without immunotherapy (Supplementary Fig 1). Baseline characteristics for patients receiving single agent immunotherapy or chemotherapy are shown in Table 2. Of the patients who received single agent immunotherapy, 70 had TMB < 20 and 19 had UHTMB. The majority of patients receiving single agent immunotherapy were receiving it as fourth or later line therapy (67.4%) while 46.5% of patients were receiving their first-line chemotherapy after 4 or more prior non-chemotherapy treatments. Performance status of patients receiving single agent immunotherapy was poorer (30.1% vs. 16.8% of patients with Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status ≥2) than for patients receiving chemotherapy. Most patients treated with immunotherapy received pembrolizumab (85.4%). Discussion

UHTMB MBC is a rare yet clinically important subset of advanced BC that is associated with APOBEC mutagenesis. Many UHTMB BCs have ER+ lobular histology and frequently harbor alterations in CDH1 and PIK3CA. Patients with UHTMB were more likely to benefit from single agent immunotherapy than patients with TMB < 20 with significantly increased TTNT of 4.9 vs. 2.6 months and increased rwOS of 14.1 vs. 4.9 months. Importantly, these data do not demonstrate that patients who have UHTMB live longer regardless of treatment course, as patients with UHTMB treated with chemotherapy without immunotherapy were not found to have improved rwOS compared to patients with TMB < 20. While the numbers of patients treated with UHTMB is small, this analysis is consistent with an enriched clinical benefit to patients with UHTMB treated with single agent immunotherapy.

These data retrospectively show that immunotherapy is of greater benefit to patients with UHTMB compared to those with lower TMB, and a prospective trial would help to more definitively define the benefit of immunotherapy in this population and clarify the optimal TMB cutoff at which breast tumors are most likely to respond. Exploratory analysis of the NIMBUS trial of dual immunotherapy with ipilimumab and nivolumab in HER2-negative MBC found that the response rate was 60% for tumors with TMB ≥ 14 compared to 4% for tumors with TMB 9 < 1412. A recent separate analysis using data from the Flatiron database which assessed rwOS in patients with advanced stage cancers treated with single-agent PD-(L)1 inhibitor, investigated benefit from these agents at different TMB cut points. This analysis found that in all BCs combined, the strongest HR for benefit was a cut point of TMB = 20, which motivated us to select TMB=20as our threshold to define UHTMB13.

The current standard of care treatments for patients with ER + MBC include endocrine therapies, often given in combination with targeted therapies, and chemotherapy or antibody-drug conjugates for endocrine resistant disease. The FDA-approved opportunity to treat patients with ER + MBC and high TMB (or MSI-high status) with pembrolizumab, which is often better tolerated than chemotherapy, may be underrecognized by providers17, as demonstrated in this real-world dataset where only 19 of the 48 patients (40%) with UHTMB received immunotherapy at some point in their treatment. Currently, information about the use of single agent immunotherapy is only listed in the “targeted therapies” section of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network® guidelines and is not highlighted explicitly in the systemic therapies for ER + MBC section like other treatments for targetable high frequency mutations18.

There are several limitations to this study. The number of patients treated with immunotherapy in this cohort is relatively small, the data used are retrospectively collected, and patients in this cohort had their treatments selected for them by their clinician rather than being randomized to receive a prescribed treatment. The factors that go into selecting patients to receive certain treatments are numerous and skewed by individual patient factors. It is possible the patients who ultimately received immunotherapy may have had more favorable clinical characteristics not accounted for in the data collected for this analysis, which may have biased the results. However, overall, the small group of UHTMB patients that received single agent immunotherapy was more heavily pre-treated and had worse performance status, which could not be adjusted properly with propensity scores, limiting the ability to conduct a multivariate analysis comparing outcomes with immunotherapy vs. chemotherapy in this group.

This analysis shows that patients with UHTMB have certain clinical and genomic characteristics and treatment with single agent immunotherapy results in clinically meaningful prolonged OS compared to TMB < 20, indicating that single agent immunotherapy is an important FDA-approved option for these patients even in the late-line treatment setting.

Methods

Study design and patient cohorts

Two distinct cohorts of patients with CABC defined as metastatic or locally advanced disease were investigated: cohort A) patients who received CGP at Foundation Medicine as part of routine clinical care, and cohort B) patients in the Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine Breast Clinico-Genomic Database (FH-FMI CGDB) with HR + /HER2- BC receiving single agent immunotherapy or chemotherapy. All trials were conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki. For cohort A the Western Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Protocol No. 20152817) issued a HIPAA waiver of authorization and an approval waiver of informed consent for this study. For cohort B, prior to study conduct IRB approval was obtained. This included a waiver of informed consent due to the observational and noninterventional nature of the study (WCG IRB, Protocol No. 420180044).

Cohort A: Specimens and analysis

Cohort A: Using either paraffin tissue blocks or a minimum of 40-micron thick unstained tissue section on glass slides, DNA was extracted from 2049 clinically advanced cases of CABC with available ER and progesterone IHC data. HER2 fluorescent ISH and IHC were also recorded where available. Hybrid capture-based CGP was then performed to examine all classes of genomic alterations (GA) as previously described19–21. TMB levels and MSI-high status were determined from the sequencing data as previously described22,23. PD-L1 was determined by IHC using the Dako 22C3 tumor proportional score system with 0% = negative; 1–49% = low positive and ≥50% = high positive expression. Results between the UHTMB and TMB < 20 groups were compared using the Fisher exact system with the Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment to correct for false discovery.

Cohort B: clinical data and analysis

Cohort B: Clinical data for some patients were available from the FH-FMI CGDB. The CGDB includes longitudinal, retrospective, deidentified clinical data collected from electronic health records (EHRs) from over 800 clinical sites associated with approximately 280 cancer clinics in the United States between January 2011-December 2023. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, time of therapy exposure, and survival data from patient-level structured and unstructured content were extracted from clinical notes as well as radiology and pathology reports (https://flatiron.com/database-characterization). These data were associated to genomic data derived from Foundation Medicine testing by deidentified, deterministic matching24. TTNT was defined as the treatment start date to the start of the next line of treatment (due to any cause) or death. Patients who had not reached next treatment line or death were right-censored at the date of the last clinical visit, medication order, or laboratory results. PFS was defined as the treatment start date until time of disease progression or death. Patients not reaching progression or death were right-censored on the date of the last clinical note. rwOS was calculated from the start of the specified treatment to death from any cause. Patients with no record of death were right-censored at the date of the last clinic visit or structured EHR activity. As patients do not enter the database until a CGP report is completed, rwOS risk intervals were left truncated to the CGP report date to account for immortal time25,26. The mortality information in the Flatiron Health database is derived from de-identified patient-level data from the EHR as well as the public Social Security Death Index and a commercial death dataset which includes data from obituaries and funeral homes. This mortality information has previously been validated with the National Death Index27. The Flatiron Health and Foundation Medicine CGDB has reproduced survival findings observed in biomarker subgroup analyses of randomized controlled trials28,29. TTNT, PFS, and rwOS were compared between patients receiving single-agent immunotherapy and between patients receiving chemotherapy without immunotherapy by TMB status (UHTMB vs. TMB < 20 mut/Mb and UHTMB vs TMB 10-19 mut/Mb) by Cox models. Patients were included in the chemotherapy group if they received a chemotherapy regimen without combination of a clinical trial drug, immunotherapy, hormone therapy, or targeted therapy. If a patient received multiple lines of chemotherapy meeting this criterion, only the first line was included. Multivariable analyses adjusting for age, ECOG performance status (ECOG reported closest to treatment initiation date), line of therapy, socioeconomic status index, bone-only vs. visceral metastasis, number of metastatic sites, and opioid prescription pre-therapy were performed (Supplementary Fig 2). TTNT, PFS, and rwOS were compared between patients receiving single-agent immunotherapy vs. chemotherapy by Cox models, applying inverse probability of treatment weighting to adjust for potential treatment selection biases. Inverse probability of treatment weights was calculated targeting the average treatment effect in the single-agent immunotherapy treated population, implemented with R package “MatchIt.” Weights were capped at 10 equivalents to limit influence per observation. Among patients receiving chemotherapy, those with characteristics most similar to patients receiving single-agent immunotherapy were weighted more, and those less like patients receiving single-agent immunotherapy were weighted less. These weights were included in all Kaplan-Meier visualizations and Cox PH models unless otherwise noted. Available features related to treatment assignment of single-agent immunotherapy versus chemotherapy included for adjustment in propensity models were stage at diagnosis (stage IV vs. III), ECOG performance status, age, opioid prescription pre-therapy, number of metastatic sites, line of therapy, and visceral metastasis. Standardized mean difference was utilized to assess balance, and within 10% was considered acceptable30.

Chi-squared and Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used to assess differences between groups of categorical and continuous variables, respectively. R version 4.1.3 software was used for analyses.
