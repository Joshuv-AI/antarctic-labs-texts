Diagnostic Considerations in the Evaluation of Athletes With Syncope

History of Presentation

A previously healthy 20-year-old man is evaluated in the Sports Cardiology Clinic after experiencing a brief syncopal episode while playing basketball. While walking to the bench in between games, he began to experience progressive lightheadedness, dizziness, and visual dimming. He did not have chest discomfort or palpitations. Upon standing from the bench, he fell to the floor with syncope that lasted for a few seconds. After regaining consciousness, he continued to feel lightheaded and fatigued until he drank water. After appropriate rehydration, he resumed play without recurrence of symptoms. During his clinical evaluation, he reported a several-year history of similar prodromal symptoms without syncope during basketball games. He also described intermittent, nonexertional, pressure-like chest discomfort lasting minutes to hours. Vital signs demonstrated elevated blood pressure (149/82 mm Hg), and cardiovascular examination was otherwise unremarkable, with no murmurs elicited even with dynamic maneuvers.

Past Medical History

He had no medical comorbidities, no family history of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) or sudden cardiac death (SCD), and no substance use. He consumed energy drinks occasionally but was actively reducing intake.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for exertional syncope in this young athlete included orthostatic syncope, reflex syncope, vasovagal syncope, HCM with dynamic left ventricular (LV) outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction, ventricular arrhythmias, and valvular pathology such as aortic stenosis.

Investigations

An electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated normal sinus rhythm with marked ST-T wave abnormalities concerning for apical HCM (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed normal LV systolic function with moderately increased septal wall thickness without significant apical tapering, further supporting the diagnosis of apical HCM. There was no LVOT obstruction with rest or Valsalva.

An exercise stress echocardiogram was performed for further evaluation. The stress ECG portion was nondiagnostic because of resting ST-T wave abnormalities, though there was no echocardiographic evidence of ischemia or LVOT obstruction with maximal exertion. In addition, no arrhythmias were observed.

Cardiac magnetic resonance demonstrated findings consistent with apical HCM with diffusely increased LV myocardial wall thickness (Figure 2) and normal biventricular systolic function. Focal midmyocardial late gadolinium enhancement was noted within the mid-to-distal anteroseptal LV wall (Figure 3). T1 mapping times and extracellular volume were within normal limits throughout the remainder of the myocardium.

Management

After comprehensive evaluation and structured risk stratification, his syncope was attributed to orthostatic syncope in the context of a preload-dependent structurally susceptible ventricle sensitive to intravascular volume depletion. Through shared decision-making, he continued to play basketball with education on appropriate hydration and stimulant avoidance, annual transthoracic echocardiography, ambulatory rhythm monitoring, and referral for genetic counseling and testing per the 2024 multisociety Guideline on the Management of HCM.

Outcome and Follow-Up

With attention to sufficient fluid intake and vigilance for prodromal symptoms, he has had no recurrent episodes of syncope and continues participating in basketball. Annual SCD risk stratification is ongoing, and genetic testing results are pending.

Discussion

This case illustrates the nuanced evaluation of syncope in an athlete with apical HCM and the evolving landscape of risk assessment and sports participation in individuals with genetic cardiomyopathies. An important initial observation is that historical features that may suggest benign etiologies in the general population can carry different diagnostic implications in patients with structural heart disease. Orthostatic or reflex syncope is classically preceded by prodromal symptoms such as lightheadedness, visual dimming, or dizziness, whereas arrhythmogenic syncope more often presents as an abrupt loss of consciousness without prodromal symptoms and in the absence of positional triggers. Accordingly, a detailed history and physical examination are central to the initial assessment of syncope and to differentiating between cardiac and noncardiac causes.1 Key historical elements include the timing of symptoms in relation to exertion, the absence or presence of prodromal symptoms, associated symptoms (eg, palpitations, chest pain, and shortness of breath), and a family history of SCD. This is where a collaborative team approach, including a sports cardiologist, can be helpful.

Importantly, syncope is relatively common in HCM, reported in approximately 15.8% of patients, and the underlying mechanism remains unexplained in the majority (91%) of cases.2 This highlights the diagnostic uncertainty that often surrounds syncope in HCM and underscores the importance of careful clinical adjudication in athletes rather than reflexively attributing symptoms to malignant etiologies and restricting from sport. In this athlete, several features strongly supported a more benign clinical diagnosis consistent with orthostatic syncope, including the presence of prodromal symptoms and the positional trigger of standing from a seated position. Nevertheless, although the historical features were quite reassuring in this case, comprehensive evaluation of syncope in athletes requires integration with structured risk stratification to appropriately contextualize symptoms and guide management.

Initial studies in the evaluation of syncope in athletes with HCM include a 12-lead ECG, echocardiogram, and an exercise stress test, commonly performed as an exercise stress echocardiogram to assess for dynamic LV outflow tract obstruction. If nondiagnostic or if high-risk features are present, advanced imaging (eg, cardiac magnetic resonance) should be considered.1 To rule out underlying arrhythmia as a cause, ambulatory ECG monitoring and implantable loop recorders may also be used.3,4 Genetic testing may be helpful to exclude other types of arrhythmic risk such as catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia.

As in all patients with HCM, athletes must also undergo risk stratification for SCD, considering any history of unexplained syncope, a family history of SCD, maximal LV wall thickness, the presence of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, and imaging findings such as apical aneurysm or extensive fibrosis.5,6 A strong predictor of SCD is when a recent syncopal episode is thought to be arrhythmogenic, which then may warrant consideration of implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation.3,5 This risk assessment for SCD should be repeated every 1 to 2 years, especially in the younger population whose phenotype may evolve over time.1,5

Historically, athletes with HCM were uniformly advised to restrict or avoid sports participation. Now, individualized, risk-based recommendations are in place. Those with phenotype-positive HCM may compete in sports in the setting of appropriate therapy, expert assessment, and emergency action planning (access to automated external defibrillator), as long as the athlete shows no signs of high-risk features (eg, exercise-induced syncope or documented ventricular arrhythmias).1,7,8 The LIVE-HCM and Return-to-Play in Elite Athlete studies further support this approach. In this prospective cohort study, the risk of death or life-threatening arrhythmia was not higher among individuals with HCM or genotype-positive/phenotype-negative patients who continue vigorous exercise compared with those who were sedentary or engaging in moderate intensity exercise.9,10 These findings reinforce the role of shared decision-making in this population and suggest that appropriately evaluated and monitored athletes with HCM may safely engage in higher-intensity sports.

In summary, the evaluation and management of syncope in athletic patients with HCM requires a multidisciplinary, evidence-based approach. Careful history-taking, targeted diagnostics, and ongoing risk assessment are essential for optimizing outcomes and guiding safe return-to-play decisions.1,5,7

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of comprehensive evaluation, multimodality imaging, and individualized risk stratification in athletes with apical HCM and syncope. Etiologies range from benign orthostatic intolerance to ventricular arrhythmias, each necessitating unique management strategies and shared decision-making regarding sports participation.
