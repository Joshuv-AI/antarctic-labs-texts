The impact of treatment processes on depression and anxiety outcomes in behavioral health treatment: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Lara Baez, Kelsey L McAlister, Chris Pleman, Tony Kaufmann, Jennifer Steiner, Nicole Siegfried, Jennifer L Huberty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnxiety and depression are prevalent mental health disorders, but traditional treatments often have limited effectiveness. Process-Based Therapy (PBT), which targets the underlying mechanisms driving these conditions, offers a promising alternative. The purpose of this retrospective study was to examine how processes of change relate to changes in anxiety and depressive symptoms in patients with elevated anxiety and/or depression symptoms who underwent PBT at Lightfully Behavioral Health, a behavioral health treatment clinic.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed data from adults (≥ 18 years) with elevated anxiety (n = 396) and depression (n = 434) who received treatment at Lightfully Behavioral Health, a PBT-based behavioral health treatment clinic. Mental health symptoms and PBT processes (i.e., psychological inflexibility, emotion dysregulation, thwarted belongingness, self-compassion, and personal values alignment) were measured at admission and discharge. Multiple logistic regression models, including stepwise models, tested associations between changes in processes and achievement of remission at discharge (yes vs. no) among those with elevated anxiety and depression. Bayesian structural equation models assessed whether changes in processes mediated the relationship between treatment dose and achieving anxiety and depression remission.ResultsImprovements in all PBT processes were related to achieving anxiety and depression remission (p < 0.001). However, only improvements in self-compassion (OR 2.92, p < 0.001) and psychological inflexibility (OR 1.05, p = 0.007) were associated with greater likelihood of achieving anxiety symptom remission, and self-compassion (OR 2.33, p < 0.001), alignment with personal values (OR 1.06, p = 0.006), psychological inflexibility (OR 1.08, p < 0.001), with greater likelihood of achieving depression symptom remission. Self-compassion was supported as a mediator in the relationship between treatment dose and anxiety remission, while both self-compassion and psychological inflexibility were supported as mediators for depression remission, as their 95% credible intervals excluded zero.ConclusionSelf-compassion and psychological inflexibility may be important for the remission of anxiety and depression symptoms among patients receiving PBT. These findings suggest that changes in self-compassion and psychological inflexibility are associated with remission and may represent important targets for intervention. While this study contributes to the limited evidence on PBT, further research is needed to better understand the long-term effects of these therapeutic processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05115-y.Keywords: Process-based therapy, Mental health, Mental health treatment, Internalizing symptoms, Psychiatric care

Introduction

Roughly half of adults in the United States will experience a clinically significant mood or anxiety disorder, such as Major Depressive Disorder or Generalized Anxiety Disorder, in their lifetime [1], and many more people experience significant distress and impairment from subclinical forms of these conditions [2, 3]. In the hope of decreasing the personal and societal burden of these conditions [4], decades of research and countless resources have been dedicated to searching for effective treatments. While there is evidence that some treatments work (namely, antidepressant medications and some forms of psychotherapy), average effect sizes reflect considerable individual variability in treatment response [5], and despite more than 1,000 randomized trials examining treatments for depression, there has been limited progress in identifying which patients benefit from which interventions [6].

This limited progress may be partially due to the field’s overreliance on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM) [7]. While the DSM remains the gold standard for clinical classification, it presents with well-documented constraints to guiding individualized treatment [8]. For instance, most individuals meet the criteria for more than one disorder simultaneously, leading to criticism that the DSM does not delineate psychopathology appropriately [9, 10]. Moreover, within-diagnosis heterogeneity (i.e., two individuals with the same diagnosis presenting with non-overlapping symptoms) is significant. For example, there are over 200 unique symptom profiles that meet the diagnostic threshold for Major Depressive Disorder alone [9]. Lastly, the DSM has shown limited predictive validity for treatment selection [11], suggesting that a diagnosis alone provides little guidance to clinicians for choosing among the variety of interventions available [6].

Research has largely focused on finding the “right” treatment for each DSM-defined disorder, as evidenced by the fact that most clinical trials of psychotherapy set out to evaluate a specific treatment for a specific disorder [12]. Yet this “one-size-fits-a-diagnosis” approach overlooks arguably the most important factors when selecting a treatment: the individual characteristics, history, needs, and goals of the patient [13]. Treatment approaches that consider these individual characteristics and contextual factors may offer a more precise way to conceptualize cases and design treatment plans for specific individuals. This perspective aligns with longstanding principles in clinical psychology that emphasize matching interventions to individual needs and circumstances [13].

Process-based therapy (PBT) builds on this individualized approach by shifting the focus from treating a specific diagnosis to targeting the underlying mechanisms, or processes, that drive and maintain mental health symptoms. Processes are defined as theory-based, multilevel mechanisms of change that are thought to occur in empirically established sequences oriented toward desired treatment outcomes [14] and are distinct from therapeutic procedures (i.e., the specific techniques used to target outcomes). PBT is a transtheoretical, person-centered approach to case conceptualization and treatment that targets the biopsychosocial processes that influence treatment outcomes. Unlike disorder-specific treatment protocols, which apply a fixed set of techniques to a DSM-defined syndrome, PBT does not prescribe a predetermined intervention sequence. Other dimensional frameworks, such as the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC; [15]), organize psychopathology around neurobiological constructs. However, PBT is unique in that it is oriented toward identifying modifiable biopsychosocial processes that can be directly targeted in clinical practice [14], offering a personalized, mechanism-focused approach to care.

PBT begins by identifying which processes are maintaining a given patient’s symptoms and then selects evidence-based techniques to target those processes specifically [14]. For example, one patient’s symptoms of depression may be maintained primarily by psychological inflexibility, whereas others may be rooted in low self-compassion or disconnection from personal values, each requiring a different therapeutic approach despite sharing the same diagnosis. These processes, rooted in therapeutic models, are theorized mechanisms of change that may contribute to more adaptive functioning when targeted therapeutically. To date, many processes of change, such as emotion regulation and psychological flexibility, have been examined extensively as mediators in intervention research, yet they are rarely synthesized into a single coherent treatment framework as PBT calls for, and PBT processes have not been extensively examined in real-world clinical settings.

Lightfully Behavioral Health is a large behavioral health provider that implements a PBT-based precision mental health treatment model. Lightfully’s approach to care focuses on five key processes of change, and aims to reduce psychological inflexibility, emotion dysregulation, and thwarted belongingness, and to enhance self-compassion and alignment with personal values. Upon admission, clinicians assess which of these processes are most relevant for a given patient and build an individualized treatment plan accordingly. For example, a patient identified as high in psychological inflexibility may receive Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)-based acceptance exercises, while one with low self-compassion may receive mindfulness-based self-compassion interventions, each selected for their established efficacy in shifting those specific processes [16]. These processes are well-established in foundational theories of therapeutic change, including cognitive-behavioral, acceptance-based, mindfulness, and interpersonal approaches [17–20]. Furthermore, these processes are established mediators of treatment outcomes. For example, a recent review found psychological flexibility was a significant mediator of treatment in 34 studies, emotion regulation in 5 studies, belongingness in 5 studies, self-compassion in 9 studies, and personal values alignment in 6 studies [21]. Despite evidence supporting the role of these processes in treatment, how these processes interact within structured clinical interventions remains largely unexplored, emphasizing the need for further research in applied settings.

Therefore, the purpose of this retrospective study was to examine how changes in psychological inflexibility, emotion dysregulation, thwarted belongingness, self-compassion, and personal values alignment relate to changes in anxiety and depressive symptoms in patients with elevated anxiety and/or depression symptoms who underwent PBT at Lightfully Behavioral Health. We aimed to: (1) investigate the association of each process of change with the likelihood of anxiety and depression symptom remission at discharge and (2) understand whether these processes of change mediate the associations of treatment dose and remission at discharge.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study analyzed secondary data from 552 adults with elevated anxiety and/or depression symptoms who received treatment from Lightfully Behavioral Health, a behavioral health treatment center whose approach is based on PBT. Lightfully providers are specialized in treating individuals at risk for suicide, as well as individuals with mood disorders and anxiety disorders. Adults were included in the study if they (1) were aged 18 years or older, (2) received treatment from Lightfully Behavioral Health between January 1, 2023 and January 29, 2025, and (3) had elevated (i.e., moderate to severe) anxiety and depression symptoms at admission. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational cohort studies (see Supplement, Table S1).

Ethical considerations

All patients at Lightfully Behavioral Health provide informed consent before beginning treatment, which includes details about available services and privacy policies. Because this study is a retrospective analysis of secondary data, it was exempt from additional consent requirements under human subjects regulations and received approval from the Biomedical Research Alliance of New York (BRANY) Institutional Review Board (Study ID 24-243-1858). All study data were de-identified before analysis, and participants did not receive compensation.

Study measures

Patients report demographic information including age, biological sex, gender identity, race, ethnicity, primary diagnosis, and level of care at admission. Treatment dose is measured using the number of days billed to insurance, which serves as a proxy for the amount of treatment received. At admission and at discharge, patients complete a questionnaire assessing symptoms of depression and anxiety, as well as mental health processes of change, including psychological inflexibility, emotional dysregulation, thwarted belongingness, self-compassion, and personal values alignment.

Anxiety and depression symptoms (Outcomes)

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) is a seven-item self-report measure used to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms over the past two weeks. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Not at all”) to 3 (“Nearly every day”). The total score ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety severity [22, 23]. Elevated anxiety symptoms were defined as a score of 10 or higher, as this indicates moderate to severe anxiety [22, 23]. The GAD-7 has demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89–0.92; 17,18).

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is a nine-item tool designed to assess the severity of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks. Responses are scored on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (“Not at all”) to 3 (“Nearly every day”). The total score ranges from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating greater depression severity [24]. Elevated depressive symptoms was defined as a score of 10 or higher, as this indicates moderate to severe depressive symptoms [24]. The PHQ-9 has demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89). Anxiety and depression remission statuses (achieved remission; yes versus no) were used as the primary outcome in this paper. Remission was defined as a score of less than five at discharge for both the GAD-7 and the PHQ-9, as a score less than 5 indicates minimal to no symptoms [25].

Mental health processes of change

Psychological inflexibility

The Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II is used to measure psychological inflexibility and experiential avoidance. Respondents rate seven items on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Never true”) to 7 (“Always true”). The sum of all items is used to generate a total score (score range: 7–49), with higher scores indicating greater psychological inflexibility and experiential avoidance [26]. The AAQ-II has demonstrated adequate to high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.78–0.88 [26], .

Emotion dysregulation

The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale-Short Form (DERS-SF) is used to measure emotion regulation deficits across six domains including awareness of emotions, understanding of emotions, acceptance of emotions, the ability to engage in goal-directed behavior when experiencing negative emotions, the ability to refrain from impulsive behavior when experiencing negative emotions, and access to emotion regulation strategies perceived as effective [27]. Participants rate 18 items on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“Almost never”) to 5 (“Almost always”). The average item score was computed, with higher scores indicating greater difficulties with emotion regulation. The DERS-SF has demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.96; [28].

Thwarted belongingness

The Thwarted Belongingness Subscale from the Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire assesses the extent to which individuals feel disconnected or lack a sense of belonging. Participants rate nine items on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (“Not at all true for me”) to 7 (“Very true for me”). The sum of all items was used to generate a total score (range = 7 to 63), with higher scores indicating greater thwarted belongingness [29, 30]. The Thwarted Belongingness Subscale has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.85; [30].

Self-compassion

The Self-Compassion Scale assesses six aspects of self-compassion: self-kindness, self-judgment, mindfulness, common humanity, isolation, and over-identification. Participants rate 12 items on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Almost never”) to 5 (“Almost always”). The average item score was computed, with higher scores indicating greater self-compassion [31]. The Self-Compassion Scale has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.86; [32].

Personal values alignment

The Progress Subscale of the Valuing Questionnaire assesses the extent to which individuals engage in behaviors aligned with their personal values and goals. Participants rated five items on a 7-point Likert scale from 0 (“Not at all true”) to 6 (“Completely true”). Item-level examples include “I did things in line with what matters most to me” and “I acted in ways that I am proud of.” The sum of all items was used to compute a total score (range = 0–35) with higher scores indicating better alignment with personal values [33]. The Progress Subscale of the Valuing Questionnaire has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.86; [33].

Treatment

Lightfully Behavioral Health is a behavioral health treatment center rooted in the PBT treatment model and offers personalized, evidence-based treatment of suicidality, mood, and anxiety disorders, among other mental health conditions. The overarching treatment goal is to improve patient wellbeing, prevent relapse, and reduce vulnerability to future mental health disorders by addressing the fundamental, interconnected processes that underpin mental health symptoms.

Treatment at Lightfully is delivered by a multidisciplinary team of providers, including psychotherapists, psychiatrists (at PHP and RTC), nurses, and behavioral health technicians. Across all levels of care, patients receive individual therapy, group therapy, skills-based interventions, and psychiatric care. Lightfully’s stepped-care model includes Residential Treatment Centers (RTC), Partial Hospitalization Programs (PHP), Intensive Outpatient Programs (IOP), and Virtual IOP. In RTC, patients receive approximately three individual therapy sessions per week, daily group sessions (up to five per day), weekly family sessions, and regular psychiatric and nursing support within a 24-hour care setting. In PHP, patients typically participate in 5–6 h of programming per day, including approximately two individual therapy sessions per week, multiple daily group sessions, weekly family sessions, and psychiatric care. In IOP, patients engage in 3–4 h of programming per day, including one individual therapy session per week, daily group sessions, and ongoing family therapy, with psychiatric support as needed. Virtual IOP follows a similar structure to in-person IOP, delivered via telehealth.

Upon admission to treatment and at discharge, patients complete a comprehensive assessment designed to evaluate both the severity of their mental health symptoms and underlying processes of change. Therapists utilize assessments to identify underlying processes of the patient’s mental health symptoms and create a process-based case conceptualization and treatment plan. PBT is not a manualized treatment protocol, but provides clinicians with a conceptual framework to identify and target processes that are most relevant for a specific patient. At Lightfully Behavioral Health, this process is operationalized through a structured assessment battery and process-based case conceptualization administered at admission. This flexibility is a core feature of PBT, allowing clinicians to tailor treatment to the individual patient. To ensure high-quality implementation of PBT, Lightfully provides comprehensive and robust onboarding and on-going training to support clinicians in the application of therapeutic techniques used within the model.

Within this framework, specific evidence-based interventions are selected to target identified processes of change. For example, psychological inflexibility is commonly addressed using ACT-based strategies, including cognitive defusion, acceptance, and values-based behavioral activation. Self-compassion may be targeted through mindfulness-based and self-compassion interventions. Thwarted belongingness is commonly addressed through interpersonal and group-based interventions that emphasize social connection, communication skills, and relational engagement. Emotion dysregulation may be targeted using skills drawn from dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) and other emotion regulation frameworks. These interventions are flexibly applied based on each patient’s process profile rather than delivered in a fixed sequence.

Statistical analysis

Only adults with elevated anxiety and/or depression symptoms at admission (i.e., a GAD-7 and/or PHQ-9 score ≥ 10) were included in analyses, with separate models conducted for each condition. As a result, some individuals were included in both analyses. As this was a retrospective study using routinely collected clinical data, the sample size was determined by the number of patients with complete data at admission and discharge. The final sample size was consistent with similar real-world observational studies and included a sufficient number of outcome events to support the planned multivariable analyses, consistent with methodological guidance on regression modeling [34, 35]. Descriptive statistics (i.e., mean and standard deviation (SD); frequency and percent) were computed for demographic and clinical characteristics. Change scores were computed for anxiety and depression symptoms, as well as for each process of change (i.e., psychological inflexibility, emotion dysregulation, thwarted belongingness, self-compassion, personal values alignment). Change scores were calculated so that negative values reflected reductions in psychological distress, psychological inflexibility, emotion dysregulation, and thwarted belongingness from admission to discharge, while positive values indicated increases in self-compassion and personal values alignment. Effect sizes (i.e., Cohen’s d) were calculated for each change score to quantify the magnitude of change from admission to discharge and to facilitate comparisons across constructs. Paired t-tests were used to compare scores from admission to discharge, assessing whether changes in anxiety, depression, and PBT processes scores were statistically significant.

Separate logistic regression models were used to examine the association between changes in process of change variables and the likelihood of achieving remission (yes versus no) for anxiety and depression. For ease of interpretation, the direction of psychological inflexibility, emotion dysregulation, and thwarted belongingness was reversed so that higher values reflected reductions in these processes. As a result, odds ratios (ORs) represent the association between a one-unit decrease (i.e., improvement) in these variables and the odds of achieving remission. Models adjusted for age (continuous; in years) biological sex (dichotomous; female versus male), and treatment dose (continuous; number of days billed to the insurance company). Level of care (categorical; RTC versus PHP versus IOP versus multiple levels) was excluded from the models due to its high correlation with treatment dose and lack of significance as a covariate (p’s > 0.10). We also tested including both elevated anxiety and depression at admission as a covariate, but it was not significant (p’s > 0.10). To maintain model parsimony, we opted to exclude these from the final analyses. A forward stepwise logistic regression model was used to identify the process of change variables most strongly associated with anxiety and depression remission. Variables were entered into the model if p < 0.05 and removed if p ≥ 0.10, allowing only the most predictive variables to remain in the final model.

Mediation analyses were conducted to examine whether treatment dose influenced anxiety and depression remission indirectly through psychological inflexibility, emotion dysregulation, thwarted belongingness, self-compassion, and personal values alignment. Given the dichotomous outcome variable (achieved remission: yes/no), Bayesian structural equation modeling was used in Mplus (version 8) with a logit link function to model the outcome. Indirect effects were estimated using posterior distributions, and statistical significance was determined using credible intervals (CIs), where effects were considered significant if the 95% CI did not include zero. The total effect of treatment dose on remission was decomposed into direct effects (treatment dose → remission) and indirect effects (treatment dose → mediators → remission). Covariates included age and admission symptom score, which were retained in all models to control for potential confounding effects. All mediators were included in the model regardless of statistical significance, following the PBT framework, which emphasizes understanding how multiple interacting mechanisms contribute to symptom change [36]. Model fit was evaluated using posterior predictive p-values (PPP), with values close to 0.50 indicating acceptable fit. Mediator residuals were allowed to covary to account for shared variance, and all models were run using Bayesian estimation procedures. For interpretability, odds ratios for psychological inflexibility, thwarted belongingness, and emotion dysregulation were inverted so that values greater than 1 reflect increased odds of remission when these processes decrease. Analyses were conducted using complete cases. Participants with missing data on variables of interest were excluded from the respective analyses. Given the retrospective nature of the dataset and the use of routinely collected clinical data, no imputation procedures were applied.

Bias mitigation

To mitigate potential conflicts of interest, the following safeguards were applied: (1) research questions were developed by the Fit Minded scientific team based on scientific merit and were not prescribed by the client; (2) study outcomes were not guaranteed as contractual deliverables; (3) the analysis plan was pre-specified prior to data access; and (4) data analysis and interpretation were led by Fit Minded authors operating independently of Lightfully Behavioral Health. No author's employment status or compensation is contingent upon the direction or outcome of the findings.

Results

Data availability and descriptive characteristics

Of the 1,435 patients who received treatment during the study period, 552 had complete data at both admission and discharge across all variables of interest and were included in the analytic sample. The remaining 883 patients were excluded due to missing data on one or more variables. Out of these 552 patients, 396 (71.74%) had elevated anxiety symptoms, 434 (78.62%) had elevated depression symptoms, and 361 (65.40%) had both elevated anxiety and depression symptoms. Participants with elevated anxiety had a mean age of 34.48 (± 11.49) years, while those with elevated depression had a mean age of 33.78 (± 11.25) years. Most patients were female (73.74% and 74.19%), identified as their birth gender (92.42% and 92.86%), and were White (44.95% and 43.78%). Most patients had a primary diagnosis of depression (56.90% and 63.82%). At discharge, 125 (31.57%) of those with elevated anxiety at admission achieved remission (i.e., minimal to no anxiety symptoms), while 126 (29.03%) of those with elevated depression symptoms achieved remission (Table 1).

Changes in anxiety, depression, and processes from admission to discharge

Participants with elevated anxiety (n = 396 out of 552) showed notable reductions in both anxiety (Cohen’s d=-1.20) and depression symptoms (Cohen’s d=-1.10) from admission to discharge with large effect sizes. They also experienced decreases in psychological inflexibility (Cohen’s d=-0.82), emotion dysregulation (Cohen’s d=-0.79), and thwarted belongingness (Cohen’s d=-0.74), and increases in self-compassion (Cohen’s d = 0.95) and personal values alignment (Cohen’s d = 0.90). Similarly, those with elevated depression (n = 434 out of 552) showed notable reductions in anxiety (Cohen’s d=-1.06) and depression (Cohen’s d=-1.28) from admission to discharge. They also experienced decreases in psychological inflexibility (Cohen’s d=-0.83), emotion dysregulation (Cohen’s d=-0.80), and thwarted belongingness (Cohen’s d=-0.80), and increases in self-compassion (Cohen’s d = 0.97) and personal values alignment (Cohen’s d = 0.92). Paired t-tests confirmed that changes from admission to discharge were statistically significant (p < 0.001 for all) across all mental health symptoms and PBT processes (Table 2).

Association of changes in processes and achieving anxiety and depression remission

Separate logistic regression models were used to assess how each PBT process of change was associated with remission at discharge (Table 3). Among participants with elevated anxiety at admission (n = 396 out of 552), reductions in psychological inflexibility (OR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.11, 1.16, p < 0.001), emotion dysregulation (OR = 3.72, 95% CI 2.62, 5.28, p < 0.001), and thwarted belongingness (OR = 1.06, 95% CI 1.04, 1.09, p < 0.001) were associated with greater odds of anxiety symptom remission. Increases in self-compassion (OR = 4.43, 95% CI 3.11, 6.31, p < 0.001) and personal values alignment (OR = 1.11, 95% CI 1.07, 1.15, p < 0.001) were also associated with higher odds of anxiety symptom remission. Among participants with elevated depression at admission (n = 434 out of 552), reductions in psychological inflexibility (OR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.12, 1.20, p < 0.001), emotion dysregulation (OR = 5.38, 95% CI 3.62, 8.0, p < 0.001), and thwarted belongingness (OR = 1.10, 95% CI 1.07, 1.13, p < 0.001) were associated with higher odds of depression symptom remission. Increases in self-compassion (OR = 5.07, 95% CI 3.53, 7.27, p < 0.001) and personal values alignment (OR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.10, 1.18, p < 0.001) were also linked to greater likelihood of depression symptom remission.

A forward stepwise logistic regression model was used to examine the associations between changes in processes and anxiety and depression symptom remission among those with elevated anxiety and depression at admission, respectively. For those with elevated anxiety at admission, increases in self-compassion (OR 2.92, 95% CI 1.87, 4.57, p < 0.001) and reductions in psychological inflexibility (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.02, 1.10, p = 0.007) were associated with greater likelihood of achieving anxiety symptom remission and were the only selected processes of change. Lower admission anxiety severity (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.76, 0.90, p < 0.001), older age (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01, 1.05, p = 0.01), and higher treatment dose (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.05, 1.26, p = 0.003) were also associated with greater likelihood of remission.

For those with elevated depression at admission, increases in self-compassion (OR 2.33, 95% CI 1.50, 3.62, p < 0.001) and alignment with personal values (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.02, 1.10, p = 0.006), as well as reductions in psychological inflexibility (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.04, 1.12, p < 0.001), were associated with higher odds of depression symptom remission and were the only selected processes of change. Lower admission depression severity (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.76, 0.87, p < 0.001), female sex (OR 0.48, 95% CI 0.27, 0.86, p = 0.013), and higher treatment dose (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.08, 1.34, p = 0.002) were also associated with greater remission likelihood.

Processes as mediators of anxiety and depression symptom remission

A Bayesian structural equation model was used to test whether changes in self-compassion, psychological inflexibility, emotion dysregulation, thwarted belongingness, and values alignment mediated the relationship between treatment dose and remission among those with elevated anxiety and elevated depression at admission. In models in individuals with elevated anxiety, model fit was acceptable (PPP = 0.32). Higher treatment dose was associated with greater anxiety remission (OR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.03, 1.22). Treatment dose was associated with increased self-compassion (β = 0.002, 95% CI 0.000, 0.004), which in turn was associated with higher remission rates (OR = 1.88, 95% CI 1.363, 1.933). The indirect effect via self-compassion was supported, as the 95% credible interval excluded zero, indicating that self-compassion was supported as a mediator. Thwarted belongingness, emotion dysregulation, and values alignment were not significant mediators, as their indirect effect credible intervals included zero (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The purpose of this retrospective study was to examine how changes in psychological inflexibility, emotion dysregulation, thwarted belongingness, self-compassion, and personal values alignment related to changes in anxiety and depressive symptoms in patients with elevated anxiety and/or depression symptoms who underwent PBT at Lightfully Behavioral Health. We found that changes in each PBT process were associated with a greater likelihood of achieving symptom remission (i.e., minimal or no anxiety/depression) when PBT processes were modeled separately. In individuals with elevated anxiety, changes in self-compassion and psychological inflexibility were significant predictors of remission. However, self-compassion was the only supported mediator of the relationship between treatment dose and anxiety symptom remission. In patients with elevated depression, self-compassion, psychological inflexibility, and personal values alignment were the strongest predictors of remission. Self-compassion and psychological inflexibility were associated mediators in the relationship between treatment dose and depression remission. This study provides preliminary real-world support consistent with the PBT framework, demonstrating that targeting the processes hypothesized to maintain anxiety and depression was associated with better symptom outcomes. Though, given the naturalistic, hypothesis-generating nature of this work, findings from this study are intended to identify promising process targets and inform the design of future controlled investigations.

We found that changes in self-compassion and psychological inflexibility were associated with a greater likelihood of achieving anxiety and depression remission. Interventions that improve self-compassion and psychological inflexibility target shared vulnerabilities across anxiety and depression, such as experiential avoidance, thus increasing the likelihood of remission [37]. Furthermore, changes in the process of personal values alignment may have been particularly important for depression remission because this process directly targets anhedonia, a core symptom of depression characterized by decreased pleasure and interest in previously meaningful activities [38]. Greater personal values alignment may help individuals engage in activities that are meaningful, which in turn increases motivation for and pleasure in these activities. These findings underscore the need for interventions like Lightfully, that enhance self-compassion and psychological flexibility in individuals with anxiety or depression and highlight the unique role of values-based interventions for managing depressive symptoms.

Self-compassion was the only supported mediator of the relationship between treatment dose and anxiety remission, suggesting that longer treatment may foster greater self-kindness and reduced self-judgment, which in turn leads to anxiety symptom improvement. Self-compassion may be particularly important in anxiety remission as this process helps individuals respond to emotional distress with understanding rather than avoidance [39]. Furthermore, increasing self-compassion may directly reduce the negative self-evaluations that are common in people with anxiety, particularly social anxiety [40]. Given the unique role of self-compassion in linking treatment dose to anxiety remission, interventions that actively cultivate self-compassion may further enhance treatment effectiveness. Future research should explore how different therapeutic approaches best facilitate self-compassion. For example, further research may explore whether components of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) or ACT-based self-compassion components produce faster or more durable mental health outcomes. Moreover, as patients who are acutely dysregulated may not be psychologically ready to engage with self-compassion work until initially stabilized, another future direction would be to explore the optimal timing of introducing self-compassion interventions within treatment.

Findings suggested that self-compassion and psychological inflexibility mediated the association between treatment dose and depression remission. These findings suggest that associations between treatment dose and remission may partially operate through changes in key therapy processes. Specifically, increasing self-compassion may help depressed individuals develop a more balanced view of themselves and their flaws, and ultimately decrease the harsh self-judgement often present in depressed patients [41]. In parallel, decreases in psychological inflexibility may enable patients to engage with their emotions rather than avoiding distressing internal experiences [37, 42]. Given the strong mediating effects of self-compassion and psychological inflexibility, treatment plans for depression should prioritize interventions, such as Lightfully, that actively enhance these processes.

We found that the processes of emotion dysregulation and thwarted belongingness were neither predictors nor mediators of anxiety or depression symptom remission. For emotion regulation, this finding may have stemmed from the conceptual and statistical overlap of emotion dysregulation and psychological inflexibility, which was the strongest predictor when both processes were included in the same model. Emotion dysregulation can be thought of as one manifestation of psychological inflexibility–for example, experiential avoidance (a core component of psychological inflexibility) can be thought of as a maladaptive emotion regulation strategy [43]. An alternative explanation is that the therapeutic approach implemented by Lightfully (notably ACT interventions) is explicitly targeting psychological inflexibility, and is less focused on teaching patients emotion regulation strategies [18]. The finding that thwarted belongingness did not emerge as a key predictor or mediator was surprising given the strong link between thwarted belongingness and depressive symptoms, particularly among individuals with more severe symptomatology [44]. Because thwarted belongingness is also not a core target for ACT interventions, this process may not have been targeted as explicitly at Lightfully. Future research should examine whether integrating structured emotion regulation training (e.g., DBT skills groups) and interpersonal interventions targeting social connection (e.g., Interpersonal Therapy) enhances outcomes for patients whose symptoms are maintained by emotion dysregulation or thwarted belongingness, ideally through randomized trials comparing standard PBT to PBT augmented with these targeted modules.

Findings from this study provide implications for mental health treatment and the growing adoption of PBT, where many clinicians have relied on diagnosis-specific protocols despite modest effect sizes and variable treatment responses [5]. By demonstrating that process change was associated with and mediated symptom remission in a real-world clinical sample at Lightfully Behavioral Health, this study adds to the emerging body of literature that suggests that targeting transdiagnostic processes, as opposed to diagnoses, may provide a more precise and effective path to symptom remission. Identifying self-compassion and psychological inflexibility as potential mechanisms of change within a PBT framework lays the groundwork for future research to determine whether targeting these processes produces more consistent and durable outcomes than diagnosis-driven approaches alone. The results of this study may also have implications for how clinicians structure and sequence treatment. Rather than selecting interventions, clinicians may benefit from using process assessments (such as those administered at Lightfully Behavioral Health) to identify which mechanisms are most active for a given patient. Doing so may allow clinicians to prioritize treatment interventions accordingly. Process-informed treatment sequencing, as presented here, may represent a practical application of the PBT framework, though comparative designs are needed to establish whether this approach produces more robust outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the retrospective design used to analyze real-world data from patients undergoing PBT at Lightfully Behavioral Health, which provides valuable insights into the effectiveness of psychological processes in symptom remission. The inclusion of multiple psychological processes (self-compassion, psychological inflexibility, emotion dysregulation, thwarted belongingness, and values alignment) allowed for a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing treatment outcomes. This study specifically focused on individuals with elevated anxiety and depression, a population that is in need of effective treatment interventions. By examining how changes in these psychological processes contribute to symptom remission, the study emphasized the importance of targeting these factors to improve outcomes for those who need help the most. This study also contributes to the growing body of literature on PBT, providing evidence for the role of key psychological processes in symptom remission for anxiety and depression. By highlighting the importance of these processes, the study adds to our understanding of how PBT can be tailored to target specific psychological mechanisms for more effective treatment outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, missing data on race and ethnicity limited our ability to fully describe the demographics of our sample, which is common in retrospective, real-world studies (Liu & Panagiotakos, 2022). Our sample also had a disproportionate number of females compared to males, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, a substantial proportion of patients (61%) were excluded due to incomplete admission or discharge data, which may have introduced selection bias. Moreover, this study did not include structured clinical interviews to confirm diagnosis or characterize clinical severity beyond self-report measures. The absence of these demographics and characteristics limits the precision of the sample evaluated in the present study. Second, process variables were only measured at admission and discharge, precluding the ability to assess whether process change preceded or occurred concurrently with symptom change. Future studies should incorporate midtreatment assessments to evaluate the temporal sequencing of change and better capture the dynamic relationship between processes and symptom outcomes over time. Third, we did not assess the implementation of PBT treatment procedures, so we were not able to take into account how consistently providers applied the PBT model in practice. Furthermore, specific therapeutic techniques were not available as part of the data in this study, which limits the interpretability of findings with respect to active treatment ingredients. Future research should gather data on PBT treatment fidelity to evaluate the impact of specific interventions. Fourth, the pre-post study design and lack of a control group limited our ability to estimate a true mediation model and draw causal conclusions about Lightfully treatment. Future investigations should take place in the context of a randomized controlled trial with additional data collection time points. Moreover, establishing that a process variable mediates the relationship between treatment dose and remission in an observational design does not confirm that process changes causally produce symptom improvement. To establish causal mechanisms, experimental manipulation is necessary. Fifth, models were adjusted for age, biological sex, and treatment dose as these covariates have demonstrated associations with treatment outcomes [45]. However, other potential confounders, such as prior treatment history, medication use, and comorbid diagnoses, may also influence symptom remission, but were not controlled for in this study. Lastly, the final mediation model did not control for all potentially relevant covariates, such as level of care, to avoid overfitting, and the findings need to be replicated in a larger, more diverse sample.

Conclusion

This retrospective study highlights the importance of psychological processes in the remission of anxiety and depression symptoms in patients receiving PBT. While changes in all processes were significant predictors of symptom remission, self-compassion and psychological inflexibility were particularly important for both anxiety and depression remission, with the addition of personal values alignment for depression remission. Mediation analyses revealed that self-compassion was a supported mediator in the relationship between treatment dose and anxiety remission, while both self-compassion and psychological inflexibility mediated the relationship between treatment dose and depression remission. Clinicians may benefit from incorporating interventions that target these processes as potential contributors to symptom remission. Future research should explore how different therapeutic approaches can better cultivate these psychological processes, as well as investigate whether enhancing self-compassion and psychological flexibility leads to sustained symptom remission over time. Additionally, examining the potential for integrating these processes into other therapeutic models could offer insights into optimizing treatment strategies for individuals with anxiety and depression.
