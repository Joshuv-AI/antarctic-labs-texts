A novel treatment score (QUAD score) to promote treatment optimization in heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction
Authors: Henry Oluwasefunmi Savage, Jason N. Dungu, Anthony Dimarco, Brian Li, Samantha Langley, Jonathan Kojo Amoah, Paraic Cliffe, Archana Ganapathy, Peter Watters, Patricia Campbell, Nicola Melarkey, Simon Duckett, Sean Davies, Matthew Dewhurst, Karen Hann, Louise Clayton, Rhys Williams, Victoria Ruszala, Teresa Onwere‐Tan, Fozia Zahir Ahmed, Ibrahim Arosi, Kimberly Gray, Mark C. Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo help avoid therapeutic inertia, we developed a pragmatic treatment score (QUAD Score) for use in daily practice by healthcare professionals managing patients with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 50% and heart failure. We now investigate the association between achieved QUAD scores and 1 year outcomes.MethodsThis was a multicentre cohort study in consecutive patients with incident heart failure and LVEF <50%, who completed therapy titration between January 2021 and June 2023. The primary outcome was a composite of first hospitalization for heart failure (HHF) and all‐cause mortality at 1 year after final therapy titration, for QUAD scores that were poor (<8), good (8–14) or excellent (15–24).ResultsData were analysed from 1691 participants, collected from 10 UK centres, of whom 30% were women and 82% were White. Median age, N terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NTproBNP) and LVEF were 70 (59–78.5) years, 1624 (536–4138) ng/L and 34 (25–38) %, respectively. At the start of therapy titration, only 97 (5%) patients were naïve to any of the four pillars of therapy. After investigator‐declared final titration, QUAD scores were excellent in 806 (48%), good in 382 (22%) and poor in 503 (30%) patients. Patients who failed eventually to achieve a good or excellent QUAD score were more often women, older and had poorer renal function and higher plasma NTproBNP (P < 0.01). The median number of days to final therapy titration was longer in those who achieved an excellent QUAD score, [174 (99–290) days,133 (80–232) days and 108 (57–193) days P < 0.01, for excellent, good and poor QUAD groups, respectively. There was wide variation in titration schedules across participating centres and overall, 33% of patients completed therapy titration within 90 days, 63% within 6 months and 88% within 1 year. The primary composite outcome at 1 year for those with poor, good and excellent QUAD scores were respectively 16.9%, 9.4% and 5.6%, (log rank P < 0.01), for mortality were 13.1%, 6.5% and 2.4% (log rank P < 0.001) and for first HHF were 7.7%, 3.9% and 3.2% (log rank P < 0.001).ConclusionsThe QUAD score is a simple tool that can help audit and incentivize uptake of guideline‐recommended therapy for HFrEF and prevent treatment inertia. Excellent QUAD scores are associated with better outcomes.

Introduction

The four pharmacological therapeutic pillars recommended by international guidelines for patients with a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and heart failure (HFrEF) have substantially improved their prognosis. 1 , 2 Ensuring timely implementation of all four classes of agents and achieving target doses is associated with more favourable outcomes. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 However, therapeutic inertia is common, for which there are many causes. 7 , 8 Concerns about possible side effects make many patients and healthcare professionals (HCP) reluctant to add treatments or increase doses. Side effects, even if transient, may deter optimization of doses, or lead to down‐titration and even cessation of therapy. Side effects, even without symptoms, such as low blood pressure, hyperkalaemia and renal dysfunction often deter initiation of treatment or attaining target doses. HCP may also be reluctant to alter therapy in patients who are symptomatically stable. 9 , 10 However, possibly the most common reasons for failing to initiate and optimize treatment is that the HCP simply forgot to do so, compounded by lack of specialized education and training, resources and time, or the belief that it was someone else's responsibility. 11

Overcoming therapeutic inertia is required to deliver guideline‐recommended medical therapy (GRMT). We developed the QUAD score (Figure 1) as a tool to measure and promote the success of GRMT implementation for HFrEF in daily clinical practice. We now describe the application of the QUAD score and its association with morbidity and mortality in a large cohort of patients with new‐onset heart failure and an LVEF <50%.

Methods

QUAD score calculation

The QUAD score was derived from a single‐centre contemporary patient cohort with HFrEF and has demonstrated an association with short‐term outcomes. 12 The QUAD score is calculated from the number and achieved doses of the four pharmacological pillars of GRMT including beta‐blockers (BB), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA) and sodium glucose co‐transporter II inhibitors (SGLT2i) or one of any one of the following; angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin II receptor blockers (AIIRB) or angiotensin II receptor blocker/neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI). One point is given for each therapeutic class prescribed at <50% of guideline target dose and four points, for each therapeutic class, if ≥50% of target dose is achieved. For patients taking all four foundational therapies, an additional eight points are given irrespective of individual medicine dosing. Where there is a valid reason to withhold a treatment (e.g., not up‐titrating a BB when ventricular rate is <50 bpm), an ‘e’ (for exemption) is added as a suffix to the score which is documented in the clinical notes for review at the next patient contact.

Study population

This was a multi‐centre observational study. We invited centres from across the United Kingdom to participate in a survey of consecutive patients who had previously presented with incident HF and an LVEF <50% and therefore potentially indicated for four‐pillar GRMT according to contemporary guidelines. Uncertainty persists about the diagnostic criteria for heart failure when LVEF is preserved (≥50%; HFpEF) and the ability of treatment, especially BBs, to modify disease progression. Moreover, many heart failure services in the United Kingdom do not routinely include patients with HFpEF. For these reasons, patients with HFpEF were not included in this study. Centres were required to have an established heart failure clinic that routinely documented patient characteristics and pharmacological therapy at each visit in an electronic medical record.

Patients ≥18 years of age with an LVEF of <50% and medical records, who started and had completed titration of therapy between January 2021 and June 2023 were included. This timeframe was chosen because it was contemporary, allowed at least 12 months of follow‐up, and post‐dated the COVID‐19 pandemic and the introduction of Sodium Glucose Co‐transporter II inhibitors (SGLT2i) into clinical practice. 13 Patients were excluded if LVEF was ≥50% or if they were on a palliative care pathway or did not have dates for starting and completing therapy titration. Patients who died before therapy titration was considered complete were not included in order to avoid biases created by variations in the duration of the optimization process.

Data collection and QUAD score classification

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected from electronic clinical records at prespecified intervals and outcomes were cross‐referenced with national Hospital Episode Statistics for hospital admissions. The QUAD score was calculated for each patient at both start and completion of therapy titration (Graphical abstract). The main analysis focused on the QUAD score achieved on the date that therapy titration was considered complete for each individual patient, by their managing HCP. These were mainly HF specialist nurses, pharmacists or cardiologists. Patients were classified as having an excellent (15–24 points), good (8–15 points) and poor (<8 points) QUAD score.

Outcomes

The primary outcome for the purpose of this study was a composite of first unplanned hospitalization for heart failure (HHF) or all‐cause mortality at 1 year. Secondary outcomes included composites of the primary outcome, time taken to final therapy titration, its association with outcomes and reasons for variation in achieving four‐pillar therapy.

Assessing HCP‐reported usability of the QUAD score in routine practice

We assessed the practical utility of the QUAD score and its impact on optimizing treatment in a subset of HCPs with prescribing privileges, each of whom received an information sheet describing the QUAD score. HCPs were then presented, at random, with three hypothetical clinical scenarios of haemodynamically stable patients with uncomplicated HFrEF, each receiving a poor, good or excellent standard of GRMT. The time taken to calculate the QUAD score was recorded using a stopwatch, and any changes they proposed to optimize therapy were documented. None of the HCPs surveyed were connected to data collection or the manuscript.

Ethical considerations

To allow for data collation, a data sharing agreement consistent with General Data Protection Regulations was implemented between each participating centre and the host centre (Mid and South Essex NHS Foundation Trust), which acted as the data controller. The study conformed to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by relevant local research and development departments. Only de‐identified data, routinely collected as part of usual care, were used for this study and therefore written patient consent was not required.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are expressed as median (25th–75th centiles). Categorical data are expressed as numbers and percentages. Comparison between these data was by independent sample median test (with Bonferroni correction for multiple tests) for medians and the χ 2 test to compare proportions between groups. Time‐to‐event based on each QUAD group was analysed by the method of Kaplan–Meier and compared using the log‐rank test. In addition, the relationship between several clinical variables was tested in a multivariate Cox model for these outcomes and described with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software package version 27.0.1.0.

Results

Between January 2021 and June 2023, data were analysed from 1691 patients with new‐onset heart failure and LVEF < 50%, from 10 centres across the United Kingdom, of whom 54% were enrolled during a hospitalization, 30% were women, 82% were ethnically White and median [IQR] age was 70 [59 to 79] years (Table 1). Median [IQR] LVEF was 34 [25–38] % and median plasma NTproBNP was 1624 [536–4138] ng/L. Frequently reported comorbidities included hypertension (47%), diabetes mellitus (27%), atrial fibrillation (39%) and chronic kidney disease (28%). At the start of therapy titration, only 97 (5%) patients were naïve to any of the four pillars of therapy. After investigator‐declared final titration, QUAD scores were excellent in 806 (48%), good in 382 (22%) and poor in 503 (30%) patients (Table 1). Patients who failed eventually to achieve a good or excellent QUAD score were more often women, older and had poorer renal function and higher plasma NTproBNP (P < 0.01).

Data for HHF and/or mortality could not be verified in 53 patients, so they were excluded from the final analysis of the primary endpoint. For the remaining 1638 patients, the composite outcome of mortality or HHF at 1 year for those with poor, good and excellent QUAD scores was 16.9%, 9.4% and 5.6%, respectively (log rank P < 0.01) (Figure 3). Mortality at 1 year was 13.1%, 6.5% and 2.4% and HHF at 1 year was 7.7%, 3.9% and 3.2% for poor, good and excellent QUAD scores respectively (log rank P < 0.01). We evaluated several clinical variables including age, LVEF, NYHA class, baseline blood pressure and heart rate, and comorbidities including atrial fibrillation, hypertension, diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, previous stroke, COPD, dementia and liver disease (Data S1). Compared with a poor QUAD score, an excellent QUAD score was associated with a lower rate of HHF [adjusted HR, excellent vs. poor, 0.35 (95% CI, 0.17–0.70, P < 0.01)] and a much lower one year mortality [adjusted HR, excellent vs. poor, 0.26 (95% CI, 0.14–0.50, P < 0.01)].

At final medicines titration, 885 patients (52%) had not been initiated on quadruple therapy, including 29% not on SGLT2i and 22% not on MRA. The reasons for failure to prescribe was documented in only 319 (36%) of these patients with the predominant reasons given being hypotension (35%) and renal dysfunction (17%) (Data S1).

There were 70 respondents to our clinical scenarios assessing the practicality of using the QUAD score. On a 5‐point Likert scale (1, poor; 5, excellent), most (77%) of participants rated the QUAD score ≥3 for ease of use, and 89% rated it ≥3 as an incentive for treatment optimization. The median time taken to calculate the QUAD score was 100 (70–156) seconds. After using the QUAD score, 94% of participants recommended medication changes to optimize treatment further (Data S1).

Discussion

This analysis shows that achieving higher QUAD scores is associated with fewer unplanned hospitalizations for heart failure and better survival in the following year. We also show that HCP found the QUAD score is quick and easy to use and thought that it would improve implementation of the four therapeutic pillars for patients with HFrEF. Accordingly, it seems both feasible and valuable to consider using the QUAD score in routine clinical practice. Indeed, it has already been adopted by several heart failure services in the UK (Data S1) and can be adapted as guidelines evolve.

The QUAD score promotes the prescription of the four pillars of GRMT for HF, at ≥50% evidence‐based dose for each pillar. 5 Documenting the QUAD score at each patient contact serves as an objective visual representation of the current treatment status, acting as an incentive for HCPs, and appropriately informed patients, to attain GRMT targets. The QUAD score weighting ensures an excellent score is possible only if medicines from all four therapeutic pillars are initiated. Furthermore, the requirement for documentation of deviations from GRMT goals encourages critical review of each patient's treatment at every contact, including valid reasons for treatment de‐escalation, such as BBs in the presence of profound bradycardia.

Other treatment scores are described in literature such as the adherence score calculated as a fraction of a theoretical treatment score and the actual score, based on expected and current prescription, respectively. 14 The optimal medical therapy score was developed primarily to compare baseline evidence‐based medical therapy in clinical trials. 15 Neither include SGLT2i in their development, and to our knowledge, neither are widely used in routine clinical practice. The QUAD score may be the next evolutionary step as a proactive tool towards medicines optimization.

Patients with HFrEF are frequently multimorbid and non‐cardiac comorbidities are common. 16 Consequently, these patients frequently interact with specialists outside of cardiology who may not fully grasp the prognostic importance of the medicines prescribed. While adjustments to GRMT may be justified in cases of clinically relevant hypotension, hyperkalaemia or renal dysfunction, gaps in understanding can also lead to reductions in GRMT for reasons that may not be clinically valid, such as asymptomatic systolic blood pressure of 95 mmHg. In an era that increasingly requires multi‐stakeholder collaboration for the management of heart failure, 17 an objective scoring tool to support continual optimisation of GRMT for HFrEF that can be shared with patients and across specialities could prove highly beneficial for patients.

Patients who achieved better QUAD scores had longer therapy optimization times. GRMT optimization should be a continuous process, but early initiation and rapid titration of GRMT should also be encouraged. Evidence from STRONG‐HF, now incorporated into clinical practice guidelines, supports this, even for patients hospitalized with worsening HF. 18 However, the importance of evaluating current therapy at every patient contact beyond the acute phase, where opportunities to re‐introduce medicines or escalate doses that had not previously been tolerated, is also important. Achieving the destination of quadruple therapy is important even if it takes longer.

Within our cohort, older patients, women and those with higher plasma NTproBNP were less likely to achieve a good or excellent QUAD score. Women were also more likely to be diagnosed in an outpatient setting, had better LVEF and less coronary artery disease. This suggests that treatment inertia may be related to a HCP's perception of future risk. Up‐titration of therapy may be more aggressively pursued in younger patients perceived to be at higher risk. However, patients with less severe disease might have the most benefit from treatment in terms of quality adjusted life‐years gained. Equity of access and implementation of good care is important across the spectrum of heart failure disease severity. Furthermore, 11.5% of the cohort studied had a mildly reduced LVEF (41%–49%) (Data S1). Most of these patients (56%) had a good or excellent QUAD score.

More than 90% of patients with new‐onset HF with an LVEF<50% were already prescribed at least one and >70% at least two foundational HF therapies prior to treatment optimization, but 52% still had not achieved quadruple therapy at final therapy titration. Clearly, treatment inertia exists even in dedicated HF clinics, which argues for implementation of the QUAD score, in all heart failure clinical services to minimize the impact of treatment inertia on patient outcomes.

The commonest recorded reasons for not implementing GRMT were factors such as hypotension, bradycardia and renal dysfunction. However, for two thirds of patients who did not achieve an excellent QUAD score, no documented reason was provided in their medical records. This is not unusual even in randomized trials. 19 The lack of easily accessible information about why treatment has not been optimized may contribute to therapeutic inertia at the next clinic visit. Mandatory documentation of exceptions to GRMT and clinician and patient education could lead to successful initiation of treatments that had previously been stopped or poorly tolerated.

Our study also highlights the heterogeneity in HF medication titration practices in the United Kingdom. Amongst participating centres, the percentage of patients achieving an excellent QUAD score ranged from 25% to 72%, with between 6% and 40% of patients achieving final therapy titration within 90 days. Despite recommendations to support rapid titration, overall, 36% of patients required between 6 and 12 months, to complete the titration process. Variations in the staff to patient ratio probably make an important contribution to the speed of GRMT titration. HF services in the United Kingdom are seldom staffed to the recommended standard of 2 to 4 full‐time specialist nurses per 100 000 population. 20 Utilizing other HCP, such as clinical pharmacists, and empowering other clinical specialities, such as specialist primary care physicians or geriatricians, may provide additional capacity.

A strength of the QUAD‐HF study is the inclusion of a large cohort of patients with incident heart failure from across all four nations of the United Kingdom. This highlights the possibility and opportunity to support the development of robust ambulatory heart failure databases in the UK healthcare system. In England and Wales, a National Heart Failure Audit exists, but this focuses on patients hospitalized with a primary HF diagnosis and only provides re‐hospitalization and mortality data after discharge, although data can potentially be linked to long‐term prescribing records. 21 However, the system in the United Kingdom is currently less integrated and less accessible than in countries such as Denmark and Sweden. 22 , 23 However, it is unclear whether any of these administrative systems are sufficiently integrated into clinical pathways to be of practical use for clinical care. Initiatives from the ESC or elsewhere should ensure that patient management system are developed that improve care without placing an additional workload on HCPs. 24

This study is limited by its retrospective nature. A prospective QUAD‐HF Registry in the United Kingdom (and potentially other countries), starts recruitment in 2025, using the same inclusion criteria (IRAS ID 354730). We were unable to address all selection bias for patients, participating centres and HCP surveyed. Most patients in this study were Caucasian, and our results may not reflect the experience for other races/ethnicities. We did not include patients who died during therapy optimization, which would provide a more comprehensive picture but also potentially introduce bias due to the competing risk of speed of therapy titration and mortality. The current analysis included patients who had contra‐indications to one or more GRMT elements, those who did not achieve target doses of GRMT and those for whom de‐escalation of therapy was necessary. However, in this retrospective analysis, it was not possible to collect granular data on the reasons for the failure to achieve a high QUAD score, to warrant further analysis. Some patients will have had poor outcomes because they failed to achieve a high QUAD score due to contra‐indications and poor tolerance. Other patients will have had poor outcomes because no attempt was made to achieve a high QUAD score, either because of failure on the part of the HCP or, less likely in our opinion, the patient declined additional treatment. Further prospectively collected data might allow us to explore the performance of the QUAD score in patients with and without contra‐indications to GRMT. ACEi, AIIRB and ARNI were grouped together for simplicity of the score and consequently do not reflect the individual benefit of ARNI. The study was conducted only in the United Kingdom. External validation in prospective cohorts in other countries would be welcome, including a pre‐ and post‐QUAD score implementation analysis for a more robust assessment of its applicability, and to determine whether the QUAD score is an effective tool for HF GRMT dose optimization.

In conclusion, the QUAD score offers a pragmatic, cost‐free tool to help overcome treatment inertia in routine clinical practice that should improve patient outcomes.
