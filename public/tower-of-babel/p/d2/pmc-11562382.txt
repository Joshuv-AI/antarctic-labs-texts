Introduction

Renal Osseous metaplasia (OM) is a distinctive pathological phenomenon characterized by the presence of mature bone within the renal parenchyma that typically manifests as a focal cortical lesion [1]. This condition involves the transformation of adult tissue into bone tissue, resulting in the formation of atypical bone structures at extraskeletal sites [2]. Despite its rarity and peculiar nature, OM has garnered attention because of its occurrence in various organs, including native kidneys, dating back to the 18th century [3].

Notably, OM is often accompanied by osteoblasts, osteoclasts, adipocytes, and hematopoietic stem cells, further emphasizing its unique nature [2]. While OM is generally regarded as a spontaneous lesion with less significant pathological implications, its occurrence in seriously damaged renal allografts, benign and malignant renal cystic lesions, and other parts of the urinary tract has underscored its noteworthy presence in clinical settings [4].

To the best of our knowledge, only one case has been reported in the native kidney and very few cases of kidney allografts [1]. We report a case of a native kidney with a simple renal cyst in which histopathological examination after tumor enucleation revealed OM. These studies followed the SCARE criteria [5].

Case report

A 37 years old female patient presented to our hospital with an incidental finding of a left kidney mass on ultrasound imaging for a workup of intermittent abdominal and left flank pain for nine months. She had a history of three cesarean deliveries of live children and one recent emergency surgery for ectopic pregnancy 14 months prior to visiting our hospital. She had no known chronic medical illnesses, such as Diabetes Mellitus or Hypertension. She denied any history of tuberculosis diagnosis or treatment. She had no history of previous self-or family history of malignancies, such as renal malignancy. The patient had no history of smoking or alcohol consumption. On physical examination, she had a midline infraumbilical old surgical scar, no palpable abdominal or flank mass, and the other system examination was normal.

The Comprehensive blood and urine test results were within the normal range, and the renal function test results were creatinine and urea was 0.53 mg/dl (0.57–0.87) and 15 mg/dl (16.6–48.5) respectively. Serum electrolyte sodium, potassium, phosphorus, Calcium, Uric acid, and parathyroid hormone levels were normal. On ultrasound finding, there was a 4.2 cmx4.2 cmx3cm anechoic cyst with a relatively thick wall and echoes in the dependent part, in the lower pole of the left kidney, and with the impression of a left renal complex cyst (Fig. 1). The remaining parenchyma and contralateral kidneys were normal. Abdominopelvic Ct-scan revealed normal right kidney with the pertinent finding was on the left kidney showing 4.5 cm ∗ 3.5 cm ∗ 2.5 cm well-defined fluid density cystic ass seen at the lower cortex. The lesion had a thick calcified wall and showed no postcontrast enhancement. The patient was advised on the surgical management of the mass, with the impression of a complex left renal mass. Enucleoresection of the mass was performed using the left flank approach. Intraoperative findings of the mass were a Stoney, hard, well-circumscribed mass at the lower pole of the left kidney (Fig. 5). Macroscopic examination of the mass revealed a unilocular cyst with a calcified wall and smooth inner surface; the microscopic section showed cyst wall fragments lined by simple cuboidal cells, stroma consisting of bony trabeculae with hematopoietic elements, and adjacent normal-looking renal parenchyma; the final histopathologic report showed a simple renal cyst with metaplastic bone and hematopoietic elements (Fig. 6). The cyst content was fluid filled with low-level debris that had no malignant cells on cytology and did not show infection on gram stains or culture. Discussion

Osseous metaplasia (OM) is characterized by the development of bone outside the skeletal system and is associated with various factors, such as chronic ischemia, trauma, inflammation, unabsorbed hematoma, cancer, hypocalcemia, and hypervitaminosis D. This process involves multiple stages, including the transformation of mesenchymal pluripotent cells into osteoblasts, secretion of bone matrix, and provision of a favorable environment [6,7]. The pathogenesis of heterotopic bone formation relies on three main components: osteogenic precursor cells, stimulating factors, and a conducive setting [2,8]. The time interval between the absence of evident heterotopic bone and identification of OM can be sudden, spanning from six months to two years [4,9].

For bone induction to occur in soft tissues, three conditions must be met: the presence of an inducing agent, osteogenic precursor cells, and an environment conducive to osteogenesis [8]. Ischemia and inflammation are thought to act as inducing factors, whereas fibroblasts are osteogenic precursor cells [2]. In one study, ligation of renal vessels in rabbits led to OM, where ischemic and inflammatory conditions recruited young fibroblasts into the renal tissue, forming a membrane that laid down the bone [10]. In humans, bone development in the native kidney is associated with papillary necrosis, chronic pyelonephritis, and chronic interstitial nephritis [1]. The complex developmental and molecular pathology of OM may contribute to a better understanding of the more common forms of heterotopic ossification, potentially leading to more effective prevention and treatment strategies [7].

Osseous metaplasia (OM) is an uncommon occurrence in renal pathology and is observed in native kidneys, severely damaged renal allografts, and various parts of the urinary tract [11]. RCC with OM is characterized by visualization of a renal mass showing areas of fat attenuation surrounded by peripheral calcification. The mechanism of OM in RCC and other tumors remains unclear, with hypotheses including metaplastic or reparative responses, production of bone by tumor cells, or ossification of pre-existing mucin or calcified foci. OM may also occur because of ischemia, necrosis, or inflammation of the tumor or surrounding tissues [12,13]. Some studies have suggested a potential link between the hormone “parathyroid hormone-related peptide” [14].Although the prognostic value of OM in RCC remains unclear owing to its rarity, it has generally been considered a favorable sign associated with early stage disease and a lack of local invasion or distant metastasis [15,16]. However, some reports have suggested an association between high-grade tumors and poor prognosis [17,18].

Various disease conditions involving soft tissue and organ calcification and ossification have led to inconsistent terminology usage across different literature sources. Xia et al. documented the first instance of kidney tumor calcinosis with radiopathological findings similar to those of our case report. Tumoral calcinosis commonly presents in the soft tissues surrounding major joints, such as the hip, shoulder, elbow, and knee, and occasionally affects smaller joints in the hands, feet, head, and neck [19]. Olsen and Chew suggested restricting the term “tumoral calcinosis” to denote a condition resulting from a hereditary metabolic disorder involving phosphate regulation, specifically associated with extensive periarticular calcinosis [20].

Osseous metaplasia (OM) and calcification are distinct processes that require differentiation. Calcification refers to the formation of calcium-based salts and crystals within cells and tissues, and can be either dystrophic or metastatic in nature. Dystrophic calcification occurs in areas of damaged or necrotic tissue, whereas metastatic calcification occurs in normal tissues due to abnormal calcium metabolism, which is often associated with conditions such as hyperparathyroidism [2,4].

In contrast, OM involves the transformation of living tissue into bone tissue, which is characterized by the presence of osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Although the exact mechanisms triggering OM development are not fully understood, they may be influenced by factors such as heterotopia, dystrophic calcifications, ossification of damaged areas, and metaplasia in the healing tissue [2,7]. Radiologically, OM can sometimes be mistaken for ectopic calcifications because of their similar appearance. However, it is crucial for physicians to differentiate between calcifications in the renal pelvis (lithiasis) and intraparenchymatous calcifications, particularly during allograft ultrasonography. In the latter case, physicians should be aware of the possibility of OM as it could indicate chronic renal issues. OM represents a significant example of regenerative medicine, highlighting the presence of progenitor cells in various bodily regions that are capable of generating tissues specific to their germ layer origin [2,21].

Computed tomography (CT) scans help identify calcifications and ossification with greater sensitivity. Calcifications typically exhibit a broad range of Hounsfield unit (HU) values, typically falling between 100 and 400 HU, while bone demonstrates even higher HU values, with trabecular bone registering around 700 HU and cortical bone surpassing 1500 HU [22]. OM can be challenging to differentiate from calcification based solely on radiological imaging, particularly in computed tomography (CT) scans that display extensive calcifications [15]. Differential diagnosis may include RCC, metastatic carcinoma, adrenal neoplasm, soft tissue sarcoma, and mature cystic teratoma [11]. A definitive diagnosis requires histopathological examination and immunohistochemical staining, which are often unanticipated before surgery [14,21].

OM treatment primarily depends on factors such as the cause, location, size, and associated symptoms of the lesion. Surgical excision is considered the primary treatment for primary OM in the absence of any identifiable underlying metabolic disturbances. Complete surgical resection of the lesion is recommended without the need to remove part of the renal parenchyma [19]. In cases of OM affecting the kidneys or other organs, it is crucial to evaluate the metabolic factors underlying the condition, especially in patients with OM related to chronic dialysis or renal allografts.

Conclusion

This case report highlights a rare manifestation of Osseous Metaplasia (OM) within a native kidney, presenting as a simple renal cyst. This unexpected discovery during histopathological examination underscores the importance of a meticulous diagnostic approach, particularly for differentiating OM from calcification on radiological imaging. Although OM is generally regarded as a benign lesion, its occurrence in severely damaged renal allografts and in various urinary tract locations emphasizes its clinical significance. This case contributes to the limited literature on OM in native kidneys, prompting further research into its pathogenesis and clinical implications. This report highlights the necessity for heightened awareness among clinicians and radiologists, emphasizing the potential diagnostic challenges associated with OM and advocating for thorough histopathological examination to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate patient management.
