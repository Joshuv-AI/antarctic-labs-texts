The association between air pollution and cognitive impairment: a systematic review and meta- analysis of global studies
Authors: Abebe Kassa Geto, Sefineh Fenta Feleke, Ali Yimer, Atitegeb Abera Kidie, Natnael Amare Tesfa, Esuyawkal Mislu, Hassen Ahmed Yesuf, Molla Hailu, Birtukan Gizachew Ayal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAir pollution remains one of the most significant global health threats, affecting populations everywhere. Its widespread and persistent presence has long posed serious risks, and its far-reaching effects underscore the significant threat it poses to public health. Although numerous investigations have been conducted worldwide, their outcomes have remained inconsistent and lacked definitive conclusions. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis sought to determine the association between air pollution and cognitive impairment across the globe.MethodsDatabases such as PubMed/MEDLINE, Jane, Science Direct, Cochrane Library, LIVIVO, CINAHL, Global Index Medicus, African Journals Online, Web of Science, HINARI, Semantic Scholar, Google, and Google Scholar were used to retrieve all the relevant articles. Data were analyzed using STATA 17 software to determine the pooled associations between air pollution and cognitive impairment with a 95% confidence interval using a random-effects model. Moreover, the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA 2020) guidelines were followed for this study.ResultFifty-four articles with 711,918 study participants were included in this meta-analysis to determine the association between air pollution and cognitive impairment. The findings of the study indicated that for every 1 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5, PM10, PM1 and black carbon concentration, the cognitive function was decreased by − 0.79 (95% CI β: −0.90, -0.68; I2 = 0.0% and p = 0.730), − 0.48 (95% CI β: −0.82, -0.15; I2 = 99.99% and p = 0.00), − 0.90 (95% CI β: −0.97, -0.82; I2 = 94.60% and p = 0.00), and − 0.53 (95% CI β: −1.03, -0.02; I2 = 99.44% and p = 0.00) respectively. Moreover, the pooled odds of developing cognitive impairment following exposure to a 1 µg/m3 increment in concentration of PM2.5 was 1.02 (95% CI OR: 1.01, 1.02; I2 = 97.02% and p = 0.00).ConclusionExposure to particulate matter air pollutants such as PM2.5, PM10, PM1, and black carbon was associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. The results may have substantial public health significance for preventing cognitive impairment through strong air pollution interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24560-9.

Introduction

Air pollution is one of the biggest threats to public health globally. It is a pervasive and ubiquitous hazard that took no less than a devastating pandemic to spur its recent worldwide reduction [1]. Exposure to air pollution represents the most significant environmental health risk. It is ranked ninth among modifiable disease risk factors, surpassing other prevalent factors like insufficient physical activity, elevated cholesterol levels, and substance use [2]. In 2018, the World Health Organization (WHO) released its latest report on air pollution, identifying that seven million people die annually due to poor air quality. Moreover, it is estimated that 90% of the world’s population is exposed to dangerous levels of air pollution [3].

Air pollution is a major cause of disease and death. Most deaths related to air pollution are caused by human exposure to fine inhalable particles or fine particulate matter, also known as PM2.5. Billions of people in developing countries live in area where the ambient and indoor concentrations of PM2.5 are multiple times higher than the health-based guideline values for air quality established by the WHO [4].

According to the 2019 Global Burden of Diseases (GBD)report, an estimated 6.4 million people died prematurely worldwide due to exposure to PM2.5 air pollution, and about 95% of those deaths occurred in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [5]. Seventy percent of the deaths occurred in East Asia and the Pacific, and South Asia. China and India accounted for 52% of global deaths from PM2.5 [5].

Cognitive impairment (CI) is a frequent symptom of neurological disorders, characterized by disruptions in the brain’s ability to uptake, store, organize, and process information. These abnormalities result in deficits in memory, attention, executive function, and orientation [6]. Cognitive function is a broad term that refers to mental processes involved in the acquisition of knowledge, manipulation of information, and reasoning. It includes the domains of perception, memory, learning, attention, decision making, and language abilities [7]. Cognitive function is an important indicator reflecting health status and directly impacting individuals’ daily life abilities and quality of life [7]. Air pollution, a major environmental threat, contributes significantly to disease, premature death, and is now recognized as a potential risk factor for cognitive impairment, highlighting its profound impact on health [8]. The cause of cognitive impairment is considered to be a combination of genetic and environmental factors. As a modifiable environmental factor, air pollution plays a crucial role in central nervous system diseases, including cognitive impairment [9]. Recent evidence increasingly indicates that air pollutant exposure acts as an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment, with no clear safe threshold identified for its detrimental effects [10]. The continuous decline in cognitive scores represents a gradual quantitative shift toward cognitive impairment. Identifying this decline early is vital for prompt intervention and effective treatment [11]. As many as 42% of older adults around the globe have been documented to experience mild cognitive impairment [12].

Particulate matter (PM) are solid compound and liquid droplets suspended in air that are sufficiently small to be inhaled [13, 14]. Among the various types of air pollutants, they are a heterogeneous combination of particles of different sizes and types, including chemical compounds, which have been extensively studied for their adverse health-related effects [15, 16]. These include human-made emissions from fuel combustion (such as coal, oil, and biomass), transportation, and high-temperature industrial activities. Additionally, it can result from atmospheric chemical reactions involving various gaseous pollutants [15]. Factors contributing to regional air pollution spikes include local emission sources, proximity to busy roadways, stagnant air conditions, and temperature inversions, as well as transport of pollutants over intermediate and long distances within the troposphere [14, 15].

PM are categorized by particle size: PM10 encompasses particles with diameters of 10 μm or smaller, PM2.5 consists of particles 2.5 μm or smaller, and PM1 includes those 1 μm or smaller. This classification highlights the increasingly finer particulate sizes and their potential implications for health and environmental impact [14, 17]. According to the WHO, black carbon, measured using optical methods, represents airborne soot-like carbon and correlates with elemental carbon concentration determined chemically. It originates from incomplete combustion of fossil fuels, biofuels, and biomass, with emissions stemming from both human activities and natural sources [18]. It refers to a variety of carbon-based substances, ranging from partially charred plant materials to highly graphitized soot, characterized by ordered molecular structures similar to graphite. These particles are formed as a result of incomplete combustion processes [19]. Elevated black carbon levels were also associated with a decline in cognitive function, impacting assessments of both verbal and nonverbal intelligence as well as memory performance [20]. Globally, various literatures described and reported that exposure to air pollution particularly, PM air pollutants such as PM1 [21–27], PM2.5 [17, 21, 23, 24, 26–65], PM10 [17, 21–24, 26, 27, 29, 31, 35, 37, 40, 41, 52, 58, 66–68], and black carbon [32, 49, 50, 55, 69] impaired human cognition.

However, studies providing pooled evidence on the association between air pollution and cognitive impairment are very scant. Even though some studies were conducted on the topic of interest, they mainly relied on observational data that might affect exposure-effect estimates, resulting in bias, and limited to include longitudinal studies, which are suggested to check the association between air pollution and cognitive impairment to establish a temporal relationship [70]. Some of the studies also addressed only the descriptive synthesis and lacks strong quantitative evidence on this global pressing public health concern [71–73]. Moreover, a positive association between air pollution and cognitive impairment was found in some studies [74–76], whereas significant association was not observed in other studies [77–79], which signals the presence of inconsistencies across different studies. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the association between air pollution and cognitive impairment around the globe. The findings of this study highlight the urgent necessity of giving precedence to global initiatives aimed at curbing and alleviating or mitigating air pollution. The research-based findings serve as a vital resource for decision-makers, public health officials, and relevant parties in crafting and executing holistic plans to address, manage, and lessen the adverse effects of air pollution worldwide. Such insights have the potential to pave the way toward a future where the weight of this universal challenge is markedly reduced and effectively managed.

Methods and materials

Study protocol registration

The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was officially documented in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), listed under the registration number CRD42024581037. This registration ensures transparency and adherence to established research standards.

Search strategy

Four independent authors (AKG, SFF, AAK, and NAT) were involved in the searching process of the articles. The selected studies were transferred into EndNote version 8 software, where redundant records were removed. Relevant studies were then chosen in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework [80] (Supplementary material 1).

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, scholarly articles were retrieved from various electronic databases such as PubMed/MEDLINE, Jane, Science Direct, Cochrane Library, LIVIVO, CINAHL, Global Index Medicus, African Journals Online, Web of Science, HINARI, Semantic Scholar, Google, and Google Scholar. The search was carried out using the following keywords: “Air Pollution”, “Indoor Air Pollution”, “Ambient Air Pollution”, “Household air pollution”, “Outdoor Air Pollution”, “Air Pollutant*”, “Particulate Matter”, “PM10”, PM2.5”, “PM1”, “Black Carbon”, Cognitive Impairment”, “Cognitive Decline”, “Cognitive Function”, “Cognitive Performance”, “Cognitive Dysfunction”, and “Mild Cognitive Impairment”. All keywords were combined using the appropriate Boolean operators: “AND” or “OR”.

Inclusion criteria

This systematic review and meta-analysis included all available published and unpublished studies from any country of the world, regardless of the time frame. The included studies were carried out in both community-based and institutional settings, employing various study designs such as cross-sectional, cohort, longitudinal, and case-control. Only articles written in English language were considered for inclusion in this meta-analysis.

Exclusion criteria

This systematic review and meta-analysis excluded studies that lacked full text, were unidentified, were abstracts, editorials, unretrievable, letters, or did not report the association of particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10, PM1) and black carbon air pollution, and cognitive impairment as the primary outcome. Moreover, studies that use non-human subjects were also excluded.

Outcome assessment

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the association between particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10, PM1) and black carbon air pollution and cognitive impairment among people of the globe.

Quality assessment

The quality of each study was rigorously appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical evaluation checklist for observational studies [81] (Supplementary material 2). Three reviewers (AKG, BGA, and AY) independently assessed each article, assigning scores on a 100-percent scale. Studies with a quality rating exceeding 50% were selected for further analysis [82, 83]. In cases of discrepancy, an average score was calculated from the evaluators’ assessments to reach consensus during the quality appraisal process.

Data extraction procedure

A standard data extraction template consisting of several details such as author name, year of publication, country, study design, sample size, and major results (including effect estimates such as beta coefficients (β), hazards ratio (HR), or odds ratio (OR) including their corresponding confidence intervals), concentration of air pollutants was prepared. Duplicate articles were removed after the relevant articles for inclusion were carefully screened. Four independent authors (AKG, EM, MH and HAY) were involved to undertake the required data extraction activities.

Certainty assessment

The quality of evidence in the current meta-analysis articles was evaluated based on the five domains outlined in the GRADE (Grade of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) framework, including risk of bias, result inconsistency, evidence indirectness, result imprecision and publication bias [84]. The overall GRADE rating is categorized into four levels: high, moderate, low and very low. The GRADE evaluation of the reviewed articles was conducted independently from the original review authors’ findings. AKG and SFF independently assessed and verified the ratings of the final included articles, resolving any disagreements through discussion. A detail evaluation and explanation of the GRADE process was performed (Supplemental material 3).

Standardization of air pollutant concentrations

For consistency purposes, all the beta coefficients (βs), hazard ratios (HRs), and odds ratios (ORs) were standardized to an increment of 1 µg/m3 of particulate matter (PM1, PM2.5, and PM10) and black carbon concentration. Statistical analysis procedures

Once the data were successfully imported, the analysis was conducted using STATA (Corporation, College Station, Texas, USA). To evaluate variability among the included studies, heterogeneity was measured using the Higgins I² statistic, where values of 75%, 50%, and 25% indicated high, moderate, and low heterogeneity, respectively [86].

Using a random-effects model with a 95% confidence interval, the association between particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10, PM1) and black carbon air pollution, and cognitive impairment was determined.

The association between air pollution and cognitive impairment was presented using a forest plot. To determine the influence of an individual study on the pooled estimate of air pollutants, a sensitivity analysis was performed. Sub-group analysis for PM2.5 was performed to identify the possible sources of heterogeneity based on the year of publication (after 2020 versus 2020 and before), country category (China, Germany, Mexico and the USA), continent (Asia, Europe and North America), and sample size category (lower than 1000, and 1000 and above). Moreover, subgroup analysis was done for PM10 based on publication year category, country, continent, and sample size category. Additionally, a funnel plot and Egger’s test were used to determine the presence of potential publication bias [87].

Results

Search results and study selection

Overall, 3332 studies were identified from an electronic database and reference searching. An EndNote 8 reference manager was used. One thousand and two (1002) duplicated articles were removed. The total number of articles excluded based on their titles and abstracts due to the failure to meet the inclusion criteria was 2251. Moreover, thirteen articles were removed as they did not report the outcome of interest, and nine articles were excluded as they failed to meet quality assessment methods. Regarding the continent in which the studies were conducted, thirty-three studies were from Asia [17, 21–27, 29–32, 36–39, 41–43, 45, 46, 49, 52, 55, 57, 60–66, 88], twelve from North America [28, 33, 34, 44, 47, 48, 53, 59, 89–91], eight from Europe [35, 40, 50, 54, 56, 58, 67, 69] and one from Africa [68]. Meta-analysis

Fifty-four studies [17, 21–69, 88–91] were included to determine the pooled association between air pollution and cognitive impairment in this meta-analysis. The meta-analysis for each category of PM (PM2.5, PM10, and PM1), and black carbon was computed individually based on the reported measure of association.

Association between particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10, and PM1) and black carbon air pollution and cognitive impairment

Beta coefficient

The findings of this study indicated that for every 1 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5, PM10, PM1 and black carbon concentration, the cognitive function was decreased by − 0.79 (95% CI β: −0.90, −0.68; I2 = 0.0% and p = 0.730) (Fig. 2), − 0.48 (95% CI β: −0.82, −0.15; I2 = 99.99% and p = 0.00) (Fig. 3), − 0.90 (95% CI β: −0.97, −0.82; I2 = 94.60% and p = 0.00) (Fig. 4), and − 0.53 (95% CI β: −1.03, −0.02; I2 = 99.44% and p = 0.00) (Fig. 5) respectively.

Odds ratio

The result of this systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that the pooled odds of developing cognitive impairment was 1.02 (95% CI OR: 1.01, 1.02; I2 = 97.02% and p = 0.00) following an exposure to 1 µg/m3 increment of PM2.5 concentration (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, the association was not significant; 1.02 (95% CI OR: 0.18, 1.86) and 1.02 (95% CI OR: 0.98, 1.06) for a 1 µg/m3 increment of PM10 and PM1, respectively.

Hazard ratio

For an exposure to a 1 µg/m³ increment in PM2.5 and PM10 concentration, the risk of cognitive impairment was increased by 1% (1.01: 95% CI HR: 1.00 to 1.03; I2 = 95.17% and p = 0.00) (Supplementary Fig. 2) and 7% (1.07: 95% CI HR: 1.04 to 1.10; I2 = 0.07% and p = 0.70) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis found suggestive evidence regarding the relationship between air pollution and cognitive impairment. Particulate matter in both urban and rural settings consists of a diverse array of components with varying chemical and physical properties. This complexity makes research on particulate matter and the evaluation of exposure risks challenging. The potential for these particles to cause harm depends on factors such as size, chemical composition, physical characteristics, and sources. Understanding these variations is crucial for accurately assessing health risks and environmental impact, as different particles interact uniquely with biological systems, influencing air quality and human health outcomes [18].

Our findings indicate a significant positive association between PM2.5, PM10, PM1, and black carbon and decline in cognitive function. The finding of this study is supported by a systematic review and meta- analysis conducted by Xu et al. [93], which elucidated the presence of a statistically significant positive association between PM2.5 pollutants and cognitive function decline. Another evidence also revealed that prolonged exposure to air pollution also leads to progressive neuronal degeneration and cognitive decline, driven by the accumulation of neurotoxic effects over time [94]. Moreover, extensive researches have explored the association between PM2.5 exposure and cognitive impairment [95, 96], which signified that exposure to PM2.5 is strongly linked to the declining of cognitive function.

The results of another systematic review and meta- analysis are in congruent with the findings of this study and demonstrated that exposure to PM2.5 was significantly related to an increased risk of cognitive impairment. More specifically, for every 5 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 concentration, the risk of cognitive impairment increased by 8% [9].

This systematic review and meta- analysis revealed that exposure to PM10 has a strong relation with cognitive impairment. Similarly, a pertinent study identified a link between PM10 exposure and a reduction in overall cognitive function, along with declines in specific test-based cognitive abilities [97]. According to the findings of a study conducted in China, it was found that exposure to PM2.5, PM1, and PM10 were significantly associated with the cognitive function, and improving air pollution exposure could alleviate cognitive decline [98]. However, other studies have not observed any association between exposure to PM10 and cognitive function [34, 99]. These conflicting findings may be attributed to the varying sample sizes, different measurements of exposure, different measurement outcomes, and different exposure windows. The finding of this study found that exposure to PM1 air pollution was associated with cognitive impairment. This finding is supported by a study which found that long-term PM1 exposure could contribute to cognitive impairment and abnormal cognitive trajectories [100].

The findings of this study revealed that exposure to black carbon is associated with a decline in cognitive function. This finding is supported by other studies [101] which elucidates the observed relationship between BC exposure and cognitive impairment. This is due to the reason that ambient air pollution particles such as BC are able to translocate to the human brain and accumulate in multiple brain regions involved in cognitive functioning. This phenomenon may contribute to the onset and development of neurodegenerative disorders [102]. The association between particulate matter air pollution and cognitive impairment, as revealed by this systematic review and meta-analysis, carries significant clinical and policy implications. Clinically, these findings highlight the need for increased awareness among healthcare providers regarding the potential neurotoxic effects of air pollution, encouraging them to consider environmental factors in the assessment and management of cognitive impairment, particularly in at-risk individuals. From a policy perspective, this evidence strengthens the rationale for implementing and enforcing stricter air quality regulations, especially concerning particulate matter emissions, to protect public cognitive health and promote healthy aging.

Despite following the latest PRISMA 2020 guidelines, our systematic review and meta-analysis had several limitations. First, we identified a high degree of between-study heterogeneity, which may have affected the generalizability of our findings. Although we conducted subgroup and meta-regression analyses that examine factors such as country category, continent, publication year category, and sample size category, none significantly explained the heterogeneity, suggesting that unmeasured or unreported variables played a greater role. Second, this study has publication bias. While efforts were made to mitigate this bias through a comprehensive search of multiple databases and grey literature sources, it is possible that some relevant studies were not identified, which could affect the overall findings. Third, variations in exposure assessment methods affected the accuracy of individual exposure estimates, potentially destabilizing results. Fourth, inconsistencies in the definition of cognitive impairment led to misclassification bias. Lastly, most studies included in our analysis focused on single-pollutant models, preventing an exploration of synergistic or additive effects within multi-pollutant mixtures. Addressing these gaps in future research could provide a more nuanced understanding of how air pollution influences cognitive health and other related outcomes.

Conclusion

The results of this systematic review and meta- analysis revealed that the association between particulate matter air pollution and cognitive impairment was very pronounced. Exposure to particulate matter air pollutants such as PM2.5, PM10, PM1, and black carbon was associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. These findings underscore the importance of implementing air pollution interventions such as robust air quality regulations, investment in clean energy technologies, sustainable transportation systems, and urban planning strategies along with public health interventions to mitigate the detrimental effects of air pollution on cognitive impairment. This evidence-based information urges policymakers, healthcare planners, and other stakeholders to be collaborative in developing and implementing comprehensive strategies to prevent, control, and mitigate the impact of air pollution all over the world.
