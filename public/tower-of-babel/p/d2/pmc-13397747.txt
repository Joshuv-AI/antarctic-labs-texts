Prognostic impact of platelet count trajectories in acute decompensated heart failure: a retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Gil Marcus, Maya Barzilay, Shiri L. Maymon, Eran Kalmanovich, Gil Moravsky, Ido Minha, Avishay Grupper, Shmuel Fuchs, Sa’ar Minha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsTo determine whether in-hospital platelet count trajectories during admission for acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) are associated with early and longer-term outcomes after discharge.MethodsWe performed a single-centre retrospective cohort study of adults hospitalized for ADHF (2007–2017). After excluding in-hospital deaths and cases without paired platelet measurements, 6,789 patients were analyzed from an initial 8,332. Platelet status was defined by WHO thresholds at admission and discharge and categorized into four trajectories: Normal→Normal (n = 5,453), Low→Low (n = 700), Normal→Low (n = 325), and Low→Normal (n = 311). Outcomes from the date of discharge were: 30-day readmission and 30-day mortality (binary), and time-to-event 1-year readmission and 1-year and 5-year all-cause mortality. Multivariable logistic regression modeled 30-day endpoints; Cox models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for 1- and 5-year outcomes, adjusting for prespecified clinical covariates. A multinomial-propensity-score inverse probability weighting sensitivity analysis assessed robustness.ResultsRelative to the Normal→Normal group, the Normal→Low trajectory (normal platelets on admission, thrombocytopenia at discharge) was associated with higher 30-day readmission (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.08–1.80, p = 0.011) and markedly higher 30-day mortality (OR 2.40, 95% CI 1.58–3.63, p < 0.001). It was also associated with higher 1-year mortality (HR 1.66, 95% CI 1.37–2.02, p < 0.001) and 5-year mortality (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.21–1.56, p < 0.001), while 1-year readmission did not differ significantly. The Low→Low trajectory showed no association with 5-year mortality (HR 1.05, 95% CI 0.95–1.15, p = 0.332), and Low→Normal trended toward lower 5-year mortality (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.77–1.01, p = 0.061). Findings were directionally consistent in inverse-probability-weighted analyses.ConclusionsIn patients hospitalized with ADHF and discharged alive, a decline in platelet count from normal at admission to thrombocytopenia at discharge was associated with excess 30-day readmission and mortality and with persistent excess mortality at 1 and 5 years after discharge. The incremental discrimination over established clinical covariates was modest, indicating that platelet trajectory is unlikely to serve as a standalone risk tool. Because platelet counts are inexpensive and routinely available, the in-hospital trajectory warrants further evaluation as a candidate prognostic adjunct to established HF risk frameworks, pending prospective external validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06035-w.

Introduction

Acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) represents a major global health burden, characterized by frequent hospitalizations, substantial morbidity and mortality, and significant healthcare expenditure [1]. Given the heterogeneous prognosis of these patients, accurate risk stratification upon admission and discharge is critical for guiding clinical decision-making, optimizing resource allocation, and planning post-discharge care [2, 3]. While established clinical models and biomarkers, such as natriuretic peptides, are central to this process [4], the search for additional, easily accessible, and inexpensive prognostic indicators remains an area of active investigation [5, 6]. Routine laboratory tests, performed universally in hospitalized patients, offer a valuable and often underutilized source of prognostic data [7].

Thrombocytopenia has been recognized as a marker of poor prognosis in various acute settings, including sepsis [8], intensive care [9], acute coronary syndromes [10], heart failure (HF) [11], and atrial fibrillation [12]. In the context of HF, platelet activation markers such as mean platelet volume and platelet distribution width have been associated with disease severity and mortality [13, 14] However, the implications of low absolute platelet count in ADHF are less clearly defined. Importantly, thrombocytopenia in hospitalized patients may reflect two biologically distinct phenomena: a reversible manifestation of acute illness (e.g., infection, hemodilution) [15], or a persistent indicator of advanced or multisystem disease (e.g., bone marrow dysfunction, increased destruction) [16, 17]. Assessing platelet count at a single time point may therefore conflate these entities. A dynamic view – tracking changes in platelet count from admission to discharge – may help distinguish transient from persistent thrombocytopenia, with meaningful implications for prognosis.

In this study, we investigated the prognostic value of thrombocytopenia in a large cohort of patients hospitalized for ADHF. We evaluated platelet count both at admission and discharge, and categorized patients based on their trajectory during hospitalization. We aimed to determine whether platelet count trajectories during hospitalization were associated with short- and long-term outcomes after adjustment for prespecified clinical covariates.

Methods

Study design and population

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at Shamir Medical Center (formerly Assaf Harofeh Medical Center), a government-funded tertiary academic referral hospital located in Be’er Ya’akov, Israel, affiliated with the Gray Faculty of Medical and Health sciences at Tel Aviv University. The center has approximately 850–900 inpatient beds and serves as a referral center in central Israel, although it does not provide advanced heart failure surgical services, as it lacks a cardiac surgery division. The study included all adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) hospitalized with ADHF between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2017. ADHF was identified by the presence of an International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) code 428.xx, 429.xx, or 514 as both the admission and discharge diagnosis to ensure diagnostic accuracy. For patients with multiple ADHF admissions during the study period, only the first hospitalization was considered the index admission. Patients who died during the index hospitalization and those lacking platelet count data at either admission or discharge were excluded. The institutional review board approved the study protocol and waived the need for informed consent because of the retrospective design.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical, and pharmacological data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records. Laboratory results, including complete blood counts and basic chemistry, were collected for admission, peak, and discharge time points. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was recorded when available. Mortality data were retrieved from the Israeli Ministry of Interior Affairs, which provides complete coverage of death events but does not include cause-specific mortality.

Platelet measurements and trajectory groups

Platelet counts were measured at admission (first available) and discharge (last available before discharge). Thrombocytopenia was defined as a platelet count < 150 × 10^3/µL. Thrombocytosis was defined as a platelet count > 450 × 10^3/µL. At admission, thrombocytosis was present in 169 patients (2.5% of the analytic cohort), supporting its low prevalence and the decision to merge these patients into the Normal (non-Low) group rather than analyze them as a separate trajectory category. Patients were stratified into four mutually exclusive groups based on the trajectory of platelet count from admission to discharge: Low→Low (thrombocytopenia at both admission and discharge), Low→Normal (thrombocytopenia at admission with normalization at discharge), Normal→Low (normal platelet count at admission with thrombocytopenia at discharge), and Normal→Normal (normal platelet count at both admission and discharge, serving as the reference group).

This four-category scheme was adopted as a pragmatic operationalization of platelet dynamics, anchored to the WHO threshold for thrombocytopenia (150 × 10³/µL) and intended to define clinically recognizable exposure categories that can be applied without specialized analytic infrastructure. The continuous and spline analyses described under Sensitivity Analyses were prespecified to test whether this categorical scheme imposed an artificial threshold or obscured an underlying gradient of risk.

Outcomes

The outcomes of interest were categorized as short-, intermediate-, and long-term. Short-term outcomes included 30-day readmission and 30-day mortality. Intermediate-term outcome was all-cause mortality at 1 year. Long-term outcome was all-cause mortality at 5 years. These complementary outcomes were analyzed to capture immediate post-discharge risk, intermediate prognosis, and persistent long-term risk. The index date for all post-discharge analyses was defined as the date of discharge from the index hospitalization.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges. Comparisons between the four platelet trajectory groups were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to estimate cumulative survival, with differences between groups assessed using the log-rank test.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate adjusted associations with 30-day mortality and 30-day readmission. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to estimate adjusted associations with 1-year and 5-year mortality. Variables were pre-specified for inclusion in all models based on their established clinical relevance [18] in heart failure outcomes, including age [19], sex [20], ischemic heart disease [21], diabetes mellitus [22], chronic kidney disease [23], anemia [24], atrial fibrillation [25], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [26], and peripheral vascular disease [27]. Platelet trajectory groups were entered as categorical predictors, with Normal→Normal serving as the reference group.

Sensitivity analyses

A prespecified sensitivity analysis was conducted using inverse probability weighting (IPW) to test the robustness of our findings. Because IPW relies on complete covariate data for propensity score estimation, we first assessed missingness in the included variables. Anemia status was missing in 80 patients (1.2% of the cohort). Comparison of baseline characteristics between patients with and without missing anemia values revealed no significant differences, consistent with a missing completely at random (MCAR) mechanism. These patients were therefore excluded from the IPW analysis.

Stabilized weights were then derived from a multinomial logistic regression model incorporating all covariates from the main multivariable models. The adequacy of the weighting procedure was evaluated by inspecting the distribution of the weights and confirming covariate balance using standardized mean differences (SMDs). Post-weighting analyses of all prespecified outcomes were conducted as univariable models, as the weighting inherently adjusts for measured confounders.

Because the length of stay (LOS) accrues during hospitalization and may reflect in-hospital processes occurring during the index hospitalization (trajectory defined using the first and last in-hospital platelet counts), LOS was not included in the prespecified primary adjustment set. To address the potential influence of LOS on observed associations, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis in which LOS (continuous days from admission to discharge) was added to each multivariable model.

As platelet count data were unavailable for 911 patients in the initial cohort – of whom 75 died during hospitalization and 836 were discharged alive – we examined the mechanism underlying this missingness among the 836 survivors by comparing baseline characteristics and laboratory data availability between excluded and included patients. We then performed a sensitivity analysis restricting the analytic cohort to patients admitted during 2008–2017, the period in which electronic laboratory capture was near-complete, and repeated all primary multivariable models in this restricted cohort.

In addition, to quantify the incremental discriminative value of platelet trajectory, we compared C-statistics between a base model containing only the prespecified clinical covariates and a model additionally including the four-category trajectory variable. C-statistics were computed as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for logistic models and as Harrell’s concordance index for Cox models. The incremental contribution of platelet trajectory was assessed using a likelihood ratio test comparing the nested models.

Finally, to assess whether the categorical trajectory classification obscured clinically relevant gradients of risk, we performed a sensitivity analysis replacing the four-group exposure with relative platelet change as a continuous variable, defined as ([discharge – admission] / admission) × 100; for interpretability, this variable was reverse-coded so that higher values represent greater platelet decline, and effects are reported per 10% decrease. This variable was entered into each multivariable model alongside admission platelet count (to separate the prognostic effect of change from the effect of baseline level) and the same prespecified covariate set. In addition, to visualize the shape of the dose–response relationship between platelet change and long-term mortality, we modeled relative platelet change using a restricted cubic spline with four knots (placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the distribution) in a Cox proportional hazards model for all-cause mortality, adjusting for admission platelet count and the same prespecified covariates. Nonlinearity was assessed by comparing the spline model with a linear-only model using a likelihood ratio test.

Software and significance threshold

All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Python version 3.11 (Python Software Foundation). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study cohort and platelet trajectory groups

The initial cohort comprised 8,332 patients hospitalized with ADHF between 2007 and 2017. After excluding 911 patients lacking platelet count data and 632 who died during the index admission, 6,789 patients were included in the final analysis. Patients were stratified into four platelet trajectory groups based on counts at admission and discharge: Normal→Normal (n = 5,453, 80.3%), Low→Low (n = 700, 10.3%), Normal→Low (n = 325, 4.8%), and Low→Normal (n = 311, 4.6%).

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics differed significantly across the platelet trajectory groups (Table 1). Patients in the Low→Low and Low→Normal groups were older (median age 79 and 81 years, respectively) than those in the Normal→Normal group (77 years, p < 0.001). The Normal→Normal group had a higher proportion of females (53.0%) compared to the other groups (35.9%–39.4%, p < 0.001). Diabetes mellitus was more prevalent in the Normal→Normal group (51.9%) than in the Normal→Low group (43.4%, p < 0.001), while chronic kidney disease was more frequent in the Low→Low (39.0%) and Normal→Low (38.8%) groups compared to the Normal→Normal group (30.6%, p < 0.001). Laboratory indices also varied: the Low→Low group exhibited lower median white blood cell counts (6.9 K/µL) and higher urea levels (54.0 mg/dL) compared to the Normal→Normal group (9.5 K/µL and 47.6 mg/dL, respectively, p < 0.001).

The Normal→Low group had higher rates of percutaneous coronary intervention (15.1%) compared to the Normal→Normal group (9.9%, p < 0.001). Unadjusted clinical outcomes

Unadjusted short and intermediate clinical outcomes are detailed in Table 3. Sensitivity analyses

Assessment of the performance of the IPW procedure is presented in Supplementary Table S5. The table reports standardized mean differences (SMDs) for each covariate before and after weighting across the platelet trajectory groups. After weighting, covariate balance between groups was excellent, with all weighted SMDs below 0.1 (for example, the maximum SMD for age decreased from 0.188 before weighting to 0.064 after weighting).

The weighted outcome analyses are presented in Fig. 4. In the weighted cohort, the Normal→Low trajectory remained associated with higher 30-day readmission (28.2%, p = 0.015) and markedly higher 30-day mortality (9.2%, p < 0.001) compared with the other groups, consistent with the findings of the multivariable models. For 5-year survival, the Low→Normal group had the highest survival probability (35.8%), followed closely by the Normal→Normal group (34.1%), whereas the Normal→Low group had the lowest survival probability (25.1%) (log-rank p = 0.001). Thus, the Normal→Low trajectory consistently remained the group with the worst outcomes across both multivariable and weighted analyses.

The additional sensitivity analysis with LOS included as a continuous covariate in all models is presented in Supplementary Table S6. After LOS adjustment, effect estimates for mortality outcomes were modestly attenuated but remained statistically significant for the Normal→Low trajectory, including 30-day mortality (OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.32–3.08, p = 0.001), 1-year mortality (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.20–1.78, p < 0.001), and 5-year mortality (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.19–1.57, p < 0.001). The association with 30-day readmission was essentially unchanged (OR 1.40, 95% CI 1.08–1.81, p = 0.012), and 1-year readmission remained non-significant (HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.98–1.32, p = 0.086).

Examination of the 836 discharged survivors excluded for missing platelet data revealed that all were simultaneously missing all complete blood count parameters (including both admission and discharge platelet count), and 98.6% were also missing creatinine values – with 88.9% admitted during 2007–2017 when electronic laboratory capture was incomplete (Supplementary Table S7). In the sensitivity analysis restricting the cohort to 2008–2017 admissions (n = 6,454), all primary findings were essentially unchanged (Supplementary Table S8). The Normal→Low trajectory remained associated with higher 30-day mortality (OR 2.38, 95% CI 1.55–3.66, p < 0.001), 30-day readmission (OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.06–1.80, p = 0.015), 1-year mortality (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.37–2.05, p < 0.001), and 5-year mortality (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.20–1.56, p < 0.001), reducing concern that incomplete electronic laboratory capture materially influenced the primary findings.

In the continuous exposure sensitivity analysis, each 10% relative decrease in platelet count was associated with higher 30-day mortality (OR 1.082, 95% CI 1.027–1.140, p = 0.003), 1-year mortality (HR 1.023, 95% CI 1.003–1.043, p = 0.022), 5-year mortality (HR 1.024, 95% CI 1.013–1.036, p < 0.001), and 1-year readmission (HR 1.017, 95% CI 1.005–1.029, p = 0.004), with a borderline association for 30-day readmission (OR 1.022, 95% CI 0.999–1.045, p = 0.059) (Supplementary Table S9). The restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed a smooth dose-response relationship between platelet decline and long-term mortality (overall association p < 0.001; nonlinearity p < 0.001), with risk increasing gradually across the full range of platelet decline and no evidence of an abrupt threshold effect at 150 × 10^3/µL (Supplementary Figure S1).

Adding the four-category platelet trajectory variable to the base clinical model significantly improved model fit for 30-day readmission (likelihood ratio χ²=8.0, p = 0.047), 30-day mortality (χ²=15.9, p = 0.001), 1-year mortality (χ²=28.4, p < 0.001), and 5-year mortality (χ²=26.7, p < 0.001). The corresponding improvements in C-statistic were modest (ΔC 0.002–0.007), consistent with the expected contribution of a single categorical variable to an established covariate set (Supplementary Table S11).

Discussion

In this study of patients hospitalized with ADHF and discharged alive, we found that a decline from normal platelet count on admission to thrombocytopenia at discharge was associated with markedly higher risk across different time horizons. In the short term, this trajectory predicted a more than two-fold increase in 30-day mortality and higher 30-day readmission. Excess risk remained evident at 1 year and persisted through 5 years of follow-up. These associations were observed specifically among patients discharged alive and should not be extrapolated to the broader hospitalized population, in which in-hospital deaths (excluded from the present analysis) were enriched for the same Normal→Low pattern (Supplementary Table S10). No excess risk was observed in patients with thrombocytopenia on admission whose platelets either remained low or recovered to normal. Thus, the downward trajectory of platelets, rather than baseline thrombocytopenia, predicted poor outcomes.

Prior studies have mostly assessed a single platelet count, typically at admission. Yamaguchi et al., in 425 hospitalized ADHF patients, found that those in the lowest admission platelet tertile (< 170 × 10^3/µL) had higher mortality (HR 2.02) [11]. In stable HFrEF outpatients, Mojadidi et al. reported increased one-year mortality (HR 1.84) with platelet counts < 100 × 10^3/µL [28]. Our findings diverge importantly, as admission low platelets alone were not associated with worse prognosis in our cohort, whether persistently low or recovering. Several explanations may account for this divergence. First, our analysis excluded in-hospital deaths and therefore specifically addressed post-discharge outcomes among patients who survived the index admission; this differs from studies that include early in-hospital mortality, where platelet abnormalities may reflect acute deterioration before discharge. Second, Mojadidi et al. focused on more severe thrombocytopenia in a stable outpatient HFrEF population, which may represent a different chronic phenotype. Third, differences in cohort size, setting, platelet thresholds, and grouping strategy may also contribute to differences between studies. One study by Wang et al. did examine dynamic platelet changes in ICU AHF patients, linking persistent decline to in-hospital mortality [29]. Their findings support the prognostic value of trajectories but differ by focusing on critically ill patients and short-term outcomes, while our analysis extends this concept to a broader ADHF population and long-term prognosis.

Beyond HF, recent studies in critically ill populations reinforce the prognostic importance of dynamic changes in platelet counts. In a 2024 prospective study of septic ICU patients, serial monitoring of platelet counts revealed that rapid or persistent declines were strongly associated with in-hospital mortality, and dynamic patterns outperformed static measurements in predicting outcomes [30]. Similarly, trajectory analyses in other critically ill cohorts have identified distinct platelet evolution patterns, with worsening or persistently low platelets consistently conferring excess risk [31]. A comprehensive review further emphasizes that progressive declines in platelets during acute illness integrate multiple pathophysiological processes – such as systemic inflammation, coagulation activation, and organ failure – making them potent mortality markers [32]. Although these studies often report poor outcomes with persistent thrombocytopenia, this likely reflects (as previously discussed) the inclusion of patients who died during hospitalization, a group excluded from our analysis. Collectively, this broader literature supports our finding that a new-onset decline in platelet count (Normal→Low) during ADHF hospitalization is a clinically meaningful marker of adverse prognosis.

At the biological level, several pathways have been proposed in the literature that could plausibly link an in-hospital platelet decline to adverse outcomes in HF, although none can be directly tested with the present data. Dynamic thrombocytopenia in HF has been attributed to convergent processes of inflammation, coagulation activation, bone-marrow dysregulation, and comorbid illness [17]. In ADHF, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) have been reported to alter platelet kinetics [33]: IL-6 stimulates thrombopoietin release and megakaryocyte activity [34], while acute inflammation has been associated with platelet activation, adhesion, and consumption in microvascular thrombi together with splenic sequestration [32, 35]. These processes can be aggravated by hemodilution and intercurrent complications such as infection or acute kidney injury, and elevated TNF-α has been linked to suppression of platelet production [35]. Within this framework, a rapid in-hospital decline has been interpreted as a composite signal of severe physiologic stress and cytokine-driven coagulopathy, whereas stable chronic HF features persistent but milder inflammation and neurohormonal activation, with gradual platelet turnover compounded by comorbidities affecting thrombopoiesis and sequestration [36, 37]. The Low→Low pattern, consistent with this latter chronic adapted state, has been reported to carry less prognostic impact [36]. We emphasize, however, that these pathways are candidate explanations consistent with the observed Normal→Low association rather than mechanisms demonstrated in our data; intercurrent infection, bleeding events, antithrombotic exposure, and inflammatory biomarkers were not captured in the registry, and the prognostic signal we observed should be interpreted as an integrative marker of unmeasured in-hospital severity rather than as evidence of a specific causal pathway.

Natriuretic peptide concentrations are central to prognostic assessment in ADHF and merit specific consideration in relation to in-hospital platelet dynamics. NT-proBNP was not measured systematically in our registry during the study period and is acknowledged as a limitation below. The available literature suggests that platelet activation markers in HF, including mean platelet volume and platelet distribution width, correlate with disease severity and with natriuretic peptide concentrations [13, 14, 17], raising the hypothesis that natriuretic peptide elevation and an in-hospital platelet decline may partly reflect shared upstream drivers (neurohormonal activation, congestion, systemic inflammation). Whether NT-proBNP and the Normal→Low trajectory carry overlapping or independent prognostic information cannot be resolved with the present data and is an important question for prospective registries that capture both variables in parallel.

Additional exploratory unadjusted findings are provided in Supplementary Results.

This study used IPW as a sensitivity analysis to assess whether the main findings were robust to an alternative covariate-balancing approach. IPW, which uses propensity scores to create a weighted pseudo-population in which measured baseline covariates are more evenly distributed across exposure groups [38, 39] This approach has two features particularly valuable in this context. First, propensity-based methods, including IPW, are increasingly applied in contemporary observational research to mitigate bias and confounding, and are recommended as best practice in causal inference analyses [40], making it a useful complementary approach for evaluating the consistency of observational findings. Second, and highly relevant to our study design, IPW is well suited to handle imbalance in group sizes because, unlike propensity score matching, it retains all patients in the analysis, preserving the small exposure group and maximizing statistical power [39]. After confirming successful covariate balance post-weighting (all SMDs below 0.1 [40], see Supplementary Table S5), the consistency of IPW-adjusted findings with those from the main multivariable model further strengthens confidence in the robustness of our results.

Although adding platelet trajectory to the base clinical model yielded statistically significant improvements in model fit for four of five outcomes, the incremental gains in discrimination were modest (Supplementary Table S11), consistent with a marker that contributes prognostic information within a multivariable framework rather than serving as a standalone prediction tool. We did not assess whether identifying the Normal→Low phenotype changes management decisions or post-discharge outcomes; demonstrating clinical utility in this stronger sense would require prospective evaluation, ideally with a pre-specified intervention triggered by the trajectory and a comparator arm. The development and validation of a comprehensive risk score integrating platelet trajectory with other established predictors, and the prospective testing of any clinical pathway it might inform, are important next steps that lie beyond the scope of this observational analysis.

Established in-hospital and post-discharge HF risk tools, including the MAGGIC [41], OPTIMIZE-HF [42], and Get With The Guidelines-Heart Failure scores [43], integrate demographics, comorbidities, vital signs, and a small set of laboratory variables; none currently incorporates an in-hospital platelet trajectory [3]. The modest C-statistic gain we observed (Supplementary Table S11) indicates that platelet trajectory is unlikely to function as a standalone risk tool, but its consistent association with mortality across the full follow-up window suggests potential value as a low-cost adjunct that captures a phenotype (Normal→Low) not represented in existing variables. Whether incorporating platelet trajectory into validated HF risk scores meaningfully improves discrimination, calibration, or net reclassification requires dedicated head-to-head modeling and external validation in independent cohorts, which was not the aim of the present analysis.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, as a retrospective study, the analysis cannot establish causality, only associations. Second, the exclusion of 632 patients who died during the index hospitalization introduces survivorship bias. When we classified these patients using their last available platelet count as a proxy for a discharge value, the Normal→Low pattern was substantially overrepresented compared with discharged survivors (13.3% vs. 4.8%; Supplementary Table S10). Although this classification is approximate – the last available platelet count reflects an arbitrary point in the hospital course rather than a true end-of-treatment value – the enrichment of the high-risk trajectory among in-hospital deaths suggests that their exclusion attenuated rather than inflated the observed post-discharge associations. Importantly, the prognostic associations we report apply specifically to patients who survive to discharge and characterize their post-discharge trajectory; they should not be extrapolated to the broader hospitalized ADHF population, in which the prognostic landscape is dominated by in-hospital risk and is appropriately addressed by separate in-hospital prediction frameworks [2, 42] Third, reliance on electronic health records introduces the possibility of data entry errors and incomplete capture of relevant variables. In particular, in-hospital events and exposures that may influence both platelet dynamics and outcomes – such as intercurrent infection, bleeding events, use of antithrombotic therapies, hemodynamic instability, and inflammatory biomarkers – were not systematically captured in the registry, and residual confounding by these unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Fourth, the primary analysis used a dichotomized platelet threshold (150 × 10^3/µL), which may lose granularity for patients near the cut-point, and patients with thrombocytosis (> 450 × 10^3/µL) were merged into the normal group rather than analyzed separately given their low prevalence (2.5%). However, a sensitivity analysis using continuous relative platelet change and a restricted cubic spline analysis both confirmed a smooth dose – response relationship consistent with the categorical findings (Supplementary Table S9 and Supplementary Figure S1). With only two platelet measurements per patient (admission and discharge), latent class or group-based trajectory modeling would define groups based on the same two values and would therefore largely recapitulate the observed four-category scheme; future studies with serial in-hospital platelet measurements could employ such methods to identify more granular trajectory phenotypes. Fifth, information on cause-specific readmission or mortality was unavailable, and relevant biological markers – such as inflammatory cytokines, thrombopoietin, and natriuretic peptides (NT-proBNP) – were not routinely measured, limiting mechanistic interpretation to proposed pathways rather than established causal mechanisms. Finally, the single-center design limits generalizability to other institutional settings and patient populations, and the historical study period (2007–2017), which largely predates the widespread adoption of SGLT2 inhibitors and sacubitril/valsartan, may limit applicability to contemporary heart failure practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in patients hospitalized with ADHF and discharged alive, a decline in platelet count from normal on admission to thrombocytopenia at discharge was associated with higher risk of 30-day readmission and 30-day mortality, and with persistent excess mortality through 1 and 5 years of follow-up. These associations are observational and apply to the discharged-alive population; the modest gain in discrimination over established clinical covariates argues against use of platelet trajectory as a standalone risk tool. Because platelet counts are inexpensive and routinely available, the in-hospital trajectory warrants further evaluation as a candidate prognostic adjunct to established HF risk frameworks, with prospective external validation and demonstration of impact on clinical decision-making required before clinical adoption.
