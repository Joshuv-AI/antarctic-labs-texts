Cuboid height measured using ultrasound is related to sex and dorsal arch height
Authors: Daichi Kawamura, Takashi Komatsu, Yasuyuki Umezaki, Saki Takahashi, Hikaru Narita, Masanobu Suto, Hiroshi Shinohara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between cuboid height (CH) measured using ultrasound (US) and sex differences, as well as clinically assessed foot characteristics and physical characteristics. The study included 73 university students (43 males and 30 females). Basic participant attributes, including age, height, body mass, and foot length in a static standing position, were recorded and measured. Cuboid height (CH) was measured using US to evaluate the lateral longitudinal arch of the foot. Dorsal arch height (DAH) and the Foot Posture Index-6 (FPI-6) were measured as indicators related to the medial longitudinal arch of the foot. Statistical analysis was performed to compare age, height, weight, foot length, CH, DAH, and FPI-6 between men and women, depending on the results of normality tests. Subsequently, multiple regression analysis was conducted on the combined male and female group. CH values were greater in men than in women. The results of the multiple regression analysis indicated that the factors influencing CH were DAH and sex (DAH: Standardized β = 0.911, sex: Standardized β = 0.290) Furthermore, the intercept = -16.29, the adjusted R² = 0.531, with p < 0.001. Introduction

The foot is the only part of the human body that makes contact with the ground, and its functions include applying force to the ground to generate propulsion, as well as supporting body weight and absorbing shock1–4. These functions are achieved through the foot’s complex anatomical and biomechanical characteristics. In addition to its joint structures, the foot features an arch structure3. The arch structures include the medial longitudinal arch (MLA) and lateral longitudinal arch (LLA) in the longitudinal plane, and the transverse arch in the transverse plane3.

The MLA is the highest of the three arch structures. While structural assessments of the medial longitudinal arch (MLA) using X-ray images are available5, several methods have been developed for the simple clinical evaluation and measurement of the MLA which assesses using electronic calipers, including the navicular height, Dorsal Arch Height (DAH), and the Foot Posture Index-6 (FPI-6), which evaluates foot morphology through visual inspection and palpation6–9. Using these evaluation methods of MLA can help predict the risk of sports injuries of the foot, ankle, and knee joints10–12. Furthermore, to clarify why foot morphology is associated with sports injuries, three-dimensional motion analysis systems are being used to elucidate the dynamics of the MLA during jumping and running, as well as the biomechanical differences between foot morphologies13–15.

The LLA is a longitudinal arch structure located on the lateral side of the foot. It is supported by active support mechanisms such as the peroneus longus, peroneus brevis, and abductor digiti minimi muscles, as well as passive support mechanisms such as the long plantar ligament, short plantar ligament, and plantar fascia16. Although research on this structure is limited, physical therapy often focuses on the LLA in clinical practice. This is because the cuboid bone, which serves as the keystone of the LLA, is believed to play a crucial role in the foot: it forms the Chopart joint in the midfoot, and the peroneus longus tendon runs along its inferior surface. Therefore, understanding the kinematics and basic characteristics of the LLA is essential for providing physical therapy to the lateral aspect of the foot.

Although the LLA is a clinically important structure, reports on its kinematics have been limited, and basic characteristics—such as the factors influencing the LLA itself—remains unclear. Kinematic analyses of the LLA have been reported using bone pins to directly observe the dynamics of the cuboid bone or utilizing X-rays17–19; however, given the invasiveness of these methods and the risk of radiation exposure, they are considered to have a significant impact on the subjects. While there are scattered reports of studies evaluating LLA dynamics by attaching reflective markers to the body’s surface or indirectly assessing LLA dynamics based on plantar pressure during walking20,21, it is presumed that the accuracy of these methods is compromised by artifacts from the skin and soft tissues. Therefore, we believe that biomechanical research on the LLA remains open to debate.

Even before a biomechanical evaluation of the LLA, we consider it a structure with many unknowns regarding its fundamental characteristics, whether it is influenced by sex, age, or foot morphology, and whether it can serve as a risk factor for sports injuries. In fact, while dynamic studies on the LLA are scattered, there are limited studies regarding static evaluation used X-ray imaging to investigate the correlation between LLA assessment indices (CH) and MLA assessment indices (navicular height, cuneiform height, and the calcaneal-first metatarsal angle), reporting r = 0.53, 0.56, −0.59 respectively22. Conversely, regarding MLA, there are reports investigating MLA morphology across different nationalities, age groups, and sex. A previous study comparing foot evaluations between men and women in Spain involving 423 participants reported that men had higher absolute values for arch height of MLA—such as navicular height and DAH—as well as higher arch height ratios normalized by foot length compared to women23. Furthermore, Nakao et al.24 investigated DAH in 689 Japanese individuals aged 18 to 83 years and reported that DAH was higher in men than in women and showed a significant correlation with physical characteristics such as body weight and BMI. If we can identify the characteristics of LLA through static evaluation, this information could potentially be utilized as foundational data in biomechanics.

Thus, there is little basic information on the LLA, and we believe this is due to the lack of development of quantitative static evaluation methods for the LLA. To date, evaluations have been limited to those using foot X-ray imaging22, or those using computed tomography (CT) to assess the shape of the cuboid bone25. We believe that establishing basic information and quantitative evaluation methods for LLA will be useful for the rehabilitation of lateral foot sports injuries, such as fifth metatarsal stress fractures and cuboid syndrome. Therefore, we developed a method for measuring cuboid height (CH) using ultrasound imaging26. However, it remains unclear whether CH is associated with evaluation indices or physical characteristics related to MLA. We believe that clarifying the relationship between LLA and static indicators of MLA will lead to the establishment of new evaluation perspectives and treatment methods in clinical practice. For example, whereas previous approaches to pronated or supinated feet focused on MLA height and foot shape, we believe it is possible to devise foot orthotics that also take LLA height into account. Clinically, while insoles designed for LLA have been reported to improve static single-leg standing balance27, this study allows us to relate the extent to which LLA elevation is necessary for individual participants. Furthermore, we believe this will aid in the creation of custom-made insoles that take into account the participant’s MLA and LLA characteristics.

The objective of this study was to investigate the relationship between CH measurements assessed using ultrasound (US) and sex differences, as well as clinically evaluated foot and physical characteristics.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was approved by the ethics committee of the Komatsu Orthopedic Sports Clinic (Ethics Approval Number: 2024.09). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. After being explained the steps of the study, all participants were subject to the study if consent was obtained to participate in the study. The study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The required sample size for this study was determined using G-Power 3.1.9.7. The analysis was based on the assumption of multiple regression analysis, with a large effect size (f2 = 0.35), α = 0.05, power = 0.8, and 8 factors. As a result, the required sample size was set at 52 participants.

A total of 73 university students (43 males and 30 females) participated in this study. Furthermore, the participants in this study were recruited from a single university. The exclusion criterion was the presence of acute/chronic orthopedic conditions. For example, the study included participants who had not sustained a lateral ankle ligament injury or a fracture of the foot or ankle within the past three months. A preliminary survey revealed that none of the participants had a history of acute orthopedic trauma and chronic ankle or foot pain. In this study, no exclusion criteria were established regarding foot morphology—including pronated or supinated foot—in order to examine the characteristics of CH regardless of foot type. Basic participant characteristics, including age, height, body mass, and leg length in a static standing position, were recorded and measured.

For LLA assessment, CH was measured using the method described by Kawamura et al.26. This method is a highly reliable measurement technique, with Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC) values of 0.99. Ultrasound (SONIMAGE HS-1, Konica Minolta) was performed using a 10 MHz linear probe (L18-4, Konica Minolta). The measurement procedure involved standing with equal weight distribution on both feet on the platform shown in Fig. 1. The platform consisted of two 20 cm high standing platforms with a 6 cm gap between them, bridged by a 1 mm-thick Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) plate (Fig. 1). The participants stood evenly loaded on the two platforms, ensuring the 5th metatarsal tuberosity and cuboid bone were positioned over the 6 cm gap. The feet were spaced 15 cm apart and kept parallel, consistent with radiographic conditions. Ultrasound gel was applied between the foot and PVC plate, as well as between the plate and probe. First, the fifth metatarsal tuberosity was visualized from the plantar surface; subsequently, the probe was moved proximally and medially to visualize the cuboid bone along its short axis. CH was defined as the shortest distance from the PVC plate to the cuboid bone (Fig. 2). To minimize calculation errors, CH was calculated three times from the same image, and the average value was used as the representative value.

As evaluations related to MLA, DAH and the Foot Posture Index-6 (FPI-6) were measured. DAH was defined as the height at the 50% point of foot length, measured using electronic calipers while applying equal weight to both feet, based on the method of Franettovich et al8.. FPI-6 was assessed using the method of Redmond et al7,9., with six items evaluating the hindfoot, midfoot, and forefoot on a scale ranging from − 2 to + 2 points. Since the FPI-6 is an ordinal scale, we converted the FPI-6 scores to an ordinal scale based on the values obtained from the Rasch analysis presented in previous studies and included them as variables (Modified-FPI-6)28.

Statistical analysis was performed using Modified R Commander 4.3.2. First, the normality of all measured variables was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Subsequently, depending on the results of the normality test, age, height, weight, foot length, CH, DAH, and FPI-6 were analyzed between men and women using either an independent t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Effect sizes r were classified as small (≥ 0.1), moderate (≥ 0.3), and large (≥ 0.5). Subsequently, a multiple regression analysis was performed on the combined male and female data using the stepwise method based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Unlike conventional stepwise procedures that use predefined p-value thresholds for variable inclusion or exclusion, the BIC-based approach selects variables according to improvement in overall model fit while penalizing model complexity29. Therefore, variables were retained or removed based on whether the BIC value decreased, and the model with the lowest BIC was ultimately selected. The dependent variable was CH, and the independent variables were sex, age, height, weight, foot length, DAH, and Modified-FPI-6. Sex was included as an indicator variable (dummy variable) (male = 1, female = 2). Note that Modified-FPI-6 was included as a continuous variable. To check for multicollinearity in the fitted model, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was examined, and it was confirmed that the VIF was 5 or less for all variables. The significance level for all statistical analyses was set at less than 5% using two-tailed tests.

Results

The results of the Shapiro-Wilk test revealed that in the male group, non-normality was confirmed for age, weight, foot length, and FPI-6 (p < 0.05). In the female group, non-normality was confirmed for age, foot length, and FPI-6 (p < 0.05).

Discussion

Until now, the only method for quantitatively assessing LLA has been through X-ray imaging. Compared to the MLA, this approach lacks basic information regarding the kinematics of the foot’s arch structure and the effects of factors such as age, gender, and ethnicity, resulting in limited evidence. However, since the LLA is a critical structure in foot physical therapy, there has been a need to develop a method for its assessment. In a previous study, we established a method for assessing CH using US26. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the correlations between CH measurements obtained using ultrasound and sex differences, as well as clinically assessed foot and physical characteristics. The results showed that CH values were higher in men than in women. Furthermore, multiple regression analysis revealed that the factors influencing CH were DAH and sex.

Within the scope of this study, basic attributes such as age, height, weight, and foot length were not independent predictors of CH, suggesting that their influence as confounding factors was minimal. However, since the study was limited to a single facility and a small sample size, further research is needed before these findings can be generalized to other studies. Previous studies have shown correlations between MLA measurements and sex, height, and weight23,30, and similar correlations were observed for CH; however, these variables were not significantly correlated with CH. On the other hand, since sex was included in the model based on the results of multiple regression analysis, sex differences may be a factor influencing CH. In other words, it can be said that there are specific reference values for CH in both men and women. In this study, the 95% confidence interval for CH in men was 19.45–19.77 mm, and in women, 17.07–17.40 mm; these values can be used as reference values for preliminary normative data for a specific Japanese university cohort. In a report by Gwani et al22., the 95% confidence intervals for CH measured using X-ray imaging equipment were 1.03–1.21 cm for men and 0.95–1.30 cm for women. In our previous study, we compared CH measurements obtained via X-ray imaging equipment with those obtained via ultrasound, referencing Gwani et al.’s measurement method, and reported that the measurement error was less than 1 mm26. In other words, although there are differences in the evaluation methods between ultrasound and X-ray imaging, considering the results of our previous study26, we believe that the Japanese university students who participated in this study exhibited higher CH than the Nigerians aged 18–35 reported by Gwani et al22., suggesting that CH may vary depending on race and age. In other reports on foot evaluation, a study comparing baseline foot data between Filipinos and Japanese reported differences between the two groups in various measurements, including heel width, foot width, foot length, first toe angle, and fifth toe angle31. Furthermore, there are reports indicating that Caucasians have a threefold higher incidence of pronated feet compared to African Americans, while conversely, the incidence of supinated feet and hallux valgus is five times lower, suggesting that foot shape is influenced by race32. Therefore, CH values may vary depending on racial differences from Japanese individuals and age differences. On the other hand, since the results of this study are limited by a lack of evaluations across different age groups and by limited data on height and weight, it is necessary to consider that these reference values are strictly limited to Japanese university students.

Next, we will discuss the relationship between CH and MLA evaluations. DAH was selected as a factor determining CH. We believe that the fact that DAH serves as a factor determining CH is due to anatomical characteristics. The cuboid bone is located on the lateral aspect of the foot between the Chopart and Lisfranc joints and is classified as part of the midfoot14,33. DAH measures the height of the dorsum of the foot at the 50% point of foot length8, which corresponds to the first cuneiform bone and, like the cuboid bone, is classified as part of the midfoot24. This measurement method has high ICC (0.98) and a high correlation coefficient (0.98) with radiographic findings, making it a valid method for evaluating MLA8. In other words, this suggests that CH is correlated with MLA height, and that it is particularly strongly associated with midfoot indices.

Finally, we will discuss why FPI-6 was not selected as a factor defining CH. The FPI-6 is an index that evaluates the foot by dividing it into the forefoot, midfoot, and hindfoot. Under this evaluation method, a score of 0 to 5 is classified as a normal foot, 6 or higher as a pronated foot, and − 1 or lower as a supinated foot7,9. While the assessment consists of evaluations of the subtalar joint, the shape of the medial longitudinal arch (MLA), and the forefoot, it does not include an evaluation of the lateral aspect of the foot7,9. In other words, although the FPI-6 can assess foot morphology related to the MLA, it cannot assess the lateral longitudinal arch (LLA). Therefore, we believe that by combining the FPI-6 with CH as an evaluation of the lateral aspect of the foot, it is possible to perform a comprehensive assessment of the entire foot.

We describe the limitations of this study. The sample was limited to healthy Japanese university students, and the sex ratio was uneven. Additionally, due to the skewed age distribution, the height of the CH may vary across different age groups, so we cannot comment on the influence of age. Furthermore, the range of height and weight was limited, and since factors such as past exercise habits were not controlled for, it is possible that similar trends may not be observed in individuals of the same age in other countries or across different age groups. Furthermore, this study included only a limited number of variables in the analysis, so it is possible that unmeasured factors influenced CH. In addition, the use of the stepwise method may have led to overfitting due to sample characteristics, potentially reducing the model’s generalizability. Therefore, as a future study direction, we believe it is important to conduct measurements across all age groups, from young adults to the elderly, and to aim for the generalization of CH values through the use of machine learning34. Finally, because the method used to measure CH in this study employs a specially designed platform, it is not possible to perform dynamic measurements such as those involving walking; therefore, we cannot comment on the functional aspects of the LLA.

Conclusion

Given that physical characteristics were not found to be factors determining CH in the results of this study, we consider them to be non-confounding factors. Furthermore, CH was found to be influenced by sex, suggesting that reference values differ between men and women. In this study, the 95% confidence interval for CH in men was 19.45–19.77 mm, and in women, 17.07–17.40 mm; these values can be used as reference values for preliminary normative data for a specific Japanese university cohort. Furthermore, DAH was found to be a factor determining CH, indicating a relationship between the medial and lateral support mechanisms of the midfoot.

Finally, since FPI-6 did not appear to be related to CH, it was suggested that CH is an independent assessment of the lateral foot, and that using it in combination with FPI-6 allows for a detailed evaluation of the foot.
