Semi‐Rational Engineering of Formate Dehydrogenase Reveals Loop Dynamics as a Key Determinant of Thermostability
Authors: Rattima Boonkumkrong, Sahachat Soithongcharoen, Pitchayathida Mee‐udorn, Paweenapon Chunthaboon, Somchart Maenpuen, Thanyada Rungrotmongkol, Pratchaya Watthaisong, Ruchanok Tinikul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFormate dehydrogenase (FDH) catalyzes the reversible formate oxidation using NAD+ to yield carbon dioxide and NADH. FDH is widely utilized for cofactor regeneration, enabling the continuous supplying of NADH for chiral chemical biosynthesis. Herein, we employed semi‐rational engineering to improve the thermostability of highly catalytic FDH from Bacillus simplex (BsFDH). Through two rounds of mutational screening, BsFDHQ125F variant was identified to exhibit significantly improved thermostability with a 25‐fold increase in half‐life at 60°C. Additionally, the BsFDHQ125F variant revealed significant stability against chemo‐inactivation. The 100‐ns molecular dynamics stimulation demonstrated a reduced overall root‐mean‐square deviation for the BsFDHQ125F, with enhanced local packing primarily driven by intra‐ and inter‐subunit π–π interaction among four tandem histidine and phenylalanine residues, thereby restricting the movement of flexible loop130−160. The implementation of the BsFDHQ125F variant for cofactor regeneration in 4‐nitrophenol detoxification demonstrated its superior stability and efficiency under bioconversion, rendering it suitable for various biocatalytic industrial applications.Keywords: biocatalysis, cofactor regeneration, enzyme engineering, formate dehydrogenase, thermostability

Introduction

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)–dependent formate dehydrogenase (FDH) catalyzes the oxidation of formate to carbon dioxide (CO2) and transfers two electrons to NAD+ (oxidized form) to yield NADH (reduced form). The FDH reaction is highly advantageous for biocatalysis. The NADH generated can be utilized as a co‐substrate in the enzymatic synthesis of various expensive chemicals, particularly stereospecific active medicinal ingredients [1, 2, 3]. The direct utilization of commercially available NADH in biocatalytic reactions is not economically viable owing to its high cost, thereby limiting the feasibility of its application. Consequently, the FDH reaction is widely used for NADH regeneration. The advantage of using FDH reaction is that the regenerated NADH can be produced from inexpensive formate, a simple compound that also serves as an effective buffering system. A gaseous nature of the CO2 byproduct facilitates its diffusion from the mixture without requiring an expensive downstream step [5, 6]. As the expensive NADH substrate is continuously regenerated, the amount of NADH used is significantly reduced, lowering synthesis costs by approximately 20‐ to 100‐fold compared to direct stoichiometric NADH addition. This markedly enhances the economic viability of NAD+‐dependent enzymatic processes [7, 8].

FDHs, a diverse group of enzymes, are typically classified into two major groups based on their requirement of a metal ion cofactor as metal‐independent and metal‐dependent FDHs [5]. The metal‐independent FDHs are NAD+‐dependent enzymes belonging to the D‐specific 2‐hydroxy acid dehydrogenases superfamily. They utilize NAD+ as a co‐substrate and are commonly found across prokaryotes and eukaryotes. The metal‐dependent FDHs are bacterial multi‐subunit enzymes that contain a heavy metal ion, typically tungsten (W) or molybdenum (Mo), as a cofactor within their active sites. They are classified under the mononuclear metal dimethylsulfoxide reductase family [10, 11]. Owing to their simple reactions, NAD+‐dependent FDHs have been extensively used as cofactor‐regenerating enzymes to feed NADH for biocatalytic process [6, 13, 14, 15]. Among the characterized FDHs, bacterial FDH shows higher activity (8–15 U/mg) compared to those in yeast and eukaryotes (4–6 U/mg) [1,3,5,17]. However, bacterial FDHs are highly susceptible to chemical inactivation. The eukaryotic FDHs, particularly yeast FDHs, have superior resistance to chemical inactivation. However, the low activity of yeast FDH necessitates the use of large quantities of enzyme for biocatalytic processes [18, 19]. Recently, a novel bacterial FDH from Bacillus simplex (BsFDH) has been reported which exhibits high activity (84 ± 3 U/mg), representing a 4.4‐fold increase compared to the FDH from Pseudomonas sp101 (PsFDH) (19 ± 1 U/mg). BsFDH distinguishes itself from other bacterial FDHs by its strong resistance to chemical inactivation by oxidizing agents and sodium azide [20]. However, BsFDH exhibits relatively low thermostability, with complete loss of activity following incubation at 60°C for <1 h. This renders it unsuitable for industrial applications that require enzymes to withstand temperatures of 50°C–60°C for extended periods [21].

Loss of FDH activity during operational processes is generally a consequence of thermal denaturation and chemical modification of the catalytic residues. Enzymes that exhibit high thermostability allow for elevated process temperatures and possess enhanced enzyme activity [22, 23]. Thus, enzyme engineering approaches have been successfully used to enhance FDH thermostability to meet the industrial process requirements. For example, the substitution of glutamine or lysine residues with glutamate at position 61 has been shown to improve the thermostability of Mycobacterium vaccae N10 FDH [25, 26]. In the case of FDH from Candida boidinii (CbFDH), a quadplex mutation variant CbFDHE151D/R178S/K306R/T315N showed a 5.7‐fold increase in thermal stability compared to WT enzyme [1, 27]. In FDH from soya Glycine max, the substitution of conserved F290 in the co‐enzyme binding domain with aspartate or glutamate residues increased their T m by 4.3–7.8°C compared to the WT, owing to increased number of H‐bond interactions [23, 24]. Protein hydrophobization–mediated modification of the α‐helices in PsFDH resulted in PsFDHS(131/160/184/228)A variants with 1.5‐fold increased thermal stability [22]. By introducing proline residue to reduce the flexibility of loops in FDH from Lodderomyces elongisporus, LeFDHK21P/K22R exhibited lower conformational flexibility with high root‐mean‐square fluctuation (RMSF), resulting in a 2.1‐fold increase in thermostability [28]. Introduction of a disulfide bond in the CbFDHA10C variant resulted in a sevenfold increase in half‐life at 60°C and higher resistance to oxidative inactivation [29].

Several previous studies have demonstrated that computational predictions can be used to guide the rational design of enzyme variants with higher stability [30, 31, 32, 33]. Therefore, in this study, we employed semi‐rational mutagenesis approach based on computational predictions from the FireProt program [31], along with structural analysis, to engineer BsFDH with enhanced structural stability (see workflow in Figure 1). Using this approach, we identified a BsFDHQ125F variant that exhibited increased thermostability and tolerance against organic solvents and chemical/oxidizing agents. Molecular dynamic (MD) stimulation revealed that the structural compactness of the BsFDHQ125F variant via π–π and hydrophobic interactions contributes to its structural rigidity. The application of this thermostable variant for cofactor regeneration in the denitration reaction to detoxify 4‐nitrophenol revealed efficient NADH generation, with high activity retention throughout the operational process.

Overall strategy used for the rational engineering of thermostable BsFDH variants. Site‐directed mutagenesis at selected positions was performed based on the implementation criteria, and a second‐round site‐saturation mutagenesis was performed on the improved thermostable variant to generate the variant libraries.

Results and Discussion

Selection and Screening of Thermostable BsFDH Variants

In this study, the FireProt program [31], a web server for automated design thermostable mutants, was used to predict stabilizing variants. The program integrates both energy‐ and evolution‐based computational approaches using FoldX and Rosetta engineering tools to calculate the ΔΔG fold referring to the difference of folding free energy change between WT and variant. From the FireProt result of submitted BsFDH model structure (Figure S1), >60 thermostable variants were identified. To narrow down the number of candidate variants, we first selected the variants with a ΔΔG fold < −1 kcal/mol. Then, we applied additional selection criteria based on known structural and functional characteristics of the enzyme [32]. Highly conserved residues that are likely to have an important role in catalysis, the residues located in the active‐site milieu (approximately 10–15 Å away from the binding sites of formate and NAD+), and the residues previously reported to be crucial for FDH activity were omitted to avoid altering the catalytic function [20]. In contrast, the residues residing in the subunit interface and flexible regions, which have the potential to interact with other residues between subunits or secondary structures or motifs, were considered (Figure 2A). As a result, only 11 variants, including T43M, S50L, S59L, A69F, Q125L, G136Y, G151Q, L196W, T213C, A216I, and S262M, were selected for expression, purification, and further investigations (Table S1).

Thermostability analysis revealed that the BsFDHQ125L, BsFDHA216I, and BsFDHS262M variants exhibited enhanced thermostability while retaining catalytic activity comparable to that of WT (Table S1). The enhanced thermostability of BsFDHA216I and BsFDHS262M variants may be attributed to their position on the enzyme surface. The substitution with longer side chains at these positions may facilitate interactions with adjacent residues, thus augmenting structural stability. Improving electrostatic interactions on the protein surface is commonly employed to optimize the surface electrostatic interactions and stability of globular proteins [35, 36]. Among the selected variants (BsFDHQ125L, BsFDHG136Y, and BsFDHG151Q) located at the subunit interface within a flexible loop (residues 130–160), only BsFDHQ125L demonstrated catalytic efficiency comparable to that of WT and markedly enhanced thermostability relative to the WT (Table S1). The substitution of glycine residues at positions 136 and 151 in the loop (residues 130–160) with more polar residues is speculated to strengthen the flexible loop and enhance the structural integrity of the enzyme [28]. However, the catalytic activity of the BsFDHG136Y and BsFDHG151Q variants was markedly impaired, with specific activities of 0.25 U/mg (20‐fold reduction) and 0.04 U/mg (120‐fold reduction), respectively. Structural analysis revealed that in the native dimeric assembly, this loop is inserted into the cleft of the adjacent subunit and directly interacts with part of the active‐site wall, where H298 and Y279, essential catalytic residues [20], are located (Figure S2). Substitution of small G136 and G151 with large and polar residues (tyrosine and glutamine) might cause steric hindrance and perturb catalytic H298 function. This structural arrangement likely explains why mutations at positions distal to the active site within a single subunit nevertheless have a pronounced impact on catalytic activity.

To further enhance enzyme thermostability, the Q125, A216, and S262 positions were selected for a second round of site‐saturation mutagenesis. Following the design of NNK primers for the plasmid library, we used the Q pool score to assess our library quality and confirm each randomized nucleotide position [30]. The results indicated that the Q pool values of the Q125, A216, and S262 libraries were in a range of 0.61–0.71. Following plasmid transformation, over 300 colonies were screened for each library, and the BsFDHQ125F variant with higher thermostability than the WT and BsFDHQ125L variants was obtained. In contrast, for the A216 and S262 residues, no variants with enhanced thermostability, compared with the BsFDHA216I and BsFDHS262M variants, were obtained. As the BsFDHS262M variant showed higher thermostability than the BsFDHA216I variant, we constructed a BsFDHQ125F/S262M double mutant to investigate whether this BsFDH variant exhibits improved thermostability. All variants were purified to homogeneity and subsequently characterized (Figure S3 and Table S2).

Enhanced Thermostability of BsFDH Variants

As shown in Figure 2B, all the variants exhibited enhanced thermostability. After incubation at 60°C for 20 min, the BsFDHQ125F and BsFDHQ125F/S252M variants retained over 80% of their formate oxidation activity, whereas WT enzyme exhibited a substantial loss of activity, retaining only 23.2 ± 1.1%. Time‐course thermal inactivation (Figure 2C) experiments also showed that the BsFDHQ125F variant exhibited the highest thermostability improvement compared to the BsFDHQ125L variant, as it retained >50% activity even after 120 min, whereas BsFDHWT enzyme lost its activity completely after 50 min. Although the BsFDHS262M variant showed improved thermostability, its combination with Q125F failed to produce a synergistic improvement. The BsFDHQ125F/S262M double mutant showed lower thermostability than the BsFDHQ125F single mutant. The half‐life t 1/2 (Table 1) of the BsFDHQ125F variant (478 min) at 60°C was 25 times higher than that of WT (18.7 min). In contrast, the BsFDHQ125F/S262M variant demonstrated an improved half‐life of 194 min. The antagonistic effect observed in the BsFDHQ125F/S262M variant is likely caused by steric hindrance. The bulky phenyl side chain of F125 is positioned near the α‐helix9–loop–β‐sheet10 motif (residues 225–273), which includes M262 (Figure S4). The simultaneous presence of these two large residues in the BsFDHQ125F/S262M variant likely induces distortion of the backbone in this structural region, thereby destabilizing the overall protein fold. In contrast, this steric conflict is absent in the BsFDHQ125F single mutant, as the native serine at position 262 is sufficiently small to accommodate the phenylalanine side chain without introducing structural strain. Thermofluor assay indicated that the elevated T m values of both the BsFDHQ125F and BsFDHQ125F/S262M variants corresponded to enhanced structural stability. In addition, the BsFDHQ125F variant showed the optimum activity at 70°C, which is higher than that of WT (60°C) and the BsFDHQ125L variant (65°C) (Figure 2D).

We also compared the thermostability of the BsFDHQ125F variant with that of the well‐characterized thermostable PsFDH at ambient temperature. The results showed that the thermostable BsFDHQ125F variant retained its activity for over 49 days, whereas WT PsFDH and BsFDH completely lost their activities after 42 and 28 days, respectively (Figure 2E). The data strongly supports the improved stability of the BsFDHQ125F variant. Thus, these results indicate that the substituting glutamine with a more hydrophobic residue, such as leucine or phenylalanine, improves structural integrity. This may be attributed to increased hydrophobic interactions with local hydrophobic residues, thus strengthening the interaction within the inner core of the protein structure [37, 38]. This approach of improving enzyme stability by introducing hydrophobic residues into the core structure of proteins has been successfully applied in various enzyme systems, including Candida antarctica lipase B (CalB) and Bacillus subtilis lipase A (BSLA) [40, 41].

Comparison of Formate Oxidation Kinetics Among BsFDH Variants

The results presented in Table 1 indicate that both thermostable variants, BsFDHQ125L and BsFDHQ125F, exhibit comparable specific activity and K m values for NAD+ and formate to that of WT. In contrast, the thermostable BsFDHS262M and double mutant BsFDHQ126F/S262M showed a twofold increase in their K m values for NAD+, resulting in a decrease in catalytic efficiency to half of that of WT. However, their K m values for formate were similar. These data suggest that changing the surface amino acid residues has no significant effect on overall enzyme catalytic activity.

Effects of pH on Activity and Stability of BsFDH Variants

The pH‐relative activity profiles (Figure 3A) revealed that WT and BsFDHQ125L exhibited optimal activity at pH 8.0, whereas BsFDHQ125F and the double mutant BsFDHQ125F/S262M exhibited a broad pH range maintaining 90%–100% relative activity over pH of 6.5–10.0. When examining the pH stability of the variants by incubating them at different pH at 4°C for 24 h, the pH‐residual activity profile revealed (Figure 3B) that BsFDHQ125F and BsFDHQ125F/S262M exhibited higher tolerance to alkaline pH conditions (8–9.5), which was slightly better than that of WT and BsFDHQ125L. These data suggest that BsFDHQ125F and BsFDHQ125F/S262M display boarder pH‐activity profile and enhanced pH tolerance.

Chemical Tolerance of BsFDH Variants

Replacing Q125 with bulky hydrophobic residues (leucine and phenylalanine) improves structural stability of the enzyme. Therefore, we focused on the Q125 variants (BsFDHQ125L and BsFDHQ125F) to further examine their characteristics relevant to prospective biotechnology applications. The chemical tolerance of WT and BsFDHQ125 variants to two reactive chemicals, H2O2 and NaN3, was tested and compared to that of the well‐characterized PsFDH, which was known to be highly susceptible to reactive chemicals [27, 42]. The results indicated that both H2O2 (Figure 4A) and NaN3 (Figure 4B) markedly inhibited PsFDH activity, whereas WT and the variants exhibited higher chemical tolerance. Time‐course inactivation assay using 0.5% (v/v) H2O2 showed that PsFDH activity decreased markedly to <50% after 20 min and was totally lost after 50 min. However, only 20%–30% of the enzymatic activity of WT and BsFDHQ125L were lost after 50 min. Notably, >95% of the activity was retained by the BsFDHQ125F variant. These results indicate that the BsFDHQ125F variant exhibits enhanced tolerance to H2O2 compared to WT.

NaN3, an antimicrobial agent commonly present as a microimpurity in biocatalytic processes, has been identified as a potent inhibitor for various FDHs [1, 43]. Therefore, we examined the effect of azide ion by incubating the enzyme with NaN3 (20 μM). The result showed that the activities of all the FDHs were reduced following NaN3 treatment (Figure 4B). PsFDH activity was markedly inhibited by the azide anion, retaining <5% of its original activity. The BsFDH WT demonstrated higher tolerance, retaining approximately 50% activity, whereas the BsFDHQ125F variant exhibited even superior performance, retaining >80% activity. The data indicate that the thermostable BsFDHQ125F variant exhibits superior resistance to reactive substances.

To obtain further insights into azide binding affinity, we performed the azide inhibition kinetics assays to determine the inhibition constant (K i) for the BsFDH variants, relative to the WT. The double‐reciprocal plots of initial rates versus formate concentrations at varying concentrations of azide revealed a pattern of intersecting lines, indicative of competitive inhibition in both BsFDHQ125L (Figure 4C) and BsFDHQ125F (Figure 4D) variants of reaction kinetics. Based on the secondary plot of slopes versus azide concentrations, the K i value for the BsFDHQ125L variant was determined to be 4.05 ± 1.69 μM, which was comparable to that of the BsFDH WT reported previously (3.06 ± 1.07 μM) [20]. In contrast, the K i for the BsFDHQ125F variant was approximately twofold higher (7.32 ± 1.38 μM). The results indicate that the BsFDHQ125F variant exhibits reduced affinity for azide, resulting in diminished susceptibility to inhibition compared to both WT and the BsFDHQ125L variant. This is consistent with the high residual activity of the BsFDHQ125F variant observed in the previous assay (Figure 4B).

Solvent Tolerance of BsFDH Variants

Bioconversion of nonpolar substrates is typically performed in organic solvents to produce various high value‐added products. Therefore, high solvent stability of enzymes is critical to prevent enzyme inactivation in the presence of high concentrations of organic solvents, ensuring efficient bioconversion [44, 45]. The, the BsFDHQ125F variant, which exhibited superior thermostability and chemo‐tolerance compared to the BsFDHQ125L variant, was tested for its ability to tolerate organic solvents, including MeOH, EtOH, DMSO, acetonitrile, and hexane relative to the WT enzyme.

As shown in Figure 4E, the BsFDHQ125F enzyme activity was elevated in all concentrations of EtOH and DMSO. The activity increased up to 1.5‐fold in the presence 30% (v/v) EtOH and 20% (v/v) DMSO, with both solvents showing no negative effect on enzyme activity up to 50% (v/v) concentration. In contrast, the WT enzyme exhibited only a 1.2‐fold increase in activity at 5%–10% EtOH and DMSO. To explore the mechanism underlying solvent‐induced enhancement of enzyme activity, structural integrity and secondary structure were evaluated under varying cosolvent conditions (ethanol and DMSO) using a thermofluor assay (Figure S5) and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (Figure S6). However, neither approach provided evidence of solvent‐induced structural stabilization or significant changes in secondary structure. Instead, increasing solvent concentrations resulted in decreased thermal stability (T m) for both enzymes. Notably, the BsFDHQ125F variant consistently exhibited higher stability under these conditions, indicating enhanced solvent tolerance. CD analysis further showed that, in the presence of ethanol (10%–30% v/v)—a concentration range previously reported to enhance enzyme activity—both the WT and BsFDHQ125F variant displayed spectra comparable to those obtained in the absence of ethanol. At higher solvent concentrations of 50% (v/v), the activity of the WT enzyme decreased markedly, and only 45%–65% activity remained. MeOH also slightly enhanced activity of the BsFDHQ125F variant, whereas it significantly inhibited the WT. Hexane and acetonitrile exerted significant inhibitory effect on both BsFDHQ125F variant and WT, with stronger effect on WT enzyme. In the presence of 30% (v/v) hexane, the BsFDHQ125F variant retained its activity, whereas that of WT dropped to 76%. In the presence of 30% (v/v) acetonitrile, the BsFDHQ125F variant exhibited a relative activity of 75%, whereas that of WT decreased to less than 50%. DMSO was previously demonstrated to enhance Rhodococcus jostii FDH and CbFDH activity by 1.3–1.4 fold [20, 42]. The presence of hydrophilic DMSO may support the three‐dimensional water structure around the protein molecule, enhancing enzymatic function [46].

The results of the solvent tolerance assay (Figure 4F) revealed the high solvent tolerance of the BsFDHQ125F variant toward MeOH, hexane, and DMSO, comparable to WT, retaining > 80% relative activity after 24‐h treatment. After EtOH treatment, the activity of the WT enzyme reduced to approximately 14%, whereas that of the BsFDHQ125F variant remained unchanged. Notably, both enzymes did not tolerate acetonitrile treatment, and their activities were completely lost within 30 min of treatment. These results indicate that the BsFDHQ125F variant exhibits superior resistance toward EtOH compared to WT, suggesting that the substitution of Q125 with a bulky hydrophobic phenylalanine enhances solvent tolerance in addition to structural stability.

MD Simulations Reveal Thermostability Mechanisms of BsFDH Q125 Variants

To understand how the substitution of Q125 with hydrophobic residues, such as leucine and phenylalanine, enhances the structural stability of BsFDH, MD simulations were conducted with the modeled structures of the BsFDHQ125 variants and compared with that of the WT (Figure S1). Root‐mean‐square deviation (RMSD) of the backbone atoms over 100 ns simulations at different temperatures was used to examine the conformational fluctuations of the protein and identify the potential structural factors that stabilize the protein structure [41, 47]. The results indicated that at 300 K (27°C), the WT enzyme exhibited the most stable structure with the lowest overall RMSD over 100 ns simulations, whereas upon temperature increment, the overall RMSD values of the Q125 variants were lower than those of the WT (Figure 5A and Figure S7). At 400 K (127°C), the BsFDHQ125F variant showed the highest stability with the lowest average RMSD at 0.771 Å compared with 1.064 and 1.054 Å of BsFDHQ125L and WT, respectively. This result indicates more rigidity of the BsFDHQ125F variant under elevated temperature. The pattern of RMSF value obtained from the last 20‐ns MD simulations of protein backbone atoms, which represents the degree of freedom, showed three regions in the BsFDH structure with high RMSF values. These are associated with the flexible loop region located on the protein surface (loop 9–21, loop 130–160, and loop 274–285) (Figure 5B,C and Figure S7). At 300 K, all enzymes showed comparable RMSF values; however, at higher temperature, the WT enzyme showed high RMSF for atomic position, especially in the 130–160 loop region, indicating higher movement of residues compared with the Q125 variants, with the BsFDHQ125F variant exhibiting the most stability. These data are consistent with the previous results showing enhanced thermostability of the BsFDHQ125F variant compared to BsFDHQ125L. Superimposition of the representative structures at different temperatures using the Chimera program revealed that the loop 130–160 differed significantly and exhibited the highest fluctuation at 400 K (Figure 5D). This indicates that this fluctuation loop is a major contributor to the structural stability of the enzyme. The analysis of secondary structure (Figure S8) indicated greater changes of overall WT structure compared to the other variants during the 100‐ns simulations. The loop 130–160 in WT obviously transitioned to random coils. In contrast, the loop 130–160 in BsFDHQ125F and BsFDHQ125L changed from an α‐helix to a turn conformation and remained in that conformation throughout the simulations, confirming a major contribution of loop 130–160 to structural stability. The Q125 residue is in the core of the loop basement, and not within the flexible loop region. Thus, a single substitution with phenylalanine augments the stability of this loop area and overall structural rigidity, resulting in enhanced thermostability.

Analysis of interaction around the L125 and F125 variant residues (Figure 5E) revealed the surrounding hydrophobic residues, including A272, A292, and V426. Substitution with leucine or phenylalanine results in increased hydrophobic interactions. In addition, the aromatic side chain of phenylalanine engages in aromatic stacking with the H121 imidazole ring (4.6 Å distance). Histidine residue is significantly involved in both π–π and cation interactions with aromatic and polar residues, which are crucial for protein stability and contribute to conformational alterations in protein structure [24, 48]. Moreover, phenylalanine substitution also creates inter‐subunit π–π stacking with two tandem rings of F133 and H137 from another subunit, illustrating how a single change significantly enhances structural stability (Figure 5E). Thus, substituting the polar Q125 with hydrophobic residues enhances the core packaging and fortifies the enzyme structure. Based on MD simulation results, aromatic interactions appear to play an important role at position Q125. Therefore, we investigated whether substitution with aromatic residues, tyrosine or tryptophan, could enhance enzyme stability. The BsFDHQ125Y variant (5.25 ± 0.29 U/mg) exhibited a specific activity comparable to that of the WT enzyme (5.06 ± 0.18 U/mg). In contrast, the tryptophan substitution markedly impaired both enzymatic activity and expression yield. The BsFDHQ125W variant displayed a specific activity of 0.17 ± 0.07 U/mg—corresponding to a 31‐fold decrease relative to the WT. Based on these findings, the thermal stability of the BsFDHQ125Y variant was further evaluated at 60°C. However, this variant exhibited significantly lower thermal stability than the WT enzyme, with a t 1 / 2 of 2.50 min at 60°C (Figure S9). These results suggest that the introduction of a polar hydroxyl group in tyrosine may disrupt hydrophobic packing, whereas the larger side chain of tryptophan may perturb local van der Waals interactions and π–π stacking. Cofactor Regeneration to Support Denitration Reaction

The BsFDHQ125F variant exhibited superior thermostability and chemical tolerance in the previous assays. Therefore, we sought to examine whether the BsFDHQ125F variant efficiently functions as a cofactor‐regenerating enzyme under severe conditions. Denitration of 4‐nitrophenol (4‐NP) catalyzed by HadA, flavin‐dependent monooxygenase, has a significant implication in detoxifying 4‐NP, a toxic and persistent environmental pollutant [52] and pesticide detection [53]. This reaction was used as a model system in this experiment because 4‐NP conversion can be easily monitored by spectrophotometry, and the reaction effectively represents biocatalytic systems that produce H2O2 as a byproduct [54] or use it as a co‐substrate [55, 56]. In principle (Figure 6), the FDH reaction generates NADH for the flavin reductase, which subsequently produces the reduced FAD (FADH−) for the HadA reaction to transform 4‐NP into bezoquinone (BQ). The WT and BsFDHQ125F variant were tested as NADH‐regenerating enzymes under three distinct conditions: (i) preheating enzymes at 65°C for 25 min prior to assay, (ii) pretreating enzymes with 0.2% (v/v) H2O2 at 25°C for 4 h before assay, and (iii) performing the reaction in the presence of azide ion. The stability of the enzymes during the bioconversion process was evaluated by monitoring their apparent activity. As shown in Figure 6, preheated WT enzyme failed to regenerate NADH cofactor for 4‐NP denitration, compared with the control reaction (without heat treatment), as evidenced by the absence of any change in absorbance at 400 nm. In the BsFDHQ125F variant reaction, despite a slower conversion rate compared to the control reaction (untreated enzyme), the reaction successfully converted 4‐NP to BQ (as evidenced by the gradual depletion of 4‐NP), and the conversion was complete in 75 min. The monitoring of FDH activity throughout the bioconversion process also indicated that heat treatment reduced the activity of the BsFDHQ125F variant from 0.35 to 0.2 U/mL; however, it retained an activity level of 0.18 U/mL over the operational time. The WT activity decreased by fourfold following heat treatment and was entirely lost after 10 min. This suggests that heat treatment completely inactivates BsFDHWT, whereas the BsFDHQ125F variant remains active and efficiently regenerates the cofactor.

Application of thermostable BsFDHQ125F variant for NADH regeneration in the 4‐NP denitration reaction. The depletion of 4‐NP substrate (bar graph) was monitored at 400 nm, and residual activity of BsFDH enzymes (line graph) in the reaction was measured by withdrawing enzyme aliquots from the reaction at different time points. 4‐NP conversion and enzyme activity were compared in the following treatment groups: 65°C‐pretreated FDHs, 0.2% (v/v) H2O2‐pretreated BsFDH, and reaction performed in the presence of 20 μM NaN3.

Bioconversion using H2O2‐pretreated enzymes revealed that both WT and BsFDHQ125F exhibit comparable conversion rates, achieving complete conversion within 25 min. Assessment of enzyme stability during the operation demonstrated their capacity to maintain activity throughout the process. Compared to the control reaction, H2O2 treatment had a lesser impact on the BsFDHQ125F variant (>80% activity retained) than on the WT (approximately 50% activity retained). These results are consistent with the observation that the BsFDHQ125F variant exhibits increased H2O2 tolerance.

Following treatment with NaN3, the reaction system containing BsFDHQ125F variant (85 min) exhibited an accelerated bioconversion rate compared with WT (100 min) system. Furthermore, when aliquots of the FDH were withdrawn from the reaction system to assess their activity, the activity of the BsFDHQ125F variant (0.25 U/mL) exceeded that of BsFDHWT (0.1 U/mL). These results demonstrate that the BsFDHQ125F variant is more stable than BsFDHWT under azide ion‐containing bioconversion conditions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we used a semi‐rational enzyme engineering approach to generate BsFDH variants with enhanced properties suitable for industrial biocatalytic processes. The BsFDHQ125F variant exhibited a 25‐fold increase in half‐life at 60°C, while maintaining efficient catalytic activity. This variant also demonstrated significant stability against organic solvents including H2O2 and azide inactivation. The results of MD simulation revealed that enhanced structural stability was achieved through increased rigidity in the flexible loop topology of the enzyme. The increased structural stiffness and thermostability were predominantly a result of intra‐ and inter‐subunit π–π and hydrophobic interactions. The thermostable variant actively functioned as a cofactor‐regenerating enzyme even after pretreatment with heat and H2O2 in biotransformation processes. Thus, this rationally engineered FDH variant constitutes a promising biocatalytic enzyme for industrial applications.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

All chemicals and reagents used in the study were of analytical grade and obtained from Sigma–Aldrich (Burlington, MA, USA) and Tokyo Chemical Industrial (Chuo‐ku, Tokyo, Japan). The molecular biology reagents, including DNA polymerase and restriction endonucleases, were purchased from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, MA USA). The plasmid DNA extraction/preparation and FavorPrep Gel/PCR purification kits were from FavorGen Biotech Corporation (Ping‐Tung, Taiwan). Oligonucleotides were customized and purchased by Pacific Science Corporation (Bangkok, Thailand). DNA sequences were verified using an automated DNA sequencing service provided by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Korea). All chromatography media were purchased from GE Healthcare (Uppsala, Sweden). The molar extinction coefficients (ε) at pH 7.0 were used as follows: ε260NAD(P)+, 18 mM−1cm−1; ε340NAD(P)H, 6.22 mM−1cm−1; ε280PsFDH, 58.71 mM−1cm−1; ε280BsFDH, 37.49 mM−1cm−1; ε458C1, 12.8 mM−1cm−1; and ε280HadA, 74.9 mM−1cm−1.

In Silico Prediction of Thermostable Variants

Thermostable variants of BsFDH were computationally designed using FireProt 2.0 [31], an online tool that integrates FoldX and Rosetta algorithms with evolution‐ and energy‐based calculations. As the X‐ray structure of BsFDH is not yet available, a modeled three‐dimensional structure of BsFDH was generated using SWISS‐MODEL Workspace based on the CbFDH X‐ray structure (PDB: 5DN9) [20]. This homology modeled structure aligned well with the structural model built by AlphaFold (Figure S2, see MD simulation section). The FireProt program predicts the thermostable enzyme variants by combining structural and evolutionary information. Thermostable candidates were selected based on folding free energy change (ΔΔG fold) of <−1 kcal/mol and other criteria based on the enzyme structure and function [32, 33].

Site‐Directed and Site‐Saturation Mutagenesis

The pET11a‐Bsfdh plasmid was used as a template for mutagenesis. The primers used to create site‐specific mutations using PCR are listed in Table S4. The reaction consisted of pET11a‐Bsfdh template, 500 nM each of the forward and reverse primers, 200 µM dNTPs, and 2.5 units of Phusion DNA polymerase in 1× reaction buffer. The thermocycler program was initiated with denaturation at 98°C for 5 min, followed by 16 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 45 s, annealing at 50°C–55°C for 1 min (depending on the T m of primers), and extension at 72°C for 4 min. A final extension was performed at 72°C for 10 min. The PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis and treated with DpnI overnight at 37°C to digest the WT template and then transformed into Escherichia coli XL1Blue competent cells supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin. The plasmids were extracted, and the sequences were verified. For site‐saturation mutagenesis experiments, primers containing NNK codon sequences were used to generate the plasmid library. The library quality was evaluated using the quality score (Q‐value), determined directly from the pooled sequencing data [30]. The library with a Q‐value score > 0.7, which indicates complete randomization in the target position, was used for screening variants.

Enzyme Expression, Purification, and Assay

The expression and purification of BsFDH variants were carried out following the previously reported protocol for the WT [20], with minor modifications. In brief, the recombinant BsFDH WT and variants were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) using autoinduction media at 25°C for 16 h. The enzymes were first purified by precipitation using 0.5% (w/v) polyethylenimine and 40%–60% ammonium sulfate saturation, followed by anion exchange column chromatography on a DEAE‐Sepharose column at pH 7.0. The purified enzymes were stored at −80°C in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer pH 7.0 containing 10% (v/v) glycerol until use. Flavin reductase (C1) and 4‐halophenol 4‐monooxygenase A (HadA) were expressed and purified according to the method described by Maenpuen et al. [33] and Pimviriyakul et al. [58], respectively.

Formate oxidation assay was performed at 25°C in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer pH 7.0 consisting of 400 μM NAD+, 800 mM sodium formate, and 0.25 μM FDH. The rate of NADH formation was calculated based on the molar extinction coefficient of NADH at 340 nm. One unit of enzyme was defined as the amount of enzyme used to produce 1 µmole of NADH per min at 25°C.

Screening of Thermostable BsFDH Variants Using a 96‐Well Plate Assay

The variant plasmid library was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells. Individual colonies were inoculated into 200 μL of LB medium containing 50 μg/mL ampicillin in 96‐well plates and cultured overnight at 37°C with shaking at 150 rpm. The starter cultures (1%; v/v) were inoculated into fresh 200 μL of LB medium containing 50 μg/mL ampicillin and cultured at 37°C with shaking at 150 rpm for 3 h. The temperature was then reduced to 25°C. Isopropyl β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (final concentration, 1 mM) was added to induce protein expression, and the cultures were incubated further for 16 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. For activity screening assay, cells were resuspended in 100 μL of ice‐cold lysis buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate buffer pH 7.0 containing 1 mM DTT, 60 μM PMSF, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 1 mg/mL lysozyme) and incubated at 25°C for 1 h. The enzyme activity in the crude lysate was then assayed. To select thermostable variants, the lysate was separately challenged by incubating at 60°C and 65°C for 2 h and 20 min, respectively. Next, both treated lysates were assayed by adding an assay cocktail containing 400 μM NAD+ and 800 mM sodium formate and measuring the NADH production at 340 nm using a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The FDH activity remaining was calculated and compared with that of the enzyme without incubation at the elevated temperatures.

Characterization of Biochemical and Kinetics Properties of BsFDH Variants

Measurement of Kinetic Parameters

Steady‐state kinetic parameters were determined by varying the concentrations of sodium formate (50–1200 mM) while maintaining the NAD+ concentration at 400 μM. Additionally, when the concentration of sodium formate was maintained at 800 mM, the NAD+ concentration varied from 30 to 1500 µM. Direct plots were constructed using the initial velocities calculated from slopes of NADH production at different substrate concentrations. Steady‐state kinetic parameters were analyzed using the Kaleida graph program.

Effect of Temperature and pH on BsFDH Activity and Stability

To evaluate the thermostability of BsFDH, a thermofluor assay was performed using the CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, USA). The reaction contained 40 μM BsFDH and 50× SYPRO orange dye in 100 mM 4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid, HEPES buffer (pH 8.0). The temperature was increased from 10°C to 95°C at a constant rate of 1°C/min. The melting temperature (T m) was determined from the plot of intrinsic fluorescence signal versus temperature.

To assess the effect of temperature on BsFDH activity, both WT and variants were incubated at various temperatures (30°C−70°C) for 20 min. Residual activity of each BsFDH variant was assayed in triplicate and reported as mean ± SD, compared relative to the WT. The relative residual activity for each BsFDH variant was calculated accordingly and plotted as a function of temperature. A temperature at 60°C was selected for further analyses as the residual activity of the enzyme started decreasing beyond that temperature.

To further investigate time‐dependent thermal inactivation, each BsFDH (5 µM) variant was incubated at 60°C for various time points (0−120 min) and assayed. The relative residual activity of each BsFDH variant was determined and plotted against the incubation durations, which is described by the first‐order kinetics equation, ln(A/A o ) = ‒kt, where A o is the initial activity, A is the residual activity at the time of t during incubation, k is the inactivation rate constant (min−1), and t is the incubation time. The half‐life (t 1/2) of thermal inactivation was then calculated using the equation t 1/2 = ln2/k.

To determine the optimum temperature for BsFDH activity, the enzyme activity was assessed at different temperatures ranging from 4°C to 70°C. The initial rates were then calculated and plotted against temperature.

To investigate the effect of pH on BsFDH activity, the reactions were performed in 20 mM sodium phosphate–sodium pyrophosphate buffer system at pH 5.5–10.0. The initial rates were measured and plotted as a function of pH to determine the optimum pH for BsFDH activity. To evaluate the stability of the enzyme at different pH, the enzyme was incubated at different pH at 4°C for 24 h. Relative residual activity was calculated and compared to that prior to incubation.

Assessment of the Tolerance of BsFDH Variants Toward Organic Solvents and Reactive Chemicals

Effect of Organic Solvents on BsFDH Activity and Stability

Solvent tolerance of BsFDH against commonly used organic solvents, including water‐miscible solvents, i.e., methanol (MeOH), ethanol (EtOH), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and acetonitrile, and the water‐immiscible solvent hexane, was examined. The oil–water partition coefficient of the solvents is (log p value) as follows: MeOH (−0.76), DMSO (−1.30), acetonitrile (−0.33), EtOH (0.24), and hexane (3.76). The FDH activity was measured either in the presence of different concentrations of an organic solvent (5%–50% (v/v)) or by incubating the enzyme (10 µM) with 30% (v/v) organic solvent at 25°C. The relative residual activity was calculated and compared with that at the start time of incubation (t = 0).

Inhibition of BsFDH by Reactive Chemicals

The stability of enzymes against H2O2 was investigated by incubating the enzymes with 0.5% (v/v) H2O2 for various time intervals, after which aliquots were withdrawn to measure enzyme activity. Tolerance to azide ion inhibition was evaluated by incubating the enzyme with 20 μM sodium azide (NaN3) for 10 min at 25°C prior to measurement of the activity.

The inhibition constant (K i) of the enzyme was determined by assaying BsFDH in the presence of various concentrations of azide ions (1–16 µM NaN3). Each reaction contained 0.5 μM BsFDH and 400 μM NAD+ with varying formate concentrations (50–500 mM). A double‐reciprocal plot of the initial rates and formate concentrations was described by the competitive inhibition (Equation (1) ), where v and V represent the initial and maximum velocities, A and I are the substrate and inhibitor concentrations, respectively, and K A is the Michaelis constant for substrate. The secondary plot of the primary slope versus inhibitor concentration was used to determine K i.

MD Simulation

To assess the conformational changes in BsFDH and its variants at different temperatures, MD simulations were performed using the AMBER22 program [59, 60]. The initial structure of WT was generated through homology modeling using the SWISS‐MODEL web tool [61]. The modeled structure, which exhibited a Ramachandran favored percentage of 97.10% (Figure S2), was selected as the representative structure of WT. The WT structure was then modified using the Discovery Studio Visualizer program [62] to create the BsFDHQ125F and BsFDHQ125L variants. The ionizable residues of both WT and BsFDH variant structures were predicted at pH 7.0 using the PROPKA tool on the PDB2PQR web server [63]. MD simulations were conducted for 100 ns under periodic boundary conditions at a pressure of 1 atm across seven temperature settings: 300, 310, 320, 330, 340, 350, and 400 K. Subsequently, the thermostability and conformational changes of each complex were analyzed using RMSD and RMSF.

Denitration of 4‐Nitrophenol (4‐NP) Using Thermostable FDH Variant as an NADH‐Regenerating System

As the conversion of 4‐NP to quinone can be monitored at 400 nm, this denitration of 4‐NP to benzoquinone (BQ) was used to assess the efficiency of NADH cofactor regeneration by the thermostable BsFDH variants. The reaction contained 0.25 μM FDH, 25 μM HadA, 4 μM FAD, 50 µM 4‐NP, 10 μM NAD+, 80 mM formate, and 1 μM C1 in 100 mM HEPES‐NaOH pH 8.0. The reaction was initiated by the addition of NAD+, incubated at 25 °C, and the conversion of 4‐NP to quinone was monitored at 400 nm. The concentration of 4‐NP was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 18.0 mM−1cm−1. FDH activity during the bioconversion reaction was assessed by withdrawing equal enzyme volumes from all reactions for the activity assay.
