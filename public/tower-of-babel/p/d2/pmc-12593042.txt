Investigation of Aggregation-Induced Emission Mechanism of a Styrene Derivative: A Theoretical Unraveling of S1/S0‑Conical Intersection
Authors: Aarzoo, Ram Kinkar Roy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The mechanisms behind the fluorescence quenching of the styrene derivative, 4-dimethylamino-2-benzylidene malonic acid dimethyl ester (BIM) in solution are investigated computationally in the present study. Utilizing a penalty-constrained search algorithm of geometry optimization along with spin-flip long-range corrected time-dependent density functional theory (SF-LC-TDDFT), we examined the energetic and structural changes of the BIM molecule at the minimum energy conical intersection (MECI) between S1 and S0 states. Our results reveal that the S1 excited state approaches the MECI through simultaneous rotation of the α-bond and the pyramidalization of the ethylenic CC bond. Notably, the S1–S0 energy gap at MECIs are 1.83 and 9.26 kJ/mol (nearly degenerate), and the oscillator strength (f) values are 0.000008 au, and 0.00003 au, indicating a nonradiative decay in solution state. Quantitatively, the radiative decay rate (k r ) is found to be 2.44 × 106 s–1, whereas the nonradiative rates via MECIs reach 1011–1012 s–1, underscoring the efficiency of internal conversion. The geometry observed at the MECI, which corresponds to a charge-transfer state, strongly indicates that the BIM molecule operates as a twisted intramolecular charge transfer (TICT) system (as evidenced from the response coefficients of the corresponding frontier orbitals), with quenching facilitated by α-torsional rotation. Our findings delineate a powerful approach to molecular design that effectively manipulates the aggregation effects for enhanced optical emission.

Introduction

Over the decades, organic electroluminescence material has emerged as a vital force in the advancement of science and technology, profoundly enriching the quality of everyday life. In recent decades, its applications have expanded significantly, substantially impacts flexible displays, organic lasers, organic light-emitting diodes, fluorescent chemical and biological sensors, among others. − For these practical applications, it is essential for organic materials to exhibit a high luminescence quantum efficiency in the solid state. While organic molecules with extended π-conjugation can emit vibrant light in solution, they can encounter issues with luminescence quenching due to concentration or aggregation effects. , In 2001, Ben Zhong Tang’s research group introduced the concept of “aggregation-induced emission” (AIE), highlighting a phenomenon where organic molecules display a dramatic increase in luminescence when they come close to each other to form aggregates, which breaks the barrier caused by traditional ACQ (aggregation-caused quenching) molecules. This discovery has paved the way for innovative design strategies in high-efficiency solid-state organic materials, ultimately influencing a range of applications from advanced materials to innovative sensing technologies. ,

A comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying the AIE phenomenon is essential for effectively designing innovative AIE-active molecules and advancing their applications. One of the most widely recognized mechanisms proposed to explain AIE is the restriction of intramolecular motion (RIM). − This mechanism encompasses two key aspects: the restriction of intramolecular rotation (RIR) and the restriction of intramolecular vibration (RIV). Moreover, the restricted access to the twisted intramolecular charge-transfer (TICT) state, which is crucial for enabling AIE in various systems, has also gained considerable attention. − Molecular rotors, typically composed of donor–acceptor (D–A) conjugated fluorophores, play a pivotal role in this process. Upon excitation, these rotors form TICT complexes. The efficiency of fluorescent emission in these systems inversely depends on the free rotation of the molecular rotor. When this rotation is restricted in the high-viscosity environment or aggregate state, nonradiative deactivation of the twisted complex is significantly reduced, resulting in a remarkable enhancement of fluorescence. − A TICT molecule is a prime example of a molecular structure in which a 90° rotation of a molecular rotor results in a configuration that decisively favors internal conversion (IC) over radiative emission.

To thoroughly understand the complex relationship between intramolecular motion and AIE properties, it is crucial to understand the theoretical mechanism behind this AIE phenomenon. In 2013, Li and Blancafort conducted quantum-chemical investigations to analyze the mechanism of the AIE-active compound diphenyldibenzofulvene (DPDBF) using a model known as restricted access to a conical intersection (RACI). Following this, the RACI approach has been applied to simulate the decay of several other AIEgens, including dimethyltetraphenylsilole (DMPTS), phenyleneimide, and tetraphenylethenes (TPEs), showcasing its versatility and impact in the field. − The AIE phenomenon fundamentally depends on controlling the nonradiative decay pathway. In the solution state, the conical intersection (CI) remains easily accessible because it is lower in energy than the Franck–Condon (FC) point. In contrast, in the solid state, access to the CI decreases significantly, as it is higher in energy than the FC point, leading to radiative decay. Quantum-chemical calculations predict a highly distorted structure near the minimum energy conical intersection (MECI). As a result, the RACI mechanism is generally accepted to explain AIE phenomenon in fluorescent molecules in their transition from the solution to the solid state. −

In 2011, Cariati et al. synthesized and reported a prototype compound, i.e., 4-dimethylamino-2-benzylidene malonic acid dimethyl ester, which is less emissive in solution or in the amorphous phase, but it is highly emissive in the crystalline phase. This phenomenon is referred to as crystallization-induced emission (CIE). By using a combination of rigidochromism, thermochromism and NMR spectroscopic studies at varying temperatures, they concluded that the cause of nonradiative relaxation processes in solution was the rotation around the aryl main axis. In the crystalline form, however, this rotation is restricted by crystal packing, which minimizes geometric relaxation and leads to increased radiative decay. In 2016, Wang et al. reported a theoretical study on the phenomenon of fluorescence quenching in solution state, as well as fluorescence enhancement in the crystal state for 4-dimethylamino-2-benzylidene malonic acid dimethyl ester (abbreviated as BIM). Their findings revealed that, in the solution state, the weakening of the ethylenic CC bond following photoexcitation initiates a barrierless relaxation through β-bond rotation. This leads to S1/S0-MECI, responsible for the fluorescence quenching in the solution state. Notably, the results obtained by Wang et al. contradict the experimental findings reported by Cariati et al. (i.e., rotation around the α-bond in solution). However, to gain a comprehensive understanding of the nonradiative decay mechanism associated with BIM in solution, Aarzoo et al. have theoretically demonstrated that in the molecular state the relaxation process of the charge-transferred excited state is facilitated due to rotation of the α-bond. This finding aligns perfectly with the experimental observations of aryl rotation around the α-bond, as illustrated in Figure .

A significant decrease in the oscillator strength (f) value was observed, which approached zero at the optimized geometry in the S1-state. Thus, this specific state, which corresponds to a charge-transfer state, strongly indicates that the BIM molecule operates as a TICT system, undergoing quenching facilitated by α-torsional rotation. Spin-flip long-range corrected time-dependent density functional theory (SF-LC-TDDFT) was employed to conduct a thorough PES scanning at the S1 excited state by systematically varying the α-angle. However, despite rigorous efforts, pinpointing the MECI was not successful. So, the nonradiative mechanism of the BIM molecule in solution environment was explained in an articulate approach, highlighting the pronounced reduction of the f-value in the S1-state as a key indicator of this process.

In the present study, we are resolutely focused on identifying the MECI that lies energetically below the FC energy in the solution state. This pivotal finding, if successful, will unequivocally demonstrate the nonradiative decay behavior of the BIM molecule and strongly support the assertion that the RACI model is fundamental in elucidating the mechanism behind AIE.

Computational Details

Martinez and colleagues introduced a penalty-constrained optimization approach to efficiently locate minimum energy conical intersections (MECIs). This method involves minimizing an objective function defined as 1 Fσ(R)=12[EI(R)+EJ(R)]+σ([EI(R)−EJ(R)]2EI(R)−EJ(R)+α) here, the stability parameter (α) is a fixed value introduced to prevent singularities, and the Lagrange multiplier (σ) penalizes deviations from degeneracy. This effectively drives the energy gap between the electronic states toward zero. By systematically increasing σ during optimization, the algorithm effectively drives the energy difference between the two electronic states toward zero, thereby guiding the system to the conical intersection. A notable advantage of this method is that it does not require the explicit evaluation of nonadiabatic coupling vectors, allowing for the efficient location of MECIs at the SF-TDDFT level.

In our study, we employed this penalty-constrained optimization method − to effectively locate the conical intersection hypersurface between the S0 and S1 states, referred to as the S1/S0 MECI, using the SF-LC-TDDFT for both states. For our calculations, the stability parameter (α) was set to 0.020 au, while the Lagrange multiplier (σ) was initially set at 3.50. The value of σ was systematically doubled whenever the gradient criteria were met; specifically, this occurred when the maximum Cartesian gradient component was below 0.0005 au, and the root-mean-square (rms) gradient was below 0.0003 au However, this adjustment was only made if the energy gap between the required two states was still larger than the threshold value (0.001 hartree) during optimization. The MECI is confirmed when the energy gap falls below 0.001 hartree and the gradient criteria are satisfied. The penalty-constrained optimization method is considered a highly reliable and robust approach for accurately locating MECIs in various molecular systems. This method is especially effective in medium-sized conjugated systems, where excited state degeneracies are crucial for understanding photochemical processes. −

Optimized coordinates of the S1 state and the coordinates at α = 120° along the minimum energy pathway (MEP), where we identified the minimum energy gap (MEG) as a function of α-bond rotation (as provided in the Supporting Information, Table S1), were utilized to calculate the MECI using the penalty-constrained optimization method as discussed above. The S1/S0-MECI electronic structures were generated using range-separated hybrid (RSH) functional (i.e., ωB97XD), − and the basis set used was cc-pVDZ. To address solvent effects, a linear-response conductor-like polarizable continuum model (LR-CPCM) was employed, with a dielectric constant (ε) of 32.61 for the solvent methanol. − All electronic structure calculations were executed using Q-Chem 5.3 version.

Results and Discussion

In a previous study it was observed that the most stable structure of the S1 state of the BIM molecule is achieved when the α-angle is rotated to −86.9°. This specific angle is critically significant and supports our findings, which align with experimental observations indicating that the BIM molecule undergoes a rapid α-angle rotation upon photoexcitation. In addition, our detailed examination of the oscillator strength during de-excitation revealed a marked decrease in oscillator strength, dropping to nearly zero at the α angle of −86.9°. This compelling evidence revealed the BIM molecule as a TICT system, and it strongly suggested that the observed quenching phenomena in the BIM system arise from a significant reduction in emission yield linked to the rotation of the α-angle.

The minimum energy gap (MEG) of 153 kJ/mol was calculated at the α-angle of 120°, located between the PESs of the S0 and S1 states. This demonstrates that we could not locate the MECI using only the α-angle rotation. Even we calculated the PESs of the S0 and S1 states about β-angle rotation. An energy difference of 74.15 kJ/mol was obtained between the S0 and S1 PESs at β = −100°, indicating that there is no CI. To further explore the implications of these findings, we executed a comprehensive 2D scan around α and β angles. We identified 110 optimized points by combining various α and β torsional angles. At the specific coordinates (α, β) = (−60°, −100°), we determined the MEG to be 63.88 kJ/mol between the PESs of the S0 and S1 states. Once again, our results confirmed that the energy variation was not sufficient to claim the presence of a conical intersection.

The above discussion points to the fact that maybe the S1-minimum energy pathway (MEP) crossed the region where the S0 and S1 PES approach the point of CI, but the SF-LC-TDDFT method using constrained geometry optimization by fixing either the α or β torsional angle across a range of 0–180° as well as a 2D-scan using both α and β are unable to demonstrate that. To verify this assumption, here we further carried out penalty-constrained optimization of the S1 state at the SF-LC-TDDFT level. We observed that the S1/S0 MECIs are reached when not only the α-bond is twisted to −74.7° and −120.7° but also pyramidalization of the ethylenic CC bond occurred. The pyramidalization is achieved by torsion of λ angle (as illustrated in Figure and Table ). This phenomenon closely aligns with the behavior observed in the molecules having ethylene and stilbene moieties. , Figure depicts the optimized structures associated with the S1/S0-MECI1 and S1/S0-MECI2 of the BIM molecule. By doing so, the MEG between the S1 and S0 PESs could be narrowed down from 153 kJ/mol (when α is set at 120°) to within 1.83 kJ/mol (at S1/S0-MECI1) and 9.26 kJ/mol (at S1/S0-MECI2), which indicates that the two states are nearly degenerate (as illustrated in Table ).

Radiative and Non-Radiative Decay Rates

To better understand the photophysical behavior of the BIM molecule in solution and to clarify its AIE mechanism, we calculated the radiative (k r ) and nonradiative (k nr MECI) rate constants from the S1-min and the MECIs, respectively. The radiative lifetime (τ) for spontaneous emission was estimated using the Einstein transition probability expression (in atomic units) − 2 τ=c32(Eflu)2f In eq , c is the velocity of light, E flu is the fluorescence energy, and f is the oscillator strength of the S1 → S0 transition. A shorter τ indicates a more efficient radiative process. The corresponding radiative decay rate is given by 3 kr=1τ

Our results show that the BIM molecule exhibits a radiative lifetime (τ) of 410.234 ns, calculated using E flu = 1.66 eV, and f = 0.00205. Consequently, the radiative decay rate (k r ) is found to be 2.44 × 106 s–1. Thus, the relatively long lifetime (higher value of τ) results in a lower k r , indicating nonradiative deactivation from the S1-min to ground state.

Next, we calculated the nonradiative decay rates (k nr MECI) using the Eyring equation 4 knrMECI=kBThexp(−ΔG≠RT) where ΔG‡ is the Gibbs free energy of activation. In this context, the transition from the FC structure to the MECI structure is treated as the rate limiting step for nonradiative decay. The Gibbs free energy was determined as 5 ΔG≠=ΔH≠−TΔS≠=ΔHMECI−ΔHFC−T(ΔSMECI−ΔSFC) In eq , ΔH MECI and ΔH FC are the enthalpies of the S1 state at the MECI and FC structures, respectively, while ΔS MECI and ΔS FC are the corresponding entropies. The temperature (T) was set to 298 K. These thermodynamic quantities were obtained directly from the frequency calculations performed on the BIM molecule using SF-LC-TDDFT method.

We found the k nr MECI values for both the MECIs to be 9.73 × 1010 s–1 for S1/S0-MECI1 and 1.89 × 1012 s–1 for S1/S0-MECI2. These results clearly indicate that the nonradiative decay pathways via MECIs dominate over the radiative decay pathway from the S1-min. The quantitative insight underscores the critical role of CIs in facilitating efficient internal conversion, which is central to understanding the AIE behavior of BIM.

From the perspective of AIE, it was already demonstrated that the nonradiative TICT behavior is substantially suppressed in the presence of molecular aggregation, resulting in the AIE phenomenon. By adopting a rational approach and avoiding the computationally expensive calculations of the crystalline state, we successfully demonstrated that even a minimal rotation of the α bondapproximately 10° or lesscan significantly inhibit the occurrence of TICT within the crystal structure. Consequently, the AIE behavior of BIM system also aligns with the RACI model. Our compelling findings in the present study provide convincing explanations for the photochemistry and photophysics of BIM in solution state while offering valuable insights into its AIE behavior. These results unequivocally underscore the critical role of the CI seam in photoinduced processes involving styrene derivatives.

Conclusions

In this study, we employed the penalty-constrained search algorithm to unveil the CI between the PESs of the S1 and S0 states. This algorithm has been effectively utilized in earlier investigations, establishing itself as a reliable method for identifying the S1/S0-MECI. − ,, At the same time, our earlier work did not yield the MECI through individual rotation of either α or β bond in the excited state. Additionally, an extensive 2D scan around the α and β bonds, although reduced the energy gap between the PESs of S1 and S0, the reduction was not sufficient enough to be classified as a CI. Using the penalty-constrained search algorithm, in the present study we have effectively located two MECIsS1/S0-MECI1 and S1/S0-MECI2with energy gaps of 1.83 and 9.26 kJ/mol, respectively, along with f-values of 0.000008 au and 0.00003 au. Our results reveal that the S1 excited state approaches the MECI through the combined effect of rotation of the α-bond and the pyramidalization of the ethylenic CC bond. This compelling finding demonstrates that the BIM molecule transitions from S1 to the S0 state via the S1/S0-MECI, forming a TICT system, which results in fluorescence quenching, thereby enriching our understanding of the molecular dynamics involved.

Furthermore, our calculated radiative and nonradiative decay rates provide quantitative support for this quenching mechanism. The BIM molecule exhibits a radiative lifetime (τ) of 410.234 ns (corresponding to k r = 2.44 × 106 s–1). In contrast, the nonradiative decay rates via MECIs are significantly higher, with k nr MECI = 9.73 × 1010 s–1 for S1/S0-MECI1 and 1.89 × 1012 s–1 for S1/S0-MECI2. These results clearly indicate that the k nr MECIdominates overk r , consolidating the role of CIs in governing the AIE behavior and fluorescence quenching of BIM in solution.

The authors believe that the present work will significantly enhance the understanding of AIE and contribute to the innovative design of new luminescent materials with AIE characteristics.
