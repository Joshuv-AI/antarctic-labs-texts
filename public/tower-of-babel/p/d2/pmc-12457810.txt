Calcium and Phosphorus Intake in Very Low Birth Weight Preterm Infants: Implications for Osteopenia Prevention and Growth
Authors: Piyarat Kajohntridach, Premsak Laoyookhong, Tippawan Siritientong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeSufficient calcium and phosphorus supplementation in preterm infants may ensure proper bone growth and reduce the risk of osteopenia. This study aimed to assess—in comparison to controls—the calcium and phosphorus intake via both enteral and parenteral routes, as well as growth, of preterm infants with very low birth weight (VLBW) who were diagnosed with osteopenia. Assessment took place during the first 28 days of life; infants were followed throughout hospitalization.MethodsWe conducted a single-center, retrospective study of preterm infants weighing <1,500 g who were admitted to a tertiary healthcare center between January 1, 2017 and June 30, 2022.ResultsThe study included 55 infants with osteopenia (case group) and 220 control infants (control group). The average weekly calcium intake in the case group during the first 4 weeks after birth was significantly lower than that of the control group, with values of 1.45, 1.98, 3.06, and 4.02 mEq/kg/day versus 1.64, 3.10, 4.89, and 5.69 mEq/kg/day, respectively (p<0.05). Similarly, phosphorus intake was significantly lower in the case group (0.75, 1.24, 1.29, and 1.45 mmoL/kg/day) compared to the control group (0.75, 1.38, 1.62, and 1.79 mmoL/kg/day; p<0.05). Preterm infants with VLBW and osteopenia exhibited lower average daily weight gain (19.03 g vs. 21.45 g; p<0.001) and a smaller gain in head circumference (0.57 cm vs. 0.68 cm; p<0.001) compared to controls.ConclusionThis findings highlight the necessity for timely provision of calcium and phosphorus to prevent osteopenia and promote optimal growth in this vulnerable population.

INTRODUCTION

Preterm infants weighing <1,500 g are at a high risk of developing osteopenia, with the incidence ranging from 16% to 40%; potentially, this rate can increase to >50% for infants weighing <1,000 g [12]. The third trimester is a crucial period for bone mineralization, in which up to 80% of calcium and phosphorus accumulate in the bones [34]. If the infant does not receive sufficient minerals from the mother, there may be inadequate mineral accumulation in the body for proper bone mineralization. Therefore, providing immediate postnatal nutrition care to preterm infants and those with very low birth weight (VLBW) is necessary to ensure optimal growth rates that are comparable to those of a mother’s fetus and to prevent metabolic bone disease [5].

Adequate calcium and phosphorus should be provided to preterm infants with VLBW. Unfortunately, preterm infants often abstain from feeding for a prolonged time during hospitalization due to severe gastrointestinal complications, and their underdeveloped digestive systems impede proper absorption of critical nutrients, including calcium and phosphorus. Moreover, fluid restriction in infants with major organ failure further compromises the intake of these essential minerals [6]. As a result, insufficiencies in the intake of minerals persists in many preterm infants. This study aimed to determine the calcium and phosphorus intake—via both enteral and parenteral routes, during the first 28 days of life—of preterm infants with VLBW who were diagnosed with osteopenia and compare them to those of a control group. The serum laboratory profiles and growth information of the infants were also followed throughout the period of hospitalization.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This study used a case–control design and was conducted at the Queen Sirikit National Institute of Child Health, Bangkok, Thailand. This tertiary care facility with 435 beds delivers specialized clinical care and referral services for children with complex diseases. Ethical approval for the study protocol was obtained from the Human Ethics Committee (certificate number REC.109/2565, dated October 17, 2022). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and anonymous nature of the data reported.

We included infants (i) with a gestational age of <37 weeks and birth weight of <1,500 g, and (ii) admitted to the neonatal intensive care or neonatal ward for at least 4 weeks. We excluded infants (i) referred from other hospitals at day of life 2 or over, and (ii) with congenital diseases, or chromosomal or metabolic anomalies.

We retrospectively reviewed the medical and administration records of infants prescribed enteral and parenteral nutrition between January 1, 2017 and June 30, 2022. Eligible participants were randomly selected using a systematic sampling method. A detailed description of participant screening and selection is provided elsewhere [7]. The recruitment flowchart is depicted in Fig. 1. We determined that a 1 to 4 ratio of case to control would result in the greatest statistical testing power to conclude [8].

Flowchart showing infant recruitment. Adapted from Kajohntridach et al. (Thai Journal of Pharmacy Practice 2024;16:826-42) with permission [7].

The case group comprised eligible infants documented in the medical records as diagnosed with osteopenia according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) code E83.3. In our setting, osteopenia is normally diagnosed at 1–2 months after birth using laboratory blood tests as alkaline phosphatase (ALP)—a bone turnover marker—levels greater than 500 units/L [910] with phosphorus levels of less than 3.5 mg/dL [11]. This method is a noninvasive procedure with acceptable sensitivity and specificity compared to dual energy X-ray absorptiometry measurements [12]. The control group comprised preterm infants with VLBW, but without documented osteopenia.

The optimal mineral intakes were determined based on the evidence-based international guidelines provided by the European Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) [5]. The guideline recommends that calcium intake should be 0.8–2.0 mmoL/kg/day (1.6–4.0 mEq/kg/day) for preterm infants on the first day after birth, increasing to 1.6–3.5 mmoL/kg/day (3.2–7.0 mEq/kg/day) thereafter. As per ESPGHAN 2018 recommendations, preterm infants should receive 1–2 mmoL/kg/day of phosphorus on the first day after birth, increasing to 1.6–3.5 mmoL/kg/day thereafter.

Data collection

Data were collected using structured case record forms. For the enteral route, we calculated the corresponding calcium and phosphorus contents of all types of enteral feeding, including breast milk, human milk fortifier, preterm formula, and infant formula. For the parenteral route, calcium was exclusively administered as an injection of 10% calcium gluconate (0.45 mEq/mL), whereas phosphorus was administered as either dipotassium phosphate (K2HPO4; 0.5 mmoL/mL) or sodium glycerophosphate (NaGP; 1 mmoL/mL). When using K2HPO4—an inorganic phosphate with a high risk of precipitation with calcium—either the amount of calcium mixed in the same bag was limited or calcium and phosphorus were administered separately. This was particularly important, despite the high calcium and phosphorus requirements of the preterm infants. However, if NaGP—an organic phosphate—was used, the risk of precipitation with calcium was lower [13], allowing both to be mixed in the same bag, and the infants’ mineral requirements to be achieved.

We also recorded any additional supplementation of calcium and phosphorus that the infants received enterally and intravenously. Infants’ body weights and lengths, measured using an infant scale; head circumference, measured using a nonelastic tape; as well as serum levels of calcium, phosphorus, and ALP throughout weeks 1–8 were retrieved from medical records. The lengths of hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) stays, as well as the number of days of ventilation were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using STATA version 14.0 software (Stata Co.). Categorical data were compared between groups using either the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Quantitative data were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Quantitative data with a normal distribution are presented as the mean and standard deviation; they were compared between groups using an independent t-test. Skewed data are presented as the median and interquartile range; the Mann–Whitney U-test was used for group comparisons. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Characteristics of preterm infants

Overall, 275 participants had a median birth weight of 1,240 g (1,030–1,360 g; Table 1). The case group comprised 55 infants, and the control group 220 infants, with a median gestational age of 200 (189–212) days and 210 (202–217) days, respectively. The median peak ALP levels were significantly higher in the case group (729 U/L) compared to the control group (379 U/L; p<0.001). The median nadir phosphorus levels were significantly lower in the case group (3.13 mg/dL) compared to the control group (4.14 mg/dL; p<0.001). The ALP and phosphorus levels of the control group were within the normal range, consistent with the diagnostic criteria for an absence of osteopenia.

Mineral intake of preterm infants with VLBW

Amount of calcium and phosphorus via the parenteral route

All infants received parenteral nutrition from the first day of life. The total amount of calcium and phosphorus received intravenously included both compounding parenteral nutrition and additional supplementation in an injectable form. Among infants in the case group receiving phosphorus supplementation, 25 (45.5%) and 30 (54.5%) received NaGP and K2HPO4, respectively. Among infants in the control group, 75 (34.1%) and 145 (65.9%) received NaGP and K2HPO4, respectively. Infants in the case group received a significantly higher amount of intravenous calcium than those in the control group during the first 4 weeks after birth, specifically, when the infants were 11 days old (Fig. 2A). Similarly, the amount of phosphorus received intravenously was significantly higher in the case group compared to the control group when the infants were 9 days old (Fig. 2B).

Amount of calcium and phosphorus via the enteral route

Preterm infants with VLBW in the case group started enteral nutrition later than those in the control group. Infants in the case group received their first feeding at a median age of 4 days, whereas those in the control group received theirs at a median age of 3 days (p<0.001). Both groups orally consumed different types of milk (Table 2). The calcium and phosphorus the infants received through the enteral route came from enteral feeding and additional oral supplements. Calcium was supplemented using a 600 mg calcium carbonate tablet containing 240 mg of elemental calcium; phosphorus was administered as Joulie’s solution at a concentration of 1 mmoL/mL (Fig. 2C, D).

The study revealed that, during the first 28 days of life, the enteral intake of calcium and phosphorus in preterm infants with VLBW in the case group was noticeably lower than that of infants in the control group from when the infants were 3 days old. This observation aligns with the information shown in Fig. 2, namely, that infants in the case group relied on parenteral nutrition for a longer period than those in the control group, resulting in reduced nutrient intake through the gastrointestinal tract.

Total amount of calcium and phosphorus consumed by the infants

This study included the calcium and phosphorus intake from parenteral nutrition, enteral nutrition, and supplements in both injectable and oral forms. The results revealed that the average weekly calcium intake in the case group during the first, second, third, and fourth weeks after birth was 1.45, 1.98, 3.06, and 4.02 mEq/kg/day, respectively, and significantly lower than that in the control group, which was 1.64, 3.10, 4.89, and 5.69 mEq/kg/day. The recommended calcium intake was reached later in infants in the case group than in those in the control group, on days 19 and 11 after birth, respectively (Fig. 3A).

Preterm infants with VLBW in the case group received average phosphorus intakes of 0.75, 1.24, 1.29, and 1.45 mmoL/kg/day, respectively, whereas infants in the control group received average phosphorus intakes of 0.75, 1.38, 1.62, and 1.79 mmoL/kg/day. The recommended phosphorus intake was not reached in infants in the case group during the first 4 weeks after birth, whereas it was reached in infants in the control group by day 17 (Fig. 3B). Overall, during the first 28 days of life, the average daily intake of phosphorus was 1.18 mmoL/kg and 1.38 mmoL/kg, but the daily calcium intake was 2.63 mEq/kg and 3.82 mEq/kg in the case and control groups, respectively. The recommended daily intake of calcium (3.2–7 mEq/kg/day) was reached in a total of 154 (56.0%) infants—15 from the case group and 139 from the control group—during the 28-day period. However, the recommended intake for phosphorus (1.6–3.5 mmoL/kg/day) was only reached in 72 (26.2%) preterm infants with VLBW: 9 from the case and 63 from the control groups.

Growth and laboratory values

This study revealed that infants in the case group had a daily average weight gain of 19.03 g, which was significantly lower than that of the control group (21.45 g). However, when adjusted by birth weight, the weight gains between the groups were not significantly different (p=0.175; Table 3), indicating that the growth rate relative to body size was comparable between the groups.

Infants in the case group also exhibited a lower weekly increase in head circumference compared to those in the control group, with increases noted of 0.57 cm and 0.68 cm, respectively (p<0.001). The weekly increase in the length of preterm infants was 0.91 cm in the case group and 0.94 cm in the control group, though this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.439).

According to the laboratory results, infants in the case group had lower serum calcium and phosphorus levels compared to the control group. Moreover, the case group had statistically significantly higher levels of ALP than the control group (p<0.001). For both groups, the average levels of ALP were higher in weeks 5-8 compared to weeks 1–4. The case group had average ALP levels of >500 U/L in weeks 5–8 (Table 3).

Lengths of hospital and ICU stays, and ventilation days

Infants in the case group had significantly longer hospital and ICU stays, and a greater number of ventilation days compared to the control group (Fig. 4). The median hospital stay was 72 (57–92) days for the case group, significantly longer than the 46 (38–58) days observed in the control group (p<0.001). Similarly, the duration of ICU stay was significantly longer in the case group, with a median of 21 (3–38) days compared to the 2 (0–10.75) days in the control group (p<0.001). The number of ventilation days also showed a marked difference, with the case group requiring a median of 44 (28–64) days of ventilation, compared to the 14.5 (7–36) days in the control group (p<0.001).

DISCUSSION

The neonatal care and treatment provided in the delivery room in this study setting were standardized across both the case and control groups, ensuring that all infants received the same level of care. After birth, all preterm infants with VLBW primarily received parenteral nutrition, provided no contraindication was present, as recommended by the international neonatal guidelines [1415]. This nutrition is crucial for proper growth and prevention of various complications [16].

Enteral feeding helps prevent atrophy of the intestinal mucosa and reduces the risk of feeding intolerance, necrotizing enterocolitis, osteopenia, and complications arising from prolonged intravenous feeding in infants [1718]. Standardized feeding regimens are implemented at the Queen Sirikit National Institute of Child Health. Enteral feeding is initiated as early as possible by starting breast milk if available and appropriate; otherwise, pasteurized donor breast milk is considered. If neither is available, a preterm formula designed to meet the nutritional needs of preterm infants may be considered. Infants are given minimal enteral nutrition, or trophic feeding, of between 10–24 mL/kg/day of milk every 3 hours, starting as early as 48 to 72 hours after birth. If the infant tolerates the milk well and shows stable symptoms, the volume is gradually increased to reach the requirement.

The findings of the present study indicated that infants with VLBW in the case group relied on parenteral nutrition for a longer period than those in the control group. The infants in the case group also received lower average amounts of calcium and phosphorus and reached the recommended target levels of these nutrients at a slower rate. Previous studies reported a relationship between osteopenia in preterm infants and inadequate calcium and phosphorus intake from parenteral feeding [1920]. Early initiation of parenteral nutrition with calcium and phosphorus has been shown to play a significant role in preserving bone strength among preterm infants during the first weeks of life. Preterm infants who were started on an infusion of phosphorus later than day 4 of life had a seven times higher risk of developing osteopenia [7]. In a randomized controlled trial conducted by Pereira-da-Silva et al. [19], 40 preterm infants received a low dose of calcium (45 mg/kg/day) while 46 infants received a high dose (75 mg/kg/day). Phosphorus was added to the parenteral nutrition at a calcium to phosphorus ratio of 1.7 to 1. After 6 weeks, the high-dose group had significantly higher bone strength than the low-calcium group. Motokura et al. [20] conducted a retrospective study examining 22 infants who received low doses of phosphorus (15–31 mg/kg/day) and 26 infants who received high doses of phosphorus (22–46 mg/kg/day) along with calcium (40–64 mg/kg/day). The study revealed that infants who received low doses of phosphorus had higher levels of ALP in their blood (p=0.014) and were more likely to exhibit bone changes on radiographs (p=0.141).

In the current study, infants with VLBW in the case group had shorter gestational periods, lower birth weights, and more comorbidities than those in the control group, which resulted in limitations in their care, including fluid intake. Consequently, high susceptibility of minerals precipitation in parenteral nutrition occurred, resulting in low, slow attainment of the recommended doses of calcium and phosphorus; thus, infants may have received insufficient calcium and phosphorus for proper bone development, which could contribute to osteopenia over time [7].

Regarding infant growth, the present study showed that the average daily weight gain was lower in the case group (19.03 g) than in the control group (21.45 g). The recommended daily weight gain for infants weighing between 500 and 1,000 g is 19 g/kg/day, whereas those weighing between 1,000 and 1,500 g should gain 17.4 g/kg/day. The weight gained by all infants in the present study was within the recommended range. Previous studies tracking preterm infants weighing <1,000–1,500 g up to 18–22 months of age have shown that, on average, they gained more weight per day during their hospital stay, resulting in a lower incidence of severe neurodevelopmental impairment and cerebral palsy [2122]. Pieltain et al. [23] reported on the temporal changes in body weight among preterm infants with a birth weight of <1,750 g, comparing those fed fortified human milk to those fed preterm formula. Infants in the formula-fed group exhibited a significantly higher weight gain than those in the fortified milk group (average 19.9 g/kg/day vs. 15.9 g/kg/day, respectively; p<0.05) during the first weeks of life. In our study, the median weight gain was not significantly different between the groups (18.73 g/kg/day vs. 17.41 g/kg/day), suggesting that relative to their body size, infants in both groups exhibited comparable growth rates.

A key limitation of the current study was its retrospective design, which may have introduced biases and confounding variables that were not adequately controlled for, potentially affecting the validity of the findings. The lack of detailed data on energy and protein intake introduced uncertainty regarding its impact on the outcomes. Despite these challenges, the study offers significant strengths, including a focused examination of preterm infants with VLBW, which provides valuable insights into this vulnerable population at risk for osteopenia. Overall, the findings confirm the critical importance of adequate mineral provision in preventing osteopenia, emphasizing the need for continued research in this area.

CONCLUSION

In summary, ensuring sufficient intake of calcium and phosphorous is crucial for preventing osteopenia in preterm infants with VLBW. Clinicians should prioritize early nutritional assessment and intervention, particularly for infants with feeding difficulties or underlying conditions that may impair nutrient absorption. Routine monitoring of bone mineral status may help identify early signs of bone weakness, enabling timely interventions that promote bone development.
