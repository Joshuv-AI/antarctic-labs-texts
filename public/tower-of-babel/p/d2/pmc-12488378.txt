Biological Control of Root-Knot Nematodes, Meloidogyne javanica by Antagonistic Fungus Cladosporium sphaerospermum
Authors: Mostafa A El-Qurashi, Ali A Almasrahi, Fahad A Al-Yahya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoot-knot nematodes (RKNs, Meloidogyne spp.) are significant threats to global agriculture because their host range is broad, and therefore, their adverse effects on crop productivity are substantial. Cladosporium sphaerospermum PQ394940 was isolated from egg masses of RKNs infecting eggplant and examined for its ability as a bioagent against the M. javanica. In an in vitro assay, the fungus effectively inhibited egg hatch (95.3%) and increased the mortality percent of second-stage juveniles (J2s, 73.3%) through direct parasitism. Moreover, after observation, the fungus showed a capacity for paralyzing eggs and juveniles under a compound microscope. Fungal culture filtrate (CF) has been examined against egg hatch and J2s mortality. Data revealed that CF can increase J2s mortality percent and suppress egg hatching. In the seed germination test, C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 significantly increased eggplant seed germination compared with control treatment. Fungus has been identified by amplifying ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS-rDNA) using ITS4 and ITS5 primer pairs. A fragment with 570–580 bp was amplified and the result of rDNA sequencing was recorded in GenBank (PQ394940). Under greenhouse conditions, C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 significantly promoted eggplant growth. Moreover, fungus significantly reduced M. javanica reproduction and root galling. Additionally, fungus significantly reduced the root gall index, egg mass index, and reproductive factor. Thus, C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 is considered larvicidal and ovicidal, can also be added to the soil as a bio-fertilizer. As highlighted in this study, C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 will be useful in integrated pest management, improving crop productivity, and decreasing environmental impact.Keywords: biocontrol, egg mass index, parasitism, reproductive factor, root gall index

Plant-parasitic nematodes (PPNs) seriously threaten agricultural productivity, as they have a wide range of hosts and can establish quickly and cause considerable crop losses. Globally, Sasser and Freckman (1987) estimated crop losses due to PPNs by 8.8–14.6%. However, in 2020, losses caused by PPNs were estimated to have risen to 21.3% of total agricultural production (Kumar et al., 2020).

The root-knot nematodes (RKNs), Meloidogyne spp., are the most destructive, causing loss in vegetable crops worldwide due to their distribution and broad host range (Sasser and Freckman, 1987). Meloidogyne spp. are collectively referred to as RKNs due to their characteristic root galling symptoms. More than 90 species of RKNs have been described, exhibiting reproductive modes that vary from obligatory mitotic parthenogenesis to amphimixis (Chitwood and Perry, 2009; Moens et al., 2009). Four major species of RKNs are widespread globally. They are highly polyphagous and bred on thousands of cultivated and wild plants (De Guiran and Netscher, 1970). The mitotic species include M. incognita, M. arenaria, and M. javanica, while one meiotic species is M. hapla.

Severe economic losses have been attributed to detecting RKNs in all horticultural-producing areas of Saudi Arabia (Mohamed et al., 2023). M. javanica was the most prominent species infecting the most cultivated plants, followed by M. incognita, and M. arenaria, which were found to be mostly associated with trees and shrubs (Al-Hazmi et al., 2009; Mohamed et al., 2023; Mokbel, 2014). Root-lesions, cyst nematode, and RKNs were responsible for more than 50% crop losses out of the total losses caused by PPNs (El-Qurashi et al., 2023; Quintanilla and Yazdani Fazlabadi, 2023).

PPNs have traditionally been controlled using soil fumigants and non-fumigant nematicides (Talavera et al., 2012). However, these chemical-reliant practices are under rising scrutiny in light of their harmful effects, including contamination of water resources, negative consequences for non-target organisms, and risks to human health.

Biological control agents (BCAs) are used as an alternative to chemical pesticides (El-Qurashi and Al-Yahya, 2025). However, BCAs continue to encounter challenges like inconsistent performance, difficulty in implementation, and limited evidence of their aggregate effectiveness in the field. One possible practice that might improve BCAs performance would be the application of organic soil amendments. These amendments supply vital micronutrients that enhance soil fertility and establish beneficial environments for biocontrol agents (Abd-Elgawad and Askary, 2018).

The genus Cladosporium is one group of BCAs that is receiving attention. Cladosporium spp. are widespread saprophytic fungi that can develop various strategies to occupy ecological niches in different biomes. C. sphaerospermum, in particular, has been isolated from various environments, including the air, organic matter, food, and plants (Park et al., 2004; Pereira et al., 2002). The production of melanin, siderophores, and additional secondary metabolites that bolster survival in hostile environments is associated with their ecological spread and environmental viability (Yew et al., 2016). Previous studies showed the potential of Cladosporium spp. as a biocontrol agent against multiple diseases and pests. C. tenuissimum and C. sphaerospermum, for example, have been described as decreasing Meloidogyne spp. through parasitism of their egg masses and interference with their life cycle (Gin et al., 2013, 2016).

This study investigates C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 as a candidate for the biocontrol of RKN (M. javanica). Egg masses of M. javanica and C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 were isolated from infected eggplant plants. Through morphological and molecular identification techniques, the antagonistic fungus was identified. It was further tested in vitro against juvenile mortality and egg hatching of the M. javanica. We also explored the fungus’s ability to enhance plant growth. Under greenhouse conditions, the ability of bioagent fungus to control nematodes and promote plant growth was examined.

Materials and Methods

RKN source

Eggplant root samples were collected from infected plants in Riyadh Region, KSA and kept on eggplants grown in the greenhouse of the Nematology lab, King Saud University, and used in this study. The pure culture of M. javanica was identified before using morphological features depending on perineal pattern (El-Qurashi et al., 2017). Moreover, two species-specific primer pairs (Meng et al., 2004; Zijlstra, 2000) were used for molecular identification and published previously (El-Qurashi et al., 2025).

Fungal isolation and identification

Fungal isolation

A fungal isolate was obtained from RKN egg masses on infected eggplant roots collected in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Roots were washed with tap water and sterilized with 0.5% sodium hypochlorite for 2 min. Egg masses of RKNs were collected by antiseptic forceps and transferred to a Petri plate containing sterilized distilled water (SDW). Then egg masses were moved to potato dextrose agar (PDA; Condalab, Laboratories Conda S.A., Madrid, Spain) medium and kept at room temperature (27 ± 2°C) for 3–5 days (3 egg masses per plate). A small disk of each growing fungal colony was transferred to a new Petri dishes containing water agar (WA) medium and kept for 5 days at room temperature. The purified fungal isolate was sub-cultured on PDA slant and kept in the refrigerator (4°C) till used.

Morphological characterization

Fungal spores and hyphae were stained with cotton blue dye and permanent slides were prepared. Slides were examined under a compound microscope equipped with a Nikon Digital Sight DS-5M-L1 camera (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) to acquire images. Morphological identification relied on the shape of spores and mycelia, colony shape, and color on PDA following the standard mycological monographs outlined by Sutton (1981) and Nag Raj (1993).

Molecular characterization

DNA extraction

For DNA extraction, the fungal isolate was grown in Czapek culture broth (supplemented by 1% glucose and peptone) for 7 days at room temperature with agitation (120 rpm) to obtain a rich mycelial culture (Hamayun et al., 2009). A quantity of 0.5 g of air-dried mycelia was transferred to a 2 mL Eppendorf tube and broken carefully with a plastic rod. UltraClean Microbial DNA Isolation Kit (Mo Bio Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used for DNA extraction.

PCR performance and sequencing

Molecular characterization of isolated fungal strain was based on sequencing the internal transcribed region (ITS-rDNA) using universal primers ITS4 (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′) and ITS5 (5′-GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3′) as described by White et al. (1990). PCR mixture (25 μL) contained 5 μL of 5× Firepol master mix (Soils BioDyne, Tartu, Estonia), 1 μL of DNA, and 1 μL of each primer, rest volume was adjusted with 17 μL of autoclaved distilled water. The PCR cycle consisted of 4 min initial denaturation at 95°C, followed by 35 cycles of 45 s denaturation time (95°C), 45 s of annealing (55°C), and 45 s extension (72°C), then the final extension (72°C) for 10 min.

The PCR product was examined using 1.5% agarose gel. The positive sample was purified and sequenced at the Macrogen sequencing facility (Macrogen, Seoul, Korea). DNA sequence result was cleaned and edited using BioEdit 7.2 software (https://getwinpcsoft.com/BioEdit-208576). The final DNA sequence was searched on BLAST website (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi?) to look for nucleotide sequence homology for ITS region for fungi. The sequences obtained were aligned by Clustal W and the neighbor-joining tree was generated using MEGA version 11.0.13 software (Tamura et al., 2007).

Effect of fungal culture filtrate on egg hatch and J2s mortality

Fungal culture filtrate (CF) was prepared following the methods outlined by El-Qurashi et al. (2019); Subhash et al. (2022). Strain PQ394940 was sub-cultured on PDA plates and incubated at 27 ± 2°C. Seven days later, a 0.5 cm fungal disc (in diameter) was transferred to 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 100 mL of potato dextrose broth medium. The flask was then incubated at room temperature (27 ± 2°C) for 15 days with shaking (120 rpm). The CF was obtained by centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 15 min followed by filtration of the cell-free extract using Whatman filter paper (Hyclone Life Sciences, Bengaluru, India). The obtained CF was further passed through a 0.22 μm Millipore filter (Millex-GP, Millipore, Ireland) and kept at 4°C for further experiments.

Ten milliliters of CF were transferred to Petri plates (9 cm). A volume of 1.0 mL of fresh hatched M. javanica’s J2s (100) or eggs (1,000) was added to the plates. SDW was added to adjust the concentrations of CF to 75 and 50%. Each treatment was replicated three times, and the experiment was designated following CRD (Durner, 2021). Plates were kept at room temperature (27 ± 2°C). Eggs and J2s added to SDW only served as a control. After 3 days of incubation, survival and dead J2s were counted, and the mortality percentage was determined following the previous formula. On the other hand, the percentage of egg hatch inhibition was determined after 10 days of incubation.

Greenhouse experiment

Fungal inoculum preparation

C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 was sub-cultured on PDA plates and incubated at 27 ± 2°C. Seven days later, two discs (0.5 cm in diameter) were transferred to a 500 mL flask containing barley sand medium (100 g of barley grains, 20 g of sand, and 100 mL of SDW) as described by Sallam Nashwa et al. (2014). C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 was inoculated in three flasks. Fifteen days after incubation at room temperature, medium was used to inoculate the soil. Pots were filled with 1,500 mL sterilized soil (sand and peat moss at a ratio of 1:1 (v/v)), soil was sterilized previously by formalin (2%) for 10 days (Warcup, 1951). The inoculum was added at 3% and pots were kept watered under greenhouse conditions. After 7 days, eggplant seedlings (cv. Black Beauty) were transferred to pots (1 per each), watered, and fertilized as needed.

Nematode inoculum preparation

Eggplants infected with M. javanica (identified previously by perineal pattern and two species-specific primer pairs) were uprooted and washed with tap water. Then plant roots were sterilized by sodium hypochlorite (1.0%) for 1 min. Consequently, egg masses were picked up and transferred to Petri dishes containing SDW using antiseptic forceps and kept at room temperature for 5 days. Fresh hatch J2s were collected and adjusted to 1,000 J2s per 1.0 mL.

Greenhouse experimental design

Under greenhouse conditions, 25-day-old eggplant seedlings were transplanted to pots containing bioagent. Seven days later, nematode inoculum in 1.5 mL (containing 1,500 fresh hatch M. javanica J2s) was added in three holes around the plant root in five pots (El-Qurashi et al., 2025). Another five pots were inoculated with bioagent only served as bioagent control. The non-inoculated plants (neither nematode nor bioagent) served as the negative control (non-infected), while plants inoculated only with nematodes served as the positive control (infected) for nematode measurements. Each treatment was repeated 5 times. The experiment was designated following a randomized complete block design in vivo at 30 ± 2°C.

Plant growth and nematode assessment

Sixty days later, the experiment was terminated, and plants were uprooted and sent to the nematology lab for plant and nematode measurements. Plants were washed with tap water and dried on clean paper. Shoot and root fresh weight were determined. Shoots were then dried in an oven at 70°C for 5 days (Chaimala et al., 2020), and shoot dry weight was measured.

Roots were cut down to 0.5 cm, and 5 g were used to extract eggs using 1.0 % NaOCl for 5 min (Hussey and Barker, 1973). Using the nematode count slide, the number of eggs was determined under a compound microscope (Nikon YS100). Another random 3 g root was used for gall number detection, and also 3 g was used for egg masses counted after being stained with Phloxin B (0.15 g/L) as described by Holbrook et al. (1983). On the other hand, the soil was mixed and 250 mL from each pot was measured and used for nematode extraction. Nematodes were extracted by flotation and centrifugation techniques as described by Ayoub (1977). Quantification of bioagent fungal colonies in soil

This experiment was conducted after 7 and 60 days of bioagent inoculation. One gram of soil was sampled from each pot, and the number of fungal colony-forming units (CFUs) was determined. Soil (1.0 g) was added to a test tube containing 10.0 mL of SDW and shaken at 120 rpm for 20 min. Then 1.0 mL was transferred to a new test tube containing 9.0 mL of SDW and mitigated for 10 min at 120 rpm. This serial dilution process was repeated up to tube 5. One hundred microliters of dilutions 3, 4, and 5 was spread on a rose-bengal chloramphenicol agar base media (CM0549, Oxoid Ltd., Hampshire, England) and incubated at room temperature (27 ± 2°C). Each dilution was repeated 3 times. Three days after incubation, plates were examined and colonies of the bioagent were counted. Statistical analysis

All collected data were analyzed using the Statistix 8 program, version 8.1 (Statistix Analytical Software, 2003). The multi-comparison between means of treatments was performed using the LSD. The standard error of the antagonistic, seed germination, and greenhouse examination was calculated using Statistix 8 program, version 8.1 (Statistix Analytical Software, 2003).

Results

Fungal isolation and identification

Fungus isolated from egg masses of RKNs was identified morphologically by the shape of spores and hypha. Also, the culture characters on PDA referred to the Cladosporium spp. (Fig. 1). The colonies grow slowly, mostly grey to blackish brown. Colonies became powdery due to the production of abundant conidia. Conidiophores were straight, unbranched, or branched in the apical region. Conidia were produced in branched acropetal chains, mostly one to two cells. Molecular identification of fungus was carried out by amplifying the ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS-rDNA) region. BLAST search results of the PCR product sequence identified our isolate as C. sphaerospermum (GenBank accession no. PQ394940). Phylogenetic analysis with type-derived sequence revealed that our isolate is C. sphaerospermum (Fig. 2) with 100% identity.

Morphological characteristics of Cladosporium spp. (A) Conidiophores and conidia. (B) Conidia. (C) Fungal culture shape on potato dextrose agar medium.

Phylogenetic tree derived from internal transcribed spacer sequences of Cladosporium species based on a maximum likelihood analysis with 1,000 bootstrap replications.

Phylogenetic tree showed the relationship among C. sphaerospermum isolates constructed by sequence alignment. Notably, isolate C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 was closely clustered with C. sphaerospermum MK118899.1 and C. sphaerospermum PP464188.1 (bootstrap value of 92%) (Fig. 2), the latter of which was confident in the grouping. Phylogeny of C. sphaerospermum isolates demonstrated multiple clades with bootstrap values ≥ 90%, suggesting the diversity of evolutionary relationships among the isolates. As part of this phylogenetic cluster, PQ394940 likely had a similar evolutionary history to the rest of the taxa in this branch. The branch that encompasses PQ394940 was well-supported, with a bootstrap value of 100%, highlighting a solid phylogenetic bond among the incorporated isolates. This study showed the genetic similarity of strain PQ394940 with other known C. sphaerospermum isolates and provides a basis for more specific research on its pathogenicity, ecology, or resistance traits in hydroponic and soilless systems.

In vitro parasitism assay

C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 parasitism was evaluated against egg hatch and mortality of juveniles of M. javanica in vitro. Table 1 shows that the fungus significantly inhibited egg hatching (95.3%) compared to the control (6.4%) (P ≤ 0.05). Furthermore, the mortality percent of J2s was highest when treated with strain PQ394940 spores and mycelia with an average of 73.3% compared with control (4.8%).

Fungal CF assay

An in vitro assay was conducted using fungal CF at two concentrations (50% and 75%) to evaluate their effects on egg hatching and J2 mortality rates. Results in Table 2 showed that CF has the ability to suppress egg hatch with an average of 93.8% compared to control (35.1%). Overall, non-significant difference was found between the average of the two concentrations (P ≤ 0.05) on hatch inhibition percent. Regarding J2s mortality percent, the average mortality percent of J2s was 65% in the case of strain PQ394940 compared to control (1.6%). Generally, a non-significant difference was found between the average of both concentrations (P ≤ 0.05) in J2s mortality percentage.

Greenhouse experimental results

This experiment was conducted under greenhouse conditions. After 60 days, the experiment was terminated, and data on plant shoot and root weight was determined. Overall, C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 significantly promoted plant growth via shoot fresh and dry weight, and increased root fresh weight. In the case of root fresh weight (Table 3), C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 increased the weight of roots with an average of 46.1 g compared with the control (43.1 g). However, there are no significant differences (P ≤ 0.05) between the average of treatments or the average of infected (40.8 g) and non-infected (48.4 g) plants.

Discussion

This study reveals the biocontrol potential of C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 against the RKN (M. javanica) besides its plant growth promoter role. These results add to the growing body of literature on C. sphaerospermum and show its potential as a natural nematicide and a good candidate to provide a sustainable alternative to chemical nematicides in light of the emerging threat that nematodes pose globally to agricultural sustainability.

First, direct parasitism and CF assays indicate that C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 markedly decreased egg hatching and increased M. javanica J2s mortality. Under the controlled conditions, strain PQ394940 inhibited egg hatching by 95.3% and was virulent against the J2s by 73.3%. These results agree with previous similar reports of nematicidity from Cladosporium spp. (Elhady et al., 2017; Meyer et al., 2004). The fungal parasitism of strain PQ394940 observed in our experiment, such as through eggs penetration and J2s penetration whereby the mycelium penetrated through the eggs, were clear evidence of the active antagonistic abilities of C. sphaerospermum as described previously (Ashraf and Khan, 2005).

C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 was able to infect nematode eggs and juveniles, suggesting it is a possible biocontrol agent in integrated pest management systems. Such biocontrol agents are essential, mainly since chemical nematicides are increasingly restricted due to environmental and health concerns (Talavera et al., 2012).

It showed a potent nematicidal activity when applied to C. sphaerospermum PQ394940, which demonstrated vigorous nematicidal activity with CF, suppressing egg hatching by 93.8% and inducing 65% mortality of J2s. This activity might be due to bioactive compounds like cladosporin and other secondary metabolites that have nematicide activity (Popli et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2013). In this study, the cell walls of J2s and the eggs were also observed to degrade by these metabolites. Such reports have also been provided for other Cladosporium species that secrete enzymes and toxins, inhibiting nematode development (Qureshi et al., 2012; Sakagami et al., 1995).

C. sphaerospermum produces cladosin, 2-methylacetate-3,5,6-trimethylpyrazine, gibberellins, and tetramic acids. Gibberellins are diterpenoid hormones involved in many aspects of plant growth and development, hence playing a role in the mutualistic plant-endophyte interactions (Hedden and Sponsel, 2015). Cladosin has many functions including antiviral, cytotoxic, and antibacterial activity (Lee et al., 2019). According to Hamayun et al. (2009), C. sphaerospermum endophytic fungi isolated from soybean plants were found to have the plant growth-promoting potential.

In addition to its nematicidal activity, C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 effectively promoted eggplant seed germination and enhanced seedlings growth. Cladosporium spp. are known to potentiate plant growth, indole-3-acetic acid production, siderophore activity, and volatile organic compound synthesis (Jiang et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023). Furthermore, they can facilitate nutrient uptake, improve root architecture, and contribute to key plant hormone pathways. This effect was also observed among strains of endophytes and species of biostimulants from Cladosporium in other crops (Li et al., 2019; Răut et al., 2021).

These results highlight the dual potential for C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 as a biocontrol agent and a plant growth promoter. The efficacy results against M. javanica, and the possible enhancement of seed germination suggest their potential use in sustainable agriculture. C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 is a biocontrol agent that is better than pesticides as it permits the limited application of pesticides, reduces environmental harm, and promotes soil health.

In this study, C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 significantly promoted eggplant growth in vivo. Such a result was obtained by Li et al. (2019), who exposed tobacco seedlings to the culture of C. sphaerospermum TC09, which increased stem height by up to 25-fold and plant biomass by up to 15-fold. Additionally, pre-exposed pepper plants to C. sphaerospermum TC09 before being planted in the greenhouse resulted in earlier flowering by 20 days compared to the control and yielded 213% more than untreated plants (Li et al., 2019). Gene expression analysis of tobacco seedlings exposed to the bioagent for 10 days supports these findings, revealing upregulation of genes involved in various biological processes. These processes include cell expansion and the cell cycle, photosynthesis, phytohormone homeostasis, and defense responses.

Another finding in this study was that C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 successfully suppressed M. javanica reproduction and root galling in vivo. In soil, Cladosporium spp. can compete with RKNs for resources and alter the root environment, indirectly benefiting the plant and deterring nematode activity (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020). Moreover, Cladosporium is found to colonize plant tissues as endophytes. This endophytic association can contribute to plant health by enhancing plant defenses against nematodes. Endophytic fungi can induce systemic resistance in plants, often by modulating plant hormone pathways (e.g., salicylic acid and jasmonic acid), which can make the host plant less susceptible to nematode infection and development (Schouten, 2019). Healthy root systems are generally more resilient to nematode attack. Cladosporium (endophytic strains) can sometimes promote plant growth and root development, indirectly leading to a healthier plant that can better withstand nematode infection (Poveda et al., 2022). While not a direct anti-gall mechanism, improved plant vigor can lessen the impact of galling.

In conclusion, this work will help identify C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 as an effective biocontrol agent and plant-growth-promoting agent against M. javanica. Moreover, the use of C. sphaerospermum PQ394940 denotes an essential step in enhancing agricultural productivity while minimizing pests’ ecological effects (Islam, 2022). Field experiments must verify their efficacy in ecological environments and agricultural systems. Likewise, strain PQ394940 may provide insight into this organism’s secondary metabolite pathways and adaptation mechanisms. It will enhance the use of this organism as a biocontrol agent for nematode management and plant health enhancement. Not only do the findings of this work advance the understanding of the mechanisms of fungal biocontrol, but they also provide sustainable options for nematode management and support global efforts to encourage environmentally sustainable agricultural practices and minimize reliance on chemical control.
