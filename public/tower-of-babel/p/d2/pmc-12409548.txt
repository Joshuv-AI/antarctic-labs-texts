In Vitro Evaluation of Amide-Linked Coumarin Scaffolds for the Inhibition of α‑Synuclein and Tau Aggregation
Authors: Taiwo A. Ademoye, Susantha K. Ganegamage, Behrad Masoudi, Omnia M. H. Ibrahium, Heba Alnakhala, Arati Tripathi, Ulf Dettmer, Raluca Ostafe, Babak Borhan, Jessica S. Fortin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) are the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorders characterized by continuous loss of functional neurons. The numbers of AD and PD patients will likely double by 2060 and 2040, reaching 13.9 and 1.2 million, respectively, in the US alone. Although both AD and PD are multifactorial in origin, the accumulation of misfolded proteins such as α-synuclein (α-syn) and tau contribute to nerve function disruption. Therefore, inhibition of α-syn and tau aggregation via small-molecule disruptors of oligomer and fibril formation presents a promising method for treating AD and PD. Coumarin scaffolds possess a wide range of bioactivities, particularly their antiamyloidogenic potential, which was explored in this study. Our previous work demonstrated that amide linkers and amino indole moieties have antioligomer and antifibrillar effects. This study involves coupling the coumarin scaffold with various aromatic moieties, including aminoindoles, methoxy-substituted phenyl, and polyhydroxy aromatic functionalities, via an amide linker for establishing the structural activity relationship (SAR) for the inhibition of oligomer and fibril formation. In total, 38 coumarin-based amide compounds were prepared to first explore the antifibrillar activity on recombinant α-syn. The best compounds were then tested to assess the antioligomer effects, tau aggregation activity, inclusion inhibition, and dimerization in cells. Biophysical methods such as thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence assays, photoinduced cross-linking of unmodified proteins (PICUP), survival assays, and electron microscopic observations were used to evaluate the inhibitory effects of analogs on α-syn and tau aggregation. The coumarin-amide-dihydroxybenzene derivatives demonstrated superior effects on the inhibition of α-syn aggregation when compared with the coumarin-amide-indole derivatives. The methoxy (nondemethylated) counterparts of compounds 13 and 17 failed at reducing α-syn fibril formation. The coumarin-amide-dihydroxybenzene derivatives 13 and 17, exhibited different degrees of inhibition on the α-syn oligomer and inclusion formation. Compound 13 inhibited tau (2N4R isoform) oligomer formation and reduced tau dimerization in a cell-based assay. In conclusion, the results presented herein will guide future optimization of molecules with inhibitory effects on prone-to-aggregate proteins and may pave the way for disease-modifying treatments for neurodegenerative disorders.

Introduction

Protein fibrillization forms the basis of neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD). Fibril formation is a pathological event in which normally soluble proteins misfold and subsequently aggregate into insoluble fibrillar structures. These fibrils accumulate within neuronal tissues, disrupting cellular functions and progressively damaging nerve cells. In AD, the two primary pathological hallmarks widely recognized are the accumulation of toxic amyloid-beta (Aβ) proteins, which form extracellular plaques, and the development of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) derived from hyperphosphorylated tau proteins. Both amyloid plaque and NFT accumulation over time contribute, among additional factors, to impaired cell signaling and the degeneration of synaptic neurites, which leads to a failure in information transmission. , These conditions are considered central to the disease’s progression and its devastating effects on cognitive function and neural health. While Aβ plaques appear before NFTs in AD patients, epidemiological studies indicate that the severity of cognitive deficits is closely associated with the regional distribution of NFTs. ,

NFTs are formed by the aggregation of tau protein, which generally associates with and stabilizes microtubules in neurons. When tau proteins undergo hyperphosphorylation, they detach from the microtubules to which they were originally bound. This detachment increases their propensity to interact with other tau monomers. As these individual tau proteins bind together, they form small oligomeric structures. As tau oligomers elongate, they adopt a β-sheet structure and form granular-like insoluble aggregates that merge to form larger, more complex aggregates (i.e., tau fibrils), which eventually develop into NFTs. NFTs initially appear in the transentorhinal region of the temporal lobe. Over time, they progressively spread to limbic regions, including the hippocampus, and ultimately extend their reach to encompass extensive areas of the neocortex, further driving the progression of AD.

The human brain expresses six tau isoforms generated by alternative splicing of the MAPT gene. These isoforms differ by the presence of either three (3R) or four (4R) microtubule-binding repeats. Under normal conditions, the adult human brain maintains an approximately equal ratio of 3R to 4R tau isoforms. However, in diseased states, this balance can be disrupted. For example, in AD, 4R tau isoforms are more predominant, comprising roughly 60% of the total tau protein levels. This increased abundance of 4R tau isoforms, specifically 0N4R and 2N4R, has made them a major focus of study in AD, as they are thought to play a critical role in tau aggregation and disease progression. ,

PD is distinguished by the pathological process wherein alpha-synuclein (α-syn) aggregates form characteristic Lewy bodies (LBs), contributing to the disease’s progression. α-Syn is abundantly expressed in most neurons of the brain. Accompanied by various post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation, acetylation, glycation, and ubiquitination, α-syn undergoes a transformational shift in its shape. Interactions with lipid membranes and an excess of membrane-associated helical secondary structures may also drive oligomer and fibril formation. This altered form tends to assemble into dimers and oligomers, marking a critical step in the aggregation process associated with neurodegenerative diseases. These α-syn oligomers aggregate into immunoreactive structures within neurons, ultimately leading to the formation of LBs, which obstruct neurotransmitter transmission and activates pathways that lead to neuronal dysfunction. ,

Existing treatments for AD and PD mainly target symptom alleviation but not addressing the root causes of these neurodegenerative disorders. As a result, while they may provide temporary relief, they fail to deliver long-term improvements in overall health and quality of life. The ongoing focus of our work is to explore the potential of amide-linked coumarin compounds as multitarget inhibitors against the aggregation of α-syn and tau proteins. Coumarin exists naturally in plants as an aromatic lactone compound. It has been noted for its diverse range of biological activities, depending on its chemical composition. Certain coumarin derivatives are recognized for their antioxidant, antibacterial, antiproliferative, and anticancer properties. In this study, various phenolic groups were linked to the amide linker of the coumarin scaffold. Each derivative of coumarin was assessed for its pharmacological efficacy in inhibiting the aggregation of α-syn and tau proteins using ThT and ThS-based aggregation assays, respectively. The most promising compounds were further screened for their antioligomeric (α-syn and tau cell-free assays), anti-inclusion (α-syn cell-based assays), and antidimerization (tau cell-based assays) effects.

Results and Discussion

Chemistry

Final compounds were all synthesized by coupling the coumarin-3-carboxylic acid with primary and secondary aromatic amines, respectively (Scheme and Table ). Secondary amides were formed under EDCI/DMAP conditions, while tertiary amides were obtained by first converting coumarin-3-carboxylic acid to the corresponding acyl chloride using oxalyl chloride, followed by the addition of secondary aromatic amines. Methoxyphenol derivatives were further deprotected with BBr3 to yield phenol derivatives, resulting in a total of 38 analogs for structure–activity relationship studies.

Structure–Activity Relationship for the Inhibition of α-Syn Fibril Formation

To study the inhibitory effects of our compounds on α-syn fibrillation, we employed ThT fluorescence to monitor the fibrillation kinetics. ThT binds to the grooves of β-sheet protein chains aligned parallel to the fibril axis, restricting rotation around the C–C bond in the ThT molecule, which leads to an increase in ThT fluorescence. Each of the tested compounds (100 μM) was incubated with monomeric α-syn at a concentration of 6 μM for a duration of 27 h. During this incubation period, the ThT fluorescence intensity was continuously measured at 20 min intervals to monitor the changes. The addition of compounds 1–8, 10–12, 14–16, 18, and 20–38 resulted in a nonsignificant decrease in fluorescence intensity. The cutoff to advance the compounds to a higher level of testing (i.e., antioligomer assessment) was set at 80–90% reduction of ThT fluorescence intensity. In contrast, compounds 9 (4-hydroxybenzene), 13 (4,5-dihydroxybenzene), 17 (5,6-dihydroxybenzene), and 19 (3,5-dihydroxybenzene) decreased the fluorescence intensity compared to the control, where monomeric α-syn was incubated with DMSO (Figure ), suggesting that these compounds are capable of inhibiting α-syn fibril formation. The methoxy counterparts (compounds 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, and 18) of these compounds, as well as the N-2,3-dihydro-1,4-benzodioxin-6-yl derivative (compound 20), failed at curtailing α-syn aggregation. Using a higher concentration of α-syn (6 μM instead of 2 μM), the ThT fluorescence intensity for samples containing compounds 13, 17, and 19 was recorded as 0.8 ± 0.2%, 10.7 ± 0.2%, and 20.2 ± 1.2%, respectively (Table ). After the initial ThT assay was conducted, dose–response curves were specifically generated for compounds 13 and 17 (Figure ). The analysis performed using Prism software showed a Log­(agonist) versus normalized response correlation with a variable slope. This analysis provided Log EC50 values of 0.81 ± 0.23 for compound 13 and 10.71 ± 0.19 for compound 17. These values highlight the differential potency of the two compounds in their respective dose–response relationships. In addition, the tau (isoform 2N4R) antifibrillar activity of compounds 13 and 17 was tested at approximately a 1:8 ratio (12 μM tau, 100 μM compound) (Figure ). Compounds 13 and 17 exhibited variable reduction in tau fibril formation as reflected by ThS fluorescence intensities of 27.6 ± 2.8% and 56.4 ± 4.4%, respectively. In addition to evaluation of their effects on tau 2N4R, the impact of compounds 13 and 17 on tau 0N4R fibril formation was assessed (Figure ). At the end of the experiment, compound 13 exhibited the lowest ThS fluorescence intensity, indicating a significant reduction in the level of 0N4R fibril formation. Based on the ThS data, compound 13 showed a similar inhibition trend across both isoforms.

The increased fluorescence observed with compounds 16 and 18 suggests that they may enhance the formation of α-syn fibrils. The R1 groups of these two compounds may be promoting the formation and stabilization of β-sheet structures in the protein, thereby accelerating fibril formation. Additionally, interactions or phenomena leading to intrinsic fluorescence may overlap with ThT, contributing to the observed increase in the signal. Further studies are needed to determine the specific role of the R1 groups in promoting fibril formation. The scope of this study were the antiaggregation effects of the synthesized compounds. One of the primary assays used for initial screening was the ThT assay, and compounds showing a reduction in the fluorescence intensity of about 15% or more proceeded to further evaluation. Given the increased fluorescence observed with compounds 16 and 18, these compounds were unlikely to be effective against other processes that lead to the progression of AD and related dementia, such as inclusion formation and oligomerization.

Concerning the best compounds (compounds 13 and 17) and their nondemethylated counterparts (compounds 12 and 16), the potential to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) was evaluated using the Multiparameter optimization desirability (MPO) score established by Pfizer (Table ). All compounds evaluated in this study have central nervous system (CNS) MPO scores greater than 4.0 (Compound 12 = 4.6; Compound 13 = 4.5; Compound 16 = 4.6; Compound 17 = 4.3), a well-established parameter developed by Pfizer to assess the physicochemical properties of compounds that influence CNS activity and BBB permeability. Comprehensive studies involving animal models are required to accurately determine BBB permeability and oral bioavailability, but these are beyond the scope of the current work. Nevertheless, the in silico data and favorable MPO scores (greater than 4) indicate that these compounds hold promise for administration in future CNS-targeted applications using animal models.

Compound 13 Reduced the α-Syn Oligomer Formation by Photoinduced Cross-Linking of Unmodified Proteins (PICUP)

The aggregation of fibril-forming proteins into oligomers marks a critical step in the pathogenesis of protein-misfolding diseases. These oligomers, characterized by their cytotoxicity, play a pivotal role in disease progression and are thus significant targets for therapeutic intervention. In our previous studies, we utilized the PICUP assay to generate stable α-syn and tau oligomers. This approach allowed us to evaluate the effectiveness of our compounds in inhibiting or reducing the formation of these oligomers. We investigated the effect of our compounds on α-syn, tau 0N4R, and tau 2N4R oligomerization. For the initial evaluation of α-syn antioligomerization, the compounds were assessed at a concentration of 100 μM against 6 μM of the protein (Figure ). The most promising compounds from this initial screening were subsequently tested to determine their dose–response effects on the prevention of α-syn oligomer formation. Compounds 9 (4-hydroxybenzene), 13 (4,5-dihydroxybenzene), and 19 (3,5-dihydroxybenzene) demonstrated the most significant inhibition of α-syn oligomerization compared to the control, which contained 0.25% DMSO (Figure ). In contrast, the other tested compounds (5, 8, 12, and 16–18) showed very weak to no inhibitory effect. The pixel density of the α-syn bands located between 35 and 40 kDa, which correspond with the oligomer formation, was measured with ImageJ. Compounds 13 and 17, both dihydroxybenzene derivatives with strong antifibrillar activity, exhibited a reduction of 94% and 18% of α-syn oligomer formation, respectively, in comparison with the control (0.25% DMSO). The dose–response studies of the promising compound 13 indicated that it has a strong, concentration-dependent inhibitory effect on α-syn oligomerization (Figure ). The α-syn antioligomer effect of compound 13 vanished at about 12.5 μM. The antioligomer effect of compounds 13 and 17 was confirmed using our orthogonal testing approach via Western blot analysis. The compounds were coincubated with α-syn for 7 days at a 2:1 ratio (100 μM compound to 50 μM α-syn) as shown in Figure a. Samples were separated in a 16% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane for Western blot analysis using an antibody to detect α-syn oligomers located between 35 and 40 kDa (anti-syn 33). The α-syn samples treated with the vehicle (0.25% DMSO) displayed a more intense band between 35 and 40 kDa than those treated with compounds 12, 13, and 17 (Figure b). Compounds 13 and 17 reduced the level of α-syn oligomer formation by 15% and 76%, respectively. The oligomer reduction observed with the treated protein using the Western blot approach coincided with the anti-inclusion activity observed in neuroblastoma cells (results discussed in a later section). In addition, tau 0N4R monomer (6 μM) was challenged with both compounds 13 and 17, as well as their nondemethylated counterparts (compounds 12 and 16), to assess the tau antioligomer activity. For tau 0N4R, coumarin-amide-based derivatives did not halt the formation of tau 0N4R oligomers (Figure ), while compound 13 was effective at inhibiting the formation of 2N4R oligomers by about half when compared to the control (Figure ). Next, the effect of compound 13 on preventing tau 1N4R oligomer formation was assessed. The compound (100 μM) was incubated with 1N4R (6 μM) for 3 days, and Western blot was performed using the Tau-5 antibody. In terms of oligomer band intensity, compound 13 reduced oligomer formation by 28% when compared to compound 16 (without any antifibrillar activity) and DMSO-treated tau 1N4R (Figure ).

Confirmation of the Antifibrillar Activity by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

To investigate the impact of compound treatment on ultrastructural alterations in α-syn and tau 2N4R fibrils, we performed TEM analysis. Figure displays TEM images of α-syn fibrils extracted from the 96-well plate post-ThT assay. The images contrast fibrils formed under control conditions (without test compounds) with those formed in the presence of test compounds. The control sample, containing 0.25% DMSO, exhibited a molecular architecture marked by extensive, densely packed fibrils. In the sample containing compounds 13 and 17, there was a notable and significant reduction in both the length and density of the fibrils, with compound 13 being more effective, which is consistent with the ThT data. Compound 19 showed some effect, but the fibrils were more densely packed compared to the most effective compounds, 13 and 17. In contrast, compounds 8, 12, 16, and 18 showed no observable effect. In the TEM images of tau 2N4R (Figure ), it was observed that compounds 13 and 17 effectively inhibited the formation of protein fibrils. Effect of Compound 13 on Tau 2N4R Secondary Structures

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was used to examine the secondary structure changes of tau 2N4R and α-syn in response to treatment with compounds 13 and 17. Figure A shows representative CD spectra of α-syn in its nonaggregated state. Figure A–C illustrates the expected progression of tau secondary structure over time in the absence of compound treatment and serves as a reference for its typical behavior under aggregating conditions. In antiaggregation assays where the protein and compound are incubated together, a stronger signal around 200–210 nm and a weaker one between 220–230 nm usually means that more β-sheet structures have formed. On the other hand, if the signal drops in the 200–210 nm range and increases in the 220–230 nm range, it suggests that β-sheet structures are being lost. In our antiaggregation experiments, α-syn (15 μM) and tau 2N4R (6 μM) were treated with 100 μM compounds 13 and 17 and incubated for 72 h at 37 °C. Compared to the DMSO control, treatment with compounds 13 and 17 caused distinct changes in the CD spectra of α-syn (Figure B) and tau 2N4R (Figure ), characterized by lower signals around 200–210 nm and higher signals between 220–230 nm, indicating reduced β-sheet content. Since a loss in β-sheet structure is associated with decreased fibril formation, these CD results are consistent with the findings from the ThT/ThS fluorescence assays (Figures and ). In the tau 2N4R CD spectroscopy experiment, compound 12 served as a negative control and did not produce a significant spectral shift, suggesting limited interference with tau 2N4R aggregation.

Compound 17 Decreased α-Syn Inclusion Formation in Neuroblastoma Cells

To investigate the effects of various compounds on α-syn inclusion formation and cell survival, we utilized M17D neuroblastoma cells engineered to express the αS-3K:YFP fusion protein in a doxycycline (dox)-inducible manner. This model is particularly relevant for studying the pathological consequences of the familial PD-associated α-syn E46K mutation. The pathological effect is further intensified by a triple mutation (E35K + E46K + E61K, referred to as αS3K), which leads to the formation of distinctive round cytoplasmic inclusions in cultured cells, thereby providing a valuable system for examining disease mechanisms and testing potential therapeutic strategies. Previous studies have consistently shown that αS3K expression in cultured cells leads to the formation of cytoplasmic inclusions that are enriched in membranes and lipids and are strongly positive for α-syn. −

Cells were exposed to various concentrations of compounds 13, 15, and 17 starting 24 h after plating. At 48 h, doxycycline was added to induce transgene expression. By 96 h, the dose-dependent effects of the compounds on the formation of inclusions were analyzed. Compound 13 reduced α-syn inclusion formation minimally at concentrations of 10, 20, and 40 μM (Figure A). In contrast, compound 17 decreased α-syn inclusions at these concentrations, exhibiting a dose-dependent effect with the greatest reduction at 40 μM (Figure A). These results are consistent with the α-syn antioligomer effects observed with the incubations and subsequent Western blot analysis, as presented in Figure . In contrast, compound 15, which is a weak inhibitor of α-syn fibrillation, exhibited no relevant effect on α-syn inclusion formation. None of the compounds changed the cell confluence (Figure B).

Effect of Compounds on Tau Aggregation in ExpiCHO Cells

This assay involves intracellular tau aggregation via two constructstau-GFP10C and tau-GFP11Cwhere fluorescence occurs only when tau-tau interactions enable complementation of the split-GFP fragments. Thus, fluorescence serves as a direct readout of tau oligomerization. The strong signal observed in the DMSO-treated control confirms that the tau-tagged constructs successfully associate and reconstitute GFP under baseline conditions. As established in the literature, split-GFP fragments such as GFP10 and GFP11 do not reliably reconstitute fluorescence in cells unless they are brought into proximity by interacting protein partners or forced interaction domains.

The split-GFP assay was evaluated using flow cytometry as a high-throughput, quantitative method to assess the fluorescence intensity in live cells, reflecting tau–tau interactions and GFP fragment complementation. Flow cytometry was chosen over microscopy because it enables consistent and objective comparison across multiple treatment conditions with all samples measured under identical detector settings. In contrast, quantitative fluorescence microscopy requires specialized imaging systems, high-content imaging platforms, and image analysis pipelines to ensure reproducibility and comparability, which represent different experimental setups with distinct strengths and limitations.

To evaluate the impact of test compounds on tau oligomerization, ExpiCHO cells were transfected with split GFP-Tau fusion constructs (pmGFP11C-Tau and pmGFP10C-Tau) and treated with compounds (20 μM) or vehicle control (0.1% DMSO) for 72 h. Flow cytometry analysis was used to quantify GFP-positive cell populations, reflecting tau aggregation, and ethidium homodimer-1 exclusion was used to confirm the cell viability. Only viable GFP+ cells were used for the analysis. Among the tested compounds, only compound 13 significantly reduced GFP fluorescence compared to the vehicle control, indicating its unique ability to inhibit tau aggregation (Figure ). None of the other compounds (5, 8, 9, 12, 17, 16, 18, and 19) demonstrated a measurable effect on GFP-positive populations. These findings are consistent with the results observed in the TEM and ThS analyses using tau isoform 2N4R, further supporting the inhibitory effect of compound 13 on tau oligomer and fibril formation.

Compound 13 Binds to Monomeric α-Syn

Previous studies have identified specific binding sites on α-syn monomers and fibrils. In our investigation, the best fit was obtained with the binding site model called the Sequential Three Site on the α-syn monomer. Similarly, Hsieh et al. reported three distinct binding sites on α-syn fibrils with high ligand accessibility and affinity, while Sobek et al. identified four structurally and functionally distinct sites with specific ligand interaction preferences. In this study, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was used to determine the binding affinity (K d) and the thermodynamic interactions of compound 13 with the monomeric α-syn.

The curve fitting model in ITC is a universally recognized and widely accepted method for evaluating binding interactions between a ligand and a target. The titration was performed using a molar ratio of protein:compound of 1:10 to ensure saturation. The binding curve was obtained after subtraction of the solvent effects on the protein and fitting with the binding site model, i.e., sequential three site (solid line in Figure A). Thermodynamic parameters obtained from the fitting of the binding curve, along with the confidence intervals, are indicated in Figure B. The binding affinity K d of compound 13 was determined with the sequential three site model to be 1.1 μM for the first site, 0.1 M for the second site, and 0.4 μM for the third site (Figure ). The results indicate that site 3 exhibits the highest binding affinity for compound 13, as evidenced by its lowest dissociation constant (K d) and highest association constant (K a) among the three sites.

Conclusion

A total of 38 coumarin-based amide compounds were designed and synthesized primarily to evaluate the antiaggregation activity on two proteins involved in neurodegenerative diseases: α-syn and tau. The coumarin-amide-dihydroxybenzene derivatives demonstrated superior α-syn antiaggregation activities when compared to coumarin-amide-indole and methoxybenzene derivatives. Compounds 13 and 17 showed different degrees of α-syn oligomer reduction depending on the assays utilized, such as cross-linking by PICUP or protein-compound incubations, followed by Western blot analysis. The results obtained from the Western blot analysis were consistent with the anti-inclusion effects observed using the neuroblastoma cells. Compound 13 (which bears a 4,5-dihydroxybenzene moiety) was best at reducing tau oligomer formation, tau fibril formation (cell-free assay), and tau dimerization (cell-based assay) but failed at reducing α-syn inclusions substantially in neuroblastoma cells. In contrast, compound 17, a 5,6-dihydroxybenzene derivative, reduced the α-syn inclusions but failed at inhibiting tau fibrils (cell-free assay) and tau dimerization (cell-based assay). This study sets the stage for the future exploration of dual inhibitors of aggregation to improve neuronal health in transgenic animal models of synucleinopathies and tauopathies.

General Synthetic Procedure

Synthesis of Amides with Primary Aromatic Amines

The coumarin 3-carboxylic acid (1.00 equiv), the respective primary aromatic amine (1.00 equiv), and DMAP (0.10 equiv) were dissolved in DCM at room temperature. The mixture was cooled to 0 °C in an ice bath. Then, EDCI (10 mL in DCM) was added dropwise at 0 °C for 10–20 min and gradually warmed to room temperature overnight. The mixture was stirred until all of the starting material was consumed. The reaction mixture was filtered and washed with HCl (10%, 25 mL), water (25 mL), and DCM (10 mL). The precipitate was dried overnight to obtain the dry product.

Synthesis of Amides with Secondary Aromatic Amines

The coumarin 3-carboxylic acid (1.00 equiv) and DMF (50 μL) were dissolved in DCM (8.0 mL). The mixture was cooled to 0 °C in an ice bath. Oxalyl chloride (1.25 equiv) was added dropwise at 0 °C and gradually allowed to warm to room temperature overnight. The next day, the solvent was evaporated by vacuum, and crude acyl chloride was redissolved in DCM (8.0 mL) and cooled to 0 °C. The secondary aromatic amine (1.00 equiv) was added, followed by Et3N (1.00 equiv), and allowed to warm gradually to room temperature overnight. The next day, the reaction mixture was treated with aqueous HCl (10%, 25 mL) and extracted with EtOAc. The organic layer was washed with brine (25 mL), dried with anhydrous MgSO4, and evaporated to obtain the crude. The crude product was recrystallized with hexane and ethyl acetate to obtain a clean amide product.

Demethylation of Coumarin-Amides with BBr3 (Analogs: 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 17, 19)

The methoxy amide substrate (1.00 equiv) was dissolved in DCM (5.0 mL) at room temperature and cooled to 0 °C in an ice bath. Boron tribromide (5.00 equiv per each methoxy functionality) was added dropwise at 0 °C over 15 min. Then, the reaction mixture was stirred at 0 °C and gradually warmed to room temperature gradually overnight. The reaction progress was followed by TLC. Upon completion, the reaction was quenched with saturated NH4Cl, and the crude was purified by recrystallization with hexane and ethyl acetate (1:1).

Biophysical Experiments

Chemical and Protein Source

Heparin sodium salt was acquired from Millipore-Sigma; Thioflavin-T (ThT) for the α-syn ThT assays was from Alfa Aesar (Ward Hill, MA); and recombinant α-syn and tau 2N4R were from rPeptide (WatKinsville, GA). The expression and purification of tau 0N4R were carried out as previously described. Dr. Benjamin Wolozin (Boston University) generously provided a bacterial expression plasmid that contains the vector pET30a with a cDNA sequence encoding the human tau 0N4R isoform. Rosetta BL21 E. coli cells (CamR) containing the pET30a­[0N4R tau wt] plasmid (KanR) were cultured in LB media with 50 μg/mL kanamycin and 50 μg/mL chloramphenicol. Protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM IPTG and incubating for about 18 h at 37 °C. The cells were collected by centrifugation at 6,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The cells were mixed with lysis buffer containing 10 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM PMSF, 1X protease inhibitor cocktail, and 0.5 mM DTT. Subsequently, the cell suspension underwent sonication in cycles of 30 s on and 1 min off, operating at 30–45% power for a total duration of 5 min. Following sonication, the lysate underwent centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then gently mixed with 7.8 mL of 3 M NaCl and incubated in an 80 °C water bath for 10 min, followed by cooling on ice for an additional 10 min. After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the resulting supernatant was carefully transferred into fresh tubes. The supernatant was subjected to overnight dialysis against a cation exchange buffer (50 mM MES, 1 M NaCl, 1 mM DTT, pH 6.0). Following dialysis, the sample was applied onto a HiPrep SP HP column, where proteins were separated using a linear gradient of NaCl concentration ranging from 50 mM to 1 M. Fractions containing the tau isoform 0N4R were combined, and the resulting protein solution underwent dialysis against PBS (pH 7.4) before being stored at −80 °C.

Thioflavin T (ThT) Fluorescence Assays

The synthesized compounds were evaluated for their effects on α-synuclein fibril formation using the ThT fluorescence assay, as previously described. , A 96-well microplate was utilized for the experiment. The compounds were added at a final concentration of 100 μM, and ThT was also added at a final concentration of 100 μM. α-Syn was introduced to each well to achieve a final concentration of 2 or 6 μM, from a stock solution of 277 μM, prepared by dissolving it in 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4). The assay conditions also included 10 mM PBS buffer (pH 7.4), 300 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM SDS, and a 3 mm thick borosilicate bead. The 96-well plate was loaded into a Synergy HT multimode microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT) and incubated at 37 °C. The ThT fluorescence intensity was monitored with excitation at 440 nm and emission at 485 nm. Measurements were taken every 20 min, with the plate shaken for 10 s before each reading. The kinetics were observed over a period of 27 h, with each sample analyzed in triplicate.

Thioflavin S (ThS) Fluorescence Assay

A black 384-well microplate with a flat bottom (ref 784076, medium binding; Brand) was used for the ThS assay. PBS (pH 7.4) (Gibco, catalog number 10010-023), treated with Chelex beads (Biosciences, BTNM-0024), was dispensed into each well to a final volume of 10 μL. Tau isoform 2N4R was added to each well at a final concentration of 12 μM, followed by the addition of DTT to a final concentration of 5 mM. The compound was then added at a final concentration of 100 μM, and heparin was added at a final concentration of 150 μM. The reaction mixture was supplemented with arachidonic acid to a final concentration of 0.092 μg/mL (A0781, Fisher TCI). ThS was added to each well at a final concentration of 40 μM to monitor the kinetics of fibril formation. Fluorescence intensity was measured every 5 min with 30 s of agitation before each measurement.

Photo-Induced Cross-Linking of Unmodified Proteins (PICUP)

α-Syn and tau (0N4R isoform) were diluted in PBS (pH 7.4) to achieve a final concentration of 6 μM for each protein in a PCR tube. Two μL of tris­(bipyridyl)­ruthenium­(II)­chloride [Ru­(bpy)3]3+ (RTD) (final concentration 300 μM) and 2 μL of ammonium persulfate (final concentration 6 mM) were added to initiate cross-linking. The tube was then placed in a homemade dark enclosure (a small box). To assess the progressive impact of our compounds on suppressing protein oligomerization, we employed controls that were kept in the dark and lacked Ru­(bpy) (i.e., 0.125% DMSO). Light exposure was achieved using a 53 W (120 V) incandescent bulb. α-Syn was exposed to light for 1 s, while tau 0N4R was exposed for 60 s. To stop the PICUP reaction, 15% 2-mercaptoethanol was added immediately following exposure to light. The reaction mixture was heated on a heating block at 95 °C for 10 min. A 16% SDS-PAGE gel was used to evaluate the samples with the aid of a protein marker (PageRuler Prestained Protein Ladder), which was used to examine the molecular weights of the samples. After electrophoresis, gels were incubated in the fixing solution for 1 h and then stained with Coomassie Blue for up to 48 h to improve sensitivity. The gels were then destained with 10% acetic acid and incubated in deionized water for about 1 h to remove the background staining before scanning with G:BOX F3 (Syngene).

Western Blot

α-Syn and tau 1N4R were incubated with the lead compounds to assess their antioligomer effects. For α-syn, the protein was incubated with compounds 13 and 17 at concentrations of 50 μM and 100 μM, respectively, at 37 °C for 7 days. Following incubation, the samples were loaded onto a gel for Western blot analysis. Syn-33 (ABN2265-25UG, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as the primary antibody at a 1:1000 dilution, and HRP-conjugated antirabbit antibody was used as the secondary at a 1:2500 dilution. For tau 1N4R, the protein was incubated with compound 13 at concentrations of 6 μM and 100 μM, respectively. Anti-Tau-5 (MABN162, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as the primary antibody at a 1:1000 dilution, while an HRP-conjugated antimouse antibody served as the secondary antibody.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

TEM was performed following previously established methods. , Tau isoform 2N4R (12 μM), along with compounds of interest and DMSO (0.25% as a control), was incubated in a solution containing 10 mM PBS buffer (pH 7.4), 150 μM heparin, 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and 10 mg/mL arachidonic acid for 5 days at 37 °C. For α-syn, the samples used for the ThT fibril kinetic formation assay were subsequently analyzed by TEM. Ten μL of the samples were applied onto a 400-mesh Formvar-carbon-coated copper grid (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA) and incubated for 1 min before rinsing with water. After the grid was rinsed with distilled water, it was air-dried, and a solution of 1% uranyl acetate was applied for 1 min and then dried using filter paper. The samples were examined using a JEOL 1400 Flash (Japan) electron microscope operating at 100 kV.

Circular Dichroism (CD)

CD spectra were recorded at 25 °C using a JASCO-1500 spectropolarimeter (Jasco, Easton, MD) under a constant nitrogen flow. The measurements were taken over a wavelength range of 195–250 nm using a 1 mm path length quartz cuvette. The instrument was set to a scanning speed of 100 nm/min, a response time of 2 s, and a bandwidth of 1 nm. Tau protein (isoform 2N4R) was prepared at a final concentration of 6 μM and incubated with the test compounds or 0.25% DMSO (vehicle control) in a solution containing 10 mM PBS buffer (pH 7.4), 150 μM heparin, 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and 10 mg/mL arachidonic acid. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 3 days to promote aggregation. A baseline spectrum from a buffer-only sample (without protein) was subtracted from the spectra of each sample containing the protein and compound. For α-syn, the protein (15 μM) was incubated with the compound at 100 μM for 2 days; thereafter, the samples were analyzed on a JASCO-810 spectropolarimeter (Jasco, Easton, MD). For this assay, the α-syn protein used was derived from bacterial culture (and not the purchased protein), as described previously.

α-Syn Inclusion-Forming Neuroblastoma Cell Assay

Previous studies have utilized the doxycycline-inducible neuroblastoma cells M17D-TR/αS-3K:YFP. Cells were seeded at a density of 30,000 cells per well in 96-well plates. Compounds were introduced after 24 h, and induction of αS-3K:YFP transgene expression occurred 48 h later. This induction involved adding 1 μg/mL (final concentration) of doxycycline to the culture media. Cells were cultured in the Incucyte Zoom 2000 platform (Essen Biosciences), where continuous imaging (green, bright field) was conducted. Analysis of inclusion formation or growth was performed at 48 h postinduction (96 h postplating). The Incucyte processing definition “Inclusions” was configured as follows: Parameters were set to fixed threshold with a threshold (GCU) of 50; edge split was activated with edge sensitivity set to 100; cleanup included hole fill (μm2): 10 and adjust size (pixel s): 0; filters applied were Area (μm2): max 50, mean Intensity: min 60, integrated intensity: min 2000. Cell confluence was assessed using the following criteria: “Cells” processing definition with parameters set at segmentation adjustment 0.7, Cleanup with all parameters at 0, and filters applied with an area (μm2) minimum of 345.

Cell-Based Tau Aggregation Assay

ExpiCHO cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were cultured according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The split GFP-Tau fusion constructs, pmGFP11C-Tau and pmGFP10C-Tau (Addgene plasmids #71434 and #71433), encoding the tau isoform 0N4R, were transformed into XL10-Gold Ultracompetent E. coli cells (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Plasmids were purified using the PureLink HiPure Plasmid Maxiprep Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Transfection of ExpiCHO cells was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Three days post-transfection, cells were treated in triplicate with test compounds (20 μM) or vehicle control (0.1% DMSO) for 72 h. Flow cytometry analysis was carried out at 72 h post-transfection using a BD LSRFortessa analyzer equipped with a 488 nm laser (530/30 nm filter) for GFP detection and a 561 nm laser (610/20 nm filter) for ethidium homodimer-1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to evaluate cell viability.

For each sample, a minimum of 10,000 events were recorded using BD FACSDiva software. Data analysis was conducted in FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA), with gating performed to identify live cells based on exclusion of ethidium homodimer-1, followed by quantification of GFP-positive populations. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC)

Binding experiments were performed using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC at 25 °C. A solution (molar ratio 1:10) of 0.5 mM compound 13 (20% DMSO in 10 mM PBS, pH 7.4) was prepared and titrated into an ITC cell containing 170 μL of α-syn at a concentration of 0.05 mM in an identical buffer. Twenty injections of ligand (2.47 μL each) were injected every 200 s into the ITC cell (at 25 °C) to the point that α-syn was fully saturated with the ligand. Calorimetric data were plotted, and the control data were subtracted. The resulting curve was fitted using the sequential three site binding model. The control consisted of titrating the cell containing α-syn with a solution of PBS supplemented with 20% DMSO.
