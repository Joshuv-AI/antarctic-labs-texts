Arthroscopic transition from suture anchors to transosseous suture technique for treating rotator cuff tear with osteoporosis- a case report
Authors: Kairui Zhang, Zheng Yan, Zhefeng Jin, Guoqing Cui, Liguo Zhu, Jie Yu, Yinzhe Cui, Minshan Feng, Jiawen Zhan, Jia Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Rotator cuff tear is one of the most frequent shoulder disorders, especially common among elderly individuals and those performing manual labor. It remains a leading cause of shoulder pain and loss of function [1]. Arthroscopic repair is now the standard treatment, with suture anchors (SA) fixation widely applied. However, in patients with osteoporosis, large rotator cuff tears, poor fixation, or intraoperative technical challenges, anchors can loosen or pull out, which undermines the stability and final outcome of the repair [2–4]. In recent years, growing awareness of complications linked to anchors has renewed interest in transosseous rotator cuff repair (TO-RCR), once considered the gold standard in open procedures. As an anchorless arthroscopic option, TO-RCR offers broader tendon-bone contact, more even stress distribution, less concentrated stress at the repair site, and a lower risk of implant-related issues [5–8]. Despite these benefits, conventional TO-RCR configurations such as the X-box(XBOX)and simple suture techniques have shown limited biomechanical strength in certain studies [9, 10]. To overcome these limitations, we introduced a novel high-strength transosseous suture technique, termed Open-Box (OBOX), and biomechanical testing confirmed that OBOX delivers significantly higher strength than earlier transosseous approaches, while performing comparably to the widely adopted anchored double-row suture bridge (ADRSB) technique [11]. In this case, the patient experienced anchor pullout during surgery, and the repair was successfully converted to the OBOX technique, which demonstrated the advantages of this transosseous method.

Case report

The patient, a 73-year-old female, presented on November 1, 2024, with a three-month history of intermittent right shoulder pain and restricted motion.After two weeks of oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug therapy, during which her pain was partially relieved but no improvement in range of motion was noted, her symptoms progressively worsened after discontinuing the medication, prompting her to seek further care at our department.Physical examination and MRI (Fig. 1) revealed a full-thickness rotator cuff tear involving the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons on the right side. She was admitted for further evaluation and surgical planning.

73-year-old female patient with supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendon tears. 1a. Preoperative oblique coronal MRI of right shoulder showing retracted supraspinatus tendon.1b. Preoperative axial MRI of right shoulder demonstrating supraspinatus tendon tear

On examination, there was point tenderness over the greater tuberosity of the right humerus, with no tenderness elsewhere. Active range of motion was as follows: abduction—right 80°, left 170°; adduction in neutral—right 20°, left 40°; forward flexion—right 90°, left 180°; extension—right 10°, left 30°; external rotation (arm at side)—right 10°, left 30°. Passive range of motion was as follows: abduction—right 150°, left 170°; adduction in neutral—right 30°, left 40°; forward flexion—right 160°, left 180°; extension—right 20°, left 30°; external rotation (arm at side)—right 20°, left 30°.All provocative tests on the right side were positive, including Neer, Hawkins, Hug-up, Jobe, Napoleon, and Lift-off tests. The Constant-Murley score was 33, and the UCLA shoulder score was 5. As symptoms had not improved with conservative measures, arthroscopic surgery was scheduled.

Surgical procedure

After induction of anesthesia, the patient was positioned in the lateral decubitus position with arm traction applied to facilitate arthroscopic access. Standard sterile preparation and draping were completed. The glenohumeral joint was entered through a standard posterior portal, followed by creation of an anterior accessory portal via the rotator interval. A complete diagnostic arthroscopy was performed (Fig. 2a and b). Inspection of the long head of the biceps tendon revealed no significant pathology. The arthroscope was then redirected into the subacromial space through the posterior portal.

A lateral subacromial portal was established, and a cannula was inserted. Subacromial bursectomy was carried out, along with acromioplasty.The arthroscope was moved to the posterolateral portal to provide improved visualization of the rotator cuff tear. A burr was used to debride the greater tuberosity and prepare the bone surface at the tendon footprint.

A 4.5 mm non-absorbable suture anchor was inserted into the medial footprint near the cartilage margin. Sutures were passed through the tendon using a suture passer and tied in a horizontal mattress configuration. However, during knot tying, the anchor dislodged from the bone (Fig. 2c). The sutures were cut, and the anchor was removed.

Two sets of vertically crossed bone tunnels, approximately 15 mm apart, were created using the Lightning Tunneler (Delta Co., Beijing) (Fig. 3). In one set, the medial tunnel corresponded to the original anchor hole. In the other set, the medial tunnel was freshly created using the drill device, with a diameter of approximately 4 mm. The lateral tunnel in both sets was approximately 2 mm in diameter and positioned about 1.5 cm inferior to the apex of the greater tuberosity.A loaded suture inserter—preloaded with the guide suture—was introduced through the lateral tunnel (Fig. 2d and e). The suture inserter and the Lightning Tunneler were then sequentially withdrawn, allowing the guide suture to pass transosseously through the greater tuberosity.This initial guideline loop served as the shuttle for pulling subsequent sutures through the bone tunnels. This shuttle was then used to pull two No. 2 highstrength braided composite sutures (Delta Co., Beijing) through each tunnel set (Fig. 2f), totaling four sutures. Two additional sutures were continuously looped through both tunnel sets, with the other guideline positioned between the lateral cortex of the humerus and the lateral openings of the two tunnels.

Self-developed transosseous tunneling device (Lightning Tunneler, Delta Co., Beijing)

The eight ends of six sutures were passed through the tendon at eight evenly spaced points using a suture passer. The paired limbs from the two remaining sutures were tied on the bursal side and reverse-pulled through the lateral cortex of the humerus to secure the tendon to the tunnels. Each of the four suture limbs was tied again using reverse-pulling at the lateral tunnel exits.

The two looped sutures were secured at the lateral tunnel exits (Sutures No. 5 and No. 6 in Fig. 4), then shuttled through the pre-positioned suture loops, allowing the inner and lateral row limbs to cross and be fixed in place (with Limbs 5a and 6a tied together, and 5b and 6b tied together as shown in Fig. 4) (Fig. 2g). Finally, the four sutures passed individually through the tunnels were retied at their respective limbs, completing a double-row OBOX suture bridge construct (Figs. 2h and i and 4).

Sutures 1b and 3b, as well as 2b and 4b, are then pulled laterally and tied at the lateral openings of two transosseous bone tunnels on the lateral cortex of the humerus. The guiding suture reserved at the loop site is used to shuttle both Limbs of the No. 5 suture (5a and 5b) across the preformed suture loop at the medial row. Suture 5a is tied with 6a, and 5b is tied with 6b. 3c Schematic illustration of the final suturing configuration

Inspection confirmed firm tendon fixation without gapping and with appropriate tension across the repair site. The joint was irrigated, portals were closed, and the arm was placed in an abduction brace set as 30 degree of abduction. Early passive motion exercises were conducted 3 to 4 times per day during the abduction brace-wearing period.

At the 8-month follow-up, the patient showed active forward flexion of approximately 170°, abduction of approximately 160°, and external rotation at the side of approximately 70° (Fig. 5a, b and c). The Constant-Murley score rose from 75 at 2 months postoperatively to 85 at 4 months and 93 at 8 months. The UCLA score increased from 24 at 2 months to 29 at 4 months and 33 at 8 months. MRI at 2 months confirmed an intact rotator cuff repair with a postoperative Sugaya classification of type II and no sign of re-tear. (Fig. 6)

Discussion

Rotator cuff tears are frequently encountered in orthopedics, with the majority occurring in patients over 60 years old. Osteoporosis is common in this group [12]suggesting that the proximal humerus may have compromised bone quality, which increases the risk of suture anchor pullout or failure during or after surgery [13]. This report describes a 73-year-old patient with a rotator cuff tear and coexisting osteoporosis. The initial arthroscopic repair using SA fixation failed intraoperatively due to anchor pullout during knot tying, likely caused by poor bone quality. The surgical team then switched to an anchorless transosseous suture method using the OBOX configuration. This case explores intraoperative strategies for managing anchor pullout in osteoporotic patients and evaluates the clinical application of the OBOX technique in rotator cuff repair.

Previous studies have shown that anchor pullout during rotator cuff repair is not rare. Jung et al. [14]. conducted a retrospective study of 1,076 patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. Among 483 patients treated with screw-in-type bioabsorbable or biocomposite anchors, 16 cases of anchor pullout were reported (3.3%). In comparison, 32 out of 593 patients treated with soft anchors (5.4%) experienced similar complications. These findings confirm that intraoperative anchor pullout is a recognized clinical issue. Early identification and proper intraoperative management are essential to the success of the procedure.

The literature outlines several strategies to address anchor pullout during rotator cuff repair, including repositioning the anchor to an area with stronger bone stock, using bone cement to reinforce fixation, applying all-suture anchors, switching to transosseous suture methods, employing the buddy anchor technique or Steinmann pin fixation, and using novel knotless transosseous anchor systems [15]. Among these, the transosseous technique is especially suited for osteoporotic patients because it does not rely on implant-based fixation. It is considered a reliable salvage option when anchor failure occurs. In this case, prompt recognition of anchor pullout allowed a timely switch to the OBOX configuration, which secured tendon fixation and rescued the procedure.

In recent years, arthroscopic anchorless transosseous suture techniques have progressed by combining the minimally invasive advantages of arthroscopy with the biomechanical reliability of open surgery [16, 17]. These methods also reduce the likelihood of nerve injury and other complications [18]. Compared to conventional SA repair, transosseous approaches have shown better early functional outcomes (within 3–6 months postoperatively), which may improve pain control and patient adherence to rehabilitation [19]. Like other transosseous methods, the OBOX technique limits implant-related cyst formation and bone loss [8]. A case-control study by Jeong et al. [8]which compared 19 patients treated with transosseous anchorless repair (ToR) to 57 patients who underwent SA repair, found no cyst formation in the ToR group, while 17.9% of patients in the SA group developed peri-implant cysts. In addition, avoiding the use of anchors lowers procedural costs, making it a more affordable treatment option [19, 20]. Recent studies have reported that transosseous fixation offers improved biomechanical strength, with better compression at the tendon footprint and more favorable conditions for healing [6, 21]. In contrast, sutures used in the suture anchor technique primarily generate circumferential tension around the tendon but provide relatively limited tendon-to-bone compression [18].

Anchor-based fixation can lead to incomplete footprint coverage and uneven suture tension, which may hinder proper anatomic restoration. In contrast, transosseous techniques allow sutures to pass directly through both tendon and bone, supporting more uniform tension distribution and fuller footprint coverage [7]. The OBOX method builds on traditional transosseous strategies by using six sutures, including two looped through both bone tunnels, resulting in eight terminal limbs—matching the suture count used in ADRSB repair. These eight limbs are evenly spaced through the tendon, helping to reduce stress concentration and increase repair strength. Unlike the XBOX technique, which uses a single wide-range mattress stitch, OBOX applies four narrow-range horizontal mattress stitches, improving medial fixation. One of the defining features of the OBOX configuration is the sequence of knot tying: first on the bursal side, followed by reverse-pulling through the bone tunnels and final knotting laterally. This approach compresses the tendon into the tunnel, maximizing tendon-bone contact, which aligns with the foundational principle of strong repair. This structure contributes to the biomechanical advantages of OBOX over both traditional and other transosseous methods. Prior biomechanical research has shown that the OBOX configuration offers higher ultimate failure strength and better fatigue resistance than the XBOX approach, while achieving comparable strength to ADRSB constructs [11]. Although increasing the number of sutures could theoretically reduce tendon vascularity and affect healing, transosseous repair has been associated with improved subcuff vascularity and improved early postoperative perfusion [22]. However, the OBOX technique is technically challenging, with a steep learning curve, which may limit its widespread use.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that the OBOX transosseous suture method offers a viable alternative when anchor pullout occurs during rotator cuff repair. With biomechanical performance similar to ADRSB techniques and the added benefit of avoiding anchor-related complications, OBOX provides a promising option in difficult surgical situations. Nonetheless, further research is needed to assess long-term outcomes and determine its broader clinical utility.
