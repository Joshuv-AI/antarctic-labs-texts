Copper-histidine therapy: a targeted metabolic intervention for Menkes disease
Authors: Ailiya Batool, Huzaifa Mahmood, Anmol Awan, Muhammad Zaid Saeed, Afnan Ahmad Qureshi, Muhammad Adnan Qamar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Menkes disease (MD) is a rare, X-linked recessive disorder of copper metabolism, characterized by progressive neurodegeneration, connective tissue abnormalities, and the distinctive phenotype of pili torti. The disease is caused by pathogenic variants in the ATP7A gene, which encodes a copper-transporting P-type ATPase essential for cellular copper efflux and delivery of copper to secreted cuproenzymes. Severe forms of MD typically present in early infancy and are associated with rapid neurological decline and death within the first 3 years of life. Currently, therapeutic options are limited, and outcomes are highly dependent on the timing of intervention. Copper-histidine represents a biologically rational intervention designed to bypass defective intestinal copper transport and restore systemic copper availability. Experimental and clinical observations suggest that early administration may partially correct copper deficiency, improve biochemical parameters, and, in selected cases, alter neurological outcomes and survival. This letter summarizes the pathophysiological basis of MD, the rationale for copper-histidine therapy, and the existing evidence supporting its potential role as a disease-modifying intervention when initiated early in life.

To the Editor,

Menkes disease (MD) is a lethal multisystem disorder of copper metabolism inherited in an X-linked recessive manner and caused by mutations in the ATP7A gene[1]. ATP7A encodes an energy-dependent transmembrane copper transporter responsible for exporting copper from cells and delivering it to copper-dependent enzymes. Loss of ATP7A function disrupts intracellular copper trafficking, leading to systemic copper deficiency despite paradoxical copper accumulation in certain tissues. Clinically, MD is characterized by progressive neurodegeneration, connective tissue abnormalities, vascular fragility, seizures, and the characteristic “kinky hair” phenotype. In its classical form, the disease manifests during infancy and is associated with death, often before the age of 3 years[1].

The disturbance of copper homeostasis in MD is complex and tissue specific. Impaired copper export from intestinal epithelial cells leads to reduced systemic absorption, while metallothionein sequestration limits copper toxicity in peripheral tissues[1]. In the brain, however, the consequences are particularly severe. ATP7A plays a critical role in regulating copper transport across the blood–brain and blood–cerebrospinal fluid barriers, and its inactivation results in copper trapping at these interfaces with deprivation of neurons and glial cells[1]. This deficiency contributes to demyelination, impaired mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and altered neurotransmission. Experimental data suggest that ATP7A-mediated copper release is linked to NMDA receptor activation, implicating disrupted synaptic signaling as an additional contributor to seizures and neurodegeneration in MD[1].

Neuroimaging studies further underscore the early and aggressive neurodegenerative course of MD. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrates rapidly progressive cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, basal ganglia involvement, and widespread white matter abnormalities within the first months of life[2]. These changes are largely irreversible and progress despite symptomatic management, reflecting profound metabolic vulnerability of the developing brain. Importantly, neuroradiologic findings indicate that significant brain injury occurs early, emphasizing that therapeutic interventions initiated after clinical onset are unlikely to reverse established damage[2].

Given this pathophysiological framework, copper-histidine therapy has emerged as a targeted metabolic intervention aimed at restoring systemic copper availability. Copper-histidine delivers copper in a bioavailable form that can partially circumvent defective intestinal transport and replenish circulating copper pools. Biochemically, treatment consistently increases serum copper and ceruloplasmin concentrations, reflecting improved copper delivery to the circulation[3]. From a molecular perspective, copper(II)-histidine represents an exchangeable copper complex in equilibrium with plasma proteins, allowing controlled copper distribution while minimizing the generation of toxic free copper species[4].

Clinical observations suggest that the timing of copper-histidine administration is critical. Retrospective analyses indicate that treatment initiated during the neonatal period, before the onset of overt neurological symptoms, is associated with improved survival and, in selected cases, preservation of motor function and seizure control[5]. In contrast, treatment initiated after neurological deterioration has begun appears far less effective, likely due to irreversible neurodegenerative and neurochemical changes that have already occurred. Importantly, early clinical signs of MD – such as abnormal hair, prolonged neonatal jaundice, and feeding difficulties – are often nonspecific and frequently fail to prompt timely diagnosis, contributing to delays in treatment initiation[5].

Despite these encouraging observations, the clinical response to copper-histidine therapy is not uniform. The phenotypic spectrum of MD is broad, ranging from severe infantile neurodegeneration to milder variants with predominantly connective tissue manifestations. Variability in clinical response likely reflects underlying genetic heterogeneity, residual ATP7A activity, and differential capacity for copper transport at the cellular level[3]. Consequently, while some patients demonstrate meaningful neurological stabilization or prolonged survival, others show biochemical improvement without clear neurological benefit.

Taken together, the available evidence suggests that copper-histidine therapy addresses a fundamental metabolic defect in MD and has the potential to modify disease trajectory when administered sufficiently early. However, its effectiveness appears constrained by narrow therapeutic timing and significant interindividual variability. These observations underscore the urgent need for effective newborn screening strategies, earlier diagnosis, and improved genotype–phenotype correlation to identify patients most likely to benefit from intervention[3,5].

In conclusion, copper-histidine represents a biologically sound and clinically impactful intervention for MD, particularly when initiated in the presymptomatic or early symptomatic phase. While it does not constitute a universal cure, its ability to alter survival and neurological outcomes in selected patients highlights the importance of early metabolic correction in this devastating disorder. Continued efforts toward early detection and refined therapeutic strategies are essential to further improve outcomes in MD.
