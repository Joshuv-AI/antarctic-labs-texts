Thermal and shear stress-induced phenotypic changes in Bacillus paralicheniformis TB197 in submerged liquid cultures
Authors: Estefany Chavarria-Quicaño, Miguel A Hernández-Oñate, Jorge A Rodríguez-González, Francisco De la Torre-González, Ali Asaff-Torres
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractLipopeptides (LPs) are secondary metabolites predominantly produced by Bacillus species and have significant industrial applications. The synthesis of these compounds is regulated by quorum sensing systems, which are activated in response to abiotic stress. This study examined the impact of heat and shear stresses on the growth, LPs production, and phenotype formation of the Bacillus paralicheniformis TB197 strain in controlled submerged cultures. A differential expression analysis was performed on genes associated with phenotypes, including biofilms (tasA), motility (hag), sporulation (sspB), and the production of lipopeptides (fenB and lichA). The findings demonstrated that, under conditions of 30 °C, an increase in agitation levels from 300 to 600 rpm resulted in a 1.5-fold enhancement in cell concentration and a 2.8-fold increase in LPs production. At 40 °C, these values were 2.80- and 2.06-fold, respectively. Furthermore, fenB and lichA expression levels increased by 17- and 36-fold, respectively, suggesting that the combined stress of 40 °C and 600 rpm promotes the development of LP-producing phenotypes. However, at 45 °C, although cell concentration showed no difference compared to 40 °C, LPs production decreased 2.49-fold, with reductions in lichA (sixfold) and fenB (65-fold) expression. Under these conditions, a predominance of sporulating and biofilm-forming phenotypes was observed, supported by increased expression of tasA and sspB genes. These findings show that thermal and mechanical stresses differentially modulate the expression of phenotypes and the production of LPs in B. paralicheniformis TB197.Key points• Shear and thermal stress differentially modulate Bacillus paralicheniformis TB197 phenotypes.• Conditions of 40°C and 600 rpm induce lipopeptide-producing phenotypes, thereby optimizing their biosynthesis.• Shear and thermal stress suppress flagellate phenotypes while promoting sporulation and biofilm formation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13571-6.Keywords: Lipopeptides, Bacillus-cell phenotypes, Biofilms, Spores, Flagella

Introduction

Lipopeptides (LPs) are secondary metabolites primarily synthesized by Bacillus species and are classified into four families: surfactin, iturin, fengycin, and bacillomycin (Rangarajan et al. 2015). These molecules have molecular weights ranging from 900 to 1600 Da and feature a chemical structure that includes a hydrophilic peptide ring covalently bonded to a hydrophobic fatty acid chain (Kurniawan et al. 2018). Due to their amphiphilic nature and diversity, LPs exhibit a wide range of biological activities, including antifungal (Xiao et al. 2021), antibacterial (Hafez et al. 2020), antiviral (Yuan et al. 2018), antitumoral (Zhou et al. 2018), anti-inflammatory (Zhang et al. 2015), insecticidal (Wang et al. 2023), and nematicidal (Nadeem et al. 2021) effects. Furthermore, LPs possess emulsifying properties, making them valuable in the food industry (Kiran et al. 2017), in bioremediation processes for petroleum-contaminated soils (Farias et al. 2018), and in the degradation of polyethylene (Nehal and Singh 2021). Given their current and potential applications across various industrial sectors, including agriculture, research into lipopeptide production has recently gained significant importance (Geissler et al. 2019).

The main strategies for achieving high yields in LPs production include optimizing culture media and/or culture conditions, as well as using genetically modified microorganisms (GMMs) as effective tools (Wang et al. 2023). Optimization efforts focus on adjusting carbon and nitrogen sources and incorporating trace elements, such as MOLP (Medium Optimal for Lipopeptide Production), developed through response surface methodologies (Sharma et al. 2020; Satya Eswari et al. 2016; Gu et al. 2005). Other studies emphasize the optimization of critical culture parameters, including pH, aeration, agitation, and temperature in batch culture bioreactors. Pretorius et al. (2015) and Klausmann et al. (2021) reported that surfactin production can increase with high agitation speeds if appropriate strategies such as using reactors with foam traps and/or defoamers, are implemented, since foam accumulation reduces LPs titers (Yeh et al. 2005). Regarding temperature, most research suggests that the optimal range for lipopeptide production lies between 25 °C and 30 °C, although this can vary depending on the Bacillus species and even the strain (Wang et al. 2023; Jiang et al. 2022). Some studies indicate that temperatures above 37 °C enhance surfactin production by B. subtilis RB14 (Jacques 2011) and lichenysin synthesis by B. licheniformis (Jacques 2011).

On the other hand, the synthesis of LPs in the Bacillus genus relies on quorum sensing (QS), a tightly regulated mechanism of intercellular communication that enables microorganisms to adapt their development to environmental conditions and population density (Bin et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2020). This mechanism involves the secretion and detection of specific pheromone-type compounds in the surrounding environment, which accumulate in proportion to the number of congeneric bacteria (Bareia et al. 2018). Once the minimum number of participating members is reached, a sequence of synchronized events is triggered at the molecular and genetic levels, such as the activation of the surfactin operon (Jacques 2011). Three QS signaling systems (ComQXP, Rap-Phr, and DegQ) regulate the expression of the surfactin (srfA) operon and play a crucial role in protecting cells against various environmental stresses, including nutrient starvation, extreme temperatures, pH fluctuations, oxygen availability, exposure to heavy metals, and mechanical stresses, as well as in coexisting with other microbial populations (Qi et al. 2023; Bareia et al. 2018; García-Contreras et al. 2015). Depending on bacterial population density and environmental conditions, these systems activate different survival strategies to ensure the integrity of the bacterial community and/or bacterial survival (Lyons and Kolter 2015; Kalamara and Spacapan 2018).

Traditionally, all cells within bacterial colonies have been considered to perform homogeneous or collaborative tasks. However, recent studies provide a new perspective, suggesting that QS systems are associated with cellular differentiation (Bettenworth et al. 2019), leading to phenotypes such as spores, motile bacteria (flagellated), biofilm-forming cells, and lipopeptide-producing cells (Mielich-Süss and Lopez 2015; Bin et al. 2021). Despite these advancements, our understanding of how these phenotypes can be induced or modulated by process parameters remains limited. For instance, some reports offer insights into how Bacillus phenotypes vary based on carbon and nitrogen sources in solid cultures (Petri dishes), focusing on colony architecture and biofilm formation (Mielich-Süss and Lopez 2015; Wang et al. 2016; Martin et al. 2020), but they do not consider the potential relationship between phenotypes and LPs production. Under submerged conditions, the influence of medium composition and culture conditions on Bacillus phenotypes remains poorly understood (Rangarajan et al. 2015).

Bacillus paralicheniformis TB197 is a native strain from the Sonoran Desert that exhibits high potential for controlling phytopathogenic nematodes (Chavarria-Quicaño et al. 2023b). This strain has been shown to produce LPs, primarily from the lichenysin and fengycin families, and their combination demonstrates an even greater capacity to control nematodes than the LPs synthesized by B. subtilis (Chavarria-Quicaño et al. 2023a). Given the significance of these molecules in agriculture and their numerous potential applications, investigating the regulatory systems that govern the biosynthetic pathways is essential for developing strategies to enhance LPs production.

This study evaluated how shear and thermal stress induce and modulate the formation of phenotypes in the Bacillus paralicheniformis TB197 strain in submerged cultures, aiming to identify conditions that promote LP-producing phenotypes and enhance their production. Additionally, to analyze the relationship between phenotype and genotype, the differential expression of specific genes associated with distinct phenotypes was measured at different times. These genes include the amyloid protein (tasA) gene related to biofilm-producing cells (Cámara-Almirón et al. 2020), the flagellin protein (hag) gene associated with flagellated cells (Molière et al. 2016), the small soluble acidic protein B (sspB) gene linked to spores (Schoenborn et al. 2021), as well as genes related to LP-producing cells, such as the fengycin B synthetase (fenB) and lichenysin A synthetase (lichA) genes. Understanding the exceptional flexibility and adaptability of members of the Bacillus genus, particularly the B. paralicheniformis TB197 strain, to environmental conditions through the development of targeted cellular phenotypes related to LPs production opens up new avenues for microbial engineering via precision fermentation to enhance the production of specific compounds.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Malt extract, yeast extract, peptone, and bacteriological agar were purchased from BD Difco (Sparks, MD, USA). KH2PO4, MgSO4, CuSO4, FeCl3.6H2O, Na2MoO4, KI, MnSO4. H2O, MgSO4, ZnSO4.7H2O, H3BO3, methanol, acetonitrile, isopropanol, chloroform, and hydrochloric acid were provided by J.T. Baker (Xalostoc, Edo. Mex., Mexico). Saccharose, NaCl, citric acid, and CaCl2 were obtained from Faga Lab (Guamúchil, Sin., México).

Strain and inoculum conditions

Bacillus paralicheniformis TB197 strain (in deposit at CNRG, México; an international public culture collection, CM-CNRGTB197) was cultivated on ISP2 agar (10 g/L malt extract, 4 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L glucose, and 20 g/L bacteriological agar) and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Then, two individual colonies were selected and transferred into 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL of MOLP broth, an optimized medium for lipopeptide production composed of 30 g/L peptone, 7 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L saccharose, 1.9 g/L KH2PO4, 0.001 mg/L CuSO4, 0.005 mg/L FeCl3.6H2O, 0.004 mg/L Na2MoO4, 0.002 mg/L KI, 3.6 mg/L MnSO4. H2O, 0.45 g/L MgSO4, 0.14 mg/L ZnSO4.7H2O, 0.01 mg/L H3BO3, and 10 mg/L citric acid (pH 7) (Gu et al. 2005). Bacterial cultures were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 180 rpm (NewBrunswick Scientific, Innova 44).

Batch fermentation

Batch cultures of B. paralicheniformis TB197 strain were developed simultaneously in a twin-automated 3 L stirred tank bioreactor system (Applikon my-Control; New Brunswick, NJ, USA, FPGA vs 1.0). Each bioreactor, containing 1.5 L of MOLP medium, was sterilized at 120 °C for 20 min and then cooled to the working temperature. Afterward, 45 mL of inoculum was added, while maintaining a constant pH of 6.5 by adding 4 M NaOH or 4 M H3PO4 solutions and aerating at one vvm. To prevent foam formation, a sterile 5% (v/v) defoamer (SAG 1572) was used. Four treatments at 30 °C (300 rpm), 30 °C, 40 °C, and 45 °C, all at 600 rpm, were evaluated independently. The fermentation period lasted 84 h, with sampling at 12-h intervals (25 mL each) for cell mass and phenotypic analyses, total lipopeptide production, and gene differential expression analysis.

RNA isolation and complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis

According to the manufacturer's instructions, total RNA from the fermented samples was isolated using a Trizol MaxTM Bacterial isolation kit (Ambion, USA). RNA concentration was determined by the A260 nm, and purity by the A260 nm/A280 nm ratio using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The RNA integrity was verified by horizontal 1% agarose gel electrophoresis with gel red as a fluorescence marker. To eliminate residual genomic DNA, DNAse I (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) was used to treat the total RNA (Supplementary Figures 3 and 4). Three cDNA synthesis reactions were performed for each RNA sample using the Quantitec Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, CA, USA) with 1000 ng of total RNA. The concentration and purity of the synthesized cDNA were determined by measuring the absorbance at 260/280 nm (NanoDrop 2000).

Relative phenotype expression analysis

The primers listed in Table 1 were designed using the Primer Designing Tool available on NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) with the Bacillus paralicheniformis whole genome database, Bac84 (Accession number: CP023665.1). The expression levels of fenB (fengycin B synthetase gene), lichA (lichenysin A synthetase), hag (flagellin protein gene), tasA (tasA amyloid protein gene), and sspB (small soluble acidic protein B gene) were evaluated through real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) using gene-specific primers. The gyrA gene, which encodes the gyrase DNA protein, was used to normalize the relative expression data, as it has been reported as a housekeeping gene and internal standard in Bacillus species (Rocha et al. 2015). For each cDNA, three qPCR reactions were performed in a final volume of 15 µL, mixing 1 µL of cDNA (500 ng), 0.5 µM (fenB, lichA, and hag) and 0.25 µM (tasA, sspB, and gyrA) of each primer, 7.5 µl of 2X SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA), and the final volume was adjusted with nuclease-free water. qPCR analysis was conducted using a QIAquant real-time PCR cycler 96 2plex (Qiagen, Netherlands, Germany). The detector was SYBR Green, and the fluorescence value was recorded during the threshold cycle (CT). The cycling conditions were 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s, and 65 °C for 60 s, with fluorescence detection at each extension step for every reaction. At the end of the program, a melting curve analysis was performed, ranging from 65 to 95 °C, with fluorescence data acquired every 0.3 °C, to confirm the specificity of the primers. The efficiency of reactions was determined by running standard curves from 1 to 1000 ng of cDNA.

Analytical methods

Cell mass

Twenty milliliters of fermented broth collected from the bioreactor were centrifuged at 10,034 × g (Eppendorf 5804R) for 30 min. The supernatant was collected and stored at 4 °C for lipopeptide analysis. The pellet was washed, resuspended in water, and centrifuged at 10,034 × g for 30 min. Finally, the pellet was transferred to a dish and dried at 100 °C until constant weight. The cell mass was reported in g/L.

Production of spores

Three milliliters of culture were collected from the bioreactors at preselected time intervals (24, 48, and 72 h). A heat treatment was then applied by exposing the culture to 80 °C for 15 min to selectively eliminate vegetative cells, following the procedure described by Vignesh et al. (2022). Subsequently, 1 mL of the samples was serially diluted (up to 10–7) and plated onto Petri dishes containing nutrient agar. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and spores were counted (CFU/mL). The results were expressed as specific spore productivity (SPOsp) [(SPOsp) = CFU spores/g biomass].

Total lipopeptides extract (TLP)

Lipopeptides were extracted and measured according to the methodology proposed by Yang et al. (2015) with some modifications. The pH of the collected supernatant was adjusted to 2.0 using a 6 M HCl solution and stored refrigerated overnight at 4 °C. It was subsequently centrifuged at 10,034 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet containing crude lipopeptides was dissolved in 10 mL of methanol and sonicated for 60 min. Insoluble material was removed by centrifugation (10,034 × g, 30 min), and the methanol was evaporated at 60 °C in a drying oven. The total crude lipopeptide extract was reported in grams per liter.

Reverse phase High-Pressure Liquid Chromatography- Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry Analysis (RP-HPLC ESI–MS)

The RP-HPLC ESI–MS analysis was performed using conditions reported by Yang et al. (2015) and Hamley et al. (2013) with some modifications. The total lipopeptide extracts were dissolved in methanol (0.25 g/L) and filtered through a 0.2 μm membrane filter. HPLC ESI–MS analyses were conducted with a Thermo Spectra HPLC system (USA) equipped with a Hypersil GOLD aQ RP18 column (Agilent, USA; 5 μm, 250 mm × 4.6 mm). Water and methanol containing 0.1% formic acid (FA) served as mobile phases The non-isocratic elution was performed as follows: 0–3 min, 70% methanol to 75% methanol; 3–8 min, 75% methanol to 85% methanol; and 8–30 min, 85% methanol to 95% methanol. The total flow rate of the mobile phases was maintained at 0.8 mL/min, and the chromatograms were obtained at 254 nm. An HPLC ESI–MS full scan in positive mode was performed from m/z 50 to 2,000 with a resolution of 30,000. Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted at a temperature of 275 °C, a voltage of 9 V, a tube lens voltage of 95 V, and an ion spray voltage of 3.3 kV. Nitrogen and helium were used as sheath and auxiliary gases, with flow rates of 25 and 5 arbitrary units, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Three biological replicates of the experiments were conducted. Kinetic parameters were calculated as the mean ± confidence interval and compared using the Tukey–Kramer test with a 95% confidence interval. Normality data and variance equality of the relative expression of the genes (fenB, lichA, tasA, hag, and sspB) were determined by the D'Agostino-Pearson Kurtosis test. The statistical differences in gene expression among each temperature over time were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the means of relative expression were compared using the Tukey–Kramer test with a 95% confidence limit. All statistical analyses were conducted using NCSS (Number Cruncher Statistical System for Windows, Kaysville, UT, USA, version 2023).

Results

Cell mass and total lipopeptide production

Figure 1 illustrates how temperature and agitation rates influence the growth rate of the B. paralicheniformis TB197 strain (Fig. 1a) and total lipopeptide production (Fig. 1b). The growth profile at 45 °C (600 rpm) shows that the maximum biomass concentration was reached at 36 h; afterward, it declined, likely due to biofilm formation on the bioreactor walls, as well as on the sensors and agitation system (including baffles and the Rushton impeller), as visually observed (Supplementary Fig. 5). It is important to note that after 36 h of culture, biofilm-forming cells may not have been present in the bulk liquid-phase samples, which could lead to an underestimation of total biomass. Therefore, under these culture conditions, growth kinetic modeling, using the 4PL model, incorporated experimental data up to 36 h when biofilm formation was minimal and maximum biomass was achieved. At 40 °C, 30 °C (600 rpm), and 30 °C (300 rpm), the maximum biomass concentration was reached around 48 h and remained constant until the end of fermentation (84 h). At 40 °C, some biofilm formation was observed on the bioreactor walls, while at 30 °C (regardless of the agitation speed), biofilms were absent (Supplementary Fig. 5).

A similar pattern was observed in total LPs production, which reached the stationary phase at the same time as bacterial growth. The highest total LPs production value occurred during fermentation at 40 °C, which was 2.5-fold higher than the average titers obtained at 30 °C (600 rpm) and 45 °C (showing no significant differences between them), and 4.3-fold higher than that achieved at 30 °C, 300 rpm (p ≤ 0.05). In all experiments, dissolved oxygen (DO) was continuously monitored in real time, and no values below 30% were recorded (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7).

The estimated kinetic parameters are summarized in Table 2. The experimental data from all treatments were well-fitted by the 4PL model, with R2 values exceeding 93%, demonstrating that this model adequately describes bacterial growth. The highest maximum biomass (xmax) values were recorded at 40 and 45 °C, averaging 13.91 g/L, with no statistical differences between them (based on fiducial limits). However, these values were 1.5-fold and 2.7-fold higher than those achieved at 30 °C at 600 rpm and 300 rpm, respectively (p ≤ 0.05). While no differences (p ≥ 0.05) were found among the maximum specific growth rate values (µmax), numerically, the lowest µmax was associated with fermentation conducted at 30 °C and 300 rpm (1.54-fold lower than the average of the other conditions). Conversely, the experimental data for LPs production were fitted using the Luedeking-Piret model, which adequately described (R2 ≥ 0.84) LPs biosynthesis. The growth-associated coefficients (α) were significantly higher (p ≤ 0.05) than the non-growth-associated coefficients (β) across all cases, indicating that LPs production is primarily linked to bacterial growth.

Gene expression profile associated with bacterial phenotypes

This study examined the effect of temperature and agitation on the relative expression of genes linked to specific phenotypes, including lipopeptide production (fenB and lichA), biofilm formation (tasA), flagellar motility (hag), and sporulation (sspB). The results are displayed in Fig. 3, with heat maps provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. To assess gene expression throughout fermentation, the relative expression of these genes was measured at four stages of bacterial growth: 12 h (mid-exponential phase), 24 h (early stationary phase), 48 h (stationary phase), and 72 h (late-stationary phase or decline phase).

Relative expression of the fenB, lichA, tasA, sspB, and hag genes under different culture conditions over time. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three biological replicates, each with three technical repeats. Lowercase letters indicate statistically significant differences in gene expression between time points within the same temperature condition, as determined by the Tukey–Kramer test (p ≤ 0.05). The asterisk (*) on the 30 °C label signifies that cultures were shaken at 300 rpm, while all other cultures were shaken at 600 rpm

Figure 3a illustrates the relative expression of the fenB gene under the four conditions evaluated in this study, showing distinct expression patterns. At 30 °C (300 rpm), the highest relative expression was observed at 24 h, with values 3.15-fold, 4.3-fold, and 107-fold greater than those at 12, 48, and 72 h, respectively (p ≤ 0.05). Similarly, at 45 °C (600 rpm), the maximum expression occurred at 24 h, which was 8.3-fold higher than at 12 and 48 h, and 140-fold higher than at 72 h (p ≤ 0.05). At 30 °C (600 rpm), peak expression occurred at 48 h, representing values 9.75-fold, 1.4-fold, and 25-fold greater than those obtained at 12, 24, and 72 h, respectively (p ≤ 0.05). Interestingly, at 40 °C (600 rpm), the expression pattern was notably different from the other conditions, with the highest expression level occurring at the mid-exponential phase (12 h), resulting in values 12.31-fold, 41.91-fold, and 89.2-fold higher than those at 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively (p ≤ 0.05). Notably, under this condition, the highest value was 10–15-fold greater than the maximal values obtained in the other conditions.

The relative expression of the lichA gene (Fig. 3b) remained constant (p ≥ 0.05) throughout microbial growth at 40 °C (600 rpm), and 45 °C (600 rpm). However, at 40 °C, the expression level was 9.7-fold and 646-fold higher than those recorded at 45 and 30 °C (300 rpm), respectively (p ≤ 0.05). In contrast, a divergent pattern was observed at 30 °C (600 rpm); at 12 and 24 h, the relative expression levels were similar but 17.3-fold higher (p ≤ 0.05) than the values recorded at 48 and 72 h (with no differences between them), yet 37.3-fold lower than the maximum value observed at 40 °C (600 rpm) (p ≤ 0.05).

In the case of the relative expression of the tasA gene (Fig. 3c), a similar pattern was generally observed, with maximal expression at 12 h under all culture conditions, decreasing over the fermentation time. Except for the 30 °C (600 rpm) condition, the highest value was noted at 24 h, which was 1400-fold higher than the minimum value at 72 h. However, at 45 °C (600 rpm), the maximum value was approximately sevenfold higher than the maximum values seen for the other cultures and 415-fold higher than the minimum values recorded at 72 h (p ≤ 0.05).

The relative expression of the sspB gene (Fig. 3d) in cells cultured at 30 °C (300 rpm), 40 °C (600 rpm), and 45 °C (600 rpm) exhibited asymmetric Gaussian patterns, with maximum values observed between 24 and 48 h. In contrast, a random pattern was noted at 30 °C (600 rpm). The peak relative expression level occurred at 45 °C (600 rpm) at 48 h, which was 4-, 11-, and 51-fold higher than the maximum values observed at 40 °C (600 rpm) at 24 h, 30 °C (600 rpm) at 72 h, and 30 °C (300 rpm) at 24 h, respectively (p ≤ 0.05).

Finally, the relative expression of the hag gene (Fig. 3e) was observed only in cells cultured at 30 °C, with the highest levels reached at 12 h. Under low agitation conditions (300 rpm), the maximum value was 25- and 250-fold higher than those obtained at 24 and 48 h, respectively, and 12.6-fold higher than that observed at 600 rpm at 12 h (p ≤ 0.05).

Identification of lipopeptide variants under different culture conditions

Table 3 displays the molecular masses, retention times, and variants of the lipopeptide families. Chromatograms of total lipopeptide extracts obtained from the fermentation of TB197 at 30 °C (300 rpm), as well as at 30, 40, and 45 °C (600 rpm), are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Approximately 16 distinct lipopeptides were identified. Peaks 1 to 8 correspond to variants of the fengycin family, peaks 11 and 12 to variants of the surfactin family, and peaks 13 to 16 to the lichenysin family. Notably, the lipopeptide extract obtained from the fermentation broth at 45 °C exhibited ion fractionation patterns consistent with a fengycin A C19 variant, as Mnif et al. (2023) described. An unidentified compound was also observed in the supernatant from the culture at 30 °C (600 rpm) (peak 4), which was not detected at higher temperatures. The molecular mass and mass spectra of the lipopeptide variants identified in this study aligned with the results of previous studies conducted by our research team (Chavarria-Quicaño et al. 2023b), as well as with the findings of Yang et al. (2015), Li et al. (2008), and Yakimov et al. (1999).

Discussion

The present work describes the impact of changes in temperature and agitation on the formation of phenotypes (spore, biofilm-forming, flagellated, and lipopeptide-producing cells) of the native Bacillus paralicheniformis TB197 strain in controlled submerged cultures. Furthermore, a phenotype/genotype analysis aims to determine the correlation between these phenotypes and the production of LPs regarding both quantity and composition. The abundance of lipopeptide-producing phenotypes was estimated by analyzing the biosynthetic genes fenB and lichA, which encode the lipopeptide synthetases fengycin B and lichenysin A, respectively, since strain TB197 produces both families of LPs (Chavarria-Quicaño et al. 2023a). The expression of the tasA gene, which encodes a crucial amyloid protein for maintaining biofilm stability, was also assessed for biofilm-forming phenotypes (Cámara-Almirón et al. 2020). The hag gene, which encodes the flagellin protein, was employed to identify flagellated phenotypes, as this protein constitutes the primary structural subunit of the flagellum in Bacillus species (Molière et al. 2016). Finally, the expression of the sspB gene, which is involved in sporulation, was evaluated as a marker of this process. This gene encodes a small, acid-soluble spore protein that plays a crucial role in spore formation and stability in Bacillus species (Schoenborn et al. 2021).

The lowest temperature and agitation conditions (30 °C and 300 rpm), along with culture parameters such as aeration (1 vvm) and pH (6.5), were selected based on the most documented average conditions in the literature for lipopeptide (LPs) production by Bacillus species (Chen et al. 2015; Yeh et al. 2005; Biniarz et al. 2020; Guez et al. 2021). Considering that the B. paralicheniformis TB197 strain was isolated from a desertic area characterized by extreme environmental conditions (Chavarria-Quicaño et al. 2023b), higher temperatures (40 and 45 °C) and agitation rates (600 rpm) were also tested.

As Fig. (1) illustrates, at 30 °C, increasing the agitation rate from 300 to 600 rpm led to a 1.5-fold increase in biomass and a 2.08-fold increase in the production of LPs. It has previously been reported that high agitation in submerged culture systems promotes biomass formation and LPs production in Bacillus species, as it facilitates oxygen transfer and uniform nutrient distribution (Rangarajan et al. 2015).

However, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, both cases demonstrated a sharp decrease in DO (%) during the initial 12–16 h, a period characterized by bacterial exponential growth. Interestingly, the minimal DO reached at that point correlates with the time at the inflexion point (tif) of the exponential growth (Longhi et al. 2017). Afterwards, the growth rate slows down and DO begins to increase. In any case, the minimal DO remained above 30%, indicating that oxygen limitation did not occur during fermentation, as the critical oxygen threshold reported for Bacillus species ranges from 5 to 10% (Liu 2017). Remarkably, hag gene expression occurred only at 30 °C; however, at 300 rpm, it was 12.6-fold higher than that observed at 600 rpm at the same time (12 h). Although reduced, its expression continued at 24 and 48 h, while it did not persist at 600 rpm. It seems that differences in biomass production are closely related to the phenotype, with flagellated cells possibly reproducing more slowly than non-motile cells. This phenomenon, as well as LP production, appear to be influenced by shear stress rather than oxygen availability. The fenB gene and the lichA gene also responded to stressful factors, showing significant increases in expression of up to fourfold (48 h) and tenfold (24 h), respectively, higher in cultures at 600 rpm than those at 300 rpm. Additionally, the results of this study demonstrate that shear stress significantly affects the synthesis of lipopeptide variants by the TB197 strain. At 600 rpm, in addition to an overall increase in LPs production, there was a rise in LPs variant formation, with 9 distinct lipopeptides detected. In contrast, at 300 rpm, only 6 variants were identified.

Agitation and the resulting shear stress significantly affected spore formation as well (Fig. 2). At 72 h and 600 rpm, specific spore productivity (SPOSP) was nearly tenfold higher than at 300 rpm. Correspondingly, the expression of the sspB gene at 600 rpm was substantially greater at any time compared to 300 rpm (Fig. 3d). The literature on this subject is diverse and extensive. While some studies report that an increase in agitation induces sporulation (Posada-Uribe et al. 2015), others suggest that shear stress inhibits spore formation (Elisashvili et al. 2019; Lemos et al. 2015). These discrepancies may relate to the intrinsic adaptive capacity of species and even specific Bacillus strains.

Regarding the temperature effect, the study's results demonstrated a significant increase in temperature-induced LPs production at 40 °C compared to 30 °C, with the same agitation conditions (600 rpm). Specifically, there was a 1.6-fold increase in cell mass concentration and a 2.1-fold increase in LPs titer. Notably, the LPs titers achieved at 40 °C (3.15 g/L) were remarkably high for wild-type strains (Ali et al. 2022). These findings are consistent with earlier reports (Ghribi and Ellouze-Chaabouni 2011; Coronel-León et al. 2015), which documented a correlation between increased cell growth and LPs production, suggesting that their synthesis is associated with microbial growth. Indeed, at 40 °C, the coefficient alpha was 257-fold higher than the coefficient beta (Table 2), indicating that LPs production was primarily linked to biomass growth. Interestingly, although there was no significant difference in biomass concentrations at 40 °C and 45 °C, LPs production was negatively affected at the higher temperature (2.5-fold lower), despite the α/β ratio being similar to that estimated at 40 °C (Table 2). Similarly, the estimated LPSP at 40 °C and 48 h (0.21) was threefold higher than at 45 °C at the same time (0.07). This indicates that at 45 °C, while total biomass production was similar, fewer LP-producer cell phenotypes were formed. Additionally, a clear correlation was observed between the increase in temperature and spore formation (Fig. 2), with a 19- and 42.4-fold increase in specific spore productivity (SPOSP) in cultures at 40 and 45 °C, respectively, compared to 30 °C (600 rpm).

Although the fenB gene and the lichA gene, both involved in lipopeptide biosynthesis, respond to shear stress, as previously mentioned, temperature had an even more pronounced effect on their expression, with maximum levels observed at 40 and 45 °C. Interestingly, these genes showed significant differences in their expression patterns in response to temperature and culture time (Fig. 3a and b). The highest expression of the fenB gene occurred at 12 h in cells growing at 40 °C, reaching levels 117-fold and 49-fold higher than those observed at 30 °C and 45 °C, respectively. Similarly, the highest expression of the lichA gene was found in cells cultured at 40 °C (showing no significant differences based on culture time), approximately 18-fold greater than in cells growing at 45 °C (with no significant differences according to culture time) and 38-fold higher than those at 30 °C (600 rpm) after 12 or 24 h of culture. In contrast to cultures at 40 and 45 °C, where the expression of the lichA gene remained unchanged throughout fermentation, at 30 °C, the relative expression level dropped eightfold at 48 and 72 h.

The factors regulating the expression of fengycin synthase genes are an underexplored area of research. It is known that the pleiotropic regulator DegQ induces the expression of genes related to fengycin synthesis, and that its overexpression correlates with increased production of this lipopeptide (Wang et al. 2015). The DegQ protein is part of the quorum sensing system in Bacillus, and its expression is induced in response to environmental stress (Wang et al. 2015). This suggests that stressful factors such as high temperatures and shear, associated with high agitation rates (40 °C and 600 rpm), induce DegQ expression in LP-producer cells, leading to enhanced fengycin production. This phenomenon was previously observed by Ha et al. (2018), who reported a 1.36-fold increase in surfactin production when agitation was increased from 300 to 500 rpm. However, at 45 °C, other factors, such as spores and biofilm-forming cells, appear to govern the system.

Lichenysin synthases are responsible for synthesizing lichenysins and surfactins (Tapi et al. 2010), which are secondary metabolites that, unlike primary metabolites, are not essential for cell survival. However, the high and consistent expression of the lichA gene at elevated temperatures suggests that these compounds may be crucial for cell survival under stress conditions. While further validation is needed to support this hypothesis, surfactins, in addition to their inhibitory and competitive functions, have been shown to act as signaling molecules in quorum sensing systems, promoting cell differentiation under stress (Qi et al. 2023; Schoenborn et al. 2021). The results of this study indicate that molecules belonging to the lichenysin family may also act as cell signaling molecules, as a higher abundance of biofilm- and spore-forming phenotypes was observed in cultures at 45 °C (Figs. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 5).

Interestingly, the results of this study show that temperature modulates the synthesis of lipopeptide variants by the TB197 strain as well. At 40 °C, alongside an overall increase in LPs production, a rise in LPs variant formation was observed, with 14 distinct lipopeptides detected. Conversely, at 30 and 45 °C (600 rpm), only 9 and 13 variants were identified, respectively (Table 3, Supplementary Fig. 2). Despite the relatively limited number of variants identified in these treatments, some interesting observations should be noted. For example, a new potential variant of the fengycin family (peak 3; Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2), namely fengycin A C19, was evident (Mnif et al. 2023). Our previous studies did not detect this variant (Chavarria-Quicaño et al. 2023a). Additionally, the C12 variant of the lichenysin family was unique to the 40 °C treatment. It has been previously reported that specific carbon sources may influence the production of certain LPs variants over others (Bartal et al. 2018). However, there is currently no evidence to suggest that agitation and temperature play a role in the synthesis or inhibition of specific LPs variants. Therefore, this study contributes novel insights to the field, as it is the first to demonstrate that temperature can affect the synthesis and diversification of lipopeptide variants in controlled liquid cultures of strain TB197.

On the other hand, to evaluate the prevalence of biofilm-producing phenotypes, the relative expression of the tasA gene was analyzed (Fig. 3c). The expression of the tasA gene was strongly induced by temperature, showing a 4.3- and 33-fold increase at 40 and 45 °C, respectively, compared to 30 °C (600 rpm; 12 h). These genotypic results suggested that higher temperatures, such as 45 °C, promoted biofilm formation (a phenotypic response), visible on the bioreactor walls and agitation components as abundant, yellowish, robust structures (Supplementary Fig. 5). Other researchers have previously documented the impact of temperature on biofilm formation in Bacillus species (Liu et al. 2023). However, most studies have primarily focused on biofilm formation in solid cultures, emphasizing the characteristics of colonies and biofilms. Elhariry (2014) reported that B. coagulans and B. stearothermophilus strains exhibited enhanced biofilm formation at 45 °C when grown on solid media. The resulting colonies were significantly larger and more robust than those formed at 37 °C. A comparable phenomenon was documented by Rajitha et al. (2021) in B. haynesii strains cultivated at 45 °C, where enhanced amyloid protein formation and increased colony size and roughness were observed.

In alignment with these findings, the results of this study illustrate that elevated temperatures in submerged cultures in controlled bioreactors also stimulate biofilm formation. It is important to note that even at 30 °C and 40 °C, the expression of the tasA gene was observed; however, the phenotypic response was minimal, with limited biofilm formation, unlike the results at 45 °C (Supplementary Fig. 5). This outcome may be attributed to the fact that, although tasA was used as a marker gene associated with biofilm-forming phenotypes, it is not the only gene involved in this complex process. Biofilm formation is a multifactorial phenomenon involving multiple genes and regulatory networks; therefore, tasA expression alone does not necessarily ensure the structural development of a biofilm (Diehl et al. 2018). These findings highlight the need for further studies in agitated liquid cultures to better understand the dynamics of gene expression and their relationship with biofilm morphogenesis under varying environmental conditions.

As the tasA gene, the relative expression of the sspB gene, which is related to spore-forming cells, displayed a strong response to stressful factors such as high temperatures (40 and 45 °C; 600 rpm) (Fig. 3d), particularly between the later exponential (24 h) and earlier stationary (48 h) phases. The most significant response occurred at 45 °C and 48 h, being 3.6- and 15.8-fold higher than the maximum relative expression of the sspB gene observed at 24 h in cultures at 40 °C and 30 °C (600 rpm), respectively. As indicated by previous genotypic results, the relative expression patterns of the sspB gene strongly support the phenotypic response shown in Fig. 2. Additionally, Isticato et al. (2020) showed that elevated temperatures enhance sporulation and alter spore structure and functionality, reinforcing our findings. Sporulation in Bacillus species serves as a resistance mechanism typically activated in response to nutrient starvation and abiotic stress, often manifesting during the stationary phase (Elhariry 2014). However, our results indicate that the relative expression of the sspB gene begins as early as the mid-exponential phase (12 h). Since sporulation is an energetically expensive process and is generally considered the final survival strategy, this premature activation suggests that the conditions evaluated were, in some instances, stressful, prompting the cells to initiate sporulation at early growth stages.

Finally, as previously mentioned, relative gene expressions linked to flagellated phenotypes (hag) were only detected in cultures grown at 30 °C (Fig. 3e). The lack of hag gene expression at the highest temperatures (40 and 45 °C) shows that heat stress completely suppresses its expression and, consequently, the development of flagellated phenotypes.

The formation of flagella and biofilms represents a significant energetic cost for Bacillus cells; therefore, a high level of regulation governs the development of these phenotypes (Bucher et al. 2015). Steinberg et al. (2020) indicate that a regulatory switch in Bacillus cells allows for the normalization of biofilm production and repression of motility and vice versa. This regulatory switch primarily depends on two master regulators: the homologous proteins SinR and SlrR. During Bacillus’s planktonic growth, SinR represses the expression of biofilm-forming operons (epsA-O and tapA-sipW-tasA) and the expression of SlrR, leading to the promotion of flagellated phenotypes (Vlamakis et al. 2008). Conversely, once biofilm formation is initiated, the SlrR protein inhibits SinR activity by binding to it and preventing SinR from binding to DNA, thus activating the operons associated with biofilm formation. SlrR, in turn, binds to SinR, forming a heterodimer that represses the fla/che operon, which encodes essential motility components (Steinberg et al. 2020). Other explanations may be associated with the high LPs titers observed at 40 °C, where hag gene expression was undetectable. It has been previously demonstrated that LPs belonging to the surfactin family can inhibit the expression of genes involved in motility (Bin et al. 2021). Many Bacillus species secrete surfactin, reducing the surface tension between bacterial cells and substrates, thereby facilitating bacterial movement on surfaces without flagella (Bin et al. 2021; Luo et al. 2015).

The main results of this work are summarized as follows: (1) Increased agitation and temperature stimulate the growth of the TB197 strain and, to some extent, the production of LPs. (2) The kinetic analysis conducted at 40 and 45 °C revealed comparable biomass concentrations; however, notable differences were observed in LPs production, linked to variations in the cell-phenotype composition of the biomass. At 40 °C, the LPs titer was 3.14 g/L, suggesting that this temperature may promote a higher abundance of LP-specialized phenotypes. (3) Exposure to elevated temperatures induces the formation of sporulating and biofilm-forming phenotypes in strain TB197, indicating adaptive mechanisms that enable this strain to thrive under such conditions. Interestingly, the relative expression analyses of genes associated with specific phenotypes correlated closely with the biomass and LPs production kinetic results, as well as LPs composition. However, since the increase in relative expression level did not correlate with a linear physiological response (e.g., relative expression level of LP-related genes vs LPs titers), translational and/or post-translational regulatory systems and/or even negative feedback loops in enzyme activity may also be involved.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that shear and heat stress play a crucial role in modulating Bacillus paralicheniformis TB197 phenotypes in submerged cultures.

At low temperatures (30 °C), high agitation (600 rpm) enhances lipopeptide production, apparently by promoting the prevalence of non-motile cells due to shear stress, which significantly diminishes the relative expression of the hag gene related to flagellated cells.

Temperature is a key factor because it significantly influences growth, phenotypic diversification, and the synthesis of a wider variety of lipopeptides. Conditions of 40 °C and 600 rpm were identified as optimal for producing highly lipopeptide-producing phenotypes, resulting in a notable increase in the biosynthesis of these industrially significant molecules by wild-type strains. High temperatures, such as 40 and 45 °C, promote the expression of genes related to spore (sspB) and biofilm-forming (tasA) phenotypes. Indeed, in some cases, the early expression of the sspB gene during the exponential phase indicates that the experimental conditions evaluated in this study were stressful for B. paralicheniformis TB197 cells.

Finally, our findings indicate that lipopeptide genes (lichA and fenB) respond differently to temperature stress. The high and stable expression of the lichA gene at elevated temperatures indicates that molecules from the surfactin and lichenysin families may be essential for cell survival under stress conditions, beyond their known roles in signaling for cell diversification. In contrast, although the specific role of fengycins remains unclear, the co-expression pattern between fenB and tasA raises the possibility that fengycins contribute to biofilm architecture or regulation.
