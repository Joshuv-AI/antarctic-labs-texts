Matching-Adjusted Indirect Comparison of the Efficacy and Safety of Difamilast 1% and Delgocitinib 0.5% in Patients with Moderate-to-Severe Atopic Dermatitis
Authors: Takeshi Nakahara, Hiroyuki Murota, Miyuki Matsukawa, Hiroe Takeda, Yilong Zhang, Tomohiro Kondo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic condition with an increasing incidence in Japan. Difamilast and delgocitinib are both new topical drugs for AD proven to be efficacious and safe in phases 2 and 3 clinical trials in Japan. However, there are no head-to-head trials comparing their efficacy and safety. The aim of this study was to determine the proportion of patients by severity and compare the clinical efficacy and safety of difamilast with delgocitinib among patients with moderate-to-severe AD using a matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC).MethodsPhase 3 clinical trials of difamilast and delgocitinib for treating AD were included. The trials had similar designs but differed in baseline population characteristics. Anchored MAIC was used to align the baseline characteristics and calculate clinical outcomes. The primary outcome was to determine severity stages of the proportion of patients with AD through Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI), while the secondary outcome included comparing other clinical efficacy and safety of difamilast with delgocitinib.ResultsA total of 340 patients were selected (170 each received difamilast and placebo) from the difamilast trial, with 158 (106 received delgocitinib; 52 received placebo) from the delgocitinib trial for the analysis. After matching patients from the difamilast trial with those from the delgocitinib trial, the effective sample sizes (ESS) reduced to 32.7–43.3% of the original difamilast (treatment/placebo) patients. At week 4, the ESS in the difamilast group demonstrated no statistically significant differences in the distribution of AD severity stages, as per EASI scores, compared with the delgocitinib group. In addition, no significant differences were found in modified EASI (mEASI) scores, mEASI 50 and 75 scores, and safety outcomes between the two treatments.ConclusionsThe anchored MAIC analysis indicates that difamilast treatment, like delgocitinib, is a useful option for the treatment of patients with moderate-to-severe AD in Japan.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, Delgocitinib, Difamilast, EASI, MAIC

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a long-term disorder that is becoming more common in Japan.Difamilast and delgocitinib are both recent drugs that have shown to be effective and safe in treating Japanese individuals with AD, yet there is a lack of direct comparative trials to evaluate their effectiveness and safety.This research employed a matching-adjusted indirect comparison to assess the clinical effectiveness and safety of difamilast versus delgocitinib in Japanese patients suffering from moderate-to-severe AD. What was learned from the study? The study suggests that individuals receiving difamilast treatment did not show statistically significant differences compared with those receiving delgocitinib in the distribution of AD severity stages within a 4-week period on the basis of Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI) scores.As to the average percentage reduction in modified EASI (mEASI), and the proportion of patients achieving mEASI-50 and mEASI-75 at week 4, there were no notable differences between the two treatments.Difamilast exhibited no statistically significant difference in the safety outcomes compared with delgocitinib.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a widespread chronic inflammatory skin condition characterized by intense pruritus, eczematous lesions, recurrent flare-ups, and periods of remission, affecting an estimated 230 million people worldwide [1, 2]. In Japan, there has been a gradual rise in the incidence of AD in recent years, according to a survey by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (the number of people affected increased from 350,000 in 2008 to 510,000 in 2017) [1]. Also in Japan, about 36% of patients with AD are aged 0–19 years, 44% are from the 20–44-year range, 16% fall into the 45–69-year category, and 4% are 70 years of age or older [1]. The largest proportion of patients with AD in Japan are from the 20–44-year age group [1].

The revised Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Management of Atopic Dermatitis 2021, released after consolidating the practical guidelines from the Japanese Dermatological Association and the Japanese Society of Allergology, indicate that the goal of an AD treatment plan is to achieve and sustain a state in which patients exhibit few or no symptoms, ensuring their daily activities remain unaffected [3–5].

The present approaches for managing AD in Japan encompass: (i) primary management of inflammation through the use of topical corticosteroids, tacrolimus ointment (a topical calcineurin inhibitor), and delgocitinib ointment (a Janus kinase inhibitor [JAKi]); (ii) application of emollients topically to address skin barrier dysfunction; and (iii) psychological counseling and recommendations for daily living and avoidance of apparent exacerbating elements [5, 6].

However, some treatments for AD raise safety concerns with long-term use [7]. An elucidation of the intricate mechanisms of AD has led to the development of new therapies that aim to control inflammation and pruritus with improvements in barrier function while minimizing adverse events (AEs) [8].

Delgocitinib, a JAKi, was first approved in Japan for the treatment of AD in January 2020 [9]. Difamilast, also referred to as OPA-15406 or MM36, is an innovative selective phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4) inhibitor developed by Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., based in Tokyo, Japan, for topical use. It received approval for use in Japan as of September 2021 [10].

Difamilast and delgocitinib are both new pharmaceuticals that have been proven to be efficacious and safe in phases 2 and 3 clinical trials involving Japanese patients with AD [11–15]. However, there are no head-to-head trials comparing their efficacy and safety.

Indirect treatment comparisons aid decision-making, because it is not feasible to conduct randomized controlled trials to determine the comparative treatment effects of alternative interventions. When the baseline characteristics of the patients enrolled in the trials of the two treatments are not similar, but study designs and patient populations are similar, a matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) provides valid relative treatment effects and reduces bias in treatment comparisons by matching patient-level data from one treatment to aggregate data from another treatment [16, 17].

This study explored the relative clinical efficacy and safety of difamilast compared with delgocitinib among patients with AD and the proportion of patients by severity for difamilast and delgocitinib of AD using a MAIC.

Methods

Literature Review

An in-depth review of the published literature identified one phase 3 clinical trial for difamilast [14] and two studies for delgocitinib [11, 18]. Combined data on the baseline characteristics of the delgocitinib arm were obtained from a phase 3 clinical trial [11], and the documented proportion of patients by severity for delgocitinib, as indicated by the Investigator's Global Assessment (IGA) score, was derived from another study [18]. Both phase 3 trials selected for difamilast [14] and delgocitinib [11] had very similar study designs: they were randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trials. However, the baseline characteristics, such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), duration of AD in years, severity of AD, baseline modified Eczema Area and Severity Index (mEASI) score, proportion with prior treatment, and percentage of body surface area (BSA) affected, of their populations varied. Both the phase 3 studies [11, 14] were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and local regulations. The protocols for these two studies were approved by the authorities and the ethics committees of the respective institutions, and signed informed consent was obtained from all patients. Informed consent for this analysis was not required given the use of anonymized patient-level data from previously published articles.

Study Design

MAIC was chosen as the appropriate method for comparison. To ensure comparable baseline characteristics among the difamilast and delgocitinib studies, several latent variables recommended by clinical experts were considered, including age, sex, BMI, the duration of AD in years, baseline IGA scores, baseline mEASI scores, history of prior treatment, and the percentage of BSA affected. Individual patient data for those with moderate-to-severe baseline AD severity in the phase 3 difamilast trial were adjusted to align with those of patients with a baseline IGA score of 3 or 4 in the phase 3 delgocitinib trial. Consequently, the sample size for the targeted difamilast group was recalibrated to a smaller effective sample size (ESS). Referring to the conversion chart by Leshem et al. (2015) [19] (Table 1), the IGA and Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI) scores were translated into the respective AD severity stages. The ESS was then summed up for each AD severity category.

Selection of Baseline Characteristics for Matching

The baseline characteristics used for matching were selected on the basis of their potential influence on key efficacy outcomes (EASI score) and safety outcomes. To balance patients’ baseline characteristics between difamilast and delgocitinib trials, potential effect modifiers were age, sex, BMI, the duration of AD (years), baseline IGA scores, baseline mEASI scores, the proportion with prior treatment, and the percentage of BSA affected. The baseline characteristics, consisting of sex and prior treatment, were reported as the number of patients and corresponding proportions. The continuous baseline characteristics consisting of age, BMI, duration of AD (years), baseline IGA scores, baseline mEASI scores, and the overall percentage of BSA affected were reported as means (standard deviation).

Two scenarios were created, each with a different set of matching variables. Initially, a primary analysis was conducted that replicated all possible matching variables. Subsequently, a sensitivity analysis was performed, selecting only those variables that had a statistically significant (p value less than 0.05) effect on the crucial clinical outcomes, namely, the EASI score at week 4, as well as the safety outcome, which is reported as number of patients experiencing AEs within the first 4 weeks. The variables selected in this way were chosen for inclusion in the MAIC analysis.

Anchored Matching and Adjusting Individual Patient Data to Published Aggregate Data

In this study, an anchored MAIC was conducted, using placebo as the shared control between the two trials. Upon aligning the individual patient data (IPD) for patients in difamilast phase 3 trial, it was anticipated that the initial proportions of categorical variables and the initial median values of continuous variables for the subset of patients with a baseline IGA score of 3 in the difamilast study would correspond to those of the subset of patients with a baseline IGA score of 3 or 4 in the delgocitinib study. Consequently, this alignment led to a decrease in the ESS of the chosen difamilast cohort.

Calculating Clinical Outcomes Using Weighted Patient Data

Weights were applied to recalculate the results for each IPD from the difamilast phase 3 trial. This was done to estimate the proportion of patients at each level of severity, which was categorized by the EASI score separately. These assessments were made at baseline, as well as at week 4 for both difamilast and placebo. In contrast, the published clinical outcomes from the delgocitinib phase 3 trial, along with the published proportion of patients by severity for delgocitinib, were characterized using the IGA score rather than the EASI score. Consequently, the severity stages were determined by the IGA score, which was based on a conversion table that correlates different measurements, as presented in Table 1.

Patient Selection

In the phase 3 difamilast trial, the initial distribution of AD severity was categorized as mild (6.6%), moderate (76.4%), and severe (17.0%), while aggregated data from the phase 3 delgocitinib trial showed 68.9% of moderate and 31.1% of severe cases. To ensure comparable subject enrollment criteria across both trials, the patient population from the difamilast trial was adjusted to include those with moderate and severe baseline severity, representing 100% of the population, which means only the subgroup of patients with a moderate or severe baseline severity of AD from the difamilast phase 3 trial was weighted to match the selected patients with a baseline IGA score of 3 or 4 from the delgocitinib phase 3 trial. The suggested approach aligned with the latest guidelines on MAIC provided by the National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence Decision Support Unit [20]. Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with AD by severity stage as defined by the EASI score. For difamilast and placebo, EASI scores were assessed separately at week 4, and for delgocitinib, proportions reported on the basis of IGA scores were converted to EASI scores. [18]. The secondary outcomes were to compare the clinical efficacy (mean percent changes from baseline to week 4 in mEASI score, and proportion of patients achieving mEASI-50 or mEASI-75 at week 4) and safety outcome (the percentage of patients experiencing any target AEs from baseline to week 4) of difamilast after the MAIC with the reported value of delgocitinib.

Data Analysis

Two MAIC analyses were conducted on the basis of two distinct sets of independent variables. Given that the difamilast and delgocitinib studies involved two separate groups of patients, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for comparison of the proportion of EASI score at the week 4 mark between the two treatment arms. Additionally, a t-test was employed to evaluate the differences in mean percentage changes from baseline to week 4 in the mEASI score. Furthermore, a chi-squared test was used to compare differences in the proportions of patients achieving mEASI-50 or mEASI-75 at week 4, as well as those in the proportion of patients experiencing any AEs within 4 weeks from baseline.

For continuous variables, frequencies (N), percentages (%), means, standard deviations (SDs), medians, 95% confidence intervals, and minimum and maximum values were reported. In contrast, for categorical variables, frequencies (N) and percentages (%) were reported. All analyses relied on observed data. All analyses were conducted using SAS® Release 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC), and the results were presented in Excel tables. No data imputation occurred. Patients who lacked EASI scores at week 4 were not included in the calculations for the proportion of patients by severity.

Results

Effective Sample Size

Following the application of the inclusion criteria and before matching, a total of 340 patients were selected (170 received difamilast and 170 received placebo) from the difamilast phase 3 trial [14], and 158 were chosen (106 received delgocitinib and 52 received placebo) from the delgocitinib phase 3 trial [11] for the MAIC analysis. All patients had moderate or severe AD.

After matching the patients from the difamilast phase 3 trial with those from the delgocitinib phase 3 trial, the ESSs reduced to 32.7–43.3% of the original difamilast (treatment/placebo) patients (Table 3). Most weights were concentrated around zero, resulting in a substantial reduction in the ESS (Fig. 1).

Following the matching process, the baseline characteristics of the difamilast treatment group were comparable to those of the delgocitinib treatment group. The mean age of patients in both groups was around 31 years, with approximately 60% being male. The mean BMI of the patients was about 22.8 kg/m2 for difamilast and 22.9 kg/m2 for delgocitinib. Discussion

The PDE4 inhibitor difamilast and the JAK inhibitor delgocitinib have different mechanisms of action, and their efficacy and safety have been verified in phase 3 and approved in recent years. However, no direct comparison studies have been conducted to date. This anchored MAIC analysis was conducted to indirectly compare the clinical effectiveness and safety of difamilast with delgocitinib in patients with moderate-to-severe AD in Japan, as there are no direct comparative studies for these two drugs. A thorough review of the published literature revealed two studies on delgocitinib [11, 18] and one phase 3 trial for difamilast [14]. Baseline characteristics for the delgocitinib group came from a phase 3 trial [11], while the documented proportion of patients by severity for delgocitinib, as measured by the IGA score, was sourced from a separate study [18]. A sample of adult patients with moderate-to-severe AD were selected for this analysis, and placebo was used as the shared control between the trials. mEASI, EASI, and AE were analyzed to compare the efficacy and safety of difamilast with delgocitinib.

There were no notable statistical differences between difamilast and delgocitinib in terms of proportion of patients by severity, as measured by EASI at the 4-week mark.

Regarding other efficacy measures, such as the mean percentage change in mEASI at week 4 and the achievement proportion of mEASI-50 and mEASI-75, the two treatments did not show significant differences. Difamilast exhibited a nearly similar mean percentage improvement in mEASI at week 4, mEASI-50 and mEASI-75 to delgocitinib. As regards safety outcomes, the incidence of AEs at week 4 was not significantly different between the two treatments. On the basis of the results, it is suggested that difamilast is comparable in clinical efficacy and safety with delgocitinib.

An indirect comparison study using network meta-analysis (NMA) was conducted by Zhang et al. in 2021, which also reported that difamilast and delgocitinib were equivalent in terms of efficacy (IGA) and safety (number of AEs) with no statistically significant differences [21]. Our study yielded similar results, with the limitation of NMA, in terms of different patient backgrounds, being overcome by MAIC. This further supports the possibility that the efficacy and safety of the two drugs are equivalent.

This study analyzed the proportion of patients by severity on the basis of EASI score but did not include an analysis on the basis of IGA score. There are several reasons for this. First, the outcome measurement definitions for IGA varied; the difamilast study used a 5-point IGA scale, while the delgocitinib study adopted a four-tier scale from the Health, Labour and Welfare Science Research Group. There were differences in evaluation stages and judgment criteria, wherein the IGA score for patients taking difamilast was determined by the condition of the skin rash, and for delgocitinib, it was based on the area of skin rash. Additionally, IGA and EASI assessments were conducted independently, which could have led to discrepancies in results. For example, a patient might have been classified as clear/almost clear according to the IGA score but still could have had a mild score on the EASI scale. There was the concern that compatibility would be significantly reduced with the use of IGA score. Hence, no analysis with IGA score was performed.

There are several limitations to this study. Firstly, the statistical strength of the analysis may be compromised due to more than 60% reduction in ESS in the difamilast group after MAIC was performed. This reduction in ESS suggests a possible imbalance in the initial cohorts of the difamilast and delgocitinib trials. In fact, the delgocitinib trial had a larger percentage of patients with severe AD than the difamilast trial (31.1% versus 17.0%) [11, 14].

Secondly, as with all indirect comparisons, there is potential for bias due to unobserved effect modifiers or prognostic factors in the studies that were not accounted for.

Due to the above limitations, even though the MAIC method allowed for an indirect comparison of difamilast and delgocitinib studies within an equivalent population, the patients qualified for difamilast were different from those treated with delgocitinib. This underscores the importance of conducting direct comparison studies. Moreover, the results of efficacy are dependent on the data from week 4; hence, they are insufficient to evaluate long-term efficacy.

Conclusions

The results of this MAIC analysis, conducted on the basis of data from a 4-week phase 3 study of difamilast and delgocitinib, showed that difamilast had similar severity distribution of patients with AD on the basis of EASI score and efficacy measured by mEASI-50 and mEASI-75 at 4 weeks of treatment compared with delgocitinib. The incidence of AEs up to 4 weeks was also similar between difamilast and delgocitinib. This is consistent with the results of a previously published NMA using a different methodology for performing indirect treatment comparison. These results may provide useful information for clinicians and decision-makers in the treatment of AD.
