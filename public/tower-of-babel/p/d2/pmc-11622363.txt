Innovations in Preconception Care: Optimizing Health for All Individuals
Authors: Cynthia Nypaver, Amanda Yeager
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptimizing the overall health of individuals prior to pregnancy can improve both pregnancy and lifelong health outcomes. Despite extremely high financial expenditure on birth in the United States, maternal and infant mortality rates continue to rise. Moreover, significant racial and ethnic disparities persist in perinatal health outcomes. Preconception care, or health care provided before or between pregnancies, has the capacity to meet these challenges. Preconception care can be integrated into every health care visit, including visits with primary care, reproductive health, and mental health care clinicians. Increasing awareness among clinicians of the benefits of preconception care and equipping them with innovative strategies to implement this care into practice, the number of people receiving optimal care could be increased. Recent innovations in preconception care include group care, health applications (apps), reminders in electronic health records, social marketing campaigns, social media movements, community‐based partnerships, health care policy and access, and improving public and clinician education on preconception health topics. Ultimately, improving preconception health is best done in partnership between the consumer and clinician. Midwives, whose care is person‐centered and partnership‐focused, are well positioned to champion the innovation and implementation of equitable preconception care. The purpose of this State of the Science review, therefore, is to synthesize the literature on recent evidence‐based innovations that may be used to improve preconception health and counseling.Keywords: interconception care, preconception care, postnatal care, primary care

INTRODUCTION

According to Rae et al, the average cost of having a newborn in the United States is approximately $19,000, 1 and there are approximately 3.66 million births annually. 2 That equates to a staggering $69.5 billion spent annually on pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum care. Despite the high cost of standardized, evidence‐based models of prenatal care, the United States continues to have high maternal mortality rates (MMRs) and infant mortality rates (IMRs) compared with other developed nations. 3 , 4 The US MMR for 2021 was 32.9, which is nearly 10 times higher than in Australia, Germany, Japan, the Netherlands, Spain, and Sweden, 5 and the US IMR was 5.6, which is nearly 2 times higher than in Australia, Italy, Israel, Ireland, the Netherlands, and Spain. 6 Additionally, stark disparities in the MMR and the IMR persist and are highest among non‐Hispanic Black individuals. The MMR for non‐Hispanic Black women is more than 2.6 times higher and the IMR is 2 times higher among non‐Hispanic Black people compared with non‐Hispanic White people. 3 , 7 Disparities are also evident in other groups, especially those living in rural regions and those with less privileged socioeconomic status. 8 Evidence for these disparities is grounded in structural racism that has created inequitable access to quality health care. Social determinants of health can also contribute to wear and tear of the body and cumulative stress over generations, referred to as weathering. 8 , 9 Evidence has demonstrated that weathering contributes to higher rates of chronic illness, one of the leading causes of disparate IMRs and MMRs. 8 , 9

Reducing the MMR and IMR is a leading imperative of all major health organizations. 10 The US Department of Health and Human Services has set a goal to reduce maternal mortality by 10% with Healthy People 2030. 10 The Office of the Surgeon General has called for comprehensive action to improve maternal morbidity and mortality rates, with an emphasis on improving health both before and after pregnancy. 11 There is growing evidence that optimal health in all individuals prior to pregnancy improves the health of parents and offspring, which may contribute to a reduction in morbidity and mortality rates. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Due to the stark and persistent disparities in the MMR and the IMR among traditionally marginalized and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, preconception interventions need to include a health equity, social justice, and reproductive justice framework. 17 Such frameworks recognize the social determinants of health and the systemic barriers that affect one's behaviors and abilities to act on suggested interventions to achieve optimal health. 17 Upstream approaches are needed that prioritize preconception health, for all gestationally capable people. Therefore, the purpose of this State of the Science review is to synthesize the literature on recent evidence‐based innovations that may be used to improve preconception health. A search of relevant literature was conducted in several databases with peer‐reviewed journals as well as clinical practice guidelines to ensure completeness. Innovative strategies that may enhance uptake of preconception care (PCC), barriers to implementation, and recommended surveillance indicators will be reviewed.

PRECONCEPTION HEALTH CARE

Preconception health is the general health status of all individuals with reproductive capacity, regardless of pregnancy intentions, and is primarily focused on preventive health care. 17 , 18 PCC likewise is a continuum of care that is provided by a health care provider (HCP) before and between pregnancies, consisting of risk assessment, preventive screening, health promotion, and health care interventions. 17 Optimizing the health of all people of reproductive age improves their health status and, if they decide to reproduce, improves conception rates, pregnancy outcomes, childhood health, and the health of future generations. 18 Although all people of reproductive age could benefit from PCC that optimizes health, those who endure higher risks, such as those who experience health inequity or disadvantage along with other broader social determinants of health, may substantially benefit.

The preconception timeframe consists of 14 weeks or more prior to conception, whereas the periconception timeframe is the time before pregnancy plus the first 10 weeks' gestation. It is during the periconception timeframe in which gametogenesis, embryogenesis, and placentation occurs, a time when most adverse pregnancy outcomes originate. 19 Poor preconception health, particularly uncontrolled chronic illness, contributes to poor perinatal outcomes including decreased fertility, early pregnancy loss, congenital anomalies, fetal growth restriction, low birth weight, macrosomia, preterm birth, and increased maternal morbidity and mortality. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Furthermore, pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia and low birth weight increase the risk of chronic diseases (obesity, cardiovascular disease, and type 2 diabetes mellitus) during one's life course. 19

Barriers to PCC

Over the past 2 decades, several professional organizations and workgroups have defined components and surveillance indicators of PCC (see Tables 1 and 2). 24 , 25 Additionally, educational resources and recommendations to aid HCPs in PCC and counseling have been published. 26 Yet barriers to PCC uptake persist and occur most commonly at the HCP and client levels. 27 , 28 , 29 More barriers have been identified than facilitators for participation in PCC, which may explain why uptake is persistently low. 28 Issues with reimbursement for services also exist because PCC consultations are not always reimbursed by health insurance. 27 , 28 , 29

Awareness of evidence‐based PCC guidelines is low, especially for HCPs who are in nonperinatal practice settings. 27 , 28 Many HCPs will only address specific elements of PCC like contraceptive counseling, with less priority given to other areas such as screening tests for depression and intimate partner violence. 27 , 28 , 29 This likely contributes to fragmented implementation of PCC recommendations and guidelines. Lack of HCP education and training about PCC within the context of primary care and confusion among HCPs about whose responsibility it is to provide PCC are other common barriers. 27 , 28 , 29 Furthermore, the HCP may have competing priorities due to limited time, workload, and staffing during the health care visit to fully or even partially address PCC, placing more importance on the client's reason for the visit. 28 Another barrier to PCC is provider concerns that clients will not follow through with recommended behavioral changes (eg, tobacco, alcohol, or weight loss). 29 If clients are consistently not following recommendations, then the HCP may lose confidence that components of PCC are worth their time and effort. On the other hand, clients may be unable to act on suggested behavioral changes due to structural determinants like poverty, circumstances in which they live, and immigration status. 29

Approximately 43% of pregnancies in the United States are unintended. 30 Thus, people of reproductive age may not recognize that their health status can affect fertility and future pregnancy outcomes. People who are not contemplating pregnancy in the near future are unlikely to be engaged in perinatal services; therefore, interventions that improve health need to be included in primary care settings, outside of the perinatal health setting. 11 Risk factors that can negatively affect a pregnancy may go unaddressed until the first prenatal care visit, which is often too late to initiate preventive interventions to achieve optimal health. Chronic diseases and some medications that are used to treat chronic diseases can adversely affect pregnancy and pregnancy outcomes. 28 Optimizing management of these conditions and ensuring that individuals are prescribed medications with the lowest risk profile to a fetus is imperative during the preconception period. 27 , 28 , 29

There is a need to promote knowledge about preconception risk factors, improve motivation for behavioral changes that optimize health, and implement interventions that improve communication and messaging regarding preconception wellness, especially with people who are younger, nulligravid, and with lower incomes. 31 Strategies that bridge the evidence for PCC to improve health with actual practice of the guidelines is also needed. Nacev et al 29 suggest that clarification is needed about whose responsibility it is to provide PCC, when it should be initiated, and how it should then be incorporated into all patient encounters. HCPs need improved familiarity with screening skills and tools, particularly around depression and intimate partner violence. They also need to increase their awareness of their own biases (implicit and explicit) and understanding about how structural inequalities influence patient behavior. Establishing and creating partnerships (with other disciplines and in the communities) can strengthen awareness of the importance of preconception health (PCH). 29 Evidence supports integrating components of PCC at every health care encounter for people of reproductive age with various clinicians, regardless of desire for pregnancy or not. 12 , 18 , 26 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 Various HCPs include but are not limited to midwives, physicians, nurses, medical assistants, dieticians, dentists, social workers, and mental health workers.

INNOVATIVE STRATEGIES TO IMPROVE PRECONCEPTION HEALTH

Innovative strategies have been used to improve quality and accessibility to PCC and to motivate people to improve their preconception health. 35 , 36 Interventions that support a healthy lifestyle, including optimalization of weight; healthy diet that includes supplementation with folic acid; adequate sleep and physical activity; mental health; immunizations; and well‐controlled management of chronic illnesses like diabetes, hypertension, and obesity are needed for people with reproductive capacity to optimize long‐term health. Optimizing health via PCC improves pregnancy outcomes should pregnancy occur. 12 , 18 , 37 Interventions that use a single‐ or multistrategy approach involving various levels including individuals, groups of people within the community, and populations can be used. Multistrategy, multilevel interventions may be more effective. 11 , 16

Individual Level Interventions

Applications and Technology‐Based Interventions

Health applications (apps) on smart devices is the umbrella term under which medical apps fall. Some medical apps are classified as medical devices and as such are regulated by the US Food and Drug Administration. 38 Health apps, which are not necessarily regulated, are software programs on smart phones or other mobile digital devices like tablets that can improve health for individuals and their communities by processing data on or for the users. 38 Health apps can empower individuals to engage in self‐care and shared decision‐making by providing information about wellness and fitness, managing chronic illness, and tracking health maintenance. Use of these apps can thus empower people to adopt healthier lifestyles, promote mental and social well‐being, and enhance health consumer autonomy. 38 Other user benefits include convenient access to health information and health care, cost effectiveness, improved client and HCP communication, increased self‐awareness of any needed changes in behaviors, and adherence to healthy lifestyle habits. 38 Health apps and devices can also be programmed to send user reminders and push notifications to promote engagement. Examples include pedometers (step counts), fitness apps, weight reduction control tools, and monitors that measure data like blood pressure, pulse, body weight, mood, and sleep.

An example of a technology‐based intervention is the Before, Between, and Beyond Pregnancy website and its content. 39 This website is supported by the National Preconception Health and Health Care (PCHHC) Initiative, a private‐public partnership of more than 70 organizations focused on improving preconception health and wellness. It contains resources for HCPs, health care consumers, and researchers including toolkits, reports, clinical guidelines, key publications, and biweekly research updates. 39 Additionally, materials from the PCHHC Initiative toolkit can be readily accessed using the Preconception Care Quick Ref app. 40 This app provides quick reference at the point of care for HCPs, students, and residents to guide components of PCC with clients.

Smarter Pregnancy 35 , 36 , 41 is another evidence‐based PCH app that was developed and tested in the Netherlands and the United Kingdom. It is used in conjunction with an interactive website 41 containing modules that gather health screening data. An individualized personal risk profile is then generated. Subsequently, individualized, motivational coaching texts and emails are generated and sent to health care consumers. Built into the program are incentives, tips, tricks, recipes, and rewards. This program aims to motivate and empower individuals to adopt healthier lifestyles. 35 , 36 It has demonstrated improved fertility rates, healthier eating including use of folic acid, greater rates of smoking cessation, and decreased alcohol use. 35 , 36 Most Dutch insurance companies reimburse the costs of subscription. 40 Pregnant Faster 42 is an app‐based nudging program that is currently being tested and is based on the Smarter Pregnancy program. Nudging is an intervention that changes behavior through incentives. Pregnant Faster aims to increase engagement in PCC and to facilitate scheduling an appointment with an HCP for a PCC visit. Health care consumers can earn tokens to buy things like folic acid, fruits, and cosmetics. Results of this study are expected to be published soon. 42

One pilot study in the Netherlands, by van der Windt et al, 43 aimed to evaluate the use and acceptability of the Smarter Health app, a digital lifecourse care platform. This app integrates lifestyle care and change with clinical care, recognizing that people are more motivated to change unhealthy behaviors during the periconception period. In addition to increasing access to information, the app provides lifestyle coaching to individuals contemplating pregnancy and to those who are already pregnant to improve self‐management of care. Information and education are continuously available without restriction of office visits and is offered when it is most relevant to the user. Of the 440 participants in this study, 66.6% activated the app, and of those, 38.4% used the app, logging in an average of 8 times during their clinical care. The authors concluded that integrating lifestyle care with health care is a feasible intervention that is acceptable by health care consumers. 43

Another technology that is demonstrating promising results for optimizing PCH and behavior is the use of a chatbot. 44 A chatbot is a computerized program that uses either decision trees or artificial intelligence to process and simulate responses to human conversation. It can be programmed with predetermined scenarios and scripts to provide preconception screening and health education in an efficient, easy‐to‐use fashion with diverse populations. 44 Chatbots have the ability to reduce modifiable risks such as substance use by increasing motivation in clients and reminding them to make appointments with HCPs for health checkups. They also increase access to care and enable individuals to discuss sexual and reproductive topics in a nonjudgmental manner. Lastly, people are empowered to make informed decisions about their health because they are receiving relevant information. 44

Counseling and Education

Studies have demonstrated that one‐on‐one counseling and education with HCPs can increase patient knowledge about the importance of PCC, behavioral change, and health outcomes. 16 , 34 , 45 , 46 One‐on‐one counseling sessions provide an opportunity for interactive dialogue between the HCP and the client to arrive at a mutually agreed upon, individualized PCC plan. These interactions do not necessarily need to be in person, but rather can be conducted using the telephone, virtual reality, and avatars.

One small study in Canada trialed an intervention using public health nurses to conduct a preconception risk assessment, education, and follow‐up with postpartum clients. 45 The preconception risk assessment included family and reproductive history, prior adverse perinatal outcomes, nutrition, physical activity, weight, immunizations, chronic and infectious illness, sexual health, mental health, medications, psychosocial stressors, and environmental exposures. 45 Using the data from the risk assessment, the nurses created standardized and tailored health education plans and referrals (eg, dietician, addiction counselor, etc) as needed. Follow‐up telephone calls were made at one week, one month, and 3 months postintervention to measure feasibility, adherence, and acceptability and to assess preconception health awareness, knowledge, and risk factors, respectively. The researchers concluded that the intervention was feasible, acceptable by the participants, and raised the awareness of the importance of preconception health. Most of the participants preferred face‐to‐face interactions with the public health nurse rather than by telephone. 45

Virtual reality technology can also be used to provide preconception education. Virtual reality uses computerized simulations to make the user feel like they are interacting with someone in the real world. 47 Virtual avatars, which are graphical representations, can be customized to look like their users. An example of a virtual avatar that has been used in research in the United States is the Gabby PCC conversational agent, developed and tested in African American women aged 18 to 35 years. 48 Participants completed an online survey that screened for 108 preconception risk factors. After risk factors were identified, Gabby provided one‐on‐one counseling and education about PCH. Gabby, who appeared racially congruent with the participants, conducted periodic, face‐to‐face PCH counseling using verbal and nonverbal conversational behavior over a one‐year timeframe. More than 96% of the participants indicated that they had implemented suggested behavioral changes to improve health or were planning on doing so in the future, making this a viable option for delivering longitudinal preconception counseling to African American teenagers and young adults. 47 , 48

Community and Population‐Level Strategies

A very successful population‐level PCC strategy that many countries have implemented to reduce risk for neural tube defects is mandating folic acid fortification in flour and rice. 12 Additionally, adding iodine to table salt has helped to prevent impairment of fetal brain development. 12

A potential area that warrants additional research to promote PCH is group interventions within the community and public health setting. Most of the evidence for interventions to improve preconception health are at the individual level through preconception counseling and education about healthy lifestyle. 34 Evidence for group interventions specifically for promoting preconception health is sparse, most likely due to the difficulty targeting this population. 27 , 28 Therefore, the use of social marketing may be one strategy to reach this population.

Social Marketing Strategies and Campaigns

Social marketing is a discipline that applies marketing principles and techniques to influence behavior that will “improve health, prevent injuries, protect the environment, and contribute to community.” 49 (p7) It is a consumer‐oriented approach focused on targeted audiences to create a change that has a positive impact on society. Social marketing has the potential to reach large groups of people about the importance of how healthy lifestyle behavior choices improve individual health as well as the health of future generations. In other words, it aims for buy‐in from the public connecting how optimal health in the present contributes to healthy future generations. Tools that can be used to improve knowledge about PCC, preconception health, or components thereof include billboards, radio, television advertisements, posters, and pamphlets posted or distributed in community health centers, grocery stores, shopping centers, childcare centers, schools, pharmacies, waiting rooms, and hospitals. 50

The APROPOS‐II study 50 used a preconception social marketing intervention in 4 municipalities in the Netherlands. Surveys were administered to participants, including prospective parents and HCPs (midwives and general practitioners). In planning the study, the researchers worked with marketeers, HCPs, and representatives from the target population to design this social marketing intervention. Eight social marketing principles, a 4‐P Framework, and the Health Belief Model were used to design the Woke Women Campaign. 50 The 4 Ps were (1) Product (preconception health information), (2) Price (free preconception health information), (3) Promotion (messaging to people of reproductive age), and (4) Place (where to receive PCC). The primary aim of this study was 2‐fold: (1) to improve uptake of PCC by people of reproductive age and (2) to increase provision of PCC by HCPs. 50 Messaging was provided on social media by local ambassadors who were members of the targeted population, and local HCPs to strengthen a sense of community and to serve as an access point. The slogan for this campaign was “Wake up, smart (future) mama! Let's make your baby strong.” 50 Offline and online marketing tools were used, including posters, flyers, business cards, PCC advice cards, banners, a website, and social media platforms. Hoodies with the Woke Women logo were worn by HCPs during preconception consultations. Results of the study showed that more women and men in the intervention group adhered to the recommended lifestyle behaviors (reduced smoking or alcohol use during the preconception period and adherence to a healthier diet) than in the control group, although this was not statistically significant. Competence in providing PCC was improved in HCPs. The authors report that the effect of intervention may not have been fully captured because the study was conducted during the COVID‐19 pandemic, resulting in a smaller sample size than expected and a significant size difference between the intervention and control groups. 50 A strength of this study is the multidisciplinary co‐creation of the social marketing strategy.

The Consumer and Community Involvement (CCI) framework is another multidisciplinary framework that was developed by the Health in Preconception, Pregnancy and Postpartum (HiPPP) Global Alliance out of concern for the potential negative health effects of obesity in women of reproductive age. 51 The HiPPP alliance consisted of health care consumers, HCPs, and researchers across 5 continents who recognized the need for a framework that could be used to develop evidence‐based interventions during the preconception and interconception period for HCPs and consumers that would reduce excessive weight gain. Using the CCI framework, health care consumers were invited into every stage of the process to set research and translational priorities surrounding healthy lifestyle and obesity prevention in women of reproductive age. 51 This initiative highlights the importance of incorporating all stakeholders, recognizing that translation of research into interventions or approaches is more authentic and meaningful when stakeholders are involved.

Another example of a national campaign is the Show Your Love 52 campaign led by the Preconception and Preconception Care Initiative. The Show Your Love website 52 provides materials for people to learn about various wellness topics. These include healthy eating and physical activity, taking a daily multivitamin with folic acid, mental health, avoidance of tobacco products, protecting against infections, managing chronic conditions, and setting and tracking health goals. 52 Its main message is to love oneself and other loved ones by taking charge of your own health. The campaign provides printable and sharable resources in English and Spanish for HCPs and health care consumers promoting healthy lifestyle choices and wellness.

Social Movements

Social movements may have potential to improve uptake of and access to PCC and ultimately preconception health because they reach the whole population, not just those planning a pregnancy in the health care setting. 12 Social campaigns are one component of social movements because they provide education and advice to the population. More broadly, social movements aim to engage the population and stakeholders to perceive the need for behavioral changes that may optimize health before a pregnancy and spark sustained motivation to maintain that change. Additionally, they are a good way to raise awareness of the importance of reproductive planning in the general public. 32 These endeavors must occur in a social context that is supportive of improving health before pregnancy, involving all stakeholders to create advocacy coalitions (eg, professional organizations, governmental agencies, media, and commercial interest groups) to generate policy changes needed from a system standpoint. 12 An example of a social movement in the United Kingdom is the Make Every Contact Count (MECC). 53 MECC is an effective and evidence‐based approach that uses multiple daily interactions that people have among themselves and organizations to support positive changes in physical and mental health to improve the health of the population. This social engagement approach uses health champions to provide consistent and concise healthy lifestyle education followed by brief discussion. Health champions may include HCPs or others in allied health, social services, first responders, and housing services. 53

Policy and Health Care Accessibility

Health care must be accessible and accommodating for people of reproductive age to improve preconception health. Accessibility to care includes factors like location, hours of operation, transportation, health care coverage (insurance), and affordability. Health care facilities need to be available in communities where people live and offer extended hours. Improving affordability of care through public health systems and insurance allows patients to receive care. Health care facilities, both private and public, must be welcoming and accessible to all individuals regardless of age, gender, sexual orientation, ethnicity, and disability status.

Lack of health insurance coverage is associated with decreased knowledge about the importance of PCH and healthy preconception behaviors. 53 Expanding Medicaid eligibility for people of reproductive age not only improves health care coverage but also increases use of services and decreases avoidance of care. 34 Although the Affordable Care Act of 2010 expanded preventive services including PCC, 54 recommended vaccinations, and screening for women, it has not been adopted by all states. 54

A 2022 March of Dimes report 55 offers policy solutions that may decrease ethnic and racial disparities and improve health outcomes, including fully integrating the midwifery model of care and doula services in all states. The authors also suggest expanding health care coverage by raising income limits for Medicaid eligibility and expanding Medicaid coverage for the postpartum period to 12 months from the current 60 days. 55 These suggested policies would increase access to care by providing coverage and reimbursement for telehealth services, as well as health system reformation addressing social determinants of health. 55 It is vital that HCPs become engaged in health care policy to serve as advocates for patients’ welfare and community well‐being.

Implications for Clinician Education and Training

HCPs can be trained on theory‐based skills that will support behavior change including how to engage and motivate clients through brief collaborative conversations using open‐ended questions, reflection, listening, directing, and self‐selected goal setting. 12 Rather than the HCP providing education and advice, these brief conversations and interactions basically empower the client or couple to draw out their own motivation and capacity for change. 32 , 56 Examples include healthy conversations skills and motivational interviewing. 56 Motivational interviewing has shown to be effective at reducing alcohol drinking and increasing effective contraceptive use, with a sustained effect at 9 month follow‐up. 32 This training can be achieved through online or in‐person refresher courses. Mapping elements of PCC throughout nursing and midwifery curricula can develop HCPs’ competence in providing client education and PCC. 57

Screening Tools

Screening tools like One Key Question, 58 Reproductive Life Plan, 59 and PATH questions (pregnancy attitudes, timing, and how important is pregnancy prevention) 60 provide a framework for HCPs to routinely ask about reproductive intentions without alienating those who do not wish to conceive currently or in the future. Incorporating simple questions like “Would you like to become pregnant in the next year?” or “Do you think you might like to have (more) children in the future?” enables people to reflect about their reproductive intentions and goals.

Implications for Practice

Most interventions that aim to improve preconception health are at the individual level through risk screening, assessment, and counseling. Prompts in the electronic health record (EHR) are a good resource for HCPs, reminding them to engage in various components of PCC during a health care visit. Reminders like colored banners or pop‐ups in the EHR have been shown to increase the likelihood that providers will offer preventive interventions like screening and behavioral change counseling. 61 Lacking is a universal community‐based model of PCC. The literature suggests that higher level models that use multiple modalities at multiple levels through a holistic, life course approach may be more effective at improving preconception health for all people of reproductive age. 32

Midwives are well positioned to spearhead PCC interventions because they are primary care providers who care for clients across the life span, particularly during the reproductive years, creating many touchpoints in which PCC can be shared. Additionally, midwives are advocates for legislation and policies that promote quality health care and are experts in collaboration with other members of the interprofessional health care team. 62

CONCLUSION

Over the last 2 decades, the concept of and the components of PCC have been clearly delineated. More recently, researchers have begun testing interventions through which PCC can be delivered in collaboration with health care consumers and the general public. Everyone must share the responsibility for creating a healthier population, where all members aim to achieve optimal health, especially during the reproductive years. Adult optimal health improves intergenerational health and the health of future generations. Many innovative strategies have been identified, and others are currently being tested, that can be used to achieve optimal health for all individuals. Midwives are well positioned in their communities and beyond to serve as champions leading the social movement for equitable preconception health.
