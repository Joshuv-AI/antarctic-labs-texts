Psychomotor development in children aged 12–23 months from communities in southern Ecuador: a descriptive research note
Authors: Diana Estefanía Vásquez-Muñoz, Blanca Priscila Maldonado-Pacheco, Samuel Olegario Iñiguez-Jimenez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Psychomotor development is an evolutionary and integrative process through which children progressively acquire physical, psychological, emotional, and complex response abilities [1–5]. Although each child follows an individual rhythm of maturation, development tends to occur in predictable sequences, it may be affected by contextual conditions during early childhood [6–9].

In Latin America, inequalities in early interventions and socioeconomic conditions negatively affect child development, and sex and gender-related differences may also influence developmental outcomes and caregiving practices [10–12]. In Ecuador, the National Health and Nutrition Survey (ENSANUT) shows that children from rural areas and low-income households are exposed to fewer stimulating environments, which may limit learning opportunities [13, 14]. Despite the implementation of early childhood programs, gaps in access to healthcare, nutrition, and educational services persist, particularly in rural areas such as the province of Loja [15–17].

However, coverage remains insufficient to fully address developmental needs [18]. Previous studies in Ecuador have reported heterogeneous findings across regions, reflecting differences in sampling strategies, socioeconomic conditions, and access to health services, which limit direct comparability [19–22].

In this context, this study aimed to describe the psychomotor development in children aged 12 to 23 months from the cantons of Calvas and Gonzanamá, in the province of Loja, using the Abbreviated Development Scale (EAD). Given the non-probabilistic sampling design, findings are presented as a localized descriptive overview in an underserved population.

Methods and materials

This study employed a descriptive, cross-sectional design. Data were collected in July 2023 as part of a broader community-based research initiative led by the Center of Health Research in Latin America (CISeAL), assessing the psychomotor development of children residing in the communities of Cariamanga, Quilanga, Lucero, Colaisaca, Chingulle, Nambacola, Sacapalca and Changaimina, located in the cantons of Calvas and Gonzanamá, Loja, Ecuador.

A total of 138 children aged 12 to 23 months were recruited during community health fairs. All eligible participants whose caregivers provided written informed consent were included. This recruitment strategy may have introduced selection bias, as participants may not represent the general population, and voluntary participation may have favored caregivers with greater health-seeking behavior.

Assessments were conducted individually, with caregivers present to provide demographic and health information. Data were anonymized and coded to guarantee participant confidentiality.

Psychomotor development was assessed using the Abbreviated Development Scale (EAD-1), which assesses four domains: gross motor, fine motor-adaptive, hearing-language, and personal-social skills [23]. Each item is scored dichotomously (1 = achieved, 0 = not achieved), and total scores are categorized as high, medium-high, medium, or alert level according to age-specific criteria [24].

The EAD-1 has demonstrated strong reliability (ICC = 0.9; Kappa = 0.8) in Latin American pediatric populations [25]. However, although widely used in the region, it has not been formally validated in Ecuadorian populations.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee on Human Beings (CEISH) of the Pontifical Catholic University of Ecuador (CEISH-493-2023). Written informed consent was obtained from caregivers prior to data collection. All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0.0. Descriptive statistics, (frequencies, percentages, means, and 95% confidence intervals) were used to summarize the data. Given the non-probabilistic sampling design, inferential analyses were considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Associations between developmental domains and overall classifications were explored using contingency tables and Cramer’s V as a measure of effect size.

Key sociodemographic variables such as maternal education, socioeconomic status, and ethnicity were not available, limiting the ability to assess potential confounding factors.

Results

Results are presented as descriptive findings within this specific sample and should not be interpreted as representative of the broader population.

Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic characteristics of the study population. A total of 138 children were included, of whom 56.5% were male. The mean age was 17.75 months [CI = 17.14–18.36]. Most participants were from the canton of Calvas (51.4%), while 48.6% were from Gonzanamá. Most of the sample was from rural areas (60.1%), compared to 39.9% from urban settings.

In fine motor-adaptive domain, 52.9% were classified in the medium category, whereas 2.9% reached the high category.

In the hearing and language domain, 58.7% of participants presented adequate development, whereas 28.3% were classified as alert category. Also, in the personal-social domain, 42.8% of children were classified in the alert category, followed by 39.9% in the medium category.

Psychomotor development by community

The distribution of overall development classification by community is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Most children were categorized in the medium level 51.4% (n = 71), with variability across communities. Only one child (0.7%) was classified in the high category in Lucero. Chingulle showed a higher proportion of alert classifications (66.7%); however, this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size in that community (n = 3).

Exploratory analysis using Cramer’s V showed moderate associations between domain-specific performance and overall classification. The strongest association was observed for the personal-social domain (V ≈ 0.62), followed by the fine motor-adaptive domain (V ≈ 0.53). Gross motor (V ≈ 0.47) and hearing-language (V ≈ 0.49) domains also showed moderate associations. These findings reflect associations within this sample and should not be interpreted as causal.

Discussion

Neurodevelopment results from the interaction between biological and environmental factors, including family and community contexts, which influence developmental outcomes [14]. Children raised in socioeconomically disadvantaged environments may experience limited access to stimulation, which can affect developmental progress across domains [7, 13, 26].

The findings of this study should be interpreted as descriptive observations within a non-representative sample. Most children were classified in the medium category, while 24.6% were in the alert level, suggesting potential developmental vulnerability within this group.

Comparisons with previous studies in Ecuador should be made cautiously due to methodological differences. For example, Córdova Cando et al. [22] reported a high proportion of inadequate gross motor development in Loja, whereas Molina Calderón y Piñon Gamez [21] reported generally adequate performance in Chone. These discrepancies may reflect differences in sampling strategies and socioeconomic conditions.

Fine motor-adaptive development showed relatively favorable distributions, with 52.9% of children classified as medium and 31.9% as medium-high. However, studies in Latin America report a substantial proportion of infants at risk of developmental delay, particularly in low-income settings, which may affect later motor and cognitive outcomes [27–29].

The personal-social domain showed the highest proportion of alert classifications (42.8%). This domain reflects social interaction and emotional expression [10, 30]; however, the use of the EAD-1 without prior validation in Ecuador may introduce measurement bias, particularly in culturally sensitive domains.

Exploratory analysis using Cramer’s V showed moderate associations between domains and overall classification, with the personal-social domain showing the strongest association. These findings reflect patterns within this sample and should not be interpreted as causal.

In the hearing-language domain, most children were classified in the medium category (58.7%). Variability in language development has been reported in similar contexts and may be influenced by environmental and cultural factors [1].

Although socioeconomic differences between communities were not formally assessed, most participants were from rural areas, which may influence access to early stimulation [31]. Early interventions may support developmental outcomes in such contexts [32].

Although sex- and area-specific differences in early development have been highlighted in previous research, the present study was not designed to perform stratified analyses due to sample size limitations and the descriptive nature of the data. However, the inclusion of both sex and rural-urban distribution provides relevant context, and future studies should incorporate stratified analyses to better understand how these factors influence developmental outcomes in similar populations.

It is also noteworthy that only one child (0.7%) was classified in the high developmental category within the overall sample; however, due to the small number of cases, no meaningful interpretation by geographic subgroup (rural or urban) can be made.

Overall, these findings highlight the importance of monitoring psychomotor development in early childhood, particularly in underserved settings. Results should be interpreted cautiously and considered preliminary. Future research should include larger, more representative samples and incorporate key sociodemographic variables.

Limitations

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. The use of a non-probabilistic convenience sample and recruitment during community health fairs may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the uneven distribution of participants across communities, with small sample sizes in some localities, restricts meaningful comparisons between groups.

Key sociodemographic variables such as socioeconomic status, maternal education, and ethnicity were not available, limiting the assessment of potential confounding factors. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and the evaluation of developmental trajectories over time.

Future research should aim to include larger and more representative samples, as well as incorporate key sociodemographic variables to better assess potential confounding factors. In addition, longitudinal study designs are needed to evaluate developmental trajectories over time and to identify early predictors of developmental risk. Further research in other regions of the country and in similar underserved communities would also be valuable to expand the evidence base and inform targeted early intervention strategies.
