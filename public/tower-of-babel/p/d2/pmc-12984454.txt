Modeling Early Events in Food Sensitization: Complementary Insights from Caco-2 and T84 Epithelial Barriers Exposed to Peanut Allergens
Authors: Faiza Zafar, Milena Zlatanova, Isidora Protić-Rosić, Lidija Burazer, Marija Gavrović-Jankulović
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Food allergies are increasing worldwide, yet the early epithelial mechanisms that initiate allergic sensitization remain incompletely defined. As the intestinal epithelium governs both allergen translocation and epithelial–immune crosstalk, it constitutes a critical but underutilized model for predicting allergenicity. In this study, we used Caco-2 and T84 intestinal epithelial monolayers cultured on Transwell® inserts to compare barrier properties and responses to peanut protein extract. Phenotypic characterization included biomarker profiling, transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements, tight junction integrity assessment, and analysis of cytokine levels as well as oxidative and nitrosative stress. Peanut exposure caused moderate TEER reductions without overt tight junction disruption while allowing translocation of the major allergen, Arachis hypogaea allergen 1 (Ara h 1), likely via transcellular pathways. Peanut protein extracts also induced epithelial stress responses, characterized by increased reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide production, alongside time-dependent secretion of innate and type 2-associated mediators, including IL-1β, TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33, indicating epithelial activation in the absence of complete barrier breakdown. Notably, basolateral supernatants from peanut-exposed epithelial monolayers activated THP-1-derived macrophages and enhanced IL-6 secretion, demonstrating that limited allergen passage across an otherwise intact epithelial barrier is sufficient to elicit early innate immune responses. Collectively, these findings indicate that peanut extract induce subtle functional perturbations in the intestinal epithelium while promoting downstream immune activation, highlighting Caco-2 and T84 cells as complementary in vitro platforms for studying barrier-dependent mechanisms of allergic sensitization.

1. Introduction

Food allergy is an immunological disorder affecting more than 220 million people worldwide and steadily increasing in prevalence over the past few decades [1]. Although key mechanisms underlying aberrant immune responses to otherwise harmless dietary proteins—known as allergens—have been identified, critical aspects of the sensitization phase remain incompletely understood, particularly the molecular and cellular events that initiate and regulate the development of allergic sensitization [2]. During this phase, components of the innate immune system recognize and process the allergen, subsequently activating the adaptive immune response and promoting the production of allergen-specific IgE antibodies [3]. Furthermore, because current methods for evaluating allergenicity often lack predictive accuracy and validity [4], the increasing demand for alternative food sources highlights the need to develop novel predictive strategies for potential sensitization [5].

The human gastrointestinal tract (GIT) constitutes a primary interface for exposure to exogenous substances, including dietary allergens, in addition to the skin and respiratory system [6]. The intestinal epithelial monolayer forms the first line of defense against luminal antigens [7]. This barrier is composed of specialized intestinal epithelial cell (IEC) populations, including absorptive enterocytes, goblet cells, Paneth cells, stem cells, and enteroendocrine cells, which collectively line the intestinal lumen [8]. Their polarization is critical for epithelial barrier function, as it influences antigen transport, absorption, and presentation to underlying immune compartments [8,9].

Intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) also express a diverse array of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) capable of sensing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) as well as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released during cellular stress or injury, thereby enabling rapid activation of innate immune signaling and epithelial repair responses [10]. PRR families identified in IECs include Toll-like receptors (TLRs) [11], NOD-like receptors [12], C-type lectin receptors [13], RIG-I-like receptors [14], and scavenger receptors, such as the receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE) [15]. Although these receptors recognize distinct ligands, they converge on signaling pathways that activate pro-inflammatory transcription factors, including nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3). Activation of these pathways drives transcription and translation of various cytokines and chemokines, modulating the innate immune response [16].

The development of food allergies involves a complex interplay of genetic, immunological, and environmental factors [17]. Although any food, at any time, can trigger an allergic reaction, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) declared that nine foods account for approximately 90% of the cases. These major allergens include milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame [18]. Among them, peanut allergy is the most common, typically emerging in early childhood and often persisting into adulthood [19]. Arachis hypogaea (peanut) is a widely cultivated leguminous crop of significant agricultural and nutritional importance, valued for its characteristic flavor, high nutrient content, and broad culinary applications [20]. Despite these benefits, peanuts contain proteins capable of eliciting allergic reactions, which can lead to severe, life-threatening anaphylaxis [21,22]. These reactions are closely linked to intrinsic structural features of peanut allergens, including resistance to proteolytic degradation, thermal stability, and the presence of persistent IgE-binding epitopes [23]. Among the twenty peanut allergens currently documented in the Allergen Database (https://allergen.org/, accessed on 10 December 2025), Ara h 1 is one of the most extensively studied due to its high IgE-binding capacity and clinical relevance [24]. Ara h 1, a vicilin-like globulin, is known to be one of the main storage proteins with molecular size of 63–65 kDa [25]. It constitutes a significant portion (12% to 16%) of the total protein content in peanuts [26]. Structural analyses have shown that the crystallized core region of Ara h 1 (residues ~170–586) adopts a conserved bicupin fold comprising two homologous β-barrel cupin domains, consistent with duplication and fusion of an ancestral domain. In the crystalline state, this core region assembles into stable homotrimers exhibiting pseudo-D3 symmetry, stabilized by extensive hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding across large monomer–monomer interfaces [27]. Small-angle X-ray scattering studies further demonstrate that the core region alone is sufficient to drive trimerization and higher-order oligomer formation in solution. In contrast, several flexible, surface-exposed loop regions are disordered in the crystal structure, a feature that may enhance epitope accessibility and IgE recognition [26,27]. Overall, Ara h 1 displays the highly conserved bicupin architecture characteristic of vicilin allergens, providing a stable structural scaffold for oligomerization and immune recognition.

A variety of in vitro intestinal epithelial and co-culture models have been developed to investigate food-induced barrier dysfunction and immune activation [28,29]. Among these, the human colon carcinoma cell lines Caco-2 and T84 are widely used as simplified surrogates of the intestinal epithelium [30,31]. Upon differentiation, Caco-2 cells acquire characteristics of small intestinal enterocytes, whereas T84 cells more closely resemble colonocytes [32]. Although they do not fully replicate the cellular complexity of the human intestinal mucosa, both models establish robust tight junctions, form polarized monolayers, and exhibit key transport functions observed in vivo [31,32,33,34]. In addition to their barrier and transport functions, intestinal epithelial cells actively interact with underlying immune cells and play a central role in shaping mucosal immune responses through antigen sampling, cytokine secretion, and cell–cell signaling [35,36,37]. Epithelial-derived cytokines, including pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and type 2-associated alarmins such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), IL-25, and IL-33, are increasingly recognized as early signals that link epithelial sensing of dietary antigens to the activation and polarization of innate and adaptive immune responses [38,39]. These cytokines can act locally to modulate barrier function and antigen handling, as well as basolaterally to condition underlying myeloid cells, thereby amplifying immune signaling without affecting extensive epithelial damage.

A hallmark of nearly all inflammatory processes, irrespective of the initiating stimulus, is the induction of cellular oxidative stress, with reactive oxygen intermediates serving as important mediators in the regulation of genes involved in inflammation [40]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated primarily by members of the NADPH oxidase (NOX) family, including NOX1–5 and the dual oxidases DUOX1 and DUOX2, which are expressed in non-phagocytic epithelial cells [41]. Dysregulated NOX activity can result in excessive ROS production, leading to activation of redox-sensitive pathways such as NF-κB, a central regulator of inflammatory and immune responses [42]. In parallel, nitric oxide (NO), produced by nitric oxide synthases, functions as a key reactive nitrogen species that modulates epithelial signaling, barrier regulation, and cytokine production [43,44]. Crosstalk between ROS- and NO-dependent pathways is known to amplify epithelial stress responses and inflammatory signaling. Activation of these redox-regulated pathways has been implicated in epithelial cytokine secretion, antigen sensing, and the initiation of immune signaling at mucosal surfaces [45,46,47]. In this context, combined assessment of ROS and NO production provides mechanistic insight into epithelial stress responses and early danger signaling events that may contribute to allergen-induced immune priming.

To investigate the epithelial–immune interactions in vitro, the human THP-1 cell line is widely used as a model of the monocyte–macrophage lineage. THP-1 cells originate from the peripheral blood of a patient with acute monocytic leukemia and retain many functional characteristics of human monocytes, including responsiveness to microbial components and inflammatory stimuli [48]. Upon chemical differentiation, most commonly with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), THP-1 cells acquire a macrophage-like phenotype characterized by adherence, phagocytic capacity, and robust cytokine production [49]. Due to their well-defined and reproducible inflammatory responses, genetic and phenotypic uniformity, and compatibility with co-culture systems, differentiated THP-1 macrophages have been extensively employed as surrogates for intestinal myeloid cells in studies examining epithelial–myeloid crosstalk, immune activation, and modulation at the gut interface [48].

In this study, we employed these simplified epithelial monolayer models to investigate how exposure to peanut allergens affects barrier integrity, allergen translocation, and downstream immune activation. Peanut proteins that crossed the monolayers were subsequently assessed using THP-1-derived macrophages to evaluate their immunological reactivity. Together, these analyses provide insight into the molecular and immunological mechanisms that contribute to peanut allergenicity, particularly the structural and biochemical factors influencing sensitization and immune recognition. Such findings may support the development of more predictive in vitro systems and guide efforts to design safer, hypoallergenic food products.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Cell Culture

Caco-2, T84, and THP-1 cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Caco-2 cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium Eagle (MEM Eagle; PAN Biotech™, Aidenbach, Germany) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), penicillin (100 IU/mL), streptomycin (100 µg/mL), and 200 mM L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich). T84 cells were maintained in a 1:1 mixture of Ham’s F12 medium and Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM/F-12; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 IU/mL), streptomycin (100 µg/mL), and 200 mM L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, penicillin (100 IU/mL), streptomycin (100 µg/mL), 0.05 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 200 mM L-glutamine (all from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, with media changes performed three times per week. For passaging adherent cells, a 0.25% trypsin/0.53 mM EDTA solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used.

2.2. Characterization of Caco-2 and T84 Cell Monolayers

2.2.1. Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) Analysis

To analyze mRNA expression of toll-like receptors (TLRs), tight junction proteins, and brush border enzymes (ALP), Caco-2 and T84 cells were seeded in 48-well tissue culture plates (SARSTEDT AG & Co., Nümbrecht, Germany) at a density of 0.03 × 106 cells/well and allowed to differentiate for 7, 14, and 21 days. For the undifferentiated state (Day 0) 0.12 × 106 cells were collected immediately after passaging. Total RNA was isolated using TRI Reagent® (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Gene expression analysis was carried out using the SYBR FAST One-Step qPCR Kit (Kapa Biosystems, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Relative expression levels of TLR2, TLR5, TLR7, tight junction proteins (claudins, ZO-1), E-cadherin (adherens junction protein), and the brush border-associated enzyme alkaline phosphatase (ALP) were determined in both cell lines at Days 0, 7, 14, and 21.

qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: Reverse transcription with reverse transcriptase (RT) at 42 °C for 5 min (1 cycle), inactivation of reverse transcriptase at 95 °C for 3 min (1 cycle), followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at the specific primer annealing temperature (AT) for 20 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 20 s, with a final elongation step at 72 °C for 5 min. Primers and their corresponding annealing temperatures are listed in Table 1. All primers were derived from GenBank NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) and purchased from Invitrogen (San Diego, CA, USA). The housekeeping gene GAPDH (Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) was used for normalization, and expression levels were calculated relative to Day 0 Caco-2 cells.

2.2.2. Analysis of Transepithelial Electrical Resistance (TEER)

To assess the formation and integrity of epithelial monolayers, Caco-2 and T84 cells were seeded at a density of 0.01 × 106 cells/well and 0.03 × 106 cells/well respectively, into 24-well plates containing Transwell inserts with a 0.4 µm pore size (SARSTEDT AG & Co., Nümbrecht, Germany) and maintained in the appropriate culture medium. Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was measured using a Millicell-ERS Volt-Ohm Meter (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The instrument was calibrated before each use according to the manufacturer’s instructions. TEER values were monitored until monolayer formation was achieved and the cells reached characteristic resistance values. Results were expressed in Ω·cm2 and normalized to the surface area of the insert. Fully differentiated monolayers were identified by reaching resistance values of approximately 500 Ω·cm2 for Caco-2 cells and 1000 Ω·cm2 for T84 cells.

2.2.3. Immunofluorescence Detection of the Tight Junction Protein ZO-1

Caco-2 and T84 cells were seeded in 24-well plates (SARSTEDT AG & Co., Nümbrecht, Germany) containing sterile glass coverslips at a density of 0.05 × 106 cells/well and cultured in their respective media. Immunofluorescence staining of the tight junction protein ZO-1 was performed on Days 0, 7, 14, and 21. After aspiration of the medium, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Fixed cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 15 min, followed by blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h. Cells were incubated with anti- ZO-1 primary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Cat# 33-9100, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) for 3 h. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Fluorescence images were acquired using an A16.2701 fluorescence microscope (Opto-Edu, Beijing, China) equipped with a 40× objective lens and fluorescence channels were overlaid using FIJI (ImageJ; version 1.54g; National institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

2.3. Preparation of Peanut Protein Extracts and Isolation of Ara h 1

Raw and roasted peanuts, Arachis hypogaea, were obtained from the Paul Ehrlich Institute (PEI, Langen, Germany). A total of 30 g of each type was weighed, shelled, and deskinned, followed by manual fine grinding. The ground peanuts were defatted using hexane at a 1:5 (w/v) ratio of hexane to peanut flour for 30 min under continuous stirring. The grinding and defatting steps were repeated twice to ensure complete lipid removal. The resulting defatted peanut powder was subsequently used for protein extraction following the method described previously [50]. Ara h 1 was isolated from both raw and roasted defatted peanut powder using ammonium sulphate fractionation, starting from 5 g of defatted peanut powder. The partially purified protein fraction was further purified using ConA affinity chromatography on ConA-Sepharose matrix (Pharmacia, Uppsala, Sweden). A detailed protocol for the isolation and purification of Ara h 1 is described previously [50].

2.4. CCK-8 Cell Proliferation Assay

Cell proliferation of Caco-2 and T84 cells were evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cells were seeded in flat-bottom 96-well cellculture plates (SARSTEDT AG & Co., Nümbrecht, Germany) at a density of 0.01 × 106 cells/well in 100 μL of complete medium and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. After 24 h, cells were exposed for 4 h to raw or roasted peanut protein extract at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL prepared in serum-free medium. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Escherichia coli O127:B8 was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA. LPS in concentration of 1 µg/mL was used, and cells incubated in serum-free medium alone served as a negative control. Following exposure, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a BioTek Synergy LX Multimode Reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Cell proliferation was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated control cells (100%).

2.5. Measurement of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

Caco-2 and T84 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 0.01 × 106 cells per well and cultured for 48 h. Cells were then exposed to 1 mg/mL raw and roasted peanut extracts, 0.1 mg/mL raw and roasted Ara h 1, 1 mg/mL gelatin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 4 h and 24 h. Following treatment, culture supernatants were removed, and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production was assessed using the cell-permeable fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). After cellular uptake, DCFH-DA is de-esterified by intracellular esterases and subsequently oxidized by ROS to form the fluorescent compound 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein. For ROS detection, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA at a final concentration of 50 µM for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Intracellular ROS levels were quantified by spectrofluorometric analysis using excitation and emission wavelengths of 488 nm and 525 nm, respectively, with a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy LX Multimode Reader, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

2.6. Measurement of Nitric Oxide Levels

Nitric oxide (NO) production, used as an indirect indicator of nitric oxide synthase (NOS) activity, was quantified using the Griess reaction, as previously described [51]. Cell culture supernatants collected from the treatment of Caco-2 and T84 with 1 mg/mL raw and roasted peanut extracts, 0.1 mg/mL raw and roasted Ara h 1, 1 mg/mL gelatin, and 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 4 h and 24 h were used for NO analysis. Briefly, equal volumes of 1% sulfanilamide and 0.1% N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine dihydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), prepared in 5% phosphoric acid (H3PO4), were mixed and subsequently combined with cell culture supernatants at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio. Reaction mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 30 min in the dark, after which absorbance was measured at 548 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy LX Multimode Reader, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

2.7. Effects of Peanut Protein Extract on Membrane Integrity in Caco-2 and T84 Cell Monolayers

2.7.1. TEER Measurement

Caco-2 and T84 cells were seeded in 24-well microtiter plates with Transwell inserts of 0.4 µm pore size (SARSTEDT AG & Co., Nümbrecht, Germany) and maintained in their respective growth media until a confluent monolayer was formed. The transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) of the monolayers was measured using a Millicell-ERS Volt-Ohm Meter (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Prior to each measurement, the instrument was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. TEER values were expressed in Ω·cm2 and normalized to the surface area of the inserts. Only fully polarized monolayers exhibiting an initial resistance greater than 500 Ω·cm2 for Caco-2 cells and 1000 Ω·cm2 for T84 cells were used for further experiments. After reaching confluency, the monolayers were exposed for 4 h to 1 mg/mL of raw or roasted peanut protein extract prepared in serum-free medium. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 µg/mL) was also used, whereas cells incubated in serum-free medium alone served as negative controls. Peanut protein extracts (1 mg/mL) were added to the apical chamber of each insert, and TEER values were recorded after 4 h of treatment. Background resistance of cell-free membranes was subtracted, and the results were expressed as a percentage of the initial monolayer resistance (set as 100%). Data represent mean ± SEM from one representative experiment out of three independent experiments.

2.7.2. Detection of Ara h 1 in the Basolateral Compartment Using Western Blot

Cultured Caco-2 and T84 cell monolayers were treated with raw or roasted peanut protein extracts as described in the previous section. Following a 4 h exposure, samples from basolateral compartments were collected to evaluate the transepithelial passage of the major peanut allergen, Ara h 1. Proteins were separated using 4/14% SDS–PAGE gels. For each lane, 40 µL of sample was mixed with sample buffer in a 4:1 ratio and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were loaded onto the gel along with purified Ara h 1 (10 µg) as a positive control and pre-stained molecular weight markers (Cleaver Scientific, Rugby, Warwickshire, UK). Electrophoresis was performed at 80 V for 30 min, followed by 150 V for 90 min using Mini-PROTEAN Tetra Vertical Cell (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Proteins were then transferred onto a AmershamTM ProtranTM 0.2 µm nitrocellulose membrane (Cat# 10600001, GE Healthcare Life Sciences, now Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) using a semi-dry transfer system (PEQLAB Biotechnologie GmbH, Erlangen, Germany). The membrane was blocked with 1% BSA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in tPBS for 1 h at 37 °C with continuous shaking. Subsequently the membrane was incubated with rabbit anti-Ara h 1 polyclonal antibodies, kindly provided by the Institute of Virology, Vaccine and Sera “Torlak”, diluted 1:100,000 in 0.1% BSA in tPBS for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000; Cat# FNSA-0004, Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) secondary antibody diluted 1:10,000 in 0.1% BSA in tPBS for 30 min at 37 °C. Finally, the blot was developed using a luminol-peroxidase chemiluminescent substrate (ClarityTM Western ECL substrate, BIO-RAD, Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) mixed in a 1:1 ratio, and the signal was visualized using a Chemidoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

2.7.3. Detection of Ara h 1 Passage into Basolateral Compartment by ELISA Inhibition

Caco-2 and T84 cells were cultured and treated with raw and roasted peanut protein extract as described in the previous section. Following a 4 h exposure, samples from both the apical and basolateral compartments were collected to evaluate the transepithelial passage of the major peanut allergen Ara h 1, using an ELISA inhibition assay. Because the rabbit polyclonal antibodies used in this study were produced against raw Ara h 1, we additionally evaluated their binding specificity toward both raw and roasted forms to ensure that detected signals accurately reflected antigen levels in the samples rather than differences in antibody recognition. For this purpose, purified raw and roasted Ara h 1 (0.001–100 µg/mL) were included as reference standards. ELISA plates were coated with purified native Ara h 1 (20 µg/mL; 1 µg/well) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Wells were washed three times with tPBS (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 8.1 mM Na2HPO4, 1.5 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.2–7.4; 0.1% Tween 20) and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 1 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, rabbit anti-Ara h 1 polyclonal antibodies were diluted 1:150,000 in 0.1% BSA in tPBS, mixed with equal volumes of samples collected from the apical and basolateral compartments as well as purified raw and roasted Ara h 1 proteins (1:1 ratio), respectively. The mixtures were added to the coated wells and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C to allow competition between soluble Ara h 1 from the samples and the immobilized antigen for antibody binding. After washing, wells were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000; Cat# FNSA-0004, Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) for 1 h at 37 °C. Color development was achieved by adding TMB substrate (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), and the reaction was stopped after appropriate color formation. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy LX Multimode Reader, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The obtained absorbance values were used to calculate the percentage inhibition and transport efficiency using the following formulas:Percentage Inhibition = [1 − (AI (absorbance of sample)/AN (absorbance of Blank))] × 100 Percentage Transport Efficiency = [AA (Apical)/AB (Basolateral)] × 100

Higher inhibition percentages indicate higher concentrations of Ara h 1 in the samples, reflecting enhanced allergen transport across the epithelial monolayers, whereas lower inhibition values correspond to limited or negligible translocation of the allergen.

2.7.4. Immunodetection of Tight Junction Protein ZO-1 After Treatment with Peanut Protein Extracts

Caco-2 and T84 cells were seeded in 24-well microtiter plates containing sterile glass coverslips at a density of 0.05 × 106 cells/well and maintained in their respective growth media until reaching confluence. On Day 14, the medium was removed, and cells were exposed to raw or roasted peanut protein extracts for 4 h as previously described. After treatment, supernatants were collected, and the cells were processed for ZO-1 immunostaining as outlined in Section 2.2.3.

2.8. Effect of Peanut Protein Extract on IL-1β and Alarmin Release

2.8.1. Gene Expression Analysis of IL-1β, TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33

Caco-2 and T84 cells were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 0.03 × 106 cells/well and cultured for 7 days. Cells were then exposed to 1 mg/mL raw and roasted peanut extracts, 0.1 mg/mL raw and roasted Ara h 1, 1 mg/mL gelatin, and 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 4 or 24 h. Following treatment, total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions using TRI Reagent® (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Gene expression analysis was performed using the SYBR FAST One-Step qPCR Kit (Kapa Biosystems, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). The PCR cycling conditions were identical to those described in Section 2.2.1. Relative mRNA expression levels of IL-1β, IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP were determined in both cell lines at 4 and 24 h. All primers were derived from GenBank NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) and were purchased from Invitrogen (San Diego, CA, USA). Primers and their corresponding annealing temperatures are listed in Table 2. The housekeeping gene GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) was used for normalization, and gene expression levels were calculated relative to untreated control cells (cells only) using the comparative ΔΔCt method.

2.8.2. Protein Expression Analysis of IL-1β, TSLP, and IL-25, IL-33

For cytokine protein analysis, culture supernatants were collected from each experimental condition. As treatments were performed in duplicate wells, supernatants from replicate wells corresponding to the same condition and time point were pooled prior to analysis. Cytokine concentrations (IL-1β, IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP) were quantified using commercially available human ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA; Cat No; IL-1β: DY201-05, IL-25: DY1258-05, IL-33: DY3625B-05, TSLP: DY1398-05) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density was measured at 450 nm with wavelength correction at 570 nm using a BioTek Synergy LX multimode microplate reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Corrected absorbance values were obtained by subtracting the 570 nm readings from the 450 nm readings, and cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves.

2.9. Differentiation and Treatment of THP-1 Macrophages

THP-1 human leukemia monocytes (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were differentiated into macrophages in 96-well plates (0.08 × 106 cells/well) using complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Following differentiation, the cells were incubated for an additional 72 h in complete RPMI 1640 medium without PMA to allow recovery, as described previously [52]. Supernatants collected from the basolateral side of Caco-2 and T84 cell monolayers after 4 h raw and roasted peanut protein extract treatment were applied to the differentiated THP-1 macrophages. The supernatants were mixed with RPMI medium without fetal bovine serum (FBS) at a 2:1 ratio (supernatant:RPMI, v/v) to a final volume of 100 µL per well. After 24 h of incubation, supernatants were collected, and IL-6 cytokine concentrations were determined using a human IL-6 DuoSet enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

2.10. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). All data are presented as mean ± SEM. One-way ANOVA was used for statistical differences between the means of three or more populations. Post hoc analysis was conducted using Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Two-way ANOVA was used to assess statistical significance when comparing two or more parameters across multiple populations, followed by Dunnett’s test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The graphs presented in the results section represent one representative experiment from at least three independent biological replicates performed in technical duplicates, unless stated otherwise. Selected illustrations were generated with BioRender (https://biorender.com; accessed on 19 January 2026).

3. Results

3.1. Establishment and Characterization of Polarized Caco-2 and T84 Cell Monolayers

3.1.1. mRNA Expression of Biomarkers

Caco-2 and T84 intestinal epithelial cell lines were seeded in 48-well culture plates and allowed to differentiate for 21 days. Gene expression profiles were assessed at days 0, 7, 14, and 21 of differentiation to characterize temporal changes in epithelial maturation. A panel of biomarkers (Table 1) was analyzed to delineate functional differences between the two cell lines, including the brush border-associated enzyme alkaline phosphatase (ALP); tight junction components (claudins and zonula occludens-1 [ZO-1]); adherens junction proteins, specifically the Ca2+-dependent transmembrane adhesion glycoprotein E-cadherin; and pattern-recognition receptors, including Toll-like receptors TLR2, TLR5, and TLR7. qRT-PCR analysis revealed a significant induction of TLR2 expression in both Caco-2 and T84 cells during differentiation. Relative to day 0, TLR2 expression increased at day 7 (p < 0.01 in Caco-2; p < 0.001 in T84), day 14 (p < 0.001 in Caco-2; p < 0.0001 in T84), and day 21 (p < 0.001 in Caco-2; p < 0.01 in T84). Throughout the differentiation period up to day 14, TLR2 expression appeared more pronounced in T84 cells than in Caco-2 cells. By day 21 of differentiation, TLR2 expression in Caco-2 cells modestly exceeded that observed in T84 cells. Analysis of TLR5 expression revealed a significant induction exclusively in Caco-2 cells at day 14 (p < 0.01) and day 21 (p < 0.05) relative to day 0, whereas no significant changes were detected in T84 cells. In contrast, TLR7 expression was significantly upregulated in T84 cells at all differentiation time points (p < 0.0001) compared with Caco-2 day 0, while Caco-2 cells exhibited significant induction only at day 14 (p < 0.01) and day 21 (p < 0.01). Notably, the fold increase in TLR7 induction was more pronounced in T84 cells than in Caco-2 cells across all time points (Figure 1a). mRNA expression analysis of ZO-1 in Caco-2 cells demonstrated a significant induction at day 14 (p < 0.001) and day 21 (p < 0.01) relative to day 0, with maximal expression observed at day 14. In T84 cells, ZO-1 expression was significantly elevated from day 7 through day 21 (p < 0.01 at day 7, p < 0.0001 at day 14, and p < 0.001 at day 21) relative to day 0. Across all differentiation time points, ZO-1 expression was more pronounced in T84 cells than in Caco-2 cells (Figure 1b). In contrast, E-cadherin expression was consistently greater in Caco-2 cells throughout differentiation (p < 0.0001 relative to day 0; Figure 1b).

Analysis of mRNA expression of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), a marker of brush border differentiation, revealed that Caco-2 cells exhibited a significant and continuous increase in ALP expression from day 7 (p < 0.01) to day 21 (p < 0.0001) in comparison to day 0, while T84 cells maintained relatively low levels throughout the differentiation period and only showed significant expression at day 21 (p < 0.0001) (Figure 1c). The pronounced upregulation of ALP in Caco-2 cells suggests enhanced absorptive enterocyte-like differentiation, aligning with their small intestinal phenotype.

Relative mRNA expression analysis of claudin family members (CLDN1, CLDN2, CLDN3, and CLDN4) in Caco-2 and T84 cells across differentiation revealed distinct, cell line-specific expression patterns. In Caco-2 cells, CLDN1 expression was significantly upregulated on day 7 (p < 0.0001), day 14 (p < 0.001), and day 21 (p < 0.0001) relative to day 0. In T84 cells, CLDN2 and CLDN3 expressions were robustly induced throughout the differentiation period with p < 0.0001 on day 21. Comparative analysis across cell lines indicated that CLDN1 and CLDN4 were preferentially expressed in Caco-2 cells, whereas CLDN2 and CLDN3 predominated in differentiated T84 cells (Figure 1d).

3.1.2. Transepithelial Electrical Resistance (TEER) of Cell Monolayers

Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements of cell monolayers were used to analyze their functional characteristics in terms of barrier integrity. At day 15 from the time of cell seeding, Caco-2 cells exhibited TEER values around 500 Ω·cm2 (Figure 2), which were considered indicative of the establishment of a fully differentiated and functionally intact epithelial monolayer [53,54]. In contrast, T84 cells reached TEER values exceeding 1000 Ω·cm2 as early as day 7 post-seeding, confirming their ability to form tight and highly resistive epithelial barriers rapidly under the employed seeding conditions (Figure 2). The markedly higher TEER values in T84 monolayers reflect their pronounced tight junction expression profile, in line with results from mRNA expression for CLDN2–3 and somewhat CLDN4 along with ZO-1, observed during differentiation. Furthermore, following monolayer formation, TEER values for both cell lines remained above the commonly accepted threshold values indicative of intact epithelial barriers throughout the remainder of the experiment, indicating the preservation of epithelial barrier integrity rather than progressive deterioration. These results reinforce the molecular findings, indicating that while both Caco-2 and T84 cells can form functional epithelial barriers, T84 monolayers exhibit a barrier with TEER values above 1000 Ω·cm2 after 7 days of cultivation, while Caco-2 cell monolayers achieved TEER values above 500 Ω·cm2 after 14 days of cultivation, which highlights the differences in the suitability of both cell lines for modeling distinct aspects of intestinal epithelial physiology in allergen exposure studies.

3.1.3. Immunofluorescence Analysis of ZO-1 Localization

Immunofluorescence staining of the tight junction protein ZO-1 in Caco-2 and T84 monolayers at Days 0, 7, 14, and 21 showed clear junctional localization as early as Day 7, indicating early tight junction formation in both models, which aligned with the TEER measurements (Figure 3a,b). Day 0 represents the time point immediately after cell seeding following trypsinization, when cells are either in suspension or in the early stages of attachment. At this stage, epithelial cells have not yet established cell–cell contacts, polarity, or tight junctions. As junctional localization of ZO-1 requires cell attachment, spreading, and polarization, processes that typically occur after at least 24 h in culture, ZO-1 immunostaining at Day 0 does not allow for meaningful comparison. In contrast, by day 7 of differentiation, ZO-1 displays clear localization at intercellular junctions. Minor progressive refinements in ZO-1 distribution were observed during differentiation, particularly in Caco-2 cells, where junctional continuity and definition became more pronounced at later stages (Figure 3a). Comparatively, T84 monolayers exhibited a more compact ZO-1 localization at cell–cell junctions compared to Caco-2 cells, reflecting a more tightly packed epithelial organization, consistent with their higher TEER values and more robust barrier architecture (Figure 3b).

3.2. Cell Proliferation Following Peanut Extract Exposure

Cell proliferation following 4 h exposure to peanut protein extracts was assessed using the CCK-8 assay in differentiated Caco-2 and T84 epithelial monolayers. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) treatment resulted in a modest but significant reduction in CCK-8 signal in both cell lines (p < 0.01), with the percentage of proliferation remaining close to 90% of untreated controls. In contrast, exposure to raw and roasted peanut protein extracts induced a more pronounced decrease in signal intensity. At 1 mg/mL, Caco-2 cells exhibited significantly reduced proliferation to 66% (p < 0.001) and 50% (p < 0.0001) of control levels following exposure to raw and roasted extracts, respectively (Figure 4a). Correspondingly, T84 cells showed reductions to 80% (p < 0.001) and 54% (p < 0.0001; Figure 4b). These results indicate that peanut protein extracts, particularly the roasted form, exert an effect on intestinal epithelial cells and their proliferation, with Caco-2 cells exhibiting greater sensitivity than T84 cells. To complement these measurements, LDH activity was also assessed as an indicator of membrane damage. While LPS caused a slight, non-significant increase in LDH release, peanut extracts produced unexpectedly low LDH activity in both cell lines (Figure S1a,b). Additional testing confirmed that the extracts inhibited LDH enzyme activity (Figure S2), indicating assay interference and preventing reliable interpretation of LDH-based cytotoxicity.

3.3. Impact of Peanut Protein Extracts on Intestinal Epithelial Barrier Integrity and Allergen Transport

3.3.1. Effect on Transepithelial Electrical Resistance (TEER) of Cell Monolayers

The effects of raw and roasted peanut protein extracts on the intestinal epithelial barrier were evaluated using multiple complementary approaches in Caco-2 and T84 cell monolayers. Initial TEER measurements confirmed the establishment of intact and differentiated monolayers, with values above 500 Ω·cm2 for Caco-2 cells and above 1000 Ω·cm2 for T84 cells. Following 4 h exposure to peanut protein extracts (1 mg/mL), TEER significantly decreased in both cell lines, with a reduction of approximately 20% in Caco-2 cells following exposure to raw (p < 0.0001) and roasted (p < 0.001) peanut extracts and approximately 15% in T84 after exposure to both raw (p < 0.01) and roasted extracts (p < 0.01; Figure 5), indicating that peanut proteins affect the integrity of the epithelial barrier. When examining the TEER profiles more closely, raw peanut extract appeared to induce a greater reduction in barrier function than roasted extract, particularly in Caco-2 cells, suggesting that the processing method may influence the disruptive potential of peanut proteins.

Despite these TEER changes, the Lucifer Yellow (LY) exclusion assay showed that paracellular permeability remained largely unaffected, with LY rejection percentages comparable to untreated controls at both 4 and 24 h. As a reference control, preactivated Act d 1 cysteine protease [55] reduced LY rejection in Caco-2 cells after 24 h, while T84 cells maintained barrier function, reflecting their stronger tight junction architecture (Figure S3).

3.3.2. Translocation of Major Peanut Allergen, Ara h 1 Across the Barrier

The passage of the major peanut allergen Ara h 1 across the epithelial barrier was assessed using a Western blot and ELISA inhibition assay. A purified Ara h 1 standard (10 µg) was included as a positive control. Due to the high sensitivity of chemiluminescent detection, the Ara h 1 control lane appears overloaded compared with the basolateral samples. An additional faint band at approximately 37 kDa is observed in the Ara h 1 control lane, likely reflecting a minor contaminant or degradation product in the Ara h 1 preparation; this band was not detected in any basolateral samples. Western blot analysis revealed the presence of ~65 kDa bands in basolateral compartments, corresponding to the reported molecular weight of Ara h 1 [56]. Following exposure to raw peanut extract, the band intensity in basolateral samples was greater in Caco-2 monolayers than in T84 monolayers. Upon treatment with roasted peanut extract, a distinct band was detected in the basolateral compartment of Caco-2 monolayers, whereas only a weak signal was observed in T84 monolayers. No bands were detected in the untreated cells (Only Cells) lanes (Figure 6).

In the ELISA inhibition assay, a similar pattern was observed, with raw peanut extract inducing the greatest level of allergen transport across epithelial monolayers. Inhibition values reached approximately 22% in Caco-2 cells (p < 0.0001) and 15% in T84 cells (p < 0.0001) compared to untreated cells. Although exposure to roasted peanut extract also resulted in significant allergen transport (p < 0.0001 in Caco-2; p < 0.01 in T84), transport levels were lowerthan those induced by the raw extract (Figure 7a). Analysis of transport efficiency corroborated these findings, with raw peanut extract achieving approximately 25% efficiency in Caco-2 monolayers (p < 0.0001) versus 17% for roasted extract (p < 0.0001); T84 monolayers exhibited lower transport efficiencies than Caco-2 cells for both extract forms (p < 0.0001 for raw; p < 0.001 for roasted) when compared with untreated cells (Figure 7b). To ensure that these measurements represented true differences in Ara h 1 passage rather than antibody preference toward one form, purified raw and roasted Ara h 1 were compared across matched concentrations. The anti-Ara h 1 polyclonal antibodies showed similar binding to both raw and roasted Ara h 1, with no significant differences in signal intensity (Figure S4). This confirms that the observed transport differences reflect biological variation rather than antibody bias.

3.3.3. Assessment of Tight Junction Integrity by Immunofluorescence Analysis of ZO-1

Fourteen-day-old Caco-2 and T84 monolayers were used for treatment with raw and roasted peanut extracts for 4 h and for subsequent staining of ZO-1 as TEER analysis indicated that both cell lines reached the minimum resistance threshold consistent with monolayer formation at this time point. When these monolayers were treated to assess potential effects on tight junctions, no major differences were observed between treated and untreated cells in either cell line, as shown in Figure 8a,b.

3.4. Gene and Protein Expression Profile of IL-1β, TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33 in Caco-2 and T84 Monolayers Following Peanut Extract Exposure

Transcriptional analysis of IL-1β, TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33 by Caco-2 and T84 epithelial cells was quantified by qRT-PCR after 4 h and 24 h exposure to raw or roasted peanut protein extracts (1 mg/mL), purified Ara h 1 (raw or roasted, 0.1 mg/mL), LPS (1 µg/mL) and gelatin (1 mg/mL) (Figure 9a–d). In Caco-2 cells, exposure to raw peanut extract resulted in a robust upregulation of IL-1β mRNA expression at both 4 h (p < 0.0001) and 24 h (p < 0.001) relative to untreated controls. A significant increase in IL-1β transcript levels was also observed following exposure to purified roasted Ara h 1 at 4 h (p < 0.001). No other treatment induced a statistically significant increase in IL-1β expression at either time point. Although modest elevations in IL-1β mRNA were detected after 24 h exposure to roasted peanut extract and purified roasted Ara h 1, these changes did not reach statistical significance. In T84 cells, raw peanut extract similarly induced a significant increase in IL-1β expression at 4 h (p < 0.01), whereas all other treatments produced no significant effects at this early time point, despite a trend toward increased expression following LPS exposure. By 24 h, however, IL-1β transcription was significantly elevated under all treatment conditions except gelatin, with the highest expression observed following raw peanut extract exposure (p < 0.0001), followed by roasted peanut extract (p < 0.0001), LPS (p < 0.001), and purified Ara h 1 (raw and roasted) (p < 0.01; Figure 9a).

Analysis of TSLP expression in Caco-2 cells revealed a marked and selective induction following exposure to raw peanut extract at both 4 h (p < 0.0001) and 24 h (p < 0.001), while all other treatments remained comparable to untreated cells. In T84 cells, exposure to both raw (p < 0.0001) and roasted (p < 0.001) peanut extracts resulted in significantly elevated TSLP expression at 4 h, followed by more modest but significant induction with LPS (p < 0.001) and purified roasted Ara h 1 (p < 0.05); raw Ara h 1 and gelatin did not induce significant changes. At 24 h, both raw (p < 0.0001) and roasted (p < 0.0001) peanut extracts again elicited significant TSLP upregulation, with expression levels markedly higher following raw peanut exposure. LPS induced a strong TSLP response at this time point (p < 0.0001), followed by purified Ara h 1 in both raw and roasted forms (p < 0.01; Figure 9b).

Analysis of IL-25 expression in Caco-2 cells revealed a significant upregulation following exposure to raw peanut extract at both 4 h (p < 0.01) and 24 h (p < 0.01) compared with untreated controls. Although roasted peanut extract induced elevated IL-25 transcript levels, these changes did not reach statistical significance at either time point. All other treatments remained comparable to controls. In T84 cells, raw peanut extract significantly increased IL-25 expression at 4 h (p < 0.01), whereas no other condition elicited a significant response at this early time point. By 24 h, IL-25 expression was markedly elevated following exposure to both raw (p < 0.0001) and roasted (p < 0.0001) peanut extracts. Significant induction was also observed with purified Ara h 1 in both roasted (p < 0.0001) and raw (p < 0.001) forms, as well as with LPS (p < 0.001; Figure 9c).

Analysis of IL-33 expression in Caco-2 cells demonstrated a significant increase following exposure to raw peanut extract at both 4 h (p < 0.001) and 24 h (p < 0.0001). No other treatment induced significant IL-33 expression at 4 h. At 24 h, roasted peanut extract also significantly increased IL-33 expression (p < 0.001), followed by more modest induction with purified raw Ara h 1 (p < 0.05) and LPS (p < 0.05), whereas roasted Ara h 1 and gelatin had no significant effect. In T84 cells, exposure to raw peanut extract (p < 0.05) and LPS (p < 0.05) resulted in a significant increase in IL-33 expression at 4 h, while all other treatments remained non-significant. At 24 h, both raw (p < 0.0001) and roasted (p < 0.01) peanut extracts significantly upregulated IL-33 expression, along with LPS (p < 0.0001), whereas no significant induction was observed with purified Ara h 1 or gelatin (Figure 9d).

Secretion of IL-1β, TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33 by Caco-2 and T84 epithelial cells was quantified by sandwich ELISA after 4 h and 24 h exposure to raw or roasted peanut protein extracts (1 mg/mL), purified Ara h 1 (raw or roasted, 0.1 mg/mL), LPS (1 µg/mL) and gelatin (1 mg/mL) (Figure 10a–d). In Caco-2 cells, IL-1β secretion was significantly elevated following exposure to raw peanut extract at both 4 h (p < 0.01) and 24 h (p < 0.001) compared with untreated cells. Roasted peanut extract induced a modest increase in IL-1β secretion at 24 h; however, this did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, purified Ara h 1 (raw or roasted), gelatin, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) failed to induce significant IL-1β release. In T84 cells, raw peanut extract similarly elicited a significant increase in IL-1β secretion at 4 h (p < 0.01) and 24 h (* p < 0.05). Although LPS exposure resulted in elevated IL-1β levels at 24 h, this response was not statistically significant, and no significant effects were observed with the remaining treatments at either time point (Figure 10a).

Analysis of TSLP secretion demonstrated a significant induction in Caco-2 cells following exposure to raw peanut extract at both 4 h (p < 0.01) and 24 h (p < 0.01), whereas no other treatment produced a measurable effect. Similarly, in T84 cells, raw peanut extract significantly increased TSLP secretion at 4 h (p < 0.05) and 24 h (p < 0.01), while all other experimental conditions remained comparable to controls (Figure 10b).

For IL-25, Caco-2 cells exhibited a marked increase in secretion in response to raw peanut extract, reaching statistical significance at both 4 h (p < 0.0001) and 24 h (p < 0.01). Roasted peanut extract also induced a significant increase at 4 h (p < 0.01); however, this effect was not sustained at 24 h, likely due to increased variability. No significant IL-25 induction was observed with other treatments. In T84 cells, raw peanut extract increased IL-25 secretion at both time points, although statistical significance was observed only at 24 h (p < 0.0001). Roasted peanut extract likewise induced a significant increase in IL-25 secretion at 24 h (p < 0.05), whereas all other treatments were without effect (Figure 10c).

IL-33 secretion in Caco-2 cells was significantly increased following treatment with raw peanut extract at both 4 h (p < 0.001) and 24 h (p < 0.001). In T84 cells, no significant changes in IL-33 secretion were detected at 4 h under any treatment condition. However, at 24 h, both raw (p < 0.001) and roasted (p < 0.01) peanut extracts induced a significant increase in IL-33 secretion (Figure 10d).

3.5. Effects of Peanut Protein Extracts on Intracellular ROS Levels

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was quantified in Caco-2 and T84 cells following 4 h and 24 h exposure to raw or roasted peanut protein extracts (1 mg/mL), purified Ara h 1 (raw or roasted; 0.1 mg/mL), lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 µg/mL), or gelatin (1 mg/mL; Figure 11). In Caco-2 cells, exposure to raw peanut extract induced a marked increase in ROS levels at both 4 h (p < 0.01) and 24 h (p < 0.0001) compared with untreated cells, with a more pronounced effect observed at 24 h. Roasted peanut extract also significantly elevated ROS production at both time points (p < 0.01 at 4 h and p < 0.0001 at 24 h), albeit to a lesser extent than the raw extract. Treatment with purified raw Ara h 1 resulted in modest but significant increases in ROS at both 4 h (p < 0.05) and 24 h (p < 0.01), whereas purified roasted Ara h 1 did not induce a significant increase at any time point. As expected, LPS treatment served as a positive control and induced robust ROS generation at both 4 h (p < 0.01) and 24 h (p < 0.0001; Figure 11a). In T84 cells, exposure to raw peanut extract resulted in a significant increase in ROS levels at 24 h (p < 0.001) compared with untreated cells. Roasted peanut extract also significantly elevated ROS production at 24 h (p < 0.01), although to a lesser extent than raw extract. In contrast, treatment with purified Ara h 1 (raw or roasted) did not induce significant ROS generation at either time point, indicating a limited capacity of Ara h 1 alone to elicit oxidative stress in T84 cell lines. LPS treatment again induced a significant increase in ROS production at 24 h (p < 0.01) (Figure 11b).

3.6. Effects of Peanut Protein Extracts on NO Levels

Nitric oxide (NO) production by Caco-2 and T84 cells was quantified following 4 h and 24 h exposure to raw or roasted peanut protein extracts (1 mg/mL), purified Ara h 1 (raw or roasted; 0.1 mg/mL), lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 µg/mL), or gelatin (1 mg/mL) (Figure 12). In Caco-2 cells, exposure to raw peanut extract at 4 h resulted in a significant increase in NO production compared with untreated cells (p < 0.001), whereas roasted peanut extract elicited a lower but still significant response (p < 0.01). Treatment with purified Ara h 1, in both raw (p < 0.05) and roasted (p < 0.001) forms, also induced significant NO production at 4 h time point. In contrast, gelatin and LPS did not significantly alter NO levels at 4 h. After 24 h, NO production was further elevated following exposure to raw peanut extract (p < 0.05), indicating a time-dependent response. Although roasted peanut extract and purified Ara h 1 (raw or roasted) induced moderate increases in NO production at 24 h, these effects did not reach statistical significance, likely due to variability in the data. LPS treatment resulted in a significant increase in NO levels at 24 h (p < 0.01) (Figure 12a). In T84 cells, exposure to raw peanut extract at 4 h induced a significant increase in NO production relative to untreated cells (p < 0.001), followed by a lower but significant response to roasted peanut extract (p < 0.05). In contrast, purified Ara h 1 (raw or roasted) did not induce significant NO production at this early point. LPS treatment resulted in a significant elevation of NO levels compared with controls (p < 0.01). After 24 h, NO production was further increased following exposure to raw peanut extract (p < 0.0001), again demonstrating a time-dependent effect. Roasted peanut extract also induced a significant, albeit lower, increase in NO production (p < 0.01). Purified Ara h 1 (raw) induced a modest but significant increase in NO production at 24 h (p < 0.05), whereas purified Ara h 1 (roasted) did not. LPS treatment again resulted in significantly elevated NO levels (p < 0.01; Figure 12b).

3.7. IL-6 Response of THP-1 Macrophages to Basolateral Supernatants

To evaluate the immunological consequences of peanut protein exposure, differentiated THP-1 macrophages were treated with basolateral supernatants collected from Caco-2 and T84 cell monolayers previously exposed to raw or roasted peanut extracts. Treatment with supernatants from both intestinal epithelial cell lines resulted in elevated IL-6 release compared to untreated controls. Notably, macrophages treated with basolateral supernatants from Caco-2 monolayers exposed to peanut extracts released significantly higher levels of IL-6 compared with untreated cells. Specifically, IL-6 levels reached approximately 20 pg/mL following exposure to supernatants from raw peanut-treated Caco-2 cells (p < 0.05) and ~38 pg/mL following roasted peanut exposure (p < 0.01). These responses were markedly greater than those induced by supernatants derived from T84 monolayers (~14 pg/mL for raw and ~7 pg/mL for roasted), which did not reach statistical significance compared with untreated cells (Figure 13b). In T84-derived samples, the raw peanut extract induced higher IL-6 secretion than the roasted extract, whereas in Caco-2 cells, exposure to roasted peanut extract elicited a stronger IL-6 response than the raw extract. These findings indicate that both epithelial barrier properties and cell-specific characteristics modulate macrophage cytokine responses to translocated peanut allergens.

4. Discussion

Understanding the earliest events that govern allergic sensitization is essential for improving both mechanistic insight and predictive allergenicity assessment. Although the principal immune pathways activated by dietary allergens have been extensively characterized, the molecular and cellular processes that initiate allergen-specific IgE production remain incompletely understood [3]. In parallel, current allergenicity assessment approaches exhibit limited predictive accuracy, underscoring the need for more physiologically relevant experimental models that better reflect epithelial and immune mechanisms of sensitization, particularly in the context of emerging and novel food sources [57]. Peanut allergy exemplifies these challenges as a highly prevalent and potentially life-threatening condition. Despite extensive study, the mechanisms by which peanut allergens interact with, traverse, and modulate the intestinal epithelial barrier, a key regulator of immune priming, are still not fully defined. Increasing evidence implicates epithelial barrier dysfunction, including altered tight-junction integrity and enhanced permeability, in the pathogenesis of food allergy [58], yet no consensus exists on which in vitro epithelial model best reflects physiological allergen transport, as commonly used models such as Caco-2 and T84 monolayers, intestinal organoids, and gut-on-chip systems differ substantially in differentiation status, barrier properties, and immunological relevance [30,31]. To address this gap, we compared two widely used, although simplified, intestinal epithelial models—Caco-2 and T84 monolayers, which differ in their differentiation status, tight-junction organization, and innate immune responsiveness [32,59]. Transcript analysis revealed distinct temporal patterns of Toll-like receptor expression. Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2), which contributes to epithelial inflammatory regulation and barrier maintenance, was slightly higher in early-differentiated T84 cells [60], but increased later in Caco-2 cells. Early TLR2 elevation in T84 may support their rapid tight-junction assembly through PKC-dependent ZO-1 recruitment via PI3K/Akt and MyD88 signaling [61], whereas higher TLR2 expression in mature Caco-2 monolayers aligns with previous reports [62], though their reduced TLR1/6 co-receptor levels may limit functional signaling and contribute to their weaker junctional phenotype [62,63].

Both epithelial models expressed Toll-like receptor 5 (TLR5), a pattern-recognition receptor for bacterial flagellin, whose activation in intestinal epithelial cells is known to elicit robust NF-κB-dependent inflammatory signaling, promote chemokine secretion, and support epithelial restitution and early antibacterial defense [64]. Caco-2 cells exhibited greater TLR5 transcript abundance, consistent with their more differentiated, brush border-like epithelial phenotype [32,65]. In contrast to T84, where TLR5 is basolaterally restricted and triggers a strong, sustained NF-κB/IL-8 response, Caco-2 can respond to flagellin at both surfaces, albeit with a weaker and rapidly downregulated signaling profile [65,66]. These differences in response patterns likely reflect distinct epithelial polarity and differentiation state between the two models.

T84 cells also exhibited elevated expression of the endosomal pattern-recognition receptor TLR7, which detects single-stranded RNA and is implicated in the regulation of epithelial cytokine production and barrier transport processes [67,68]. This suggests a greater capacity for antiviral and inflammatory signaling compared with Caco-2. Collectively, differential expression of TLR2, TLR5, and TLR7 between the two epithelial models underscores the importance of model selection for studies of epithelial innate immunity, while also highlighting that transcript abundance does not always predict functional responsiveness, emphasizing the need for complementary signaling analyses in future work.

T84 cells exhibited stronger tight-junction organization, with higher ZO-1 and claudin-2, and -3 expression and formation of a markedly tighter paracellular barrier, consistent with reports of high-resistance T84 monolayers [69]. In contrast, Caco-2 cells showed higher E-cadherin and ALP expression levels, reflecting their enterocyte-like absorptive phenotype and common use as small-intestinal models [32]. TEER measurements corroborated these differences: Caco-2 monolayers reached >500 Ω·cm2 by Day 15, whereas T84 cells exceeded 1000 Ω·cm2 within one week and can surpass 1500–2000 Ω·cm2 under optimized conditions [69,70] confirming their substantially tighter barrier.

These differences in TJ architectures have important implications for peanut allergen transport. The dense junctional network and very high TEER of T84 monolayers create a restrictive paracellular environment in which allergen passage is expected to be minimal and likely limited to low-level transcellular routes or subtle, transient TJ alterations [71]. In contrast, the lower TEER and more absorptive phenotype of Caco-2 cells provide a more permissive interface, allowing allergens to use both paracellular and transcellular pathways [34,54,72]. Thus, T84 cells primarily model a highly restrictive barrier where TJ disruption is required for significant paracellular movement, whereas Caco-2 monolayers better approximate the physiological small-intestinal epithelium, where allergens traverse via combined leak and active uptake mechanisms [32,73,74]. Together, these complementary models offer a broader view of epithelial processes relevant to peanut allergen transport.

The intestinal epithelial barrier plays a central role in regulating immune responses and preventing inappropriate allergen uptake, and its disruption has been linked to food allergy and other chronic diseases [75,76]. To assess how peanut allergens affect barrier function, Caco-2 and T84 monolayers were exposed to raw and roasted peanut protein extracts, followed by assessment of transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER), Lucifer Yellow permeability, epithelial cytokine release, allergen translocation, and ZO-1 localization.

Peanut exposure caused a significant TEER reduction within 4 h, indicating rapid functional changes in tight-junction pore pathways without overt structural damage. Lucifer Yellow flux, however, remained unchanged—consistent with its requirement for larger-scale junctional opening which is consistent with previous studies [54]. Because TEER is sensitive to subtle claudin-mediated alterations [77,78], these data suggest that early peanut-induced junctional modulation is detectable electrically but not via macromolecular tracers.

Despite the absence of dye flux, low levels of Ara h 1 were detected basolaterally by ELISA inhibition and immunoblotting, demonstrating that allergens can cross the epithelium even when paracellular leak is not apparent. This indicates transcellular routes, such as endocytosis or receptor-mediated uptake, as early mechanisms of allergen transport [54,79]. ZO-1 levels remained unchanged after 4 h; however, previous studies have reported peanut-induced redistribution of other tight junction components, which may account for the observed moderate reduction in TEER in the absence of increased paracellular dye permeability [54].

Collectively, these results reveal a disconnect between TEER and allergen passage vs. dye permeability, showing that peanut extracts trigger subtle early junctional disturbances while enabling selective, transcellular allergen transport. These findings underscore the importance of using multiple complementary readouts, as dye-based assays alone may underestimate early permeability changes relevant to sensitization.

As the transport of allergens across the intestinal epithelium affects their allergenicity [4], even low-level allergen translocation can be immunologically meaningful. In our study, macrophages exposed to basolateral samples from raw and roasted peanut protein extract-treated epithelial monolayers secreted increased IL-6, indicating that small amounts of Ara h 1 crossing the barrier are sufficient to activate innate immune cells. This aligns with prior work showing that peanut allergens are highly sensitizing even at low concentrations [80]. It has been demonstrated that human macrophages directly recognize and internalize natural Ara h 1, triggering dose-dependent cytokine release and engaging receptors such as scavenger receptor class A type I (CD204) depending on macrophage phenotype [81]. Thus, even minimal allergen passage, such as that observed under conditions of partial TJ alteration, may be biologically sufficient to initiate mucosal immune activation.

Cytokine profiling revealed that complex peanut protein extracts, particularly in their raw form, elicited a coordinated epithelial cytokine response marked by induction of IL-1β and the type 2-associated alarmins TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33 in both Caco-2 and T84 cells. The heightened bioactivity of raw peanut extracts may reflect the preservation of heat-labile components, including peanut lectins [82] and native protein conformations [83], which are known to interact with epithelial surface glycans and pattern-recognition receptors, thereby enhancing epithelial activation. In contrast, thermal processing such as roasting can alter protein structure through denaturation, aggregation, or Maillard reactions [84,85], potentially reducing the availability or accessibility of epithelial-interacting motifs. Together, these factors may enhance the capacity of raw peanut extracts to interact with and perturb epithelial barrier function, a process known to promote the release of epithelial-derived alarmins, such as TSLP and IL-33, thereby activating innate immune surveillance pathways [86]. The lack of comparable responses to purified Ara h 1, gelatin, or LPS suggests that epithelial activation is not necessarily mediated by a single allergenic protein or endotoxin contamination but rather reflects matrix-dependent properties of whole peanut extract. This supports the concept that food allergenicity arises from integrated structural and biochemical features of complex food matrices [87]. The prominent induction of epithelial alarmins is consistent with their established roles in initiating type 2 immune polarization at barrier surfaces [88,89] and suggests that peanut exposure can prime a pro-allergic epithelial milieu without overt barrier disruption. Cell line-specific differences in cytokine magnitude and kinetics further highlight the influence of epithelial identity on allergen sensing. Notably, IL-1β induction by raw peanut extract, but not LPS, implies engagement of non-canonical innate pathways, potentially involving epithelial stress or inflammasome-associated signaling [90]. Collectively, these findings identify epithelial-derived cytokines as key early mediators linking peanut exposure to downstream immune activation.

The induction of oxidative and nitrosative stress by peanut extract exposure further supports the capacity of complex peanut extracts to directly activate epithelial innate stress pathways. It is known that inflammatory mediators, including cytokines and reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, can destabilize tight junctions through oxidative stress-mediated post-translational modification of junctional proteins and modulation of kinase- and phosphatase-dependent signaling pathways [91]. Consistent with these mechanisms, whole peanut extracts elicited coordinated increases in ROS, NO, and epithelial-derived cytokines, indicative of epithelial stress signaling that may precede functional barrier alterations without overt tight junction disruption. Raw peanut extracts consistently induced the strongest oxidative responses, whereas roasted extracts produced a comparatively attenuated but still significant effect, suggesting partial modulation by thermal processing. In contrast, purified Ara h 1 showed limited capacity to induce ROS, indicating that oxidative stress responses are largely driven by the composite peanut matrix rather than a single allergen.

Similarly, nitric oxide production followed a matrix- and time-dependent pattern, with whole peanut extracts inducing significantly higher NO levels than roasted and purified Ara h 1, particularly at later time points. The time-dependent accumulation of NO in response to raw peanut extract suggests sustained epithelial activation rather than an acute toxic effect [92]. The modest or absent responses to purified Ara h 1 further indicate that full epithelial activation requires multiple peanut-derived components or processing-dependent structural features.

Peanut extracts also rapidly affected epithelial metabolic activity, as evidenced by reduced CCK-8 signal in both Caco-2 and T84 cells. Because CCK-8 reflects mitochondrial dehydrogenase activity [93], these decreases suggest early metabolic stress and reduced proliferation, consistent with the absence of morphological damage or major junctional disruption. Roasted extracts induced stronger effects than raw extracts, implying that processing enhances the metabolic impact of peanut components. LDH release, however, could not be interpreted reliably: both extracts directly inhibited LDH enzymatic activity, as confirmed using purified LDH, masking any true changes in cytotoxicity. Such inhibition likely reflects interactions between peanut-derived compounds, such as polyphenols or Maillard products, and LDH cofactors.

Together, these findings show that peanut extracts can trigger early metabolic disturbances and low-level immune activation without causing overt epithelial injury. This highlights the need for multiple complementary readouts to accurately interpret epithelial responses to food allergens. However, despite their utility, in vitro epithelial monolayers such as Caco-2 and T84 remain simplified systems that lack the physiological complexity of the human intestine, including mucus secretion, immune–epithelial crosstalk, dynamic flow, and microbial interactions. As such, they capture only a subset of the mechanisms relevant to allergen uptake and early sensitization. Advancing toward more physiologically relevant platforms, such as co-cultures, organoids, and gut-on-chip systems, will be essential for better modeling the intricate barrier, immune, and metabolic processes that shape allergic sensitization in vivo.

5. Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that Caco-2 and T84 epithelial models, though often used interchangeably, differ substantially in barrier tightness, junctional architecture, transport capacity, and sensitivity to allergen-induced functional changes. These intrinsic differences determine how each model captures distinct aspects of peanut–epithelium interactions, with T84 cells representing a highly restrictive barrier and Caco-2 cells more closely reflecting absorptive small intestinal physiology. We further show that peanut allergens can induce early functional and metabolic disturbances and traverse the epithelial barrier at low levels without overt tight junction disruption, emphasizing the active role of the epithelium in initiating immune signaling. Together, these findings advance our understanding of epithelial-level responses and early allergen–epithelium interactions that may be relevant to the initial stages of allergen exposure and highlight the value of using complementary epithelial platforms and more physiologically relevant systems in future work to improve predictive models for food allergenicity and safety assessment.
