Effect of concomitant chemotherapy on edoxaban plasma concentration in patients with colorectal cancer
Authors: Jihyung Song, Kei Kimura, Kozo Kataoka, Hirofumi Ota, Masayoshi Yasui, Taishi Hata, Takashi Daimon, Masataka Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeDirect oral anticoagulants are a treatment option for cancer-associated venous thromboembolism (VTE). In patients receiving chemotherapy for cancer, there may be pharmacokinetic drug–drug interaction between anti-cancer agents and direct oral anticoagulants. However, there are no data available in patients with colorectal cancer (CRC). In this study, we investigated edoxaban plasma concentration in patients with CRC receiving chemotherapy.MethodsThe study included patients with unresectable or metastatic CRC undergoing systemic chemotherapy and simultaneously receiving anticoagulant therapy with edoxaban for VTE or atrial fibrillation. Blood sampling was performed prior to chemotherapy and after 24 h and 48 h depending on the chemotherapy regimen.ResultsData of 21 and 26 patients were analyzed for edoxaban plasma concentration and safety, respectively. Edoxaban plasma concentration was not statistically different before and after 24 h and 48 h between patients receiving treatment with a chemotherapy regimen, cytotoxic drug, and a molecular targeted drug. One patient had major bleeding requiring transfusion; however, edoxaban plasma concentration was not elevated before and 24 h after chemotherapy (19.98 and 9.63 ng/ml, respectively).ConclusionIn patients with CRC, edoxaban plasma concentration did not change after chemotherapy, implying that drug–drug interaction between edoxaban and anti-cancer agents was minimal.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed type of malignant cancer worldwide, with increasing incidence and mortality rates [1]. Although surgical treatment [2, 3] and adjuvant treatment [4] have been improved, there remains a need for systemic chemotherapy for recurrent or locally advanced unresectable CRC [5]. Because of the improvement in anti-cancer drugs and treatment regimens, the exposure of patients to such agents became longer and more complex.

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is a leading cause of death among patients with cancer [6]. Patients with cancer are at an increased risk of thromboembolic disease because of their hypercoagulability, particularly in cases with advanced and recurrent disease. Besides hypercoagulability, patients with CRC have risk factors such as immobilization, surgeries including extended lymphadenectomy, intravenous catheter use, and chemotherapy use (e.g., cytotoxic agents, targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors) [7]. The incidence of VTE is low in patients with CRC compared to those with gastric or pancreatic cancer. However, due to the large number of patients with CRC, we frequently encounter VTE in patients with CRC in daily practice [8]. VTE is often observed in patients with active cancer receiving chemotherapy. Therefore, it is important to avoid interaction between anticoagulant therapy for VTE and cancer treatment.

Recently, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have been widely used for cancer-associated VTE. For the safe administration of DOACs, drug–drug interaction should be considered [9], because it may cause unexpected bleeding. Inhibitors or inducers of P-glycoprotein and cytochrome P450 family 3 subfamily A member 4 (CYP3A4) influence the metabolism of DOACs and potentially alter their efficacy and/or safety profiles. Nevertheless, the extent to which these drugs affect the blood concentrations of DOACs is unknown. Oxaliplatin, irinotecan, and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) are commonly used cytotoxic agents in chemotherapy for CRC. Apart from cytotoxic agents, molecular targeted therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors have been widely used in recent years. However, there are no clinical data on the effect of chemotherapeutic agents on the plasma concentration of DOACs in patients with CRC.

In this study, we investigated the influence and safety of orally administered edoxaban in patients with CRC undergoing chemotherapy by measuring its plasma concentration.

Patients and methods

Study design

This was a single-arm, multicenter prospective study. The investigation was funded by Daiichi Sankyo Co., Ltd., (Tokyo, Japan). Patient enrollment occurred from December 21, 2020 to December 31, 2023. The protocol was carried out in accordance with the tenets stipulated in the Declaration of Helsinki, Japanese ethical guidelines on clinical research, and Ethical Guidelines for Clinical Studies. The study was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (protocol ID: UMIN 000043850). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Hyogo Medical University Clinical Research Review Committee (protocol ID: 3676) on December 21, 2020. All patients provided written informed consent. The institutional review board at each participating center approved the protocol.

Patients

Patients with metastatic CRC who were undergoing systemic chemotherapy and simultaneously receiving anticoagulant therapy with edoxaban for VTE or for prevention of systemic thrombosis by atrial fibrillation were eligible to participate in this study. Additional inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥ 18 years; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status 0–2; written informed consent; white blood cell count ≥ 3,000/µl; platelet count ≥ 100,000/µl; serum total bilirubin level ≤ 2.0 mg/dl; serum aspartate aminotransferase level ≤ 100 IU/l; serum alanine aminotransferase level ≤ 100 IU/l; and serum creatinine level ≤ 1.5 mg/dl. Patients with bleeding, bleeding tendency, use of unfractionated heparin, low molecular heparin, danaparoid, anti-thrombin agents such as argatroban, vitamin K antagonist, thrombolytic agents, anti-platelet agents, or strong inhibitor for CYP3A4, and history of cranial bleeding were excluded from the study.

Outcome assessment

The primary endpoint of this study was the assessment of change in edoxaban plasma concentration utilizing plasma Xa activity in patients treated with commonly used chemotherapy regimens for CRC. The dose of edoxaban was prescribed by the attending physicians, and a dose of 30 mg was prescribed when patients met any of the following criteria: body weight ≤ 60 kg; moderate renal impairment (creatinine clearance 30–50 ml/min) or concomitant use of potent P-glycoprotein inhibitors. The blood sampling schedule is shown in Fig. 1. Patients with unresectable or metastatic CRC were scheduled to receive cytotoxic agents (i.e., 5-FU, oxaliplatin, or irinotecan) with or without molecular targeted agents (i.e., anti-angiogenic, vascular endothelial growth factor [VEGF] agent, or anti-epidermal growth factor receptor [anti-EGFR] agent). Blood samples (2–5 ml) were collected by venipuncture with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid immediately before the administration of edoxaban and initiation of chemotherapy. For the 1-day regimen (capecitabine + oxaliplatin), the next blood sampling was performed 24 h after the initiation of chemotherapy before the administration of edoxaban. For the 2-day regimen (FOLFOX [folinic acid, fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin]/FOLFIRI [folinic acid, fluorouracil, and irinotecan]+/-anti-VEGF agent or anti-EGFR agent), blood sampling was performed 24 h and 48 h after the administration of edoxaban and chemotherapy (Fig. 1). Blood samples were immediately centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 5 min, and plasma was stored at − 20℃ until analysis. Edoxaban is a direct anti-Xa anticoagulant; hence, its concentration was determined with the chromogenic assay using BIOPHEN Edoxaban Control and BIOPHEN edoxaban Calibrator (Hyphen BioMed; Neuville-sur-Oise, France). Edoxaban concentration was determined thrice for each sample, and the average of the three values was used for the analysis by LSI Medience Corporation (Tokyo, Japan).

Any adverse events, such as bleeding and thrombotic recurrence, were recorded up to 12 months after enrollment. Bleeding events were defined as a combination of major or clinically relevant non-major bleeding events. Major bleeding events were considered those characterized by a decrease in hemoglobin by ≥ 2 g/dl in 24 h, requiring transfusion of ≥ 2 units of blood, occurring at a critical site (i.e., intra-cranial, intra-spine, intraocular, intrapericardium, retroperitoneum), or contributing to death according to the criteria defined by the International Society of Thrombosis and Heamostasis [10]. Clinically relevant non-major bleeding events were considered those that did not meet the criteria for major bleeding, but were associated with the need for medical intervention (e.g., contact with a physician or discontinuation of drug administration). Cases of recurrent thrombosis included those identified by imaging studies with or without symptoms, and those in which a thrombus resulted in death.

Statistics

Continuous variables are presented as medians and ranges, and categorical variables are presented as numbers. Serial edoxaban plasma concentrations were compared among treatment regimen groups: 1) oxaliplatin-based regimen; 2) oxaliplatin-based regimen + anti-VEGF agent; 3) oxaliplatin-based regimen + anti-EGFR agent; 4) irinotecan-based regimen + anti-VEGF agent; 5) irinotecan-based regimen + anti-EGFR agent (Comparison #1). Mixed-effects models with repeated measures were used with the main effects of time point and treatment regimen group as categorical variables, the interaction effect between them, and the dose of edoxaban as a categorical covariate. In addition, the following treatment regimen groups were investigated: 6) cytotoxic agents + anti-VEGF agent versus 7) cytotoxic agents + anti-EGFR agent (Comparison #2); and 8) molecular targeted agent + oxaliplatin versus 9) molecular targeted agent + irinotecan (Comparison #3). These effects were included as fixed effects in the mixed-effects models with repeated measures. The fixed effects were tested using the F-test in analysis of variance with their corresponding type III sum of squares. A p-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and R software, version 4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Sample size was based on the feasibility to determine the plasma concentration of edoxaban in patients with CRC undergoing chemotherapy. Enrollment of 50 patients (10 patients for each of the five candidate regimens) was planned.

Results

A total of 26 patients were enrolled in this study. As the number of eligible patients was limited, all 26 patients were registered during the planned study period. None of patients enrolled in this study used strong inhibitor or inducer of CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein. One patient received combination therapy with oxaliplatin and irinotecan; as this regimen was not allowed in the present study, this patient was excluded from the primary analysis. For four other patients (oxaliplatin-based regimen [n = 2]; oxaliplatin-based regimen + anti-VEGF agent [n = 1]; and oxaliplatin-based regimen + anti-EGFR agent [n = 1]), the sampling time was invalid; therefore these patients were also excluded from the primary analysis (Fig. 2). Hence, the final analysis included 21 patients. Table 1 shows the characteristics of the 21 patients (male: n = 16, female: n = 5; median age: 74 years; and median body mass index: 21.4 kg/m2). Among them, 11 and 10 patients had rectal and colon cancer, respectively. Edoxaban was indicated for the treatment of VTE and atrial fibrillation in 17 and four patients, respectively. Regarding the dose of edoxaban, one, 16, and four patients received 15, 30, and 60 mg, respectively. Mean (SD) value of all measured concentration of edoxaban was 33.9 (31.6) ng/mL. These values were fall within the expected range of trough edoxaban concentration (12 ~ 43 ng/mL) [11]. All chemotherapy regimens are listed in Table 2: oxaliplatin-based regimen (n = 4); oxaliplatin-based regimen + anti-VEGF agent (n = 3); oxaliplatin-based regimen + anti-EGFR agent (n = 8); irinotecan-based regimen + anti-VEGF agent (n = 3); and irinotecan-based regimen + anti-EGFR agent (n = 3). Figures 3, 4 and 5 show the average change in plasma concentration before and after chemotherapy depending on the dose of edoxaban in Comparison #1–3, respectively. In the three comparisons, there were no statistically significant differences in edoxaban plasma concentration between the time points of before and 24 h and 48 h after the initiation of chemotherapy (Tables 3, 4 and 5). During the observation period, one patient had major bleeding (i.e., grade 3 gastrointestinal bleeding). In this patient, administration of edoxaban was discontinued and transfusion was performed. This patient received treatment with capecitabine, oxaliplatin, and bevacizumab. The plasma concentration before and 24 h after the initiation of chemotherapy was 19.98 and 9.63 ng/ml, respectively. There was no occurrence of other bleeding events.

Discussion

In patients with advanced or unresectable CRC, continuation of chemotherapy is important and interruption due to VTE treatment should be avoided. In the era of vitamin K antagonists, treatment cessation was mandatory because of the use of 5-FU. At present, DOACs can be used for the treatment of cancer-associated venous thrombosis. Therefore, investigation of interaction between DOACs and anti-cancer agents is necessary. In this study, the plasma concentration of edoxaban was not statistically different after 24–48 h of chemotherapy compared with baseline values. This finding suggests minimal interactions between edoxaban and CRC treatment regimens. In addition, we did not find any relationship between adverse events and high edoxaban plasma concentration.

DOACs may have drug–drug interaction with numerous anti-tumor agents. For drugs that result in strong modulation of CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein (e.g., doxorubicin, imatinib, and sunitinib), care should be taken when used with DOACs [9, 12]. In clinical practice, concomitant administration of anti-tumor agents and apixaban did not increase the risk of VTE recurrence and major bleeding compared with no administration of anti-tumor agents. This evidence suggested that drug–drug interactions were minimal between DOACs and anti-tumor agents [13]. Cytotoxic agents and molecular targeted agents for advanced or unresectable CRC might influence the metabolism of DOACs. However, changes in the plasma concentration of DOACs during chemotherapy in patients with CRC have not been examined. The plasma concentration of edoxaban has been investigated in patients with ovarian and uterine corpus cancer receiving carboplatin, paclitaxel, epirubicin, and bevacizumab [14]. The trough concentration of edoxaban in patients undergoing chemotherapy was not significantly increased after treatment. Carboplatin, paclitaxel, and epirubicin do not exert an inhibitory effect on CYP3A4 [15]. Thus, there was no elevation of edoxaban plasma concentration observed in this setting. Oxaliplatin is a key cytotoxic drug for the treatment of CRC. It is metabolized nonenzymatically; therefore, its effect on DOACs would be negligible [ 16]. 5-FU is catabolized to its inactive form by dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase and does not interact with CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein [17]. Irinotecan is another cytotoxic agent frequently used in the treatment of CRCs; this drug exerts an inhibitory effect on CYP3A4 [18]. Therefore, we hypothesized that there would be differences in plasma concentration between regimens based on oxaliplatin and irinotecan (Comparison #3); however, we did not detect such differences. This result may be attributed to the small sample size of this study. Bevacizumab, cetuximab, and panitumumab are monoclonal antibodies. Consequently, they are not cleared by cytochrome p450, and drug–drug interactions are minimal [19].

In one patient who received FOLFOX+panitumumab and 30 mg of edoxaban, we found high plasma concentration (232.76 ng/ml) at 48 h after the initiation of chemotherapy. In this patient, plasma edoxaban concentration before chemotherapy was 22.69 ng/mL and 31.64 ng/ml at 24 h, which were comparable to the trough value [11]. As the high concentration at 48 h was comparable to the Cmax value [20], we assume that this was due to the delayed sampling time. Nevertheless, bleeding did not occur in this patient. In another patient, major bleeding was observed on the following day after blood sampling; the plasma concentration of edoxaban was 19.98 and 9.63 ng/ml before and 24 h after the initiation of chemotherapy, respectively. As chemotherapy did not increase the plasma concentration of edoxaban, this bleeding event was not due to the concomitant treatment.

In all patients included in this study, the primary lesion had already been resected and there was no recurrent tumor invading and exposing the gastrointestinal tract. In the Hokusai-VTE Cancer study, some gastrointestinal bleeding was observed in patients with a primary tumor [21, 22]. As edoxaban is active in the gastrointestinal tract, the existence of a primary tumor is a risk factor for bleeding. In this study, edoxaban could be safely administered in patients without a primary tumor and gastrointestinal tract invasion, however we need more cases to prove the safety of concomitant administration of edoxaban in patients with colorectal cancer.

This study had several limitations. Firstly, the sample size was small and limited pharmacokinetic sampling time; hence, the investigation did not have sufficient statistical power to detect small differences. Secondly, only trough concentrations were examined so that AUC could not be calculated. Thirdly, measurements were performed only at 24 h and 48 h; thus, the effect of chemotherapy on the plasma concentration of edoxaban beyond 48 h was not determined, and only trough concentrations without verifying more accurate pharmacokinetics were measured. We also did not include a drug-free control group. Finally, in patients receiving strong CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein, and in patients with renal dysfunction, this study data cannot be applied. In same way, analgesics were not examined in this study. Analgesics (e.g., morphine, steroids, and antiemetics) are often used in patients with cancer. These drugs are metabolized by CYP3A4 [23, 24]; consequently, the plasma concentration of edoxaban may be affected by these drugs. We should always consider bleeding risk, renal function, tumor location, and concomitant drug-drug interaction when using a DOAC.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this is the first study investigating the effect of concomitant chemotherapy on the plasma concentration of edoxaban in patients with CRC. Edoxaban plasma concentration was not different in samples obtained before and after the initiation of chemotherapy. The present results imply minimal drug–drug interaction between edoxaban and chemotherapy agents used for the treatment of CRC. Further studies with a larger number of patients and detailed blood concentration measurements are warranted.
