The transcription factor SREBP2 supports the activation and homeostasis of CD4+ Foxp3+ regulatory T cells
Authors: Acacia N Shouse, Alejandro V Villarino, Thomas R Malek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriodic activation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) is necessary to restrict autoreactive T cells and maintain immune homeostasis. IL-2–STAT5 signaling instructs several metabolic processes underlying Treg activation, including the mevalonate pathway, which is crucial for optimal Treg responses. However, direct links between IL-2–STAT5, mevalonate, and Treg function have yet to be established. To explore this relationship, we deleted sterol regulatory binding element protein 2 (Srebp2), a critical mediator of mevalonate metabolism, selectively in Tregs. Here, we show that when Srebp2-deficient Tregs are placed under physiological stress by competing with wild-type Tregs, the former exhibit impaired proliferation, survival, and activation. RNA sequencing of Tregs in this competitive setting demonstrate that Srebp2 contributes to pathways controlling IL-2R signaling, cholesterol homeostasis, activities associated with the mevalonate pathway, and expression of genes associated with activated Tregs. These findings link Srebp2, the IL-2R, and the mevalonate pathways for efficient Treg homeostasis and activation. These effects, however, require a competitive setting, as mice with only Srebp2-deficient Tregs were outwardly normal with respect to their development, homeostasis, metabolic activity, and function. Thus, Srebp2 is necessary to optimally tune Treg homeostasis and activation, but sufficient redundancy exists to compensate for the lack of Srebp2 to maintain Treg development and function in the absence of physiological stress.Keywords: homeostasis, IL-2, Srebp2, Tregs

Introduction

Regulatory T cells (Tregs) are essential to restrain autoreactive CD4+ T cells that escape thymic negative selection. This homeostasis depends in part on the transition of resting Treg cells (rTregs) to effector or activated cells (aTregs) in the face of immunologic stress such as during inflammation or infection.1–3 Signals from the T cell receptor (TCR), IL-2, and costimulation are critical for the transition from rTregs to aTregs.4–6 One way that TCR and IL-2R signaling promotes Treg differentiation is by instructing mevalonate metabolism and protein prenylation.7–10 When the mevalonate pathway or protein prenylation are ablated in Tregs, activation is impaired,7 leading to reduced Treg function and inability to constrain an antitumor response.8 Importantly, mTORC1 and STAT5 are each downstream of IL-2R and known to regulate T cell mevalonate metabolism,9,11,12 suggesting a key role for IL-2 in regulation of this pathway. Indeed, germline ablation of CD25 selectively in Tregs or treating Tregs with a strong IL-2 agonist show reciprocal effects on the expression of genes participating in mevalonate metabolism.13,14 IL-2R signaling controls the relative abundance of rTreg and aTreg subsets,3,4,13 and may do so in part through regulation of the mevalonate pathway.

The mevalonate pathway supports multiple cellular processes through synthesis of cholesterol, dolichol, heme-A, and ubiquinone.15 Cholesterol is the main product of the mevalonate pathway; however, multiple essential nonsteroid, nonhormonal cellular processes are dependent on pathway intermediates, namely farnesyl pyrophosphate and isopentenyl pyrophosphate. Elaboration of the mevalonate pathway is mainly controlled by 2 transcription factors: sterol regulatory element binding protein 1 (Srebp1) and Srebp2.16 Srebp1 and Srebp2 are structurally related, sharing 47% of their overall sequence and 71% of the basic helix-loop-helix-leucine zipper region, which allows them bind to many of the same gene loci.17 However, Srebp1 is the predominant regulator of genes involved in fatty acid synthesis and Srebp2 is more potent in activating genes involved in the mevalonate pathway.18,19 A key target of Srebp2 is HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme in the mevalonate pathway (Fig. 1A). Normally, Srebp2 is sequestered at the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum, but when cellular cholesterol is low, it is cleaved and translocates to the nucleus, where it binds and upregulates expression of genes in the mevalonate pathway.16 To study the relationship between Srebp2, mevalonate metabolism, and aTreg generation, we developed a mouse model in which Srebp2 is selectively deleted in Tregs, then assessed downstream cellular and molecular consequences. These studies revealed that Srebp2 is dispensable under steady-state conditions but critically important for Treg homeostasis and IL-2–dependent activation when stressed by competition with wild-type Tregs.

Methods

Mice

C57BL6 Foxp3YFP-cre (Model 000664) and Srebp2FL/FL (Model 031792) mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. All strains were bred within the specific pathogen–free animal facility at the University of Miami. Genotyping was performed by polymerase chain reaction amplification using the primers and protocols recommended by the Jackson Laboratory. Females and males were used interchangeably in these studies, unless otherwise stated. To measure the Treg response to IL-2 in vivo, mice were injected intraperitoneally with mouse IL-2/CD25 (20 µg)20 dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or PBS as the control. Animal studies were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Miami (Protocol number 22-128).

Lymphocyte isolation

Whole spleen, thymus, and lymph nodes were dissected out, and single-cell suspension was obtained by mechanical disruption in cold 1× Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS). Bone marrow was collected by isolating the femur from surrounding soft tissue, crushing the bone using a ceramic mortar and pestle in cold 1× HBSS. Red blood cells were lysed by resuspending in ammonium-chloride-potassium buffer and incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 1 min. Cells were washed in 1× HBSS, and a 70 µm mesh was used to filter the cells prior to cell surface staining.

Small intestinal lymphocytes and Peyer’s patches were collected by isolating the small intestine 0.5 cm proximal to the cecum and 0.5 cm distal to the stomach. Fecal matter was expelled using the flat end of a pair of forceps and the lumen was distended by inserting a probe through the lumen to display the Peyer’s patches. The Peyer’s patches were collected using forceps and fine-tipped scissors, placed in a petri, and prepared for flow cytometry using the same steps as for the other tissues described previously. After the Peyer’s patches were removed, the lumen of the small intestine was revealed by making an incision along the entire intestinal wall with scissors. Mucous was gently scraped from the lumen using the flat end of forceps. The remaining intestinal tissue was then placed in a petri dish with 5 mL of wash buffer (calcium- and magnesium-free HBSS containing 2.5% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine) and minced very finely with scissors. The contents of the petri dish were then poured into a 50 mL tube and washed twice in 25 mL wash buffer. The tissue was incubated in 30 mL calcium- and magnesium-free HBSS with 10% fetal bovine serum, 5 mM EDTA, and 15 mM HEPES (EDTA buffer) for 15 min at 37 °C with gentle agitation. Then, the tissue pieces were allowed to settle in the tube by gravity and the supernatant was carefully poured off and discarded. This was repeated by incubating again under the same conditions, allowing the tissue pieces to settle by gravity and pouring off the supernatant. Then, 25 mL of wash buffer was used to wash the tissue 2 times. After decanting the wash buffer, 30 mL wash buffer with 300 U/mL Collagenase Type 3 (Worthington; Cat#LS004182) and 10 µg/mL DNase I (Worthington; Cat#LS002006) was used to resuspend the tissue in the 50 mL tube. After allowing gravity to settle the pieces of tissue at the bottom of the tube, a 10 mL pipet was used to take up only the larger pieces at the bottom of the tube and transfer them to a petri dish where they were minced with fine-tipped scissors. The minced tissue was then recombined with the remaining sample in the 50 mL tube and incubated with gentle agitation at 37 °C for 40 min. Gravity again settled the tissue pieces at the bottom of the tube and the larger pieces were transferred to a petri dish in the same manner. These pieces were then briskly pipetted against the dish 10 times to further break up the tissue. The entire petri dish contents were then poured in with the remaining sample in the 50 mL tube and incubated for another 15 min with gentle agitation at 37 °C. After this time, 25 mL of wash buffer was used to thoroughly wash the cells 3 times followed by filtration using a 70 µm mesh. A 40/70% Percoll gradient (GE 1.130 g/mL; Cat#17-0891-01) was used to isolate lymphocytes by centrifuging at 700 g for 20 min at room temperature with no brake. The cells were then prepared for flow cytometry using the same protocol as other tissues.

Flow cytometry and FACS

Table S1 contains fluorescently labeled antibodies used for flow cytometry and FACS. Prior to cell surface staining, cells were incubated in 2.4G2 and washed in staining buffer (HBSS containing 0.2% bovine serum albumin and 0.1% [w/v] sodium azide). A total of 3 × 106 cells per sample (1 × 106 for small intestinal lamina propria) were stained for extracellular markers in staining buffer at 4 °C in the dark for 20 min. eBioscience Foxp3/transcription factor staining buffer set (Cat#00-5523-00) was used for fixation and permeabilization per manufacturer’s instructions and intracellular staining was performed in permeabilization buffer at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. Cells were washed in staining buffer and filtered a second time using 70 µm mesh, and flow cytometric analyses were performed using the Aurora Cytek (BD Biosciences) or CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter). FCS files were analyzed using Kaluza software (version 2.4).

Direct ex vivo phospho-staining was performed by immediately transferring 200 µL of mechanically dissociated cells into complete media (RPMI 1640 with 5% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 0.05 mM 2-mercaptoethanol) with 2% paraformaldehyde. The cells were incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 10 min. Cells were centrifuged and decanted. Cells were permeabilized in 100% methanol (500 µL) and incubated on ice for 30 min. The cells were washed twice with wash buffer (PBS with 5% bovine serum albumin and 0.02% [w/v] sodium azide). Both intracellular and extracellular staining was performed simultaneously for 1 h at 4 °C in the dark.

For cell sorting, a single-cell suspension of spleen and lymph nodes was generated using the steps outlined previously. CD4+ T cells were isolated from this suspension using negative selection (CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit [Miltenyi Biotec; #130-104-454] and LS Columns [Miltenyi Biotec; #130-042-401]). DAPI (Miltenyi Biotec; #130-111-570) was used at a final concentration of 0.1 μg/mL to determine viability. BD FACSAria II system was used to purify live CD4+ YFP+ Tregs from each sample. Cells were sorted into collection buffer (RPMI 1640 media containing 10% fetal bovine serum).

RNA sequencing

A minimum of 1 × 106 FACS-purified Tregs per sample were used for RNA isolation. RNA was isolated using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen; #74104) according to the manufacturer’s protocol for isolation of total RNA from animal cells using spin technology. Quality control, library preparation, and sequencing was conducted by the Oncogenomics Core at the University of Miami. The Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent) was used for quality control. KAPA’s RNA Hyperprep protocol with RiboErase (HMR) kit for rRNA depletion was used for library preparation and sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 on 100 bp paired-end reads with a S1-200 reagent kit.

HISAT2 (ver.2.2.1) aligner mapped RNA sequencing reads to the Mus musculus genome mm10. The mm10 reference based on UCSC annotations was used to generate raw counts. edgeR was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by glmQLF between Srebp2WT and Srebp2CompKO Tregs with a Log2 fold-change cutoff of 0.5 and adjusted P value <0.05. clusterProfiler determined upregulated and downregulated pathways from the DEG lists.

Apoptosis staining

A total of 2 × 106 splenocytes from Srebp2WT or Srebp2CompKO mice were stained with extracellular markers and washed twice with cold PBS as described previously in preparation for flow cytometry. The cells were resuspended in 2 mL 1× Annexin-V binding buffer (BD; #556454). 100 µL of each solution was transferred to a fresh tube and 5 µL of APC-Annexin-V (BD; #550474) and 5 µL of propidium iodide (BD; #556463) were added. Tubes were vortexed gently and placed at room temperature for 15 min. 400 µL of Annexin-V binding buffer was added to each tube and cells were immediately analyzed by cytometry.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism V.10 (GraphPad Software). Individual dot plots include the mean ± SD. Unpaired t tests were used to analyze the significance between groups. Significance is indicated by *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Treg function, development, and oxidative phosphorylation are outwardly normal in the absence of Srebp2

Foxp3YFPCre+/+ mice were crossed with Srebp2FL/FL mice to generate conditional “knockouts” in which Srebp2 is selectively ablated in Foxp3-expressing Tregs (Srebp2cKO). Tregs from these mice were compared with those from Foxp3YFPcre+/+Srebp2WT/WT controls (Srebp2WT). Efficient knockout of Srebp2 was confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction in purified YFP+ Tregs with primers to detect Srebp2 and 2 downstream targets, Hmgcs and Hmgcr (Fig. 1B). Srebp2 deficiency in Tregs did not obviously impact homeostasis as reflected in a similar frequency of YFP+ Tregs and conventional CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in lymphoid and nonlymphoid tissues (Fig. 1C, D). Absolute numbers of YFP+ Tregs and conventional CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the spleen were also comparable (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, Srebp2cKO mice were outwardly healthy and did not show signs of immune activation based on the expression of CD62L, CD44, KLRG1, Bcl-2, Ki67, CD39, or CD73 (Fig. 1F, G). Because the mevalonate pathway supports the generation of heme-A,15 a critical component of oxidative phosphorylation,21,22 oxygen consumption and extracellular acidification were compared in Tregs from Srebp2WT or Srebp2cKO mice. No change in oxygen consumption or extracellular acidification was detected at baseline, nor after challenge with oligomycin, FCCP, or rotenone/antimycin A (Fig. 1H). Collectively, these data indicate that Treg development and function are outwardly normal in the absence of Srebp2 at steady state.

Srebp2CompKO Tregs have reduced frequencies when in competition with wild-type Tregs

To further explore the role of Srebp2 in Tregs, we established a setting in which Srebp2-deficient Tregs are in competition with Srebp2WT Tregs. To that end, female Foxp3-CreYFP+/- mice were crossed with Srebp2FL/FL mice such that, due to X-inactivation, half of the Tregs in these mice are wild-type (WT) and half are Srebp2 deficient, annotated as Srebp2Comp. Only the Srebp2-deficient Tregs express YFP in this mouse (Srebp2CompKO) (Fig. 2A). WT Tregs that were fixed in paraformaldehyde and costained with anti-Foxp3 and anti-YFP are derived from Srebp2Comp mice when possible (Foxp3+YFP−, Srebp2CompWT); however, multiple experiments were performed with live, unfixed cells, for which Foxp3-CreYFP+/+Srebp2WT/WT mice are used (Srebp2WT).

If Srebp2CompKO Tregs successfully compete with WT Tregs in Srebp2Comp mice, then YFP+ events would represent half of the total Foxp3+ events. However, that is not what was found. Instead, YFP+ events consistently fell significantly below this threshold, with the lowest proportion seen in the small intestine lamina propria (Fig. 2B, C). This competition defect was measured by serial blood draws out to 80 wk, at which point YFP+ events constituted only a small fraction of total Tregs (Fig. 2D). Thus, ablation of Srebp2 selectively in Tregs in this competition setting impairs their ability to maintain normal frequencies in multiple tissues.

Srebp2CompKO Tregs have defects in activation throughout multiple tissues when in competition with WT Tregs

Along with the reduced abundance, Srebp2CompKO Tregs exhibited phenotypic changes in Srebp2Comp mice. Srebp2CompKO Tregs showed a shift away from an activated phenotype, with significantly reduced proportions of CD62LloCD44+ activated cells (aTregs) compared with Srebp2WT Tregs (Fig. 3A). Consistent with this shift, these Srebp2CompKO Tregs had reduced expression of Klrg1, which marks highly suppressive, terminally differentiated Tregs that have undergone multiple rounds of proliferation (Fig. 3C, D).23 Expression of CD103, an important tissue-homing and retention integrin, was also reduced (Fig. 3E, F). Given CD103 enables access to the gut lamina propria,24 we interpret that diminished expression of CD103 underlies the observed defect of lamina propria Srebp2CompKO Tregs. In addition, baseline expression of CD25, a key component of the high-affinity IL-2 receptor and itself a STAT5 target gene, was unchanged on Srebp2CompKO Tregs across multiple tissues (Fig. 3G, H), suggesting that Srebp2-deficient and WT Tregs have equivalent IL-2R signaling, even when in competition. In conclusion, Srebp2-deficient Tregs exhibit a competitive deficit in activation, reflecting a requirement of Srebp2 in aTregs during physiological stress that is independent of high-affinity IL-2R expression.

Srebp2 influences Treg proliferation, apoptosis, and Foxp3 expression

The reduction of Srebp2CompKO Tregs observed in Srebp2Comp mice may be explained by reduced survival and/or proliferation. To address these nonexclusive possibilities, we first compared expression of a marker of proliferation (Ki67) in Srebp2CompWT and Srebp2CompKO Tregs and found that Ki67 was decreased in Srebp2CompKO Tregs (Fig. 4A). Apoptosis was examined outside of the competitive setting for Srebp2cKO and Srebp2WT Tregs. The proportion of Annexin-V+ propidium iodide+, but not total Annexin-V+ PIneg, cells increased among Srebp2cKO Tregs (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the viability of Srebp2cKO cells in the competitive setting, as assessed by using eFluor450 viability dye directly ex vivo, showed that the proportion of nonviable (eFluor450+) Tregs was elevated in Srebp2-deficient Tregs (Fig. 4C). In addition to reduced viability, Foxp3 protein was reduced in Srebp2-deficient Tregs when in competition across multiple tissues (Fig. 4D). Given high Foxp3 expression is needed to preserve lineage identity,25 these data suggest Srebp2-deficient Tregs may be prone to losing Foxp3 expression. Collectively, these findings suggest that the reduced frequency of Srebp2-deficient Tregs detected in the competitive model is, in part, due to lower proliferation, enhanced cell death, and increased instability.

Expression of IL-2/STAT5 and mevalonate pathway genes is reduced in the absence of Srebp2

To explore downstream consequences of Srebp2 deficiency, we compared transcriptomes in Srebp2CompKO and Srebp2WT Tregs. This analysis uncovered 358 DEGs, most of which were downregulated in Srebp2CompKO cells, in line with its role as a transcriptional activator (Fig. 5A). As expected, Srebp2 transcripts were reduced 10-fold in Srebp2CompKO Tregs (Fig. 5B). This effect was associated with a reduction in Hmgcr and Hmgcs (31% and 29% lower in Srebp2CompKO; adjusted P values 0.009 and 0.02, respectively), which is similar to what was found in the noncompetitive setting (Fig. 1B). Genes involved in lipid uptake were among the genes with the greatest reduction, such as Ldlr and Apobr (Fig. 5B). Importantly, expression of genes associated with aTregs, including Klrg1, Pdcd1, Maf, Tbx21, and Tigit, was diminished in Srebp2CompKO Tregs, whereas rTreg genes, like Sell and Bcl2, were elevated (Fig. 5C). Ki67 transcripts were also reduced by Srebp2 deficiency (Fig. 5C), consistent with data shown in Fig. 4. Thus, Srebp2 appears to influence expression of genes associated with both sterol metabolism and Treg biology. Factors associated with the transition from rTregs to aTregs were particularly impacted, in line with the idea that Srebp2 may support differentiation from the resting state.

We next performed gene set enrichment analysis to identify biological pathways impacted by Srebp2 deficiency (Fig. 5D). As expected, cholesterol homeostasis was the most enriched Hallmark pathway among Srebp2-dependent DEGs. Myc targets 1, inflammatory response, and IL-2/STAT5 pathways were additionally enriched, suggesting that these pathways are also Srebp2 dependent. Consistent with gene set enrichment analysis, further analyses of all DEGs using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes metabolic pathways also identified steroid biosynthesis and terpenoid backbone synthesis as the most sensitive to a loss of Srebp2 and are inclusive of many of the genes in cholesterol homeostasis (Fig. 5E). Two genes that were differentially expressed in the IL-2/STAT5 pathway, Bcl2 and Sell, are known to be highly expressed genes in rTregs1 and were increased in Srebp2CompKO, which likely reflects the reduction of aTregs seen in the competition model (Fig. 3A–F). The remaining IL-2/STAT5 pathway DEGs were reduced in the absence of Srebp2. Notably, 50%, or 14 of these (Csf1, Batf, Il1r2, Cd81, Nfil3, Cd83, Abcb1a, Tnfrsf1b, Tnfrsf8, Ahr, Il1rl1, Il18r1, Cxcl10, and Itgae), have previously been found more highly expressed in aTregs when compared with rTregs.1 We were surprised that several canonical IL-2/STAT5 genes such as Myc, Il2ra, Gzmb, Prdm1, Foxp3, Socs1, and Cish were not among the statistically significant DEGs (Fig. 5F). This finding likely reflects sample variation for some of these genes. This trend for reduced Socs1, Prdm1, and Gzmb are consistent with their role in aTregs. In addition, when compared with other hallmark pathways, the DEGs of the IL-2/STAT5 pathway had the greatest overlap with inflammatory response pathway (7 genes, or 25%). Overlap was minimal when compared with other pathways (Fig. S1A). Two other DEGs belonging to IL-2/STAT5 and cholesterol homeostasis, Alcam and Nfil3, were significantly downregulated in the absence of Srebp2. These two genes are not directly involved in cholesterol synthesis but indirectly affect this pathway by contributing to inflammation and atherosclerosis.26,27 Thus, beyond expected metabolic pathways and aTreg genes, Srebp2 appears to be important for mobilization of elements of IL-2/STAT5 that tune the response of Tregs to inflammatory signals.

Given the observed decrease in viability of Srebp2CompKO Tregs ex vivo, we investigated all DEGs in the apoptosis hallmark pathway from our RNA sequencing data (Fig. 5G). Antiapoptotic genes Bcl2l2, Mgmt, and Hgf show lower expression, consistent with reduced protection against cell death.28–30 Proapoptotic genes Jun, Txnip, and Pdcd4 were upregulated in Srebp2CompKO Tregs, indicating that they are susceptible to cell death signaling.31–33 This molecular signature suggests that Srebp2CompKO Tregs are primed for cell death and is in line with the higher levels of apoptosis measured in Fig. 4.

mTORC1 signaling also emerged among the top hallmark pathways enriched in Srebp2WT relative to Srebp2CompKO Tregs (Fig. 5D). However, examination of the underlying mTORC1 leading-edge genes revealed that the majority are themselves sterol biosynthesis enzymes and cholesterol uptake mediators (e.g. Hmgcr, Sqle, Dhcr24, Cyp51, Idi1, Insig1, Stard4, and Ldlr) (Fig. S1B), indicating that the apparent mTORC1 enrichment is largely a consequence of overlap with the cholesterol synthesis program rather than independent regulation of canonical mTORC1 effectors. This is consistent with the well-established role of mTORC1 as an upstream activator of SREBP-mediated lipogenic gene expression11 and underscores that the IL-2/STAT5 signature, by contrast, captures genes that are not redundant with the cholesterol homeostasis module.

Srebp2 does not regulate proximal IL-2/STAT5 signaling

Given that expression of IL-2/STAT5 genes was diminished in Srebp2CompKO, and that IL-2R is organized in lipid rafts on the cell membrane, we hypothesized that Srebp2 may impact IL-2R signaling through its role in cholesterol synthesis.34,35 To test whether proximal IL-2 signaling was affected by Srebp2 deficiency, Srebp2cKO or Srebp2WT mice were treated with IL-2/CD25 fusion protein, a potent in vivo IL-2 agonist,20 or PBS, then pSTAT5 was measured directly ex vivo, 72 h later. The result was clear. STAT5 activation was unchanged between Srebp2WT and Srebp2cKO Tregs (Fig. 6A, B). To follow-up, we also compared pSTAT5 in Srebp2WT and Srebp2cKO Tregs cultured with recombinant mouse IL-2 in vitro and found similar dose-response kinetics (Fig. 6C, D). Thus, proximal IL-2/STAT5 signaling is intact in the absence of Srebp2, consistent with normal expression of CD25 (Fig. 3G, H) and transcriptional data showing core IL-2/STAT5 target genes were not significantly impacted in Srebp2CompKO Tregs.

Srebp2CompKO Tregs expand in response to IL-2/CD25 but exhibit reduced cellular cholesterol

Because IL-2 promotes Treg expansion and induces transcription of genes in the mevalonate pathway, we reasoned that IL-2 immunotherapy may reverse the competitive disadvantage of Srebp2CompKO Tregs. To address this hypothesis, we treated Srebp2Comp mice with IL-2/CD25, then compared effects on Srebp2CompWT and Srebp2CompKO Tregs. We found that both proliferated vigorously, such that the proportion of Srebp2CompWT and Srebp2CompKO Tregs (YFP+Foxp3+:total Foxp3+) remained unchanged (Fig. 7B). Accordingly, expression of Ki67 was similar in Srebp2CompWT and Srebp2CompKO Tregs (Fig. 7C), a reversal of the phenotype in untreated mice (Fig. 4B). CD25, a key component of the high-affinity IL-2R that is upregulated by IL-2/CD25 in the majority of all Tregs in the competition setting, resulted in approximately 40% reduced CD25 expression by Srebp2CompKO Tregs (Fig. 7D). Despite this reduced expression, this amount of CD25 provided enough IL-2 signaling to allow Srebp2 deficient Tregs to proliferate to a level similar to WT Tregs. To determine if cholesterol synthesis and/or uptake can be restored by IL-2/CD25, we treated Srebp2CompWT and Srebp2CompKO mice, then measured cellular cholesterol 72 h later at the peak of the proliferative response. As expected, we found that Srebp2CompKO Tregs had less cellular cholesterol than Srebp2CompWT Tregs after IL-2/CD25 (Fig. 7E) indicating that Srebp2 supports the generation and/or extracellular uptake of cellular cholesterol in response to IL-2. In conclusion, the IL-2–driven expansion of Srebp2-deficient Tregs is not limited by their reduced amounts of CD25 or cellular cholesterol.

IL-2–dependent development of aTregs is impaired in Tregs lacking Srebp2

Given that IL-2 promotes expression of aTreg genes,36 we next determined if IL-2/CD25 can rescue the aTreg defect seen in Srebp2CompKO mice. Strikingly, Srebp2-deficient Tregs were unable to upregulate CD103, ICOS and CD39 in response to IL-2/CD25, unlike WT counterparts (Fig. 8A–C). This pattern was evident across multiple tissues. Instead, Srebp2-deficient Tregs became CD62Lhi and Bcl-2+, indicative of a “resting” state (Fig. 8D, E). Thus, rather than rescuing the aTreg defect, IL-2/CD25 exacerbated the disparity between Srebp2CompWT and Srebp2CompKO, further shifting the balance toward rTregs. Importantly, Foxp3 expression was also reduced in Srebp2CompKO Tregs (Fig. 8F), consistent with that measured directly ex vivo without IL-2/CD25 (Fig. 4D). Thus, there appears to be an IL-2–dependent link with Srebp2 to optimally support the differentiation and function of aTregs in most lymphoid and nonlymphoid tissues.

Discussion

Previous studies have established that the mevalonate pathway is important for aTreg differentiation and suppressive function.7,8,37 Yet the signals required for Tregs to engage this pathway are less clear. IL-2 upregulates lipid programs in Tregs and controls the expression of genes involved in the mevalonate pathway.13 Using mice lacking Srebp2 selectively in Tregs, we have confirmed the role of the mevalonate pathway in aTreg generation and studied how IL-2/STAT5 signaling may engage it. Consistent with published studies, Srebp2 was not essential for Treg development and homeostasis,7,8 likely due to redundancy with Srebp1, a closely related transcription factor that controls similar metabolic pathways, and/or importing of cholesterol from the extracellular space. Previous studies have shown that Treg-specific knockout of Hmgcr, the rate-limiting step of mevalonate metabolism, leads to profound defects in Treg numbers, aTreg generation and scurfy-like phenotype,37 a pattern that was not seen in our Srebp2 deficient models. Therefore, despite an approximate 40% reduction in expression of Hmgcr in Srebp2-deficient Tregs, a threshold was met to support a normal immune compartment at homeostasis.

However, in vivo competition studies revealed defects in Srebp2CompKO Tregs that indicate diminished fitness. Most notably, Tregs in this setting were unable to reach an activated state in the absence of Srebp2. Total counts were also reduced across lymphoid and nonlymphoid tissues with a more striking inability to populate mucosal tissues, rendering the compensatory effect of Srebp1 insufficient. This may in part be explained by the fact that the Srebp1c promoter requires oxysterol intermediates produced by the Srebp2-driven cholesterol biosynthesis program.19 In a quiescent Tregs, residual sterols are likely sufficient to allow some Srebp1 expression and meet metabolic demands. However, when Tregs try to upregulate the lipogenic program in response to competitive pressure, the absence of Srebp2-derived sterol ligand may prevent the necessary boost in Srebp1c, rendering the compensatory role of Srebp1 no longer sufficient.

Importantly, when Tregs in competition were treated with IL-2/CD25, Srebp2CompKO Tregs expanded at the same rate as their WT Treg counterparts. However, IL-2/CD25 did not rescue the aTreg defect, suggesting that IL-2/CD25-driven aTreg generation, rather than proliferation, requires Srebp2. The preservation of IL-2’s proliferative signal in the absence of Srebp2 was surprising, given that cholesterol is an important structural component during cell division. This result highlights the fact that cholesterol is not the only downstream mediator of the mevalonate pathway with an important role in Treg biology. Indeed, activation of T cells is known to be dependent on protein prenylation, which is downstream from the mevalonate pathway that is in part controlled by Srebp2.7,37,38 Therefore, Srebp2 likely plays a dominant role in supporting IL-2/STAT5-dependent protein prenylation and aTreg generation. Specifically, the mevalonate pathway supports post-translation prenylation of Ras, Rho, and Rab GTPases, which allow for anchoring at the cellular membrane. These proteins participate in numerous signaling cascades required for T cell activation, most notably TCR signaling.7,39 Because GTPase function relies on intermediates upstream of cholesterol, extracellular uptake of cholesterol would not rescue this defect. Therefore, the impairment in aTreg generation in Srebp2CompKO could, in part, be a result of GTPase dysfunction.

IL-2/CD25 reverses the proliferative defect of Srebp2-deficient Tregs seen in the competitive setting. After IL-2/CD25, Ki67 expression is equivalent between Srebp2CompWT and Srebp2CompKO Tregs and their expansion is proportional. However, as compared with WT, some defects in Srebp2CompKO are exacerbated by IL-2/CD25 such as reductions in Foxp3 expression and aTreg genes. These results suggest that Srebp2 is engaged by IL-2/CD25 to influence critical components of Tregs, but IL-2’s proliferative signaling is independent of Srebp2. This effect is not mediated by proximal signaling machinery because Stat5 phosphorylation is unchanged and suggests that more peripheral components of the IL-2/STAT5 pathway require Srebp2. However, Foxp3 is reduced in the competition setting both with and without IL-2, suggesting that Srebp2 may play a role in Treg stability. One possible molecular mediator of this pattern is Batf, whose transcripts per million were reduced 2-fold in Srebp2CompKO Tregs. Batf expression in Tregs is required for differentiation, homeostasis, and stabilization of Foxp3 expression.40 Therefore, IL-2/STAT5 signaling may require Srebp2 to properly support expression of Batf, Foxp3, and aTreg differentiation.

A remaining area of interest is why Srebp2cKO mice can generate a normal T cell compartment, yet defects emerge when Srebp2CompKO Tregs are placed in competition with WT Tregs. The mevalonate pathway is known to support many critical cellular processes such as protein prenylation, steroid synthesis, and plasma membrane structure. A global reduction in flux through the mevalonate pathway via loss of Srebp2 could result in several basic cellular defects. However, we were unable to measure defects at steady state; defects only arise in competition with WT Tregs. Our experiments show that IL-2 is not the limiting factor. It is possible that Srebp2CompKO Tregs have an impaired response to TCR signaling or poor ability to compete for limited self-antigen. Additionally, mevalonate metabolism supports integrin expression and cellular migration, which may explain the ability of WT Tregs to populate tissues more effectively than Srebp2CompKO Tregs.41–43 Thus, Srebp2 is dispensable in Tregs under homeostatic conditions; however, it is necessary for Treg activation and survival in the competition setting and in response to IL-2. This finding yields critical insight into Treg processes in settings of physiological stress and may impact an immune response to infection or immune reconstitution.

Past results showed that the inducible deletion of IL-2ra selectively in Tregs in peripheral tissues, which also lowered genes in the cholesterol pathway, dominantly lowered rTregs, whereby aTregs dominated.4 Here we show that deletion of Srebp2, which disrupts the cholesterol pathway, largely spared rTregs. Many DEGs associated with the IL-2/STAT5 pathway that help to define aTregs were reduced after deletion of Srebp2. This finding is consistent with the marked reduction in aTregs, rather than rTregs, when in competition with wild-type Tregs and suggests that the cholesterol pathway is more important for the function of aTregs. This dichotomy leads us to refine our previous model concerning the role of IL-2R signaling in Tregs subsets.4 Our past model supports a requirement for IL-2R signaling for the survival rTregs and as checkpoint that favors the development of aTregs. The current findings uncouple Srebp2 from this dual activity of IL-2R signaling on rTregs and aTregs. Thus, the effect of IL-2R signaling on the cholesterol pathway is largely to shape the proliferation, stability, and development of aTregs.
