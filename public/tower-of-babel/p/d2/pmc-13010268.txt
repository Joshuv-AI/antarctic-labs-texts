Promising advances and remaining challenges in AI-driven QTc estimation
Authors: Zekai Yu
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the article by Plagwitz et al.1 describing QTcNet, a convolutional neural network for automated QTc interval estimation. The authors demonstrate impressive reductions in mean absolute error from 23.4 to 13.4 ms across three external validation cohorts, addressing a critical gap in clinical ECG analysis where automated measurements frequently diverge from expert readings.

While we commend the authors’ rigorous validation approach and commitment to open-source dissemination, several methodological considerations warrant discussion. First, the training paradigm relies entirely on algorithm-labelled data with a post hoc 15 ms bias correction for Emergency Department Münster (EDMS) measurements. Although the model successfully learned to outperform its training labels—a phenomenon consistent with noise-robust learning literature2—the generalizability of this correction factor across different ECG systems and populations remains uncertain. Institutions employing alternative annotation algorithms may require recalibration before deployment.

Second, the subgroup analyses reveal notable performance degradation in patients with atrial fibrillation or flutter, where irregular RR intervals fundamentally challenge heart rate correction methodologies. Given that QT assessment in atrial fibrillation carries particular clinical importance for antiarrhythmic drug management,3,4 future iterations should specifically address this population through fibrillation-specific training strategies.

Third, while the integrated gradients analysis confirms physiologically plausible attention to QRS onset and T-wave offset, the model’s tendency to regress towards mean QTc values (∼450 ms) for atypical signals raises concerns about edge-case reliability. The authors’ suggestion to flag discrepancies between QTcNet and conventional algorithms as a safety mechanism merits prospective validation.5

Finally, the finding that fine-tuning on expert-annotated Physikalisch-Technische Bundesanstalt (PTB) data paradoxically reduced external validity underscores a fundamental tension in medical artificial intelligence (AI): cohort-specific optimization may compromise generalizability.6 We encourage the authors to explore domain adaptation techniques that preserve cross-institutional performance.

We applaud the authors for their transparency and open-source commitment, which will undoubtedly accelerate community-driven refinement of this promising tool.
