Comparing Whole‐Blood and Plasma Tacrolimus Intra‐Patient Variability for Predicting Allograft Rejection in Kidney Transplantation
Authors: Zeyu Xia, Hui Yu, Jeong M Park, Hao‐Jie Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTacrolimus is a key immunosuppressant used to prevent allograft rejection in kidney transplant recipients. Intra‐patient variability (IPV) in tacrolimus trough concentrations has been recognized as a useful biomarker for predicting clinical outcomes in kidney transplantation. Tacrolimus therapeutic drug monitoring traditionally relies on whole‐blood concentration measurements. However, the pharmacological effect of tacrolimus is likely directly influenced by the unbound fraction in plasma, suggesting that IPV derived from plasma concentrations might be a more accurate predictor of clinical outcomes. This study aimed to compare the predictive performance of tacrolimus IPV calculated from whole‐blood versus imputed plasma concentrations for biopsy‐proven kidney allograft rejection. Plasma tacrolimus concentrations were imputed from measured whole‐blood levels in 1302 adult recipients using an established saturable hematocrit‐binding model. Tacrolimus whole‐blood and plasma IPV were calculated as time‐weighted coefficients of variation. The association between IPV and biopsy‐proven rejection occurring 60–730 days posttransplant was assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards models. We found that higher tacrolimus IPV was significantly associated with increased rejection risk, irrespective of the matrix used. Model performance analysis using the concordance index (C‐index) and bootstrap revealed that the plasma‐derived IPV model did not outperform the whole‐blood IPV model, indicating that calculating IPV from hematocrit‐imputed plasma concentrations offers no significant predictive advantage over the standard whole‐blood method. While our results indicate no predictive advantage of hematocrit‐imputed plasma IPV over whole‐blood IPV, it remains unknown whether IPV obtained from direct plasma tacrolimus measurement or more advanced plasma prediction models will outperform blood tacrolimus IPV in predicting rejection risk.Keywords: biopsy‐proven rejection, intra‐patient variability, tacrolimus, therapeutic drug monitoring

Introduction

Tacrolimus remains a cornerstone of immunosuppressive therapy to prevent allograft rejection in kidney transplantation [1]. However, maintaining appropriate and stable tacrolimus exposure continues to present a major clinical challenge due to its narrow therapeutic index and high inter‐ and intra‐patient pharmacokinetic variability [1, 2]. Underexposure to tacrolimus would lead to acute rejection, while overexposure would increase the risk of infection and toxicity, and thus, close therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of tacrolimus trough concentrations is required in clinical practice [3, 4]. Despite standardized dosing and TDM protocols, many kidney transplant recipients experience significant fluctuations in tacrolimus exposure. This inconsistency may arise from unexpected drug and food interactions, changes in the patients' physiological conditions, disease states, and variations in drug adherence.

Intra‐patient variability (IPV) of tacrolimus exposure has emerged as a useful biomarker of transplant outcomes. Several studies showed that tacrolimus IPV has been associated with graft loss, chronic immunologic‐mediated graft injury, and other adverse clinical outcomes [5, 6, 7]. Although the association is well established, there is still uncertainty about the best approach to measure and interpret tacrolimus IPV.

Conventional TDM measures tacrolimus in whole blood, where approximately 72%–98% of the drug is bound to erythrocytes due to its high affinity to intracellular FK‐binding proteins (FKBP12) [8, 9]. However, the calcineurin complex within T lymphocytes, the pharmacological target of tacrolimus, can only be reached by the unbound free drug that diffuses from plasma into lymphocytes [10]. A previous study has demonstrated a strong linear correlation between plasma concentrations of unbound free tacrolimus and total plasma tacrolimus concentrations [11]. Therefore, compared to whole‐blood tacrolimus levels, plasma tacrolimus concentration may better reflect tacrolimus concentrations at the site of action and, theoretically, is more relevant to its immunosuppressive activity. However, direct measurement of plasma tacrolimus is not routinely performed due to the need for more sensitive assays, and plasma concentrations are prone to pre‐analytical instability [11, 12]. Consequently, several groups have built computational models to estimate plasma concentrations from whole‐blood tacrolimus levels and hematocrit fractions [13, 14].

In this study, we imputed tacrolimus plasma trough concentrations from whole‐blood levels using an established hematocrit‐binding model in kidney transplant recipients. We then compared the performance of whole‐blood and plasma tacrolimus IPV in predicting the risk of biopsy‐proven rejection. Our goal was to determine whether plasma tacrolimus IPV is a better biomarker than whole‐blood tacrolimus IPV for predicting rejection risk and guiding posttransplant immunosuppressive management.

Methods

Patient Population

We conducted a single‐center, retrospective cohort study that included kidney transplant recipients who received tacrolimus‐based (Prograf or generic equivalents) immunosuppressive therapy at the University of Michigan Hospital between January 2001 and July 2025. De‐identified data were extracted from the University of Michigan electronic medical records. The study was approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients had to be at least 18 years old at the time of kidney transplantation; (2) they received tacrolimus as part of their standard immunosuppressive regimen; (3) they had complete records of tacrolimus dosing and at least five trough concentrations posttransplant. Trough concentration was defined as the whole‐blood tacrolimus level measured before the next scheduled dose; (4) they had a record of a kidney biopsy performed between 2 months and 2 years posttransplantation.

Patients were excluded if their age was < 18 years at the time of transplantation, if they did not have enough tacrolimus trough concentration records, or if they did not have any kidney biopsy records. Patients were also excluded if tacrolimus was not included in their standard immunosuppressive regimen.

Clinical Endpoint

Biopsy‐proven rejection occurring between 60 and 730 days posttransplant was the clinical endpoint to evaluate the association between the IPV of tacrolimus trough concentrations and the clinical outcome. Patients with rejection events that occurred within 60 days after transplantation were excluded, as the events might be confounded by surgical or perioperative complications. For patients who experienced multiple biopsy‐proven rejections, these patients were censored at the time of the first such event. For patients without a rejection event, they were censored at the time of the last biopsy.

Data Processing and IPV Calculation

Tacrolimus whole‐blood concentrations were quantified as part of routine TDM at the Michigan Medicine Chemical Pathology Laboratory using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry assay. This assay has a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of 1.0 ng/mL. Detailed assay specifications are available via Michigan Medicine's laboratory services (https://mlabs.umich.edu/tests/tacrolimus).

We applied a series of data filtering steps. First, we excluded patients without tacrolimus blood concentration values. Then, we included only samples collected after transplantation and before the clinical endpoint (i.e., first biopsy‐proven rejection or the last biopsy record for patients without a rejection event). In addition, to capture tacrolimus trough concentrations, we only included samples obtained between 4:00 a.m. and 11:59 a.m., typically occurring 10–14 h after the evening dose [7, 15] to minimize the impact of sample timing errors. Moreover, we removed samples with concentrations exceeding 50 ng/mL, as such extreme values were deemed physiologically implausible for trough concentrations and likely reflected measurement or timing errors [6, 16]. Finally, we excluded pediatric recipients and patients with fewer than five tacrolimus trough concentrations.

A total of 1507 patients with 100,939 tacrolimus concentration measurements were initially available for screening. After data filtering, 40,772 tacrolimus trough concentrations from 1302 patients were retained for final analysis.

Tacrolimus IPV was calculated based on the coefficient of variation (CV). The CV was defined as the ratio of standard deviation (SD) to the mean in percent (i.e., CV=SD/mean×100%). At the University of Michigan, target tacrolimus trough concentrations are 8–12, 6–10, and 5–8 ng/mL during 0–90, 91–120, and > 120 days posttransplant, respectively, for adult kidney transplant recipients. IPV was first calculated for each period to avoid variability caused by varying target levels across posttransplant periods, and the time‐weighted mean IPV for each patient was then imputed, with weights proportional to the actual span of measurements within each period.

Statistical Analysis

Numerical variables were presented as the mean ± SD, and the Mann–Whitney U test and the Welch two‐sample t‐test were used for statistical analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as proportions and were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test.

To evaluate the association between tacrolimus IPV and rejection risk, the continuous IPV values were categorized into quartiles. This categorization allowed for clear intuitive interpretation in survival analyses. In all models, the lowest IPV group (i.e., Q1) served as the reference. The effect of tacrolimus IPV on kidney rejection risk was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves, and log rank tests were performed to compare the differences in survival between groups. The effect of tacrolimus IPV on kidney graft rejection risk was further analyzed via Cox proportional hazards regression models. Model performance was evaluated using the concordance index (C‐index) with bootstrap (1000 iterations) and the Akaike information criterion (AIC). An ΔAIC > 2 indicates a meaningful difference in model performance. All statistical analyses were implemented in Python (v3.13.5). All tests were double‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Imputed Tacrolimus Plasma Trough Concentrations

The whole‐blood tacrolimus trough concentrations ranged from 1.0 to 48.9 ng/mL with a mean of 9.3 ± 3.9 ng/mL. Tacrolimus plasma concentrations were calculated based on the whole‐blood concentrations and the hematocrit fractions at the time of tacrolimus measurements using the previously reported saturable hematocrit‐binding model [14, 17]. The imputed plasma concentrations ranged from 0.02 to 4.19 ng/mL with a mean value of 0.26 ± 0.15 ng/mL. Tacrolimus IPV Affected Allograft Rejection in Kidney Transplantation

The mean whole‐blood IPV was significantly higher in the event group compared to that in the non‐rejection group (31.18% ± 9.79% vs. 29.00% ± 11.16%, p = 0.009). Similarly, the imputed tacrolimus plasma trough concentrations showed a higher IPV in patients with biopsy‐proven rejections compared with those without a rejection event (mean: 35.56% ± 12.46% vs. 32.74% ± 13.97%, p = 0.006). Therefore, both biological matrices provide evidence supporting that higher tacrolimus IPV is associated with an increased risk of graft rejection.

To further explore the relationship between tacrolimus IPV and kidney rejection risk, patients were stratified into quartiles based on their IPV values. As shown in Figure 2, the number and proportion of rejection events increased progressively across quartiles. For whole‐blood IPV, the rejection rate increased from 6.1% in Q1 (the lowest group) to 10.7% in Q4 (the highest group). Similarly, the plasma IPV model showed an increase from 5.5% in Q1 to 11.3% in Q4. The findings suggest a dose–response relationship between tacrolimus IPV and allograft rejection risks in kidney transplant recipients.

Number and percentage of biopsy‐proven rejection events across whole‐blood and plasma tacrolimus IPV quartiles.

A Kaplan–Meier analysis of rejection‐free survival within 60–730 days posttransplant was performed by IPV quartiles. The curves revealed a significant separation between the quartiles, especially for patients in the highest quartile (Q4), who had a significantly lower probability of rejection‐free survival than those in the lower quartiles for both whole‐blood and plasma concentrations (Figure 3). The results strongly support that increased IPV is associated with an increased risk of rejection.

Kaplan–Meier curves for rejection‐free survival within 60–730 days posttransplant stratified by whole blood and plasma tacrolimus IPV quartiles. Shaded areas represent 95% confidence intervals. Log rank tests showed significant differences across quartiles (whole‐blood: p = 0.037; plasma: p = 0.022).

Cox proportional hazards models were developed to analyze the effects of tacrolimus IPV on rejection risks with the lowest quartile (Q1) as reference. In Figure 4, allograft rejection risk increased in a stepwise manner across quartiles, and the hazard ratios (HRs) in Q4 and Q3 were significantly higher than Q1 for both whole‐blood and plasma IPV. These findings highlight that greater tacrolimus IPV is associated with an increased risk of kidney allograft rejection. Given that other covariates, such as age, steroid‐free protocols, high preexisting antibodies, and donor‐specific antibodies, may affect both IPV and the risk of rejection, we examined the dataset for these potential significant covariates. Unfortunately, we found substantial missing data for these covariates, except for age. As a result, we performed an additional Cox analysis by including age at transplant as a covariate. The results show that the HRs for the high IPV quartiles remained essentially unchanged after adjustment for age. In addition, we found that age at transplant was not a significant independent predictor of rejection (p = 0.082). To further evaluate model performance, we compared AIC values between the univariate and adjusted models. For the whole‐blood model, the AIC changed from 1330.5 to 1331.3 (ΔAIC = 0.8), and for the plasma model, the AIC changed from 1330.1 to 1329.4 (ΔAIC = 0.7), indicating that including age at transplant does not significantly enhance the model's predictive power or change the interpretation of the relationship.

Forest plots of the Cox proportional hazards model for evaluating the association between IPV quartiles and the risk of kidney graft rejection for whole‐blood (left) and plasma (right) tacrolimus trough concentrations.

The C‐index was 0.583 and 0.592 for whole‐blood and plasma IPV models, respectively, and AIC values (1330.5 vs. 1330.1) were also nearly identical. We conducted a bootstrap analysis (1000 iterations) to compare the predictive performance of whole‐blood and plasma IPV (Figure 5). The mean C‐index was 0.594 (95% CI: 0.535–0.653) for whole‐blood and 0.594 (95% CI: 0.533–0.653) for plasma, and the difference in C‐index was not statistically significant (p = 0.992), indicating that the whole‐blood and plasma IPV models exhibit similar performance in predicting rejection risk in kidney transplant recipients.

Discussion

This study compared the performance of tacrolimus IPV derived from whole blood versus plasma trough concentrations for predicting kidney allograft rejection. We applied quartile stratification to evaluate risk discrimination across quartiles using time‐weighted tacrolimus IPV. We found that (1) higher tacrolimus IPV was consistently associated with an increased risk of allograft rejection, regardless of the matrix used; (2) whole‐blood and plasma‐derived tacrolimus IPV demonstrated similar predictive performance.

Many studies have shown that high IPV in tacrolimus exposure is associated with poor graft outcomes [18, 19]. Consistent with previous reports, our results demonstrated significant differences in IPV between patients with and without rejection, reinforcing tacrolimus IPV as a robust biomarker of kidney graft rejection. Quartile stratification of time‐weighted tacrolimus IPV provided a clear dose–response relationship between the IPV and rejection risk, with progressively greater event rates and HRs in patients in higher quartiles. Patients in the highest quartile (Q4) had approximately twice the risk of rejection compared with those in the lowest quartile, with an HR of 2.12 (95% CI: 1.22–3.67) for whole‐blood IPV and 2.31 (95% CI: 1.31–4.06) for plasma IPV, indicating that greater tacrolimus IPV is associated with a higher risk of kidney rejection. This trend was observed in both whole‐blood and plasma analyses. Similar associations between whole‐blood tacrolimus IPV and clinical outcomes have been reported in previous studies, in which higher tacrolimus IPV quartiles corresponded to progressively greater risk of graft loss, rejection, or de novo donor‐specific antibody formation [19, 20, 21].

A key methodological challenge in calculating IPV was accounting for protocol‐driven changes in tacrolimus targets. At our center, tacrolimus target ranges are 8–12 ng/mL for 0–90 days posttransplant; 6–10 ng/mL for 91–120 days; and 5–8 ng/mL thereafter. Calculating a single IPV metric across the entire study period is problematic, as intentional dose adjustments to meet different target ranges can artificially inflate the calculated variability [22]. A high CV in that context might simply reflect a changing protocol rather than true, unstable drug exposure. To mitigate this specific confounder, we first calculated CV within each clinically defined phase to isolate variability from intentional target changes. We then calculate a mean CV for each subject, weighting the calculation by the actual duration of trough measurements in each period. This weighting is essential because the duration of measurements can vary markedly among the periods and between patients. Without it, a CV calculated from a very short interval would be given equal importance to a CV derived from a very long observation. The time‐weighted method ensures that periods that reflect a longer assessment of a patient's variability contribute more to the final IPV value. Also, using this time‐weighted IPV minimizes the risk of violating the nonproportional hazards assumption, which could occur if variability varies with protocol changes during the follow‐up period. Thus, the time‐weighted IPV provides a more accurate and clinically relevant estimate of overall exposure stability across the entire follow‐up period.

Although tacrolimus concentrations at the site of action (i.e., the intracellular space of T‐lymphocytes) should better reflect its pharmacologic activity, whole‐blood tacrolimus trough TDM remains the recommended method in clinical practice [2], as direct quantification of intracellular tacrolimus concentrations is technically challenging. Given that intra‐T‐lymphocyte tacrolimus concentrations are likely directly affected by unbound free tacrolimus in plasma, and there is a strong linear correlation between plasma concentration of unbound free tacrolimus and total plasma tacrolimus concentration [11], plasma tacrolimus has the potential to serve as a better biomarker for intra‐T‐lymphocyte tacrolimus exposure and its pharmacological effect compared to whole‐blood tacrolimus. However, directly measuring plasma tacrolimus is a technical challenge due to the fact that plasma tacrolimus concentrations are approximately 30‐fold lower than those in whole blood [23]. In addition, variations during plasma preparation, such as delayed plasma separation, hemolysis, and different temperatures for blood sample storage, can further impact quantification accuracy [11, 12]. Consequently, plasma concentrations are often estimated indirectly from whole blood using hematocrit‐binding models [13].

In our study, we estimated plasma tacrolimus concentrations from whole‐blood levels using a hematocrit‐binding model and compared the performance of plasma and whole‐blood tacrolimus IPV for predicting biopsy‐proven rejection based on C‐index and AIC. We found that both the mean C‐index and AIC were comparable between the whole‐blood and plasma models, and the bootstrap analysis further demonstrated that the C‐index difference between the two models is not statistically significant (p = 0.992). These results indicate that, compared to the whole‐blood tacrolimus IPV, the plasma IPV offers no significant gain in identifying patients at higher risk of allograft rejection within 60–730 days post‐kidney transplant.

The comparable performance of whole‐blood and imputed plasma IPV models could be partially explained by the strong linear relationship between whole‐blood and plasma concentrations (Figure 1A, r = 0.903). Although the hematocrit‐binding model is nonlinear, our data show that, within the clinical range of tacrolimus, C p remains highly proportional to C wb, resulting in similar performance of the two models in predicting allograft rejection.

However, it should be noted that the actual plasma concentrations could differ significantly from those estimated by the simple hematocrit‐binding model. Reevaluating the predictive performance of plasma tacrolimus IPV is warranted when more accurate plasma concentration imputation methods or direct plasma tacrolimus measurements become available.

Another limitation is that our Cox regression models did not account for established clinical risk factors associated with rejection. These factors include donor type, re‐transplant status, sensitization level, induction immunosuppression, and other perioperative and immunologic variables. The primary reason for this omission is that these covariates are missing in a substantial portion of the study cohort. Including these factors would significantly reduce the sample size and statistical power of the study and could introduce selection bias. As a result, the associations observed between tacrolimus IPV and rejection risk represent unadjusted effects and may be confounded by baseline clinical characteristics. Future studies incorporating detailed clinical covariates are needed to clarify whether plasma‐ or whole‐blood‐based IPV provides independent prognostic value beyond traditional risk factors for rejection.

In sum, the study demonstrated that both whole‐blood and plasma tacrolimus IPV are potential biomarkers for predicting rejection risk in kidney transplant recipients, with comparable performance. However, as this was a single‐center retrospective study, the findings may not be fully generalizable to populations with different demographics and clinical protocols. Future investigations on plasma tacrolimus IPV using direct plasma concentration measurement or an improved model for plasma concentration estimation could determine whether plasma tacrolimus monitoring provides greater value for predicting clinical events in kidney transplant recipients than the conventional whole‐blood monitoring approach.
