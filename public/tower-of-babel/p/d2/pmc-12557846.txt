Effects of kinesio tape on countermovement jump kinetic variables in physically active females with flat feet before and after fatigue: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Zeynab Nickpey, Sahar Boozari, Abbas Farjad Pezeshk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFlatfoot, characterized by a reduced medial longitudinal arch, alters lower-limb biomechanics and affects vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) during dynamic tasks like jumping. Kinesio taping (KT) is often used to support foot posture; however, its efficacy under high-load conditions—such as jumping, which increases arch stress, and fatigue, which reduces muscular support—remains unclear.MethodsThis double-blind randomized controlled trial included 46 physically active females with flexible flat feet (arch height ratio < 0.221), assigned to KT or sham groups. Participants performed countermovement jumps (CMJs) under fresh and fatigued conditions, with and without taping. Fatigue was induced via lateral hopping, and KT was applied to the foot arches and tibialis posterior. vGRF variables were assessed across all conditions and categorized into three functional domains: propulsion (eccentric and concentric peak force and power, amortization time and force, concentric rate of force development, and eccentric stiffness); flight (jump height and reactive strength index); and landing (landing stiffness and rate of force development). Navicular drop was also measured only under pre-fatigue conditions.ResultsSignificant main effects of group, taping condition, and their interaction were observed for navicular drop, all with large effect sizes. KT reduced arch collapse by 17.8%, compared to 0.9% in the sham group. No significant effects of group or taping were found for vGRF variables. In contrast, fatigue significantly reduced jump height and increased force- and stiffness-related metrics across both groups (Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05), all with large effect sizes.ConclusionsKT applied to the foot arches and tibialis posterior had no effect on dynamic performance, and the static improvement did not reach clinical significance. In contrast, fatigue significantly reduced jump height and increased force- and stiffness-related parameters across both KT and sham groups, likely reflecting compensatory neuromechanical strategies in individuals with flexible flat feet. No taping effect was observed during CMJs performed under both non-fatigued and fatigued conditions. These findings suggest that KT offers limited functional benefit during dynamic tasks in this population. Background

Flatfoot, or pes planus, is a prevalent foot deformity characterized by a diminished medial longitudinal arch, which compromises both the structural integrity and functional capacity of the foot. Given the arch's critical role in shock absorption and force transmission, its reduction has been associated with altered load distribution and reduced propulsion efficiency during weight-bearing activities [1–3]. These biomechanical alterations may lead to increased peak vertical ground reaction forces (vGRFs), higher loading rates, diminished push-off impulse during dynamic tasks such as walking, running, and jumping, and even elevated medial knee contact forces during squatting—potentially reducing performance capacity and increasing injury risk [4–9]. Effective modulation of vGRFs is therefore particularly critical during high-load tasks like jumping, where the foot must alternate between acting as a rigid lever during takeoff and as a compliant shock absorber upon landing [5, 6, 8, 9]. Consequently, interventions aimed at improving foot posture, enhancing load absorption, and optimizing movement performance are of growing interest in both clinical and athletic settings.

Various strategies—including foot orthoses, strengthening programs, and taping techniques—have been applied to enhance foot posture and function in individuals with flat feet [1, 10]. Among these, kinesio taping (KT), an elastic therapeutic method, is believed to enhance proprioceptive feedback, provide structural support, and modulate muscle activation [11]. Reported benefits of KT in individuals with flatfoot include improved arch alignment [12–15], increased ankle mobility, pain reduction [16], better balance and postural control [13, 15, 17], and altered gait mechanics [15]. However, most of this evidence has focused on static or low-load tasks such as standing and walking. To date, only two studies have investigated KT during high-demand tasks—one examining foot pressure and muscle activation during running [14], and another evaluating jump height [18]. Although both showed promising outcomes, they were limited to athletic populations and lacked detailed kinetic analyses. Consequently, KT’s effects during dynamic, multi-joint tasks such as jumping remain insufficiently characterized, particularly in non-athletic individuals whose lower neuromuscular conditioning may yield different biomechanical responses.

In addition to mechanical loading, neuromuscular fatigue presents an additional challenge during high-demand tasks. Fatigue can impair force production, disrupt proprioception, and alter movement strategies, thereby increasing injury risk [19–21]. While KT has shown potential in mitigating fatigue-related impairments—such as strength loss, reduced postural stability, or increased pain—these effects have primarily been observed in healthy individuals performing low-load tasks [22, 23]. Whether such benefits extend to individuals with structural or functional abnormalities, such as flat feet, during demanding tasks remains unclear. Notably, individuals with flat feet are more susceptible to muscular fatigue than those with normal foot structure, with fatigue-induced alterations in gait and postural control [4, 24, 25]. Combined with already altered vGRF profiles during dynamic tasks [5, 6, 8, 9], fatigue may further exacerbate biomechanical inefficiencies and injury risk in this population—highlighting the need for interventions that can counteract these effects.

Sex-based differences in fatigue response and lower-limb biomechanics are also pertinent when investigating interventions like KT. Compared to males, females have been shown to experience greater fatigue-related performance declines and to be at higher risk of lower-limb injuries during high-impact activities, likely due to differences in neuromuscular control and landing mechanics [26–29]. When combined with the structural deficits inherent to flexible flatfoot, these biomechanical characteristics may further compromise force production and shock attenuation, particularly under fatigued conditions. This underscores the importance of identifying effective strategies to support lower-limb function in this subgroup.

To our knowledge, no study has examined the influence of KT on jumping biomechanics in recreationally active females with flexible flatfoot under high-load, fatigued conditions. Most existing research has focused on KT’s effects during static or low-load tasks, such as standing and walking. To address this gap, the countermovement jump (CMJ)—a multi-joint, high-load task—may offer a more functionally relevant model for assessing lower limb mechanics. This movement places substantial stress on the foot–ankle complex and actively engages force production during takeoff and shock attenuation during landing. The few available studies involving high-impact tasks have been conducted in athletic populations and have primarily relied on metrics such as plantar pressure or jump height [14, 18], which—although informative—offer limited insight into phase-specific mechanical loading. In contrast, kinetic parameters derived from vGRF—including peak force, power output, landing stiffness, and rate of force development— provide a more comprehensive assessment of performance, particularly in relation to load generation, transfer, and absorption. These indicators are especially relevant in individuals with flexible flatfoot, who often exhibit altered load distribution and reduced shock attenuation—biomechanical deficits that may be further exacerbated under fatigue and contribute to increased susceptibility to overuse injuries. Moreover, potential differences in biomechanical profiles and neuromuscular conditioning between physically active non-athletes and trained athletes [30, 31] underscore the need for targeted investigation in recreationally active individuals.

Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate whether KT applied to the foot arches and tibialis posterior influences CMJ biomechanics before and after a fatigue protocol. Kinetic outcomes were grouped into three functional domains to reflect phase-specific adaptations: propulsion (e.g., force, power), flight (e.g., jump height, reactive strength index), and landing (e.g., landing stiffness, loading rate). Navicular drop was also assessed as a static indicator of arch support prior to the fatigue protocol. We hypothesized that KT would reduce navicular drop and enhance CMJ performance under non-fatigued conditions by increasing takeoff force, power, and jump height, and by improving shock attenuation through lower landing stiffness and loading rate. Additionally, we expected KT to mitigate fatigue-related alterations in these performance parameters.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a parallel, randomized, controlled, double-blind superiority clinical trial. Participants were randomly allocated to either the KT or sham taping group, with each participant undergoing CMJ tests under both taping conditions (taped and untaped) before and after the fatigue protocol. Accordingly, a repeated-measures design was used to evaluate the effects of two interventions (KT vs. sham), two taping conditions (with and without tape), and two fatigue states (fresh vs. fatigued) on vGRF-derived variables, which served as the primary outcomes. Navicular drop was also assessed as a secondary outcome to evaluate static foot alignment.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Tarbiat Modares University Ethics Committee, and the study was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20201128049511N4).

Participants

Fifty-eight physically active university students with bilateral flexible flat feet were initially recruited. The sample size was estimated using G*Power software [32] based on pilot data collected from two groups of 10 participants. With an alpha level of 0.05, a statistical power of 80% (β = 0.2), and a partial eta-squared effect size of 0.035 for the reactive strength index, the required sample size was calculated to be 40 participants. To account for an anticipated 15% dropout rate, the recruitment target was set at 46 participants. Of the 58 individuals initially screened, seven did not meet the inclusion criteria, and five withdrew for personal reasons, resulting in 46 participants who were enrolled and randomly assigned to the study groups.

The participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: females aged between 18 and 30 years, an arch height ratio of less than 0.221 [33], assessed using a custom-made device with previously established reliability [34], body mass index (BMI) between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m2, and engagement in aerobic exercise at least three times per week. Exclusion criteria included a history of orthopedic, neurological, or rheumatologic disorders affecting the lower limbs or lumbar spine; visible lower limb deformities other than flatfoot; or the presence of pain in the lower extremities or lower back during the testing sessions.

Participants were randomly assigned to either the KT or sham group using block randomization (block size = 4, 1:1 allocation ratio) to ensure balanced group distribution. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent researcher who was not involved in data collection or analysis. To maintain allocation concealment, group assignments were sealed in opaque envelopes, with coded identifiers: “0” for the sham group and “1” for the KT group.

This study employed a double-blind design for the primary outcomes, with vGRF data recorded and processed by a laboratory engineer who was blinded to group allocation. As the engineer had no clinical background, she was unaware of the taping intervention and worked with datasets labeled “0” and “1,” without knowledge of what the codes represented. However, navicular drop was assessed by an experienced physiotherapist who, due to the need for palpatory evaluation, was not blinded to group assignment. To reduce potential bias, this assessor was independent of the research team and was not informed of the study hypotheses. This partial unblinding is acknowledged as a methodological limitation; however, it applied solely to the secondary outcome (navicular drop).

Participants also remained blind to their group assignment and attended separate laboratory sessions to prevent interaction, minimizing the likelihood of uncovering the taping condition. Additionally, none had prior experience with KT for foot arch support, reducing expectation biases and potential placebo effects.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment, and they were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Test procedure

Data collection took place between April 25, 2023, and January 24, 2024, at the Research and Treatment Center for Movement Disorders, Tarbiat Modares University. All participants attended a familiarization session prior to testing to ensure proper execution of the CMJ and fatigue protocol. During this session, potential allergic reactions to the tape were assessed by applying a small piece of KT to the medial aspect of the participant’s arm. The tape remained in place for one hour, after which the skin was inspected for any signs of irritation or allergic response. Participants were also instructed to report any delayed allergic reactions that might have occurred after leaving the session. No participants reported any allergic reactions, either during or after the assessment.

Participants in both the KT and sham groups completed two experimental sessions—one in a fresh (non-fatigued) state and one in a fatigued state—scheduled one week apart. In the fresh session, CMJs were performed under both taped and untaped conditions, with measurements of both navicular drop and vGRF variables. In the fatigued session, both the fatigue protocol and subsequent CMJs were conducted under taped and untaped conditions. In this session, only CMJ force-related variables were measured. Post-fatigue navicular drop was not assessed, as jump data needed to be recorded immediately after the fatigue protocol to prevent recovery effects, and time constraints did not allow for additional measurements. The type of tape applied (KT or sham) depended on group assignment. The order of the sessions (fresh vs. fatigued) and taping conditions (taped vs. untaped) was randomized.

CMJ protocol

Prior to CMJ testing, participants completed a standardized dynamic warm-up consisting of 5 min of stationary cycling, followed by 10 repetitions each of walking lunges, reverse lunges, straight-leg kicks, high knees, and butt kicks. To minimize variability, all warm-up activities, instructions, and supervision were administered by the same instructor.

Following the warm-up, participants stood barefoot on a calibrated force plate with feet shoulder-width apart, hands placed on hips, and gaze directed forward. They were instructed to perform a maximal vertical jump as quickly and as high as possible using a countermovement, without any preparatory hop or pause. Each participant completed three CMJ trials per condition.

Although three trials were collected both before and after the fatigue protocol, only the highest pre-fatigue and the first post-fatigue jumps were included in the final analysis [36]. This a priori selection aimed to capture maximal neuromuscular output under non-fatigued conditions, a common practice in sports biomechanics research, and isolate the immediate effects of fatigue before the onset of compensatory strategies or partial recovery. While averaging multiple post-fatigue trials may reduce within-subject variability and yield more stable performance metrics, it can also obscure acute neuromechanical alterations resulting from recovery or adaptive movement strategies across subsequent attempts. In sport contexts, where repeated exertion without full recovery is common, capturing the initial post-fatigue response may reveal the most immediate and pronounced biomechanical changes. Accordingly, this selection strategy prioritized sensitivity to transient biomechanical changes that, although brief, may reflect the most injury-prone state and provide critical insight into the immediate efficacy of interventions such as KT.

Moreover, although only the first post-fatigue jump was analyzed, three post-fatigue trials were collected to safeguard against the possibility of invalid attempts (e.g., due to loss of balance or improper technique). Nonetheless, all participants successfully completed a valid first post-fatigue jump.

Fatigue protocol

A functional fatigue protocol involving lateral hopping was employed to replicate sport-specific neuromuscular demands. This protocol was adapted from a previous study that demonstrated neuromuscular fatigue through a post-fatigue reduction in peak-to-peak H-reflex amplitude at 20% of maximal M-wave (H20%Mmax), relative to resting conditions [37]. Unlike isolated isometric, concentric, or eccentric fatiguing protocols, this hopping-based approach incorporated continuous stretch–shortening cycle (SSC) activity, more closely reflecting the predominant mode of muscular fatigue during dynamic movements [38].

Participants completed five consecutive sets of side-to-side lateral hops on a tatami mat at a cadence of 108 beats per minute, guided by a metronome. To assist with balance, they lightly grasped two vertical bars positioned in front of them. Each set continued until the participant either failed to maintain cadence for five consecutive hops or chose to terminate voluntarily. A 30-s walking rest was provided between sets [4, 37].

To confirm sufficient fatigue, participants immediately rated their perceived exertion using the 15-point Borg scale. A minimum score of 15 ("hard exertion") was required, as this level corresponds to approximately 75% of VO₂max and a heart rate of 160–170 bpm in dynamic tasks [39, 40]. Participants who reported a score below 15 were excluded; however, none fell below this threshold.

Tape application

Tapping procedures followed the guidelines of Kenzo Kase and previous literature, with the aim of supporting the foot arches and enhancing the function of the tibialis posterior muscle [11, 13–15, 18]. Given the variations in applied tension across previous studies, we strictly adhered to Kenzo Kase’s original recommendations for tape tension [11]. All tape applications were performed by the same experienced physiotherapist. The KT (ARES Kinesio Tape, South Korea) application includes three components (Fig. 2):Fig. 2a Kinesio tape application on the longitudinal arches, transverse arch, and tibialis posterior muscle. b Sham tape application outside the treatment area

a Kinesio tape application on the longitudinal arches, transverse arch, and tibialis posterior muscle. b Sham tape application outside the treatment area

First, a “Y” strip was applied to the medial longitudinal arch. The base of the strip was anchored just proximal to the insertion of the plantar fascia at the heel. One tail extended along the first metatarsal, and the other along the fifth metatarsal. The anchors were applied without tension, whereas the tails were applied with 100% of the tape’s available elastic tension. Second, an “I” strip was applied across the transverse arch, starting at the fifth metatarsal and directed medially toward the navicular, with 50% of the tape’s available tension. Third, an “I” strip was used to facilitate the tibialis posterior muscle. This was applied from the lateral posterior aspect of the tibia, wrapping around the medial malleolus and ending near its tendon insertion, with 35% of the tape tension.

To ensure accurate tape length and tension for each participant, the maximum available stretch of the tape was assessed beforehand. The anatomical distance for each target area was then measured individually, and the tape was cut to achieve the desired percentage of tension. After application, the participants remained in a resting position for 20–30 min to allow the adhesive properties to activate, as recommended by Kase [11], before testing commenced.

In the sham condition, a single strip of KT was applied with 0% tension and positioned along the medial aspect of the malleolus, deliberately placed away from the foot arches and associated muscle bellies [41]. This configuration was designed to mimic tactile stimulation while preventing any potential biomechanical or proprioceptive effects.

Data extraction

Navicular drop was measured as the vertical displacement of the navicular tuberosity between the seated and standing positions. This method has demonstrated high intra-rater reliability (0.934) and moderate inter-rater reliability (0.712) [42].

vGRF data were recorded using a Kistler force plate (Model 9286B, Kistler Co., Winterthur, Switzerland) sampling at 1000 Hz. Force‒time data were filtered with a 4th-order Butterworth filter at a 40 Hz cut-off. A custom MATLAB script (MathWorks, Natick, MA) was developed to extract relevant variables, with its accuracy internally validated through repeated trials and manual cross-checking. All force-related data were normalized to body mass. Most previous studies have demonstrated the high reliability of vGRF measurements [43–47].

Statistical analyses

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Although some variables exhibited minor deviations from normality, repeated-measures ANOVA was employed due to its robustness to moderate non-normality and the fact that other statistical assumptions—including homogeneity of variance and covariance—were satisfied, as verified by Levene’s test for equality of variances and Box’s M test for equality of covariance matrices, respectively.

To examine baseline differences between the KT and sham groups in the fresh, untaped condition, independent t-tests were conducted. For navicular drop, which was assessed only under non-fatigued conditions, a mixed ANOVA was performed with one within-subject factor (taping condition: taped vs. untaped) and one between-subject factor (group: KT vs. sham).

For vGRF-derived variables, which were measured across four conditions (pre-fatigue and post-fatigue × taped and untaped) in both groups, a mixed ANOVA was conducted with two within-subject factors (taping condition: taped vs. untaped; time: pre-fatigue vs. post-fatigue) and one between-subject factor (group: KT vs. sham). Effect sizes were reported using partial eta squared (ηp2) and interpreted as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (0.14).

Although all participants completed the study, a small number of missing values were observed for some vGRF variables due to technical issues during data extraction. Given that the required sample size was 40 and a larger sample of 46 participants was included, the impact of these missing values on statistical power and overall findings is likely negligible. Therefore, affected cases were excluded pairwise from relevant analyses without imputation.

Moreover, to account for multiple comparisons among the primary outcomes, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate. Adjusted p-values were computed, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. In accordance with standard practice, unadjusted p-values are reported in the results tables, while adjusted p-values, calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, are presented in the text to uphold methodological rigor.

Descriptive statistics and mixed ANOVA results for jump variables are presented in Table 2. No significant differences were found between the KT and sham groups, nor between taped and untaped conditions (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). However, a significant time effect was observed for most variables, indicating changes following fatigue. To account for the number of vGRF variables analyzed and reduce the risk of false positives, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate associated with multiple comparisons. After adjustment, the following variables remained statistically significant: eccentric peak force (adjusted p = 0.008), which increased by 5.5%; concentric peak force (adjusted p < 0.001), which increased by 6.5%; RFD in the concentric phase (adjusted p < 0.001), which increased by 25.4%; RSI (adjusted p = 0.008), which increased by 17.1%; eccentric stiffness (adjusted p < 0.001), which increased by 36.2%; landing stiffness (adjusted p = 0.010), which increased by 17.1%; and jump height (adjusted p < 0.001), which decreased by 8.5%. These percentage changes reflect the overall pattern observed across all four conditions (KT taped and untaped; sham taped and untaped). In contrast, amortization time did not reach statistical significance following Benjamini–Hochberg correction (adjusted p = 0.061), and the small, directionally inconsistent changes observed across conditions suggest individual variability rather than a systematic effect of fatigue. There were also no interaction effects observed (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). Discussion

This study examined the effects of KT on CMJ performance, focusing on vGRF-derived variables in individuals with flat feet under both non-fatigued and fatigued conditions. It was hypothesized that KT would reduce navicular drop and enhance CMJ performance—particularly under fatigue—by improving vGRF parameters. However, contrary to this hypothesis, although KT significantly reduced navicular drop in the pre-fatigue state, it did not influence any vGRF metrics in either pre- or post-fatigue conditions. Additionally, no significant differences were observed between the KT and sham groups under any condition. In contrast, fatigue exerted a pronounced effect across both groups, significantly altering nearly all CMJ variables.

Regarding static foot alignment, KT application to the foot arches and tibialis posterior resulted in a statistically significant reduction in navicular drop. However, the magnitude of change (0.21 mm) was well below the minimal detectable change (MDC) reported in the literature (1.78–2.28 mm) [49], suggesting limited clinical relevance. This interpretation is further supported by the absence of changes in vGRF-derived performance metrics, suggesting that the observed reduction in navicular drop did not translate into functional improvement. Notably, navicular drop was not reassessed post-fatigue, as priority was given to capturing force data immediately and minimizing the influence of recovery processes. While this limits the ability to draw conclusions about KT’s role in maintaining alignment under fatigued conditions, the minimal pre-fatigue change in navicular drop and the absence of performance changes before and after fatigue suggest that this omission likely had limited impact on the overall findings. Nonetheless, this remains a methodological limitation. Future studies may benefit from incorporating rapid digital scanning or real-time motion analysis to evaluate foot posture under dynamic and fatigued conditions.

From a biomechanical standpoint, KT did not produce measurable improvements in vGRF-derived kinetic variables during CMJ. A likely explanation lies in the minimal change in arch height, which—as noted—fell below the MDC threshold and therefore appeared insufficient to meaningfully alter joint mechanics under substantial load. This is particularly relevant given the demands of the CMJ, where peak ground reaction forces often exceed twice body weight. During such high-impact movements, the foot alternates between functioning as a rigid lever during takeoff and a shock absorber upon landing—roles that impose considerable structural and neuromuscular demands on the lower extremity [4–6, 8, 9]. These demands may exceed the mechanical support offered by KT’s elastic properties. Moreover, motor control during dynamic activities relies on coordinated movement across proximal segments of the lower limb and whole-body strategies, which may override the localized mechanical input of KT and limit its effectiveness under high-load condition.

Previous studies have primarily reported KT-related benefits in static or low-load contexts, including improvements in foot posture, navicular drop, and balance performance [12–18], across both athletic and non-athletic populations. These findings provided a rationale for exploring KT’s potential in dynamic, high-load movements. Among the few studies conducted in such contexts, one reported reductions in navicular drop and plantar pressure, along with increased tibialis anterior activity during running using multiple arch and tibialis posterior strips [14], while another observed increased jump height with a single strip applied to the tibialis posterior [18]. However, both studies involved athletic populations. The present study’s inclusion of recreationally active individuals introduces an important distinction, as training background may modulate responsiveness to KT. Athletes often exhibit superior neuromuscular control [30, 31] and heightened sensitivity to external mechanical stimuli. Therefore, differences in participant characteristics may partly explain the discrepancy between the present null findings and prior positive outcomes. Given the lack of direct comparisons between athletic and non-athletic groups, further research is warranted to evaluate KT’s functional effectiveness across varying levels of training experience and physical conditioning.

Interstudy variability may also stem from differences in taping protocols. Prior studies have used diverse techniques varying in tape width, tension, number of strips, anatomical placement, and material properties [12–18]. Some included tibialis posterior taping [14, 15, 18], while others focused on the foot arch, targeting longitudinal or transverse regions [13, 14, 16]. Specialized methods—such as windlass taping [17], modified Low-Dye anti-pronation method [12], or multi-layer configurations designed to enhance localized stiffness and expand the area of mechanical support [14]—have also been employed. Application tension likewise varied considerably across studies, further complicating direct comparisons. Beyond these procedural differences, tape material may also influence outcomes. For instance, dynamic tape—with greater elasticity (> 200%) than standard KT (~ 140–160%)—has been shown to more effectively reduce gait variability and improve Y-balance test scores [17]. Strip design matters as well; since tape stiffness decreases with reduced width [50], cutting it into a “Y” shape—as in the present study—may have inadvertently diminished its structural support across the foot. These methodological factors may partly explain the absence of significant findings, though—as previously discussed—the biomechanical demands of jumping may simply exceed KT’s support capacity regardless of configuration. Taken together, these considerations underscore the need to evaluate taping strategies, task-specific loading, and population characteristics when interpreting the functional potential of KT. Future research should systematically examine how taping technique and movement complexity interact to influence biomechanical outcomes, particularly in structurally vulnerable groups across diverse populations.

Psychological mechanisms should also be considered, as KT may enhance perceived stability and confidence even in the absence of physiological change [51, 52]. In the present study, sham tape was applied near—but not directly over—the target areas to maintain blinding while minimizing mechanical influence. The fact that neither KT nor sham tape affected vGRF metrics suggests that both physiological and psychological effects were negligible under the tested conditions.

Regarding fatigue, KT did not mitigate fatigue-related changes in CMJ performance. While previous studies have reported beneficial effects of KT on fatigue-induced impairments—such as pain reduction, strength preservation, or improved postural control—these effects have primarily been observed under static or low-load conditions, including muscle strength testing, balance assessments, and walking tasks [22, 23]. Notably, rigid orthotic insoles have demonstrated greater efficacy in attenuating fatigue-related performance declines in individuals with flat feet during dynamic activities such as downhill walking and running [53, 54]. Although these activities differ biomechanically from CMJ, the documented benefit of orthoses is likely attributable to their non-deformable structure, which ensures consistent mechanical alignment and arch support under repetitive, high-load conditions. KT, by comparison, offers support through elastic tension rather than rigid reinforcement, making it more susceptible to external factors such as perspiration, body movement, and skin adhesion—potentially limiting its mechanical stability during dynamic tasks. As such, KT may be more appropriate for lower-impact activities, where sustained structural support is less critical. However, as this study did not directly compare KT with orthotic insoles, conclusions regarding their relative efficacy should be interpreted with caution, and future research is warranted to directly evaluate both interventions under comparable biomechanical demands in order to better inform evidence-based clinical decision-making.

An intriguing observation in this study was a paradoxical neuromechanical response to fatigue: despite a reduction in jump height, several load-related metrics—including peak force, eccentric and landing stiffness, concentric RFD, and RSI—increased. One possible explanation is the concurrent influence of fatigue and post-activation potentiation (PAP), a short-term enhancement in neuromuscular performance following intense activity [55–58]. As the net effect of PAP is modulated by factors such as task complexity, recovery duration, and individual characteristics [55–58], its role in the present context remains speculative. Given the substantial fatigue load, brief recovery period, moderate training background of participants, and the mechanical demands of the CMJ, fatigue likely served as the dominant factor—supported by the observed reduction in jump height. Nonetheless, this interaction warrants further investigation. Notably, the post-fatigue increases in force and stiffness observed here contrast with the typical pattern reported in individuals with neutral foot posture, where both parameters generally decline [59, 60]. This may reflect population-specific compensatory mechanisms in flat-footed individuals, potentially aimed at preserving force output and mechanical efficiency under structural constraints. However, the biomechanical implications of increased stiffness are phase-dependent. While elevated pre-flight stiffness may enhance energy return and facilitate force transmission, excessive landing stiffness could restrict joint excursion, impair shock absorption, and elevate injury risk. Taken together, these findings raise the possibility that individuals with flexible flat feet exhibit distinct neuromechanical responses to fatigue, potentially shaped by potentiation-like effects and compensatory motor strategies. Future studies incorporating electromyographic and kinematic analyses, and comparing responses across foot types, are needed to test these hypotheses and clarify population-specific fatigue adaptations.

Finally, it is worth noting that the post-fatigue reduction in jump height (~1.40 cm) exceeded the MDC reported in previous studies (0.14 cm) [61], suggesting a genuine neuromechanical performance decrement. For other outcome measures, MDC thresholds have not been established, limiting interpretation of the functional relevance of observed changes. Moreover, several CMJ variables—including eccentric and landing stiffness, RFD during the concentric and landing phases, and RSI—exhibited wide confidence intervals and elevated standard deviations under both fresh and fatigued conditions. These patterns likely reflect intrinsic physiological variability in CMJ mechanics among flat-footed individuals rather than methodological inconsistencies. All kinetic data were collected using a calibrated, high-resolution force platform under standardized warm-up, familiarization, and testing procedures designed to minimize procedural error. Although participants were recreationally active, they were not trained athletes; therefore, variability in training history, motor learning, and physical conditioning likely contributed to the observed inter-individual differences. These findings highlight the importance of accounting for participant-specific adaptations and motor strategies—both at baseline and under fatigue—when interpreting complex neuromechanical outcomes.

Limitations and future direction

While this study offers valuable insights, its findings were modest and should be interpreted with caution due to certain limitations. The absence of a control group with typical foot posture precluded direct comparisons and limited the ability to draw conclusions regarding foot-type–specific adaptations to both fatigue and KT intervention. Moreover, although the taping protocol followed established guidelines and targeted both the foot arches and the tibialis posterior muscle, variations in tape width, tension, and application technique may have influenced the results. Exploring alternative configurations—such as multi-strip designs—or comparing different taping materials is recommended. It would also be valuable to examine KT alongside other commonly used interventions, such as orthoses, to evaluate its relative efficacy.

Participant characteristics further limit generalizability. The sample consisted exclusively of young, recreationally active females, and the findings may not extend to other populations such as males, trained athletes, or older adults. Examining KT’s utility in sport-specific or fatigue-prone groups—such as competitive female athletes—could enhance the translational relevance of these findings.

Methodological considerations also warrant attention. While fatigue was confirmed using Borg scale ratings and corresponding performance decrements, incorporating more objective measures—such as EMG frequency analysis—could yield a more precise assessment of neuromuscular fatigue. Notably, navicular drop was not reassessed following the fatigue protocol due to timing constraints, which limits interpretation of KT’s efficacy in supporting the medial arch under fatigued conditions. In addition, pre-fatigue assessments were not blinded, introducing potential bias. Future protocols may benefit from the use of motion capture systems or 3D scanning technologies to facilitate blinded, rapid, and post-fatigue evaluation of medial arch behavior.

Although the fatigue protocol incorporated SSCs to replicate the natural pattern of muscle fatigue during dynamic tasks, its laboratory-based format may not fully replicate real-world sport conditions. Future research should investigate KT effects under field-based settings using portable technologies—such as inertial measurement units—to capture real-time biomechanics during gameplay or training. The biomechanical scope of the present analysis was also limited to vGRF; integrating vGRF with surface EMG and 3D motion capture system could yield more comprehensive insights into neuromuscular strategies and the functional effects of KT under both fatigued and non-fatigued conditions.

Finally, MDC values were not available for kinetic variables due to the lack of published reference data for jumping tasks, limiting interpretation of whether the observed changes reflect meaningful effects. Establishing MDC thresholds for dynamic measures will be essential for strengthening clinical interpretation in future research. Taken together, these limitations suggest that KT’s clinical utility in enhancing dynamic performance among individuals with flexible flat feet remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that while KT applied to the foot arches and tibialis posterior resulted in a statistically significant reduction in navicular drop, the magnitude of change fell below the minimal detectable threshold, suggesting limited clinical relevance for improving static foot alignment. Additionally, KT did not yield measurable improvements in vGRF-derived performance variables during CMJ tasks—either before or after fatigue—in physically active females with flexible flat feet.

These findings suggest that under high-load, multi-joint conditions such as jumping, KT may offer minimal functional benefit for performance enhancement. Although factors such as taping configuration, applied tension, and participant characteristics may have influenced the outcomes, the absence of significant performance gains underscores the need for cautious interpretation of KT’s role in dynamic settings.

Beyond taping effects, fatigue appeared to induce alterations in CMJ performance, including reduced jump height and elevated force and stiffness parameters, which may reflect compensatory neuromechanical strategies in individuals with flexible flat feet. While such adaptations might enhance mechanical efficiency under stress, they could also impair shock absorption and increase injury risk—though further research is needed to clarify these mechanisms and their potential clinical implications.

Accordingly, clinical recommendations should be made with caution. KT alone may not provide sufficient structural or functional support to enhance dynamic performance in this population, particularly under fatigued conditions. Broader interventions—such as orthotic support or strength training—may be more effective, though direct comparisons are needed to establish relative efficacy. Future research should incorporate electromyographic, kinematic, and psychological assessments to clarify the mechanisms underlying KT’s limited functional impact and, in turn, guide refinements in application techniques and the development of evidence-based strategies for individuals with structural foot deviations, especially under load-compromised conditions.
