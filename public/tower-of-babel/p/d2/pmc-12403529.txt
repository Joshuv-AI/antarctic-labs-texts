Multi-task machine learning for transfusion decision support in acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding: a novel ensemble approach with clinical validation
Authors: Qiongjie Li, Guolin Chen, Qun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study proposes a multi-task learning (MTL) model to predict the need for blood transfusion in patients with acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding (AUGIB), as well as to estimate the appropriate type and volume of transfusion. The proposed model demonstrates improved predictive performance over existing scoring systems and aims to support clinical decision-making in transfusion management.MethodsClinical data were retrospectively collected from 1256 emergency patients with AUGIB admitted to the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University from January 1, 2022, to December 31, 2023. An external validation cohort (n = 209) was sourced from Fenyang Hospital, Shanxi Province, using identical inclusion criteria. The MTL model integrates oversampling techniques and distribution correction to address data imbalance. A soft-voting ensemble of CatBoost and XGBoost classifiers was used for the classification task, while a stacked regressor combining random forest and XGBoost was employed for the regression task. Model performance was further optimized through a dynamically weighted loss function.ResultsIn the classification task, the model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.965, representing a 20.5% improvement over the traditional Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS). In the regression task, the two-stage stacked regressor (TSR) outperformed other machine learning models in predicting transfusion type and volume , significantly reducing the prediction error of transfusion volume. Compared to random forest (RF), XGBoost, multilayer perceptron (MLP), and backpropagation neural network (BP), the model reduced the overall loss by 9.9%, 21.0%, 38.3%, and 10.0%, respectively, validating the advantages of hierarchical feature selection and dynamic task-weighting. In the external validation set, the model exhibited favorable generalization performance with an AUC of 0.860, and showed good performance in infusion volume prediction error and weighted lossConclusionsThis study presents a robust and interpretable multi-task learning model for transfusion decision-making in AUGIB. By jointly optimizing classification and regression tasks through hierarchical feature selection and dynamic loss allocation, the model supports precision transfusion strategies and holds strong potential for broader application in emergency and critical care settings.

Introduction

Acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding (AUGIB) is one of the most lethal acute digestive system diseases globally, with an annual incidence of 84–160 cases per 100,000 individuals and an in-hospital mortality rate exceeding 10% [1, 2]. The prognosis is particularly severe for elderly patients and those with comorbidities such as liver cirrhosis and chronic kidney disease, with a 30-day rebleeding risk estimated at 15% to 20% [3]. Blood transfusion therapy serves as a core intervention for correcting hemorrhagic shock and maintaining tissue oxygenation, playing a critical role in patient survival [4]. However, it also involves a balance of risks and benefits. A meta-analysis encompassing 12,345 patients demonstrated that inappropriate red blood cell transfusion increased the risk of nosocomial infection by 1.8 times and significantly raised the incidence of multiple organ failure [5].

The current blood transfusion strategy predominantly relies on laboratory parameters, including hemoglobin (Hb) levels, coagulation function indicators such as the international normalized ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and platelet counts [6]. Specifically, a restrictive transfusion strategy recommends administering packed red blood cells (PRBC) when Hb < 7 g/dL, whereas a liberal strategy sets this threshold at Hb < 9 g/dL. Regarding fresh frozen plasma (FFP) transfusion, clinical guidelines suggest initiating it when INR >2 or APTT exceeds 1.5 times the normal range [7, 8]. Although the recommendation by international guidelines to use the Glasgow-Blatchford score (GBS) for guiding transfusion decisions [9, 10], its predictive accuracy regarding the type of blood products and the required transfusion volume remains inadequate [11]. This limitation leads to under and over-transfusion in clinical practice, underscoring the pressing need for more precise decision-support tools [12]. Accurately predicting the requirement for blood transfusions and the volume of blood components during hospitalization is critical for optimizing blood supply management [13]. Such an approach ensures adequate PRBC, FFP, and other blood products tailored to specific patient populations, enhancing patient safety [14].

In recent years, machine learning has shown significant potential in addressing high-dimensional nonlinear data for blood transfusion decision-making [15–17]. However, most existing studies have primarily focused on single-task modeling, such as independently predicting the need for a blood transfusion or estimating the volume of transfusion required [18, 19]. These approaches overlook the intrinsic correlations between classification and regression tasks critical in clinical decision-making. For example, the demand for platelet transfusion exhibits a complex nonlinear dynamic relationship with hemodynamic status indicators [20]. Traditional single-task models, which lack mechanisms for cross-task feature sharing, encounter challenges in effectively capturing complex relationships [21]. Moreover, conventional statistical methods, such as logistic and linear regression, exhibit limited capacity to model the zero-inflated right-skewed distributions frequently observed in medical datasets [22]. These methods are prone to generating non-physiological predictions for patients who do not require a transfusion, where the predicted transfusion volume should strictly be zero [23]. In clinical practice, blood transfusion decision-making involves addressing two key challenges concurrently. The first is a classification task to determine whether a transfusion is indicated, and the second is a regression task to estimate the required volume of each blood component [24, 25]. Consequentially, it becomes imperative to develop a joint modeling framework that can enhance overall decision-making efficiency by enabling collaborative optimization across tasks.

To address the aforementioned issues, this study proposes a Multi-Task Learning (MTL) framework for blood transfusion decision-making in patients with AUGIB. By leveraging hierarchical feature selection and shared feature representation, the framework jointly optimizes classification and regression tasks, effectively capturing their underlying relationships. In the classification task, clinical indicators such as shock index and hemoglobin (Hb) level are selected to reflect the severity of acute bleeding. For the regression task, features related to blood component metabolism, including INR and platelet count (PLT), are identified. The MTL architecture incorporates a soft voting ensemble classifier that combines CatBoost and XGBoost [26], as well as a stacked regressor composed of Random Forest(RF) and XGBoost. This heterogeneous ensemble approach significantly enhances model robustness and generalizability. Although the proposed MTL framework is based on classical paradigms, its contribution lies in its deep adaptation to the transfusion decision-making scenario for AUGIB. Unlike existing MTL approaches that predominantly focus on predicting a single transfusion type or combining transfusion predictions with other clinical interventions, this study enables the simultaneous prediction of four distinct blood components. This capability requires addressing distributional differences and clinical priorities specific to each element. Moreover, by overcoming the limitations of traditional MTL models, which typically share low-level features across tasks, this study introduces a hierarchical feature selection mechanism. In this framework, the classification task identifies features associated with bleeding severity, while the regression task isolates coagulation-related features within the subset of samples positively classified by the former. By incorporating hierarchical feature selection and a dynamic task activation mechanism, this MTL model is better aligned with the complex clinical decision-making needs of AUGIB patients, who often require the concurrent correction of both anemia and coagulopathy.

For the class imbalance commonly observed in medical datasets, this study integrates the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) [27] with a hard constraint mechanism [28]. During model training, SMOTE is applied to balance the sample distribution and enhance data representativeness. In the prediction phase, a hard constraint is introduced to ensure that transfusion volume is predicted as zero for non-transfused patients, thereby preserving physiologically plausible and clinically relevant outputs. By incorporating these strategies, the proposed framework not only overcomes the limitations of conventional single-task models but also provides interpretable preoperative risk assessment tools for clinicians, promoting more accurate and individualized transfusion decision-making in AUGIB.

Methods

Clinical data collection

This study is a single-center retrospective observational cohort study approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University and adheres strictly to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [12]. This study collected data from the electronic medical records of 1251 patients who presented to the emergency department with AUGIB between January 2022 and December 2023. After screening, 1177 patients were included in the final analysis. For external validation, 209 patients meeting identical criteria were sourced from Fenyang Hospital, Shanxi Province, from January 2022 to December 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows- patients aged 18 years or older, a diagnosis of AUGIB confirmed by endoscopy or characteristic clinical manifestations (such as hematemesis, melena, or coffee-ground emesis), and provision of informed consent. Patients were excluded if they were under 18 years of age, had concurrent lower gastrointestinal bleeding (LGIB), lacked outcome indicators, or had more than 20% missing data for key variables. The original data set included 67 clinical features categorized into four groups. The first group includes patient demographic information, such as gender, age, comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, liver cirrhosis), and medication history (e.g., anticoagulants, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs). The second group includes vital signs upon admission, such as systolic blood pressure, pulse, shock index, and Glasgow Coma Scale score. The third group comprises emergency laboratory indicators, such as Hb, coagulation markers (INR, APTT), and liver and kidney function markers (e.g., AST, creatinine), all recorded at the time of the first result before transfusion. The fourth group includes time-related parameters, such as the time from hospital arrival to intravenous access establishment and blood collection. Non-structured text data, such as emergency records, were extracted using natural language processing techniques to capture key diagnostic information. The outcome variables included whether blood transfusion was administered (a binary variable, with the transfusion group accounting for 40.4% of cases) and the infusion volumes of PRBC (units), FFP (mL), platelets (PLT; units), and cryoprecipitate (Cryo; units).

Data preprocessing

Categorical variables were processed using One-Hot Encoding to convert them into numerical vectors. For example, gender was coded as 1 for male and 0 for female, disease presence as 1 for having a disease, 0 for not having a disease, and drug use as 1 for using a drug and 0 for not using a drug. Missing values were handled using a multimodal imputation strategy for mixed data types. For categorical variables, such as drug use, Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) with multiple logistic regression was applied to maintain logical consistency between categories, ensuring that the imputed values reflected clinical medication patterns [29]. For integer variables, such as pulse (P) and respiratory rate (R), median imputation was used to minimize bias. For continuous laboratory indicators, such as Hb and prothrombin time (PT), random forest regression imputation was employed to capture nonlinear relationships between variables while preserving the original data distribution.

To address the class imbalance between the transfusion group (n = 475, 40.4%) and the non-transfusion group (n = 702, 59.6%), Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to the training set to balance the sample distribution. A hard threshold constraint was imposed to ensure the predicted transfusion volume for non-transfusion samples was zero, adhering to clinical physiological constraints [28]. Additionally, to correct the right-skewed distribution of transfusion volume variables such as PRBC and FFP, log1p transformation was applied, followed by Min-Max normalization, to retain data variation while improving model convergence efficiency [30]. All data preprocessing operations were implemented using Python 3.8 (scikit-learn 1.3.2). To support reproducibility and further research, we have also made the code publicly available at https://github.com/glchen777/AUGIB-tranfusion.git.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are presented as counts and percentages, and differences between groups are evaluated using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi $$\end{document}-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Model construction

This study proposes a hierarchical ensemble model based on MTL that simultaneously addresses the classification task of determining whether a transfusion is needed and the regression task of predicting transfusion volume. The model incorporates hierarchical feature selection and task-level collaborative optimization to mirror clinical decision-making. By applying a structured feature selection strategy, the model enhances interpretability, and through a hybrid ensemble architecture, it improves predictive robustness. The methodological innovations focus on three key components, which include hierarchical feature selection, hierarchical ensemble learning, and multi-task collaborative optimization. The proposed MTL framework integrates a hybrid gradient boosting classifier with a two-stage stacked regressor to achieve task synergy through differentiated feature mappings. For the classification task, we introduce the Hybrid Gradient Boosting Voting Ensemble (HGBVE), which combines the strengths of CatBoost and XGBoost. CatBoost uses the Ordered Boosting algorithm with symmetric trees to effectively handle categorical features. XGBoost applies second-order Taylor expansion to optimize the objective function and better model continuous variables [16]. This ensemble strategy is specifically designed to address the heterogeneous feature landscape in AUGIB data, which contains both high-cardinality categorical variables and complex continuous parameters. RF provides robustness against outliers in skewed laboratory values (PLT, Cryo volumes), while XGBoost captures nonlinear dose-response relationships in frequently transfused components (PRBC, FFP). The stacked architecture specifically addresses the sparse distribution of low-frequency blood products (PLT, Cryo) through hierarchical feature abstraction, where meta-features compress high-dimensional patterns into robust latent representations. The second stage applies the meta-learner (ElasticNet) to perform feature shrinkage and nonlinear combination. ElasticNet introduces sparsity constraints to eliminate redundant meta-features while preserving cross-component dosage correlations essential for modeling transfusion synergy in coagulopathy management [32]. Model evaluation

We employ stratified sampling to partition the dataset into a training cohort (n = 942) and a testing cohort (n = 235) at an 8:2 ratio, followed by validation through 5-fold cross-validation. The external cohort (n = 209) was reserved for independent validation. Bayesian optimization tunes hyperparameters of the multi-task framework. Use of cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning for internal validation is considered a robust method for model evaluation before external validation on a separate data set and maximizes the potential performance of the ML model [33]. The ElasticNet mixing coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.7$$\end{document}, and the penalty factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda =0.05$$\end{document}. The joint loss function evaluates overall performance, with classification task weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma = 2$$\end{document} and regression tasks weighted with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _i$$\end{document} at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1:1:0.5:0.5$$\end{document} to reflect clinical priorities.

Model evaluation adopts multi-dimensional metrics. Classification performance is measured using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUCROC), five-fold Cross-Validation, and decision curve analysis (DCA) [34]. For regression tasks, performance is evaluated using the mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE). The MSE highlights extreme prediction deviations, while MAE captures clinically acceptable absolute error thresholds [35]. All regression errors are computed after an inverse transformation to reflect actual transfusion volume deviations in clinical practice. The weighted loss function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal {L}}_{\text {total}}$$\end{document} allows for direct comparison between our MTL framework and traditional machine learning models (RF, XGBoost, MLP, BP), all of which were adapted to perform the same classification and regression tasks. To ensure a fair comparison, all models were evaluated using identical loss components and weighting schemes. Missing values in the external validation cohort were processed using the same method as in the primary dataset. Following standardization, the pre-constructed MTL model was applied for comprehensive validation, with performance metrics evaluated using the same criteria as the internal validation set. Lastly, the interpretability of the MTL model is enhanced through SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) visualization, illustrated by individualized clinical cases, thus increasing its potential utility in clinical practice.

The black-box nature of machine learning models often limits their interpretability. To address this limitation, we incorporated SHAP values to enhance interpretability from both global and local perspectives, thereby improving the model’s clinical utility [36]. Figure 10 illustrates a curve consisting of discrete points representing individual SHAP values, where values greater than 0 indicate an increased transfusion risk. The color gradient (from red to blue) represents the feature magnitude, with deeper red corresponding to higher values and deeper blue indicating lower values. The length of each bar reflects feature importance, visually highlighting the substantial impact of Hb, HCT, APTT, and Vasoactive Drugs on predictions. Specifically, lower Hb, reduced HCT, and prolonged APTT were associated with a higher likelihood of transfusion. Figure 11 demonstrates the nonlinear interaction effects between variables. Hb values exhibited a nonlinear relationship with SHAP values. Low Hb levels increased the predicted risk, particularly when vasoactive drugs were used, while high Hb levels reduced the risk. The color variations explicitly reveal the interaction between Vasoactive Drugs and Hb values in modulating SHAP values.

The SHAP method provides dual interpretability in machine learning models, enabling both global model explanation and individualized analysis for personalized guidance. Figure 12 illustrates that positive values of f(x) correspond to an increased transfusion probability, with key decision factors including Hb, TBIL, TP, and ALB. Figure 13 presents negative values of f(x), which are associated with a lower transfusion probability, with Hb remaining the primary determinant. Risk factors are represented in red, while protective factors are shown in blue, reflecting the directional impact of each variable. This individualized patient analysis highlights Hb and ALB as significant risk factors, while also uncovering their nonlinear relationships.

Furthermore, random forest visualization quantified MTL feature weights. Figure 14 shows importance scores, identifying Hb and HCT as the most influential features for transfusion decisions and blood product selection in AUGIB. Discussion

In recent years, multi-task learning (MTL) has emerged as an advanced machine learning paradigm, increasing attention in developing clinical decision support systems [37]. This approach enables simultaneous modeling of related prediction tasks, thereby improving both accuracy and generalizability. Previous studies have endeavored to predict postoperative bleeding risk or transfusion requirements in ICU patients using machine learning, yet have primarily been constrained to predicting single blood components or integrating other clinical interventions [38, 39]. In this study, we expanded the MTL framework to predict not only transfusion necessity but also the required volumes of PRBC, fresh frozen plasma (FFP), platelets (PLT), and cryoprecipitate (Cryo). The classification task focuses on clinical indicators reflecting the severity of acute bleeding, while the regression task targets features influencing blood component metabolism. Through hierarchical feature selection, the clinical decision logic of “qualitative assessment precedes quantitative evaluation" is realized [40].

The core innovation of this study lies in its in-depth adaptation to the transfusion decision-making process for acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding (AUGIB). At the feature selection level, the classification task screened out 27 key features using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{1}$$\end{document}-regularized logistic regression [31], with hemodynamic indicators such as hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (HCT), and total protein (TP) contributing most significantly to predicting transfusion needs. The regression task further refined this selection, isolating 21 shared features among classification-positive samples. Coagulation-related indicators, including the international normalized ratio (INR), blood glucose (GLU), and total bilirubin (TBIL), emerged as core variables for predicting transfusion volume. This hierarchical strategy mitigated the risk of the curse of dimensionality and enhanced the clinical interpretability of the model, aligning with the clinical decision-making process of “first determining the need for transfusion and then confirming the transfusion volume” [1, 4]. These results align with clinical transfusion practices, especially the importance of correcting hypoxia and managing coagulopathy during shock resuscitation [7, 8]. The model’s integration of hemodynamic parameters and baseline characteristics offers a more comprehensive view of AUGIB pathophysiology than single scoring systems [10, 41], supporting more accurate and individualized transfusion strategies.

To address the common class imbalance in medical data, where the transfusion group accounts for 40.4% in this study, the MTL framework integrates SMOTE with a hybrid ensemble classifier, HGBVE, to enhance the recognition of minority classes. HGBVE combines the strengths of CatBoost in handling categorical features and XGBoost in modeling continuous variables. Through a dynamic soft voting mechanism, it achieves an AUC of 0.965, representing a 20.5% improvement over the traditional Glasgow-Blatchford Score. Additionally, it yields an F1-score of 0.910, outperforming individual models such as RF with 0.896 and XGBoost with 0.905. For regression tasks, TSR effectively addressed the sparse distribution of low-frequency transfusion data, such as PLT and Cryo. It extracted meta-features through RF and XGBoost, combined with feature shrinkage via ElasticNet [32]. For instance, the MSE and MAE for Cryo prediction were reduced to 0.0024. This represented a 93.8% and 94.7% decrease compared to the optimal baseline model (RF). These results confirm the advantages of cross-task feature sharing in modeling sparse data.

Data preprocessing is also crucial for optimizing model performance. As shown in Fig. 3, the original distributions of PRBC and FFP transfusion volumes were significantly right-skewed. After the application of the log1p the symmetry of the distributions improved markedly, providing optimized input for regression models and reducing the MSE and MAE in PRBC and FFP predictions. A hard constraint mechanism ensured that predicted transfusion volumes for non-transfused patients were restricted to zero during the regression phase, effectively mitigating false-positive interference that could be introduced by SMOTE oversampling. This dual mechanism ensured logical consistency between classification and regression tasks, thereby enhancing the clinical applicability of the model.

External validation results confirmed the generalization ability of the MTL framework. On the independent center dataset, HGBVE maintained an AUC of 0.860, an accuracy of 0.794, and a specificity of 0.808. Furthermore, TSR achieved the lowest error metrics in PRBC and PLT predictions, with a total weighted loss of 4.206, significantly lower than that of traditional models. This indicates that despite differences in patient characteristics across centers, such as the prevalence of cirrhosis (23.8% vs 31.9%), the model can stably adapt to diverse clinical environments.

A key contribution of this study is the enhancement of model interpretability. Through global SHAP value analysis, Hb with an importance score of 0.303 and HCT with 0.171 were identified as the primary determinants of transfusion decisions. These findings align with feature ablation experiments, where removing Hb resulted in a 6.44% decrease in AUC. SHAP dependence plots revealed nonlinear variable interactions, such as the significant increase in transfusion risk when low Hb levels coincide with vasoactive drug administration. These insights provide data-driven support for personalized intervention strategies.

From a clinical translation perspective, the model optimizes transfusion management through the following pathways. First, it reduces inappropriate transfusions by achieving a high precision of 0.927, mitigating the risk of infection associated with over-transfusion, while maintaining a high recall of 0.901 to ensure timely intervention for critically ill patients. Its high specificity of 0.919 enables more accurate identification of patients not requiring transfusion, thereby reducing the risk of immunosuppression and transfusion-related complications. Secondly, it enables optimized resource allocation: accurate prediction of transfusion volumes reduces blood product waste. For instance, MAE in FFP prediction is reduced by 50.1%, potentially saving 140,000 units of blood products annually [42]. Finally, it adapts to heterogeneous environments: in external validation, the model maintained stable performance despite variations in patient characteristics across medical centers (e.g., liver cirrhosis prevalence of 23.8% vs 31.9%), demonstrating its adaptability to diverse clinical practices.

The MTL framework proposed in this study is a clinically interpretable and modular model for guiding blood transfusion decisions in AUGIB. This study introduces a clinically interpretable and modular MTL framework for transfusion decision-making in AUGIB. By combining machine learning innovations with clinical knowledge, the model offers improved prediction of transfusion necessity and volume. It supports individualized patient management and reduces healthcare utilization and blood product wastage.

This study recognizes several limitations that merit careful consideration. The reliance on a single-center retrospective cohort may introduce potential selection bias, which is a limitation of such research designs. Although potential error propagation exists, its impact on overall regression errors remains minimal due to the controlled recall rate of 0.901. Future refinement could be achieved through threshold calibration or subgroup-specific feature refinement. Additionally, the relatively small sample sizes in both the training and validation cohorts may constrain our ability to fully capture inter-patient variability. Future research will collect larger, more balanced cohort data from multiple medical institutions to further validate the robustness of the proposed model. Moreover, gaining a deeper understanding of the dynamic relationship between clinicians and human-centered artificial intelligence tools in clinical settings is crucial for enhancing diagnostic and therapeutic outcomes for patients.

Conclusion

This study proposes a multi-task learning (MTL) framework for decision-making in blood transfusion management among patients with acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding (AUGIB). MTL achieves significant clinical value in reducing inappropriate transfusions, optimizing blood product resource allocation, and adapting to heterogeneous clinical environments. Although the study data were derived from a single-center retrospective cohort, which carries the risk of selection bias, and model error propagation requires further control through threshold adjustment or subgroup optimization, the stable performance demonstrated in external validation indicates its cross-scenario applicability. These findings lay the foundation for conducting multi-center prospective clinical trials and provide a basis for the standardized application of AI-assisted transfusion decision-making in diverse medical practices.
