Employee background-check policies in long-term care facilities in the United States
Authors: Gulrukh Mehboob, Brian Kaskie, Hari Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesEmployee background checks can help protect older adults in long-term care facilities. The National Background Check Program (NBCP) offers federal support to strengthen employee background-check systems. Yet, regulations on provider requirements, disqualifying offenses, and administrative processes differ across states. We examine differences between NBCP (N = 27) and non-NBCP (N = 23) states across three domains: (1) types of LTC providers required to conduct background checks, (2) disqualifying offenses considered by each state, and (3) processes used to administer background checks. We also assess regulatory changes across these domains from 2005 to 2024 across all 50 states.Research Design and MethodsWe conducted policy surveillance of state regulations on employee background-check programs applicable to individuals seeking work at long-term care facilities. Our sample includes all 50 states, with 27 receiving an NBCP grant. We assess current regulations across three policy domains and review how these regulations have evolved since 2005, when the NBCP pilot program was initiated.ResultsNBCP states have expanded background-check regulations to include a broader range of long-term care providers and disqualifying offenses following the grant. However, minimal differences were observed between the two groups in background check processes such as fingerprinting and registry reviews.Discussion and ImplicationsWhile many NBCP states have adopted measures like disqualifying offenses and RAP back systems, their overall impact on hiring practices, F-tags, and quality of care remains uncertain.

Background

In 2020, approximately 5.8 million individuals used long-term services and supports (LTSS) and over half of older adults aged 65 years or above are expected to need LTSS at some point in their life (Chidambaram, 2024; Clarke & Pierson, 1999). LTSS can be provided through home- and community-based services (HCBS) or residential settings including nursing homes (NHs) and assisted living facilities (ALFs) (Chidambaram, 2024). Up to half of those older adults living in NHs and ALFs experience a cognitive impairment related to Alzheimer’s disease or a related dementia (Chen et al., 2023). These individuals also experience significantly higher risk of abuse and neglect than those living at home in the community (Mogoka & Bistas, 2020). Abuse and neglect of older adults’ stem from a complex interplay of structural determinants, institutional environments, and workforce dynamics. Research consistently shows that insufficient staffing levels, limited geriatric training, and poor organizational conditions significantly heighten the risk of staff-to-resident abuse in long-term care facilities (Hirt et al., 2022; Mogoka & Bistas, 2020).

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) defines abuse as “the willful infliction of injury, unreasonable confinement, intimidation, or cruel punishment that results in physical harm, pain, or mental anguish, or the deprivation of goods or services necessary to prevent such harm” (CMS Exhibit 358 for Facility Reported Incidents, n.d.). Abuse includes improper use of chemical restraints, sexual abuse, verbal and psychological abuse, such as threats, harassment, and humiliation (Clarke & Pierson, 1999; CMS Exhibit 358 for Facility Reported Incidents, n.d.). Abuse and neglect in residential LTSS settings have persisted and remain a critical concern, with approximately 10% to 16% of nursing home residents in the US experiencing some form of abuse or neglect (Marciano, 2001; Wiener, 2003).

CMS tracks such undesirable NHs abuse by issuing citations categorized under F-Tags in the CMS State Operations Manual (State Operations Manual, 2024). Key citations include the right to be free from physical and chemical restraints (F221, F222, F604, F605), free from abuse and neglect, involuntary seclusion, and misappropriation/exploitation (F223, F224, F600-F603), reporting of alleged violations, investigate/prevent/correct alleged violation, and develop/implement abuse/neglect policies (F225, F226, F607-F610) (State Operations Manual, 2024). Researchers have suggested that some nursing home staff perpetrate abuse, neglect, or exploitation of residents (Marciano, 2001; Shipman & Hooten, 2009). Recent research on abuse of older adults in NHs indicates that women, racial and ethnic minorities, and individuals with cognitive impairment face a heightened risk of abuse, with many experiencing multiple forms concurrently. Moreover, a recent study estimated that approximately 1 in 10 nursing home residents experience abuse from staff, including physical and psychological mistreatment, neglect, and financial exploitation (Mogoka & Bistas, 2020; Rivers, 2025). High prevalence of abuse-related citations underscores the need for thorough employee background checks before hiring. The documented cases of staff-related abuse show why strong and ongoing background-check systems are necessary to protect residents (Shipman & Hooten, 2009). Through fingerprint-based background checks, sharing of information across agencies, and continuous monitoring, we may be able to prevent individuals with histories of violence, neglect, or exploitation from entering into direct-care roles in long-term care facilities (Blubaugh, 2012).

Policymakers and program administrators have implemented background checks as a preventive measure to enhance resident safety and decrease such incidents of abuse. Criminal background checks are a standard method to identify individuals with a criminal history using national, state, county, or local databases (Levinson, 2024; Shipman & Hooten, 2009). These checks allow employers to review criminal history and assess the risk of crimes that could result in patient abuse or financial, physical, and reputational harm to the organization (­Shipman & Hooten, 2009).

Most relevant here, federal law mandates background checks for employees with direct access to vulnerable populations, such as older adults in long-term care facilities (Shipman & Hooten, 2009). Direct care workers in LTSS settings are the front-line paid professionals who provide hands-on direct care to the older adults. These include certified nursing assistants (CNAs), home health aides (HHAs), and direct support professionals (DSPs) (Heiks & Sabine, 2022). Researchers have found over 90% of all LTSS employers conduct criminal background checks, with 73% applying them to all applicants, including direct care workers (U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, 2014).

While these federal regulations set minimum standards, states may add or subtract from these requirements (­Shipman & Hooten, 2009). Some states mandate fingerprint-based checks for all direct care workers, while others permit name-based screenings. States could also differ in the types of long-term care facilities subject to background checks, with some states mandating screenings for all facilities and others limiting them to NHs. Similarly, several states mandate the use of electronic fingerprinting to better integrate with systems like RAP Back (Record of Arrest and Prosecution) systems (OIG, 2019).

To support state government efforts to strengthen employee background-check systems, a pilot of the National Background Check Program (NBCP) was conducted from 2004 to 2007 authorized under the Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003 (MMA). The pilot was implemented in seven states: Alaska, Idaho, Illinois, Michigan, Nevada, New Mexico, and Wisconsin. Building on this initiative, the Affordable Care Act (ACA) of 2010 expanded the pilot by establishing the NBCP and making grants available to all U.S. states (Murrin, 2020).

The program aimed to help states develop cost-effective and efficient screening systems, including fingerprint-based checks of state and FBI criminal history records, searches of state abuse and neglect registries, and reviews of relevant disciplinary and licensing records. Each participating state was eligible to receive up to $3 million in federal funding over 3 years, with a cost-sharing requirement of $1 in state funds for every $3 in federal funds received (Murrin, 2020).

Between 2010 and 2024, 28 states and DC participated in NBCP (AK, CA, CT, DC, DE, FL, GA, HI, ID, IL, KS, KY, MD, ME, MI, MN, MO, MS, NC, NM, NV, OH, OK, OR, PR, RI, UT, WV, WI), though two states (MA and WI) exited before completion (Murrin, 2020). NBCP participation required states to expand and enhance their existing employee background-check system for direct care workers applying to work in a community-based and residential long-term care facilities. For instance, states that already had background-check programs for NHs applicants were required to expand these checks to community-based LTSS providers such as hospices, home health agencies, and personal care providers. In addition, states were expected to strengthen their systems by adopting electronic fingerprinting technology to conduct background checks more efficiently (Murrin, 2020). Participating states submitted quarterly reports to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) to track implementation progress and evaluated on their ability to enhance technology for fingerprint-based state and FBI criminal history checks for direct care workers and ensure ongoing monitoring of employees for new criminal records.

During this time, 23 states chose not to participate in the NBCP and relied on their own legislative authority and regulatory frameworks to implement screening requirements without federal support. Evidence that stronger background checks improve care quality and reduce the risk of abuse further motivated these states to expand certain elements of their programs. By doing so independently, they were able to strengthen safeguards while maintaining full control over program design and implementation without being subject to federal monitoring, evaluation, enforcement, or reporting requirements that often increase administrative burden (Garcia, 2024; National Background Check Program for Long-Term Care Providers: A Final Assessment, 2024).

Between 2012 and 2024, the Office of Inspector General (OIG) conducted multiple evaluations, highlighting key achievements, including improved background-check systems and increased compliance with federal requirements (Murrin, 2020; OIG Report Assesses National LTC Background Check Program, n.d.). However, inconsistent state participation, delays in system integration, lack of legislative authority, and difficulties in interdepartmental coordination remained across different states. The final OIG evaluation in 2024 emphasized the need for standardized regulatory structures, given the wide variation in state-level approaches. Key background-check system differences relate to the specific LTSS providers required to conduct background checks, the types of disqualifying offenses, and the administrative procedures for implementing these checks (OIG Report Assesses National LTC Background Check Program, 2024).

NBCP represents a significant federal initiative to strengthen workforce screening and prevent abuse, neglect, and exploitation in long-term care settings. Prior evaluations have documented gains in fingerprint-based checks, data integration, and real-time criminal record monitoring (Murrin, 2020; OIG, 2024), but they also point to persistent variation across states in scope, implementation capacity, and sustainability. We build on these initial reports to improve our understanding of variations in the background-check program scope and processes across the states that could potentially impact abuse, neglect, and exploitation of residents in different LTSS settings. Scope refers to differences in the types of LTSS providers covered by background checks and the range of disqualifying offenses, while processes capture the regulatory mechanisms for implementation, such as IT integration and electronic fingerprinting.

We compare NBCP states (N = 27) and non-NBCP states (N = 23), examining differences in regulatory structures governing employee background checks and the extent to which each state institutionalized comprehensive safeguards from the program’s pilot phase through its conclusion in 2024. A retrospective 20-year review offers a timely and policy-relevant perspective as the long-term care system transitions to a post-NBCP environment. This period captures key inflection points, including early pilot adoption, ACA-driven expansion, and sustained system maturation alongside federal oversight. Some states, like Michigan and Illinois, built strong background-check systems that used connected databases, electronic fingerprinting, and ongoing monitoring. Others (MA and WI) dropped out early or had trouble expanding the system to cover home- and community-based services(OIG Report Assesses National LTC Background Check Program, n.d.). These differences show how laws, coordination between agencies, and funding affected how the program was put into practice. Examining both NBCP and non-NBCP states over this period allows us to identify regulatory models, reveal gaps that persisted despite federal and state investment, and assess variation in disqualifying offenses, provider categories, and monitoring mechanisms.

As policymakers consider strategies to sustain and modernize background-check systems without dedicated NBCP grant funding, lessons from states’ experiences over the past two decades provide critical guidance. Our study provides a comprehensive view of how background-check systems evolved over two decades across federally funded and nonparticipating states. Our findings will help policymakers assess state-level approaches and determine best practices for a more effective and consistent background-check system.

Research objectives

Our conceptual framework to examine employee background-check programs is grounded in Routine Activities Theory (Cohen & Felson, 1979). In 2005, Harris and Keller extended this theory to examine the role of background checks in hiring decisions (Harris & Keller, 2005). This theoretical perspective emphasizes that criminal acts occur when three elements converge: likely offenders, suitable targets, and the absence of capable guardians (Harris & Keller, 2005). Apart from these risk factors, there are several other situational and organizational factors that lead to abuse (Naughton & Drennan, 2016). These include high staff turnover and burn out, discrimination or prejudice against older individuals, facilities serving high proportions of low-income residents, weak regulatory structures, and lack of accountability systems (Phillips et al., 2013).

Abuse occurs when likely offenders encounter vulnerable older adults in settings with insufficient supervision and guardianship. Background-check programs target the offender component by screening out individuals with histories of violence or exploitation (Shipman & Hooten, 2009). However, resident vulnerability (life-course and cognitive factors) and institutional conditions such as understaffing, burnout, ageism, and weak regulatory enforcement shape opportunities for abuse and the effectiveness of prevention strategies (Naughton & Drennan, 2016).

The NBCP was designed to enhance resident safety in long-term care facilities by providing states with financial and technical resources to develop robust employee background-check systems (Murrin, 2020). However, the differences in background-check system across states is shaped by state-level variations in regulations related to scope and processes. State policy variation in scope (provider types and disqualifying offenses) and processes (fingerprinting, registry checks, monitoring) affects system strength. Protecting older adults in long-term care facilities requires integrating robust background-check systems with positive organizational culture, adequate staffing and training, and strong, ongoing regulatory oversight and accountability (Figure 1).

A conceptual framework for abuse/neglect of older adults and background-check systems in long-term care.

Based on our conceptual framework, we evaluate the scope and processes of employee background checks across three key domains: (1) the types of LTSS providers required to conduct background checks, (2) the disqualifying offenses considered by each state, and (3) the processes used to administer and enforce these checks. Additionally, we examine changes in states’ background-check policies from 2005 to 2024 to assess how regulatory frameworks have evolved relative to changes in the national and state-level policies.

Methods

We conduct a surveillance of state regulations governing employee background-check programs as applied to individuals working in licensed community-based and residential LTSS facilities, including nursing facilities, skilled nursing facilities, home health agencies, hospice providers, and intermediate care facilities for individuals with disabilities. Our surveillance involves the systematic collection and analysis of authoritative legal texts related to public health laws and regulations. We transform these texts into quantitative data, enabling comparisons and distinctions of policies across jurisdictions as a tool for capturing cross-state differences (Burris et al., 2016; Smith et al., 2021) (Table 1).

Sample and data

Our sample size contains all 50 states, including the 27 that received a NBCP grant. We identified current background-check regulations by first identifying publicly available sources related to state programs and their corresponding regulations, using primary sources such as closeout reports, and secondary sources including state-specific legislative websites. We ­compiled this text and then used Lexis to complete our ­historical survey to identify regulatory changes between 2005 (LexisNexis Academic, 2024.) To ensure data validity and reliability, we cross-checked our data against OIG reports and consulted technical experts from the Center for Vulnerable Population Protection (VPP) on key domain variables (Murrin, 2020; OIG Report Assesses National LTC Background Check Program, 2024). While the OIG reports focus exclusively on states that received NBCP grants, our study extends that framework to include non-NBCP states and provides a comprehensive assessment of all key policy domains relevant to background checks, as identified in the existing literature. Details on the key constructs drawn from the literature are provided below.

Domains and variables

We identified three key domains of state regulations related to employee background-check programs, each with distinct components: types of providers required to conduct background checks, disqualifying offenses considered by each state, and process used to administer and enforce these checks. Below is a detailed description of each domain:

Types of providers

It is important to include all community-based and residential LTSS providers in criminal background checks to ensure older adults are protected from abuse, neglect, and exploitation (Levinson, 2024; Shipman & Hooten, 2009). The NBCP requires states to conduct background checks for direct care workers from all nine types of long-term care providers (Murrin, 2020). These include Skilled Nursing Facilities (SNFs) that provide temporary care after hospitalization, Nursing Facilities (NFs) that provide 24-hr LTSS care, home health agencies (HHAs) offering skilled nursing and home health services, hospice care (HC) which provides services to terminally ill patients, long-term care hospitals (LTCH) providing extended inpatient care, personal care providers (PCPs) who assist with daily activities at home, adult daycare providers that offers daytime supervision and companionship, LTSS provider-coordinators who deliver a range of medical and personal care services, and intermediate care facilities (ICFs) that support individuals with intellectual or developmental disabilities through specialized residential care (Wright, 2012).

Disqualifying offenses

Incorporating different types of offenses in the list of disqualifying offenses in the LTSS settings is important because older individuals are vulnerable to abuse, neglect, and exploitation (Blumstein & Nakamura, 2009; Post et al., 2014). The NBCP award required states to identify disqualifying offenses for employee criminal background checks. States have implemented regulations that differ in the types of disqualifying offenses for direct care workers caring for older adults (Murrin, 2020; OIG Report Assesses National LTC Background Check Program, n.d.). For this domain, we systematically collected data on 10 key offense types for all states: incarceration, civil fines, financial exploitation, petty theft, sex offender status, child abuse, drug offenses, patient abuse or neglect, fraud, and other related violations.

Processes

As part of the NBCP, states were required to institute several processes concerning the administration of background checks including electronic fingerprinting, abuse registry screenings, and RAP Back (Murrin, 2020; OIG Report Assesses National LTC Background Check Program, n.d.). There has been an increase in the use of electronic fingerprinting or live scan technology in recent years (Blubaugh, 2012). This technology digitally captures fingerprints using a scanning device and enables electronic submission to the relevant authorities, significantly reducing errors and expediting processing times (Blubaugh, 2012). Additionally, the implementation of RAP Back systems has introduced a proactive monitoring approach (Abujarad et al., 2013). These systems automatically update an individual’s record with any new criminal activity after the initial fingerprinting and promptly notify licensing agencies of such updates (Abujarad et al., 2013). We also examined regulations related to state abuse registry databases for records of abuse or neglect allegations (Abujarad et al., 2013; Blumstein & Nakamura, 2009).

Analysis

We conducted a domain-level analysis for each state across three areas: types of LTSS providers required to conduct background checks, disqualifying offenses considered by each state, and processes used to administer and enforce these checks. States were categorized based on the presence of documented regulations within each domain and then separated by grant status. In the types of providers domain, we totalled coded regulations on a scale from 1 to 9, reflecting the number of LTSS provider types included in each state’s background-check requirements. In the disqualifying offenses domain, we added the number of offenses specified in state regulations from 1 to 10 reflecting the level of regulatory comprehensiveness. Similarly, in the administrative processes domain, we identified states with regulations that incorporate a broad set of procedures to strengthen the background-check system. We score administrative processes on a range from 1 to 7. We applied a redundant coding technique, assigning a score of 1 when explicit policies or laws were present for key domain variables, and a score of 0 when they were absent (Table 1).

After assessing states’ adoption of background-check system regulations, we examined how their regulatory frameworks have changed over time by accounting any changes to background-check-related policies following 2010, when several states received the NBCP grant. We systematically tracked each state across all policy domains annually beginning in 2005 to identify whether a regulation related to background checks was adopted following the launch of the NBCP in 2010. Temporal changes were coded as “1” if the regulation was adopted after 2010 and 0 otherwise. To illustrate longitudinal patterns, we employed color-coded tables (Tables 2 and 3) to visualize the expansion of regulatory frameworks over time in both NBCP and non-NBCP states. To ensure consistency in coding, a subset of regulations was independently coded by two researchers. We assessed inter-code reliability using percent agreement, with an agreement rate of 85% or higher considered acceptable. Any discrepancies were reviewed and resolved through consensus, and the coding protocol was updated to improve clarity based on defined keywords (see Table 1).

Finally, we conduct a comparative analysis to examine whether NBCP adoption varied by Medicaid dependency, prevalence of severe deficiencies, and political alignment. Medicaid dependency reflects the degree to which a state’s population rely on Medicaid funding. We use data from KFF Medicaid State Fact Sheets to obtain the percentage of a state’s population that was covered by Medicaid. Severe deficiencies denote violations causing actual harm or posing immediate jeopardy to residents (CMS scope and severity levels G–L). We use data from Long Term Care Community Coalition (LTCCC) to identify the percentage of deficiencies that were severe in each state. We rely on the data from the National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL) that used recent gubernatorial and legislative election outcomes to define political alignment designation for each state as Republican-dominated or Democratic-dominated. We use these contextual factors to assess whether they are associated with state participation in NBCP (LCSL, 2024; LTCCC, 2025; KFF, 2025).

Results

We observed a considerable amount of variation across states in the number of provider types covered by these regulations. For example, 23 NBCP states and 11 non-NBCP states required background checks for seven to nine different LTSS provider types. In contrast, only two NBCP states and six non-NBCP states mandated checks for four or fewer provider types, typically limited to skilled nursing facilities, nursing facilities, home health agencies, and hospice care providers. While non-NBCP states consistently include these core provider types in their criminal background-check requirements, NBCP states expanded the scope of their regulations to encompass a wider array of LTSS settings and provider categories, reflecting a more comprehensive approach to workforce screening. This expansion suggests that NBCP participation may have incentivized states to build more comprehensive screening infrastructures. By contrast, most non-NBCP states concentrated their regulations on core institutional providers such as skilled nursing facilities, nursing facilities, home health agencies, and hospice care indicating a narrower interpretation of workforce coverage and potentially leaving gaps in community-based service oversight (Figure 2).

The different types of LTC providers included in our analysis are skilled nursing facilities, nursing facilities, home health agencies, providers of hospice care, long-term-care hospitals, personal care providers, providers of adult day care, LTC providers, coordinators, and ICFS for people with disabilities.

Regarding disqualifying offenses, 16 NBCP states outline an array of disqualifying offenses including incarceration, civil fines, financial exploitation, petty theft, sex offender status, child abuse, drug offenses, patient abuse/neglect, and fraud. In contrast, seven non-NBCP states include a narrower set of disqualifying offenses (fewer than four) in their background-check regulations, typically limited to patient abuse, neglect, and child abuse (Figure 3). The broader offense coverage by NBCP states reflects a preventive orientation that emphasizes safeguarding against multiple forms of potential harm. Non-NBCP states, by contrast, typically restrict disqualifications to direct care–related offenses such as patient abuse or child neglect, suggesting a more limited risk framework centered on immediate care violations rather than broader behavioral or ethical risks.

The different types of disqualifying offenses included in our analysis are incarceration, civil fines, financial exploitation, petty theft, sex offender status, child abuse, drug offenses, patient abuse/neglect, fraud, and other offenses.

With respect to employee background-check system processes, 37 states have regulations covering a range of procedures designed to enhance the efficiency of background-check programs, with the number of processes ranging from 5 to 7. Among these, 20 NBCP states implement a wide array of processes, including the use of third-party services, electronic fingerprinting, criminal history checks, abuse registry reviews, RAP Back enrollment, and mandatory reporting. A similar pattern of broad coverage was observed among 17 non-NBCP states. However, NBCP and non-NBCP states differ notably in terms of use of third-party services, RAP Back systems, and mandatory reporting requirements. NBCP states are more likely to have formal regulations addressing these components (Figure 4).

The expansions observed in NBCP states have largely occurred following the 2010 launch of the National Background Check Program (NBCP) grant initiative. For instance, Delaware added home health agencies, long-term care hospitals, and adult day care providers; Florida included hospice and adult day care facilities; and Georgia incorporated home health agencies, hospice, personal care providers, and intermediate care facilities (ICFs) for individuals with disabilities. Other NBCP states, such as Idaho and Kansas, made similar expansions (Tables 2 and 3).

In the non-NBCP group, some states such as Arizona (AZ), Colorado (CO), Iowa (IA), Louisiana (LA), and New Jersey (NJ) also broadened their regulatory frameworks by including additional LTSS provider types after 2010. However, the majority of non-NBCP states did not expand their regulatory frameworks substantially over time.

Similarly, several NBCP states expanded their list of disqualifying offenses over time. For example, Illinois (IL), Oregon (OR), Rhode Island (RI), Connecticut (CT), Missouri (MO), Kentucky (KY), and Maine (ME) included additional offenses such as petty theft and drug-related offenses in their disqualification criteria. Among non-NBCP states, Arkansas (AR) and Montana (MT) expanded their regulations to include offenses such as sex offender status, child abuse, and drug-related offenses. However, the majority of non-NBCP states did not expand their disqualifying offenses lists over time (Tables 2 and 3).

In terms of administrative processes, both NBCP and non-NBCP states enhanced their background-check systems by expanding on the regulatory frameworks. These include adoption of electronic fingerprinting, criminal history checks, abuse registry reviews, Rap Back (continuous criminal record monitoring), and mandatory reporting. Both groups show a better expansion in the regulations related to administrative process over time. However, we observe extensive utilization of RAP Back and mandatory reporting in NBCP states relative to non-NBCP states (Tables 2 and 3). This extensive adoption of Rap Back systems and mandatory reporting provisions reflects a stronger infrastructure for real-time monitoring and coordination in NBCP states. This pattern suggests that participation in NBCP provided both financial and technical support that enabled states to move beyond one-time pre-employment checks toward ongoing workforce surveillance models. By contrast, non-NBCP states show more limited uptake of these continuous monitoring tools. Collectively, these differences underscore how federal participation and technical assistance influence states’ capacity to institutionalize dynamic, prevention-oriented background-check processes rather than static compliance mechanisms.

We present whether NBCP adoption was associated with Medicaid reliance, political alignment, and the prevalence of severe deficiencies in Table S1 and Figure 4. We considered potential correlates to the state-to-state variation by considering Medicaid dependency, political alignment, and the prevalence of harm-level deficiencies. Our comparative analysis showed minimal quantitative differences between NBCP and non-NBCP states. While roughly half of NBCP states (48%) exhibited slightly higher Medicaid dependency, political alignment and the prevalence of harm-level deficiencies (6% on average) were comparable to non-NBCP states rather than political alignment or prevalence of harm-level deficiencies. Taken together, these contextual factors suggest that participation in NBCP funding was shaped by broader governance and institutional factors rather than political alignment or prevalence of harm-level deficiencies (Galle, 2008; McLendon et al., 2016). States with greater administrative capacity, stronger regulatory infrastructures, and a history of collaboration with federal health programs may have been more inclined to formalize employee background-check systems during the NBCP period. In contrast, several non-NBCP states developed screening frameworks independently, reflecting preferences for state autonomy and locally tailored oversight mechanisms outside federal demonstration structures.

Discussion

We assessed state regulations addressing employee background-check systems applied to applicants to community-based and residential LTSS settings across the United States and identified changes across three key domains: the types of LTSS providers required to conduct background checks, the scope of disqualifying offenses considered by each state, and process used to administer and enforce these checks and its evolution over time.

Our analysis reveals notable progress among NBCP states in requiring a broader range of LTSS providers to conduct employee background checks. However, a substantial number of non-NBCP states also enforce background-check requirements across different LTC facility types, demonstrating significant regulatory coverage.

Regulations on disqualifying offenses are crucial in ensuring thorough evaluations of potential employees before hiring (Blumstein & Nakamura, 2009; Marciano, 2001). It is important to identify disqualifying offenses, but we need to ensure that these offenses have meaningful role to the direct care. Our analysis shows that most NBCP states have broadened the scope of disqualifying offenses, encompassing a range of infractions from sex offender status to petty theft and civil fines. In contrast, the majority of non-NBCP states primarily consider offenses such as sex crimes, child abuse, patient neglect, and drug-related violations as grounds for disqualification. However, since criminal background checks can often be discriminatory and socially harmful, scholars argue that they should be limited when a criminal record has no meaningful relevance to the role (Petersen, 2015). Research suggests that a criminal record should be directly relevant to the applicant’s evaluation for the specific position they are applying for (Marciano, 2001). Therefore, both job responsibilities and the broader context of the position should determine which convictions are considered in the hiring process (Petersen, 2015; Stolt, 2009). Future research is needed to examine whether overly restrictive background-check policies may be unintentionally excluding qualified individuals from the long-term care workforce. For background-check systems, it is important to have automated systems that streamline the process and continuous monitoring mechanisms that track changes in an individual’s background after the initial check (Abujarad et al., 2013). Our assessment indicates that NBCP states have strengthened regulations in these areas. However, beyond these advancements, we found minimal differences between NBCP and non-NBCP states in other aspects of background-check processes.

While NBCP states have implemented more robust systems to efficiently identify and disqualify individuals with criminal histories, concerns persist regarding the completeness and validity of these checks (National Background Check Program for Long-Term Care Providers: A Final Assessment., 2024). Abuse registries, for instance, have been reported to contain information gaps, potentially omitting critical updates or accurate records (Marciano, 2001; Maurer, 2015). Although technological improvements may help reduce abuse citations such as the official violations issued to facilities for confirmed incidents of abuse, neglect, or exploitation; further research is needed to understand how limited-scope background-check programs and outdated systems in non-NBCP states affect regulatory compliance and the safety of residents in long-term care settings.

Furthermore, these limitations disproportionately burden vulnerable groups in the labor market, particularly Black and Hispanic applicants. Without reliable, accurate, and comprehensive background-check systems, qualified minority candidates with no disqualifying records may face diminished hiring prospects (Stolt, 2009). Literature indicates that in the absence of credible information, employers may rely on crude proxies such as race, age, or gender, socially visible markers that are often unfairly associated with criminality (Holzer et al., 2006). In a labor market al.eady facing staffing shortages, such discriminatory practices further disadvantage minority applicants. Research demonstrates that when employers conduct background checks, Black applicants are more likely to be excluded based on the records revealed. Conversely, when employers do not run checks, they may still exclude Black applicants based on perceived criminality (Holzer et al., 2006; Stolt, 2009). Thus, both the presence of flawed background-check systems and their absence can reinforce racial bias in hiring, compounding barriers for minority groups in long-term care and other sectors (Holzer et al., 2006; Stolt, 2009).

The unjust exclusion when background-check systems are flawed and discrimination when employers rely on assumptions in the absence of credible screening tools create a dual layer of inequity in the hiring process. Effective long-term-care workforce policy must therefore balance resident safety with equitable access to employment (Kurlychek et al., 2019). This balance can be achieved through transparent and validated screening practices that incorporate individualized review, due-process protections, and rehabilitation opportunities (Maurer, 2015). Emerging state initiatives increasingly reflect this approach, aiming to strengthen both patient safety and workforce inclusion amid ongoing staffing shortages (Dunlap et al., 2021). In a sector facing persistent staffing shortages, such balanced approaches are strategically necessary. Background-check systems designed with accuracy, due-process protections, rehabilitation pathways, and appeals mechanisms help safeguard vulnerable older adults while preserving equitable career pathways for diverse and qualified caregivers ­(Kurlychek et al., 2019).

Background-check systems operate within a broader ecology of workforce regulation (Binns & Kempf, 2021). Beyond criminal background checks, existing research highlights other systemic factors contributing to patient abuse and neglect in long-term care facilities, including understaffing, low wages, high turnover, and inadequate training among direct care workers (Marciano, 2001). Moreover, is also possible that employees with no prior criminal record will engage in abuse, neglect, or mistreatment of older adults (Luther, 2012). Addressing broader workforce challenges is essential for a comprehensive approach to reducing abuse/neglect of older adults in long-term care settings (Maurer, 2015). These policy orientations reflect broader structural determinants of care quality, where staffing adequacy, turnover, and training interact with the design of monitoring and enforcement systems. Integrating workforce conditions into the regulatory framework clarifies that background checks are not isolated compliance tools but components of a multilayered governance strategy that responds to workforce volatility, varying administrative capacity, and differing state-level regulatory approaches toward labor regulation and resident safety (Binns & Kempf, 2021; Luther, n.d.).

Previous evaluations of the NBCP have identified significant challenges, including limitations in state legislative authority to enforce or expand background-check policies and difficulties in coordinating efforts across various state agencies (Murrin, 2020; National Background Check Program for Long-Term Care Providers: A Final Assessment., 2024). While our findings indicate that NBCP states demonstrate stronger regulatory frameworks, further research is needed to contrast background check program challenges across both NBCP and non-NBCP states. Future work should survey state officials to explore how variation in the state commitment to uphold the regulatory framework through monitoring and enforcement shapes the functioning of background-check systems in long-term care. Understanding these barriers will be critical in refining policies and ensuring more effective protections for vulnerable residents in long-term care.

Conclusion

We found that NBCP states have expanded the range of LTSS providers required to conduct employee background checks and implemented new systems to identify and disqualify individuals with criminal histories from working in LTSS settings. However, the overall impact of these changes remains unclear. Specifically, it is not yet known whether these components have significantly influenced hiring patterns, reduced F-tags, or improved care quality. The interaction between NBCP implementation and the broader regulatory environment such as new staffing ratio mandates raises questions about whether any observed improvements are directly attributable to NBCP or diluted by overlapping reforms. Future research is needed to assess the causal impact of NBCP and its components, both individually and in combination with other regulatory changes. Furthermore, we acknowledge that some regulatory processes may have differential impacts on outcomes; however, we do not currently have sufficient empirical or theoretical justification to assign weights to specific domains. For example, expanding background checks to additional provider types may have limited influence if those providers represent a small share of the long-term-care market, and not all disqualifying offenses carry equal relevance to resident safety. Future research is needed to explore developing a more nuanced approach to assessing the regulatory frameworks.
