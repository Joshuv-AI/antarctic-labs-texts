Termites and their gut microbiome in animal nutrition: Advances and biotechnological applications
Authors: Avila-Valbuena Samuel David, Pérez-Rubio María del Rocío
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Since the late 20th century, termites have attracted attention due to the vast potential of their gut microbiome and digestive enzymes, which enable them to efficiently degrade lignocellulosic biomass, making them a promising resource for animal nutrition, particularly for recovering fibrous waste. Termite gastrointestinal symbionts and enzymes are highly effective at decomposing plant fiber, thus positioning them as natural bioreactors with significant biotechnological potential. This review examines the evolving applications of termites in animal feed, including their incorporation as protein sources in diets for monogastric animals and fish, as well as the utilization of bacteria, fungi, and enzymes derived from their guts as additives to enhance the digestibility of agricultural byproducts in both ruminants and non-ruminants. Furthermore, recent developments have demonstrated the identification and heterologous expression of lignocellulolytic enzymes and metabolites with prebiotic and detoxifying properties. The diversity of termite species offers an exceptional source of microbial communities. These communities highly adaptable to various fibrous substrates due to their diet, which enhances their potential despite existing limitations in cultivation and process standardization. However, their gut microbiota remains an untapped resource with immense potential to improve feed efficiency, promote sustainability, and reduce reliance on conventional inputs. It is concluded that at larger scale in vivo studies are needed to fully realize the potential of these symbiotic systems in animal nutrition.

Introduction

Termites and their intestinal symbionts have gained significant attention in biotechnology, particularly due to their metabolites and genes that show potential for industrial applications, especially in the bioconversion of lignocellulose (Matsui et al., 2009; Varm et al., 1994). Currently, biotechnology applied to termites is divided into two specific categories, one focused on pest control, and the other mediated by termites for use in various applications at an industrial level (Scharf, 2015), the latter, mainly in the biofuels, textile, and bioremediation industry (Ali et al., 2021a,b). Although animal science has only recently begun to explore the benefits of termites and their microbiota, interest in this area is rapidly growing. This emerging focus is opening new avenues for application (Table 1). Initially, termites were included as a raw material in poultry and fish feed. More recently, research has shifted toward leveraging their microbiome for the degradation of fibrous substrates and the enhancement of non-starch polysaccharide utilization (Azizi et al., 2020; Mafa et al., 2021). Role of termites in animal feed and extrapolable applications.

These insects are xylophagous with important functions in terrestrial ecosystems, and are dominant in tropical regions (Engel et al., 2009; Govorushko, 2018). They possess a harmonious microbiome that allows them to take advantage of fiber compounds (Marynowska et al., 2020), and are recognized as efficient insects for degrading these compounds (Lazuka et al., 2015, 2018). There are currently 2977 known living termites species (Constantino, 2021), divided into two large groups, upper and lower, based on the absence or presence of flagellated protozoan symbionts (Moreira et al., 2021). Higher termites, which lack these protozoa, represent the most diverse group, accounting for more than 70% of species richness and comprising seven subfamilies (Eggleton, 2011; Engel et al., 2009; König et al., 2013).

These insects harbor a microbiome that is capable of efficiently degrading lignocellulosic biomass (Bhujbal et al., 2021). Lignocellulosic biomass is globally regarded as the most abundant yet underutilized renewable resource, largely due to its complex composition, which limits its direct application in animal feed (Chandel and Singh, 2011). Nevertheless, biotechnology has explored the use of this biomass across different areas of study (Galbe and Wallberg, 2019), and in animal feed, it has been investigated as a cost-effective alternative ingredient for certain species, with the aim of reducing feed expenses (Getabalew and Alemneh, 2019; Woyengo et al., 2014).

As previously mentioned, animal science has proposed several methods for utilizing termites over time. More recently new approaches have emerged to identify target microorganisms and express heterologous enzymes, thereby facilitating the use of these symbiotic microorganisms. According to Azizi et al. (2020), a significant number of termite species harbor microorganisms that could be valuable, necessitating further research to successfully integrate them into animal feed. The lack of in-depth knowledge on the potential of these insects within the field of animal science necessitates a present review focused on the various approaches termites have received in animal feed and the identification of new application opportunities, along with a brief overview of how termites break down lignocellulose.

Termites, microbiome, and fiber digestion

Research has shown that the breakdown of lignocellulose by termites is caused by intestinal symbionts, as well as a synergistic effect between enzymes produced by the termites themselves and, in certain instances, external microorganisms present in the nest (Moreira et al., 2021). For many years, termites have been known to contribute to the deterioration of buildings and have had a negative impact on the environment (Govorushko, 2018). However, as noted by Jouquet et al. (2011), today their role in the environment is widely acknowledged, and they are regarded as designers of tropical and subtropical ecosystems (Jouquet et al., 2011). Furthermore, they have been a highly valuable resource for biotechnologies, particularly through modeling for industrial uses (Scharf, 2015).

These living organisms display a wide variety of species, distinguished by their unique symbiotic relationships, and specific structural and functional changes to the intestinal tract (Eggleton, 2011; Moreira et al., 2021). It is anticipated that these adaptations will enable the identification of beneficial genetic resources, including genes that code for enzymes or chemical substances with antimicrobial properties (Matsui et al., 2009). Studies suggest that termites break down 74% to 99% of cellulose and 65% to 87% of hemicellulose (Ohkuma, 2003). Furthermore, they can also decompose a substantial part of the lignin (Suman et al., 2016); consequently, xylophagous termites represent a promising resource for the efficient breakdown of lignocellulosic biomass (Bhujbal et al., 2021).

The termite microbiome consists of bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes, each with distinct functions, and is regarded as the primary entity involved in the utilization of lignocellulose (Wong et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2019). The microbial diversity in the intestinal compartments is influenced by eating habits, species, and micro-environmental conditions specific to each (Huang et al., 2016; Mikaelyan et al., 2017). In a study of nine species of higher termites, it was shown that the microbiome is mainly composed of Firmicutes, Spirochaetes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria (Mikaelyan et al., 2017). Auer et al. (2017) reported that the microbiome of Microcerotermes parvus, Nasutitermes ephratae, and Nasutitermes lujae is composed of approximately 55% spirochetes, followed by fibrobacters. Similarly, in lower termites, the most representative members are spirochetes, both in abundance and species richness, representing up to 50% of all prokaryotes (Brune, 2014).

These insects have an intestinal system that is divided into three distinct compartments: the foregut, midgut, and hindgut (Ni and Tokuda, 2013). Lignocellulose degradation in each compartment progresses through a dual enzyme system, facilitating its breakdown (Bhujbal et al., 2021). In lower termites, this process involves two types of enzymes from their salivary glands: endoglucanases and glucosidases, as well as exoglucanases, β-glucanases, and hemicellulases from flagellate protist symbionts (Bhujbal et al., 2021; Ni and Tokuda, 2013). In contrast, higher termites obtain the majority of their enzymes from the epithelium of the midgut, which secretes endo-β-1,4-glucanases and β-glucosidases, along with a diverse array of enzymes from the intestinal symbionts in the hindgut, including exoglucanase, β-glucosidase, xylanase, lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and laccase (Bhujbal et al., 2021; Brune, 2014). These enzymes are considered putative in some studies (Liu et al., 2019).

With the exception of termites that exhibit mutualism with the fungus Termitomyces, a means of adaptation and evolution for termites to degrade lignocellulose (Chouvenc et al., 2021), digestion of fiber in termites begins with the reduction of particle size through chewing and muscle gizzard activity. In lower termites, this process reduces the particles to sizes of 10 to 20 μm (Hongoh, 2011), whereas in higher termites, the sizes vary between 10 to 30 μm and 100 to 300 μm (König et al., 2013). This mechanical process, along with enzymatic activity, maximizes the degradation of lignocellulose (Talia and Arneodo, 2018).

The degradation of lignocellulose is a process carried out by endogenous mechanisms and exogenous enzymes. The first step in the degradation of lignocellulose is hydrolysis, followed by the fermentative/oxidative stage and, finally, the methanogenesis/acetogenesis stage (König et al., 2013). In lower termites, the fermentation process is carried out by protozoa, which, after phagocytosing and hydrolyzing the lignocellulose particles, produce short-chain fatty acids, H2, and CO2, with acetate being the main source of energy (Brune and Friedrich, 2000; Brune, 2014). This process also occurs in higher termites, which produce the same compounds. After this stage, the resulting molecules accumulate in the intestinal fluid of the posterior portion and are reabsorbed. In the case of H2, some of it is excreted, and some is fermented during the production of short-chain fatty acids (Brune, 2014).

In acetogenesis, the reduction of H2 occurs and CO2 generating energy in the process, carried out by microorganisms belonging to the Treopnema spp. Finally, methanogenesis in termites is carried out by microorganisms of the order Methanobacterials in lower termites and Methanomicrobials, Methanosarcinales, and Methanoplasmatals in higher termites (Brune, 2014). Lignin, the main structural constraint in lignocellulose, is a topic that has been debated for several years. Initially, degradation was considered to be negligible to almost nonexistent, and it is now reported that this is partially broken down during its passage through the digestive system, specifically the aromatic units ((Tarmadi et al., 2018). However, it was demonstrated through multidimensional nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) that lignin presents minimal modifications, with the elimination of aromatic syringyl units in hardwood, and unacylated guaicil units, and tricin units in grasses being the main changes in termites Coptotermes formosanus (Tarmadi et al., 2018).

Microbiome as a biotechnological resource

Each known termite species provides a diverse source of bacteria that can be targeted for specific purposes in animal feed, justifying further research in this area (Azizi et al., 2020). Marynowska et al. (2020) concluded that only 0.18% of gene transcripts are shared between the different gut microbiomes of the termites, making this a unique reservoir of genes encoding enzymes with potential for biomass degradation. Termites represent an immeasurable source of enzymes with the potential to be utilized for this purpose. In particular, lignolytic enzymes play a key role in the bioconversion of lignocellulosic biomass. Given the number of species already mentioned, each species hosts a unique and specific microbial community based on its feeding behaviors (Tsegaye, 2019).

On the other hand, some authors consider diet to be one of the main determining factors in the structure of the termite microbiome. This could enable the modulation of the microbiome structure for the benefit of animal feed, focusing on genes of interest and specific substrates, essentially “training” the microbiome with targeted objectives. Additionally, Romero et al. (2020), recently confirmed that in higher termites, the microbiome of Nasutitermes aquilinus, was enriched with genes related to carbohydrate metabolism and cellulose degradation. In the case of Cortaritermes fulviceps, an enrichment of genes associated with oligosaccharides degradation was observed, linking the diet to genes of interest in the microbiome.

Termites in ruminant feeding

Published research utilizing termite microorganisms as a biotechnological resource often aims to capitalize on locally available harvest by-products (Azizi-Shotorkhoft et al., 2016; Purwadari et al., 2003). Abasiekong (1997) can be considered one of the pioneers in recognizing the potential of termites for animal feed applications. His initial proposal involved processing crop by-products with termites to enhance their nutritional quality. In 2016, Azizi-Shotorkhoft et al. highlighted termite-derived microorganisms as a promising tool for the pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass intended for ruminant feed. Through identification, culture, and subsequent analyses, the bacteria from the intestinal tract of Microcerotermes diversus were found to influence the chemical composition of wheat straw and date palm leaves, improving nutrient digestibility and nutritional quality of agricultural by-products. Furthermore, Bacillus licheniformis was shown to significantly reduce lignin content in wheat straw, thereby improving the digestibility of dry matter (DM), organic matter (OM), and neutral detergent fiber (NDF) in date palm leaves.

Similarly, in 2018, Azizi et al. demonstrated that the treatment of wheat straw and date palm leaves with bovine rumen inocula, along with the application of three symbiotic termite bacteria (B. licheniformis, Ochrobactrum intermedium and Microbacterium paludicola), represented a promising approach for enhancing the value of lignocellulosic residues. In their study, all the bacterial strains evaluated induced changes in chemical composition, gas production, and in vitro fermentation behavior in both substrates compared to the control group. Regarding fiber composition, NDF was the only parameter affected in wheat straw treated with B. licheniformis, showing a reduction, while no significant changes were observed in acid detergent lignin (ADL) or acid detergent fiber (ADF). Similarly, B. licheniformis led to a greater reduction in ADF in date palm leaves. In terms of in vitro fermentation behavior, the palm substrate treated with O. intermedium exhibited the highest gas production, while none of the treatments significantly altered the total production of volatile fatty acids (VFAs). However, all treatments resulted in a significant reduction in NDF loss compared to the control group.

In 2013, Lara and Acosta identified a bacterium from the intestinal tract of Nasutitermes nigriceps termites, which demonstrated probiotic potential for ruminant feed. This study was based on the experience of cattle ranchers in the Sucre region of Colombia, who provided termite mounds from trees to cattle, observing improvements in weight gain in the animals. After conducting a series of in vitro evaluations, including analysis of tolerance to different temperatures and concentrations of bile salts and sodium chloride, as well as responses to various pH values and bacterial antagonism tests, the authors determined that Enterobacter aerogenes exhibited the ability to withstand complex environmental conditions while maintaining growth without inhibiting rumen microorganisms. Additionally, they highlighted its cellulolytic capacity in vitro in a Pennisetum sp. substrate, where it adapted to the rumen and improved the digestion of this substrate.

However, as concluded by Azizi et al. (2020), the transfer of bacteria (B. licheniformis, O. intermedium, and M. paludicola) from M. diversus termites to rumen fluid extracted from sheep did not affect fermentation parameters, volatile fatty acid production, or fiber degradation. They clarified that the death of the microorganisms likely occurred before they could benefit the rumen fermentation process. Nevertheless, the inoculation of these microorganisms into rumen fluid resulted in increased protease activity and ammonia concentration in vitro, due to an elevated population of proteolytic microorganisms.

Termites in monogastric feeding

In monogastric animals, such as birds and fish, the supply of termites has been reported. However, there is no available data on the use of termites in pigs or other non-ruminant animals. In 1997, Hardouin and Thys suggested the use of termites in “mini-élevage" or minilivestock as unconventional animals, and proposed experiments on the biotransformation of plant material into animal protein using termites and worms. They also suggested raising these animals for distribution as feed for pigs, poultry, and growing fish.

Current data show that termites are used as a raw material in diets or as a substitute for other nutrient sources. Henry et al. (2015), found that the amino acid profile of termites is generally suitable for fish feeding, but some species, such as Macrotermes bellicosus, have an inadequate amino acid profile. However, termites from the same genus have a high protein content (37% to 49% crude protein), a high lipid content (30%), and a low mineral content. This leads to varying animal performance (Henry et al., 2015). Ncobela and Chimonyo (2015) also reported that termite meal contains 964 g/kg of dry matter, 301 g/kg of ether extract, 463 g/kg of crude protein, with significant levels of essential amino acids, varying according to the termite caste used (Ntukutoh et al., 2012).

Musa et al. (2004) conducted an experiment where 90-d-old Japanese quail chicks were fed diets that replaced fishmeal with termites roasted at 60 °C using dry air. The diets were evaluated over a period of three weeks based on total consumption, weight gain, and feed conversion. The results showed that substituting fishmeal with termites had no significant effect on weight gain and feed conversion. Furthermore, the weekly consumption of the chicks remained relatively similar (Musa et al., 2004). Similarly, Pousga et al. (2019) reported a study with M. bellicosus fresh and Macrotermes subhyalinus dried. The results showed that over a period of eight weeks, there were no significant differences between the termite-based diet and the control diet in terms of daily feed intake, weight gain, and carcass characteristics and quality. This suggests that both fresh and dried termites can be a suitable alternative to fishmeal for feeding chickens and guinea fowl. It was also reported that M. subhyalinus had a higher protein content, at 43.5%, compared to M. bellicosus, which had a protein content of 37.1% (Pousga et al., 2019).

On the other hand, Ketaren et al. (2001) reported that including 1.50% dry termites in broiler diets mixed with rice bran resulted in better growth rates and carcass weights. However, the poultry carcasses showed increased abdominal fat deposits and heavier weights for gizzard and heart. In contrast, Uhi et al. (2001) reported that adding 1.5% fresh termites to poultry diets over a 50-d period significantly improved feed conversion. This is different from the findings of Ketaren et al. (2001), who discovered that the best conversion was achieved with an inclusion level of 0.75% dry termites. This could indicate that termite pretreatment (fresh or dry) is a critical factor influencing animal performance.

Recently, Tsukahara et al. (2022) evaluated the use of increasing levels of freeze-dried meal from the lower termite Hodotermopsis sjostedti as a feed substitute for broiler chickens (0, 3.3%, 6.7%, and 10.0%, 8 days old). The study evaluated the effect of these levels on weight gain, feed intake, and physiological parameters, including liver and muscle weight, and plasma calcium, over a 3 days period. The chickens were slaughtered at 11 days of age. The results showed no significant differences or effects on the indicators evaluated, although improved intake and weight gain were observed with the inclusion of 6.7% termite meal, indicating that termite meal can be used as an alternative protein source in chicken feed at levels below 10%, to avoid adverse effects.

In fish feeding, Sogbesan and Ugwumba (2008) conducted an experiment using termite meal (M. subhyalinus) in diets for clay catfish (Heterobranchus longifilis), with an average weight of 1.98 ± 0.08 g, evaluating weight gain, specific growth rate, survival rate, and feed conversion, among other parameters. The termites were dried at 80 °C for 3 h and ground for later use. The meal was used to replace fishmeal in different proportions (0, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%), with the 50% inclusion rate resulting in the highest relative growth rate and the lowest feed conversion ratio. This finding suggests that termite meal could be proposed as a partial economic substitute for fishmeal for local producers.

On the other hand, despite being considered as a potential source of animal protein, the production of termites' biomass under natural conditions has significant limitations. The composition of termites' biomass is affected by the stage of their development and the substrate they feed on, as well as environmental factors (FAO, 2021). Furthermore, in terms of nutrient quality, Macrotermes nigeriensis has been found to have a lower protein quality, although with better digestibility compared to crickets, grasshoppers, and moth caterpillar (Oibiokpa et al., 2018). In the semi-arid region of northeastern Brazil, winged termites were nutritionally evaluated. The researchers found that these termites yielded approximately 2.3 kg of protein and 2.7 kg of fat per hectare per year, which translates to 164.7 MJ per hectare. It is evident that these yields are considerably lower compared to other conventional animal protein sources. Therefore, for their large-scale implementation in animal feed, it is essential to develop cultivation or collection systems that substantially improve their productivity (Silva et al., 2021).

Likewise, studies in Africa and Latin America have shown that termites are rich in protein, essential fatty acids, and various micronutrients. e.g., M. bellicosus has a protein content of up to 39.74%, while M. subhyalinus contains between 33.51% and 52.74% protein, along with significant amounts of linoleic acid and other essential fatty acids. M. subhyalinus also has high levels of iron (11.76 mg/100 g) and zinc (13.18 mg/100 g), suggesting its potential to help address micronutrient deficiencies (Kinyuru et al., 2013; Séré et al., 2022). Similarly, Syntermes aculeosus, which is consumed by indigenous communities in Venezuela, contains approximately 64% protein, along with essential amino acids like tryptophan, and minerals such as iron and calcium, highlighting its nutritional value (Paoletti et al., 2003).

Regarding harvesting challenges, some authors have reported on methodologies in some African regions. Traditional practices involve collecting winged termites, such as Hodotermes mossambicus, during specific seasons. These termites are used not only for human consumption but also as animal feed, including for pigs, chickens, guinea fowls, and fish. The annual production of up to 50 kg per termite mound (termiter exchanged for termite mound) is reported. In Kenya, advancements have been made by combining traditional knowledge with modern technologies, such as the use of light traps, to enhance the mass collection of M. subhyalinus, with the goal of industrial-scale harvesting (Ayieko et al., 2011; Van Huis et al., 2013). Similarly, in Congo and Southeast Asia, termite farming strategies have been developed, allowing for harvests within 3 to 4 weeks. Additionally, the use of fluorescent lights over fish ponds has been implemented to attract winged insects, which then fall into the water and are consumed by the fish, serving as a natural food source (Van Huis et al., 2013; Van Huis, 2020).

Finally, the biological risks associated with termites can be controlled through good handling and hygiene practices at each stage of production, including rearing, harvesting, processing, storage, and transportation of the insects and their by-products. In fact, as noted by Niassy et al., in 2018, there is still a need to prioritize the analysis and standardization of termites as a source of nutrients in animal feed, as is the case in Africa.

Termites as a source of metabolites and additives

Due to the chemical composition of crop residues, their use in animal feed is limited, requiring pre-treatments to increase their utilization rate. The factors that restrict their use in animal feed are similar to those that limit their application in the biofuels industry (Donkin et al., 2013). On the other hand, enzymes have become a tool to increase nutrient availability, reduce feed costs, mitigate waste to the environment, and contribute to animal health (Bedford and Masey, 2018; Bonneau and Laarveld, 1999). Furthermore, termite enzymes have been characterized and proposed for the detoxification of raw materials used in animal feed (Lima et al., 2020).

Recently, Arevalos-Sánchez et al. (2021) discovered two new cellulases in the bacteria Trabulsiella odontermitis, which are found in the intestinal tract of Heterotermes spp. The researchers found that these enzymes have significant potential as feed additives, as they can release high concentrations of glucose from crop residues. This could lead to improved cellulose degradation in fibrous substrates, making corn and sorghum by-products more digestible and allowing for a greater inclusion of these by-products.

In a recent study, Mafa et al. (2021) identified a feruloyl esterase in termites Trinervitermes trinervoides, that has an additive effect with xylanase (GH10). This enzyme has significant potential to break down non-starch polysaccharides in animal diets. When the two enzymes were combined, a greater amount of reducing sugar was released, with a ratio of 75% xylanase (XT6) to 25% feruloyl esterase, resulting in a 1.23 times increase in sugar release. Furthermore, this combination improved the degradation of arabinoxylans in wheat (Mafa et al., 2021). Likewise, other authors have stated that the enzymatic conversion of corn cobs into xylooligosaccharides and hydroxycinnamic acid, is improved through the synergy of feruloyl esterase enzymes from the intestinal tract of termites T. trinervoides and xylanase. This synergy optimizes the release of reducing sugars, thereby enhancing the production of xylooligosaccharides at an industrial level, whether the substrates undergo chemical or hydrothermal pretreatment (Mkabayi et al., 2020).

Bai et al. (2016) previously identified an enzyme endo-1,4-β-glucanase that has potential applications in the feed and silage industry. This enzyme was isolated from the bacterium Citrobacter farmeri A1, which was found in the termite intestine Reticulitermes labralis. Notably, the enzyme is adaptable to cold temperatures, which allows for reduced energy consumption, slower chemical reactions, and protection of thermolabile substrates. In addition to its cold adaptability, the enzyme also exhibits thermostability, retaining its residual activity up to 60 °C. These characteristics, combined with other benefits, contribute to increased efficiency in catalytic processes, making the enzyme suitable for various industrial applications, including the production of silages.

The mannanase have been identified, studied, and proposed for use in animal feed to improve the quality of raw materials. Mairizal et al. (2018) identified a considerably high activity of Bacillus cereus in the gut of termite Microtermes spp. This enzyme can hydrolyze mannans from raw materials such as palm kernel flour, which is of limited use in monogastric feed. Of the eight isolates, B. cereus began to show an increase in its activity considerably at 8 h post-incubation, decreasing at 96 h, with an optimal pH of 8 and a temperature ranging between 30 and 50 °C, being optimal in its upper range.

In the study and characterization of enzymes, the heterologous expression of these metabolites has made it possible to characterize and study metabolites with biotechnological potential. An enzyme of interest, apart from carbohydrases, is the lignin-depolymerizing enzyme due to the role of lignin as a major barrier in the utilization of fibrous raw materials. Recently, Xiao et al. (2021) identified a laccase enzyme with promising industrial applications for bio-delignification. Similarly, Suman et al. (2016) identified a bacterium, Trabulsiella guaminensis, with the potential to degrade lignin. In their study, the authors used a synthetic substrate model of lignin (guaiacylglycerol-β-guaicyl ether), achieving a degradation of 62% at a temperature of 35 °C and pH 7, with the ability to maintain its activity for 25 d, showing potential for the depolymerization of lignin in lignocellulosic materials for subsequent use (Suman et al., 2016).

Another type of additive gaining significant interest in animal feed is prebiotics (Anadón et al., 2019). One emerging approach involves producing these compounds from agricultural residues by harnessing metabolites produced by microorganisms. Ratnadewi et al., 2016a, Ratnadewi et al., 2016b developed a methodology to obtain xylooligosaccharides from cassava peel, cassava crop residues, and soybean waste through chemical pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis with an endoxylanase from soil termites. The results showed that xylopentose was the dominant product for both substrates, followed by xylotetralose. However, further research is needed to determine the optimal conditions of pH, temperature, and incubation time in order to achieve the maximum yield (Ratnadewi et al., 2016b). In a separate study, using endo-1,4-D-β-xylanase from soil termites, similar results were obtained, with xylopentose being the major compound, followed by xylobiose and xylose (Ratnadewi et al., 2016a).

In addition, regarding the direct application of microorganisms, Lee et al. (2015) found that several anaerobic fungi have significant potential in rice straw silage. Specifically, the fungus Neocallimastix M010, isolated from the gut of a termite, demonstrated considerable potential for degrading dry matter and improving digestibility, compared to fungi isolated from goats and cattle. To evaluate this potential, the researchers used the nylon bag technique to assess dry matter loss in 5 g samples of rice straw silage over 72 h in the rumen. After 30 d of ensiling, the results showed that the termite-derived fungus caused a greater effective degradation of rice straw, with a degradation rate of 25.19%, compared to other treatments and the control group. This difference was especially evident after 48 h of in situ incubation. Furthermore, in terms of the ensiling process, the termite-derived fungus helped maintain a good pH level in the rice straw, with a noticeable effect after 30 d of ensiling.

On the other hand, Opazo et al. (2012) identified that the fermentation of soybean meal in solid state with cellulolytic bacteria isolated from different environments (Streptomyces pseudovenezuelae of bovine rumen content, Cohnella termotolerans of corn silage, and Cellulosimicrobium sp. of termites) led to a significant reduction in non-starch polysaccharides (23.6%), stachyose (88%), and raffinose (76%), as well as an increase in protein levels (57% vs. 51% of the non-inoculated group). Termite isolate Cellulosimicrobium sp. presented one of the highest levels of β-xylanase activity (>400 μmol of p-nitrophenol release/min per mL), and together with the isolate of corn silage, they stood out in the fermentation process of soybean meal. This technique presents a viable option that requires testing in vivo and large-scale production to increase the use of soybean meal in animal diets, including aquaculture.

Additionally, Molnar and collaborators identified a new yeast from the intestinal tract of the termite Mastotermes darwiniensis with the ability to detoxify mycotoxins. Under laboratory conditions, this yeast can inactivate 400 μg/L of ochratoxin A in 2.5 h, 1 mg/L of zearalenone after 24 h of incubation, degrading them to carbon dioxide without producing detectable metabolites such as α-zearalenol or β-zearalenol (Molnar et al., 2004). The use of yeasts from the same genus has previously shown promising results in poultry, including reduced mortality and improved performance of broilers challenged with ochratoxin (Schatzmayr et al., 2003). Based on these findings, Molnar et al. (2004) suggested that termite derived yeast strains would be useful in deactivating mycotoxins in animal feed.

Purwadari and his team proposed the use of lower termites Glyptotermes montanus, as a source of hydrolytic enzymes to take advantage of fibrous by-products (rice bran, coarse wheat bran, palm kernel cake, and dry oil mill effluent) in poultry diets (Purwadari et al., 2003). However, the authors noted that enzyme activity was low, limiting its use and requiring a high number of individuals to achieve significant enzyme activity. In the study, they used fresh and dried worker-caste termites as a source of enzymes and identified the activities of β-D-1,4-mannanase, β-D-1,4-xylanase, β-D-1,4-glucosidase, and avicelase, with fresh termites showing the highest activity. In in vitro incubation with the substrates, rice bran showed the highest hydrolytic activity by termite extract.

Recent research by Liu et al. (2023) investigated the ability of Bacillus subtilis RLI2019 to degrade wheat straw. This strain, which is predominant in the lower termite gut of R. labralis, exhibited strong enzymatic activity in the breakdown of lignocellulose. The level of enzymatic activity was measured as follows: 4.06, 1.97, 4.12, 0.74, and 17.61 units/mL for endoglucanase, β-glucosidase, phosphoric acid swollen cellulose (PASC) enzyme, filter paper enzyme, and xylanases, respectively. To gain a deeper understanding of the genetic mechanisms involved in cellulose degradation, the whole genome of B. subtilis RLI2019 was sequenced. The genomic analysis revealed a high probability (99.41%) of using this strain as a probiotic. The 145 genes of B. subtilis RLI2019 were classified into six families of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZyme) and 57 subfamilies. It was found that eight of these genes are related to enzymes involved in cellulose metabolism and nine genes are related to hemicellulose. Fermentation of wheat straw with B. subtilis RLI2019 for seven days resulted in significant reductions in the content of NDF, ADF, hemicellulose, and acid ADL by 5.8%, 10.3%, 1.0%, and 4.7%, respectively. The fermentation process also led to the production of 664.9 μg/mL of reducing sugars. Furthermore, the fiber structure was effectively disrupted, and the crystallinity of cellulose was significantly reduced from 40.2% to 36.9%.

Finally, at the same time, Zhang et al. (2023) collected samples from the gut of the lower termite C. formosanus and isolated bacteria that can break down cellulose. Among these bacteria, they selected a colony of Bacillus velezensis BV-10, which could potentially be used as an additive in silages. The researchers conducted silage tests on King Grass with various additives and B. velezensis BV-10, evaluating the effect on silage quality after 30 d of fermentation. The results showed that this bacterium had a positive effect, rapidly lowering the pH and significantly reducing the ADF content (P < 0.05). The combination of B. velezensis BV-10 with molasses and cellulase improved organic acid production and promoted the reduction of NDF and ADF. This study confirmed that B. velezensis BV-10 improves silage quality, and its activity is more effective when combined with other additives.

Conclusion

Utilizing the biological activity of microbial communities found in termite intestines and their capacity to break down fibrous materials is a biotechnological approach for incorporating lignocellulosic agricultural waste into animal feed.

Fibrous by-products have been enhanced nutritionally for animal feed by utilizing termites, in addition to serving as raw material in rations for chickens and fish, or as a source of enzymes for alternative feed options; this has led to the demonstration of their effectiveness and feasibility in novel strategies for animal nutrition.

The feasibility of termite production can be attributed to their low need for arable land and the abundance of lignocellulosic biomass available. The meal is a reliable supply of protein, with a comparable amino acid profile to that of fishmeal. Termite production is hindered by two significant factors: the complexity of its cultivation process and the direct contribution of methane emission, both of which restrict its practical application.

Using lignocellulosic biomass in combination with specific metabolites, including multimeric enzymes like β-glucosidase, β-xylosidase, and α-arabinosidase, derived from microorganisms associated with termites, offers substantial potential to boost efficiency and sustainability in animal feeds, promote the use of alternative feed ingredients, and enhance their nutritional value. In this sense, the considerable number of identified termites constitutes a potential source of microorganisms with specific applications for animal feed. Additional research is necessary to confirm that these microorganisms can effectively conduct biological pre-treatment of lignocellulosic biomass for feed purposes.

The termite microbiome comprises microorganisms that have the potential to be utilised in bioprospecting and the development of methods to harness lignocellulosic biomass, but to date, research has focused on laboratory studies of microorganisms and their metabolites, necessitating further investigation into large-scale applications and the effects of these microorganisms in vivo.
