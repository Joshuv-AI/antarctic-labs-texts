Characterisation of pullulan polysaccharide membranes cross-linked with epigallocatechin gallate and Fe3+ for passion fruit preservation☆
Authors: Jiale Zhao, Guanglong Yao, Jian Chen, Lin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Traditional packaging materials can cause serious environmental pollution, prompting increased interest in the development of green bioplastic packaging alternatives. Herein, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) and Fe3+ cross-linked pullulan polysaccharide (PUL) composite films were prepared using the solution casting method. Their structures and properties were measured. The application of PUL composite coatings in passion fruit preservation was also investigated. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction analyses confirmed strong biocompatibility and interaction among PUL, EGCG and Fe3+. Scanning electron microscopy results further demonstrated good compatibility between the polymer and both EGCG and Fe3+. The metal-phenol-aldehyde network structure formed by EGCG and Fe3+ crosslinks with the PUL film, enhancing its structural density and significantly reducing water vapor permeability by 15.24 %. Additionally, the mechanical properties of the composite film were enhanced, with tensile strength increasing from 14.36 MPa to 27.68 MPa, 92.76 % improvement over pure PUL film. Concurrently, the incorporation of EGCG and Fe3+ substantially boosted the PUL film's UV barrier properties and antioxidant capacity. In passion fruit preservation experiments, PUL/EGCG/Fe3+-coated passion fruit exhibited the lowest weight loss rate (19.60 %) and shrinking index (3.3) after 10 days of storage compared to other treatment groups. This effectively delayed fruit quality deterioration and maintained appearance.

Introduction

As global concern for environmental and food safety increases, the environmental impact of traditional packaging materials is becoming more evident. Traditional polymer packaging materials, such as polypropylene and polyethylene, have caused serious environmental pollution owing to their poor biodegradability, particularly with long-term impacts on marine ecosystems and soil (Bu et al., 2023). Since plastics entered the market in the 1950s, global plastic production has grown exponentially, reaching 359 million tons worldwide in 2018(Wayman & Niemann, 2021). However, global plastic waste management faces immense challenges. Only about 9 % of plastics are recycled, while nearly 80 % are discarded, landfilled, or abandoned in the environment. This results in approximately 4 to 12 million metric tons of plastic waste entering the oceans annually, posing long-term threats to ecosystems and human health (Reis et al., 2019). This reality starkly contrasts with the requirements of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) concerning sustainable consumption and production patterns (SDG 12), climate action (SDG 13), and protection of marine ecosystems (SDG 14) (Manzoor et al., 2024). Plastic pollution remains a major obstacle to achieving these goals, with its negative impacts on terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems exacerbating climate change and threatening biodiversity. Food packaging systems sit at the intersection of these challenges, affecting resource consumption, waste generation (SDG 12), and food loss (SDG 2) (Rajic et al., 2022).Therefore, to promote sustainable development aligned with Industry 4.0 to Industry 6.0, developing biodegradable green packaging materials has become an urgent global research priority (Abedi-Firoozjah, Ahmadi, et al., 2025).

Against this backdrop, biomass-based degradable functional food packaging films are regarded as one of the most promising alternatives. Such films not only fundamentally address plastic pollution but also extend food shelf life through their unique functional properties (Abedi-Firoozjah, Bahramian, et al., 2025). Activated food packaging film is a thin layer of edible material that effectively protects food from the effects of atmospheric water vapor, oxygen and carbon dioxide, thereby extending the shelf life of food products. Such packaging films are typically synthesised from plant- and animal-derived polymers, including polysaccharides and proteins (Guo et al., 2025; Sun, Zhang, Yin, et al., 2025). Among these, polysaccharide-based films are particularly attractive because of their excellent barrier properties, which protect food from environmental factors and prevent spoilage. In addition, polysaccharides can be blended with bioactive ingredients, such as polyphenols and essential oils, to develop active packaging with antioxidant and antimicrobial properties (Chang et al., 2025).

Pullulan (PUL) is a biodegradable, linear, nonionic polysaccharide produced by a pullulan short-stalked mould. It comprises maltotriose units connected by α-1,6-glycosidic bonds. This unique structure endows PUL with many excellent physicochemical properties, including good water solubility, film-forming ability, biocompatibility and thermal stability, and its production is relatively environmentally friendly, primarily involving fermentation and extraction processes (Yu et al., 2025). Nevertheless, pure PUL films have certain disadvantages, such as high brittleness, which render their mechanical properties insufficient for practical food packaging applications (Chen et al., 2023). Current research frequently employs crosslinking techniques to enhance the physicochemical properties of films. Zhang et al. (2022) demonstrated that incorporating various metal cations into pure pectin films enables direct interaction with polysaccharide chains, thereby improving the water vapor barrier properties and mechanical performance of the composite films. However, the direct crosslinking approach using a single metal ion exhibits limitations in functional diversity.

In recent years, the construction of multifunctional material systems leveraging the synergistic interactions between polyphenols and metal ions has garnered significant attention. This type of metal-polyphenol coordination is readily assembled and substantially enhances the mechanical strength, thermal stability, and functional properties of materials (Lin et al., 2025). Among numerous phenolic ligands, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) is the most abundant and potent monomeric component in tea leaves. It possesses not only remarkable antioxidant capacity but also exhibits promising biological activity in combating diabetes, inhibiting cancer, suppressing angiogenesis, exhibiting antimicrobial effects, and promoting cardiovascular health (Bahramian et al., 2025). Due to its rich phenolic hydroxyl groups, EGCG not only functions as a potent reducing and chelating antioxidant but also forms stable complexes with high-affinity metal centers, such as ferric ions (Fe3+) (Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2021). Previous studies have demonstrated that co-incorporating EGCG with Fe3+ into a sodium alginate matrix significantly enhances the physicochemical and functional properties of the film (Mao et al., 2023). Therefore, combining the biological activity of EGCG with its coordination synergistic effects with Fe3+ holds promise for enhancing the mechanical properties of PUL films while endowing them with superior antioxidant functions, thereby overcoming their inherent limitations. However, to our knowledge, no studies have been reported on systematically enhancing the performance of PUL films by exploiting the synergistic interaction between EGCG and Fe3+.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically investigate the synergistic mechanism by which EGCG and Fe3+ enhance the structure, properties, and freshness-preserving effects of PUL films. Here, we prepared PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ composite films with antioxidant activity using the solution casting method. The structure of the composite films was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray diffraction (XRD). Concurrently, the mechanical properties, water vapor barrier properties, and ultraviolet shielding properties of the composite films were evaluated, and the effect of PUL composite films on the freshness preservation of passion fruit was investigated.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pullulan (PUL) was purchased from Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG, ≥99 %) was purchased from Shanghai Bide Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd., anhydrous ferric chloride was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., and passion fruit was sourced from the North-South Fruit Market in Haikou City, Hainan Province.

Preparation of composite films

PUL composite films were prepared using the solution casting method, referring to the method of Xu et al. (2023) with appropriate modifications. PUL (4 g) was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water, and 20 % (w/w) of glycerol was added. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 h using a magnetic stirrer to obtain a 4 % PUL solution. Similarly, 0 %、1 %、3 %、5 %、7 %、9 % EGCG (w/w) and 0 %、0.05 %、0.10 %、0.15 %、0.20 % ferric chloride (w/w) were separately added to the PUL solution, and the mixture was thoroughly stirred to obtain PUL/Fe3+、PUL/EGCG and PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ solutions. Based on the preliminary performance test (supplementary materials), the optimal addition amounts of EGCG and Fe3+ were finally determined to be 5 % (w/w) and 0.10 % (w/w), respectively, and the subsequent preparation and analysis of the composite film were carried out in this proportion. Each film-forming solution (20 mL per dish) was poured into a standard circular plastic culture dish. The dishes were dried at 50 °C for 10 h and then placed in a container with constant humidity (50 % ± 3 % humidity) and temperature (25 °C ± 1 °C) until they were completely peeled off.

Microstructure of composite films

The microstructure of the films was analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS GeminiSEM 300, Germany) (Yang, Fei, et al., 2023). Film samples were freeze-fractured in liquid nitrogen for cross-sectioning. Subsequently, the samples were sprayed with gold and fixed on a sample stage. SEM observations of the surface and cross-section of thin film samples were conducted at 500 × magnification and an acceleration voltage of 5.0 kV.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

A Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) was used to test the chemical structure changes of the films, which were analyzed in the ATR mode with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and a scanning range of 4000–500 cm−1, and collected data from 32 scans (Sun, Zhang, Yang, et al., 2025).

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The crystal structure of the films was analyzed using XRD (Ultima IV, Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan). The test conditions were: scanning range 2θ = 5° ∼ 60° and scanning speed 5°/min (Y. Zhao et al., 2025).

Color and optical properties of composite film

Carefully peel the completely dried film from the petri dish and place it on a standard white background board. Under uniform indoor lighting conditions, capture the film's appearance using the digital camera of an Honor smartphone (Honor Terminal Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). The color of the film was tested using a precision colorimeter (WF28, Weifu Photoelectric Technology Limited Company, Shenzhen, China) to record the L* (luminance), a* (red/green values), and b* values (yellow/blue values, with a standard white board as the background. Calculate the color difference (ΔE) using the following formula:∆E=L−L02+a−a02+b−b02where L, a and b are the chromaticity elements of the various films and L0, a0 and b0 are the color parameters of a standard whiteboard.

The films were cut into rectangular strips of 10 mm × 30 mm, placed in a cuvette, and the UV–visible transmittance of the composite films was determined using a UV–visible spectrophotometer ((TU-1901, China) in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm (Xu & Zhao, 2024).

Mechanical properties of composite films

The mechanical properties of the films were determined using a texture analyzer (TA; XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems, England). The composite films were cut into strips measuring 4 cm × 1 cm and fixed to the tensile probe of the texture analyzer. The tensile speed was set to 10 mm/min, and the probe slowly stretched the film until it ruptured. Each sample was tested three times (Yang et al., 2025).

Water contact angle (WCA)

The water contact angle of the films was measured using an interfacial tension analyzer (OCA 15EC, DataPhysics, Germany) (Zhu et al., 2023). The film samples were cut to a size of approximately 15 × 15 mm, placed flat on a slide, and pressed gently so that there was no gap between the sample and the slide and the surface was flat. A 3.0 μL water droplet was carefully placed on the surface of each film, and hydrophobicity was assessed by using an optical contact angle system and recording the profile of the droplet with a high-speed video camera.

Water vapor permeability (WVP) and moisture content (MC) of composite films

The water vapor permeability of the film was measured according to previous method (Wang et al., 2023). Film samples were wrapped and fixed in weighing bottles containing anhydrous calcium chloride. Then, all the weighing bottles were placed in a desiccator filled with distilled water, and the mass of the weighing bottles was measured every 2 h. The water vapor transmission rate was calculated as follows. The formula for calculating the water vapor permeability is as follows:WVP = (Δm × d)/(t × S × Δp)where Δm is the weight change of the weighing bottle (g); d is the thickness of the film (mm); t is the interval time (s); S is the area of the mouth of the weighing bottle (m2); and Δp denotes the pressure difference between the inside and outside of the bottle, which is 3167 Pa.

The determination of film moisture content was based on the method described by Travalini et al. (2019) with appropriate modifications. The prepared film samples were cut into squares (3 cm × 3 cm) and weighed and recorded as W0. The samples were then dried in an oven at 105 °C for 12 h and weighed again and recorded as W1. Moisture content was calculated by recording the weight loss of the film samples in triplicate.MC (%) = (W0-W1)/W0 × 100

where W0 and W1 denote the weight of the specimen before and after drying, respectively.

Antioxidant capacity of composite films

The antioxidant capacity of the films was evaluated using DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging assays (Yang et al., 2024). The film sample (50 mg) was dissolved in 5 mL of distilled water and centrifuged for 10 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 3 mL of 1 mM DPPH/ethanol solution, and the mixture was incubated in water at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm. A 7.4 mM ABTS solution was prepared in advance, and an equal volume of 2.6 mM aqueous potassium persulfate solution was prepared as a backup solution. The solutions were mixed in equal volumes and stored in the dark for 16 h. Subsequently, the ABTS reserve solution was diluted with ethanol until absorbance at 734 nm was 0.80. Subsequently, 0.2 mL of the film extract and 1.6 mL of the ABTS solution were mixed well, and the solution was incubated in the dark for 10 min. The absorbance was measured at 734 nm.Free radical scavenging rate (%) = (A0-A1)/A0 × 100

where A1 is the absorbance of the film sample and A0 is the absorbance of anhydrous ethanol under the same conditions.

Passion fruit coating preservation experiment

Passion fruits of similar size, ripeness, and without damage or disease were selected. They were washed thoroughly with tap water and air-dried. All fruits were randomly divided into five groups: a control group (CK, no treatment), a PUL-coated group, a PUL/Fe3+-coated group, a PUL/EGCG-coated group, and a PUL/EGCG/Fe3+-coated group. The fruits in each group were immersed in their respective film-forming solutions for 30 s and then air-dried at room temperature for 3 h. The treated fruits were placed in plastic baskets and stored at room temperature. The appearance of the fruits was monitored and photographed periodically. The quality indicators of passion fruits were measured on days 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 of storage.

Water contact angle of the coating

The contact angle of the coating solutions on the passion fruit peel was measured using an interfacial tension analyzer (OCA 15EC, DataPhysics, Germany) (Zheng et al., 2024). The fruit peel surface was cleaned with distilled water, and a piece was cut and mounted on a glass slide. After setting the injection valve's opening time, a droplet of the coating solution was placed onto three different locations on the peel. The contact angles at 0 s and 30 s were recorded and calculated for each location.

Weight loss rate

The weight loss of passion fruit was determined by weighing and is expressed as a percentage. The weight loss rate was calculated using the following formula:Weight loss rate%=m1−m2m1×100

Where m1 is the initial mass of the passion fruit (g), and m2 is the mass of the passion fruit after being stored for a certain number of days (g).

Shrinkage index

The shrinkage index of passion fruit was determined with appropriate modifications to the method described by Zhou et al. (2022). The shrinkling of the fruit peel was evaluated using a 0–4 scale: 0 (no shrinkage); 1 (slight shrinkage, 0–25 %); 2 (moderate shrinkage, 25–50 %); 3 (severe shrinkage, 50–75 %); 4 (very severe shrinkage, 75–100 %). The shrinkage index was calculated as follows:Shrinkage Index=∑Degree of shrinkage×Number of passion fruitsatthat degreeTotal number of passion fruits.

Color

On days 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 of storage, five fruits were randomly selected from each treatment group. The color parameters (L, a, and b*) of the passion fruit peel were measured using a precision colorimeter (WF28, Weifu Photoelectric Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China).

Total soluble solids (TSS)

The total soluble solids (TSS) content of passion fruit during storage was measured using a digital handheld refractometer (PAL-106, Tianjin Liaowang Optoelectronics Technology Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China).

Total viable counts

The total viable counts of microorganisms on passion fruit were determined according to the Chinese National Standard method GB 4789.2–2022.

Statistical analysis

The data of this experiment are the mean of 3 replicates. The results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. SPSS Statistics 27 software and origin 2021 software were used for statistical analysis and graphing.

Results and discussion

Microscopic morphology

The SEM images of the surface and cross-section of the PUL composite films are shown in Fig. 1 (all SEM images magnified at 500×). The surface (Fig. 1-A) and cross-sectional (Fig. 1-B) SEM images of the films showed that the pure PUL film had relatively smooth and dense surfaces without cracks, which could be attributed to its excellent film-forming properties (Xu et al., 2023). After introducing EGCG or Fe3+ separately, the surfaces of PUL/Fe3+ and PUL/EGCG films remained smooth and dense with no agglomerated particles observed. This indicates the uniform dispersion of EGCG and Fe3+ in the PUL matrix, which may be due to the intermolecular compatibility and hydrogen-bonding interactions between PUL and EGCG and Fe3+ (Zou et al., 2025). The Fig. 1-B displays cross-sectional images of the films. The PUL composite films exhibit a relatively smooth and dense morphology with no noticeable voids or delamination, highlighting the integrity of the film structure. These findings indicate that the synergistic interaction between EGCG and Fe3+ contributes to enhancing the uniformity and stability of the films. This may be attributed to h hydrogen bonding, covalent bonding, and coordination bonding interactions between PUL, EGCG, and Fe3+, demonstrating excellent intermolecular compatibility (as shown in Fig. 1-C) (Bu et al., 2023).Fig. 1SEM images of the surface (A) and cross-section (B) of the PUL composite membrane (magnification was × 500), along with a schematic diagram of intermolecular interactions (C).Fig. 1

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectra of the PUL composite films are shown in Fig. 2-A. All films exhibited broad absorption peaks between 3500 cm−1 and 3000 cm−1, which were attributed to the stretching vibrations of the free hydroxyl and amino groups (P. Zhao et al., 2025). After the incorporation of Fe3+, a slight decrease in intensity was observed within this broad peak region compared to pure PUL films. This suggests that the introduced Fe3+ ions interact with hydroxyl groups in the PUL molecules, reducing the number of free hydroxyl groups participating in the vibration. Zhang et al. (2019) reported similar findings: FeCl3-doped mulberry polysaccharide composites exhibited markedly reduced hydroxyl absorption peak intensity, suggesting Fe3+ primarily interacts with polysaccharide hydroxyl groups during synthesis. The absorption peaks at 2927 cm−1, 1350 cm−1 and 1004 cm−1 corresponded to the C—H, C-O-H and C–O–C stretching vibrations, respectively, confirming the PUL polysaccharide structure (Hu et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024). Notably, the absorption peak at 1648 cm−1 in the PUL/Fe3+ films, originating from C—H stretching vibrations, showed no significant shift, indicating weak direct interactions between Fe3+ and the PUL matrix. However, upon incorporation of EGCG (PUL/Fe3+ film), this absorption peak red-shifted to 1637 cm−1, indicating intermolecular interactions likely involving hydrogen bonding between EGCG and PUL (Jiang et al., 2025). When Fe3+ was further introduced into the system (PUL/EGCG/Fe3+film), the peak exhibited a more pronounced red shift, moving from 1637 cm−1 to 1631 cm−1. This suggests that the addition of Fe3+ further enhanced intermolecular interactions, with Fe3+ potentially coordinating with oxygen atoms on the phenolic hydroxyl groups of EGCG to form a stable metal-phenol-aldehyde network structure (Zhang et al., 2025). Furthermore, compared to the PUL film, no appearance or disappearance of covalent bond characteristic absorption peaks was observed within the FTIR detectable range in the other three composite films. This indicates that the addition of EGCG and Fe3+ does not alter the chemical structure of the PUL film, and the cross-linking between molecules occurs via non-covalent interactions.Fig. 2FTIR spectrum (A) and XRD pattern (B) of the PUL composite membrane.Fig. 2

XRD analysis

Fig. 2-B shows the XRD patterns of the PUL composite films. All the films exhibited a broader diffraction peak 2θ = 18.61°, indicating that they had an amorphous structure with a dense molecular network (Chang, Korma, et al., 2024). Compared to PUL films, the PUL/Fe3+ films showed no significant changes in the position or shape of the diffraction peak at 18.61°. This indicates that the introduction of a small amount of Fe3+ ions did not alter the amorphous structure of the PUL matrix (Jiang et al., 2025). Meanwhile, the peak intensity at 18.61° increased to varying degrees in both PUL/EGCG and PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ films, which may be attributed to intermolecular hydrogen bonding interactions (Mao et al., 2023). Notably, no new diffraction peaks were observed in the PUL composite film, indicating that the incorporation of EGCG and Fe3+ into the PUL matrix does not disrupt the film's structure. This also demonstrates that EGCG and Fe3+ are uniformly dispersed throughout the PUL film, exhibiting excellent biocompatibility among the three components (Jiang et al., 2025). Yang, Cai, et al. (2023)reported similar findings using tannic acid and Fe3+ to cross-link pectin films. The position of the broad peak at 2θ = 20.88° in the pectin film did not change with the addition of tannic acid and Fe3+, only the peak width varied. This indicates that tannic acid and Fe3+ were uniformly dispersed in the PE film, consistent with the results of this work.

Color and optical properties of composite films

The visual appearance of different composite films is shown in Fig. 3-A. Clearly, the pure PUL film is colorless and transparent, while the addition of EGCG and Fe3+ causes a significant color change in the films, indicating the successful incorporation of the composite components. For a quantitative analysis of the color changes, we further measured the color parameters (L*, a*, b*) of the films using a colorimeter, with the results listed in Table 1. The PUL film had the highest L* value (82.58 ± 0.13), the lowest a* value (−1.94 ± 0.03), and a relatively low b* value (−0.64 ± 0.0.9). Upon the addition of Fe3+, the L* value of the PUL/Fe3+ film showed no significant change, while the a* and b* values increased, which is related to the inherent color of Fe3+. Simultaneously, the color indices of the PUL/EGCG film also underwent significant changes, characterized by a decrease in L* value and increases in both a* and b* values. The PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ composite film, incorporating both EGCG and Fe3+, exhibited the lowest L* and b* values and the highest a* value. This composite film appeared purple-black, likely due to EGCG's inherent pale pink hue combined with Fe3+’s black effect, indicating uniform dispersion of both EGCG and Fe3+ throughout the film (Yang, Cai, et al., 2023). The UV barrier property of a film is critical in food packaging as materials with strong UV-blocking ability can prevent lipid oxidation and thereby extend the shelf life of food products. Fig. 3-B shows the spectral transmittance of different films in the UV–visible range (200–800 nm). As shown in Fig.3-B, pure PUL films exhibit relatively high transmittance in the ultraviolet region, particularly in the UV-C and UV-B bands, indicating a weak UV blocking capability. Upon the incorporation of Fe3+, the transmittance of PUL/Fe3+ films decreased in the UV region, demonstrating that Fe3+ enhances the film's UV blocking performance. Compared to PUL/Fe3+ films, PUL/EGCG films exhibit further reduced transmittance in the UV region. This can be attributed to the strong UV absorption of chromophores such as the phenolic hydroxyl groups in EGCG (Quideau et al., 2011). The PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ film exhibited the lowest transmittance in the UV region, particularly in the UV-C and UV-B bands where transmittance fell below 1 %, indicating optimal UV blocking performance. Previous studies have shown that Fe3+ absorption occurs in the near-ultraviolet portion of the electromagnetic spectrum (Hoang-Minh et al., 2010). Thus, the UV-blocking mechanism of the composite film is closely related to Fe3+’s intrinsic absorption of near-ultraviolet light. Mechanical properties of composite films

The data in the supplementary materials determined the optimal addition levels of EGCG and Fe3+ to be 5 % and 0.1 %, respectively, to achieve the best mechanical properties in the composite film.

The Fig.4 and Table 2 show the tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EAB) of the composite film, as well as Young's modulus. For the pure PUL film, the TS, EAB, and Young's modulus were 14.36 ± 1.39 MPa, 4.6 ± 0.45 %, and 387.23 ± 68.46 MPa, respectively. Upon the introduction of Fe3+ alone, the TS of PUL/Fe3+ films significantly increased to 17.95 ± 2.09 MPa, with the Young's modulus concurrently rising to 446.00 ± 6.64 MPa, though EAB slightly decreased (4.28 ± 0.14 %). Following EGCG incorporation, the TS of PUL/EGCG films further increased to 22.74 ± 2.37 MPa, with Young's modulus reaching 624.71 ± 105.64 MPa, while EB decreased to 3.51 ± 0.22. This may be attributed to the formation of coordination bonds by Fe3+ with polar groups such as hydroxyl groups in PUL molecules, while EGCG forms hydrogen bonds with PUL via its phenolic hydroxyl groups. Both interactions enhance intermolecular bonding, resulting in a denser film structure but also restricting molecular chain mobility (Wu, Wang, Ma, et al., 2025). With the simultaneous addition of EGCG and Fe3+, the composite film's TS further increased to 27.67 ± 1.54 MPa and exhibited the highest Young's modulus (1104.26 ± 115.31 MPa), while EAB decreased to 3.81 ± 0.11 %. Though no significant difference was observed compared to PUL/Fe3+ and PUL/EGCG films, indicating that the film's rigidity was significantly enhanced by intermolecular interactions. The reason may be that EGCG forms a tight metal-phenol network structure with Fe3+ and crosslinks with PUL molecules. This structure not only strengthens intermolecular interactions but also enhances the mechanical properties of the composite film (Zhao et al., 2022). Compared with other biopolymer films for food packaging, the PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ film exhibited significantly higher TS than branched starch-gelatin films loaded with clove essential oil via nanoemulsion and Pickering emulsion (6.28 ± 0.25 MPa) (Shen et al., 2021), chitosan/gallic acid/Fe3+ composite film (17.72 ± 1.2 MPa) (Zhang et al., 2025), and melanin-like nanocomposite-reinforced chitosan film loaded with EGCG (18.05 ± 1.11 MPa) (Zhao et al., 2022). Although its EAB was lower than that of the chitosan/branched starch film (71.25 ± 2.87 %) (Chen et al., 2023), it was higher than that of the pectin/branched starch film functionalized with grape seed extract (2.7 ± 0.5 %) (Priyadarshi et al., 2022). Water contact angle

The water contact angle (WCA) of the film surface was measured to assess the hydrophilicity of the film, and the results are shown in Fig. 5-A. The initial WCA of the pure PUL film was 70.07° ± 7.68°. The incorporation of Fe3+ significantly enhanced the WCA of PUL films, likely due to coordination interactions between Fe3+ and functional groups such as hydroxyl groups in PUL molecules, thereby reducing the effective exposure of surface hydrophilic groups (Zhang et al., 2022). The WCA of PUL/EGCG films significantly decreased to 57.13 ± 4.60° upon EGCG incorporation (P<0.05). This reduction stems from EGCG's abundant hydrophilic hydroxyl groups, whose introduction increases the exposure of hydrophilic groups on the film surface, rendering PUL/EGCG films more hydrophilic (Niu et al., 2025). Notably, when EGCG was co-introduced with Fe3+, the WCA of the PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ film further decreased significantly to 39.93 ± 0.96° (P<0.05), exhibiting strong hydrophilic properties. This may be attributed to Fe3+ forming a metal-phenol network with EGCG. This network structure can more effectively adsorb water molecules, further enhancing the adhesion and wettability of the film surface, thereby significantly reducing the contact angle (Lin et al., 2025; Yang, Cai, et al., 2023).Fig. 5Water contact angle (A), water vapor permeability, and moisture content (B) of the PUL composite membrane. Water vapor permeability (WVP) and moisture content (MC)

The WVP of a film reflects its barrier performance to water vapor, and packaging films with a low WVP help maintain food quality (Yong et al., 2024). As shown in Fig.5-B, the pure PUL film exhibited the highest WVP of 1.05 ± 0.04 (× 10−7·g·s−1·m−1·Pa−1), When EGCG and Fe3+ were added individually to the film, the WVP of both the PUL/Fe3+ and PUL/EGCG films decreased slightly, but no significant difference was observed compared to that of the pure PUL film (P > 0.05). However, when both EGCG and Fe3+ were introduced together, the WVP of the PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ film decreased significantly. This reduction may be attributed to the formation of a metal-phenol network between EGCG and Fe3+, along with its cross-linking interaction with PUL molecular chains, collectively constructing a more compact network structure. This structure not only increased the tortuosity of water molecule diffusion pathways but also enhanced the film's flatness and thickness due to its uniform distribution. Consequently, it strengthened the kinetic resistance to water vapor diffusion and reduced water vapor permeability (Wu, Wang, Hu, et al., 2025; P. Zhao et al., 2025). As shown in Fig.5-A, the moisture content of the pure PUL film was 11.70 % ± 0.27 %. The MC of the PUL/Fe3+ film, incorporating Fe3+ ions, showed no significant change compared to pure PUL. However, incorporating EGCG significantly reduced the MC of the PUL/EGCG film (P < 0.05) to 10.55 ± 0.20 %. This reduction may be due to the extensive hydrogen bonding formed between EGCG and PUL molecules, leading to tighter molecular chain packing and increased film density, thereby reducing moisture content (Shen et al., 2021). However, the water content of the PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ film slightly increased after simultaneous doping with EGCG and Fe3+. This increase is attributed to the formation of a polyphenol–metal network structure between EGCG and Fe3+, which reduced the interaction between PUL and EGCG. Consequently, the phenolic hydroxyl groups became more freely available to bind with water, leading to increased moisture content in the film (Yang, Cai, et al., 2023).

Antioxidant properties

The antioxidant capacity of the films was measured using the DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging methods, and the results are shown in Fig. 6. The pure PUL film exhibited the lowest DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities at 2.94 % ± 1.17 % and 2.62 % ± 0.14 %, respectively. After the incorporation of Fe3+, the DPPH and ABTS antioxidant activities of the PUL/Fe3+ film increased, though the improvement was limited. The introduction of EGCG significantly enhanced the antioxidant activity of the PUL/EGCG film, with DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging rates increased to 67.64 % ± 0.53 % and 94.07 ± 0.07 %, respectively. The PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ film, incorporating both EGCG and Fe3+, achieved DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging rates of 65.52 ± 0.69 % and 93.46 ± 0.05 %, respectively. Further, the addition of Fe3+ slightly decreased the antioxidant capacity of the PUL/EGCG film; however, the difference was not significant (P > 0.05). This is because the phenolic hydroxyl groups in EGCG molecules can donate hydrogen atoms to free radicals, converting them into more stable species and thereby terminating the free radical auto-oxidation process. Upon the introduction of Fe3+, the phenolic hydroxyl groups in the EGCG molecules reacted with Fe3+ to form a metal phenolic network, decreasing the number of available hydroxyl groups in the EGCG molecules (Li et al., 2025; Yang, Cai, et al., 2023). Compared with other biopolymer films used in food packaging, in terms of DPPH free radical scavenging ability, although the PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ film (65.52 ± 0.69 %) was inferior to the sodium alginate film containing gallnut extract (75.80 %) (Aloui et al., 2021), it was significantly superior to the sodium alginate film containing propolis extract and graphene oxide (42.61 %) (Guo et al., 2025) and the melanin-like nanocomposite chitosan film loaded with EGCG (58.4 %) (Zhao et al., 2022) in terms of their scavenging ability against DPPH free radicals. In terms of ABTS free radical scavenging ability, the film in this study (93.46 ± 0.05 %) was superior to the pectin film enhanced by tannic acid and Fe3+ cross-linking (86.97 %) (Yang, Cai, et al., 2023) and the amylopectin polysaccharide/xanthan gum film containing grape seed extract (90.85 ± 1.54 %) (Zheng et al., 2023).Fig. 6DPPH and ABTS scavenging rates of PUL composite films. Wettability of coatings on passion fruit peels

Fig. 7 shows the WCA values of the PUL composite coating solution on the passion fruit peel at 0 and 30 s. The WCA values at 0 s for the PUL, PUL/Fe3+, PUL/EGCG and PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ coating solutions were 94.06° ± 0.91°, 99.03 ± 2.41°, 86.67° ± 3.36° and 74.60° ± 3.82°, respectively. Total soluble solid (TSS) content

TSS content is a key indicator of fruit quality and ripeness. Fig. 8-D shows the changes in the TSS content of the passion fruit during storage. In all groups, TSS levels initially increased and then decreased over the storage period. The initial increase in the TSS content was due to the hydrolysis of insoluble polysaccharides to monosaccharides in the passion fruit. In the later stages of storage, the TSS content decreased because of ageing and decay of the fruit (Liu et al., 2025). Compared with the other three groups, the PUL/EGCG/Fe3+-coated group exhibited the smallest decrease in the TSS content, indicating that the coating effectively preserved the fruit quality and moderately delayed ripening. This effect may be attributed to the barrier properties of the composite coating, which helped create a low-oxygen environment. The reduced oxygen availability inhibited fruit respiration, thereby limiting the consumption of soluble solids and delaying the decline in the TSS content (Wang et al., 2024). Alternatively, it could also be because the coating reduces fruit dehydration rates, consequently minimizing TSS loss (Jiang et al., 2019).

Total viable counts

As clearly shown in Fig. 8-E, the total viable counts of passion fruit samples across all treatment groups showed a continuous upward trend during storage. Specifically, the total viable counts for the CK group increased significantly from an initial value of 2.50 ± 0.03 log CFU/g to 6.52 ± 0.13 log CFU/g by day 10. In contrast, the PUL, PUL/Fe3+, PUL/EGCG, and PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ coating groups recorded values of 6.37 ± 0.05 log CFU/g, 6.29 ± 0.03 log CFU/g, 5.26 ± 0.07 log CFU/g, and 5.08 ± 0.08 log CFU/g, respectively, by day 10. The results indicate that the PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ coating has superior preservation efficacy, effectively inhibiting microbial growth to a certain extent. This effect is presumed to be closely related to EGCG's antibacterial activity. Furthermore, the metal-phenolic network formed by EGCG and Fe3+ may have further enhanced the coating's barrier properties and synergistic antibacterial capabilities, collectively reducing microbial infection of passion fruit (Zhong et al., 2024).

Discussion

This study employed the solution casting method to prepare composite films. This process offers promising prospects for large-scale production and economic viability due to its simplicity, low equipment requirements (primarily involving mixing and drying apparatus), and manageable initial investment costs. From a material cost perspective, the pullulan (PUL) substrate is produced via energy-efficient microbial fermentation, and its superior film-forming ability contributes to lower energy consumption during processing. Although EGCG is a high-value ingredient, its cost-effectiveness was enhanced in this work through a low-concentration addition (5 % w/w) and by leveraging its synergy with inexpensive Fe3+ to form a reinforcing network. In terms of environmental properties, the composite membrane possesses a theoretical foundation for biodegradability due to the natural origins of its components: PUL is a microbial fermentation polysaccharide, EGCG is derived from tea leaves, and Fe3+ is an environmentally friendly metal ion. Its entirely natural composition provides an intrinsic guarantee for achieving a green cycle from raw materials to waste disposal, demonstrating significantly greater environmental friendliness compared to traditional plastic packaging. Although the unit material cost of this composite film currently remains higher than that of traditional plastics, its application in passion fruit preservation significantly extends shelf life, thereby enhancing the commercial value of the fruit and reducing economic losses caused by spoilage. This demonstrates its potential for application in the field of active packaging.

Although this study confirmed the efficacy of the PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ composite film in preserving passion fruit, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the findings are based solely on passion fruit, and thus the film's applicability to other produce remains unexplored, which limits the generalizability of the results. Second, while the film's dark, purple-black color provides excellent UV blocking, it may mask the natural appearance of the coated fruit, potentially affecting consumer acceptance. Furthermore, all evaluations were performed under stable laboratory conditions, leaving the film's performance under real-world storage, transportation, and large-scale production unexamined. To address these limitations, future research should focus on: (a) systematically evaluate the film's preservation efficacy on other agricultural products, particularly high-moisture produce such as strawberries, blueberries, and leafy vegetables, to verify its broad-spectrum utility; (b) optimizing the balance between functional performance and optical properties, or targeting its use for light-sensitive or visually less critical agricultural products; and (c) Investigate the film's performance stability under various cold chain conditions and establish shelf-life models; and (d) Explore the feasibility of industrial-scale production.

Conclusions

This study used the solution casting method was used to prepare PUL composite films cross-linked with EGCG and Fe3+. The physical and chemical properties, antioxidant activities and effectiveness of the composite coating in passion fruit preservation were evaluated. SEM analysis showed that EGCG and Fe3+ were uniformly incorporated into the PUL films, resulting in a more compact film structure and enhanced barrier properties. The red shift of FTIR characteristic peaks and the increased intensity of XRD diffraction peaks collectively indicated the formation of a reinforced cross-linked network through non-covalent interactions among the three components, demonstrating excellent biocompatibility. Furthermore, the addition of EGCG and Fe3+ significantly improved the UV-blocking and antioxidant properties of the PUL films, as well as its mechanical properties and wettability. Fruit preservation results showed that the PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ coating effectively extended the shelf life of passion fruit while maintaining its good quality. Therefore, the PUL/EGCG/Fe3+ coating shows promising potential as a food coating.
