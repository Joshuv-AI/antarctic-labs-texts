Cell Trafficking Disorders Play an Important Role in the Pathogenesis of Skeletal Dysplasias
Authors: Beyhan Tüysüz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cell trafficking is the transfer of signals and metabolic products between cell compartments to maintain crucial biological functions. In recent years, more than 370 genes have been shown to be associated with defects in cellular transport. The aim of this review is to draw attention to the importance of cell trafficking in the pathogenesis of skeletal dysplasia and to attempt to establish a relationship between clinical findings and the functions of the disrupted proteins. Cell trafficking disorders are divided into four main categories: defects in proteins involved in the transport of molecules (cargo) from the cell to the outside (exocytic pathway) or from the outside to the inside (endocytic pathway) and related to the cytoskeleton, membrane contact sites, and autophagy. A number of skeletal dysplasias result from deficiencies in proteins across different categories of cell trafficking, including glycosylation and lysosomal disorders, which are skeletal involvement. It is noteworthy that genes affected in skeletal dysplasias related to cell trafficking are impaired in signaling pathways involved in the embryonic development of bone, membranous and endochondral ossification, and skeletal morphogenesis. Studies investigating the role of cell trafficking in the development of skeletal dysplasias will shed light on the disease’s pathogenesis and increase the potential for developing new therapeutic agents.

Introduction

Skeletal dysplasias are clinically and genetically heterogeneous single-gene disorders characterized by short stature and skeletal deformities.1 In single-gene disorders, the proteins encoded by the genes are grouped according to their function and localization as transcription factors, extracellular matrix proteins, cell surface growth factors, receptor proteins, signaling proteins, and peroxisomal biogenesis and lysosomal enzymes.2 Over the past 20 years, studies of protein function have revealed the sites of expression and roles of many proteins, and a large group of proteins known as cell trafficking proteins have been identified. Cellular trafficking is the transfer of signals and metabolites between cell compartments to maintain important biological functions.3-8 Genetic disorders associated with intracellular trafficking were first classified as a distinct disease group by Olkkonen and Ikonen in 2000.3 In 2006, the same researchers reported that there are 30 different diseases related to disorders of intracellular membrane trafficking, with neurological disorders being the main problem.4 The 2013 Nobel Prize in Physiology and Medicine was awarded to three researchers for their discovery of the mechanisms that control vesicle trafficking, an important transport system in cells.9

With the development of next-generation sequencing technologies, genes involved in cellular trafficking have been shown to play a role in the etiopathogenesis of many well-defined genetic diseases as well as some rare diseases that were previously undiagnosed. Most diseases in this group are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, but there are also autosomal dominant and X-linked recessive and dominant inheritances.10-14 In 2007, Gissen and Maher6 reported that cellular transport defects can be categorized into four groups, depending on the predicted function of the impaired proteins involved in intracellular transport: 1) defects in cargo uptake and vesicle biogenesis, 2) defects in Rab and other GTPases and related proteins, 3) defects in proteins associated with the cytoskeleton, and 4) defects in vesicle binding and fusion. In a comprehensive review, García-Cazorla et al13,14 recently proposed to classify cell trafficking disorders (CTD) into four categories based on the function and location of the affected protein: 1) vesicular trafficking (including organelle-related), 2) cytoskeleton related, 3) membrane contact sites, and 4) autophagy.

Approximately 2000 genes are known to be responsible for cell trafficking. In recent years, the number of new genetic diseases associated with cell trafficking has increased significantly; to date, more than 370 genes have been linked to CTD.10-14 These genes mostly cause multisystem syndromes, in which neurological findings are common, as well as single system disorders and skeletal dysplasias.13,14 The aim of this review is to understand the biological basis of cell trafficking and to emphasize its importance in the pathogenesis of skeletal dysplasias.

Biological Basis of Cell Trafficking Disorders

Cell trafficking is a fundamental process responsible for the biosynthesis, secretion, processing, and transportation of “cargo” (hormones, growth factors, antibodies, proteins, lipids, etc.) to specific compartments where they perform their functions and is essential for cellular life. Many cellular functions depend on the transport of vesicles containing proteins or lipids to specific intracellular targets. Human cells communicate with each other in two ways: by the uptake of molecules from outside the cell into the cell (endocytic pathway) or by the release of molecules synthesized inside the cell into the extracellular space (exocytic pathway). Cellular transport occurs in highly organized, balanced, and directed pathways from the plasma membrane to early endosomes or from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the Golgi apparatus and to lysosomes.15-18

Cell trafficking disorders were initially limited to membrane (vesicular) trafficking, but the demonstration that many diseases are related to cellular transport has contributed to the discovery of new biological pathways. Although cellular transport consists primarily of vesicular and organelle transport, cytoskeletal motor proteins also play an important role in transport. Membrane contact sites, and autophagy are also two newly identified cellular transport pathways.13,14

Vesicular (Membrane) and Organelle Trafficking

In cellular traffic, the transport of proteins and lipids between different cellular compartments takes place via vesicles. The first step in vesicular transport is vesicle formation. The molecule to be transported (cargo) binds to the receptor on the cell or target compartment membrane via adaptor proteins (APs) and coat proteins (clathrin, COPI, and COPII), and interacts with inositol phospholipids in the membrane content. Molecules that accumulate in this region of the membrane due to the changing structure of inositol phospholipids form a bud with the participation of coat proteins and adaptors. Then the bud-shaped membrane bends and breaks away from the stalk region, completing vesicle formation when the bud with the cargo is released. The cargo-carrying vesicles are transported by the cytoskeletal motor proteins kinesin and dynein along microtubular structures and are guided to the recipient compartments. The recipient is bound to the compartment via fusion proteins and the vesicle membrane fuses with the target organelle’s membrane, releasing the cargo in the vesicle.12-20

The endocytic pathway begins with the movement of cargo from the extracellular space through the plasma membrane into the cell: cargo proteins are pulled into the plasma membrane by clathrin-coated vesicles. After labeling by the mannose‐6‐phosphate receptor in the trans‐Golgi network, the cargo passes through early and late endosomes and reaches the lysosomes or is recycled back to the plasma membrane (recycling endosome) (Figure 1A).12-20

The exocytic pathway involves the movement of molecules (proteins, lipids, or carbohydrates) that are synthesized, folded, and processed in the ER, through the Golgi to the cell membrane and into the extracellular space (Figure 1B). When the ER-derived cargo is ready for sorting after protein folding and post-translational modifications such as hydroxylation and O-glycosylation, it is recognized by the sorting system and packaged into transport vesicles to be transported to the Golgi apparatus. On the way to the Golgi, proteins are collected in the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) and enter the Golgi via the cis-cisternae, where O- and N-glycosylation is completed. They then pass through the medial and trans-cisternae and are transported to the plasma membrane via vesicles emerging from the trans-Golgi membrane. Cargo-carrying vesicles are transported out of the cell from the plasma membrane by membrane fusion with the help of proteins of the vesicle membrane (v-SNARE) and the target membrane (t-SNARE).21-25

The main protein families that coordinate the functions of vesicular, organelle, and interorganelle transport are responsible for vesicle formation and transport, binding, and fusion with the target membrane.12-14

The COAT proteins clathrin, COPI, and COPII alter the structure of membrane phospholipids, recruit and concentrate cargo molecules in the membrane and contribute to vesicle formation. During the formation of clathrin-coated vesicles, clathrin does not bind directly; proteins called APs bind clathrin to the cargo and the membrane. COPI plays a role in transport from the Golgi to the ER, and COPII in transport from the ER to the Golgi (Figure 1A and B). SEC23A is an essential component of COPII-coated vesicles.16-20

Adaptor proteins play a role in vesicle transport by coordinating vesicle formation, transport, binding, and fusion with the target membrane. Sec24 is a cargo adaptor that contains multiple cargo binding sites to capture different proteins. It is a component of COPII that mediates protein transport from the ER. Adaptor proteins (AP1-AP5) are a component of clathrin-coated vesicles. They contain binding sites that recognize specific cargo proteins, concentrate cargo molecules in the vesicle and transport the cargo to its destination. The APs Sec24, AP2, and AP4 play a role in vesicle trafficking, AP1 and AP3 in organelle trafficking, and AP5 in autophagy (Figure 1A and B).2627 Disorders of the APs are known as “Adaptinopathies.”

The Rab protein family with its more than 70 members forms the largest group of small GTPases related to Ras. Rab proteins are specific regulators of vesicular trafficking, and a different Rab protein may be required for each step of cellular trafficking. In addition to their role in budding, formation, transport, binding, and fusion of vesicles, they are also involved in autophagy.22-25,28 They are also markers for transport vesicles; Rab5c and Rab14 have been shown to be expressed in the early endosome, Rab7 and Rab9a in the late endosome, and Rab11a in the recycling endosome.

The proteins of the SNARE (soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor binding protein receptor) family play a role in the fusion of vesicles with target membranes. They are located both on the vesicle (v-SNARE) and on the target membrane (t-SNARE) and enable fusion by bringing them together.29 Rab proteins help the SNAREs to find the right target membranes. Dysfunction of SNAREs can disrupt the fusion of vesicles with the cis-Golgi cisternae and prevent ER-Golgi membrane trafficking. They also play a role in autophagy. Pathological disorders caused by mutations in SNARE proteins are referred to as SNAREopathies.

Vacuolar protein sorting (VPS) proteins play a role in intracellular sorting, sorting, and distribution of soluble vacuolar proteins. The proteins encoded by VPS33A, VPS33B, VPS16, and VPS18 are key components of the homotypic fusion and protein sorting (HOPS) complex and of CORVET (Class C Core Vacuole/Endosome Tethering), a protein complex that mediates SNARE-catalyzed endosome-lysosome fusion and autophagosome-lysosome formation.22-25

Trafficking protein particle complexes (TRAPP) are protein complexes with several subunits (TRAPPC1-12, TRAPPC2, and TRAPPC3) that are involved in the transport pathways of vesicles between organelles, and defects in this protein family are referred to as TRAPopathies.30

Membrane Contact Sites

Membrane contact sites (MCS) are defined as areas where the membranes of two organelles are in close contact with each other (Figure 1C). There is no fusion mediated by SNARE proteins as in vesicular traffic, but rather the presence of binding forces due to protein-protein or protein-lipid interactions.

The ER is a network of tubules and cisternae that occupies a large part of the cytoplasm. The discovery of numerous MCSs with other organelles has shown that the ER plays an important role in regulating the dynamics and functions of organelles. The closest relationship is between the ER and endosome; ER tubules surround endosomes during vesicular traffic, and contacts between the ER and endosomes intensify as endosomes mature. Half of early endosomes and 99% of late endosomes have MCS between the ER.22,23,31 In recent years, organelle connections have been discovered that do not involve the ER, the membrane contact sites of mitochondria and other organelles.14

Cytoskeleton-Related Trafficking

The cytoskeleton consists of microfilaments in the form of networks and microtubules in a cylindrical structure as well as intermediate filaments. The microfilaments contain the proteins tubulin, actin, and myosine. Dynein and kinesin are walking (motor) proteins that move along the microtubules (Figure 1D). During transport through the cytoskeleton, dyneins and kinesins provide bidirectional transport of cargo molecules; kinesins are responsible for antegrade transport, while dyneins are responsible for retrograde transport.13,14,18,32

Microtubule proteins are also responsible for the fixation of cell shape and intracellular structures; intermediate filaments contribute to the fixation of the nucleus in the cell. There is a relationship between the ER and the microtubules that is crucial for cellular organization and function. Kinesin and dynein move the ER along the microtubules and regulate their distribution.32

Autophagy

Autophagy is a crucial, conserved process of the cell in which damaged organelles are degraded and recycled and non-functional components are eliminated from the cell (Figure 1E). It begins with a double membrane precursor (phagophore) in the cytoplasm and continues with the formation of the autophagosome. The onset of autophagosome biogenesis is stimulated by changes in energy status and nutrients. Autophagosomes are transported along microtubules and associate with them to facilitate their fusion with the lysosome, a major site of protein degradation in the cell. The autophagy cargo is degraded by the autolysosome after fusion between autophagosomes and lysosomes, which is controlled by Rab GTPases and SNAREs.12-16

Skeletal Dysplasias Related to Cell Trafficking Disorders

Skeletal dysplasias used to be classified on the basis of radiology and morphology, but after 1997, as the number of disease-causing genes increased, they were categorized on the basis of defective protein function into structural proteins, enzymes, local regulators of growth cartilage, nuclear proteins, and defects in transcription factors.33,34 The latest classification is also based not only on molecular pathogenesis but also on common clinical diagnoses; it has been reported that there are 771 different phenotypes in 41 groups and 552 genes are responsible.35 In recent years, the pathogenesis of some skeletal dysplasias has been shown to be related to the deficiency or impairment of proteins involved in cell trafficking.13,14,36-40

In CTD, defects are present in the genes responsible for which carrier vesicle the cargo attaches to, for budding and cleavage of vesicles, for transport to organelles and movement along the cytoskeleton, and for binding, docking, and fusion with the target membrane.13,14 Here, skeletal dysplasias has been analyzed using patient samples according to the category of impaired cellular trafficking.

Skeletal Dysplasias Related to Main Protein Families Involved in Vesicular and Organelle Trafficking

Sec23 from the coat protein group and Sec24 from the AP class are components of coat protein II-coated vesicles that transport proteins from the ER to the Golgi.12-19 Pathogenic variants in the genes encoding these two proteins, which regulate ER-Golgi transport in osteoblasts, cause two distinct skeletal dysplasias.37,38 Monoallelic and biallelic pathogenic variants in SEC23A cause Craniolenticulosutural dysplasia, which belongs to the group of “NOS35-craniofacial dysostoses” and is characterized by a dysmorphic face, large fontanel, and lens opacities (Table 1).1,42 Cole-Carpenter syndrome type 2, which is due to biallelic disease-causing variants in SEC24D, is classified in the group “NOS26-osteogenesis imperfecta and bone fragility” and is characterized by severe osteopenia, recurrent fractures, craniosynostosis, hydrocephalus, and short limbs (Table 1, Figure 2A).1,42,43

The Rab23 protein from the Rab protein family plays a role in vesicle trafficking and regulates Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) signaling with negative effects. In vertebrates, SHH is involved in the early stages of mesenchymal condensation in skeletal development, is expressed in the developing face, and plays a critical role in finger formation.36 Biallelic disease-causing variants in Rab23 lead to Carpenter syndrome type 1 from the “NOS34: syndromes featuring craniosynostosis group,” which is characterized by craniosynostosis and polysyndactyly (Table 1).135 Smith-McCort dysplasia (SMC)-2 belongs to the group of “NOS13- Spondyloepi(meta)physeal dysplasias-SE(M)D,” in which the Rab33B mutation occurs. The protein encoded by this gene belongs to a small GTPase superfamily that plays a role in Golgi trafficking. Patients with SMC2 have short stature, brachydactyly, a barrel-shaped chest, platyspondyly, and typical posterior double humps on the vertebral bodies and a lacy iliac crest (Table 1, Figure 2B and C).135 ,44

TRAPPC2 (Sedlin) from the TRAPP protein family is responsible for the transport between ER and Golgi in vesicular traffic and for the transport of procollagen in the ER. Monoallelic mutations of TRAPPC2 disrupt procollagen secretion and cause X-linked spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia tarda, which belongs to the group of “NOS13- SE(M)D” (Table 1, Figure 2D).135

The VPS protein group is associated with the HOPS complex, which is a structural bridge for the fusion of late endosomes and autophagosomes with lysosomes.12-16 Biallelic mutations in VPS33A result in a syndrome termed MPS-plus, Mendelian inheritance in man(MIM) number(617303) characterized by dysostosis multiplex, coarse face, macroglossia, thick and coarse hair, cardiomyopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, and renal involvement. Electron microscopic examination of fibroblast cultures from these patients revealed abnormal ER, large and/or degenerated mitochondria, few early and late endosomes, and large vacuoles, indicating a disruption of the vesicular transport system resembling lysosomal storage disease.45 In addition, studies in fibroblasts from patients revealed reduced expression of the VPS33A protein as well as reduced expression of the HOPS/CORVET component VPS18 and the HOPS component VPS41.46’ In the Nosology of Genetic Skeletal Diseases: Revision 2023, this disease is classified in the group of “NOS22 lysosomal storage diseases with skeletal involvement.”35 VPS16 encodes a component of the HOPS complex that plays a role in the fusion of endosomes and autophagosomes with lysosomes. Although autosomal dominant (in a few cases autosomal recessive) mutations in VPS16 are known to be responsible for dystonia, an MPS-plus-like phenotype due to a biallelic mutation in VPS16 has only been described in very few patients.47-50 This phenotype has not yet been included in the MIM. One unpublished case was also observed in the center (Table 1, Figure 2E and F).

The NBAS protein is part of a t-SNARE of the ER and interacts with the Golgi and the ER to regulate the distribution of macromolecules within the cell by participating in the retrograde transport process. In addition to intracellular transport, NBAS also plays a role in the regulation of bone mineralization, osteoblast differentiation, and bone development.51 Biallelic mutations in NBAS lead to hepatic failure syndrome 2 with skeletal dysplasia, which belongs to the “NOS13-SE(M)D” group and is characterized by short stature, large anterior fontanel, osteopenia, severe liver failure, optic atrophy, senile appearances facies, and cutis laxa (Table 1, Figure 2G and H).

Skeletal Dysplasias Associated with Other Proteins Involved in Vesicular and Organelle Trafficking

Biallelic pathogenic variants in DYM, which encodes a dicmelin protein involved in the organization and intracellular transport of the Golgi apparatus and plays a crucial role in endochondral bone formation, lead to Dyggve-Melchior-Clausen (DMC) and SMC-1. These two dysplasias and SMC-2 from the “NOS13-SE(M)D” group have common skeletal findings, while those with DMC also show mental retardation.1,35

The protein eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2-alpha kinase 3 (eIF2-alpha), encoded by EIF2AK3 and expressed in the pancreas and developing bone, is a type 1 membrane protein in the ER that increases phosphorylation in response to misfolded proteins that block translation. In this way, it plays an important role in facilitating the transport of synthesized molecules from the ER to the Golgi. Pathogenic variants in EIF2AK3 cause Wolcott‐Rallison syndrome, belong to the “NOS13-SE(M)D” group and is characterized by insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus in early childhood, multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, and growth retardation in the first two years of life.1,35,52

KDELR2 is one of the receptors involved in signaling transduction and regulates retrograde traffic between the Golgi apparatus and the ER. Biallelic mutations in KDELR2, which encodes this receptor, result in HSP47 (SERPINH1; MIM600943) being unable to bind to the mutant KDELR2 and dissociate from collagen type I, leading to a severe phenotype of Osteogenesis imperfecta type 21 (NOS26).1,35

Some vesicular and organelle/interorganelle traffic disorders also exhibit a glycosylation defect, such as Achondrogenesis type 1A, Odontochondrodysplasia, Geroderma osteodysplastica, as well as “Skeletal dysplasia with amelogenesis imperfecta”.53,54 Achondrogenesis type 1A (Table 1, Figure 2I) and Odontochondrodysplasia are caused by biallelic mutations in TRIP11, which encodes a protein associated with Golgi and microtubule trafficking and is expressed in intramembranous and endochondral bone. Achondrogenesis type 1A is a lethal skeletal dysplasia characterized by severe micromelic dwarfism, while osteoporosis, delayed tooth eruption, irregular metaphyses, and brachydactyly are typical features of odontochondrodyplasia, which belongs to the group of spondylometaphyseal dysplasias (NOS12-0020).1,35,54 The trans-Golgin-RAB6-interacting protein, which is encoded by GORAB, is involved in COPI-mediated trafficking in osteoblasts. Biallelic mutations in this gene, which regulate ER-Golgi trafficking, are responsible for Gerodermia osteodysplastica from the group “NOS26-Osteogenesis imperfecta and bone fragility,” which is characterized by cutis laxa, wrinkled skin, hypermobility, osteopenia, and an aged face (Table 1, Figue 3A and B) .135 ,53 ,54 Skeletal dysplasia with amelogenesis imperfecta due to an SLC10A7 mutation, which belongs to the group of “NOS05-dysplasias with multiple joint dislocations” and is characterized by short stature, short limbs, scoliosis, advanced carpal ossification, hypermobile joints, and amelogenesis imperfecta (Table 1, Figure 3C-E) . SLC10A7, which is involved in the trans-Golgi network, has an essential function in cartilage development and bone mineralization.1,35,53,54

Lysosomal enzyme trafficking plays a role in the pathogenesis of some lysosomal storage diseases which are classified within skeletal dysplasias (NOS22-lysosomal storage diseases with skeletal involvement).15,35,54 SUMF1 encodes an enzyme that is required for the post-translational modification and catalytic activation of enzymes such as glycosaminoglycans, sulfolipids, and steroid sulfates in the ER. Biallelic mutations in this gene lead to multiple sulfatase deficiency which is characterized by dysostosis multiplex, organomegaly, and ichthyosis.35 In order for the lysosomes to degrade hydrolases, they must first be labeled at the mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) receptor in the Golgi. Pathogenic variants in GNPTAB and GNPTG result in no phosphate group being bound to the mannose residues of the lysosomal enzymes and lead to Mucolipidosis type 2 (Table 1, Figure 4A and B) or type 3 (Table 1, Figure 4C-E). Another very rare lysosomal disorder associated with the M6P from this group is Dysostosis multiplex, Ain-Nase type,which is caused by pathogenic variants in TMEM251 (LYSET).1,35,54 In addition to MBTPS1, a defect disrupts the M6P signaling pathway and causes skeletal dysplasia belonging to the “NOS13-SE(M)D” characterized by platyspondyly and epiphyseal involvement (Table 1, Figure 4F and G) with the phenotypes Spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, Kondo-Fu type (NOS13-200). Interestingly, the diseases caused by these four genes (GNPTAB, GNPTG, MBTPS1, and LYSET) have overlapping clinical symptoms and biochemical features such as growth retardation, skeletal abnormalities, and elevated lysosomal enzymes in the blood.54

Membrane Contact Sites Disorders

Most genetic defects at membrane contact sites are characterized by neurodegenerative disorders. An example of a skeletal dysplasia from this group is GM1 gangliosidosis, which is due to a genetic defect at the membrane contact site between the ER and the mitochondria.55 GM1 gangliosidosis, a lysosomal storage disease caused by a deficiency of beta-galactosidase-1 encoded by GLB1, is characterized by short stature, skeletal deformity, a coarse face, organomegaly, dilated cardiomyopathy, and dysostosis multiplex (Table 2). The recently discovered connection between mitochondria and nucleus is disrupted in LONP1 deficiency; this protein plays a role in the maintenance of mtDNA and is sometimes localized in the nucleus, indicating a connection between the nucleus and the mitochondria. Pathogenic variants in LONP1 that encode LONP1 cause the very rare disease cerebral, ocular, dental, ear, and skeletal anomalies syndrome (CODAS; NOS13-SEMD), which is characterized by cataracts and crescent-shaped irregularities of the epiphyses (Table 2, Figure 4H) .1254

The actin-binding protein encoded by FLNA is responsible for the cross-linking of actin filaments and binding to membrane glycoproteins. It contributes to changes in the shape of the cytoskeleton and to migration into osteoblasts. Pathogenic variants in FLNA lead to frontometaphyseal dysplasia, Melnick–Needle syndrome, periventricular nodular heterotopia, and otopalatodigital syndrome from the group of “NOS06 filamins and related disorders.” Melnick–Needle syndrome is characterized by a late-closing fontanel, lumbar kyphosis, hip dislocation, a dysmorphic face, irregular, banded ribs, and curvature of the tibia, ulna, and radius (Table 2, Figure 5A-C) .3540 ,41 ,56 Dynein 1 (DYNC1H1) is the major cytoplasmic dynein required for intracellular movement, while dynein 2 (DYNC2H1), which regulates retrograde protein transport in cilia, is associated with intraflagellar transport in primary cilia. Autophagy Disorders

Recent studies have shown that autophagy is important for bone homeostasis and also for the pathogenic mechanisms of bone diseases.57 -60 TCIRG1 and PLEKHM1 encode proteins involved in autophagy, and mutations in these genes prevent destruction by osteoclasts and lead to osteopetrosis from the group of “NOS24-Osteopetrosis and related osteoclast disorders.” Impaired autophagy has been observed in patients with mutations in TCIRG1 that cause autosomal recessive malignant and intermediate osteopetrosis type 1. Plekhm1 cooperates with Rab7 to regulate lysosome biogenesis and bone resorption in osteoclasts. In patients with autosomal recessive or dominant osteopetrosis type 3 and 6with PLEKHM1 mutations, an increased level of autophagy was detected in patient cells compared to wild type (Table 2, Figure 5D-G) .57-60 FIG4 gene encodes the enzyme phosphoinositide 5-phosphatase and plays a crucial role in both membrane trafficking and autophagy by regulating phosphoinositide levels. Biallelic pathogenic variants in FIG4 cause Yunis–Varon syndrome, which belongs to the group of “NOS33-Cleidocranial dysplasia and related disorders.” This disease is characterized by severe growth retardation, hypotonia, dysmorphic facial features with cleidocranial dysplasia, aplastic or hypoplastic hallux and phalanges (Table 2, Figure 5H-K) .134 SQSTM1 encodes a scaffold protein that regulates a variety of biological processes, including apoptosis and ubiquitin-mediated autophagy. Monoallelic mutations in this gene cause familial Paget’s disease of bone from the group of osteosclerotic diseases (NOS25), which is characterized by deafness, osteopenia, and bone deformity.

Conclusion

In skeletal dysplasias associated with CTD, genes involved in membranous and endochondral ossification as well as gene functions related to signaling pathways involved in embryonic development and skeletal morphogenesis appear to be impaired. In addition, some lysosomal diseases have been linked to novel identified genes involved in the fusion of endosomes with lysosomes and autophagosomes, leading to phenotypes resembling lysosomal storage diseases. Of note, most dysplasias in this group are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, but autosomal dominant and X-linked recessive and dominant inheritance also occur, and a significant proportion of disease-causing variants are associated with loss of function. Increasing research into cellular trafficking and associated disorders will lead to important advances that will create new knowledge networks between cell biology, biochemistry, metabolism, and genetics. In addition, determining the common disease mechanisms of this group of skeletal dysplasias will contribute to the development of biomarkers and new treatments for these diseases.
