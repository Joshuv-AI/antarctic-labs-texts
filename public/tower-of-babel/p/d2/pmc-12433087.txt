Development and validation of a prognostic prediction model based on coagulation-related genes and clinical factors in acute leukemia
Authors: Tian Lan, Yi Zhan, Yong Chen, Haihong Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute leukemia (AL) is one of the most prevalent pediatric malignancies with highly heterogeneous clinical outcomes. Coagulation-related genes (CRGs) play a crucial role in tumours, but their value in combination with clinical factors for prognostic prediction in AL is unclear. This study aims to develop a prognostic model based on the CRGs signature, with the goal of improving prognostic monitoring and identifying potential therapeutic targets for pediatric AL.MethodsWe collected transcriptomic and clinical data of pediatric AL patients from the Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) databases, including age, gender, and white blood cell count (WBC). Molecular subtypes related to CRGs were identified via non-negative matrix factorization (NMF). A CRGs-based gene signature was developed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and regression analyses. The model was built on a training set and validated independently. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) was used to assess the predictive accuracy of the model for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (OS). Nomograms were constructed combining CRGs characteristics and clinical factors, and their clinical utility was assessed using calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA). Immune infiltration was quantified using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and the microenvironment cell populations-counter (MCPcounter) algorithm. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis was performed to assess the correlation between signature gene expression and OS. Moreover, molecular docking was utilized to investigate the potential interactions between signature genes and small-molecule drugs. Expression of key genes was confirmed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR).ResultsA total of 103 AL patients were included as a training set. Risk stratification based on the median risk score of CRGs showed a significant difference in OS between the two groups (P<0.001), with the low-risk group having a better prognosis. The area under the curves (AUCs) of the model for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival prediction in the training set were 0.711, 0.762, and 0.718, respectively, and the AUC values in the independent validation set also showed good agreement. Analysis integrating risk scores with clinical data indicated that the CRGs signature could serve as an independent prognostic factor. The nomogram constructed based on CRGs features and key clinical variables showed good fit and potential clinical net effect. Molecular docking analysis revealed stable binding interactions between PROS1 and the small-molecule drugs, avatrombopag and lusutrombopag.ConclusionsIn this study, a robust prognostic model incorporating CRGs was constructed to effectively predict survival outcomes in paediatric AL patients. The model helps to enable individualised risk stratification and guide targeted therapy. In addition, avatrombopag and lusutrombopag as potential therapeutic agents provide new ideas for precision medicine in paediatric AL.Keywords: Childhood acute leukemia (childhood AL), coagulation-related genes (CRGs), prognosis, drug sensitivity

Key findings

• A four-gene coagulation-related genes (CRGs) signature (RAB27B, PRDM16, ErbB4, PROS1) predicts survival in pediatric acute leukemia (AL).

• Low-risk patients showed better overall survival and distinct immune infiltration patterns.

• PROS1 binds stably to avatrombopag and lusutrombopag, indicating potential therapeutic targets.

What is known and what is new?

• CRGs influence tumor progression and immune response, but their prognostic role in pediatric AL is underexplored.

• This study integrates CRGs with clinical factors to create a validated prognostic model and identifies novel drug interactions.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The model enables personalized risk assessment and targeted therapy. Clinical trials are needed to validate the efficacy of PROS1-targeted drugs.

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of childhood mortality worldwide, with incidence rates increasing from 124 to 140 per million person-years between the 1980s and the early 2000s (1). Acute leukemia (AL) is a common malignancy in children under 15 years of age. Studies have shown that the incidence of AL is 46.4 per million per year in children aged 0–14 years and 28.5 per million per year in adolescents aged 15–19 years (2,3). Nearly 80% of AL in children aged 0–14 years are acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and 15% are acute myeloid leukemia (AML) (4-6). The prognosis for children and adolescents with AML has improved over the past two decades, with overall survival (OS) rates approaching 65–70% (7-10). However, AML remains a rare and heterogeneous disease that warrants further investigation. The pathology of ALL is characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of early lymphoid precursors infiltrating the bone marrow (11,12). The classification of ALL is not easily distinguishable morphologically. According to the 2022 revision of the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Consensus Classification (ICC), ALL is divided into four main subtypes: B-cell ALL (B-ALL)/lymphoblastic lymphoma (LBL) not otherwise specified (NOS), B-ALL/LBL with recurrent genetic abnormalities, T-cell ALL (T-ALL)/LBL, and natural killer ALL (NK-ALL)/LBL (13,14). Despite substantial improvements in OS over time, a treatment gap for ALL persists among countries (15). Therefore, there remains a need to find more accessible solutions and develop promising therapeutic strategies to improve prognostic outcomes and survival of pediatric AL patients.

Under normal physiological conditions, the coagulation-anticoagulation-fibrinolysis system in the human body maintains dynamic equilibrium (16,17). However, in malignant hematological diseases, the coagulation function is disrupted, leading to the overexpression of cancer-associated coagulation factors and tissue factors. This imbalance results in severe coagulation dysfunction, subsequently triggering bleeding reactions (18,19). Malignant tumors are associated with a high risk of hypercoagulable states and thrombotic hemorrhagic complications; furthermore, the coagulation system influences tumor cell survival, proliferation, and migration (17,20). Recent studies suggest that the coagulation system interacts with the tumor microenvironment (TME) to promote or inhibit tumor progression and even affect the immunotherapeutic response of the tumor (21). He et al. demonstrated that a prognostic model of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), derived from coagulation-related genes (CRGs), serves as an independent prognostic predictor for immunotherapy (22). Notably, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is one of the most severe and fatal complications of AL, with clinical complications ranging from localized thrombosis to hemorrhage of varying severity (23,24). Chemotherapy in patients with malignant hematologic diseases will affect the coagulation function. Coagulation, anticoagulation, and fibrinolytic indices can partially reflect the body’s internal hemostatic balance. Regular monitoring helps identify the post-chemotherapeutic adverse events, enabling timely interventions in the treatment, and improving the patients’ quality of life (24-27).

Currently, early recognition and prognostic assessment of AL relies on a variety of clinical factors with molecular biomarkers. Among the clinical factors, advanced age and high white blood cell count (WBC) levels are important prognostic indicators for AL (28). In terms of molecular biomarkers, immunophenotypic characteristics (e.g., B-cell type, T-cell type, and CD58 expression status) have been widely demonstrated to be closely associated with patient prognosis (29). With the rapid development of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology, molecular features such as gene expression profiles and mutation status as biomarkers have been shown to provide important references for patient prognosis (30,31). However, due to the significant heterogeneity of AL patients in terms of clinical presentation and treatment response, it is difficult to fully reflect the clinical risk and biological status of patients by solely relying on genetic features. Here, combining clinical factors such as age and WBC with gene-level information can capture patients’ risk profiles more comprehensively and significantly improve the predictive accuracy and clinical applicability of prognostic models. Therefore, this study explored the prognosis of childhood AL patients based on CRGs signature combined with clinical factors, and developed a comprehensive nomogram to quantify the survival probability according to risk scores, thereby facilitating personalized and precise treatment for pediatric AL patients. Importantly, molecular docking was employed to predict the binding interactions between signature genes and small-molecule drugs, providing insights into potential therapeutic agents for the treatment of childhood AL. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-118/rc).

Methods

Collection and organization of the data

In this study, transcriptome data and corresponding clinical information of 135 AL patients [Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET)-ALL-P3] were obtained from the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Xena database (https://xena.ucsc.edu/), and patients with no survival information were excluded. The cohort was used as a training set for model construction. For the control cohort, transcriptome data were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/) containing samples from 70 healthy children for differential expression analysis. For independent validation, we additionally downloaded The Cancer Genome Atlas-Acute Myeloid Leukemia (TCGA-LAML) cohort from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) as a validation set to ensure the generalisation ability and stability of the model. CRGs were referenced from previous studies (22) from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database for two pathways related to coagulation: the complement and coagulation cascade (KEGG: hsa04610) and platelet activation (KEGG: hsa04611), as shown in Table S1. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Identification and analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Differential expression analysis [|log2fold change (FC)| >2, false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05] was performed between normal and tumor groups using the “limma” package, and a volcano plot was drawn. The differential genes were then intersected with the extracted CRGs to obtain differentially expressed CRGs (DECRGs). Clinical data were merged with the DECRGs expression profiles to screen the samples of tumor patients with survival time exceeding 30 days. Univariate regression analysis was conducted on DECRGs using the “survival” package. Candidate genes were screened according to P value (P<0.05, Table S2), and the expressions of candidate genes in normal and tumor groups were analyzed and box plots were drawn. Correlation among candidate genes was analyzed and heatmap was drawn.

Classification of molecular subtypes and construction of prognostic model

Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), an efficient dimensionality reduction method, was applied for cluster analysis (32). Subsequently, survival analysis was conducted on the clustered samples using the “survival” package. The single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was utilized to explore variations in immune-related functions and immune cell infiltration among the clusters.

Differential expression analysis was subsequently applied to the clustered samples to identify differential genes. Clinical data were integrated with the expression profiles of the differential genes to identify tumor samples with a survival period exceeding 30 days. Univariate regression analysis was conducted on the differential genes using the “survival” package to identify prognostic candidate genes (Table S3). To prevent model overfitting, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was applied to the candidate genes using the “glmnet” package. Cross-validation was employed to select optimal penalty parameters, aiming to eliminate highly correlated genes and reduce model complexity. Ultimately, the “survival” package was employed to conduct multivariate regression analysis on the selected prognostic candidate genes, thereby constructing the prognostic model (Table S4). The samples were categorized into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score, as shown in table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tp-2025-118-1.xls. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the “timeROC” package, and area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated. An AUC ≥0.7 was considered to have good predictive performance (33). A Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curve was constructed to perform a survival analysis of the risk model. The distributions of risk scores and survival statuses for high- and low-risk groups were visualized. Additionally, K-M curves of and heatmaps displaying the expression levels of characterized genes were generated.

Analysis of function enrichment and establishment of nomogram

GSEA v4.3.2 software was utilized to perform the functional enrichment analysis for the high- and low-risk groups. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted to explore the biological processes associated with the signature genes. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the correlation between risk scores and clinical characteristics of AL patients, and the corresponding forest plots were constructed. We summarised the clinical characteristics of the patients for the construction of the nomogram, and the clinical characteristics of the included patients included age, gender, WBC, stage, and key prognostic indicators such as OS (table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tp-2025-118-2.xls). The “rms” package was utilized to develop a nomogram to predict patient survival at 1, 3, and 5 years. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) were then utilized to validate the predictive accuracy of the nomogram.

Analysis of immune infiltration and identification of immunotherapy efficacy

ssGSEA analysis was conducted on the high- and low-risk groups using the “GSVA” packages to generate box plots of immune cell and immune function scores. Immune infiltration levels in the high- and low-risk groups were calculated using the microenvironment cell populations-counter (MCPcounter) algorithm, while immune checkpoint expression was quantified and presented in box plots. Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) expression was quantified in the high- and low-risk groups, and box plots were generated.

Prediction of drug sensitivity

To identify additional potential targets and effective therapeutic agents, the CellMiner database (https://discover.nci.nih.gov/cellminer/) was employed to screen antitumor agents significantly associated with prognostic genes. The “pRRophetic” package was employed to predict the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of various pharmaceutical agents in high- and low-risk groups. A lower IC50 value indicates higher drug efficacy in tumor treatment.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock4 v4.2.6 software to identify the optimal docking models. We utilized the DGIdb database (https://dgidb.genome.wustl.edu/) to predict drugs associated with the characteristic genes, aiming to identify potential small-molecule drugs targeting these genes. Three-dimensional structures predicted by AlphaFold were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (https://www.rcsb.org/), while the structures of small-molecule drugs were downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The interactions between proteins and small molecules were visualized using Pymol v4.6.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

The peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), 6T-CEM, and Nalm-6 cell lines were obtained from Pnuose Bio. Reverse transcription was carried out using the GeniuSaript III Select RT Kit for qPCR (Youji, China). For qRT-PCR, the uGreener Fex qPCR 2X MiX was employed. The expression levels of the target genes were normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The sequences are provided in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.1) and its associated software packages. Differences between the two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Survival outcomes were compared by generating K-M curves and statistical significance was determined by log-rank test. The “timeROC” package was used to construct ROC curves. Data visualization was done mainly with the help of “ggplot2” package. The relationship between two variables was tested by Spearman’s correlation analysis and Pearson’s correlation analysis. The median was used to define the cut-off value for the subgroups. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. P value sizes were as follows: ***, P<0.001; **, P<0.01; *, P<0.05; and ns, no significant difference (P>0.05).

Results

Identification of prognostic candidate genes related to CRGs

To identify prognostic genes related to CRGs, we first performed differential expression analysis between normal and tumor tissues in pediatric AL, result in the identification of 7,145 DEGs, visualized in a volcano plot (Figure 1A). We then intersected these DEGs with the CRGs, identifying 80 DECRGs (Figure 1B). Univariate regression analysis identified 11 CRGs-associated prognostic candidate genes (Figure 1C). Correlation analysis revealed significant relationship among the expression levels of these candidate genes (Figure 1D). Furthermore, expression analysis between normal and tumor tissues (Figure 1E) demonstrated that APBB1IP, P2RX1, RASGRP1, C5, F13A1, GP9, and ADCY9 were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues, whereas CFHR4, PROS1, FCGR2A, and ITGB3 were significantly downregulated.

Identification of molecular subtypes based on DECRGs

To gain deeper insights into CRGs, we conducted NMF clustering based on the expression matrix of DECRGs. Three clusters were identified as the optimal solution (Figure 2A), followed by heatmap analysis. The results in Figure 2B reveal substantial differences across the molecular subtypes. To assess the influence of these molecular subtypes on childhood AL prognosis, we conducted survival and immune infiltration analyses for each subtype. K-M survival analysis (Figure 2C) indicated that patients in subtype 3 exhibited a lower survival rate. Notably, the immune cell infiltration heatmap (Figure 2D) showed that subtype 3 had a higher degree of immune cell infiltration. Further analysis of immune checkpoint-related gene expression revealed that TNFSF13B, BKT, BTLA, CD40, ICOSLG, LAIR1, TNFSF9, and TNFSF4 were more highly expressed in subtype 1, CD244, ICOS, TNFSF14, and VTCN1 were predominantly expressed in subtype 2, while CD27, CD28, CD40LG, LAG3, TNFRSF18, and TIGIT were more highly expressed in subtype 3 (Figure 2E).

Construction and evaluation of prognostic models

To investigate the relationship between molecular subtypes and the clinical characteristics of AL patients, we analyzed the intersection of genes from the three molecular subtypes (|log2FC| >0.585, FDR <0.05), yielding 15 common genes (Figure 3A). Univariate regression analysis identified five candidate genes significantly associated with prognosis (Figure 3B), which were subsequently analyzed using LASSO regression analysis. As shown in Figure 3C, the gene coefficient trajectories indicate that an increase in the penalty coefficient (Logγ) reduces the number of signature genes, demonstrated by the high number of genes with a coefficient of 0. To achieve optimal predictive performance, we selected four genes with the lowest cross-validation error (Figure 3D). These genes were further analyzed using multivariate regression, culminating in the construction of a prognostic model based on four characteristic genes (Figure 3E). The risk score was calculated using the following equation, incorporating gene expression levels and regression coefficients: risk score = 0.1181 × RAB27B + 0.029 × PRDM16 + 0.057 × ErbB4 + 0.1304 × PROS1.

In the training cohort, we assessed the predictive performance of the CRGs signature for OS in AL patients using ROC curves. The AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were 0.711, 0.762, and 0.718, respectively (Figure 3F). Patients were categorized into low- and high-risk groups based on the median risk score. As shown in Figure 3G, the low-risk group demonstrated a significantly higher survival rate compared to the high-risk group. Additionally, the survival status and risk score distribution plots for the low-risk group were more favorable than those for the high-risk group (Figure 3H). To further validate the predictive capability of the signature, we obtained similar results in the validation cohort. As shown in Figure 3I, the AUC values for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival were 0.711, 0.761, and 0.86, respectively, indicating robust predictive efficiency of the prognostic model. The survival rate in the low-risk group was significantly higher than that in the high-risk group (P<0.001) (Figure 3J). Finally, the risk score distribution and survival status plots in Figure 3K showed more favorable outcomes in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group. Moreover, K-M curve analyses of the characteristic genes ErbB4 (Figure S1A), PRDM16 (Figure S1B), PROS1 (Figure S1C), and RAB27B (Figure S1D) showed that lower expression levels were significantly associated with higher OS. Additionally, the heatmap in Figure S1E demonstrated that the expression levels of the characteristic genes were higher in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group.

Functional enrichment analysis among risk groups and characteristic genes

To investigate the potential biological mechanisms underlying the high- and low-risk groups, we performed GSEA analysis. The results indicated that the low-risk group was primarily enriched in the Leishmania infection (Figure 4A), intestinal immune network for immunoglobulin A (IgA) production (Figure 4B), and PP2A AKT signaling (Figure 4C) pathways. In contrast, the high-risk group showed significant enrichment in the apoptosis (Figure 4D), porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism (Figure 4E), and calcineurin signaling (Figure 4F) pathways. Furthermore, to investigate the biological functions of the signature genes, we conducted GO enrichment analysis on these four signature genes. Figure 4G illustrates that ErbB4 may be involved in regulating immune responses and immune system processes. PRDM16 was associated with cell adhesion (Figure 4H), while PROS1 was involved in phagocytosis, regulating hydrolase activity, and the entry of symbionts into the host (Figure 4I). Figure 4J indicated that RAB27B was involved in cellular homeostasis, export from cells, and muscle organ development. Our findings elucidated the biological functions of these signature genes, offering valuable insights for the precise treatment of pediatric AL patients.

Construction and evaluation of a consolidated nomogram

Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were then performed to assess whether the risk score could serve as an independent prognostic factor. As shown in Figure 5A, univariate regression analysis indicated that the risk score was statistically significant. Similarly, multivariate regression analysis also confirmed the significance of the risk score (Figure 5B). To enhance the prediction of OS, a comprehensive nomogram was developed by integrating the risk score with clinicopathologic characteristics, including gender, WBC, age, and the WHO classification (Figure 5C). The calibration curve was then used to evaluate the accuracy of the nomogram, showing a high agreement between the predicted and the actual OS (Figure 5D). We also used the DCA curve to assess the clinical applicability of the nomogram, revealing favorable net benefits and higher predictive probabilities for 1-, 3-, and 5-year outcomes (Figure 5E). Therefore, the prognostic model based on these four signature genes serves as an independent prognostic factor.

Prediction and assessment of immune landscape based on risk score

TME significantly influences cancer progression and the efficacy of immunotherapy and drug treatments (34). To further investigate the potential relationship between the TME and the different risk groups, we performed ssGSEA analysis to assess immune cell infiltration levels in the samples. As shown in Figure 6A, the low-risk group had significantly higher infiltration levels of B cells and dendritic cells (DCs), while the infiltration levels of CD8+ T cells, mast cells, T helper cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) were significantly lower in the low-risk group. Figure 6B illustrates the differences in immune functions between the risk groups, with antigen-presenting cell (APC) costimulation and HLA function were significantly higher in the low-risk group, whereas cytolytic activity, T-cell co-inhibition, T-cell costimulation, and type II interferon (IFN) response were significantly higher in the high-risk group.

Using the MCPcounter algorithm, we found that T-cell, cytotoxic lymphocytes, NK cells, endothelial cells infiltration levels were higher in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (Figure 6C). Further analysis of immune checkpoints revealed that BTLA, ICOSLG, LAIR1, and TNFSF9 gene expression was significantly higher in the low-risk group, whereas most other immune checkpoint-related genes were significantly upregulated in the high-risk group (Figure 6D). Notably, HLA-related gene analysis showed that the expression levels of HLA-related genes were significantly higher in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group (Figure 6E). These findings provided valuable insights for the personalized treatment strategies for AL patients.

Comparison of drug sensitivity and identification of drug targets

To further explore the clinical applicability of CRGs in the personalized treatment of AL, we compared the drugs sensitivity of different risk groups by predicting IC50 values. We employed the “pRRophetic” algorithm to evaluate the IC50 values for two targeted therapies (dasatinib and midostaurin) and two common chemotherapy drugs (navitoclax and obatoclax mesylate) in AL patients. The low-risk group exhibited higher sensitivity to dasatinib (Figure 7A), midostaurin (Figure 7B), and obatoclax mesylate (Figure 7C), while the high-risk group showed higher sensitivity to navitoclax (Figure 7D).

Discussion

Although relatively rare, AL is the most common cancer in children under 15 years old, accounting for approximately 30% of all pediatric cancer cases (3,35). Epidemiological studies have identified multiple factors contributing to AL development, including genetic, infectious, and environmental influences, with ionizing radiation recognized as a significant risk factor (3,36,37). Despite advances in chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and molecular-targeted therapies, survival rates for AL patients remain suboptimal (38,39). Therefore, the identification of novel prognostic biomarkers is essential for monitoring patient outcomes and deepening our understanding of pediatric AL pathogenesis. This study presents a prognostic model for pediatric AL based on CRGs signature. Comparative analyses with clinicopathological features suggest that the risk score is an independent prognostic indicator. Moreover, K-M curve analysis revealed a significant association between the expression levels of the signature genes (RAB27B, PRDM16, ErbB4, and PROS1) and OS in pediatric AL patients.

RAB27B, a member of the Ras-like small GTPase family, plays a critical role in endocytosis, exocytosis, and vesicular transport (40,41). Recent studies have highlighted the involvement of RAB27B in various cancers, including breast cancer, bladder cancer, and glioma (42-44). In vitro studies have revealed that RAB27B expression is upregulated in AML cells, and its inhibition leads to reduced cell proliferation, cell cycle arrest, and increased apoptosis (45). Additionally, Yao et al. reported that in studies on drug resistance in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), RAB27B knockdown suppressed cell proliferation and resistance while promoting apoptosis (46). Our study revealed that patients with low RAB27B expression have significantly higher OS compared to those with high expression levels. These observation align with previous findings that inhibiting RAB27B suppresses tumor cell proliferation and significantly improves patient survival, suggesting its role as an independent prognostic predictor in childhood AL. The PRDM16, also known as MEL1, is located on human chromosome 1p36.3 and belongs to the PRDM gene family (47). PRDM16 is primarily expressed in hematopoietic stem cells, playing a vital role in hematopoiesis (48-51). Prior studies indicated that elevated PRDM16 expression was associated with poor OS in adult and pediatric AML patients (52-54). Our research similarly demonstrated that elevated PRDM16 expression correlated with lower OS, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker for childhood AL, with significant associations with treatment response and survival outcomes. ErbB4, also known as HER4, is a receptor tyrosine kinase belonging to the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) or ErbB family (55,56). It plays a vital role in the development of systems, including the cardiovascular, mammary, nervous, and endocrine systems (55,56). Activation of ErbB4 by its ligands triggers the MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways (56). Studies have associated ErbB4 expression with the progression of various cancers, including colorectal cancer (CRC) (57,58), lung cancer (59,60), and HCC (61). Furthermore, research by Huang et al. indicated that ErbB4 mitigates the damage caused by AL treatment drugs (62). PROS1, secreted by activated T cells or tumor-associated macrophages, is a recognized ligand for the Tyro3, AXL, and Mer (TAM) family of tyrosine kinase receptors (63,64). Previous research has shown that PROS1 promotes glioblastoma cell proliferation through the AXL pathway (65). Maimon et al. reported that PROS1 regulates inflammation and immune response via IL-10, thereby inhibiting metastasis in lung and breast cancer cells (66). Recent findings have revealed that PROS1 expression is significantly elevated in glioma cells, offering prognostic value and the potential to regulate the tumor immune microenvironment, which may inform novel strategies for glioma immunotherapy (67). These findings strongly align with our research, demonstrating that a risk score based on these characteristic genes serves as an independent prognostic predictor, offering strategic insights for the treatment of pediatric AL patients.

Significantly, the Leishmania infection pathway was identified through functional enrichment analysis. The coexistence of Leishmania infection and childhood AL holds important clinical research implications, as it may aid in the differential diagnosis of pediatric AL cases (68,69). Furthermore, enrichment analysis identified the intestinal immune pathway, highlighting the critical role of intestinal immunity in childhood AL. Previous studies have demonstrated that the gut microbiome undergoes substantial changes due to systemic inflammation during the treatment of childhood AL (70,71). Mucositis has been recognized as a predictive marker for bloodstream infections during febrile episodes in the treatment of pediatric AL patients (72). Chua et al. reported that interventions aimed at restoring microbial diversity can alleviate inflammation, thereby improving survival in children with advanced AL (73). Furthermore, the enrichment analysis from this study indicates that the characteristic gene ErbB4 may regulate immune response. PRDM16 is implicated in cell adhesion processes, PROS1 is associated with phagocytosis, regulation of hydrolase activity, and the entry of symbionts into the host, while RAB27B plays a role in cellular homeostasis, export from the cell, and muscle organ development. These findings provide valuable insight for the precision treatment of pediatric AL patients.

Furthermore, our analysis of characteristic genes and small-molecule drugs revealed that PROS1 can stably bind to avatrombopag and lusutrombopag. Avatrombopag is an orally active, non-peptide thrombopoietin receptor agonist that enhances platelet production by activating intracellular signaling pathways (74). Clinical studies have reported a high platelet response rate in patients with thrombocytopenia treated with avatrombopag (75,76), suggesting its potential as a therapeutic option for pediatric AL. Similarly, lusutrombopag, another thrombopoietin receptor agonist, has shown potential in liver disease research (77,78). These prior studies suggest that both drugs may hold promise for AL treatment, pointing a basis for future research. Additionally, our findings indicate that patients in the low-risk group demonstrate greater sensitivity to commonly used anticancer drugs, including dasatinib (79), midostaurin (80,81), and obatoclax mesylate (82), while patients in the high-risk group exhibit higher sensitivity to the chemotherapy drug navitoclax (83). These insights offer valuable guidance for the precise treatment of childhood AL patients, guiding personalized treatment strategies.

However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, the results of the study mainly rely on retrospective data from public databases (e.g., TCGA), which may lack sufficient clinical heterogeneity. Therefore, further validation in independent, prospective clinical cohorts is needed to enhance the reliability and clinical applicability of the results. Secondly, molecular docking and drug sensitivity analyses are currently based on computational simulations and lack experimental validation in preclinical models to confirm their potential therapeutic effects. Moreover, the present study provides a preliminary exploration of the role of CRGs in AL, but does not elucidate how CRGs, particularly PROS1, regulate the TME or its functional mechanism on AL progression. Finally, the constructed prognostic model has not been extensively validated in different paediatric patient populations, especially in rare AL subtypes, and its clinical applicability still requires confirmation through large-scale, multicentre clinical trials. In summary, the present study established the prognostic predictive capability of the CRGs signature in childhood AL, demonstrating that patients in the low-risk group have more favorable prognostic outcomes. Furthermore, we identified potential targeted drugs associated with the signature genes, providing a foundation for personalized treatment strategies in pediatric AL patients.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study employs an integrative approach combining bioinformatics and machine learning to elucidate the relationship between CRGs and pediatric AL. The development of a CRGs-based prognostic model demonstrates a significant prognostic advantage for patients classified as low-risk. Moreover, the correlation between the CRGs signature and clinicopathological features provides valuable insights for advancing precision medicine in pediatric AL. The identified associations between characteristic genes and targeted therapies further highlight the potential to improve therapeutic strategies for these patients.
