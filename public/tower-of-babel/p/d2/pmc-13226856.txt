Distal Femoral Triangle Combined With Distal Adductor Canal Block Offers Superior Analgesia and Functional Recovery After Total Knee Arthroplasty Compared to Adductor Canal Block and iPACK: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Prapairat Hemmaraj, Pathompong Choowit, Pongkrit Niamhoon, Tiraporn Khuhalerd, Suppachai Lawanaskol, Artit Laoruengthana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe distal femoral triangle block may provide better coverage of the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis compared to the adductor canal block (ACB). Furthermore, anesthetic injection into the distal ACB may spread into the popliteal fossa, potentially alleviating posterior knee pain. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the combined distal femoral triangle block and distal ACB (dFdA) for pain management following total knee arthroplasty (TKA).MethodsPatients (N = 70) were randomized to dFdA or ACB+iPACK groups. Primary outcomes were the numerical rating scale (NRS) for post-TKA pain during rest and movement, and total morphine consumption (TMC). Secondary outcomes included the timed up and go test (TUG), two-minute walk test (2MWT), active knee flexion, and five-times sit-to-stand test.ResultsThe dFdA group demonstrated significantly lower NRS for anterior knee pain during 6-48 hours and posterior knee pain during 6-18 hours post-TKA, both at rest and during movement, compared with the ACB+iPACK group. The dFdA group exhibited significantly reduced TMC at 24 ([interquartile range 4-6] vs [interquartile range 6-15], P = .003) and 48 hours (P = .022). The dFdA group had significantly superior TUG at 24 hours (59.2 vs 85.6 seconds) and 48 hours (48.3 vs 66.6 seconds), and 2MWT performance at 24 hours (17.6 vs 12.1 meters) post-TKA. Procedure time of dFdA was shorter (8.2 vs 12.7 min, P < .001).ConclusionsThe dFdA demonstrates superior analgesic efficacy and could be a good alternative approach to enhance recovery for current clinical pathways of TKA.Keywords: Distal femoral triangle block, Distal adductor canal block, Adductor canal block, Infiltration between the popliteal artery and the posterior capsule of the knee, Total knee arthroplasty

Introduction

The global trend toward shorter hospital stays following total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has heightened the importance of early functional recovery in the immediate postoperative period [1]. Optimizing analgesic approaches has emerged as a crucial element in enhancing functional outcomes and patient satisfaction [2,3]. The advent of ultrasound-guided motor-sparing nerve blocks, particularly the adductor canal block (ACB), has led to their widespread adoption within enhanced recovery after surgery protocols for TKA [4].

While the ACB effectively targets the saphenous nerve (SN) to provide analgesia to the anteromedial aspect of the knee, its coverage of the nerve to vastus medialis (NVM) remains inconsistent [5,6]. This variation arises because the NVM has been observed to pass through an independent fascial sheath, distinct from the adductor canal, below the femoral triangle [7,8]. Recent studies suggest that the distal femoral triangle block (dFTB) may offer superior coverage of the NVM, providing enhanced analgesia compared with the ACB without significantly increasing fall risk [6,9,10].

Given the complex innervation of the knee joint, a single nerve block may be insufficient for comprehensive post-TKA pain management. The posterior knee capsule's extensive sensory innervation can be addressed through motor-sparing techniques such as the infiltration between the popliteal artery and the posterior capsule of the knee (iPACK) [11]. When combined with ACB, the iPACK block has demonstrated improved outcomes, including reduced opioid consumption, enhanced range of motion, and shortened hospital stays compared to ACB alone [12]. However, the efficacy of iPACK shows considerable variation, primarily due to technical complexity and differences in physician expertise [13,14]. Notably, cadaveric studies have revealed that anesthetic injection into the distal adductor canal can reach the popliteal plexus, suggesting that the popliteal plexus block administered via the distal adductor canal (dACB) might effectively provide posterior knee analgesia [15,16].

To improve enhanced recovery after surgery for current clinical pathways of TKA, we hypothesize that a combined approach utilizing dFTB and dACB (dFdA) may offer a simplified technique for blocking the SN, NVM, and popliteal plexus. This combination may provide superior analgesic efficacy and improved functional recovery compared to the common combination of ACB and iPACK. This study aims to evaluate postoperative pain scores, morphine consumption, and functional outcomes in TKA patients receiving either dFdA or ACB+iPACK.

Material and methods

This randomized clinical trial was conducted between June 2024 and April 2025. The study enrolled consecutive patients scheduled for primary TKA. The study excluded patients with the following conditions: secondary osteoarthritis, previous knee infections, prior knee surgery, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, and known allergies to study medications, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Additionally, patients with opioid tolerance, defined as the use of ≥60 mg oral morphine equivalents daily for 7 or more days before the index surgery, were excluded. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and the trial has been prospectively registered with the registration number: TCTR20240531001. All participants provided written informed consent, and all the procedures were followed in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Of the 76 patients initially assessed for eligibility, 4 declined participation, and 2 met exclusion criteria. The remaining 70 participants were randomized into two equal groups using computer-generated blocks of 4. The study group (dFdA group, n = 35) received a combination of dFTB and dACB. The control group (n = 35) received a combination of ACB and iPACK (Fig. 1).

Standardized anesthesia

All procedures began with the completion of the surgical safety checklist. A single experienced anesthesiologist performed both the spinal anesthesia and peripheral nerve blocks. Spinal anesthesia was administered with patients in a lateral position using a paramedian approach at the L3/4 intervertebral level. The procedure utilized a 27-gauge Quincke spinal needle to deliver 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine (0.5% Marcaine, AstraZeneca, Sweden) in volumes ranging from 2.3 to 2.8 mL, with dosing individualized according to patient characteristics, and anesthetic level was checked.

Peripheral nerve block technique

Following spinal anesthesia, either dFdA or ACB+iPACK were performed under strict aseptic technique using ultrasound guidance (LOGIQ S7, GE Healthcare). All procedures utilized a 22-gauge, 80 mm insulated echogenic needle (Stimuplex Ultra 360; B. Braun Medical Inc., Melsungen, Germany). Negative blood aspiration was confirmed before all peripheral nerve blocks.

The dFdA group

The dFdA procedures were performed with patients positioned supine, with slight abduction and external rotation of the operative leg. The technique employed a lateral-to-medial in-plane approach using a linear ultrasound transducer, with hydrodissection utilized to ensure precise needle placement.

The apex of the femoral triangle was identified at the intersection of the medial borders of the sartorius and adductor longus muscles. For the dFTB, the injection site was located 1-2 cm proximal to the femoral triangle apex, where the femoral artery was visualized in a short-axis view. The SN was identified as a round hyperechoic structure lateral to the femoral artery, while the NVM located between the sartorius and vastus medialis muscles, lateral to the SN (Fig. 2a and b). Following lateral insertion of the needle relative to the femoral artery, 10 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine was administered.

Maintaining the same patient position, the popliteal plexus block was performed via the distal third of the thigh which we defined it as the distal adductor canal block (dACB). The ultrasound probe was advanced distally along the femoral artery until its most distal segment was identified, immediately proximal to its transition into the popliteal artery at the adductor hiatus (Fig. 2c). A volume of 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine was injected anteromedial to the femoral artery (approximately 10 o'clock position). Successful targeting was confirmed by observing circumferential spread of local anesthetic around the femoral artery, producing a characteristic “silhouette sign” defined by the loss of normal vascular border definition and visible arterial compression during injection.

The control group

Patients were positioned supine with slight thigh abduction and external rotation. The ACB was administered between the apex of the femoral triangle and the distal adductor canal, with 15 mL of 0.33% bupivacaine delivered superolateral to the femoral artery. Subsequently, patients were repositioned semiprone for the iPACK. A convex ultrasound transducer was placed at the popliteal fossa crease to enable visualization of the tibial nerve, common peroneal nerve, popliteal artery, and femoral condyles. The transducer was then slid proximally until identifying the flat posterior femoral shaft. Using an in-plane approach from the medial side and avoiding the tibial and common peroneal nerves, the needle was advanced toward the space between the popliteal artery and femur, where 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine was administered.

Surgical technique

Prior to surgical initiation, anesthetic block efficacy was verified. Preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis and intravenous administration of dexamethasone (8 mg) and tranexamic acid (500 mg) were administered prior to surgical initiation. Two experienced surgeons, blinded to group allocation, performed all procedures. The surgical approach utilized a medial parapatellar arthrotomy under tourniquet pressure of 300 mmHg. Following cruciate ligament excision, conventional instrumentation guided the bone cuts: an intramedullary guide for the distal femur and an extramedullary guide for the proximal tibia. The anterior-referencing technique facilitated the anterior-posterior femoral cut. Soft tissue release was performed as needed to achieve appropriate flexion-extension gap balance. All patients received cemented posterior-stabilized, fixed-bearing knee prostheses (NexGen LPS, Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) without resurfacing of the patella. A vacuum drain was placed before closure of the arthrotomy by 1 surgeon, but no drain by another surgeon, with subsequent drain and dressing removal at 24 hours postoperatively.

Postoperative management

All patients received standardized postoperative care and training on patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) device usage. Intravenous PCA delivered on-demand 2 mg morphine sulfate boluses with a 5-minute lockout period, without baseline infusion. Morphine consumption was documented at 24 and 48 hours postoperatively. The multimodal analgesia protocol included intravenous parecoxib 40 mg (Dynastat, Pfizer) every 12 hours, oral acetaminophen 500 mg every 6 hours, and oral orphenadrine 100 mg twice daily. Rehabilitation commenced on postoperative day 1, emphasizing knee range of motion and progressive weight-bearing ambulation with assistive devices. Deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis consisted of ankle pumping exercises and oral apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily for 14 days.

Outcome assessment

Primary outcome measures included pain intensity assessment using the numerical rating scale (NRS) during rest and movement. Secondary endpoints encompassed total morphine consumption (TMC) via IV-PCA and validated functional measures: active knee flexion, the timed up and go (TUG) test, two-minute walk test (2MWT), and five-times sit-to-stand test (FTSST), along with length of hospital stay [17]. Additional assessments included postoperative nausea and vomiting scores, patient satisfaction scores, and documentation of anesthesia-related complications. Blinded nurse anesthetists evaluated NRS and TMC, while blinded physical therapists assessed functional outcomes.

Statistical analyses

The study was designed as superiority trial, and sample size calculation was based on the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 1.5 for pain scores, derived from previous studies on postoperative pain after total joint arthroplasty [18]. This indicated a requirement of 34 patients per group to achieve 80% power, assuming a standard deviation of 2.2 and a significance level of 0.05. Accounting for a potential 5% data loss during follow-up, each group was adjusted to 35 patients.

Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and analyzed using Fisher's exact test. Continuous data were assessed for normality. Normally distributed variables were presented as means with standard deviations and compared using independent t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. All analyses used 95% two-sided confidence intervals, with statistical significance defined as P < .05. No post-randomization adjustments were applied.

The primary study endpoints were repeatedly measured at 6, 12, 18, 24, and 48 hours. A random-intercept mixed-effects model was prespecified to account for within-subject correlation. The independent variables included time, group, and the time-by-group interaction term. Secondary endpoints underwent Mann-Whitney U test analysis, with results visualized through box-and-whisker plots. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/BE 18.0 (StataCorp, LP, College Station, TX).

Regarding primary outcomes, independent analysis of numerical rating scale (NRS) scores revealed consistently superior pain control in the dFdA group. For anterior knee pain at rest, the dFdA group demonstrated significantly lower NRS scores across all measured time points: 6 hours (2.2 vs 3.6, P < .001), 12 hours (2.3 vs 3.7, P < .001), 18 hours (2.3 vs 3.6, P < .001), 24 hours (2.0 vs 3.3, P < .001), and 48 hours (1.5 vs 2.5, P = .026) postoperatively. Similar advantages were observed during movement, with the dFdA group reporting lower scores at 6 hours (4.1 vs 5.7, P = .015), 12 hours (4.2 vs 5.6, P < .001), 18 hours (3.7 vs 5.4, P < .001), 24 hours (3.5 vs 5.1, P < .001), and 48 hours (3.0 vs 3.9, P = .004).

Posterior knee pain assessment also favored the dFdA group, with significantly lower NRS scores at rest during the early postoperative period: 6 hours (0.8 vs 1.6, P = .004), 12 hours (0.9 vs 1.9, P < .001), and 18 hours (1.0 vs 1.6, P = .008). During movement, this advantage persisted at 6 hours (1.9 vs 2.9, P = .001), 12 hours (2.1 vs 3.0, P = .001), and 18 hours (2.2 vs 2.7, P = .016).

Multilevel Gaussian regression analysis, accounting for intrapersonal correlation and treatment-time interactions, confirmed significantly lower mean NRS differences in the dFdA group for both anterior knee pain (at rest: P < .001; during movement: P < .001) and posterior knee pain (at rest: P < .001; during movement: P = .007) (Fig. 3). Nevertheless, because preoperative pain scores showed some baseline imbalance between groups, an additional mixed-effects analysis adjusted for baseline pain score was performed. After baseline adjustment, the between-group difference in anterior knee pain during movement was attenuated, and the statistical strength was reduced, although the postoperative NRS pattern remained in favor of the dFdA group across time points (Supplementary Material 1).

For TMC, the dFdA group consumed consistently less morphine than the ACB+iPACK group in the first 24 hours (P = .003) and during 24-48 hours (P = .022) (Fig. 4).

Active knee flexion and five-times sit-to-stand test results showed no significant differences between groups (Fig. 5).

Patient satisfaction scores were significantly higher in the dFdA group (9.9 ± 0.4 vs 9.5 ± 0.7, P = .005). Neither group experienced complications related to peripheral nerve blocks or surgical procedures. Postoperative nausea and vomiting scores (0.1 ± 0.3 vs 0.1 ± 0.5, P = .782) and length of hospital stay (2.8 ± 1.1 vs 3.0 ± 1.3 days, P = .430) were comparable between the dFdA and ACB+iPACK groups.

Discussion

Effective pain control during movement is necessary for enhancing patient participation in physical therapy, facilitating early mobilization, and accelerating recovery following TKA. Our findings demonstrate that the dFdA technique provides superior pain management, with significantly lower NRS scores at rest and during movement for both anterior (6-48 hours) and posterior knee aspects (6-18 hours) compared to the ACB+iPACK approach. Although the clinical magnitude NRS differences were modest due to it did not surpass the MCID, the pain scores should be interpreted together with the opioid-sparing effect and functionalrecovery outcomes. Box plot analyses reveal a significant opioid-sparing effect with dFdA, particularly pronounced in the first 24 hours. Furthermore, the dFdA group achieved superior functional recovery, as evidenced by improved TUG test at 24 and 48 hours and enhanced 2MWT performance at 24 hours, without procedure-related complications.

Recent research has established the essential role of NVM in knee sensory innervation [6,9,10]. Its terminal branch, the medial retinacular nerve, supplies the proximal half of the medial parapatellar arthrotomy, while the SN innervates the distal half [19,20]. Cadaveric studies have demonstrated that the vastoadductor membrane forms the adductor canal's roof, with the NVM positioned external to this canal, potentially limiting ACB efficacy [5,21,22]. While femoral nerve block can effectively target the NVM, it risks compromising ambulation through motor blockade of the rectus femoris and vastus muscles. Our approach of targeting the NVM at the distal femoral triangle with 10 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine achieved superior anterior knee pain control, maintaining NRS scores below 5 during initial movement (6-12 hours) and below 4 (mild pain intensity) during 18-48 hours post-TKA. These findings align with the previous studies that reported efficacy of NVM capture using the femoral triangle block [6,23,24].

The management of posterior knee pain remains important for optimal post-TKA recovery. While Biehl et al. [25] reported that additional iPACK block significantly reduced 24-hour opioid consumption compared to ACB alone, its impact on exercise-related pain was not statistically significant. Meta-analyses support iPACK with ACB for improved range of motion and shorter hospital stays [12]. However, iPACK presents technical challenges and shows inconsistent benefits [13], as demonstrated by Chatmaitri et al. [14], who found no additive effect on pain scores or opioid use. Additionally, the iPACK block carries a risk of transient motor block of the common peroneal nerve [25].

Anatomically, the SN and genicular branches of the posterior obturator nerve traverse the distal adductor canal alongside the femoral artery, with the latter continuing into the popliteal fossa via the adductor hiatus [7,8,26]. Cadaveric studies by Runge et al. [15] and Goffin et al. [16] demonstrated that 10-20 mL of dye-mixed solution in the distal adductor canal could reach the popliteal fossa and stain relevant nerve structures. Our implementation of dACB using 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine achieved significantly better posterior knee pain control during 6-18 hours postoperative period. This technique offers procedural advantages, requiring no patient repositioning or probe adjustment following dFTB, and demonstrates safety, as confirmed by Smulders et al. [27], who showed that even 30 mL of anesthetic for the dACB did not affect the sciatic nerve.

Regarding functional outcomes, recent clinical evidence demonstrates promising results for combined nerve block approaches. Studies indicate that combining dACB with dFTB significantly reduces 24-hour opioid consumption and improves motor function as measured by the Bromage scale during the initial 3-6 hours post-TKA compared to femoral nerve block alone [28]. Sonawane et al. [29] demonstrated that patients receiving dFTB and proximal ACB with a 20/10 mL anesthetic protocol achieved sustained pain control, maintaining mean visual analog scale scores during movement ≤3 until discharge, with negligible oral morphine requirements. Their findings also revealed that the combination of low-volume dFTB (10 mL) with proximal ACB (20 mL) yielded superior mean quadriceps strength compared to the 20/10 mL regimen (3.58 vs 3.12, respectively) during 6-12 hours post-TKA, with quadriceps function approaching normal levels (grade 4-5) from 18 hours onward. This underscores the importance of optimizing anesthetic volume in peripheral nerve blocks to balance effective analgesia with preserved motor function [23]. In our study, the dFdA approach utilizing a 10/20 mL anesthetic protocol demonstrated superior functional recovery, as measured by TUG and 2MWT performance, particularly within the first 24 hours compared to ACB+iPACK. Moreover, the dFdA technique demonstrated a significantly shorter procedure time (8.2 [±2.2] vs 12.7 [±2.4] min; P < .001). Hence, this simplified dual nerve block enhances effective time management, which may be particularly meaningful in high-volume orthopedic settings to facilitate overall workflow and operative turnover.

Several limitations of our study warrant consideration. First, our study population was comprised predominantly of female patients, with a significantly higher proportion randomized to the dFdA group. While this gender distribution might raise concerns regarding outcome validity, previous research has not identified sex as a significant determinant of postoperative pain scores or functional recovery following TKA [30,31]. Second, our pain-related findings should be interpreted with caution because preoperative pain scores were not fully balanced between groups at baseline despite randomization. In particular, after adjustment for baseline pain score, the between-group difference in anterior knee pain during movement was attenuated, suggesting that this specific outcome was partly sensitive to baseline imbalance. Nevertheless, the overall postoperative NRS pattern remained favorable to the dFdA group across time points. Third, despite consistent technique by a single experienced anesthesiologist, ultrasound visualization of the NVM proved challenging in some cases. Future investigations incorporating nerve stimulator may enhance both procedural precision and safety regarding NVM targeting. Fourth, while our results demonstrated enhanced functional recovery from 24 hours onward in the dFdA group, we did not assess quadriceps strength during the immediate postoperative period. Finally, the outcomes may partially reflect individual technical expertise and single-operator proficiency, potentially limiting the broader generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

The dFdA demonstrates superior analgesic efficacy for both anterior and posterior knee pain compared to ACB+iPACK, while facilitating enhanced early functional recovery following TKA. These findings suggest that the dFdA could be a good alternative approach to conventional combination of ACB+iPACK for enhancing recovery in current clinical pathways of TKA.
