Graphicalized vision-language modeling for comprehensive lung nodule analysis and risk stratification
Authors: Danwen Zhao, Junfeng Xi, Xun Guo, Jintao Chai, Zhengshui Xu, Liang Li, Yan Xue, Qingyu Sun, Yinggang Zheng, Shiyuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer care involves coupled tasks such as precise nodule detection, patient-level survival risk estimation, and nodule count quantification, typically handled by separate systems despite clear interdependence. We present VITALIS, a multimodal vision-language framework that fuses CT and PET/CT imaging with structured radiology text using a graph-aware Transformer: Laplacian diffusion enriches token features on an image-text graph, while structural and prior-guided attention focus computation on anatomically and clinically related contexts, followed by bidirectional image-text conditioning to form a fused patient representation. This representation parameterizes a continuous-time latent risk process governed by a context-modulated Neural ODE, enabling individualized continuous-time modeling of time-to-event risk. Task-specific heads decode the latent trajectory into nodule detection, nodule malignancy classification, survival risk estimation, and nodule count prediction. Evaluated on three public cohorts, the framework delivers accurate delineations, low-false-positive localization, calibrated survival risk estimates, and consistent nodule counts across tasks. These findings indicate that coupling graph-aware multimodal encoding with continuous-time latent dynamics provides a coherent basis for integrated diagnostic and prognostic modeling in lung cancer.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Lung cancer diagnosis and management require several critical tasks, including the detection of suspicious pulmonary nodules, prognostic survival prediction for patients, and estimation of nodule count as an index of nodule burden. Precise delineation of nodule boundaries is essential for treatment planning and disease monitoring1,2, while early nodule detection via low-dose CT screening has been shown to significantly reduce lung cancer mortality3. In parallel, reliable survival prediction can inform personalized therapy and follow-up intensity, and quantifying the number of nodules aids in risk stratification and staging. VITALIS is proposed as a unified multimodal framework to address these needs by concurrently performing lung nodule detection, malignancy classification, survival risk estimation, and nodule count prediction within a single model. This integrated approach is motivated by the potential to streamline clinical workflows: enabling comprehensive analysis of imaging and clinical data in one pass, and to exploit synergies between tasks.

Several recent studies have explored advanced deep learning methods for lung nodule detection and nodule segmentation. Zhou et al.1 introduced a multitask connected U-Net with a teacher-student architecture that integrates PET and CT data to improve automated lung nodule segmentation, leveraging PET-derived knowledge to guide more precise CT-based contouring. Cai et al.4 developed MSDet, a multiscale attention network with an extended receptive field and specialized modules for tiny-objects to improve the detection of very small pulmonary nodules on CT scans. Tang et al.5 proposed an anchor-free detector called Circle-YOLO, which represents lung nodules with bounding circles and employs a novel attention mechanism to localize nodules without preset anchor boxes. Niu et al.3 presented a large-scale foundation model (M3FM) for lung cancer screening that uses a synergistic multimodal, multitask transformer architecture to handle multiple screening tasks end-to-end.

Other works have focused on prognostic modeling and multimodal data integration for lung cancer outcomes. Mikhael et al.6 developed the Sybil model, a deep learning approach that analyzes a single low-dose CT scan without any manual annotations or added clinical data to predict an individual’s 1–6 year lung cancer risk. Kanakarajan et al.7 built an ensemble predictive model that combines clinical variables, engineered radiomic features, and deep learning representations from CT images to estimate overall survival in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients. Zhang et al.8 applied deep learning to histopathology slides, using contrastive clustering on H&E-stained tissue images to discover prognostic morphologic phenotypes and predict patient outcomes in small cell lung cancer. Salmanpour et al.9 proposed a semi-supervised framework for lung cancer survival prediction that leverages pseudo-labeling and diverse multicenter PET/CT datasets to improve model generalization in outcome prediction. Liao et al.10 integrated advanced intratumoral and peritumoral radiomic features with clinical parameters in a DeepSurv model to personalize immunotherapy response prediction for lung cancer patients, demonstrating the value of combined imaging and clinical data for outcome modeling. Amaro et al.11 explored convolutional neural network approaches using CT scans to extract prognostic information, comparing multiple CNN architectures for their efficacy in predicting survival of lung cancer patients12.

Each of these studies addresses a piece of the lung cancer prediction puzzle in detection, or risk prediction, and underscores the need for a comprehensive system like VITALIS that can tackle all these aspects together.

Lung nodule detection

The performance of the model was quantified using the standard object detection metrics of Precision (positive predictive value) and Recall (sensitivity). The F1-score (F1) is reported as the harmonic mean of these two, providing a single, balanced measure of overall detection accuracy. And the average number of false positives per scan (FP/s) is included as the primary metric for clinical viability.

All detection results reported correspond to a confidence threshold of 0.5 for classifying a candidate as a nodule. This threshold was chosen on each dataset’s validation set to maximize the F1-score, thereby balancing sensitivity and precision for optimal low-false-positive performance. We found the model’s performance to be robust with respect to this choice: varying the threshold by ±0.1 yielded only minor changes in precision/recall (on the order of 1–2%) and did not alter the relative ranking of methods. This insensitivity indicates that our reported low false-positive rates are not a tuning artifact but rather an inherent strength of the VITALIS detector.

As detailed in Table 1, the VITALIS framework unequivocally outperforms all competing models on both benchmark datasets: LIDC-IDRI cohort and Lung-PET-CT-Dx cohort.

Survival risk prediction

For the task of 2-year lung cancer survival risk prediction, we evaluate model performance using both discrimination and calibration metrics. Discriminative ability is quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the concordance index (C-index), which both measure how well the model’s risk scores differentiate patients with different outcomes. Calibration and overall predictive accuracy are assessed via the Brier score (mean squared error of probability forecasts; here reported as a percentage, with lower values indicating more accurate, calibrated risk predictions). In addition to these continuous measures, we examine binary classification performance at the clinically relevant 2-year survival threshold: specifically, we compute the precision, recall (sensitivity), and F1-score for classifying patients as having survived at least 2 years or not, based on the risk estimate.

Table 2 presents the survival prediction results on the ACRIN 6668 dataset. VITALIS achieves an AUC of about 0.869 and a C-index of 0.860, which are the highest among the methods compared. In particular, the AUC is roughly 1.7–1.9 points higher in absolute terms than the strongest prior model (which attains ~0.85), indicating a meaningful improvement in the model’s ability to distinguish high-risk versus low-risk patients. This suggests that the integration of multimodal imaging and clinical text by VITALIS yields more informative risk stratification than models using imaging or clinical data alone.

VITALIS has the lowest Brier score, outperforming competing approaches, which often have Brier scores in the 16–19 range. This lower Brier score signifies that the probabilistic risk estimates from VITALIS are better calibrated and more accurate on an absolute scale, reflecting improved reliability in its predictions. From a clinical decision point of view, this means that VITALIS not only separates patients by outcome more effectively, but also provides risk probabilities that better reflect true outcome frequencies.

At the binary 2-year survival endpoint, VITALIS likewise achieves superior results. It achieves an F1-score of about 0.81. Both precision and recall for the positive class are improved relative to alternatives, indicating that VITALIS catches a greater portion of truly high-risk patients (few false negatives) while also making fewer incorrect high-risk predictions (few false positives). This balanced improvement in precision and recall underscores the model’s practical utility: it can identify those at risk of 2-year mortality more accurately, which is crucial for guiding timely interventions, without unduly overestimating risk for lower-risk patients. In summary, the risk prediction results demonstrate that VITALIS provides a markedly better combination of discrimination and calibration than existing methods. The model’s use of longitudinal latent dynamics and multimodal data appears to capture subtle prognostic patterns that enable more precise survival risk estimation.

Trajectory plausibility

To verify that the learned continuous-time latent representations genuinely reflect physiological disease progression, we evaluated trajectory plausibility on the ACRIN 6668 paired-scan subset. For VITALIS, we compared the Neural ODE-predicted latent state at the follow-up time against the actual encoded post-treatment scan. For static baseline models, we approximated future states using linear feature extrapolation. To comprehensively assess representation fidelity, we utilized three complementary topological metrics: Cosine Similarity evaluates the semantic alignment based on the direction the latent vectors point, invariant to their magnitude; Normalized ℓ2 distance quantifies the absolute spatial proximity and structural dissimilarity within the representation manifold; and Directional Agreement captures the angular consistency between the inferred and true progression velocities, ensuring the modeled path correctly tracks actual biological changes. As illustrated in Fig. 1. VITALIS effectively captures longitudinal disease dynamics, maintaining high directional agreement and semantic consistency (high cosine similarity, low normalized ℓ2 distance). In contrast, the representations of static baseline models exhibit severe semantic drift over time, confirming the necessity of explicit continuous-time dynamics modeling.

Malignancy classification

To ensure rigorous validation and direct comparability with contemporary state-of-the-art literature, we utilized the LIDC-IDRI dataset for all malignancy classification experiments. The LIDC-IDRI dataset provides malignancy ratings from four blinded radiologists on an ordinal scale of 1–5. Benign (Negative Class): Nodules with an average malignancy rating <3 (combining “Highly Unlikely” and “Moderately Unlikely”). Malignant (Positive Class): Nodules with an average malignancy rating >3 (combining “Moderately Suspicious” and “Highly Suspicious”). Exclusion Criteria: Nodules with an indeterminate average rating of exactly 3 were excluded to minimize label noise and ensure the model learns robust, definitive features of malignancy. This rigorous partitioning ensures that all nodules belonging to a single patient are strictly segregated into either the training or the testing fold, thereby eliminating the risk of data leakage via patient-specific imaging artifacts.

The VITALIS framework achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.982 and an overall Accuracy of 97.85%, as shown in Table 3, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art holder, MVSA-CNN, by margins of 0.7% and 0.75%, respectively. While these gains testify to the model’s overall robustness, the most clinically significant improvements are observed in Sensitivity (97.12%) and Specificity (98.24%).

Lung nodule count estimation

For nodule count estimation, the model predicts the total number of nodules present in a scan. We report the mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) to quantify the average and worst-case magnitude of counting errors. We also compute the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) and coefficient of determination (R2) between the predicted and true nodule counts, as measures of how well the model’s counts track the ground truth variability. The mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) is provided to contextualize the error relative to true count values. Additionally, we examine the bias (mean error) to see if the model has any systematic tendency to over- or under-count nodules (bias near 0 is ideal). Finally, we include Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), which combines precision and accuracy to evaluate how well the predictions conform to the identity line.

As reported in Table 4, VITALIS delivers the most accurate nodule count estimates among all methods tested on the LIDC-IDRI dataset. Our model achieves an MAE of 0.78 nodules and RMSE of 1.03, which are substantially lower than those of prior approaches. In terms of correlation with ground truth counts, VITALIS achieves an almost perfect Pearson r ≈ 0.96 (with R2 ≈ 0.92), indicating that predicted counts track true counts extremely closely throughout the dataset.

Ablation study

Discussion

In this work, we propose VITALIS, a multimodal deep learning framework that integrates four core tasks of lung cancer care, including nodule detection, survival risk prediction, and nodule count estimation. Using the architectural construct and multimodality of VITALIS provides learning synergism for all tasks. A shared feature encoder (derived from image and clinical inputs) is associated with task-specific prediction heads to enable shared nodule features to produce different outcomes. The detection elements of the model learn in-depth features of nodules and masses from radiography and give informative labels that are also relevant for the survival prediction head. This multitask architecture not only expedites (diagnosis and prognosis can be generated in one model), but it also helps to promote data transfer between tasks so that overall robustness is better. Training on varied data modalities—say, medical image together with patient metadata or textual reports VITALIS takes advantage of complementary data that single-modality models may miss. Clinical context (e.g., patient demographics or pathology findings, if available), together with imaging data, enables the model to enhance risk assessments and count estimates by accounting for a better, more comprehensive view of the disease condition.

Methodologically, VITALIS shows the possibility of a model that uses a single predictor for lung cancer. Our model can solve practical concerns in the sense that it generates nodule detection and patient-level risk indices within the same framework, obviating the need for a separate system. Joint training of these objectives is used as a regularization; it needs to discover latent representations that can provide detection of visible lesions and prediction of clinical outcomes, thus leading to more generalized feature learning. Based on the performance metrics and experimental results we provide for specific tasks, this ensemble strategy can provide more stable and reliable results for tasks. Instruments are even now available to count the detected nodules within a particular network for quantification of nodule burden, and this may be useful in clinic-based staging and for the prediction of disease progression over time.

In conclusion, VITALIS contributes to advancing multimodal modeling for comprehensive lung cancer analysis. Its multimodal, multitask design embodies a shift from task-specific algorithms to an integrated decision-support system. Such a system can potentially improve efficiency (by consolidating inferencing) and consistency (by using the same underlying data representation for all predictions) in clinical practice. While careful prospective validation and refinement are needed before deployment, especially to ensure generalization across different institutions and imaging protocols, the present study’s findings underscore the viability of a unified model addressing both diagnostic and prognostic aspects of lung cancer. A limitation is the interpretability of intermediate continuous-time latent states when longitudinal ground truth is sparse. The Ergodic Progression Hypothesis (EPH) is used as a modeling viewpoint to connect cohort heterogeneity with within-patient progression.

Methods

We introduce VITALIS (Variational Graph-Attentive Transformer with Latent ODEs), shown in Fig. 5, a multimodal vision language model for lung cancer prediction. The architecture consists of three components : (1) a context encoder that processes imaging and textual inputs (CT scans, PET scans, and structured radiology annotations) with a graph-aware Transformer, producing a fused patient context vector c; (2) a continuous-time latent dynamics module (Neural ODE17) that evolves a personalized latent state z(t) under an ODE defined by the context c; and (3) multitask observation heads that map the latent trajectory z(t) to clinically relevant outputs (nodule detection, malignancy classification, risk estimation, and nodule count). The entire model is trained end-to-end with the adjoint sensitivity method for the ODE, incorporating spectral regularization.

Context encoder: graph-aware vision-language transformer

The graph-aware vision-language context encoder (Fig. 6) fuses multimodal inputs (CT, PET, and radiology text) into a patient context vector c. Visual and textual tokens are first extracted by pre-trained backbones, then refined via graph diffusion and biased self-attention with structural and domain priors, followed by bidirectional image-language conditioning and pooling to yield c. We extract visual features from the volumetric CT and PET scans using a pre-trained vision transformer backbone (a Swin Transformer18). Each scan is represented as a sequence of patch embeddings (flattened 2D image patches per slice or 3D subvolumes) with positional encodings indicating spatial location. In parallel, we encode the structured radiology text (lesion attributes, radiologist annotations) into token embeddings (BioBERT19). These image and text tokens together form the input sequence for the context Transformer.

a Context-conditioned Neural ODE17 governing the continuous-time latent trajectory; b graph-aware vision-language encoder and interpretability tools; c multitask heads for detection, malignancy, risk, and nodule count; d optional continuous-time event model for survival outcomes.

While the input context c typically originates from a single time-point (static CT/PET), we model z(t) as a continuous-time latent risk-state trajectory learned from time-to-event supervision. We use the EPH as a modeling viewpoint linking cross-sectional heterogeneity in a cohort to within-patient progression20–22. Under this view, individuals occupy different regions of a shared latent manifold, and the context-modulated ODE parameterization provides a smooth evolution on this manifold that supports evaluating risk at arbitrary future times t. We interpret intermediate z(t) values as latent states rather than direct measurements of tumor growth.

The vector field fθ parameterizes cohort-conditioned latent evolution of the risk state. This learning is driven by the survival objective function on the ACRIN 6668 dataset, where the variable t corresponds to physical time (months to event). Specifically, the survival probability S(t) is modeled as a function of the latent state z(t). Consequently, the ODE solver extrapolates the patient’s current state z(0) into the future, predicting the path z(t) along the disease manifold.

For Prognosis: z(T) represents the predicted patient status at future time T. The optimization aligns the trajectory such that high-risk phenotypes exhibit faster latent velocities toward mortality-associated regions of the state space. For Detection: The integration represents an adaptive, continuous-depth feature refinement. Here, time acts as a proxy for computational complexity, allowing the network to perform iterative refinement of the boundaries, akin to level-set methods in image processing.

We integrate this ODE using an adaptive solver (Dormand-Prince Runge-Kutta23) to obtain z(t) over the time horizon of interest. The adaptive step-size control ensures accuracy and efficiency, and under mild Lipschitz conditions on fθ, the ODE has a unique, stable solution for each patient. We denote the collection of latent states over time as Z(⋅) = z(t) : t ≥ 0.

We impose stability and identifiability constraints on the ODE. In particular, we penalize the local Jacobian spectral norm ∥Jf(z, t)∥ of the vector field to enforce a Lipschitz condition, preventing explosive dynamics. This spectral regularization encourages smooth, physiologically plausible trajectories and improves extrapolation. We also cap the number of function evaluations (NFE) of the ODE solver by tuning error tolerances, effectively limiting stiffness and controlling computational cost. These constraints ensure the latent process z(t) remains well-behaved and interpretable as an underlying disease trajectory.

Multitask observation models

The latent trajectory z(t) feeds into multiple output heads designed to predict relevant clinical outcomes. We couple z(t) to each task with appropriate decoding functions, as follows.

Nodule detection

We use a Swin-Transformer-based18 detection head to identify lung nodules in the CT images. A fixed set of learned object query embeddings attends to the visual tokens (and indirectly to z(0)) to produce a set of predicted bounding boxes with confidence scores. Each predicted box bj comes with a class label indicating “nodule” vs. “no object”; in our case, we extend the classes to encode malignancy (benign vs malignant nodule, see next point). The detection head thus outputs a set B=(bj,y^j) of up to M detected nodules with their coordinates and predicted class.

By formulating detection as a direct set prediction problem with a global loss and bipartite matching to ground truth, our model can localize nodules of varying sizes without requiring hand-crafted proposals. The detection loss Ldet combines a localization error (IoU loss for box coordinates) and a classification loss for nodule presence/absence per query.

Malignancy classification

For each detected nodule, the model predicts its malignancy probability. Concretely, the detection head uses two object classes ("benign nodule" vs. “malignant nodule”) and outputs, for each detected nodule j, a malignancy probability p^mal,j∈(0,1) from the Swin-Transformer18 decoder via a sigmoid (or softmax) layer. Let ymal,j ∈ {0, 1} denote the ground-truth malignancy label for nodule j (1 = malignant, 0 = benign). We supervise this prediction with a binary cross-entropy loss

where J denotes the set of matched nodule predictions. This term is added to the overall detection loss alongside the localization and objectness components.

In addition, patient-level malignancy scores can be derived by aggregating nodule-level predictions (taking maxjp^mal,j over all detected nodules or by feeding p^mal,jj into a small fusion network). However, our primary formulation operates at the nodule level.

End-to-end training via adjoint method

The model is optimized end-to-end using a composite objective that combines multiple predictive tasks and regularization terms. A core computational challenge lies in propagating gradients through the continuous-time dynamics, as the latent trajectory z(t) is defined implicitly by numerical integration.

To address this, we employ the adjoint sensitivity method from Neural ODEs17, which enables efficient gradient computation without retaining all intermediate solver states. This involves introducing an auxiliary variable, the adjoint state a(t) = ∂L/∂z(t), whose dynamics are solved backward in time alongside the original ODE:

The resulting backward integration provides gradients both for the initial latent state and for all parameters Θ.

Practically, this yields constant-memory training and numerically consistent gradients governed by the same solver tolerance as the forward pass. We further verify gradient accuracy and stability by monitoring solver error estimates during optimization.

This formulation prevents the backpropagation of erroneous gradients from missing labels and allows the shared representation to benefit from the distinct supervisory signals of all datasets simultaneously, promoting robust generalization.

In our joint training paradigm, all tasks share a single unified model backbone and are optimized together despite drawing training data from separate cohorts. We implement a cross-dataset batching strategy wherein mini-batches are alternately sampled from each dataset, and only the loss terms for which a given batch has ground-truth annotations are applied. In practice, this means that when a batch comes from LIDC-IDRI or Lung-PET-CT-Dx, the detection and malignancy classification losses (Ldet, Lmal) are computed, whereas the survival risk loss (Lrisk) is omitted for that batch; conversely, batches from ACRIN 6668 trigger Lrisk but not Ldet or Lmal. All model parameters from the shared encoders and graph-based Transformer through the Neural ODE are jointly learned across these interleaved batches. This missing-label masking approach ensures that each task contributes to the training signal only when relevant labels are present, while still allowing shared representations and parameter sharing across tasks. By cycling through datasets during training, VITALIS effectively learns from all cohorts within one composite objective, upholding the single model paradigm and avoiding any need to train separate task-specific models.

Dataset

Two separate cohorts were utilized for the development of detection and diagnostic models. These datasets were maintained separately due to fundamental differences in their ground truth data and imaging modalities shown in Table 7.

The Lung Image Database Consortium (LIDC-IDRI) Cohort

The LIDC-IDRI24 collection is a foundational resource for the development of computed tomography (CT) lung nodules analysis algorithms. It comprises 1018 thoracic CT scans, each associated with an XML file detailing the diagnostic findings. The primary objective of the collection was to establish a comprehensive reference database for the evaluation of algorithms related to lung nodule detection and classification. The annotation protocol is a distinguishing feature of this dataset. It was generated through a two-phase process where four experienced thoracic radiologists independently reviewed each scan. In the first phase, each radiologist marked lesions they identified as nodules ≥3 mm, nodules <3 mm, or non-nodules ≥3 mm. In the second phase, for all lesions marked as nodulea ≥3 mm by at least one radiologist, all four radiologists independently provided a full, precise segmentation by outlining the nodule’s contour. This protocol does not yield a single, binary ground truth segmentation mask. Instead, it provides four independent, expert-level contours for each nodule. This data structure is exceptionally well-suited for the segmentation task, as it allows an algorithm’s performance to be evaluated not against an arbitrary single outline, but against the consensus (or variance) of a panel of experts, thereby providing a built-in measure of the problem’s inherent ambiguity.

The Large-Scale CT and PET/CT Dataset for Lung Cancer Diagnosis (LUNG-PET-CT-DX) Cohort

The LUNG-PET-CT-DX25 collection is a large-scale, multimodal dataset specifically designed for the development of diagnostic algorithms. The cohort consists of CT and Positron Emission Tomography (PT) (PET/CT) DICOM images from 355 subjects who were retrospectively identified as being suspected of having lung cancer and had subsequently undergone standard-of-care lung biopsy and PET/CT imaging.

The primary ground truth for this dataset is the definitive histopathological diagnosis of the tissue, which was used to group the subjects. This diagnostic information is encoded within the patient identifiers, where: “A” denotes Adenocarcinoma, “B” denotes Small Cell Carcinoma, “E” denotes Large Cell Carcinoma, and “G” denotes Squamous Cell Carcinoma.

In addition to the histopathological ground truth, the dataset includes XML annotation files in the PASCAL VOC format. These annotations, created and verified by five academic thoracic radiologists, indicate nodule location using bounding boxes rather than precise contours.

The ACRIN-NSCLC-FDG-PET Cohort: The ACRIN-NSCLC-FDG-PET26 collection (also known as ACRIN 6668) is a multicenter dataset designed for the development of prognostic models. The cohort contains data from 242 subjects with locally advanced NSCLC who were part of a clinical trial investigating therapy response. The primary objective was to determine if FDG-PET imaging could predict long-term clinical outcomes. A key feature of this dataset is its longitudinal protocol, which includes baseline (pre-treatment) and post-treatment whole body FDG-PET/CT scans. The primary ground truth is the definitive clinical outcome data, including patient survival and nodule response.

Training implementation details

We initialized the vision and text encoders from large-scale pre-trained models (an ImageNet-pre-trained Swin-Transformer18 for imaging and BioBERT19 for textual reports) to leverage the representational richness of foundation models. To enable the use of the pre-trained BioBERT text encoder with the structured annotations available in LIDC-IDRI and ACRIN 6668, we developed a Template-Based Serialization Protocol. This process transforms discrete radiological and clinical attributes into natural language descriptions, projecting them into the semantic embedding space of the language model.

Model optimization was carried out in two stages.

In the first stage, we froze the latent ODE module and trained the context encoder together with the task-specific output heads on a large and diverse corpus. This phase aimed to establish strong multimodal feature representations and cross-modal alignments. The initial latent state, z(0) = ginit(c), acted as a learnable summary embedding, without yet evolving through continuous-time dynamics.

In the second stage, we jointly optimized all components by unfreezing the ODE parameters fθ and the hypernetwork ϕ, allowing the latent dynamics to adapt to time-dependent clinical signals such as disease progression or outcome trajectories. During training, we monitored validation metrics for each task, including detection precision, classification AUC, and risk calibration, and tuned the ODE solver tolerances to balance numerical fidelity and computational efficiency.

Our framework constitutes a coherent multimodal foundation model that integrates medical images and text, simulates disease evolution in continuous time, and jointly predicts multiple lung cancer-related outcomes. By embedding domain graphs and clinical priors into a graph-informed Transformer encoder, and training the latent dynamics using mathematically principled tools such as adjoint gradient computation and spectral regularization, the model achieves robust and interpretable performance.

Data Availability Statement

All datasets used in this study are publicly accessible: LIDC-IDRI: https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/collection/lidc-idri/; LUNG-PET-CT-DX: https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/collection/lung-pet-ct-dx/; ACRIN-NSCLC-FDG-PET (ACRIN 6668): https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/collection/acrin-nsclc-fdg-pet/; NLST New-lesion LongCT: https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/analysis-result/nlst-new-lesion-longct/.

The source code supporting the findings of this study, including the VITALIS model architecture, training protocols, and inference scripts, is available for review at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/VITALIS-5E6A. The repository contains the complete implementation of the graph-aware multimodal fusion, neural ODE dynamics, and the multi-task learning framework described in the Methods section.
