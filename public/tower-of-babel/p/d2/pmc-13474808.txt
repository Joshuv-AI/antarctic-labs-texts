Emergency management of cerebral air gas embolism as a complication of CT-guided lung biopsy
Authors: Vinayak Sharma Ramdhun, Caryl E Richards, Zaid Hussain Khan, Abdurrahmaan Manga, Rachel Marsh, Rajini Sudhir, Bruce Mathew, Indrajeet Das, Amrita Bajaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral arterial gas embolism is a rare complication of percutaneous CT-guided lung biopsy, associated with seizures and neurological deficits such as stroke. Failure to promptly recognize and urgently treat can lead to a very poor prognosis. This case describes an 80-year-old woman undergoing a CT-guided lung biopsy for histological assessment of a suspected malignant cavitating lung lesion. Shortly after the procedure, the patient developed complete left-sided hemiparesis. A CT scan of the head demonstrated air in the right middle cerebral artery territory. Urgent transfer to a specialist centre for gold standard hyperbaric oxygen therapy was critical to optimize patient recovery. To prevent further air migration, the patient was placed in the Trendelenburg and left lateral decubitus position and high flow oxygen was administered, while awaiting transfer. Following hyperbaric oxygen therapy, the patient was stepped down to a stroke unit for rehabilitation. Full neurological recovery was made. This case highlights that radiologists should be well-versed in recognizing the signs and immediate management of air embolism and anticipate it as a rare but severe complication of lung biopsy.Keywords: cerebral arterial gas embolism, percutaneous lung biopsy, hyperbaric oxygen therapy

Clinical presentation

An 80-year-old female, ex-smoker, was referred to our local Trust from the community with worsening shortness of breath and ankle edema. She was known to have type II diabetes mellitus, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (FEV1: 42%), and IgM monoclonal gammopathy of unknown significance. A chest X-ray radiograph demonstrated a right middle zone opacity. Computed tomography (CT) confirmed a spiculated mass lesion, dorsally located within the superior segment of the right lower lobe with maximum dimension of 3.3 cm (Figure 1A). The mass had a central cavitating necrosis, 7 mm wall and associated pleural retraction, and avid FDG uptake on whole-body FDG PET scan (Figure 1B). Normal clinical observations with no signs of infection were confirmed at the local Rapid Access Lung clinic. CT-guided biopsy of the pulmonary lesion was performed for histological diagnosis of the primary lung malignancy, initially staged T2aN0M0.1–4 The patient was placed in the prone position to reduce chest wall motion. A vertical needle biopsy trajectory was taken in a plane directly perpendicular to the patient to ensure the shortest path from the skin to the lesion and minimize the lung parenchyma traversed.

(A) CT thorax with contrast axial cross-section showing a spiculated cavitating lesion (3.2 × 3.3 cm) with central necrosis and 7 mm wide rim. (B) FDG-PET highlighting avid FDG radiotracer uptake in the periphery of the mass lesion.

Case management

Five minutes after the end of the biopsy, the patient expressed feelings of tiredness and showed signs of somnolence suspicious of cerebral arterial gas embolism (CAGE). An immediate CT head confirmed air locules in the small A4 cerebral arteries of the frontal lobe and para-sagittal cerebral convexities (Figure 2). No hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, or mid-line shift was noted. Shortly after, the patient developed complete left-sided hemiparesis but remained oriented in time and space (Glasgow Come Scale [GCS] 15/15). She was placed in the left lateral decubitus/Trendelenburg position and administered 100% oxygen. Post-procedural CT chest showed a small ipsilateral pneumothorax and surrounding hemorrhage (Figure 3A) and a linear channel of air in the pulmonary vein entering the left atrium (Figure 3B).

CT head axial cross-section demonstrating air locules in the right-sided sub-arachnoid space (arrowheads).

Post-procedural CT thorax axial cross section in the left lateral decubitus position with (A) a superficial pneumothorax (arrow) and surrounding hemorrhage, and (B) a linear channel of air (arrow) in the pulmonary vein entering the left atrium.

While awaiting urgent transfer to the stroke team, observations were monitored strictly and remained reassuringly stable. The National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) on presentation to the stroke team was 7/42. She was subsequently transferred to the nearest specialized UK center for hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT). Time to HBOT from symptom onset was 6.5 hours. This was delivered using Table 6 US Navy protocol. A second short HBOT was completed in the morning after overnight observation, which was then followed by a magnetic resonance image (MRI) of the head and chest/abdomen X-ray. Importantly, the MRI was remarkably normal including the fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) study (Figure 4).

Post recovery MRI of the head (A) T2 axial, (B) T1 coronal, (C) susceptibility weighted (SWI) sagittal, and (D) the FLAIR sagittal, performed less than 24 hours after the first hyperbaric oxygen therapy demonstrating that MRI was remarkably normal including the FLAIR sequence, and irrespective of the severity of the clinical signs and symptoms.

Eleven hours after the second treatment, the patient had a tonic-clonic seizure for 2 minutes duration that was terminated with 5 mg intravenous diazepam. Further treatment included levetiracetam, aspirin, and low molecular weight heparin (LMWH). The patient was transferred back to stroke services, first locally then back to secondary care. The hemiplegia improved first in the arm during the initial treatment Table 6 session, and then in the leg within 5 days of onset. Once she started to mobilize, her care was stepped down to a community hospital rehabilitation ward.

A follow-up staging CT scan, 1-month later, demonstrated minimal growth of the lesion to 3.8 cm with an increase in cavitation area. No distal metastases were seen. Squamous cell carcinoma was confirmed on histology and referral for radical radiotherapy was made. Four weeks following the incident, she regained full functional status and made a full neurological recovery. In the community, she received ongoing support from the lung cancer specialist nurses and her general practitioners.

Discussion

CAGE is a rare but life-threatening complication of percutaneous lung biopsy with an incidence of less than 1%.1 Cerebral air embolisms arise from communication between the air and vasculature and a pressure gradient favoring the passage of air into the circulation.1,5 Air can enter either the arterial or venous system, with differing pathophysiology and variable clinical presentation. CAGE can result from direct injection of gas into the arterial system, as observed between the pulmonary vein and the systemic arterial system in this case. CAGE can also occur paradoxically from the venous system via intracardiac or pulmonary right-to-left shunts. Presentation usually occurs within seconds to minutes and is mostly associated with neurological symptoms since the brain and spinal cord are most susceptible to embolic ischemic insult from air in the systemic arterial system.

Venous gas embolisms are a less recognized type of cerebral air embolism caused by gas bubbles entering the cerebral venous system, often retrogradely through the jugular veins. These bubbles can obstruct veins, causing brain edema, venous infarction, and intracranial hypertension. They most commonly occur due to central venous catheter placement, removal, or malfunction, though they may also arise during neurosurgical procedures often presenting with respiratory or cardiac symptoms.6

Studies have indicated certain co-morbidities predispose to developing CAGE such as underlying airway disease, diabetes, and hypertension as well as right-to-left shunts.2 The presence of cavitary pulmonary lesions may also significantly increase the risk of systemic air embolism. Cavitary lesions can act as an air reservoir that can be forced into injured vessels when a sufficient pressure gradient exists, as reported in cases involving cavitating lesions secondary to tuberculosis, aspergillosis, and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome.7,8 Lesion location may also contribute to risk.9 Vessels in the lower lobes tend to be larger to accommodate gravity-dependent increased perfusion and undergo increased respiratory motion. Biopsy of the lower lobe may thus pose a higher risk of vein injury. Pressures are higher in the pulmonary artery and alveoli than in the pulmonary vein above the left atrium. A biopsy above the left atrium may cause an iatrogenic fistula between them, forcing air into the pulmonary vein leading to a systemic air embolism.

Procedural risk factors include utilizing a wider needle and the depth inserted within the lesion.3,4 Unintentionally coughing or being in a prone position can accentuate the possibility of developing an air embolism.1 Avoiding the prone positions if possible, minimizing needle depth during biopsy, and avoiding emphysematous or bullous sections of lung, are recommended where possible.4,5,10 Using saline drops as a barrier between needle exchanges while biopsy cores are being obtained could also potentially help prevent external air aspiration. General anesthesia is preferable with specific measures to avoid coughing or any Valsalva-inducing incident. Ideally, the biopsy needle should only be removed once the patient regains consciousness, can be clinically assessed and is at low risk of coughing or Valsalva. But this may be clinically impractical and is not a routine practice at our institution.

Although largely asymptomatic in most cases, approximately 0.05% of patients with CAGE present with symptoms, the most common being loss of consciousness and neurological involvement such as paralysis and sensory loss. Cardiac abnormalities are also common, and these include life-threatening arrythmias, heart failure and syncope.2 Tissue-related hypoxia of the cardiac and cerebrovascular system is also a serious concern.11 The onset of symptoms is prompt, occurring either during the procedure or shortly afterwards.1,2

Early recognition of clinical signs is crucial and immediate management should be initiated. The patient should be treated with intravenous LWMH, connected to 100% high-flow oxygen and placed in the left lateral decubitus/Trendelenburg position.2 This position prevents further air entry to the systemic circulation as it forces the left ventricular outflow tract into an inferior position and raises central venous pressure, making air less likely to reach the aorta.4 Timely administration of high flow oxygen at the time of diagnosis not only treats suspected hypoxic tissues but assists in removing gas from existing air bubbles.10,11 Depending on severity, HBOT is the gold standard treatment, given its safety record and efficacy in dissolving embolized air bubbles and providing oxygenation to ischemic tissues.12 In the event of hemodynamic instability and cardiac arrest, appropriate resuscitation equipment must be readily available. If the cerebral insult is more severe (ie, GCS below 8), the patient may remain unable to protect their own airway and will need ventilation for transfer.

Urgent action is known to greatly improve patient outcomes.10 Given not all units have a HBOT specialist center within proximity, contact details to the nearest center should be easily reachable. The patient should be kept in the left lateral decubitus/Trendelenburg position with oxygen and closely monitored until transfer arrangements are secured. Specific measures should be taken in ventilated patients to reduce any chance of further air entering the systemic circulation.

In terms of imaging, clinicians should scan the entire cardiac region in addition to the area biopsied to exclude evidence of air within the heart chambers immediately post procedure.1,2

When air enters the arterial system, it follows the blood flow and occludes one or more (depending on gas load) of the major intracranial arteries and potentially blocks terminal arterioles as the smaller bubbles cannot continue past the decreasing diameter of the lumen. Multiple distal vascular territories should thus be scrutinized when air embolism to the brain is suspected.13

CT of the brain has high sensitivity for detecting intravascular gas as hypodense areas. However, because air may resorb quickly, CT may only reveal secondary findings such as cerebral infarction or edema. Similarly, MRI, particularly diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), is mainly used to assess ischemic brain injury and support differential diagnosis rather than directly identify air emboli. However, an important consideration demonstrated here is that imaging can be less sensitive than clinical findings, as illustrated by the normal MRI head sequences in this case. This is similar to decompression sickness, where an MRI of the spinal cord can be reported as normal despite dramatic clinical signs.14

The radiologist should therefore include this rare complication both in the informed consent process and mitigate for risks of systemic air embolism during pre-procedural planning for lung biopsy. The Intensive Care Society have published a very useful reference “Management of patients with gas embolism: guidance for intensive care and resuscitation teams.”15 This provides a helpline number and details of the HBOT unit.

Learning points

We describe a rare case of systemic air embolism with neurological involvement. Fortunately, the gas load appears to have been low with only the middle cerebral distribution of the non-dominant hemisphere involved and without reduction in level of consciousness. Time-to-treatment was 6.5 hours even with early diagnosis and an immediate plan to transfer.
