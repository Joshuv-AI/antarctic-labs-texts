The role and uptake of drug and alcohol services in patients with alcohol‐associated acute pancreatitis at a large multisite health service
Authors: Cindy Ho, Hardesh Dhillon, Alexander Mitropoulos, Eliza Flanagan, Timothy Phan, Joshua Haron Abasszade, Jeremy Tse, Bowen Xia, Kyle Williams, Faris Gondal, Sally Jane Bell, David Jacka, Simon Hew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlcohol consumption remains an independent risk factor for recurrent presentations of alcohol‐associated acute pancreatitis (AAP). Although there are recognised guidelines for alcohol use disorder (AUD), there are no recommendations on targeting alcohol cessation to reduce recurrence of AAP in guidelines for acute pancreatitis.AimsThis study describes the uptake of the Addiction Medicine Unit (AMU), a specialist consultative service, in patients with AAP presenting to a tertiary hospital.MethodsRetrospective cohort with all inpatients with AAP at a large multisite tertiary health service in Melbourne, Australia from May 2022 to May 2024. Demographics, AUD diagnosis and recommended interventions from the AMU were obtained from medical records.ResultsThere were 231 patients across 314 admissions with AAP identified with a high prevalence of AUD (91.8%). Referral to AMU occurred in 56.7% of admissions, predominantly among those with AUD (93.8%), with a tendency to refer patients with higher weekly alcohol intake (P = 0.024). The AMU recommended outpatient counselling services to assist with detoxification and rehabilitation in 40.4%, both pharmacological and outpatient interventions in 37.6% and medications alone in 21.9% of admissions. Over the 2‐year period, 54 (23.4%) patients were readmitted with AAP. The adjusted analysis did not demonstrate reduced readmission with AAP associated with AMU review.ConclusionsThere is a substantial missed opportunity for specialist addiction medicine input in this cohort. AAP should be considered a sentinel alcohol‐related hospital presentation, and the observed referral gap identifies an opportunity for more systematic addiction medicine assessment, pharmacotherapy initiation and linkage to longitudinal care.

Introduction

Alcohol‐associated acute pancreatitis (AAP) is the second leading cause of acute pancreatitis (AP), and ongoing alcohol consumption remains a major independent risk factor for recurrence. 1 Abstinence from alcohol offers protection from future episodes of AP and hence reduces the risk of chronic pancreatitis 2 ; however, this is seldom achieved. Brief interventions aiming to change patients’ behaviour and motivating them to reduce alcohol consumption has been shown to be effective in a hospital setting for hazardous drinkers and those with mild alcohol use disorder (AUD). 3 Outpatient alcohol interventions are the mainstay in targeting alcohol addiction, 4 and a structured approach led by specialists, with linkage to appropriate care and rehabilitation, is often what is missing. There are no recognised guidelines to direct specific behavioural or pharmacological interventions in this population. Limited studies have explored the role of addiction medicine in patients with AAP, with only one single‐centre randomised controlled trial that has studied the effect of repeated visits to the gastroenterology clinic following discharge can reduce recurrence of AAP. 4 In this study, there was no mention of specialty input from an addiction medicine service, nor was there information on any medications used for AUD. The first multicentre randomised controlled trial is currently under way to assess the effect of a structured alcohol cessation support programme in reducing AAP recurrence. 5

A range of interventions are available for the management of AUD. Non‐pharmacological strategies include outpatient services that encompass counselling, psychology and support groups. A network of specialist services that support adults with alcohol and drug concerns including detoxification, alcohol rehabilitation and counselling in the community are vital in addressing alcohol dependence once discharged from hospital. Pharmacological agents have demonstrated efficacy in reducing alcohol consumption and achieving abstinence in patients with AUD. In Australia, only acamprosate, naltrexone and disulfiram are approved for use by the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA). Baclofen and topiramate are also used in the management of AUD, though both are prescribed off‐label despite published literature attesting to its effectiveness including supportive Cochrane reviews and meta‐analyses. 6 , 7 , 8 The use of these agents requires careful consideration due to potential side effects, toxicity and drug interactions, contributing to a lack of confidence among clinicians without specialised addiction medicine training in prescribing them. 9 An Addiction Medicine Unit (AMU) therefore can play a critical role in providing expert consultation on the necessary medications and interventions needed to address AUD effectively, including in AAP.

Materials and methods

Our centre is a large tertiary health service in Victoria, which includes three tertiary hospitals and serves a significant catchment area of approximately 1.8 million residents. We conducted a retrospective cohort study including all inpatients aged 18 years and older with a primary diagnosis of AAP at our health service from May 2022 to May 2024. Patients were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) coding for pancreatitis and included whether they met the revised Atlanta criteria (two of the following: typical symptoms, elevated serum lipase >3 times the upper limit of normal or characteristic imaging) 10 and whether alcohol was determined as the likely cause of AP by the treating team, measured by >5 years of moderate or heavy alcohol consumption (>50 g/day). 1

Patients with documented AUD included those with at least three out of the 11 DSM‐5 criteria for AUD. 11 Patients not meeting these criteria but were admitted with AAP due to a ‘binge’ episode or from hazardous episodic drinking were also identified as having likely AUD. 12 Motivation for cessation of alcohol was assessed using the stages of change: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance.

Patients were referred to the AMU at the discretion of the treating team. At our health service, the AMU is a drug and alcohol addiction specialist/psychiatrist‐led inpatient consultation service which provides assessment, interventions and follow‐up for patients with conditions caused or worsened by problematic alcohol and/or drug use. There is an AMU residential withdrawal unit adjacent to one of the major tertiary hospitals. Outpatient services often recommended by the AMU include either follow‐up in the AMU outpatient clinic or, more commonly, self‐referrals to a community specialist drug and alcohol addiction counselling and withdrawal service.

This study was exempt from Human Research Ethics Committee review and has been reviewed and approved as a quality assurance/audit project (ERM Reference Number 125457).

Patient demographics, referral data, pharmacological and non‐pharmacological interventions provided by the AMU were obtained from patients' electronic and scanned medical records.

Data were presented with percentages and medians with interquartile range (IQR). Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare median standard drinks per week between two groups. Fisher's exact test was used to determine the association between two variables. We used multivariable logistic regression with complete‐case analysis including all patients who were readmitted with AAP across the 24‐month study duration to determine factors associated with remission of AAP. The primary exposure was identified as review by AMU in their index admission controlling for other covariates including age, gender, previous pharmacotherapies for addiction, bedside index of severity in AP (BISAP) score, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), standard number of drinks per week, previous AMU input, previous alcohol rehabilitation and other substance use.

P‐values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism (Version 11.0) and IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 30.0).

There were 212 of 231 patients (91.8%) with AAP with AUD. Of the 212 patients with AUD, 89 (42.0%) were identified as being in the pre‐contemplation/contemplation phase of alcohol cessation, with only 11 (5.2%) patients in the preparation or action phases. The stage of motivation to quit alcohol intake was unknown in 112 of 212 patients with AUD (52.8%).

Of the 212 patients with AUD, 110 (51.9%) had other concurrent substance use, including tobacco or nicotine use (36.8%), illicit substances (1.9%), prescribed substances including opioids and benzodiazepines (1.4%) and a combination of these (11.8%). There were 77 of 212 patients (36.3%) with no other concurrent substance use, and in 25 (11.8%) their substance use was unknown.

There were 178 of 314 admissions (56.7%) referred to the AMU, including 167 (93.8%) with AUD. Most of the 178 patients were reviewed in person (66.9%) or via phone advice to clinicians (33.1%). There were 39 of 178 patients (12.4%) who refused referral to the AMU. Patients who were referred to the AMU had a higher median number of standard drinks per week (70 standard drinks, range 6–385 standard drinks) compared to those who were not referred (49 standard drinks, range 1–301 standard drinks) (P = 0.024). Overall, there were 173 of 231 patients (74.9%) with number of standard drinks per week recorded, with a median of 56 standard drinks (IQR 35–98) per week.

Of all 314 admissions with AAP, 28 (8.9%) had previously undergone long‐term residential therapeutic community programmes for their alcohol use, 20 (6.4%) were already linked with a community drug and alcohol detoxification service, and eight (2.5%) had both. Forty‐nine patients across 314 admissions (15.6%) had been previously prescribed a pharmacological agent for AUD, and 135 (43.0%) had not had any prior experience with community programmes for alcohol cessation, including inpatient rehabilitation or detoxification. In 123 patients of 314 admissions (39.2%), prior services were unknown.

The interventions recommended by the AMU included outpatient services alone (40.4%), both medications for addiction and outpatient services (37.6%) and medications alone (21.9%) from the 314 admissions (Fig. 1). Outpatient services included a service for community‐based assessment and counselling (86.4%), residential rehabilitation programmes and/or detox services (5.8%), follow‐up in the AMU outpatient clinic (3.9%) or a combination of these outpatient services (3.9%). There were 137 (43.6%) admissions in which patients were newly commenced on medications for AUD. Of the 314 admissions, topiramate (42.6%) and naltrexone (42.6%) were the most prescribed pharmacological agents, followed by baclofen (9.8%), acamprosate (4.1%) and disulfiram (0.8%) (Fig. 1). The median daily dose of topiramate and naltrexone on discharge was 25 mg, with specific instructions written to the primary physician to uptitrate these medications to therapeutic doses following discharge. For the 49 patients already taking these medications prior to their admission, 15 (30.6%) were advised to continue the pre‐admission doses on discharge, nine (18.4%) patients had uptitrated their pre‐existing medications for AUD on discharge, and nine (18.4%) patients were recommended additional AUD pharmacotherapies. Sixteen (32.7%) patients had downtitrated their medications for AUD on discharge. There were 92 admissions (29.3%) with patients discharged on monotherapy, 36 admissions (11.5%) with patients discharged on two pharmacotherapies and one admission (0.3%) with a patient on three different pharmacotherapies.

Most patients were not readmitted within 30 days of discharge (73.2%); however, there were 33 (10.5%) patients readmitted for pancreatitis or pancreatitis‐related complications, including pain or infection, and 18 (5.7%) readmitted to hospital for non‐pancreatitis‐related reasons. Over a 24‐month follow‐up period, 54 patients (23.4%) were readmitted with AAP. Thirty‐eight (70.4%) of these patients were readmitted twice, and 16 (29.6%) patients were readmitted more than twice, with a maximum of eight admissions for the duration of the 24‐month study period.

AMU review in the index admission (first admission within the study period with AP) was not independently associated with readmission to hospital with AP within the study period (aOR 1.69, 95% CI 0.74–3.84; P = 0.214) after adjustment for confounders including age, sex, prior pharmacotherapies for addiction, BISAP score, CCI, alcohol consumption amount (standard drinks per week), prior AMU review, previous alcohol rehabilitation programmes and other substance use. Age showed a borderline association with readmission with AP (aOR 1.04 per year, 95% CI 1.00–1.09; P = 0.062) (Table 2). Discussion

Despite there being a high prevalence of AUD among the 231 patients with AAP (91.8%), only 56.7% of patients were referred to the AMU, with referrals primarily made for those with AUD and a higher standard number of drinks per week. Clinicians may be selectively referring patients to the AMU with a particularly high volume of alcohol consumption rather than automatically referring when there is evidence of alcohol‐associated organ injury. The most common interventions recommended by the AMU included referral to outpatient services alone, particularly to a centralised drug and alcohol counselling and detoxification service, followed by a combination of pharmacological and non‐pharmacological outpatient services. Topiramate was one of the two most prescribed pharmacological agents in this study, despite being used off‐label for AUD. Topiramate potentiates the inhibitory effects of GABA 13 and causes inhibition of the excitatory glutamate pathways, which is postulated to lead to an alteration of the reinforcing properties and experience of alcohol. Miranda et al. demonstrated that topiramate dose of 200 mg daily showed a reduction in the positive reinforcing effects of alcohol. 14 The recommended initiation dose for topiramate in AUD is 25 mg daily, titrated to a maximum dose of 300 mg daily over 8 weeks. 15 This slow uptitration is to avoid the intolerable adverse effects of cognitive impairment, paraesthesia, headache, fatigue, dizziness and depression, which may contribute to medication non‐adherence. 15 Since the median topiramate dose on discharge in our study was 25 mg, it is crucial to ensure that patients have their medications uptitrated in the community to reach therapeutic levels for optimal effectiveness.

A key advantage of topiramate in the management of alcohol use disorder is its favourable pharmacokinetic profile in chronic liver disease. In patients with hepatic impairment, topiramate exposure may increase modestly and is generally well tolerated. 13 , 15 Given that alcohol‐related liver disease is one of the most common causes of cirrhosis, topiramate represents a suitable therapeutic option that can often be used without dose adjustment. 13 , 15 The prescription of naltrexone in our study may have been limited by its relative contraindication in individuals with severely impaired liver function or concurrent opioid use.

A randomised controlled trial performed by Morley et al. published in 2024 concluded that topiramate is at least as effective and safe as naltrexone as a first‐line therapy for reducing heavy alcohol intake. Topiramate had greater effects in reducing the number of standard drinks per drinking day and number of heavy drinking days, reducing alcohol craving and making a larger improvement in gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT) enzyme concentration than naltrexone. 16 Topiramate is now included in the Canadian clinical guidelines as an alternative to first‐line therapy for moderate to severe AUD. 17 A joint clinical practice guideline issued in 2021 by the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs and the U.S. Department of Defense recommends either naltrexone or topiramate for the treatment of AUD. 18 To our knowledge, no official guideline published in Australia recommends the use of topiramate; however, its use has increased among clinicians as an off‐label therapy.

Off‐label prescribing occurs when a drug is prescribed for an indication that is outside the approved indication set by the TGA. 19 Based on a drug's mechanism of action in an approved indication, the drug could be effective in treating conditions not listed in the product information. 20 This can provide access to effective therapy where no approved option exists. The responsibility rests with the prescriber to justify its use for an indication not included in the product information. 19 Using these agents requires careful consideration due to potential side effects, toxicity and dosing regimens, contributing to a lack of confidence among clinicians without specialised training in prescribing them. 9 Having an AMU can play a critical role in providing expert consultation on the necessary medications and interventions needed to address AUD effectively, including in AAP. In Australia, only acamprosate, naltrexone and disulfiram are approved for use by the TGA. However, only acamprosate and naltrexone are under the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme, as disulfiram is often in short supply and of limited effectiveness without supervision. Baclofen and topiramate are also used in the management of AUD, though both are prescribed off‐label. It is the responsibility of the prescriber to monitor not only the effectiveness of the off‐label medication but also its tolerability and adverse effects. 20

Of the 137 admissions where patients were commenced on AUD pharmacotherapies, 29.3% were prescribed monotherapy, and 11.8% were prescribed more than one medication for AUD. A future prospective cohort study assessing compliance, especially in achieving therapeutic concentrations, and evaluating the effectiveness of these medications both individually and in combination would be ideal.

Patients with AAP in our study had a high rate of readmissions with AAP, with over 15% readmitted within 30 days and more than 20% readmitted within the study duration of 24 months. These rates are similar in previous studies, 21 and these readmissions are not only associated with increased morbidity and mortality but also contribute significantly to healthcare costs. 22 , 23 The adjusted analysis did not demonstrate reduced readmission with AAP associated with AMU review across the 24‐month period (Table 2). There were 25.1% of patients excluded due to the lack of alcohol consumption data. The complete‐case analysis, along with the limited number of readmission events relative to the number of covariates in the analysis, may have contributed to the lack of statistical significance.

In a population diagnosed with alcohol‐related organ injury, the prevalence of AUD is high. A brief review from the AMU in a hospital setting is unlikely to provide effective long‐term outcomes, which highlights the need for systematic, longitudinal, specialist‐driven care. Because this was a single‐health‐service, multisite retrospective cohort study with a relatively short follow‐up period, the effect of the AMU on readmission with AAP could not be elucidated. Additionally, compliance with medications for AUD and effectiveness in the community were not assessed. Future systematic prospective studies evaluating the efficacy and adherence of interventions recommended by the AMU on recurrent AAP would be useful, particularly with a longer follow‐up period that extended beyond the in‐patient hospital setting.

Identifying patients with AUD required clinicians to document criteria met from the DSM‐5 for AUD and documenting alcohol dependence as an issue. The reliance on clinician documentation to identify AUD may have led to underreporting of hazardous or harmful alcohol consumption. The lack of standardisation for screening of AUD in our centre likely contributed to the missed opportunities for identifying patients with hazardous and harmful alcohol consumption that may not have a documented diagnosis of AUD. Incorporating the use of biomarkers, particularly objective measures including ethyl glucuronide (EtG), phosphatidylethanol (PEth) and indirect GGT and mean corpuscular volume (MCV), could help validate and corroborate self‐reported intake of alcohol consumption. This can strengthen the validity of attributing alcohol as the primary cause of AP, which in this study relied on the treating physician.

In a cohort defined by alcohol‐associated organ injury, with a very high prevalence of AUD, just over half of the admissions were referred to AMU. Our study highlights the significant referral gap for addiction medicine specialist input in a vulnerable cohort with alcohol‐associated organ injury, which should be considered a sentinel alcohol‐related hospital presentation. Efforts to improve referral practices for more systematic addiction medicine assessment, pharmacotherapy initiation and linkage to longitudinal care may be key to reducing readmissions with AAP and improving patient outcomes.
