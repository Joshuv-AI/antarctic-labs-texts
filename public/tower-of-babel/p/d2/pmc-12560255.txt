Production of human cathepsins using Expi293™ mammalian cell expression system for off‐target activity of cysteine protease inhibitor screening
Authors: Zoe Turner, Elena Arutyunova, Duong T Bui, Ling Han, Appan Srinivas Kandadai, Bing Bai, Howard S Young, Lara K Mahal, John S Klassen, M Joanne Lemieux
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing the SARS‐CoV‐2 pandemic, many direct‐acting antivirals targeting viral cysteine protease were developed. SARS‐CoV‐2, as well as other viruses, rely on cysteine proteases for their replication, suggesting future generations of antivirals targeting cysteine proteases will emerge. A major concern for these first‐generation drugs is the off‐target effects on host cysteine proteases. Therefore, screening for inhibitor specificity is a crucial step in antiviral drug development. Cathepsins are one of the most abundant human proteases, which have roles in maintaining cell health and are key to many physiological processes. Here we describe a general expression and purification protocol for cathepsins B, S, and L using the Expi293™ mammalian expression system. We characterized the glycosylation pattern and kinetic parameters of purified enzymes with commercially available cathepsin‐specific fluorogenic substrates and also established cathepsin inhibition screen assays. We tested specificity indices of peptidic inhibitors of SARS‐CoV‐2 Mpro synthesized by our team and nirmatrelvir as a benchmark for all three cathepsins. Establishing a reliable cathepsin inhibition assay would assist with screening of newly developed cysteine protease inhibitors for off‐target activity within the scope of pandemic preparedness.Keywords: antivirals, cathepsins, cysteine protease, off‐target activity, SARS‐CoV‐2 inhibitors

INTRODUCTION

Cathepsins are a family of proteases that are abundantly found in the lysosomes with diverse functions in the cell, ranging from antigen presentation in the immune response to maintaining cellular homeostasis (Brix et al., 2008; Roberts, 2005; Turk et al., 2000). Their dysregulation has been implicated in a range of pathological states (Reiser et al., 2010). Cathepsins consist of three protease families: serine (A and G), aspartic (D and E), and cysteine proteases (B, C, F, H, K, L, O, S, V, X, and W) (Patel et al., 2018). The cysteine family of cathepsins has recently been gaining more attention due to the development of a new generation of antivirals targeting the main protease of SARS‐CoV‐2. Testing for off‐target inhibition of cathepsins allows for the determination of inhibitor specificity of cysteine protease drug candidates and can predict their side effects. Since numerous viruses, in addition to SARS‐CoV‐2, have cysteine proteases required for their replication, it is foreseeable that more drugs targeting cysteine proteases will be developed and screened for specificity against host cathepsins.

The purification and expression of cathepsins B, S, and L have been reported for rodent and human enzymes in a variety of expression systems, including mammalian systems using HeLa, HEK293, and epidermal carcinoma cells (Zhou et al., 2024). However, in most cases, immunoaffinity chromatography was used for purification, resulting in low yield and high cost of protein production, or proteins were purified in pro‐forms (Bromme et al., 2004). Here, we describe a versatile Expi293 cells expression and IMAC purification method, allowing for one generalized protocol to be applied to multiple cathepsins. The importance of using human cell lines for the expression of these proteases is crucial for the physiological relevance of screening for off‐target effects. Cathepsins are shown to be glycosylated proteins, and moreover, different tissues have cell‐type‐specific variations in glycosylation, causing heterogeneity of glycoproteins (Rademacher et al., 1988). Altered protein glycosylation is detected in malignant tumors at all stages (Takahashi et al., 1984). Certain post‐translational modifications may have major impacts on protease activity, and in vitro screening using glycosylated cathepsins will allow for more reliable assessment of off‐target effects of the inhibitors (reviewed in Goettig (2016)). In the cell, cathepsins are translated into inactive pre‐procathepsins (Braulke & Bonifacino, 2009), which include a signal sequence, an inhibitory propeptide, and the active cathepsin. The signal sequence targets the protein to the lumen of the ER, where it gets cleaved by a signal peptidase, leaving the less active procathepsin, which gets transported to the Golgi. Procathepsins are tagged with mannose‐6‐phosphate and get trafficked directly to the endosome/lysosome or to the plasma membrane before eventually ending up in the endosome/lysosome. Once in the lysosome, the procathepsins undergo cleavage of the propeptide via auto‐activation or trans‐activation induced by low pH of the environment or the presence of other proteases. Cathepsins B, S, and L have all been shown to undergo the process of auto‐activation to reach their mature and most active form (Ishidoh & Kominami, 1994; Kopitar et al., 1996; Mach et al., 1994; Nomura & Fujisawa, 1997).

Based on sequence and structural features of propeptide and the mature protein, cathepsin family was divided into two subfamilies—cathepsin‐L‐like and cathepsin‐B‐like proteases (Coulombe et al., 1996; Verma et al., 2016). We selected cathepsins B, S, and L out of the 11 human cathepsins to assess off‐target activity of SARS‐CoV‐2 inhibitors as representatives for subfamilies (Roberts, 2005). Interestingly, cathepsin L has been shown to play a key role in the viral entry of SARS‐CoV‐2 and could be a promising therapeutic target for the prevention and treatment of COVID‐19 (reviewed in Jackson et al., 2022; Mellott et al., 2021). Cathepsin S is expressed predominantly in antigen presenting cells and is thought to be important in the cleavage events prior to peptide loading of MHC class II molecule and plays a crucial role in the immune response (Baker et al., 2003). Therefore, screening these three proteases for inhibition allows for a prediction of the off‐target effects on cathepsins as well as on the immune response, which is important for clearing the virus.

This study provides the details about a novel expression protocol of three cathepsins in Expi293™ mammalian expression system (a human embryonic kidney cell line), describes the purification and maturation procedures to obtain a high yield of active enzymes, assesses glycosylation of purified proteins as well as reports on kinetic and inhibition assays using commercially available substrates for cathepsins B, S, and L. This protocol will be useful for generating cathepsin mutants for mechanistic or structural studies. We used several compounds synthesized by our team to delineate the differences in specificity towards Mpro of SARS‐CoV‐2 and cathepsins. Overall, we aimed to establish a reliable and replicable inhibition assay using recombinant cathepsin proteases, which would be useful in the development and screening of antivirals for current and emerging viral outbreaks.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Cathepsins expression in Expi293™ cells

pcDNA 3.1(−) vectors (Addgene, USA) bearing the genes of cathepsins B, S, or L with C‐terminal C9 tags were modified using site‐directed mutagenesis for purification purposes by adding a 6xHis tag. We chose to mutate the existing C9 tag to a His‐tag to maintain its C‐terminal location in order not to interfere with the signal peptide on the N‐terminus, allowing for the transport of the cathepsins in the cell, and to reduce the length of flexible regions on protein termini, which are usually more susceptible to proteolysis. The main disadvantage of using mammalian cells for the expression of cathepsins is the presence of endogenous cathepsin‐like proteases, which may co‐purify and interfere with activity measurements of recombinant protein. Therefore, the high level of expression provided by Expi293™ mammalian cells and the presence of a specific tag for purification are crucial for mitigating this problem. After verifying the sequence of the resulting constructs, Expi293™ cells were transfected with full‐length His‐tagged cathepsins B, S, or L following the standard protocol. To confirm the expression of each protease and determine the optimal cell harvesting time, aliquots of cell cultures were taken for several days post‐transfection, and cell lysates and the media were analyzed separately by western blot. Western blot analysis revealed that all cathepsins were secreted into the media in their proenzyme form with the expected MW of ∼37 kDa (Figure 1a–c). Cathepsins S and L do also appear in the cell lysate, although in lower quantities than in the media. Thus, the culture media was collected after 3 days of expression for purification purposes. The stable pH and the absence of contaminating proteins in the Expi293™ expression media are the key factors contributing to the optimal yield of cathepsins in this expression system. Acidification of the media during growth in bacteria and yeasts can cause autocatalytic activation of proproteins with subsequent autodegradation and denaturation of mature proteases. Therefore, maintaining a pH higher than 6 is very important, which is achieved in well‐balanced Expi293™ medium. Moreover, the fact that it is serum and protein‐free results in a secreted cathepsin being the only major protein in the media and simplifies the purification process dramatically.

Purification and activation of cathepsins

Since the constructs for all three cathepsins and hence the purification steps were identical, here we describe the generalized protocol for cathepsins' isolation from the Expi293™ cell culture media.

The culture media was harvested 3 days after transfection and flash frozen for storage. Expi293™ expression medium is not directly compatible with the Ni‐NTA purification system; therefore, the media should be dialyzed prior to addition to Ni‐NTA resin, or a specialized resin with broad reagent compatibility should be used. We performed the dialysis of 50 mL of culture media against 4 L of 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, and 10% glycerol for 16–20 h, and after removing insoluble particles by centrifugation, cathepsins were purified using Ni‐NTA affinity chromatography. As expected, eluted protein samples contained pro‐cathepsins with MW of ∼37 kDa (Figure 2a–c). The SDS‐PAGE analysis of cathepsin S purification revealed that both pro‐form (48%) and mature, autocleaved protein (52%) seen at the expected molecular weights of ∼37 and ∼25 kDa, respectively, were present in purified samples consistent with the results from the western blot. The percentage of pro‐cathepsins and mature forms was assessed using densitometry analysis of corresponding bands on SDS‐PAGEs of purifications and presented in the tables in Figure 2a–c. Besides, cathepsin S was also observed in the cell lysate (Figure 1b); therefore, the protein purification was performed from Expi293™ cells as well. After lysing the cells by ultrasound, the same protocol as for secreted protein was applied for purification.

Ni‐NTA affinity purification and maturation steps of cathepsin B (a), cathepsin S (b), and cathepsin L (c). Titration of activity of cathepsin B (e), cathepsin S (f), and cathepsin L (g) with E‐64. 50 nM of cathepsins were preincubated with a range of concentrations of E‐64 (0–100 nM) for 30 min; the reaction was started with the addition of substrate specific for each cathepsin. The concentration of E‐64 required to fully block the activity of cathepsin corresponds to the concentration of fully active protease and can be determined from the x‐intercept of the linear regression plot of initial velocity of product formation in the presence of each inhibitor concentration versus inhibitor concentration.

To initiate autocleavage, the purified fractions were dialyzed in acidic sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) for 4 h, and SDS‐PAGE analysis was used to ensure that the maturation was complete. As shown in Figure 2a,b, lane pH 5.0, only 70% of pro‐cathepsin B and 74% of pro‐cathepsin S were cleaved after this step, whereas cathepsin L's maturation was 100% full. To complete the autocleavage, additional dialysis at pH 4.5 for 16 h was performed, resulting in the fully mature form for all three cathepsins.

Cathepsin L purification initially was attempted from both the cells and the media following the same methods as for cathepsin S, as the western blot analysis showed approximately equal quantities in both the lysate and media. However, cathepsin L purification from the cell lysate resulted in a low yield containing many contaminating proteins (data not shown). Therefore, cell lysate purification was halted and only the protein samples purified from the media underwent acidic dialysis to complete the purification.

The activity of purified cathepsins was assessed using a fluorescence‐based kinetic assay. However, one of the common concerns of the purification of proteases expressed in mammalian cell systems is contamination with endogenous peptidase activity, especially when using short peptidic substrates such as Z‐RR‐AMC (cathepsin B substrate), which is also specific for trypsin‐like peptidases, or R‐AMC (cathepsins S and L), which can be cut by non‐serine or metallo‐dependent amino peptidases. Even though we purified cathepsins from the culture media, which decreases the risk of contamination with co‐purifying peptidases, it is still crucial to make sure that the activity we monitor is only cathepsin activity. The absolute molarity of purified cathepsin samples can be determined by a classical biochemical assay of titration of active sites of cathepsin proteases with E‐64. E‐64 is a specific stoichiometric and irreversible inhibitor of most cathepsins and can be used to reveal any non‐specific activity in the protein sample and determine the concentration of fully active cathepsin protease. The titration of all three cathepsins with E‐64 demonstrated that purified protein samples contained <95% of active cathepsins (Figure 2e–g).

To assess if purified cathepsins were glycosylated, we performed glycan and glycopeptide analyses using mass spectrometry. N‐glycosylation was reported for both human cathepsin B (2 sites) and cathepsins S and L (one site), aiding in proper protein folding, stability, and intracellular trafficking to lysosomes (Mach et al., 1992; Nissler et al., 1998; Zhang et al., 2003). Glycan and glycopeptide analyses of cathepsins B and L are shown in Figure 3. The amino acid sequence for cathepsin B showed two potential N‐glycosylation sites, Asn192 and Asn289. However, the glycopeptide analyses confirmed that the glycosylation only occurred at Asn192. The N‐glycans were identified as complex type with different levels of branching and sialylation with 1–2 fucoses (Figures 3a,b, S1, and S2). The most abundant glycoforms were Hex3_HexNAc4_Fuc1 (10.8% relative abundance), Hex3_HexNAc5_Fuc1 (18.4%), and Hex3_HexNAc6_Fuc1 (13.2%). ~9.5% of the peptide V191NGSRPPCTGEGDTPKCSK209 was not glycosylated.

Glycan and glycopeptide analyses of (a, b) cathepsin B and (c, d) cathepsin L. The glycans are presented using the symbol nomenclature for glycans (SNFG). Linkage and specific branching were determined for some structures using the retention time. Location of fucose (Fuc) is putative.

For cathepsin L, N‐glycosylation was identified on Asn221 of the peptide Y217SVANDTGFVDIPKQEK237. Interestingly, the eight major glycoforms showed phosphorylated high mannose glycans with 5–7 mannose and 1–2 phosphates (Figures 3c,d, S3, and S4). In addition, bi‐antennary and tri‐antennary glycans with 1–2 fucose and 0–2 sialic acids were also observed, albeit at low abundance. The full list of detected N‐glycans from cathepsins B and L by liquid chromatography–fluorescent detection–mass spectrometry (LC‐FLD‐MS) is presented in Table S1. No glycans were detected for cathepsin S (Figures S5 and S6).

The purification methods described above allowed not only for the isolation of cathepsins B, S, and L from mammalian cell culture, but also for the maturation into their most active forms, as confirmed by fluorescent‐based assays with cathepsin‐specific substrates. An average yield from 50 mL culture was 2–3 mg of mature enzymes for follow‐up experimentations. Additionally, since the described purification methods were shown to be suitable for cathepsins B, S, and L with only slight variation, it follows that they could be used for additional cathepsins to further expand off‐target inhibition screens.

Enzyme kinetics and inhibition assay for cathepsins

To establish a reliable protocol for compound screening, it is critical to have a thorough understanding of the kinetics of cathepsins. For an inhibition assay, the substrate concentration should be carefully chosen based on the Michaelis–Menten constant (K M) of the enzyme under initial velocity conditions.

We chose to employ commercially available substrates that are widely used to measure cathepsins' activity. Even though most activity assays for cathepsins use pH 5, since it is the physiological pH of lysosomes, some cathepsins were also shown to be active at neutral pHs. Cathepsin B has been previously reported as unstable at neutral and alkaline pHs to the extent that it gets irreversibly inhibited at these pH values (Turk et al., 1995). However, the study by Yoon et al. (2021) has not only demonstrated that cathepsin B, generated in a mammalian cells expression system, was active at pH 7.2 but also that the substrate specificity of this enzyme varied depending on the pH of the reaction. We assessed the pH dependence of all three purified cathepsins within the pH range of 2–10 using a universal buffer to eliminate the effect of buffer components on protease activity. Indeed, the cathepsin B pH‐dependence curve, as well as cathepsin S pH‐dependence, were bimodal with peaks at pH ∼6.5 and ∼8.0 for cathepsin B and at pH ∼3.0 and ∼7.5 for cathepsin S, whereas cathepsin L gave a single wide peak from pH ∼4.0 to ∼6.5 (Figure 4).

Michaelis–Menten kinetics and dependence of cathepsins' activity on pH. The data represent N = 3 ± SEM.

The substrate of cathepsin B used in this study utilizes Arg‐Arg in positions P1 and P2. Yoon et al. demonstrated that with this substrate cathepsin B showed higher activity at pH 7.2 compared to pH 4.6, which is consistent with our results. This report also showed that the activity of cathepsin B decreased at 27 and 37°C by 50% after 1‐h incubation at both pHs; however, given the short length of the assays we employed here, the enzyme's instability did not impact our results. Cathepsin S also has previously been shown to remain active at neutral pHs, consistent with our data (Turk et al., 1995). Cathepsin L is the most unstable of all cathepsins at neutral pH and is susceptible to irreversible inhibition. We see a steady drop in cathepsin L activity at pHs higher than 6.5. Importantly, it was shown that the proenzymes of cathepsins B and L were stable and resistant to irreversible inhibition at neutral pH, which allowed us to use the nickel chromatography at the optimal for this purification step pH of 7.5 (Nomura & Fujisawa, 1997; Pungercar et al., 2009).

Following pH‐dependence studies, we sought to determine the catalytic parameters of cathepsins with commercial substrates. Kinetics of cathepsin B were measured at pH 5.0 and 7.5 using the Z‐RR‐AMC fluorogenic substrate (Figure 4a). K M and k cat at pH 5.0 were determined to be 858 ± 107 μM and 2968 ± 209 min−1, respectively, with catalytic efficiency (k cat/K M) of 3.5 ± 0.2 min−1 μM−1, similar to those previously reported by Phan et al. using a pH of 5.5 and the enzyme purified from mammalian cells (Phan et al., 2021). At pH 7.5, the K M value decreased to 468 ± 43 μM, but k cat stayed in the same range (2794 ± 102 min−1), with catalytic efficiency of 6.0 ± 0.5 min−1 μM−1. These data are consistent with the results presented by Yoon et al. demonstrating higher efficiency of cathepsin B at pH 7.5 compared to pH 5.0 with this substrate (Yoon et al., 2021).

For cathepsin S, kinetic parameters were also determined at two pHs (Figure 4b). The K M, k cat, and k cat/K M values at pH 5.0 buffer were found to be 54 ± 1 μM, 200 ± 10 min−1 and 3.7 ± 0.3 min−1 μM−1 respectively, and at pH 7.5 the values were 37 ± 4 μM, 167 ± 6 min−1 and 4.5 ± 0.4 min−1 μM−1. Similar to cathepsin B, cathepsin S has a higher affinity and catalytic efficiency for this substrate at the higher pH. The understanding of the catalytic activity of cathepsins at neutral pHs is important because the paradigm for cathepsins is shifting from lysosomal proteases responsible for the degradation of other proteins to being important players in the extracellular space where they can also specifically modify extracellular proteins and are involved in inflammation or tumorigenesis (Jakos et al., 2019; Vidak et al., 2019).

Finally, K M and k cat for cathepsin L were determined to be 94 ± 8 μM and 530 ± 40 min−1 with catalytic efficiency of 5.6 ± 0.6 min−1 μM−1 (Figure 4c). Cathepsin L parameters were only determined at acidic pH since the activity of the protease steeply decreases at pHs greater than 6.

To measure the off‐target effects, we used dose–response curves for the inhibition of cathepsins by cysteine protease inhibitors. The kinetic characterization of each cathepsin enabled the establishment of optimal parameters for off‐target activity screening assays. The concentration of substrate was fixed at K M values for each substrate, and the pH of the activity buffer for all three cathepsins was chosen to be pH 5.0. All cathepsins tested here were active at this pH, and, more importantly, cathepsins are most abundantly found within lysosomes where physiological pH is within 4.5–5.5 (Zeng et al., 2020).

Our group developed several series of covalent inhibitors of SARS‐CoV‐2 main cysteine protease, Mpro: irreversible with acyloxymethylketone warheads and reversible with nitrile warheads (Arutyunova et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2021, 2022; Vuong et al., 2021). Here we selected one inhibitor from each group (AVI‐8053 or 15l from previous publication (Bai et al., 2022) and AVI‐8106 respectively) and used nirmatrelvir (Pfizer) (Owen et al., 2021) as a benchmark inhibitor.

IC50 values of nirmatrelvir for SARS‐CoV‐2 Mpro and cathepsins were similar to the reported values (Owen et al., 2021) (Table 1). Selectivity indices (SI) were calculated by dividing IC50 values of the intended targets by IC50 values of the unintended targets (Table 2), demonstrating high selectivity of nirmatrelvir for viral protease over all tested human cathepsins (SI > 10).

AVI‐8053 is a potent inhibitor of SARS‐CoV‐2 Mpro with IC50 = 19 nM; however, its off‐target activity was high. Our cathepsin inhibition assays demonstrated that the compound inhibited cathepsin B at low micromolar concentrations with SI = 23.2, whereas for cathepsins S and L, AVI‐8053 represented a potent inhibitor with IC50 values of 16 and 0.17 nM, respectively, and SIs of 0.84 and 0.09. Interestingly, inhibition of cathepsin L could be another strategy to block SARS‐CoV‐2 infection in humans since cathepsin L mediates the cleavage of the S1 subunit of the SARS‐CoV‐2 surface spike glycoprotein, the event required for coronavirus entry into human host cells, virus and host cell endosome membrane fusion, and viral RNA release for the next round of replication (Liu et al., 2020). Thus, having the cathepsins inhibition assay in place can facilitate the selection of compounds with specificity towards cathepsin L.

AVI‐8106 compound was also chosen due to its potency for viral Mpro, with IC50 of 7 nM. This compound revealed lower off‐target activity, with IC50 for cathepsin L being at 6 μM and SI = 853.2 and IC50s for cathepsins B and S—0.16 and 0.4 μM and SIs of 24.2 and 57.2, respectively.

In summary, the established assay is suitable for testing the off‐target effects of compounds targeting viral cysteine proteases for current and future outbreaks. Due to the fact that some viruses rely on host protease for entry, such as Ebola virus, filoviruses, and coronaviruses (Zhou et al., 2015) cathepsin inhibition assays could allow for the identification of inhibitors that will not only target the viral protease and thus stop viral replication but also inhibit infection by preventing viral entry through inhibiting host proteases. Overall, these assays, in addition to other tests such as bioavailability and cellular toxicity, will assist in the identification of the most promising antivirals to speed up drug development.

CONCLUSION

This paper offers a generalized purification protocol of recombinant cathepsins using a facile mammalian expression system, which will be suitable for groups without extensive protein purification equipment and experience. This work builds upon decades of knowledge by other researchers in the cathepsin field (Baker et al., 2003; Brix et al., 2008; Bromme et al., 2004; Kopitar et al., 1996). We have demonstrated the effectiveness of these methods with cathepsins B, S, and L, all of which resulted in active proteases after autocatalytic activation. Glycosylation sites and glycan structures were revealed for cathepsins B and L. We characterized each cathepsin's kinetic parameters using commercially available fluorescent substrates to ensure replicability in any laboratory. The expression, purification, and characterization of cathepsins were used to develop a not expensive, reproducible assay for screening inhibitors of cysteine proteases, which was shown to work using FDA‐approved nirmatrelvir to aid in drug development and pandemic preparedness.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Site‐directed mutagenesis of the constructs of cathepsins B, S, and L

pcDNA3.1 plasmids of hCathepsin B, hCathepsin L, and hCathepsin S were obtained from Addgene (Hyeryun Choe's lab Addgene plasmids #11249, #11250, and #11251) for expression in a mammalian expression system bearing a C‐terminal C9 tag (Huang et al., 2006). The C9 tag was mutated to a 6X‐His tag for purification purposes using the QuikChange® Lightning site‐directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent) by two sequential site‐directed mutagenesis steps.

Transfection and expression of cathepsins in Expi293™ cells

6X‐His tagged cathepsin B, S, and L were individually expressed in Expi293F™ cells as described in the Expi293F™ Expression System User Guide (ThermoFisher Scientific). Briefly, 50 mL cell cultures were grown to a density of 4.5–5.5 × 106 cells/mL and then diluted to a final transfection density of 3.0 × 106 cells/mL. Cells were transfected with 1.0 μg of plasmid DNA per mL of culture. Aliquots of cells and cell media were taken each day for 3 days post‐transfection for western blot analysis of expression levels.

For the western blot analysis, the aliquots of cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X‐100, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM EDTA) for 1.5 h at 4°C, followed by the removal of cell debris using centrifugation (20,000×g for 30 min at 4°C). Cell lysate and cell media samples were denatured at 95°C for 5 min and separated by SDS‐PAGE. Proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane overnight and detected using mouse anti‐6X His antibody, followed by peroxidase‐linked rabbit anti‐mouse antibody. Images were captured using the Li‐COR Odyssey Fc Imaging system. Visualized protein bands were compared to the expected molecular weight to determine cellular localization for purification.

Purification of cathepsins B, S, and L

Cathepsins were purified from the culture media. Expi293™ cells media was harvested 3 days post‐transfection. Expi293™ Expression Medium is not compatible with the Ni‐NTA purification system; therefore, buffer exchange was performed by dialysis with 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, and 10% glycerol buffer. The dialyzed media was then cleared by centrifugation (20,000×g for 15 min at 4°C) and loaded onto a HisPur™ Ni‐NTA resin column (ThermoFisher). After washing the resin with 10 column volumes of dialysis buffer containing 30 mM imidazole, the protein was eluted with 100–500 mM imidazole. Eluted fractions were analyzed by SDS‐PAGE; the samples containing protein of interest were pooled and dialyzed against acidic buffer (50 mM sodium acetate, pH 4.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT at 4°C) for maturation. Maturation of cathepsins was analyzed by SDS‐PAGE. The protein sample was then concentrated using an Amicon Ultra‐15 filter (MWCO of 10 kDa), aliquoted, flash frozen, and stored at −80°C.

Mass spectrometry

Measurements were performed on a Q‐Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Classic) and a Q Exactive Ultra‐High Mass Range (UHMR) Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Both instruments were equipped with a modified nanoflow ESI source. NanoESI tips were produced from borosilicate glass capillaries (1.0 mm outside diameter (o.d.), 0.78 mm inner diameter (i.d.), 10 cm length) using a P‐1000 micropipette puller. To perform nanoESI, a voltage of approximately +0.7 kV was applied to a platinum wire inserted inside the nanoESI tip and in contact with the solution. The solution temperature was 25°C. Resolutions (resolving power) were 17,500. Raw data were processed using the Thermo Xcalibur 4.7 software.

Production of N‐glycan libraries

N‐glycans were produced from cathepsins using PNGase F (New England Biolab, Massachusetts, USA) digestion using the following protocol. After dialysis in ammonium acetate solution (200 mM, pH 6.8), the proteins were incubated with PNGase F at 37°C for 24 h. The proteins were then dialyzed three times in water using Amicon Ultra‐0.5 mL centrifugal filters (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA) with a 10 kDa MW cut‐off. The flow‐through containing glycans was collected and lyophilized.

HILIC‐UHPLC‐ESI‐MS analysis

Glycans were labeled with procainamide, as described previously, prior to LC analysis (Bui et al., 2022). HILIC analysis of the glycan libraries was performed using a Thermo Scientific™ Vanquish™ UHPLC system coupled with a fluorescent (FLD) detector (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and ESI‐MS (Thermo Q Exactive Orbitrap). The glycans were separated using a Waters BEH Glycan column 150 × 2.1 mm i.d, with BEH particle size of 1.7 μm. The eluents were ammonium formate 100 mM (pH 4.5) (A) and acetonitrile (B). Separation was performed at a flow rate of 0.2 mL min−1 with the following gradients: 0–46.5 min, 75%–65% B; 46.5–80 min, 65%–55% B; 80–104.6 min, 55%–50% B. The injection volume was 2 μL. The column compartment temperature was maintained at 60°C.

The fluorescence excitation and detection wavelengths were λ ex = 310 and λ em = 370 nm. The ESI‐MS parameters were set as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 40 arb; capillary temperature, 275°C; probe heater temperature, 250°C; aux gas flow rate, 10 arb; spray voltage, 2.5 kV. The m/z range was 250–3000 in positive mode. The resolution for full MS analysis was at 70,000. The maximum injection time was 100 ms and the automatic gain control (AGC) target was at 1 × 106. The MS spectra were recorded using Xcalibur (Thermo, Version 4.2).

The glycan composition corresponding to each peak identified by HILIC analysis was preliminarily assigned according to the MS, MS/MS data with the aid of the Glycoworkbench software and known biosynthetic pathways of N‐glycan (Stanley et al., 2015).

nanoLC‐MS analysis

Each solution containing ~3 μg cathepsin protein was diluted in 4 M guanidine hydrochloride (pH 8.0) with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) to a 50 μL final volume, and incubated for 1 h at 42°C. The denatured and reduced protein was then alkylated with 20 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) for 45 min at room temperature in the dark. The resulting solution was diluted 10 times with ammonium acetate (pH 8.0) and digested using either trypsin (for cathepsin S and cathepsin L) at 37°C for 20 h or thermolysin (for cathepsin B) at 65°C for 4 h. The resulting (glyco)peptides were lyophilized and desalted using ZipTip (C18 resin) prior to nanoLC‐MS analysis.

nanoLC‐MS was performed on a Waters NanoACQUITY UPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA) coupled with a 15T solariX‐XR Fourier‐transform ion cyclotron resonance (FT‐ICR) mass spectrometer (Bruker‐Daltonics, Billerica, MA). Mobile phases A and B were water and acetonitrile, respectively, in the presence of 0.1% formic acid. For each injection, a 3 μL sample solution was first injected onto an ACQUITY UPLC M‐Class Symmetry C18 trap column (180 μm i.d., 20 mm length, particle size 5 μm) with 1% B and a flow rate of 4 μL min−1 for 4 min. After trapping, the solution was loaded onto a Waters ACQUITY UPLC M‐Class Peptide BEH C18 nanoLC column (150 μm i.d., 150 mm length, with BEH particle size of 1.7 μm). The (glyco)peptides separation was performed at 35°C with a flow rate of 0.3 μL min‐1 and a gradient of 1%–40% B in 80 min. The FT‐ICR mass spectrometer was operated in data‐dependent acquisition mode (positive ion mode) using FtmsControl software (version 2.2). The ions/droplets were transferred to the mass spectrometer using a nanoESI silica emitter of 10 μm tip i.d. (New Objective, Littleton, MA), with 2000 V voltage and a source drying gas (N2, 1.2 L min−1 and 180°C) to aid with desolvation. The ions were steered by a deflector (220 V) into the funnel (150 V)–skimmer (30 V) stage and accumulated in a hexapole for 0.2 s (MS) or 1.0 s (MS/MS). For each MS scan, the top six most abundant ions were selected, sequentially isolated in the quadrupole, and subjected to MS/MS fragmentation (collision gas Ar at ~7 × 10–6 mbar and collision energies 30–50 V). Finally, the ions were injected into the ParaCell for detection (sweep excitation power 30%, pressure ~ 5 × 10–10 mbar). The resolution was set to 1 M data point per transient. The data were analyzed using BioTools version 3.2 and DataAnalysis version 5.0 (Bruker). The MS1 and MS2 mass tolerance were set to 5 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively. Variable modifications included carbamidomethylation of cysteine (+57.021 Da) and oxidation of methionine (+15.995 Da). In addition, glycosylation on the asparagine residues (i.e., predicted N‐glycosylation sites based on the protein sequence) was added for the database search. The identification of (glyco)peptides was confirmed by MS/MS fragments followed by manual inspection of the peak assignments.

Enzyme kinetics of cathepsins B, S, and L

Commercially available fluorescent substrates specific for each of cathepsins B, S, and L were used for the fluorescence‐based cleavage assay. Ac‐RR‐AFC (Abcam) or Z‐RR‐AMC (SigmaAldrich, Canada) peptides were used as cathepsin B substrates, Z‐VVR‐AFC (Abcam) or Z‐VVR‐AMC (VWR) as cathepsin S substrates, and Ac‐FR‐AFC (Abcam) as the cathepsin L substrate. The fluorescence‐based assay was performed in activity buffer (50 mM sodium acetate, pH 5.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT) at 37°C. For cathepsins B and S, a different activity buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT) was also used for Michaelis–Menten curves. For all substrates, except the cathepsin B substrate Z‐RR‐AMC and the cathepsin S substrate Z‐VVR‐AMC, an excitation wavelength of 400 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm were used. For the Z‐RR‐AMC cathepsin B substrate, excitation and emission wavelengths were 380 and 460 nm, respectively, and for the cathepsin S substrate, they were 350 and 450 nm. All readings were taken using the Cytation 5 imaging reader (Biotek). Cathepsin B and S cleavage assays were monitored for 20 min, and cathepsin L for 10 min. The optimal protease concentration was determined to be the minimal protease concentration that gave an optimal linear relationship between the amount of generated product and the chosen time. Initial velocities in relative fluorescence units per unit of time (ΔRFU/min) were converted to the amount of cleaved substrate per unit time (μM/min) by measuring the relationship between total fluorescence emitted and substrate concentrations for each cathepsin substrate. The actual active site concentration of cathepsins was determined by titration with E‐64, an irreversible inhibitor of cathepsins, and was found to be within 4% of the calculated theoretical concentration (50 nM).

Kinetic parameters (K M and V max) were derived by fitting the plot of initial velocities versus substrate concentration to the Michaelis–Menten equation, v = V max × [S]/(K M + [S]) using GraphPad Prism software. k cat/K M was calculated according to the equation, k cat/K M = V max/([E] × K M). Triplicate experiments were performed for each data point.

Determination of pH‐activity dependence for cathepsins

Carmody universal buffer was used to determine the optimal pH of protease activity for each cathepsin. The buffer was made as described in Carmody (1963) at each pH unit between 2 and 9. Briefly, solution “A” containing 0.2 M boric acid and 0.05 M citric acid and solution “B” containing 0.1 M of trisodium phosphate were mixed at different ratios. The actual pH of the final mixed solutions was determined using a pH meter. Fluorescence‐based kinetic reactions were performed as described above. Protease concentration was fixed at 50 nM and cathepsin‐specific substrate concentrations were chosen to be around K M values for each substrate. Cathepsin activity was tested over a pH range of 2–9 for cathepsins B and S and a pH range of 2–8 for cathepsin L.

Cathepsins inhibition assay

Stock solutions of three inhibitors were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). For determination of the IC50, 50 nM of cathepsin B, S, or L was preincubated with inhibitor at a concentration range from 0.1 nM to 2 mM in activity buffer for 10 min. The reactions were started by the addition of cathepsin‐specific substrate. Cathepsin B substrate (Z‐RR‐AMC) was used at a concentration of 300 μM. Cathepsin S substrate (Z‐VVR‐AFC) concentration was 30 μM, and cathepsin L substrate (Ac‐FR‐AFC) concentration was 100 μM. The GraphPad Prism software was used for calculation of the IC50 values.
