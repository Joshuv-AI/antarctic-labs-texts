Patterns of follow-up testing of abnormal eGFR and UACR for the detection of chronic kidney disease in Australian primary care: analysis of a national general practice dataset
Authors: Angela Kathy Li, Sradha Kotwal, Hannah Wallace, Daniel Bekele Ketema, James Wick, Brendon L Neuen, Michael O Falster, Jialing Lin, Sallie-Anne Pearson, David Peiris, Meg J Jardine, Mark Woodward, John Chalmers, Paul E Ronksley, Min Jun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the patterns of abnormal estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urine albumin–creatinine ratio (UACR) follow-up testing for the detection of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in Australian general practices.DesignRetrospective, population-based observational study.Setting and participants2 717 966 adults who visited a MedicineInsight participating general practice between 1 January 2012 and 31 December 2020, had ≥1 serum creatinine measurement (with or without a UACR measurement) and did not have CKD at baseline.Main outcome measure‘Guideline-concordant follow-up’ was defined as having a record of a repeat eGFR or UACR testing (assessed separately) within 6 months following the abnormal (eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73 m2; UACR≥2.5 mg/mmol in males, ≥3.5 mg/mmol in females) incident result. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify patient factors associated with receiving appropriate follow-up testing.ResultsA total of 220 841 and 114 889 patients with an abnormal incident eGFR and UACR result, respectively, were identified. Nearly half (45.0%) of the patients with an abnormal eGFR result and over two-thirds (69.7%) of the patients with an abnormal UACR result did not have a follow-up test within 6 months. Patient factors associated with a higher likelihood of follow-up eGFR testing included indicators of poorer baseline health and greater CKD risk, such as comorbid diabetes (adjusted OR 1.36, 95% CI 1.32 to 1.40) or more severe incident eGFR (adjusted ORs for eGFR categories 30–44, 15–29 and <15 mL/min/1.73 m², respectively, vs eGFR 45–59 mL/min/1.73 m²: 1.51 (1.47 to 1.55), 1.85 (1.76 to 1.95) and 1.62 (1.47 to 1.78)). Higher incident UACR level was associated with greater follow-up UACR testing (adjusted OR for severely increased albuminuria vs moderately increased albuminuria (OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.37 to 1.48).ConclusionsIn this large, population-based study, we observed substantial gaps in the follow-up of abnormal eGFR and UACR for the detection of CKD in primary care settings. Effective strategies to optimise follow-up testing for CKD detection are needed.

Introduction

In Australia, chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 1 in 10 adults, representing more than 2 million people. The burden of CKD continues to increase1 2—in 2021, CKD contributed to approximately 20 000 deaths, representing 12% of all deaths recorded.3 Early detection and the implementation of preventative CKD care strategies are critical in improving patient outcomes, including slowing progression of disease and prevention of adverse outcomes including cardiovascular disease.

Emerging evidence to date, however, suggests suboptimal detection of CKD in general practice settings. Studies in Canada4 5 found that the percentage of patients receiving follow-up testing after an initial abnormal estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or urine albumin–creatinine ratio (UACR) result ranged from 18% to 47%. Other studies assessing eGFR and UACR testing rates in patients with diabetes also reported suboptimal testing.6 7 For example, in a primary care-based study of 37 604 patients with diabetes, only 13% of patients with diabetes received annual eGFR and UACR testing for CKD.6 In Australia, Kidney Health Australia-Caring for Australians and New Zealanders with Kidney Impairment (KHA-CARI) guidelines8 recommend follow-up of abnormal incident eGFR (<60 mL/min/1.73 m2) and UACR (≥2.5 mg/mmol for males, ≥3.5 mg/mmol for females) within 3 months. However, data on the extent to which follow-up of patients in routine clinical practice is consistent with clinical guidelines are limited. Understanding the patterns and factors associated with optimal CKD detection in contemporary clinical practice can help monitor processes of care and inform the development of future quality improvement strategies.

We therefore sought to assess the extent to which follow-up of abnormal test results for the detection of CKD is consistent with guideline recommendations and identify factors associated with guideline-concordant follow-up testing.

Methods

Study design and data source

We performed a retrospective population-based cohort study based on Australian general practice data from the MedicineInsight programme. MedicineInsight is a national general practice data source based on routinely collected electronic patient medical records from participating general practices across all states and territories of Australia. It includes de-identified longitudinal electronic health records on patient information such as sociodemographic characteristics, clinical and laboratory measurements and prescribed medications collected from clinical information systems of participating general practices. Additional information on the MedicineInsight programme is available elsewhere.911

Identification of the study population

The base cohort included 2 717 966 adults (age≥18 years) who visited a MedicineInsight participating general practice (n=392) and had ≥1 serum creatinine measurement (with or without a UACR measurement) between 1 January 2012 and 31 December 2020. Patients were grouped into two separate study cohorts based on the incident eGFR (hereinafter referred to as the ‘eGFR cohort’) or UACR measurement (‘UACR cohort’; figure 1). There was some overlap in patient inclusion between the two study cohorts (9% (n=20 886) of patients in the eGFR cohort were concurrently included in the UACR cohort; 50% (n=57 742) of patients in the UACR cohort were concurrently included in the eGFR cohort). Index date (baseline) was defined as the date of the first eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or first elevated UACR (males≥2.5 mg/mmol, females≥3.5 mg/mmol). Patients with existing CKD (defined as having a record of a CKD indicator ‘flag’, eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and/or elevated UACR in the period prior to and including 1 January 2012) were excluded. MedicineInsight includes calculated eGFR which is routinely reported together with serum creatinine results. In order to account for differences in eGFR arising from the use of different eGFR equations over the study period (Modification of Diet in Renal Disease and CKD Epidemiology Collaboration), we recalculated eGFR using the 2009 CKD Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation (without race).12

Covariates

Patient sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, smoking status, rurality,13 state, socioeconomic advantage (Index of Relative Socioeconomic Advantage and Disadvantage decile (IRSAD14)), comorbid conditions (a recorded diagnosis of diabetes, hypertension, coronary heart disease, stroke and heart failure), clinical measurements (eGFR, UACR, blood pressure, lipid profile and haemoglobin A1c) and prescribed medicines (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), diuretics, statins, metformin, insulin, sulfonylureas, sodium glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP1-RA) and dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP4i); online supplemental table 1) were assessed in the year prior to the index testing date.

Study outcomes

KHA-CARI clinical practice guidelines8 recommend repeated testing of eGFR or UACR within 3 months of the abnormal incident eGFR (<60 mL/min/1.73 m2) or UACR (males≥2.5 mg/mmol, females≥3.5 mg/mmol) result. In the current study, we defined ‘guideline-concordant follow-up’ as having a record of a repeat eGFR or UACR testing (assessed separately) at any time within 6 months of an abnormal incident test, double the follow-up testing time recommended by the KHA-CARI guidelines. A follow-up period of 6 months after an abnormal incident test (ie, an additional 3-month period) was used to account for potential delays that may affect completion of follow-up testing in routine general practice. The following outcomes were assessed: (1) percentage of patients with an incident eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73 m2 who were followed up with a repeat eGFR test within 6 months, and (2) percentage of patients with an elevated UACR (males≥2.5 mg/mmol, females≥3.5 mg/mmol) who were followed up with a repeat UACR test within 6 months.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are described using percentages. Continuous variables with approximately normal distributions are reported as means with SD, while skewed variables are reported as medians with interquartile interval (IQI). We assessed the number and percentage of patients who received follow-up testing within 6 months overall and by patient subgroups (age, sex, rurality, socioeconomic advantage and disadvantage and comorbid conditions) and year (2012–2020). Unadjusted and multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate ORs and their corresponding 95% CIs to determine the factors associated with guideline-concordant follow-up testing. Age, sex, rurality, socioeconomic status, smoking status, baseline eGFR (<15, 15–29, 30–44 or 45–60 mL/min/1.73 m2) or UACR (moderately increased or severely increased albuminuria) categories, comorbid conditions and prescribed medications were included as covariates in the regression models. A two-sided p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To test for the robustness of study findings, we repeated analyses based on different definitions of guideline-concordant follow-up. First, the number and percentage of patients (and the associations between the extent of follow-up testing and patient characteristics) who received follow-up eGFR or UACR testing were assessed at different outcome ascertainment periods of 3, 9, 12, 15 and 18 months. Second, we sought to account for instances of eGFR follow-up testing that were intended to rule out acute kidney injury as per CKD guidelines8 (for repeat testing of eGFR, KHA guidelines recommend follow-up at different time periods to assess for possible acute kidney injury (repeat testing within 7–14 days)) and chronicity of disease (repeat testing within 3 months). We thus repeated analyses using guideline-concordant eGFR follow-up testing defined as (1) patients with a repeat eGFR test recorded in days 31–180 after the incident eGFR measurement, or (2) patients with an eGFR≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 in days 1–30 after the incident eGFR measurement. Third, we assessed follow-up eGFR and UACR testing among ‘active’ patients only—those who regularly visited a MedicineInsight participating general practice during the study period. An active patient was defined as someone who visited the general practice at least three times in the 24-month period prior to the index date.15

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (18.0, StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study population consisted of 220 841 patients in the eGFR cohort and 114 889 patients in the UACR cohort. Baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the cohorts are shown in table 1. Of those in the eGFR cohort, 54.5% were female and the mean age was 72.7 (SD 13.1) years. In the UACR cohort, 43.8% were female and the mean age was 64.2 (SD 16.0) years. Most patients had evidence of mild-to-moderate kidney damage at their incident test (80.2% had eGFR 45–59 mL/min/1.73 m2 while 86.8% had moderately increased albuminuria). Hypertension was the most commonly recorded comorbid condition, reported in 50.0% and 57.5% of the patients in the eGFR and UACR cohorts, respectively. Diabetes was reported in 17.9% and 44.5% of the patients in the eGFR and UACR cohorts, respectively.

Guideline-concordant follow-up testing

Over a 12-month period, the median number of days between the abnormal incident and follow-up results was 73 days (IQI 17–173 days) in the eGFR cohort. Overall, 55.0% of patients (n=121 556) received follow-up eGFR testing within 6 months of an initial abnormal eGFR result (figure 2a). Patients with lower incident eGFR values generally had higher percentages of guideline-concordant eGFR follow-up testing (figure 2b). Subgroup analysis showed that guideline-concordant eGFR follow-up testing was more frequent in patients with comorbid diabetes (66.8%), hypertension (57.9%) and coronary heart disease (62.2%) (online supplemental figure 1b).

(a) Percentage of patients who received follow-up estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or urine albumin–creatinine ratio (UACR) testing within 6 months of the incident measurement. (b) Percentage of patients who received eGFR or UACR follow-up testing within 6 months of the incident measurement by baseline eGFR and UACR categories.

Over a 12-month period, the median number of days between the abnormal incident UACR result and the follow-up result was 132 days (IQI 50–220 days). Overall, 30.3% of patients (n=34 761) received follow-up UACR testing within 6 months of an initial abnormal UACR result (figure 2a). Patients with more severe albuminuria at baseline had higher percentages of guideline concordant UACR follow-up testing (figure 2b). Subgroup analysis (according to age, sex, rurality, socioeconomic status and comorbid conditions) showed largely consistent results (online supplemental figure 1a,b).

Temporal trends in guideline-concordant follow-up testing

Figure 3a, b shows the trends in guideline-concordant eGFR and UACR follow-up testing over a 9-year period (2012–2020). There was a steady increase in the percentage of patients who received follow-up eGFR tests within 6 months, from 46.6% in 2012 to 60.7% in 2020 (p<0.001). Similarly, the percentage of patients who received follow-up UACR tests within 6 months incrementally rose from 25.5% in 2012 to 32.8% in 2020 (p<0.001). Subgroup analyses (according to age, sex and comorbid conditions) showed largely similar trends of increasing rates of guideline-concordant follow-up eGFR and UACR testing over time from 2012 to 2020 (online supplemental figures 2–5).

Factors associated with guideline-concordant follow-up testing

The factors associated with receiving guideline-concordant follow-up eGFR testing are shown in figure 4 and online supplemental table 2a. When compared with patients aged 18–29 years, the odds of receiving a follow-up eGFR test within 6 months were similar among those aged 30–69 years, but lower in those aged ≥70 years (adjusted ORs (95% CI) for age 70–79, 80–89 and ≥90 years: 0.92 (0.82 to 1.02), 0.80 (0.72 to 0.90) and 0.67 (0.60 to 0.75), respectively). Patients who lived in regional or remote Australia had higher odds (1.05 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.07)) of guideline-concordant follow-up eGFR testing compared with those living in major cities of Australia. Lower incident eGFR values (adjusted ORs (95% CI) for eGFR 30–44, 15–29 and <15 compared with 45–59 mL/min/1.73 m2: 1.51 (1.47 to 1.55), 1.85 (1.76 to 1.95) and 1.62 (1.47 to 1.78), respectively) and the presence of comorbid conditions (eg, diabetes: 1.36 (1.32 to 1.40)) were associated with higher odds of receiving follow-up eGFR testing within 6 months.

The factors associated with receiving guideline-concordant follow-up UACR testing are shown in figure 4 and online supplemental table 2b. Findings on age and the presence of comorbid conditions were largely consistent with those for follow-up eGFR testing. Females were 16% more likely to receive follow-up UACR testing compared with males (adjusted OR 1.16 (95% CI 1.13 to 1.19)). Higher incident UACR values were associated with higher odds of receiving follow-up UACR testing (adjusted ORs (95% CI) for severely increased vs moderately increased UACR: 1.42 (1.37 to 1.48)). Increasing socioeconomic advantage was associated with higher odds of receiving guideline-concordant UACR follow-up (adjusted ORs (95% CI) for IRSAD deciles 4–7 and 8–10 compared with IRSAD deciles 1–3: 1.09 (1.05 to 1.12) and 1.05 (1.00 to 1.09), respectively).

Sensitivity analysis

The percentage of patients receiving eGFR and UACR follow-up testing progressively increased as the duration of the outcome ascertainment period was extended from 3 to 18 months (up to 79.9% and 58.5% for eGFR and UACR follow-up testing at 18 months; online supplemental table 3a, b; online supplemental figure 6a–d). The associations between patient characteristics and follow-up testing (eGFR and UACR) were largely consistent across the different ascertainment periods (data not shown). Overall findings remained largely consistent in sensitivity analyses where (1) guideline-concordant follow-up was defined differently to account for instances of eGFR follow-up testing that were intended to rule out acute kidney injury (the percentage of patients receiving guideline-concordant follow-up eGFR testing was 49.9%; online supplemental figure 7) and (2) follow-up eGFR and UACR testing was assessed among active patients only (online supplemental table 4).

Discussion

In this large population-based study of over 220 000 people who visited a general practice across Australia, gaps in guideline-recommended follow-up testing of abnormal incident eGFR and UACR results for the detection of CKD were frequently observed. Gaps in timely follow-up testing existed in nearly half (45.0%) of the patients with an abnormal eGFR result and over two-thirds (69.7%) of the patients with an abnormal UACR. We found that the factors reflecting poorer baseline health, such as lower incident eGFR and higher UACR results, the presence of comorbidities, as well as concurrent medicine use, were associated with higher likelihood of being followed up.

Early identification of CKD allows for timely intervention, including the implementation of effective strategies that prevent or delay the progression of CKD and its associated complications. Our results, however, indicate considerable potential for improvement in the follow-up testing for the detection of CKD in contemporary clinical practice in Australia. Few studies have assessed eGFR and UACR follow-up testing rates across broad patient populations within primary care. Although not specifically focused on primary care settings, a study in the USA based on data from a large integrated healthcare delivery system (n=244 540 patients within Kaiser Permanente Southern California) found that 58% of patients experienced an absence in timely follow-up of their initial abnormal eGFR result.16 In a study which assessed the quality of CKD management based on Canadian primary care data, 39% of patients received a repeated UACR test within 6 months of the abnormal result.4 Other studies specifically among patients with diabetes6 7 17 show substantial opportunities for improvements in CKD detection, particularly measurement of UACR (eg, 52.9% of patients with type 2 diabetes managed in primary care had ≥1 UACR measurement over 1 year).7 Accumulating data thus indicate suboptimal processes of care for the detection of CKD in primary care.

To this end, our study provides some insights for strategies aimed at addressing this gap. Our findings confirm, within a large, nationally representative cohort of patients, that gaps in evidence-based follow-up testing for CKD are substantially more pronounced for UACR testing, highlighting a particular need to focus on increasing UACR testing in primary care. The reasons behind this care gap are likely multifaceted and related to a range of patient and provider factors. For example, findings from qualitative studies1822 indicate a lack of awareness of clinical guidelines and a perceived lack of clear definition of CKD as barriers to optimal CKD detection. It is thus possible that suboptimal follow-up UACR testing observed in our study may be attributable, in part, to a lack of awareness of the utility of UACR testing1821 2326 (independent of eGFR assessments) for the detection of CKD. This is supported by our study’s findings indicating inconsistent prioritisation of CKD testing in high-risk patient groups. While the presence of diabetes was a significant driver of increased follow-up testing (a finding consistent with several prior studies162733 which have demonstrated similar correlations between the presence of cardiometabolic comorbidities and higher quality CKD care), the presence of hypertension, a major risk factor for CKD and its progression, was associated with a lower probability of follow-up UACR testing. Quality improvement strategies that focus on optimising evidence-based UACR testing for the detection of CKD are needed.

On the other hand, we also observed a progressive increase in the percentages of patients receiving guideline-concordant follow-up testing of eGFR (46.6% to 60.7%) and UACR (25.5% to 32.8%) from 2012 to 2020. These results are encouraging, suggesting an increased uptake of clinical practice guidelines relevant to detection of CKD following the introduction of recommendations for albuminuria testing in 2012.34 Overall patterns of follow-up eGFR and UACR testing that indicate prioritisation of at-risk patient populations, including those with greater comorbid burden (notwithstanding some of the inconsistencies as described above), are also reassuring. This increasing trend in testing may be linked to several factors, including the adoption of electronic systems (automatic integration of laboratory results and clinical alerts)3537 and prior and ongoing strategic initiatives38 39 aimed at increasing awareness of CKD and improving its detection and management.

Nevertheless, our results show that follow-up testing rates remained suboptimal, even among those who are at higher risk of developing CKD such as those with diabetes (68% with diabetes did not have a repeated UACR test). The prognostic impact of this gap is particularly greater in such high-risk patient populations who might benefit most in absolute terms from readily available treatment strategies (eg, ACEI, SGLT2i and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists).40 Our findings thus highlight the importance of targeted approaches to enhancing follow-up testing rates, especially in high-risk patient groups such as those with diabetes and hypertension, as well as ongoing surveillance programmes to monitor patterns of care to inform future quality improvement strategies. Additionally, qualitative studies indicate difficulty in keeping up to date with changing guidelines as a barrier to CKD management,41 which may help explain the more modest increase in UACR testing rates relative to eGFR testing observed in the current study. Approaches for further enhancing dissemination of up-to-date guidelines are warranted.

This study benefits from the analysis of a large, nationally representative population-based cohort of patients from across various geographic regions, including urban and remote. In addition, the study assessed longitudinal data spanning a 9-year period, including time periods reflecting contemporary clinical practice. However, our study also has some limitations. While our study derived data from a cohort of over 2.7 million people from 392 general practices across Australia, we do not have data on individuals who visited general practices not participating in MedicineInsight (including those lost to follow-up following the incident eGFR and UACR test due to moving to another provider); however, patients attending MedicineInsight-participating general practices have been shown9 42 to be representative of the general Australian population with respect to sociodemographic characteristics, including age and sex. As patient registration to a single general practice is not required in Australia (compared with other countries such as Canada and the USA), this may have led to instances of follow-up testing carried out in other general practices, some of which may have occurred in practices not participating in MedicineInsight. Laboratory test results ordered in specialist care settings, including secondary and tertiary care services, may be included in MedicineInsight if they were electronically transferred to the general practice or manually entered into the practice’s clinical information system.9 However, data in MedicineInsight reflect primary care settings, and information on follow-up testing within secondary or tertiary care services (eg, following referral to a nephrologist) may not be fully captured. Further, information on clinical events that may have impacted on follow-up testing, including hospital admission, is not systematically captured. It is thus possible that these factors led to an underestimation of the rate of follow-up eGFR and UACR testing observed in the current study. Given the nature of the available data within MedicineInsight, we were unable to (1) explore the reasons for the absence of follow-up eGFR and UACR testing following an abnormal result and (2) ascertain whether the follow-up test was specifically conducted in response to the initial abnormal result (and thereby more reliably assess the extent to which clinical care relevant to the detection of CKD is consistent with clinical practice guidelines).

In conclusion, we observed substantial gaps in follow-up eGFR and UACR testing following an incident abnormal result for the detection of CKD in Australian general practice. Our findings highlight the need for effective strategies to optimise follow-up testing for CKD detection to facilitate early intervention, slow disease progression and improve patient outcomes.
