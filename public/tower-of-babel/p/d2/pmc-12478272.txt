Identification of potential therapeutic targets for problematic alcohol use using multi-omics data
Authors: Dong June Lee, Minku Song, Soohyun Lim, Sanghyeon Park, Hyejin Kim, Yeeun Ahn, Faith Choi, Woojae Myung, Hong-Hee Won
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundProblematic alcohol use (PAU) is a serious global health issue with limited treatment options. Although genetic studies have identified genomic regions associated with PAU, the specific causal genes and proteins remain unclear. This study aimed to identify these causal genes by integrating diverse genetic and molecular data to inform better treatment strategies.MethodsWe used Mendelian randomisation (MR) to identify genes and proteins that may cause PAU by combining genetic data with gene activity in brain and blood tissues. Multiple statistical tests confirmed shared genetic signals between expression and PAU. We also examined their effects on other traits and potential interactions with existing drugs.FindingsWe identified 97 genes and 13 proteins that are likely to play a causal role in PAU through MR, with strong enrichment in brain tissues. These associations remained significant after Bonferroni correction and were further supported by colocalisation analyses (posterior probability of hypothesis 4 > 0.75) and consistency across multiple datasets. Some targets also showed concordant effects with PAU on other health outcomes, supported by Bonferroni-significant associations in phenome-wide analyses, suggesting potential for drug repurposing.InterpretationThis study highlights molecular signatures in both brain and blood tissues that may contribute to the development of PAU. Integrative multi-omics analyses revealed shared biological pathways across neural and peripheral systems, suggesting the potential for developing multi-systemic interventions with favourable safety profiles.FundingThis study was supported by Samsung Research Fund, Sungkyunkwan University, 2023.Keywords: Problematic alcohol use, Multi-omics, Expression quantitative trait loci, Protein quantitative trait loci, Mendelian randomisation

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Previous genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified multiple genetic loci associated with problematic alcohol use (PAU). However, these studies have several limitations, including small sample sizes, a predominant focus on brain tissue, and a lack of multi-omics integration. Moreover, while GWASs can identify risk loci, they do not provide information on causal genes or proteins within those loci. Mendelian randomisation (MR) is a method that can help reveal cause-and-effect relationships between genes and disease, but previous MR studies rarely used data from multiple tissues or molecular layers. Consequently, the number of effective drug targets for PAU remains small, with only three approved medications that show inconsistent efficacy among patients.

Added value of this study

Our study presents a comprehensive, multi-layered genetic approach (multi-omics MR) to investigate PAU by integrating gene expression and protein data from both brain and blood tissues. By applying multiple MR methods, we identified genes and proteins that may directly influence PAU, confirming these findings across independent datasets. Among the strongest targets, genes such as AMT, CCDC88B, and YPEL3 showed consistent evidence across tissues and omics layers. We also identified other targets, such as GPX1 and PPP6C, that may have broader health benefits. Incorporating blood-derived data enabled the discovery of markers that are potentially more accessible for detection and therapeutic targeting. Overall, our study provides a robust roadmap for finding and validating treatment targets for alcohol-related problems.

Implications of all the available evidence

The integration of multi-omics data with MR provides a robust framework for identifying and validating causal targets for complex disorders such as PAU. Our findings suggest that genetic mechanisms relevant to PAU can be detected in multiple tissues, including the brain and blood, highlighting the utility of a multi-tissue approach in identifying pathways that may be centrally mediated and peripherally expressed in disease pathophysiology. The identification of genes and proteins with consistent evidence across omics layers and tissues, such as AMT, CCDC88B, and GPX1, offers promising avenues for the development of targeted therapies. Collectively, these results support the use of genetic evidence to prioritise biologically relevant targets for alcohol-related disorders. However, given that many existing drugs interacting with these targets are unsuitable for clinical use due to toxicity or regulatory concerns,1 our findings more appropriately serve as a foundation for future drug discovery rather than immediate therapeutic application. This highlights the value of a genetically informed, systems biology approach to guide the development of safer and more effective treatments.

Introduction

Excessive alcohol consumption is a major contributor to morbidity and mortality globally. Alcohol use results in three million deaths annually, accounting for 5.3% of all global deaths.2 Approximately 12% of alcohol users meet the criteria for alcohol dependence (AD) during their lifetime.3 Alcohol use significantly increases the risk of psychiatric and chronic diseases, contributing to substantial social and economic burdens.4,5 In genetic studies, problematic alcohol use (PAU) is commonly used as a composite phenotype that captures a broader spectrum of maladaptive drinking behaviours, including AD, clinically diagnosed alcohol use disorder (AUD), and index of the information pertaining to alcohol problems (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test-Problems),6 thereby enabling the identification of genetic factors relevant to both early risk and severe outcomes. A detailed description is provided in the Supplementary Notes (Supplementary Text). Currently, the Food and Drug Administration has approved only three medications for treating PAU. These medications have varying degrees of effectiveness among patients because of the heterogeneous nature of PAU, highlighting the need for new drugs with diverse mechanisms of action.7

Advances in genomic research offer promising opportunities for addressing these unmet needs. Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified numerous genetic regions linked to alcohol use.8,9 However, translating specific causal genes within these loci remains challenging, as each locus often contains multiple genes, and associations identified in GWASs do not imply causality.10 Moreover, previous studies were limited by small sample sizes,8,9 a predominant focus on brain tissue,8,9,11 and a lack of multi-omics integration.8,9 Addressing these limitations requires larger datasets, multi-tissue analyses, and integrated multi-omics approaches to identify causal and druggable targets for PAU.

Mendelian randomisation (MR) uses genetic data to assess whether a biological factor, such as a gene or protein, might play a causal role in a health outcome. This approach is increasingly used to identify causal and druggable targets, highlighting its value in evaluating therapeutic potential and enabling drug repurposing.12,13 When combined with multi-omics data, MR can integrate insights across molecular layers, enhancing the likelihood of identifying effective therapeutic targets. Drug targets supported by genetic evidence demonstrate a 2.6- to 22-fold higher success rate in development, further establishing MR as a powerful tool in biomedical research.13,14

In this study, we combined MR with multiple types of biological data, including gene expression measured through transcriptome-wide Mendelian randomisation (TWMR) and protein levels assessed using proteome-wide Mendelian randomisation (PWMR), to identify potential drug targets for PAU. We also applied a statistical approach called colocalisation to determine whether the same genetic signals influence both PAU and gene or protein levels, which helps to strengthen causal interpretations.

We prioritised targets that showed consistent evidence across independent datasets and then tested whether these targets were linked to other health conditions using phenome-wide Mendelian randomisation (PheWMR). This helped us understand the broader effects of each target, including their potential relevance to alcohol-related outcomes. This multifaceted validation approach enabled the identification of therapeutic targets detectable across tissues, indicating potential involvement in both the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral biological processes related to PAU.

Methods

Ethics

All data analysed in this study are summary-level and publicly available, with no access to individual-level information. Ethical approvals and informed consent were obtained in the original GWASs, which are cited for each dataset. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. B-2407-912-109).

Multi-omics data utilised in Mendelian randomisation

All multi-omics data utilised in the MR analysis (Supplementary Table S1) were matched to the ancestry of the corresponding GWAS datasets to ensure consistency and reliability. This matching helped minimise the effects of population stratification, maintain homogeneity in linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns, and preserve the validity of instrumental variables (IVs) within each population.15,16 We prioritised the use of HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC) symbols when reporting genes. For genes lacking HGNC-approved symbols, the Ensembl Gene stable ID was reported.

We utilised GWAS summary statistics for PAU from the largest genetic study to date, which included a multi-ancestry cohort. Although the primary analysis focused on individuals of European (EUR) ancestry (N = 903,147), we also conducted complementary analyses in East Asian (EAS) (N = 13,551), African (AFR) (N = 122,571), and South Asian (SAS) (N = 1716) populations.8 PAU is defined as a meta-analyses of traits with high genetic correlations (rg > 0.7), including AUD as per the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), AD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM–IV), and problematic drinking scores identified via the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test–Problems (AUDIT–P) questionnaire.8,9 The dataset was curated from several large-scale cohorts, including the Million Veteran Program (MVP), FinnGen, UK Biobank (UKBB), Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC), QIMR Berghofer Medical Research Institute, iPSYCH, and Yale-Penn. Collectively, the EUR dataset comprised 113,325 patients with AUD or AD, 639,923 controls, and 149,899 participants with AUDIT–P scores.8 In addition, the AFR ancestry dataset included 40,116 patients with AUD or AD and 82,455 controls; the EAS dataset consisted of 2254 patients and 11,297 controls; and the SAS dataset included 107 AUD patients, 389 controls, and 1220 individuals with AUDIT–P scores.8

To identify the causal gene expression pattern for PAU in brain tissue, we utilised three transcriptomic datasets. First, data from the PsychENCODE consortium provide detailed expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) summary statistics from the prefrontal cortex (N = 1387), with adjustments for 50 probabilistic estimation of expression residuals (PEER) factors.17 Knowledge of this region is critical for understanding the neurobiological foundations of PAU. Second, the BrainMeta V2 data included eQTL data from a broader brain cortex (N = 2865), with approximately 70% of the samples derived from the prefrontal cortex.18 Third, the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project V8 release data includes eQTL data from 13 distinct brain regions19: the amygdala (N = 129), anterior cingulate cortex (BA24) (N = 147), caudate (basal ganglia) (N = 194), cerebellar hemisphere (N = 175), cerebellum (N = 209), cortex (N = 205), frontal cortex (BA9) (N = 175), hippocampus (N = 165), hypothalamus (N = 170), nucleus accumbens (basal ganglia) (N = 202), putamen (basal ganglia) (N = 170), spinal cord (cervical c-1) (N = 126), and substantia nigra (N = 114). In addition to brain eQTL data, we utilised a blood transcriptomics dataset from the eQTLGen consortium (N = 31,684).20 This dataset captures gene expression variability in the blood, providing a peripheral perspective on genetic influences and allowing for comparison with the central effects observed in brain tissues.

For brain proteomic analysis, we utilised a protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) dataset encompassing six brain regions, as detailed by Wingo et al.21 This dataset included data from 1277 individuals, with approximately 60% of the samples derived from the prefrontal cortex, a region pivotal for understanding the neurobiological underpinnings of PAU. We used two plasma proteomics datasets to extend our analysis to the blood proteome. The first dataset from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study included pQTL data from 7213 participants.22 The second dataset was from Icelandic cohorts and encompassed pQTL data from 35,892 participants.23 These datasets provide extensive coverage of protein-level genetic associations in the blood, enabling a detailed examination of how these associations might contribute to PAU.

Tissue-specific enrichment analysis

To evaluate the tissue-specific enrichment of PAU, we employed the linkage disequilibrium score regression applied to specifically expressed genes (LDSC–SEG) method to assess heritability enrichment within genes that exhibited significant tissue-specific expression.24 To mitigate potential confounding effects arising from complex LD patterns, we excluded the human leucocyte antigen region from our analysis.24

We used precomputed annotation files (LD scores) and tissue type categories provided by Finucane et al.24 These files encompass 205 tissues derived from the GTEx V6p dataset25 and Franke lab dataset.26,27 This analysis enabled us to identify tissues with significant heritability enrichment, thereby identifying the tissues most relevant to PAU for subsequent MR analyses.

Mendelian randomisation

All MR analyses were conducted using a two-sample MR framework, which estimated the associations of genetic instruments with exposure and outcome from independent (non-overlapping) samples. This approach leverages the largest available GWAS datasets for both exposure and outcome, thereby enhancing the statistical power.28

Transcriptome-wide Mendelian randomisation

We employed the summary data–based Mendelian randomisation (SMR) software tool (version 1.3.1)29 to conduct TWMR analyses. This tool combines the SMR and heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) methods, facilitating the investigation of associations between gene expression levels and complex traits using summary-level data from eQTLs and GWASs.29 Particularly, SMR estimates the effect size of a variant on the phenotype, as mediated by the gene expression level, whereas HEIDI serves as a sensitivity analysis to assess whether the observed associations are due to heterogeneity from LD or driven by a single shared causal variant.29

For exposure, we utilised eQTL datasets from brain and blood tissues and PAU GWAS data for the outcome. SMR selects the top associated variant as a genetic instrument among those that meet the following two criteria: 1) a genome-wide significant association (P < 5 × 10−8) with the gene, and 2) location within a ± 1000 kb distance from the gene. HEIDI employs multiple variants in the region to assess whether the MR effects calculated for any variant in LD are identical to the causal variant, thereby testing for heterogeneity. HEIDI uses up to the top 20 variants in the region (including the top associated variant) that meet the following two criteria: 1) an association P < 1.56 × 1010−3 with the gene (equivalent to a chi-square value of 10 with one degree of freedom), and 2) LD r2 with the top associated variant between 0.05 and 0.90. We adhered to these settings for IV selection in both the SMR and HEIDI tests.

Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons based on the number of gene expression levels tested in the MR for each exposure dataset. For the HEIDI test, a significance threshold of P < 0.01 was used to detect heterogeneity. Given that the null hypothesis of the HEIDI test assumes a single causal variant affecting both gene expression and trait variation, a smaller P-value indicates potential heterogeneity because of linkages.

Proteome-wide Mendelian randomisation

We conducted PWMR analyses using the “TwoSampleMR” R package (version 0.5.7).30 To select the genetic instruments, we employed LD clumping in PLINK 1.9031 to obtain independent variants associated with each protein. Variants were clumped at an LD r2 < 0.001 within a 500 kb window centred on an index variant (variants with association P < 5 × 10−8). This 500 kb window was aligned with the genetic association range provided in the pQTL summary statistics from the Wingo et al.21 and ARIC datasets.22 For consistency, we processed pQTL summary statistics for each gene from the Icelandic cohorts23 by dividing them into ± 500 kb regions from the transcription start site and performing LD clumping. We harmonised the effect of a variant on protein expression (exposure) with the effect of this variant on the risk of PAU (outcome) to the same allele using built-in functions in the package.

Various MR methods were applied depending on the number of IVs available for each exposure.13,32 We employed the Wald ratio method for single IV analyses. For exposures with multiple IVs, we selected appropriate MR methods to ensure the consistency of the results across different assumptions in our sensitivity analyses. These included the Cochran’s Q test to assess heterogeneity33 and the MR-Egger intercept test to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy.34 When neither test was significant, we used the inverse variance-weighted method as the primary method, assuming that all variants were valid IVs.35 When heterogeneity was detected (P < 0.05), we applied the weighted median method, which is suitable when less than 50% of the variants exhibit horizontal pleiotropy.36 When horizontal pleiotropy was detected (P < 0.05), we employed the MR-Egger method, which is robust for causal estimation despite generally having lower statistical power than other methods.34 Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons, which were calculated based on the number of proteins tested in each exposure dataset.

Bayesian colocalization analysis

We conducted Bayesian colocalization analysis using the “coloc.abf” function from the “coloc” R package (version 5.2.2).37 This method employs the approximate Bayes factor to estimate the probability that two potentially related traits, gene or protein expression levels (trait 1) and PAU (trait 2), share a genetic causal variant.37 Default priors were set at 1 × 10−4 for P1 and P2 and 1 × 10−5 for P12, where P1, P2, and P12 represent the prior probabilities of a shared causal variant for traits one, two, or both, respectively.

Bayesian colocalization analysis calculates posterior probabilities for five hypotheses37: H0 (no causal variant), neither trait has a genetic association in the region; H1/H2 (causal variant for trait 1/trait 2 only), only trait 1/trait 2 has a genetic association in the region; H3 (two distinct causal variants), both traits are associated with different causal variants; and H4 (one common causal variant), both traits are associated and share a single causal variant.

We established a posterior probability threshold of 0.75 for hypothesis 4 (PP.H4 > 0.75) as the criterion for strong evidence of colocalization. This threshold indicates that gene or protein expression levels and PAU likely share a common causal variant,37 supporting a robust genetic linkage between traits. For cases with strong evidence of colocalization, we identified variants within the 95% credible set that had a posterior probability >0.95, suggesting causal variants for colocalization.

Phenome-wide Mendelian randomisation

To assess whether the effects of the prioritised potential causal targets on other traits were directionally concordant or discordant with their effects on PAU, we conducted PheWMR analyses using gene and protein expression as exposure variables. We utilised meta-analysed GWAS results from FinnGen (N ≤ 412,181) and European samples from Pan-UKBB (N ≤ 408,961) as outcome data.38 The disease endpoints were defined according to the ICD-10 classifications. We selected 589 traits with more than 1000 cases, considering statistical power.

The prioritised potential causal targets were defined through a rigorous three-step procedure as follows: (1) identifying significant associations in MR analyses using TWMR or PWMR data from brain and blood tissues; (2) passing sensitivity analyses, including the HEIDI test for TWMR, or heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests for PWMR; and (3) demonstrating strong evidence of colocalisation with PAU via Bayesian colocalisation (PP.H4 > 0.75). Targets with consistent evidence across multiple independent datasets were retained for downstream analysis.

In the PheWMR analyses, we employed the Wald ratio method using previously identified target genes or protein IVs (i.e., eQTLs and pQTLs). MR causal effects were considered statistically significant at P < 7.72 × 10−6 in the brain and P < 4.24 × 10−5 in the blood. We applied the Bonferroni correction for 11 genes and 589 phenotypes in the brain (0.05/11/589) and two proteins and 589 phenotypes in the blood (0.05/2/589). A total of 7657 MR tests were conducted across 589 traits and 13 prioritised targets, comprising 11 brain-expressed genes and two blood proteins, with multiple testing correction applied separately for each tissue type. Targets with consistent evidence across multiple independent datasets were retained for downstream analysis.

Cross-ancestry causal gene and protein mapping for PAU via multi-omics integration

To explore ancestry-specific genetic effects on PAU, we conducted TWMR using the SMR method,29 transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS),39 Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA) gene-set analyses40 and colocalisation analyses.37 These analyses used GWAS summary statistics from EAS, AFR, and SAS populations provided by Zhou et al. (2023).8 Each population-specific GWAS was analysed using ancestry-matched expression reference data and LD panels.

MAGMA gene-set analysis was performed to identify gene-level associations using GWAS summary statistics from each ancestry group. SNP-wise gene analysis was carried out with ancestry-specific LD reference panels from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 data, and gene-level significance was assessed using false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

For TWMR analyses, the EAS GWAS was paired with frontal cortex eQTL data from the Chinese Human Brain Bank, whereas the AFR GWAS was analysed using frontal cortex eQTL data from PsychENCODE derived from African American samples.41 For SAS, suitable eQTL data could not be obtained, and therefore, TWMR analysis was not performed.

For colocalisation analysis, we used lead SNPs derived from EAS, AFR, and SAS GWAS using the FUMA platform,42 incorporating population-specific LD reference panels from the 1000 Genomes Project. Colocalisation was conducted using Bayesian colocalisation analysis using the “coloc.abf” function from the “coloc” R package (version 5.2.2)37 across multiple eQTL datasets: 13 GTEx brain regions, PsychENCODE, BrainMeta, and eQTLGen. A PP.H4 greater than 0.75 was considered strong evidence for colocalisation.

TWAS analyses were also conducted for EAS, AFR, and SAS populations using the FUSION software39 with ancestry-specific LD panels and pre-computed expression weights derived from 13 brain tissue eQTL datasets provided by GTEx V8. Full details of the results are provided in the Supplementary Notes, including Supplementary Results.

Drug–gene interactions

The drug-gene interactions of potential causal targets were examined using Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb),43 a publicly accessible resource that aggregates drug–gene interaction information through expert curation and text mining from 44 sources, including DrugBank, PharmGKB, ChEMBL, and Drug Target Commons. DGIdb encompasses more than 10,000 genes and 20,000 drugs, representing approximately 70,000 drug–gene interactions categorised into 43 potentially druggable gene categories.

The DGIdb interaction score is a static metric that ranks drug–gene interactions based on evidence strength, combining factors such as the number of known drugs and gene partners, their relative connectivity, and supporting publications.43 While the score remains consistent across searches, it is updated periodically as new data sources and evidence are integrated. By providing a transparent and standardised measure displayed on individual interaction pages, the score helps researchers prioritise interactions for further investigation.

Results

Study design for identifying causal genes and proteins for PAU

To identify genes and proteins that may play a causal role in PAU, we used a step-by-step strategy that combined genetic data with information on gene and protein expression (Fig. 1). We identified relevant tissues for MR analyses through tissue-specific enrichment analysis and focused on brain and blood tissues, given their biological relevance to PAU (Fig. 2). TWMR and PWMR were conducted using gene and protein expression as the “exposure” and PAU as the “outcome”. Significant MR associations that passed multiple testing were further tested for pleiotropy using sensitivity analyses and Bayesian colocalization, and only robust results were retained. Genes and proteins supported by both MR and follow-up analyses, including colocalization, were considered potential causal targets for PAU. These were assessed for therapeutic relevance through drug-gene interactions and prior evidence. Targets with consistent support across datasets were prioritized, and PheWMR was conducted to evaluate their broader health effects.

Tissue-specific enrichment analysis of PAU

We investigated tissue-specific enrichment of genetic signals associated with PAU using LDSC–SEG24 and gene expression data from the GTEx project25 and Franke lab.26,27 Among the 205 tissues analysed, significant enrichment was observed in 18 CNS tissues (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S2), with the strongest signals in the parietal lobe of the brain (P = 2.61 × 10−5), substantia nigra (P = 2.91 × 10−4, LDSC–SEG), and the frontal cortex (P = 6.79 × 10−4, LDSC–SEG). Nominal enrichment (P < 0.05, LDSC–SEG) was also detected in peripheral tissues such as muscle, liver, and blood. These findings align with the predominant role of the CNS in AUD biology44 and suggest potential contributions from peripheral tissues.

Transcriptome-wide Mendelian randomisation in brain and blood tissues

To identify genes whose expression may causally influence PAU, we conducted TWMR using the SMR method29 and applied the HEIDI test to control for confounding due to LD.29

In brain tissue, TWMR was performed across three eQTL datasets: PsychENCODE,17 BrainMeta,18 and GTEx.19 We identified 60 unique genes through 205 Bonferroni-significant associations, all of which passed the HEIDI test (Table 1). Particularly, 28 genes were identified in PsychENCODE (PMR < 4.90 × 10−6; 0.05/10,197, Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S3), 42 genes in the BrainMeta (PMR < 3.08 × 10−6; 0.05/16,224, Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S4), and 41 in GTEx, based on data from 13 brain regions (Supplementary Table S1), resulting in 168 associations (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S5). The HEIDI test supported 25, 32, and 38 genes from each dataset, respectively (PHEIDI ≥ 0.01, Table 1 and Supplementary Tables S3–S5).

Proteome-wide Mendelian randomisation in brain and blood tissues

PWMR analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR method30 to identify the proteins causally associated with PAU. The primary MR method for each protein was determined by the number of available IVs, along with heterogeneity33 and horizontal pleiotropy tests34 to address potential confounding13,32 (see Methods).

In brain tissue, PWMR analyses using the pQTL dataset from Wingo et al.21 identified 12 proteins significantly associated with PAU (PMR < 2.36 × 10−5; 0.05/2,120, Fig. 3A, Table 1, and Supplementary Table S7). In the blood, 13 unique proteins were identified across two datasets (Table 1): nine from the ARIC22 (PMR < 3.12 × 10−5; 0.05/1,605, Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S8) and eight from the Icelandic dataset23 (PMR < 2.55 × 10−5; 0.05/1,961, Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S9).

Identifying potential causal targets through Bayesian colocalization analysis

To refine causal inference and determine whether the same genetic variants influence both gene/protein expression and PAU, we conducted Bayesian colocalization analyses37 on targets identified through MR. Genes and proteins supported by both MR and colocalization were defined as potential causal targets and prioritised based on consistent evidence across independent datasets.

In brain tissue, 23 genes (AMT, BRD3OS, CCDC88B, CSDC2, EVI2A, FAM180B, FUT2, GPX1, KANSL1-AS1, LINC01068, MAMSTR, MTCH2, NF1, NTN5, P4HTM, POLR3H, PPP2R3C, PPP4C, PPP6C, SLC39A13, SLC4A8, TBX6, and YPEL3) and four proteins (CTNND1, LEMD2, SLC4A8, and TMEM120B) demonstrated strong colocalisation evidence (PP.H4 > 0.75, Fig. 3A, Table 1, and Supplementary Tables S10 and S12). Of these, 11 genes were prioritised based on consistent MR and colocalisation evidence across multiple datasets (Table 2). Notably, SLC4A8 was identified as a causal target at both gene and protein levels, suggesting a potential sequential effect in PAU. Four genes (CCDC88B, KANSL1-AS1, NF1, and NTN5) had single-variant credible sets (PP.H4 > 0.95), with causal variants matching MR instruments (Supplementary Table S10).

Population-specific genetic analyses identify ancestry-relevant signals

Ancestry-specific analyses identified modest but notable associations, reflecting limitations in eQTL resources and smaller sample sizes. In EAS, ANKRD13A showed a suggestive signal and colocalisation evidence; in AFR, 14 MAGMA-significant genes included six with nominal TWAS support. In SAS, PLEKHA2 showed consistent associations across MAGMA, TWAS, and colocalisation. Additionally, ADH1B was detected in both EAS and AFR populations, highlighting its cross-ancestry relevance. Further detailed results are provided in the Supplementary Results within the Supplementary Notes and summarised in Supplementary Table S16.

Drug–gene interactions of potential causal targets

We examined the drug–gene interactions of the potential causal targets using the DGIdb,43 and identified 14 interactions involving nine targets with scores >0.5 (see Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table S13). For ADH1B, a key enzyme in alcohol metabolism,45 interacting drugs included fomepizole (score = 14.7, used for methanol/ethylene glycol poisoning), nitrefazole (score = 2.8, an alcohol deterrent), and acetaldehyde (score = 7.9, an alcohol metabolite). For GCKR, which is involved in glucose and lipid metabolism,46 interactions were identified with fenofibrate (score = 1.2), and denatured ethanol (score = 0.7). Interaction with denatured ethanol was also observed for RABGAP1L, SLC4A8, TMX2, and FUT2, suggesting a link to alcohol-related metabolism. Additionally, P4HTM was found to interact with roxadustat (score = 5.9), whereas SERPINA1 with α1-antitrypsin (score = 14.7).

Phenome-wide Mendelian randomisation and associations in independent alcohol-related outcomes

To evaluate the broader health implications of the 13 prioritised targets identified through significant MR results, robustness in sensitivity analyses (HEIDI for TWMR; heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests for PWMR), and strong colocalisation with PAU (PP.H4 > 0.75). We performed PheWMR analyses using data from meta-analysed GWAS results from FinnGen and Pan-UKBB across 589 phenotypes. The effects were categorised as concordant when the direction of effect on a given trait was aligned with their effect on PAU and as discordant when the directions were in the opposite direction. This classification was based solely on the alignment of effect directions, regardless of whether the effect on PAU indicated increased or decreased risk. The 13 prioritised targets comprised 11 brain tissue genes (AMT, NTN5, CCDC88B, GPX1, YPEL3, SLC39A13, PPP6C, MTCH2, PPP2R3C, LINC01068, and SLC4A8) and two blood proteins (ADH1B and GCKR), selected based on consistent evidence across multiple datasets (Table 2). In total, 7657 MR tests were conducted (589 traits × 13 targets), with multiple testing correction applied separately for each tissue type.

In brain tissue, a total of 149 significant associations were identified (PMR < 7.72 × 10−6; 0.05/11/589), with 101 showing concordant directions of effect with PAU (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Figs. S1–S10, and Supplementary Table S14). AMT, PPP6C, and GPX1 were significantly associated with reduced risk of conditions such as AUD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), smoking, and various neoplasms. NTN5 and CCDC88B also exhibited mostly concordant effects with PAU, though NTN5 was linked to discordant associations with digestive conditions and CCDC88B to cervical neoplasms. By contrast, YPEL3 and SLC39A13 were primarily associated with discordant outcomes, including hypertension, obesity, type 2 diabetes, and hypothyroidism. Importantly, four genes (AMT, GPX1, NTN5, and YPEL3) were significantly associated with the “AUD (Swedish definition)” phenotype, whereas four additional genes (CCDC88B, PPP6C, SLC39A13, and SLC4A8) demonstrated nominal associations (Table 2).

In blood tissue, 49 significant associations were identified (PMR < 4.24 × 10−5; 0.05/2/589), of which 25 were concordant with the effect direction of PAU (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. S11, and Supplementary Table S15). ADH1B showed primarily concordant associations, including with atherosclerosis and hypertension, although it was also linked to an increased risk of gout. GCKR had both concordant (e.g., reduced risk of type 2 diabetes and epilepsy) and discordant associations (e.g., gout). Notably, both proteins were associated with alcohol-related phenotypes, with ADH1B showing significant associations across all three outcomes (“AUD [Swedish definition]”, “Alcoholic liver disease”, and “Toxic effect of alcohol”), and GCKR with “AUD (Swedish definition)” (Table 3).

Discussion

We comprehensively analysed PAU by integrating MR with large-scale multi-omics data, incorporating transcriptomic and proteomic datasets from brain and blood tissues. Through MR analyses combined with Bayesian colocalisation, we identified 23 genes and four proteins in brain tissue and 16 genes and five proteins in blood tissue as potential causal targets of PAU (Fig. 3A and B, and Table 1). This integrative approach provides a deeper understanding of PAU pathophysiology and offers a foundation for the development of more effective and safer treatment strategies.

A key strength of our study is the integration of molecular data from both brain and blood tissues. While previous studies have predominantly focused on brain tissue,11 our inclusion of blood-based expression and proteomic data enabled the identification of peripheral mechanisms potentially involved in PAU. Tissue-specific enrichment analyses confirmed significant PAU-related signals in brain regions, including the parietal lobe and frontal cortex, while nominal associations in blood tissues suggest complementary peripheral involvement (Fig. 2). Blood-derived signals may reflect systemic physiological processes or peripherally accessible readouts of CNS activity, broadening the range of actionable therapeutic targets.

Among the prioritised targets, 13 genes and proteins were selected based on robust MR results, colocalisation evidence, and consistency across datasets (Tables 2 and 3). These included 11 genes expressed in brain tissue—AMT, NTN5, CCDC88B, GPX1, YPEL3, SLC39A13, PPP6C, MTCH2, PPP2R3C, LINC01068, and SLC4A8—and two proteins in blood tissue—ADH1B and GCKR. Notably, AMT, CCDC88B, and YPEL3 were identified in both brain and blood tissues (Table 1), suggesting their potential role in both central and peripheral regulation of alcohol use behaviours. Moreover, SLC4A8 was detected at both the gene and protein levels in brain tissue, indicating its multifaceted involvement in PAU.

Our PheWMR analysis elucidated the broader implications of the prioritised targets across various traits and diseases (Fig. 4). Notably, AMT, GPX1, PPP6C, and LINC01068 demonstrated concordant associations with PAU and with multiple health outcomes, with no discordant associations observed across the traits examined. This consistency highlights their potential as promising therapeutic targets. Similarly, NTN5 and CCDC88B exhibited effects that were largely concordant with PAU and showed minimal discordant associations, further supporting their therapeutic relevance. These insights enhance the translational relevance of our findings by providing information for future preclinical and clinical evaluations to assess the efficacy and safety of these candidates.

Importantly, several targets are supported by prior biological evidence. AMT is a key gene involved in glycine metabolism, which regulates both inhibitory and excitatory neurotransmission. Mutations in AMT cause glycine encephalopathy, and pharmacological enhancement of glycine signalling has been shown to reduce alcohol-seeking behaviour in rodents, supporting its therapeutic potential in addiction.47, 48, 49

GPX1, involved in oxidative stress regulation, showed protective effects against PAU and related conditions. Prior studies have also linked increased GPX activity with alcohol intake in individuals carrying the Pro198Leu C allele.50

CCDC88B has also been implicated as a causal risk gene for substance use traits, including AUD, in brain proteomic data.51 Mouse studies have linked CCDC88B to fasting glucose levels,47 aligning with evidence that glucose regulation can affect alcohol consumption.52 Likewise, NTN5 and PPP6C showed strong MR and colocalisation support, and have been implicated in GWASs of AUD, opioid use disorder, and smoking behaviour.53, 54, 55

Two genes with strong genetic evidence but less well-characterised biological roles, YPEL3 and SLC39A13, were also prioritised. Both have been repeatedly identified in PAU GWASs,6,7 and their consistent identification across transcriptome- and proteome-wide analyses underscores their potential significance in PAU pathophysiology.

GCKR, as a key regulator of triglyceride metabolism, may contribute to PAU through its influence on metabolic health. Elevated alcohol consumption is associated with increased plasma triglyceride levels,56 and GCKR variants have been linked to both triglyceride levels57,58 and alcohol-related traits. Our findings suggest that the GCKR-triglyceride pathway warrants further investigation as a mediator of alcohol use behaviour and a potential therapeutic target.

While our genetically informed analyses identified several biologically plausible targets for PAU, translating these findings into viable treatments remains challenging. Many existing compounds that interact with our prioritised proteins are currently unsuitable for clinical use due to safety concerns, regulatory limitations, or lack of therapeutic specificity. For example, drugs linked to ADH1B and GCKR, such as fomepizole and nitrefazole, have been associated with toxicity, market withdrawal, or indications unrelated to alcohol use, thereby limiting their repurposing potential.

These limitations highlight a critical gap between genetic target identification and the availability of actionable pharmacological interventions. Although ADH1B and GCKR are strongly supported by genetic and phenome-wide evidence, the risks associated with currently available drugs targeting these proteins highlight the need for the development of advanced compounds that can modulate their activity more precisely and safely.

By contrast, targets such as AMT, GPX1, and CCDC88B demonstrated not only strong genetic evidence but also minimal predicted discordant effects in relation to PAU in PheWMR analyses. These characteristics make them promising candidates for drug development. Establishing drug discovery pipelines that prioritise such genetically validated and biologically supported targets could accelerate the development of safer and more effective treatments for PAU.

Taken together, our study demonstrates the value of integrating multi-omics data with causal inference methods to identify and prioritise therapeutic targets. This systems biology approach offers a strategic framework for guiding the next generation of pharmacological interventions for alcohol-related disorders.

Despite these promising findings, our study has several limitations. First, although MR supports potential causality, functional studies are required to confirm therapeutic viability. Second, the limited number of genetic instruments may have reduced statistical power, though the stringent IV selection criteria (P < 5 × 10−8) minimised weak instrument bias and false positives. Third, we focused on cis-acting variants and thus, trans-regulatory effects and other epigenetic mechanisms may have been overlooked. Fourth, most data were derived from individuals of EUR ancestry, which limits the generalisability of our findings. Although we explored non-European populations, insufficient GWAS sample size and limited ancestry-matched eQTL resources reduced statistical power. Moreover, even within EUR populations, the cohorts used (e.g., UK Biobank, FinnGen, MVP) may not fully represent the general EUR population due to potential recruitment and selection biases. Expanding genetic studies and population-representative datasets across diverse ancestries will be essential for identifying additional targets and improving the global relevance of future findings.

In summary, this study demonstrates the power of combining genetic and molecular data to elucidate the biological basis of PAU and guide therapeutic target prioritisation. Our results offer a valuable resource for developing safe and effective treatments for alcohol-related disorders by highlighting targets with strong mechanistic plausibility and favourable safety profiles.
