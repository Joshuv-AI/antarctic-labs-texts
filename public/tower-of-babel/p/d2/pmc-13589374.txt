Partial carinal resection, reconstruction, and left pneumonectomy under median sternotomy combined with cardiopulmonary bypass: a case report
Authors: Yunlong Guo, Jiaqi Wang, Guangxin Zhang, Ti Tong, Chengyan Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is limited clinical data to guide surgical decisions for ACC with extensive carinal involvement in the left main bronchus. In this case, it is shown that median sternotomy and CPB are feasible in this particular anatomical setting.Case presentationCough and sputum were the presenting symptoms in a middle-aged female patient. Imaging examinations identified a left hilar mass that extended to involve the carina. The surgical intervention performed via a median sternotomy with CPB support encompassed both a left pneumonectomy and partial carinal resection along with reconstruction. Based on postoperative histopathological analysis, the diagnosis was adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC); the tumor staging was determined as pT2bN0Mx, which is equivalent to stage IIA. The patient experienced an uneventful postoperative recovery, and during the 6-month follow-up, no recurrence of the tumor was observed.ConclusionThe case illustrates that complex airway tumors can be resected with R0 margin using median sternotomy and CPB, and verifies the feasibility of surgical treatment for similar surgical cases.Keywords: Adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC), Carinal reconstruction, Left pneumonectomy, Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), Median sternotomy, Multidisciplinary team (MDT)

Introduction

ACC is a rarely encountered malignancy, accounting for less than 0.04%–0.2% of all pulmonary neoplasms [1]. Its development within the trachea has also been documented. The primary therapeutic modality remains surgical resection [2]. Nevertheless, cases involving substantial mural invasion of the carina continue to pose technical hurdles. When a tumor is situated in close proximity to the carina and exhibits extensive infiltration of the left main bronchus, conventional bronchial sleeve resection is often no longer feasible. Here, we report the case of a 47-year-old woman diagnosed with ACC originating from the left main bronchus and extending to the carina; the tumor-free distance from the carina bifurcation measured merely 0.5 cm. We demonstrate that a combination of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), median sternotomy, and carinal reconstruction constitutes a technically feasible strategy for achieving R0 resection in complex airway tumors.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old female patient presented with a 1-month history of sticky sputum and cough. No prior diagnosis of definite pulmonary disease was reported. Chest CT revealed a soft tissue density mass-like lesion in the left hilar region, measuring approximately 46 mm × 34 mm, accompanied by occlusion and narrowing of the left upper and lower lobe bronchi (Fig. 1). Bronchoscopic examination (Fig. 2) demonstrated irregular mucosal hyperplasia within the mid portion of the left main bronchus; although the tumor margin was free of malignant cells, the bronchial lumen was compromised due to the neoplasm. Histopathological analysis of the biopsy specimen confirmed a diagnosis of adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC). FEV₁ was 1.95 L, with a predicted percentage of 74%, indicating mild small airway dysfunction and mild diffusion impairment. Arterial blood gas analysis showed pH 7.37, PaO2 92 mmHg, and PaCO2 32 mmHg. Following admission, a thorough preoperative evaluation was conducted, which revealed no evidence of distant metastasis or major surgical contraindications. After appropriate preoperative preparation, the patient was scheduled for left pneumonectomy, along with carinal resection and reconstruction, performed via median sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).

For establishment of CPB inflow, dissection and suspension of right femoral artery were initially carried out, followed by cannulation with a 20-Fr arterial cannula. Venous access was achieved via insertion of a two-stage 32-framed cannula into right femoral vein. Using an electric saw, a median sternotomy was performed; the pericardium was then incised longitudinally, and its edges were lifted and secured. The pulmonary arteries, left lower and upper pulmonary veins, and superior caval vein were each meticulously dissected and slung. Complete exposure of the tracheal carina and both main bronchi was obtained.

CPB was then set up and started. Systemic anticoagulation was achieved with an intravenous bolus of 400 IU/kg heparin, and CPB was not started until the activated clotting time (ACT) exceeded 480 s. CPB was commenced with a perfusion flow of 2.2–2.5 L/min/m² and a blood flow index of 50–60 mL/kg/min.Under assisted circulation, exploration showed that the tumor in the left main bronchus was extended from the carina to the distal part of the left main bronchus, which precluded sleeve resection. Therefore, left pneumonectomy and carinal reconstruction were decided.

The left main bronchus was cut off in the proximal part (Fig. 4) and the frozen section analysis done intraoperatively confirmed the clear margins at the bronchial stump. A wedge resection of the left-sided carinal defect was performed and carinoplasty was done with a longitudinal incision and transverse closure technique using 3 − 0 Prolene suture. A partial free pericardial patch was then harvested and wrapped and embedded around the anastomosis. Carinal reconstruction was completed. There was no air leakage in lung inflation testing. Ligation of the left main pulmonary artery and pulmonary veins completed the left pneumonectomy. After weaning from CPB, heparin reversal was accomplished using protamine sulfate at a dosage of 1 mg for every 100 IU of heparin.

Wedge resection and reconstruction of the bronchi. (A) Left main bronchus tumor (up-right arrow), and resection of the tumor along the dotted line (down-left arrow). (B) Left main bronchus wall after suture (up-left arrow)

The overall surgical time was about 3 h and 20 min, including a CPB time of 57 min. Intraoperative bleeding was about 200 mL, and no blood transfusion was given intraoperatively or postoperatively. On the first postoperative day, the patient’s hemoglobin level was 101 g/L.

Chest drainage volumes on postoperative days 1 through 4 were as follows: 500 mL, 250 mL, 120 mL, and 50 mL. Postoperative chest radiography (Fig. 5) demonstrated satisfactory expansion of the right lung, a patent trachea, and absence of evident fistula. The patient’s ICU stay lasted 3 days. The chest drainage tube was extracted on postoperative day 5, and hospital discharge occurred on postoperative day 7. The pathology report from the left pneumonectomy specimen indicated findings consistent with adenoid cystic carcinoma, based on immunohistochemical staining and morphological features in comparison with the preoperative biopsy; the tumor diameter was approximately 4.5 cm, with no lymph node involvement(Fig. 6). The pathological TNM classification was pT2bN0Mx. Postoperatively, regular surveillance is recommended, and no adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy is currently required.

Discussion

Primary anastomosis with sleeve resection is the standard surgical treatment for tracheal ACC [3]. In this patient, chest CT revealed a large lesion with extensive involvement of the left main bronchus, which was greater than the safe margin for tracheal resection (50% of the length of the trachea [4]). Simple tracheal sleeve resection may be attempted, but the length of resection may be too great, leading to an exponential increase in anastomotic tension [5] and risk of anastomotic complications [6]. In addition, the lesion was situated about 0.5 cm from the carina, making R0 resection challenging. Therefore, simple sleeve resection of the trachea or left pneumonectomy alone was not thought to be feasible.

Surgical approach selection and decision-making

Traditional methods involve median sternotomy, left thoracotomy, or right thoracotomy, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. If a left-sided thoracotomy or VATS is chosen, the pulmonary hilum and left main bronchus are easily accessible. But, following left thoracotomy, the aorta and pulmonary artery block the operating field, and the trachea is relatively deep and difficult to see, and there is limited operating space, high tension of the anastomosis, and high risk of anastomotic leakage [7, 8].

When a right sided thoracotomy or VATS approach is used (the traditional approach), the main trachea and both main bronchi are easily exposed and tracheal reconstruction is relatively easy. However, the right atrium and inferior vena cava obstruct the view of the intrapericardial space [9, 10]. During tracheal reconstruction, not only may secondary intubation and mechanical ventilation be necessary, but bilateral incisions might also be required, resulting in greater trauma and more complications.

With the median sternotomy approach, either pulmonary hilum can be reached through the split sternum [11] and the main trachea and both main bronchi can be exposed at the same time without bilateral incisions. This allows for partial carinal resection and reconstruction of the trachea. Furthermore, median sternotomy offers a greater surgical field for lesions of the carina and left main bronchus [12]. It is also believed to be related to reduced mean blood loss and is more appropriate for operations that require access to both thoracic cavities for the treatment of pulmonary disease, with good postoperative results [13]. But with conventional mechanical ventilation using a standard median sternotomy incision, the deep airway is poorly visualized because of distention of the great mediastinal vessels, and even if the airway is divided during the procedure, endotracheal intubation is still necessary, which interferes with the operating field. To overcome this, a multidisciplinary discussion was held with anesthesiology and cardiac surgery and CPB was used.

Airway management strategy

The current airway management strategies include cross-field ventilation (CFV), high-frequency jet ventilation (HFJV), VV-ECMO, and CPB. CFV needs distal airway intubation, which is difficult to perform because of limited space; the tube placed in the right main bronchus also affects the visual field and the accuracy of suturing; repeated intubation increases surgical time and worsens the damage to the airway [14]. HFJV may lead to CO₂ retention, barotrauma and influence the operating field [15].

In this instance, hemodynamic collapse could be easily precipitated by intrapericardial dissection and ligation of the left pulmonary vein and left pulmonary artery. These problems are not amenable to ECMO (VV mode does not provide circulatory support and VA mode does not provide adequate perfusion at high flows) and are expensive [16, 17]. CPB, on the other hand, provides full respiratory and circulatory support, allowing for optimal oxygenation without mechanical ventilation, minimizing the risk of bronchopleural fistula, and avoiding airway injury from reintubation during reconstruction [18]. Furthermore, backflow from the pulmonary artery and bronchial arteries cannot be corrected with ECMO, which makes exposure of the pulmonary artery through the pericardial approach difficult. CPB enables full cardiac decompression, which facilitates exposure of the pulmonary artery and clear visualization of the pulmonary artery through the pericardial approach. CPB directly regulates blood gases, irrespective of the airway status, while ECMO (particularly VV mode) is more likely to lead to CO₂ retention. Mechanical ventilation is required after ECMO, which increases the risk of leakage, while mechanical ventilation can be stopped immediately after CPB and transition to spontaneous breathing, which decreases the incidence of anastomotic leakage.

To summarize, the present case demanded hemodynamic stability, a bloodless and clear operative field, controlled oxygenation during airway division, and minimization of anastomotic tension and fistula risk—all of which could be simultaneously achieved only with CPB. Furthermore, our team has acquired advanced expertise and extensive experience in CPB techniques, and the CPB duration was only 57 min, which significantly decreases the risk of complications.

Peripheral CPB via the femoral artery and vein was employed, completely avoiding any interference from tubing in the median sternotomy wound area and maximizing the operative space for deep cardiac reconstruction. In this case, the CPB time of 57 min was not considered to be in the unsafe range (CPB time ≥ 110 min is an independent risk factor for acute kidney injury after CPB [19]).

Complications and management after carinal reconstruction and pneumonectomy

The most important postoperative complication after carinal reconstruction and pneumonectomy is leakage of the tracheal anastomosis [20]. Anastomotic blood supply [21] and anastomotic tension [6] are key risk factors. CPB can indirectly decrease the incidence of anastomotic leakage by maintaining adequate oxygenation during surgery, reducing airway pressure [22] and inducing minimal changes in bronchial artery blood flow [23]. Furthermore, the risk of bronchopleural fistula after surgery may be significantly reduced by intraoperatively covering the bronchial anastomosis or stump with an autologous pericardial patch. Patients are advised to maintain head down and forward flexed position in the post-operative period to reduce the tension at the anastomosis [24].

Limitations

ACC is associated with late recurrences; overall survival is approximately 60% at 10 years and 30% at 15 years [25]. Thus, a follow-up period of 6 months is too brief. This case is a single-center, single-case report without prospective comparative data, which limits the generalizability of the conclusions.

Conclusion

Carinal reconstruction and left pneumonectomy were successfully performed via median sternotomy and CPB, despite limited visual field, high anastomotic tension, technical difficulties, bilateral incisions, and high risk of postoperative complications, with the help of multidisciplinary team consultation. And this surgical approach successfully circumvented the difficulties mentioned above. Finally, an R0 resection of the adenoid cystic carcinoma of the left main bronchus was performed. This case illustrates the feasibility of this approach for similar airway pathologies and anatomical situations.
