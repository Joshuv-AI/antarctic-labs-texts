Othering and Belonging
Authors: Ruth Montgomery-Andersen
Published: 2026

Introduction

Who am I?

I am the daughter, granddaughter and great granddaughter of Africa. My ancestors on both sides were enslaved and transported to Turtle Island on my father’s side and the Land of the Taino on my mother’s. My childhood was influenced by the civil rights movement, the aftermath of Brown v. Board of Education,1 which includes the Jim Crow Era and the desegregation of society as a whole in the United States. Enslavement, prejudice and oppression have been recognised and are a part of the DNA that, in many cases, results in generational trauma in the black community of the African Diaspora [1]. Both the Kalaallit and the decedents of the enslaved are oppressed peoples and resilient communities, and this postcolonial generational heritage has shaped the person that I am today in many ways. I consider othering as positionality and does not necessarily connect to pain. I acknowledge that oppression and belonging to an oppressed heritage or people is the trail between the two cultures that I navigate personally. I also see that in a Kalaallit context, as a non-Inuk, I do not agree that the pain is from othering—it is from not belonging. The sacredness of the narratives/stories is because of survival, memory and hope and not in spite of marginalisation.

Kalaallit Nunaat

I have had the joy of living and working in Kalaallit Nunaat for over 30 years; I have worked as a midwife and been a part of over 2000 families, most important moments. It is through these experiences that I have found a trail of understanding and a way of expressing my fundamental respect for living within a culture, especially as an “Other” on the way to “Belonging”.

At the beginning of this trail in 1995, I had two small children and had just in Kalaallit Nunaat. It did not take me long to understand that I knew nothing about the culture or the people here. It was during that period (1995–2003) that I began to understand the likenesses and common struggles and challenges in two very different cultures. I began to understand that a “decluttering” and decolonisation process was necessary in the way that I perceived my space within the lives and families that I touched. My arrival in Kalaallit Nunaat changed my perspective, and after years of being the catalyst for discussions and stories on colonialism, racism and bigotry, it was now me who needed to be willing to learn. I had to be willing to navigate the trail that led to being an active partner in creating new touchstones if I wanted to be an active advocate and support for families in Kalaallit Nunaat.

One could say that 1995 was the beginning of my personal journey together with the people, the culture and the language. I have been met with gratefulness and openness, which has allowed me access to the Kalaallit community to share my culture and my knowledge. However, I am not Inuk, and the trail has not been straight or easy. It has been filled with rocks, potholes and thorny bushes at the wayside. It has taken its toll on me as a person and as a non-Inuk, non-Danish person. Thus, I chose to create a home within my home, which includes creating a space that focuses on concepts of unity, creativity and faith and gives gifts to one’s community.

Background

As a cultural activist living within the Kalaallit community and being a part of the African Diaspora, I see culture through a multiethnic, multicultural lens. This approach focuses on the concepts of unity, creativity and faith; giving gifts to one’s community; and reflecting on meeting these cultures [2]. Because of Kalaallit Nunaat’s geo-physical placement, the road to colonisation has been dissimilar to many indigenous cultures. In many ways, Kalaallit Nunaat has been able to protect and continue to develop cultural aspects such as language and childbirth. Language and birth traditions have evolved over time, but these traditions are created and owned by the people themselves. The traditions surrounding pregnancy, childbirth and prenatal care of mothers and children have been protected and passed on by the elders through support and oral tradition, especially by the older women of Kalaallit Nunaat [3]. The process of becoming a family is under development within Kalaallit society, and as it develops, it changes the scales of power, the place of parenthood in the community and the place of the family in society. However, through pregnancy, naming practices and the creation of families, many families construct meaning in their life and position themselves and others [4,5].

The trail

Colonisation is one of the challenges that has influenced the ability of traditions to survive in a society. It leaves holes in our cultural expression, and without these traditions, it is difficult for individuals to “interact and feel that they have any influence over their environment, even decades after independence is achieved” [6,7]. An example of this is the Middle Passage. The Middle Passage is not the beginning of the African American story, but as with all colonised peoples, the story often starts at the moment when we were displaced, removed or subjugated and not at the stories and of our forefathers. The identities, stories and understanding of self within the African-American community rely on oral stories and the words of our Griots. These stories followed the people and were not connected to language, culture and/or country; rather, they were created as a hybrid—and yet deeply related to the pre-colonial consciousness of these people [8].

Often, history treats colonisation stories as tragedies that can be used as catalysts for cultural expression, resistance and activism within cultures. Within Kalaallit cultures and African-American cultures, communities also celebrate and share a consciousness and understanding that storytelling is a means to celebrate and protect their own culture (https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/nunavuts-black-history-society-shows-link-between-african-inuit-storytelling/) [9]. Both within the African diaspora and the Kalaallit cultures, there are similarities in terms of historical oppression, loss of culture and loss of self-determination. There are also several similarities, such as communities that celebrate and share consciousness and communities that understand that the art of storytelling as a means to celebrate and protect their own culture and community (https://yellowheadinstitute.org/2024/economy-racism-solidarity-iqaluit/#:~:text=Black%20communities%20and%20individuals%20continue, to%20vent%20their%20own%20frustrations) [10].

Often, in the literature of or around Kalaallit Nunaat, the concepts of culture, language and relationships with the land are named. Each is an individual concept that influences the Kalaallit way of knowing. This may be true in many instances, but through my lens and on my journey through Kalaallit Nunaat, I have not seen these as separate concepts; they are a part of the whole and have been visible as the markers along the trail. The trail and the story are a whole and cannot be compartmentalised into separate boxes of language, culture and country.

Touchstones

Throughout my research, I continue to return to the description of touchstones found in Banks-Wallace’s article: Talk that talk: Storytelling and analysis rooted in African American oral tradition. Banks- Wallace [11] uses the term “touchstones” to describe the creation of a story. In her articles, she names three important ingredients in creating a story: (1) cultural values and norms, (2) the immediate story environment and (3) the historical context. I find that reflecting on one’s own struggles and the stories told by African-American women on how gender, history and the environment all influence their lived experiences, helps to create a connection between the two cultures (excerpt from an unpublished essay: Montgomery-Andersen [2]).

Within both African diasporic storytelling and Kalaallit touchstone creation, there is a reclaiming of culture, humanity and memories. It creates a continuum supported by shared stories and cultural markers. Each seeks to reclaim and heal wounds that are defined by the people themselves and do not fall into the predefined concepts of non-Kalaallit or non-African Diasporic use to categorise the culture. Thus, it is not for me to describe or present how and why Kalaallit/Inuit uses storytelling, but I can draw parallels.

The connection can and is used in the navigation of praxis and dialogue in my work within the health system and as an advocate while working with women and families. The stories and touchstones for me were discovering and utilising the likenesses in the culture of Kalaallit Nunaat and at the same time protecting my own roots and ways of knowing and doing. The stories were created together with the families, with the women and sometimes with the children and youth. My goal was to create a room for my otherness where I could invite Kalaallit in. Creating a home within a home. My otherness is a medium for expressing myself, outside of the constraints of living not only outside of own culture but also in situations that continually challenge people’s concept of me.

“Othering and Belonging”

Othering and Belonging are integrated concepts that have been initiated as personal touchstones while living and working in Kalaallit Nunaat. The two concepts othering and belonging are the focus of my paper but also the concepts that have guided my workings over the past three decades.

Othering

The Othering and Belonging Institute at Berkley University defines the concepts as such: “While Othering processes marginalise people on the basis of perceived group differences, Belonging confers the privileges of membership in a community, including the care and concern of other members” (https://belonging.berkeley.edu) [12]. In this definition, othering is seen as exclusion and marginalisation by dominant groups, which it can be.

I would like to present Othering not a question of exclusion but of positioning. Often, it is the attitude towards sharing power and influence that define how we show up within communities [13]. Working as the other involves the contextualisation of place and interpretation [6]. It is not a continuity. It is not an imposed otherness but rather the parallel understanding that belonging can be created only when one accepts one’s own otherness. As a midwife, researcher and community member, I am “situated” both as an insider and as an outsider. This oxymoron creates an arena that reaches into the metaphysical realm of understanding one’s place within the society, in the locality and in a communal hierarchy [6,14–16].

This is not only a theoretical contextualisation but also an ethical and moral responsibility towards the spaces that we occupy, no matter who we are. As an other, and especially in connection with families, each meeting creates a touchstone. It is our own positioning that influences how we perceive otherness.

Belonging

Over the past 30 years, I have learned through my work alongside or under the direction of Indigenous leaders, researchers, research assistants, Culture Bearers and community panels and boards, mothers, fathers and parents, knowing that my otherness has influenced my connection to the community. By working alongside Kalaallit, I have been reminded that although I am within own arena, I am navigating peoples and communities that I am not culturally connected to, that I am still an other. This makes the risk that the passion that drives our work and our advocacy can influence the manner in which we are perceived and what we carry with us on our travels.

In a country as small as Kalaallit Nunaat, me being an African American, neither Kalaallit nor Danish, I am living in a society dominated by two homogenous cultures. This has been an ethical dilemma and a methodical challenge that cannot be solved but must be acknowledged. Smith’s [7] words again have great significance in creating an arena for understanding positioning and place. She states that when researching in cultures other than one’s own, it is important that researchers “clarify their research aims and think … seriously about effective and ethical ways of carrying out research” [7].

Stories and touchstones for me are discovering and utilising the likeness in the culture of Kalaallit Nunaat and at the same time protecting my own roots and ways of knowing and doing. Belonging is my goal and is a means to create a space for otherness where I thrive in my meeting with the Kalaallit culture. Creating a home within a home, belonging. Belonging as a way of living not only outside of own culture, but also a way to continually challenge the concepts of othering and belonging.

Parting comments

Both otherness and belonging within the African Diaspora and in Kalaallit Nunaat are dependent on the acceptance and the individual’s ability to navigate the communities. Olsen posits in a 2024 interview that “You are part of … (community) through your language, and it is through our language that we pass on our intergenerational knowledge. It is a value, and therefore, it relates to health”. It is up to others to accept that part of creating a home is when you see the touchstones, when you learn the language, both the physical and metaphysical language of the communities where you seek to “belong”.

These are thoughts of my metaphysical travels and the creation of a trail that continues and leads to belonging in Kalaallit Nunaat. A trail is not created by you the individual; it is something that you follow. The first challenge is to acknowledge one’s otherness. This is the head of the trail and thus the beginning of the journey. Belonging is created by being open to the stories along the journey. The touchstones of colleagues, mothers and grandmothers are the beginning to understand the likeness and common struggles and challenges in and for different cultures. The true challenge is to understand that a “decluttering” and decolonisation process is necessary in the way that others perceive themselves as a member of society and thus create space for belonging.

Brown [17] has been the torch lighting my trail; she eloquently states that “all people can learn to center in another's experience, validate it, and judge it by its own standards without need of comparison or need to adopt that framework as their own” [17] (p. 922). It is not our job to evaluate and quantify but rather to find common ground and common methods for creating safe spaces for families, women, children and the community. There is no conclusion, only ally and community members seeking to lift society.

The trail to belonging is accepting of change in status and understanding that the challenge lies in the journey from being the catalyst to discussions and stories and giving space to the society to create the trail. It is giving space, being willing to learn and being willing to navigate the trail that leads to active partnership in belonging in Kalaallit Nunaat. This is the touchstone for embracing otherness and creating belonging.

Biography

Dr. Montgomery-Andersen has lived in Greenland for over 30 - years and has a Doctoral degree in Public Health with a focus in mother-child health. She has been the primary caregiver for over 2000 Greenlandic newborns and their parents and an associated researcher with the University of Greenland. Empowerment and mentoring are at the forefront of her work, and she believes in sharing with communities in forms that are culturally relevant and that inspire to community involvement/ownership.
