Adolescent romantic breakups in Delhi-NCR, India: prevalence and associations with mental health outcomes
Authors: Vineet Chaudhary, Kritant Mishra, Kallur Nava Saraswathy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough common, adolescent romantic breakups (ARB) remain an underexamined aspect of youth well-being in low- and middle-income countries, where romantic expression during adolescence is often stigmatized and poorly supported. This study examines the prevalence and correlates of ARB and explores its association with mental health outcomes among young adults in the Delhi National Capital Region (Delhi-NCR), India.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 1,481 young adults from two universities in Delhi-NCR, India. Participants retrospectively reported their experiences of romantic breakups before the age of 18, along with sociodemographic, familial, and contextual characteristics. Mental health outcomes were assessed using standardized instruments.ResultsARB was reported by 27.8% of participants, with higher prevalence among males, those from upper socioeconomic backgrounds, and those with greater digital access. Maternal education and occupation were also associated with ARB. Participants reporting ARB had higher odds of moderate to severe depression (aOR = 1.83), moderate to severe anxiety (aOR = 1.65), suicidal behaviour (aOR = 2.82), and poor well-being (aOR = 1.39) compared to those without such experiences.ConclusionsThe relatively high prevalence and socially patterned nature of ARB, along with its association with mental health outcomes among youth in Delhi-NCR, suggest that romantic breakups may represent a relevant yet underexplored dimension of adolescent experience. These findings highlight the potential value of considering relational experiences within broader frameworks of adolescent health. Approaches that incorporate emotional literacy, school-based psychosocial support, and family-level engagement may represent useful areas for future research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05150-9.

Introduction

Romantic relationships during adolescence are increasingly recognized as important experiences that significantly influence emotional development, identity formation, and social integration [1–4]. Studies have shown that these relationships play a crucial role in the psychosocial development of young individuals, influencing how they form their identities, manage their emotions, and develop interpersonal skills [1]. Often, adolescent romantic bonds can lead to deep emotional investments, and when these relationships end (a process commonly referred to as a “breakup”), this can result in substantial psychological distress, with effects on mental health and relationship behaviour well into adulthood [5, 6]. For the purpose of this study, a romantic breakup occurring before the age of 18 years is termed an adolescent romantic breakup (ARB).

Studies suggest that relationship dynamics during adolescence are influenced by parental monitoring, peer norms, body image satisfaction, school engagement, and access to technology [2, 7–10]. With changing sociocultural values, greater autonomy, and increased digital exposure, a growing number of young people are entering romantic relationships compared to previous generations, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [11, 12].

While these experiences can promote emotional intimacy and social growth, the end of such relationships can lead to emotional distress, identity confusion, and even long-term health risks [2, 5–7]. As shown in various Western studies, breakups during adolescence, or ARBs, have been associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms, anxiety, suicidal thoughts, and low self-esteem [5, 6]. However, these findings are based on high-income contexts where romantic relationships are normalised, often receive support from peers or even family, and open dating, sex education, and youth counselling services are more accessible [13].

There is a lack of research focusing on the factors and outcomes of such experiences in South Asia, despite the region’s unique social expectations, family dynamics, and moral regulations regarding youth behaviour [13, 14]. Though breakups are a common part of the adolescent experience, the consequences of romantic dissolution during these formative years remain underexamined in LMICs [13, 15–17].

This gap in research is particularly striking in India, where social norms surrounding adolescent sexuality and romantic expression are changing rapidly due to the influences of digital modernity and traditional sociocultural structures [11, 13, 18]. In India, romantic intimacy during adolescence exists in a paradoxical space: it is widely practised yet socially condemned [18, 19]. Premarital romantic relationships, especially among girls, are often viewed as deviant or dishonourable, leading to consequences such as family surveillance, forced marriages, or even violence [11, 20]. The rise of mobile phones, social media, and dating apps has broadened the opportunities for adolescent connections, but it has also heightened concerns about morality, discipline, and control [21]. As young people navigate their romantic expressions, they do so under the influence of gendered moral codes, religious norms, and parental expectations, which make even the most private emotional experiences deeply entangled with social and political issues [21–24].

To better understand the distribution and correlates of ARB, the present study is informed by a socio-ecological perspective. Drawing on Urie Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory [25], adolescent experiences such as romantic relationships and their dissolution can be conceptualized as emerging from multiple, interacting levels of influence. These include individual-level characteristics (e.g., sex, body image, and sexual orientation), family-level factors (e.g., family structure, parental education, occupation, and household socioeconomic conditions), contextual influences (e.g., school environment and early access to digital technology), and broader societal structures (e.g., caste, religion, and migration-related factors) [26]. Within this framework, ARB can be conceptualized as a socially embedded experience that may be shaped by opportunities for relationship formation, norms governing adolescent behaviour, and the availability of emotional and institutional support.

Further, ARB within restrictive or conservative sociocultural settings may carry significant emotional, reputational, and mental health consequences, particularly for those who are already facing other forms of marginalisation. Indian youth deal with multiple contextual pressures, including caste, religion, socioeconomic status (SES), and gender norms, all of which intersect to shape their relationship choices and vulnerabilities [22–24]. A breakup may signify not just the loss of affection but also the dismantling of a rare opportunity for self-expression, belonging, or escape [15]. For many adolescents, especially girls and sexual minorities, romantic relationships can be experiences of both empowerment and vulnerability, and their end can trigger a wave of social judgment and internalized shame [20]. Such dynamics suggest the need for a layered understanding of how ARB is experienced, especially across different sociodemographic backgrounds.

Despite increasing recognition of the importance of adolescent mental health in India, there is little research examining how non-academic or non-structural events, such as romantic breakups, contribute to mental health vulnerabilities, especially in the long term. The National Mental Health Survey (2015-16) largely focused on academic stress, substance use, and familial violence as determinants of youth mental health, with little emphasis on peer and romantic relational factors [27]. This omission may be partly due to the taboo surrounding adolescent dating and partly due to the absence of validated measurement tools that capture such experiences systematically in large-scale surveys. The rising prevalence of mental health conditions makes it increasingly important to examine how ARBs are linked to these health issues in young adulthood [27].

Given this context, our study aims to address three critical gaps. First, we estimate the prevalence of ARB (romantic breakups before age 18) among young adults in the Delhi National Capital Region (Delhi-NCR) of India, based on retrospective reports of adolescent experiences. Second, we identify correlates of ARB across multiple socio-ecological levels, including individual, familial, contextual, and broader societal factors. Third, we examine the association between ARB and mental health outcomes in young adulthood, including depression, anxiety, perceived stress, suicidal behaviour, and well-being. While prior Indian studies have examined attitudes toward romantic relationships and psychosocial correlates among youth [18, 19, 22], the present study extends this literature by systematically estimating the prevalence of ARB, mapping its socio-ecological correlates across multiple levels, and examining its associations with a comprehensive set of validated mental health outcomes within a single large sample.

Methods

Study area and participants

This cross-sectional analysis utilizes data from a larger research project involving 1,843 young adults residing in Delhi-NCR, India. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 25 years (mean = 19.9 years; SD = ± 1.89), with females comprising 68.1% of the total sample. Recruitment was conducted from two universities situated in Delhi and Noida. For the present study, data from 1,481 participants who responded to the item on ARB, were included in the final analysis.

Eligibility criteria required participants to be Indian citizens residing in the Delhi-NCR region and free from any self-reported major physical or psychological illnesses. Exclusion criteria included individuals currently undergoing treatment for chronic physical or mental health conditions, those on prolonged medication, pregnant or lactating women, and foreign nationals. These exclusions were implemented to reduce heterogeneity; however, they may also limit the generalizability of findings, particularly to individuals with existing health conditions and undergoing treatment, and could result in a relatively healthier sample, potentially underestimating the strength of associations with mental health outcomes.

The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Departmental Ethics Committee, Department of Anthropology, University of Delhi (Approval Ref. Nos. Anth/2022–23/532 and Anth/2022–23/14). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of recruitment, prior to data collection.

Data collection and measures

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a pretested interview schedule. Participants provided information on a range of sociodemographic and lifestyle variables, which were conceptually grouped according to a socio-ecological framework into individual, familial, contextual, and broader societal levels. Individual-level variables comprised sex, age, sexual orientation, self-reported childhood body mass index (BMI), and satisfaction with childhood appearance. Familial-level variables included family structure, number of male and female siblings, birth order, the educational and occupational background of both parents, and SES. Contextual-level variables included the type of school attended, academic performance in school, academic stream during senior secondary education, and access to mobile phones and the internet before the age of 18 years. Broader societal-level variables included religion, caste or social group, ancestral place of origin, and years of residence in Delhi-NCR. For details on studied variables, refer to [26].

Family socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed using the Modified Kuppuswamy Scale [28]. This scale evaluates three domains: the education and occupation of the family’s head and the total monthly household income, generating a composite score ranging from 3 to 29. Based on the final score, families were categorized into four SES tiers: lower class (≤ 10), lower middle class (11–15), upper middle class (16–25), and upper class (26–29) [28].

Adolescent Romantic Breakup (ARB)

Experience of a romantic breakup during adolescence was assessed using a single-item measure: “Did you experience at least one romantic breakup before the age of 18?” Responses were recorded as a binary variable (Yes/No).

Depression

Symptoms of depression were evaluated using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) [29], a validated 21-item self-report instrument. Participants responded to each item on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 to 3, yielding cumulative scores between 0 and 63. Based on standard cut-off values, scores were interpreted as follows: 0–13 indicating minimal or no depression, 14–19 as mild, 20–28 as moderate, and 29–63 as severe depressive symptoms [29]. The internal consistency of the scale was strong (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.904). For statistical categorization, moderate and severe categories were combined into a single group to increase the sample size and enhance analytical robustness. Thus, participants were grouped as having no depression, mild depression, or moderate/severe depression.

Anxiety

Anxiety levels were measured using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) [30], comprising 21 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (severely), producing scores from 0 to 63. Severity was classified as follows: 0–7 representing minimal or no anxiety, 8–15 mild, 16–25 moderate, and 26–63 severe [30]. The BAI demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.943). For categorical analyses, moderate and severe cases were grouped together to form three distinct categories: no anxiety, mild anxiety, and moderate/severe anxiety.

Stress

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) was employed to evaluate perceived stress [31, 32]. This 10-item instrument uses a 5-point Likert format (0 = never to 4 = very often), with a maximum total score of 40. Scores were classified into low (0–13), medium (14–26), and high stress levels (27–40) [33]. The term “stress” is used interchangeably with “perceived stress” for brevity. Internal consistency for this measure was acceptable (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.68).

Suicidal behaviour

To assess suicide risk, the Suicide Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R) was used [34]. This brief, 4-item scale is validated across cultures and designed for use in non-clinical populations. Scores range from 3 to 18, with a cut-off score of ≥ 7 indicating a heightened risk of suicidal behaviour [34]. The SBQ-R has demonstrated satisfactory internal reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.76). Participants scoring 7 or higher were classified as at risk for suicidal behaviour in this study. Additionally, participants who scored at or above the SBQ-R cut-off (≥ 7) were provided with information about available mental health support services and encouraged to seek professional assistance, in accordance with ethical guidelines for research involving sensitive psychological content.

Well-being

Well-being was measured using the WHO-Five Well-Being Index (WHO-5) [35], consisting of five positively worded items rated from 0 (at no time) to 5 (all of the time). The raw score (0–25) was multiplied by 4 to generate a final score ranging from 0 to 100 [35]. A score of 50 or below indicated poor well-being, while scores above 50 reflected good well-being [35]. The scale exhibited good reliability in the current sample (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.844).

Analytic plan

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 22 and Microsoft Excel 2019. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion, as the pattern of missingness was assessed to be at random. Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of p < 0.05. The analysis was structured into two primary components:

Prevalence and correlates of ARB

The overall prevalence of romantic breakup before the age of 18 was calculated and reported as absolute counts and percentages. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine variations in prevalence across variables grouped at different socio-ecological levels (individual, familial, contextual, and societal), with differences tested using Chi-square tests. To identify factors associated with ARB, binary logistic regression analyses were performed. The dependent variable was the presence or absence of a romantic breakup before age 18. The independent variables included factors across multiple socio-ecological levels, including individual, familial, contextual, and societal variables. The regression models were adjusted for key covariates: age, sex, SES, and educational background.

Mental health implications of ARB

To examine the association between ARB and mental health outcomes, the prevalence of selected health outcomes was compared between those with and without breakup experience using Chi-square tests. Logistic regression models were then used to estimate the adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for each mental health outcome, with ARB as the independent variable and the mental health outcomes as dependent variables. All models were adjusted for key covariates, including age, sex, education, and SES, to control for potential confounding.

Results

General characteristics of the study sample

The study sample comprised predominantly young adults aged 18–21 years (85.7%), with a higher proportion of females (70%). Most participants were undergraduate students from urban, middle socioeconomic backgrounds in North India, living in nuclear families and having early access to digital devices and the internet. For detailed sample characteristics, refer to Supplementary Table 1.

Prevalence of ARB and variation across socio-ecological levels

Overall, 27.8% of participants reported experiencing ARB. Variation in prevalence was observed across factors at multiple socio-ecological levels. At the individual level, prevalence was higher among males compared to females, among participants identifying as lesbian, gay, or bisexual (LGB) compared to those identifying as straight, and among those with underweight or obese childhood BMI compared to those with normal BMI. At the familial level, differences in prevalence were observed across sibling composition, birth order, parental education, parental occupation, and socioeconomic status. Prevalence of ARB was higher among participants with no brothers, those with one sister, and those who were first-born compared to their respective counterparts. Participants whose mothers were college-educated or engaged in service or business reported a higher prevalence than those with less educated or housewife mothers. A similar pattern was observed for father’s education and occupation. A gradient was evident across socioeconomic status, with higher prevalence among participants from upper socioeconomic backgrounds compared to lower socioeconomic groups (Table 1).

At the contextual level, prevalence differed by school type, academic performance, and digital access. Students from private schools reported a higher prevalence than those from government schools. Participants with below-average academic performance had a higher prevalence compared to those with average or above-average performance. Prevalence was also higher among participants with access to mobile phones and the internet before the age of 18, particularly among those with unsupervised access (Table 1).

At the societal level, prevalence varied across religion and caste categories. Higher prevalence was observed among participants belonging to ‘other’ religions compared to Muslims, and among the Scheduled Tribe (ST) and Unreserved (UR) groups compared to the Scheduled Caste (SC) and Other Backward Class (OBC) groups. No significant differences in prevalence were observed across regions or duration of residence in Delhi-NCR (Table 1).

Correlates of ARB

Adjusted odds ratio analyses identified several factors associated with ARB (Table 1). At the individual level, sex was significantly associated with ARB. Females had lower odds of reporting ARB compared to males (aOR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.46–0.79; p < 0.001). Childhood dissatisfaction with appearance was associated with higher odds of ARB (aOR = 1.37; 95% CI: 1.02–1.82; p = 0.034). Sexual orientation and childhood BMI were not significantly associated with ARB in adjusted analyses.

At the familial level, maternal characteristics showed significant associations. Participants whose mothers had lower education (0–5 years) had lower odds of ARB compared to those with college-educated mothers (aOR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.32–0.92; p = 0.024). Higher odds were observed among participants whose mothers were engaged in service (aOR = 1.99; 95% CI: 1.42–2.77; p < 0.001) or business (aOR = 2.58; 95% CI: 1.34–4.95; p = 0.005) than those with housewife mothers. Socioeconomic status showed a gradient, with lower-middle (aOR = 0.55; 95% CI: 0.38–0.80; p = 0.002) and lower SES (aOR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.18–0.45; p < 0.001) groups having lower odds compared to the upper SES group. Other familial variables were not significantly associated.

At the contextual level, school academic performance showed an inverse association, with average (aOR = 0.58; 95% CI: 0.35–0.97; p = 0.038) and above-average performers (aOR = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.35–0.98; p = 0.040) having lower odds compared to below-average students. Digital access was strongly associated with ARB. Participants with supervised (aOR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.39–2.54; p < 0.001) and unsupervised (aOR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.31–2.70; p = 0.001) access to mobile phones, as well as supervised (aOR = 1.60; 95% CI: 1.03–2.48; p = 0.035) and unsupervised (aOR = 2.09; 95% CI: 1.31–3.32; p = 0.002) access to the internet before age 18, had higher odds of ARB compared to those without access. Other contextual factors, including school type and academic stream, were not significantly associated.

At the societal level, none of the studied variables, including religion, caste, and migration-related factors, were significantly associated with ARB after adjustment.

Mental health outcomes in relation to ARB

Participants with ARB had higher odds of adverse mental health outcomes compared to those without ARB (Table 2). Specifically, ARB was associated with higher odds of moderate to severe depression (aOR = 1.83; 95% CI: 1.36–2.47; p < 0.001) and moderate to severe anxiety (aOR = 1.65; 95% CI: 1.21–2.26; p = 0.001). ARB was also associated with higher odds of suicidal behaviour (aOR = 2.82; 95% CI: 2.09–3.81; p < 0.001) and poor well-being (aOR = 1.39; 95% CI: 1.06–1.84; p = 0.019). For stress, although the direction of association was positive, the results did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

The experience of romantic breakups during adolescence is a significant yet under-researched aspect of youth development in LMIC and Indian contexts [13, 15]. In the present study, 27.8% of participants reported an ARB. In high-income countries (HICs), romantic involvement is common during adolescence [2]. Surveys often report that about 60–70% of teens in HICs have dated by late adolescence, and among these, a sizable proportion experienc a breakup [2]. In contrast, systematic data from LMICs, including India, remain limited. The observed prevalence of ARB in this study suggests that adolescent romantic engagement in India is neither marginal nor uncommon.

This challenges longstanding assumptions in Indian public discourse that romantic involvement during adolescence is deviant or morally problematic. In reality, these relationships have become increasingly normative, enabled by urbanization, expanding access to digital technologies, and shifting youth aspirations [13, 15, 19, 21]. However, the social infrastructure to support these experiences, through school counselling, open parent–child communication, or adolescent mental health services, remains poorly developed.

The correlates of ARB identified in this study, spanning individual-level factors such as sex and dissatisfaction with childhood appearance, familial factors including maternal characteristics and SES, and contextual factors such as academic performance and digital access, provide insight into which groups are more likely to report a romantic breakup.

Among individual-level factors, sex emerged as a significant correlate of ARB, with males reporting higher prevalence. These differences may reflect underlying gender norms that shape adolescent romantic behaviour. Within patriarchal contexts, females’ romantic engagements are often interpreted through a moral lens, which may lead to greater family control and limited opportunities for open expression [20]. This asymmetry may contribute to fewer reported experiences among females, potentially reflecting under-reporting due to social desirability or conformity pressures. Males, in contrast, may experience relatively greater relational autonomy, which could increase opportunities for relationship formation and dissolution, and consequently higher reporting of breakup experiences.

Dissatisfaction with childhood appearance was also associated with higher odds of ARB. This may reflect the role of body image in shaping self-perception and interpersonal confidence during adolescence, as suggested in prior research on peer comparison and media influences [36, 37]. However, given the retrospective nature of the data, it is also possible that current psychological states influenced how participants recalled or evaluated their past appearance. The growing influence of visual social media platforms has also been linked to heightened appearance-based concerns [38], although such mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study.

At the familial level, participants whose mothers had higher educational attainment or were employed were more likely to report ARB experiences. Higher maternal education and employment may shape family environments characterized by greater adolescent autonomy and less direct supervision, which, in turn, may be linked to opportunities for relationship formation and dissolution [39, 40]. Alternatively, these family environments may also support more open expression, including experiences of romantic relationships and their endings.

Further, working mothers may experience role strain and time constraints [41, 42], which could influence patterns of emotional engagement and shape how adolescents experience and respond to the emotional complexities of romantic relationships. At the same time, maternal education may be associated with greater emotional openness and relational exploration, which may be linked to increased opportunities for romantic engagement [43]. Overall, these patterns suggest that maternal characteristics may play a role in shaping adolescents’ emotional and relational experiences. However, these interpretations should be considered speculative, as the present study does not directly assess mediational or interaction pathways, and the socio-ecological framework is used as a conceptual tool rather than an empirically tested model.

In contrast, family structure variables, including family type, sibling composition, and birth order, were not associated with ARB in adjusted analyses. While prior research has suggested links between sibling dynamics and relational development [44, 45], the present findings do not support an independent role of these factors. This may indicate that, in this context, factors related to opportunity, exposure, and social environment play a more prominent role in shaping adolescent romantic experiences.

Additionally, participants from higher SES backgrounds reported a higher prevalence of ARB. This pattern may reflect differences in opportunity structures, including greater access to digital technologies, reduced parental monitoring, and exposure to diverse peer environments, which have been linked to increased opportunities for romantic interactions [46, 47]. At the same time, these contexts may also involve greater peer comparison and exposure to idealized relationship norms portrayed in popular culture [39]. However, these interpretations remain tentative, as the present study does not directly assess these mechanisms. It is also possible that participants from higher SES backgrounds are more willing to report romantic experiences, reflecting differences in social acceptability or disclosure. These patterns may be interpreted within broader sociological perspectives, such as Bourdieu’s concept of habitus, which highlights how access to cultural and economic capital may shape dispositions toward intimacy and relational experiences [48, 49].

At the contextual level, academic performance also showed variation in relation to ARB, with lower-performing students reporting a higher likelihood of breakup. The direction of this relationship cannot be determined in the present study. It is possible that academic difficulties, which may be accompanied by stress or reduced self-esteem, influence relationship experiences. Conversely, emotional distress related to romantic relationships may also affect academic functioning [50]. These findings highlight the potential interconnectedness of academic and relational domains during adolescence, although longitudinal research is needed to clarify these pathways.

Similarly, both supervised and unsupervised access to mobile phones and the internet were associated with a higher likelihood of ARB. This may reflect the role of digital environments in facilitating communication and relationship formation among adolescents, as suggested in prior research [51, 52]. At the same time, digital interactions may also introduce new relational dynamics, including online conflict, surveillance, or exposure to interpersonal stressors [53, 54]. However, the present study does not capture the nature, quality, or context of digital engagement. Therefore, it is not possible to determine how digital access influences relationship experiences or whether these associations reflect broader social and environmental changes.

In India, where digital literacy among parents is low and sex education is inadequate [55], young people often deal with these risks in isolation. These observations suggest the potential value of approaches that integrate media literacy into school curricula, support parent–adolescent engagement with digital environments and promote safer online interactions.

Regarding social factors, caste, religion, and migration-related variables were not significantly associated with ARB in adjusted analyses in this sample. Although some differences were observed in bivariate analyses, such as lower prevalence among SC and OBC groups compared to UR groups, and among Muslims compared to Hindus and others, these did not persist after adjustment. Therefore, these factors cannot be considered independent correlates of ARB in the present study. This may suggest that, within urban and educational settings such as the present sample, broader structural identities, including caste, religion, and migration background, may play a less prominent role in shaping adolescent romantic experiences compared to other socio-ecological factors. However, given the sociocultural diversity of India, their influence should not be dismissed and may warrant further investigation in more diverse and representative populations [56–59].

Moving to the association between ARB and mental health outcomes, prior research has linked adolescent romantic breakups with higher levels of depression, anxiety, lowered self-esteem, and suicidal ideation [5, 6, 60]. These associations are well-documented in high-income settings [5, 6, 60], but remain underexplored in South Asian public health literature. In the present study, those with a history of ARB had higher odds of moderate to severe depression and anxiety in young adulthood than those without such a history. Though the direction of the associations cannot be determined, these findings suggest that ARB may be an important psychosocial correlate of mental health outcomes in young adulthood. From a developmental psychopathology perspective, adolescence is already a period of heightened vulnerability [61, 62], and emotional experiences related to romantic relationships may be shaped by broader social contexts, including stigma and the availability of support. The limited recognition of and support for such experiences may contribute to an under-recognized dimension of distress among young people.

One of the notable findings of the study was the higher odds of suicidal behaviour among those with ARB compared to those without. Suicidal behaviour is among the most severe manifestations of mental distress and constitutes a major cause of mortality among Indian youth [63, 64]. Adolescents may experience feelings such as abandonment, rejection, or shame in the context of relationship dissolution, particularly within settings where romantic expression is stigmatized. However, these interpretations remain speculative, as the study did not directly assess these pathways. These findings highlight the need for further research and consideration of supportive, adolescent-sensitive approaches.

A potential theoretical lens for interpreting these results is disenfranchised grief [65], which describes the denial of legitimacy to certain types of loss. In contexts like India, adolescent romantic relationships are often dismissed as frivolous or morally suspect, especially for girls and queer youth [66]. Consequently, adolescents may be unable to openly process or seek support for such experiences. However, this construct was not directly measured in the present study and should be interpreted as a conceptual framework rather than an empirical finding.

The study also found significantly poorer subjective well-being among those with ARB. Individuals with ARB had 1.39 times the odds of reporting poor well-being in young adulthood than those without, even after adjusting for demographic and socioeconomic variables. Distress related to adverse romantic experiences may be linked to challenges in social engagement, academic functioning, and future relational confidence [67–69], although these pathways were not directly assessed. Longitudinal research is needed to determine whether these associations persist over time.

Nevertheless, it is important to consider alternative explanations for these associations. Romantic experiences and mental health are both shaped by earlier life experiences and broader social and relational contexts, including family environments, prior emotional experiences, and systems of support. These factors may influence both the likelihood of engaging in romantic relationships and how individuals experience and respond to their dissolution. The observed associations may therefore reflect unmeasured confounding, including prior mental health status, personality traits, peer dynamics, and family environment. It is also possible that participants' current mental health status influenced recall and reporting of past breakup experiences, potentially inflating observed associations.

Implications

Despite the levels of distress observed, the institutional response to adolescent emotional challenges, particularly those related to romantic relationships, may be limited in the Indian context. Existing school health programs often focus on areas such as hygiene, reproductive health, or academic stress, with relatively less emphasis on the emotional dimensions of peer and romantic relationships. Parents and teachers may also face constraints, including cultural norms and generational gaps, which may influence their ability to recognize and respond to adolescents’ emotional experiences.

The limited emphasis on relational and emotional aspects of adolescent experiences within existing institutional and educational frameworks may represent a potential area for public health attention. Limited recognition of relational stressors could contribute to delays in support. Approaches that acknowledge relational experiences as part of adolescent development and incorporate emotional literacy, resilience-building, and peer support into adolescent health frameworks may represent useful areas for future research and program development. These implications should be interpreted cautiously, given the cross-sectional and retrospective nature of the data.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the observed findings should be interpreted as associations rather than directional effects. Reverse causation or shared underlying factors may explain these associations. Second, the study relies on retrospective self-reports of adolescent experiences, which may be subject to recall bias. Participants’ current emotional states may have influenced how they remembered and reported past experiences such as romantic breakups, body image, and academic performance.

Third, although analyses were adjusted for key sociodemographic variables (age, sex, SES, and education), other potentially relevant confounders, such as current relationship status, family history of mental health conditions, and recent life stressors, may have influenced the observed associations. Additional unmeasured psychosocial confounders, including attachment insecurity, parental conflict, and prior psychiatric history, may also influence both breakup experiences and mental health outcomes and could not be accounted for in the present analyses. Accordingly, the findings may partly reflect the influence of more proximal or unmeasured factors rather than adolescent experiences alone. Fourth, the romantic breakup experience was assessed using a single binary self-report item, which does not capture the complexity, frequency, duration, or emotional intensity of relationships and their dissolution. It does not differentiate between types of relationships (e.g., brief or long-term), potentially obscuring important heterogeneity in breakup experiences and their psychological impact. This reflects a trade-off between conceptual depth and feasibility in large-scale survey research. Future studies may benefit from multidimensional measures capturing the duration, context, and emotional intensity of relationship dissolution.

Fifth, the study sample was drawn from two universities in the Delhi-NCR region using a convenience sampling approach, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations of young people in India, particularly those from rural settings or those not engaged in higher education. The findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting urban, university-going young adults. Additionally, the higher proportion of female participants may have influenced reporting patterns and the observed distribution of outcomes; although analyses were adjusted for sex, gender-based differences in reporting and experience cannot be entirely ruled out. Sixth, given the sociocultural sensitivity surrounding adolescent romantic relationships in India, there is a possibility of under-reporting of breakup experiences, particularly among certain groups (e.g., females or individuals from more conservative backgrounds), which may have influenced prevalence estimates.

Seventh, missing data were handled using pairwise deletion. As the missingness was assessed to be at random, this approach was considered appropriate; however, pairwise deletion may still introduce minor inconsistencies across analyses. Eighth, the categorization of continuous psychological scale scores into discrete groups was based on established cut-offs. While this enhances interpretability and comparability with existing literature, it may still result in some loss of information and reduced statistical power, potentially obscuring more nuanced relationships. Ninth, the study involved a large number of bivariate and multivariate comparisons, which increases the risk of inflated Type I error; the possibility that some statistically significant findings reflect chance associations cannot be excluded. Tenth, the category “prefer not to say” for sexual orientation may encompass heterogeneous individuals, and the small number of LGB participants limits meaningful subgroup interpretation; the potential influence of heteronormative assumptions or disclosure bias on the data warrants consideration in future work. Finally, although validated instruments were used to assess mental health outcomes, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce social desirability bias, particularly for sensitive constructs such as suicidality and sexual orientation.

Future research using longitudinal designs and more detailed measures of romantic relationships is needed to better understand the temporal dynamics and underlying mechanisms linking adolescent romantic experiences with mental health outcomes.

Conclusion

The study revealed that over one in four participants experienced a breakup before age 18, with marked variation across sex, socioeconomic status, maternal background, and digital access. This suggests that relational disruption may be socially patterned rather than a random phenomenon and underscores the potential value of moving beyond individual-level explanations toward a more structural understanding of adolescent emotional experiences. The findings also show associations between ARB and adverse mental health outcomes. While these findings should be interpreted in light of the study’s limitations, they suggest that breakups may represent an important, yet under-recognized, dimension of adolescent mental health with potential public health relevance. Current adolescent health programs in India may give limited attention to relationship-related stress and emotional experiences. These findings suggest the potential value of exploring relational dimensions within adolescent health frameworks. Approaches that incorporate emotional literacy, school-based psychosocial support, digital literacy, and family-level engagement, including parental involvement, may be useful areas for future research and intervention development. Ultimately, recognizing romantic relationships and their dissolution as meaningful aspects of adolescent experience may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of youth emotional well-being.
