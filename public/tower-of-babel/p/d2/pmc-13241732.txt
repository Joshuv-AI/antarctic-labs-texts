Relationship between expression of OGFR, P16, P21 and clinicopathological features of colorectal cancer patients
Authors: Zeyu Wang, Benchang Zhang, Yanxia Li, Xiuqing Hao, Yu Wang, Tingting Qiao, Juan Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the expression of OGFR, P16, and P21 in CRC patients and to perform a comprehensive analysis in relation to clinicopathological characteristics, overall survival (OS), and disease-free survival (DFS). The findings are expected to elucidate the roles of these molecules in CRC occurrence, progression, and prognosis, thereby providing potential theoretical evidence and practical directions for precision therapeutic strategies.MethodsBioinformatics analyses, including differential analysis, survival analysis, single-cell sequencing, and spatial transcriptome analysis, were conducted to assess OGFR. We employed RT-qPCR and Western blot techniques to quantify the mRNA and protein levels of OGFR, P16, and P21 in a cohort of 30 CRC tissues. Immunohistochemistry was applied to evaluate the positive expression rates of OGFR, P16, and P21 in 194 CRC tissues. Associations with clinicopathological features were analyzed using SPSS 20.0, and survival was assessed by the Kaplan-Meier method.ResultsOGFR was upregulated in CRC and associated with advanced stage and shorter DFS, while P16 and P21 were downregulated and correlated with tumor progression. Correlation analyses showed OGFR was negatively associated with P16 and P21, whereas P16 and P21 were positively correlated. Survival analysis indicated that P21 positivity predicted poorer OS, while both P16 and P21 positivity were linked to improved DFS.ConclusionsThis study highlights the regulatory relationships among OGFR, P16, and P21 in CRC, suggesting their potential as therapeutic targets.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major gastrointestinal malignancy in China with a steadily increasing incidence and mortality, posing a serious public health challenge. According to the latest statistics, there were approximately 517,100 new CRC cases and 240,000 deaths in China in 2022, ranking second in cancer incidence and fourth in cancer-related mortality nationwide. Notably, in Inner Mongolia, Shanxi, and Hebei provinces, CRC consistently ranks among the top four most common malignancies1. Most patients with CRC are resistant to chemotherapy drugs or have poor compliance with chemotherapy regimens, leading to poor prognosis2. Hence, it is imperative to investigate novel treatment strategies for CRC. Recent advancements in gene expression studies have revealed that numerous genes, such as opioid growth factor receptor (OGFR) and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p16 and p21, exert crucial regulatory effects on the onset and progression of malignancies3, 4, 5, 6.

OGFR is a specific opioid receptor first found in brain and abnormal neural brain tissues7. The proteomic profile of this receptor differs from that of the three classical opioid receptors, and it engages with opioid growth factor (OGF) to suppress cellular proliferation8.OGFR has been extensively studied in experiments such as cells and nude mice9, 10, 11. OGFR has been demonstrated to play a notable regulatory function in human head and neck squamous cell carcinoma12, as well as in lung cancer 13. In nude mice, OGFR transcriptional activity was not affected by CRC tumor size14. P16 and P21 are proteins that belong to the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor family and function as inhibitors of kinase activity. P16 is crucial for the diagnosis and prevention of cervical cancer15, 16, while P21 has been shown to suppress the proliferation and differentiation of gastric17 and prostate cancers18. Cellular experiments, including OGFR gene knockout studies, have established P16 and P21 as key regulatory proteins within the OGF-OGFR axis4, and have elucidated their expression patterns in relation to OGFR.

This study employed multi-omics integrative bioinformatics analysis to characterize the expression profile of OGFR in CRC, evaluate its prognostic significance, and investigate its cell type–specific expression patterns and spatial heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment. Prospectively, the expression levels of OGFR, P16, and P21 were examined in CRC patients from the Inner Mongolia–Shanxi–Hebei region between 2023 and 2024, and their associations with tumor stage were analyzed. Retrospectively, the expression of OGFR, P16, and P21 was assessed in CRC patients from the same region diagnosed between 2016 and 2020, in order to explore their relationships with clinicopathological characteristics and to evaluate their correlations with OS and DFS. These findings provide a clinical data foundation for future research.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics and the analysis

Differential expression and survival analysis

The TPM expression levels of normal samples from the GTEx database were paired with those of tumor samples from the TCGA database. The data were transformed into dimensionless Z-scores using (x − μ)/σ by tumor type, thereby standardizing the data to evaluate the differences in OGFR expression between tumor and normal tissues in CRC and READ samples.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using the R package “survival”, with the optimal cutoff values for high- and low-expression cohorts determined by the “survminer” package. The survfit function was then applied to conduct log-rank tests to evaluate the significance of differences between CRC patients with high and low OGFR expression, and Kaplan–Meier curves were plotted. In addition, a meta-analysis of the results from univariate Cox survival analysis was performed using the inverse variance method, with the logarithm of the hazard ratio (log HR) as the primary effect measure, and statistical analysis and visualization were conducted with the “Meta” package.

Single-Cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomic analysis

Using the CRC single-cell sequencing dataset GSE148673, dimensionality reduction and visualization were performed with the UMAP algorithm (n_neighbors = 30, min_dist = 0.3,metric=“euclidean”). The Kruskal–Wallis test (α = 0.05) was applied to assess heterogeneity of OGFR expression across cell types. Cells were stratified into OGFR-positive and OGFR-negative groups to calculate cell-type proportions. Cell annotation followed the TISCH2 workflow, integrating canonical markers (EPCAM, PTPRC) and reference mapping with SingleR.

Spatial transcriptomics were analyzed with Seurat (v4.0) using SpatialFeatur-ePlot and deconvolution to infer spot-level cell composition19. Correlations between cell content and OGFR expression were tested by Spearman’s method (α = 0.05) and visualized with linkET. The SpatialTME workflow, including quality control, SCTransform normalization, lineage marker annotation (MKI67,ACTA2), and spatial gradient analysis with SpatialDE, was used to characterize OGFR expression between tumor core and margin. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.1).

Clinical data

Fresh-frozen CRC tissues and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues were prospectively collected from surgical resections performed between January 2023 and December 2024, while archived paraffin-embedded CRC tissue blocks from January 2016 to January 2020 were retrospectively retrieved. All specimens were obtained from patients treated at our hospital and histopathologically confirmed as CRC. The cases originated from the border region of Inner Mongolia, Shanxi, and Hebei, including Wulanchabu (Inner Mongolia), Datong (Shanxi), Zhangjiakou (Hebei), and surrounding cities. Patient enrollment strictly followed the clinical diagnostic criteria for CRC, and tumor staging was determined according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification system20. Inclusion criteria: (1) patients who underwent radical surgical resection for CRC in our hospital with postoperative pathological confirmation of CRC; (2) no prior antitumor therapy before surgery; (3) availability of complete clinical and pathological data; (4) informed consent obtained from all prospectively enrolled patients.Exclusion criteria: (1) patients who had received neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, anti-angiogenic therapy, or immunotherapy prior to surgery; (2) patients with hepatic or renal dysfunction, severe psychiatric disorders, or chronic diseases; (3) patients with a history of other malignancies; (4) patients with incomplete clinical data.This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (Approval No.K2021014), and written informed consent was obtained from all prospectively enrolled patients prior to participation.

All patients included in the retrospective study underwent annual centralized follow-up via telephone. Follow-up activities included: Regularly recording patient survival status and recurrence/metastasis status; confirming cause and exact time of death for deceased cases through family member follow-up. Death (occurring before follow-up cutoff) and loss to follow-up (uncontactable or still alive at follow-up cutoff) constituted endpoint events. Follow-up ceased upon occurrence of an endpoint event, with the time of death or loss to follow-up recorded. The final follow-up date was December 2024. OS was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the date of the first occurrence of an endpoint event. DFS was defined as the time from the date of surgery to disease recurrence or death from any cause. Both OS and DFS were calculated in months throughout the study.

The expression of OGFR, P16 and P21 mRNA was detected by RT-qPCR

The frozen specimen samples were stored at a temperature of −80°C in an RNA stabilization solution. First, 20 mg of frozen tissue was transferred to a sterile, RNase-free grinding tube. Total RNA was extracted and isolated according to the instructions of SuperbrilliantTM 6-minute High-Purity RNA Extraction Kit (ZHONG SHI, CHINA). RNA quality was evaluated by measuring the absorbance ratios at 260 nm and 280 nm with a UV spectrophotometer. For the reverse transcription process, oligo(dT)15 was utilized as a primer following the protocol outlined in the Supersmart 6-minute Thermostable First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ZHONG SHI, China). The cDNA synthesis was initiated from the poly(A) tail region of the total RNA. PCR primers were subsequently designed and synthesized based on the resulting cDNA templates.

For the OGFR gene, the forward primer sequence was 5′-AGAACCAGGAGTGAACTGGC-3′, and the reverse primer was 5′-CGACTTGAGGATGCGTGTGA-3′. Regarding the P16 gene, the forward primer was 5′-ACCAGAGGCAGTAACC-3′, with the reverse primer being 5′-AAGTTTCCCGAGGTTTCT-3′. For the P21 gene, the forward primer sequence was 5′-CTGAGACTCTCAGGGTCGAA-3′,while the reverse primer was 5′-CGGCGTTTGGAGTGGTAGAA-3′. Finally, for β-actin, the forward primer was 5′-CATCCTGCGTCTGGACCT-3′, and the reverse primer was 5′-TCAGGAGGAGCAATGATCTTG-3′. The Superbrilliant® 2 × ZAPA3G SYBR Green qPCR Mix (ZHONG SHI, CHINA) and cDNA configuration premix were employed for the experiment, the samples were processed by Eppendorf high-throughput PCR instrument. Three parallel control reaction Wells were set up for each sample. The reaction conditions were 50° C for 2 min, 95° C for 10 min, 95° C for 20 s, 60° C for 30 s, 70° C for 30 s, a total of 40 cycles. After the reaction, the fluorescence signal was automatically analyzed and converted into the starting copy number and cycle threshold (Ct).The 2-△△Ct value was used as the relative mRNA expression.

The expression levels of OGFR, P16, and P21 proteins was detected by Western blot

The CRC tissue samples are washed once with PBS, followed by grinding and homogenization with RIPA lysis buffer and proteinase inhibitors at 4°C. The supernatant is then collected through centrifugation at 4°C. Subsequently, the BCA protein concentration detection kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) is utilized to measure the protein concentration of the sample and perform dilution as necessary. Once extracted, the protein sample is blended with SDS loading buffer and subjected to heating at 95°C for 5 min. Following this, it undergoes separation through SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and is subsequently electrotransferred onto a PVDF membrane. Following this step, the membrane is blocked using 5% skim milk solution. Next, OGFR Rabbit pAb (1:500,abcam), Anti-p16 ARC Recombinant Rabbit Monoclonal Antibody (1:1000, Hua An Biotech), and Anti-p21 ARC Rabbit Polyclonal Antibody (1:500, Hua An Biotech) are added to the blocking solution for overnight. The PVDF membrane undergoes washing procedures before being exposed to horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG and incubated at room temperature for one hour. After another round of washing, ECL fluorescence imaging reagent is employed for visualization using a chemical microscope imaging system.After image capture on a film processor, ImageJ software analyzed gray values of each protein band, using β-actin as a loading control.

The protein expressions of OGFR, P16, and P21 were assessed by immunohistochemical analysis

The resected CRC tissues and adjacent normal tissues were fixed in a 10% neutral formaldehyde buffer for 24 h, followed by embedding in paraffin and serial sectioning with a thickness of 4 μm. Subsequently, the paraffin sections were subjected to baking at 65℃ for 2 h, dewaxing, hydration using gradient ethanol, and three washes with phosphate buffer solution (PBS) for 5 min each time. The slices were then placed in a sodium citrate working solution with a pH of 6.0 and heated at 100°C for 25 min before naturally cooling to room temperature. Finally, the slices were immersed in a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution for 10 min followed by washing.Rabbit polyclonal antibody against OGFR (OGFR Rabbit pAb, Abcam, UK) diluted at 1:50, anti-CDKN2A/p16INK4a antibody (Abcam, UK) diluted at 1:100, and anti-p21 antibody (Abcam, UK) diluted at 1:100 were incubated for 8 h. Subsequently, the samples were incubated after diluting the goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody to a ratio of 1:5000. For 30 min at ambient temperature. Staining was visualized using the diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen solution supplied by Zhongshan (China). Following the incubation period, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin for 60 s. They were then subjected to dehydration and clearing procedures prior to mounting. The resulting specimens were examined under a microscope, and images were captured to document the findings.

Each section was evaluated based on the intensity of staining and the presence of positive expression, following a scoring system. Staining intensity was categorized as: no staining (score 0), weak staining (score 1), moderate staining (score 2), and strong staining (score 3). Positive expression was classified as: no positive expression (score 0), ≤25% positive expression cells (score 1), >25% to ≤ 50% positive expression cells (score 2), and > 50% positive expression cells (score 3). The final score was calculated by multiplying staining intensity and positive expression scores. A final score ≥ 3 points indicated OGFR, P16, or P21 positive expression.

Statistical analysis

Use R 4.3.0 to analyze bioinformatics data. Experimental numbers were obtained via Image-J and processed by GraphPad Prism 8. Clinicopathological info and test results were statistically analyzed using SPSS 20.0. Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD; categorical variables, as n %. Chi-square tests were used for qualitative variables, and T-tests for quantitative variables, to assess associations between OGFR, P16, P21 expressions, and baseline features. Significance was set at P＜0.05. Significant results triggered group comparisons. Survival status was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method.

Transcriptomic analysis

Furthermore, patients in the high OGFR expression group exhibited significantly shorter disease-free intervals (DFI) and disease-specific survival (DSS) compared to the low OGFR expression group (Fig. 1 C, D), suggesting OGFR is a prognostic factor in CRC patients. Furthermore, similar trends were observed for overall survival (OS) and progression-free interval (PFI) in CRC patients (Fig. 1 E, F). Concurrently, meta-analysis results indicated that high OGFR expression correlates with CRC DSS and DFI and constitutes a risk factor (HR > 1), consistent with the meta-synthesis findings. After excluding 12 cases, 30 patients were included in the prospective cohort: 9 in stage I, 9 in stage II, 6 in stage III, and 6 in stage IV according to the TNM classification. In addition, 342 CRC cases treated between January 2016 and January 2020 were retrospectively reviewed. After excluding 148 cases, 194 patients were enrolled, and their paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were collected. The association between the positive expression of OGFR protein in CRC tissue and clinicopathological factors in patients

Overall, OGFR protein positivity was more frequently observed in advanced-stage, metastatic, poorly differentiated, and mucinous colorectal adenocarcinomas compared with early-stage, non-metastatic, well-differentiated, and conventional adenocarcinomas.Specifically, in CRC tissues, the positive expression rate of OGFR protein was higher in males (64.5%) than in females (59.7%); however, this difference was not statistically significant after FDR correction (P = 0.500, Padj = 0.7466). No statistically significant associations were identified between OGFR protein expression and age, BMI, operative time, or length of hospital stay after adjustment.The positive expression rate of OGFR protein was higher in the T3–T4 group (66.9%) compared with the T1–T2 group (52.2%); however, this difference did not remain significant after FDR correction (P = 0.054, Padj = 0.216). Similarly, patients with lymph node metastasis showed a higher positive rate (68.9%) than those without lymph node metastasis (56.7%), but this association was not statistically significant after adjustment (P = 0.081, Padj = 0.243). In contrast, a significantly higher OGFR protein positive rate was observed in patients with distant metastasis (100%) compared with those without distant metastasis (58.3%), and this difference remained statistically significant after FDR correction (P = 0.017, Padj = 0.102). Discussion

CRC poses a substantial global health burden, contributing significantly to cancer-related deaths, with rising incidence and mortality rates reported globally21, 22.Despite ongoing efforts, the complete pathogenesis of CRC remains incompletely understood, with emerging evidence implicating strong associations with lifestyle and dietary patterns23, 24. Although there have been notable advances in treatments like surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapies, the 5-year survival rate for CRC patients is still not ideal. In China, the 5-year survival rate for patients with advanced-stage CRC is roughly 8% 25. underscoring the urgent need to address issues related to cancer pain that significantly impact patient quality of life. Consequently26, more in-depth studies are needed to find the proteins associated with the occurrence and development of CRC to provide reference for prognosis.

The objective of this study was to investigate the correlation between the expression levels of OGFR, P16, and P21 in CRC patients and their clinicopathological characteristics, as well as to assess their potential utility as biomarkers for CRC. Our results demonstrated a significant increase in OGFR mRNA and protein expression in CRC patients, concurrent with decreased levels of P16 and P21 mRNA and protein expression. These results demonstrate a coordinated expression pattern where OGFR upregulation is associated with downregulation of P16 and P21.

Firstly, OGFR distinguishes itself from the three traditional opioid receptors. The gene encoding OGFR comprises 7 exons and 6 introns, which together code for a 677-amino acid protein situated on chromosome 20q13.3 27. OGF binding to OGFR results in nuclear translocation and plays a pivotal role in growth regulation. It is implicated in numerous biological processes, encompassing cell proliferation, migration, and survival. The level of OGFR protein is elevated in cancer tissues, consistent with findings from a case-control study involving Spanish patient28. Exogenous OGF can elevate the expression of OGFR in human osteosarcoma cells, thereby augmenting its antitumor efficacy29. Additionally, the protein expression of OGFR in CRC cells can be upregulated by the competitive inhibitor naltrexone, hinting at its role as a tumor suppressor30. When overexpressed, OGFR hinders tumor growth. In addition, clinical pathological data indicate a close correlation between high expression of OGFR protein and distant metastasis as well as TNM staging in patients, suggesting a significant association between tumor growth and metastasis and high OGFR protein expression.This seems to contradict the classical role of the opioid growth factor OGF–OGFR axis as a growth inhibitor via induction of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors. Indeed, experimental work has shown that OGF–OGFR signaling inhibits cell proliferation by upregulating p16 and p2131.A recent pan-cancer analysis reported that OGFR is consistently highly expressed across tumor types and that its prognostic impact is cancer-specific32.These data suggest that high OGFR may mark a subset of tumors with altered regulation of the OGF–OGFR pathway. One possible explanation is that OGFR is upregulated as a feedback response to unchecked tumor proliferation, but that this response is insufficient to halt progression.

The P16 gene plays a vital role as a tumor suppressor, and its product, the P16 protein, binds to cyclin-dependent kinase to regulate the CDK4/cyclin D complex's catalytic activity. This regulation helps suppress tumor cell proliferation and control malignancy33. A study demonstrated that exogenous OGF could enhance P16 protein expression, while naltrexone, an OGF inhibitor, could impede the stimulation of P16 protein expression by OGF. Furthermore, after knockout of the OGFR gene, P16 no longer responded to exogenous OGF6. Clinical pathological data has shown that low expression of the P16 protein is linked to T stage, lymph node metastasis, TNM stage, and histotype in patients. We speculate that decreased P16 protein expression disrupts the cell proliferation program, fostering tumor cell growth. On the other hand, the absence of P16 protein leads to weakened cell adhesion, making tumors more susceptible to metastasis and contributing to an advanced clinical stage. Therefore, the expression level of P16 protein can be used as an additional criterion for staging diagnosis in patients with CRC.

The P21 gene, located at 6p21.2, is a single-copy gene. It encodes a cell cycle arrest protein primarily induced by the TP53 gene and functions as a broad-spectrum cell cycle inhibitor34, 35. The P21 protein predominantly impedes cell cycle progression and suppresses tumor cell proliferation and growth. Low expression of the P21 protein in CRC tissue suggests that its loss may contribute to dysregulated cell proliferation and tumorigenesis. Experimental evidence demonstrates that the OGF-OGFR pathway upregulates P21 to inhibit hepatocellular carcinoma proliferation; silencing OGFR abolishes P21′s inhibitory effect on hepatocellular carcinoma cells, highlighting the regulatory role of the OGF-OGFR pathway in modulating P21 for tumor suppression36. However, limited reports exist regarding OGFR and P21 in human physiology. The clinical and pathological data from this study reveal a significant correlation between increased P21 protein expression in CRC tissues and factors such as T stage, lymph node metastasis, TNM stage, and histotype. These findings suggest that P21 protein expression could be a valuable auxiliary diagnostic marker. Moreover, considering its association with OGFR expression, P21 may also represent a potential therapeutic target with important clinical implications for the diagnosis and treatment of CRC.

The study revealed that OGFR, P16, and P21 play pivotal roles in the regulation of tumor development in CRC patients. The mRNA expression trends were consistent with the protein expression patterns, indicating unaffected translation processes by other factors. Notably, the expression trends of OGFR were inversely correlated with those of P16 and P21 and exhibited statistical significance. Furthermore, the expressions of these three proteins were significantly associated with clinical and pathological features in CRC patients, particularly those with lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis in advanced stages. Therefore, OGFR, P16, and P21 may be correlated with the progression of disease in CRC patients.These research findings provide new theoretical and experimental evidence, addressing a gap in studies involving Chinese patients. They offer strong theoretical support for Prognosis of CRC. Moreover, since exogenous OGF is currently under clinical investigation for pancreatic cancer, our results indicate that OGFR’s role may have prognostic value, but its utility for therapy requires further investigation.

In Chinese CRC patient organizations, OGF and OGFR are present and exhibit progressively increasing high expression levels as histopathological stages advance. A poor prognosis in CRC patients is associated with high expression levels of OGFR. Conversely, P16 and P21 are present but show progressively decreasing low expression levels as histopathological stages progress. Low expression of P16 and P21 is also associated with a poor prognosis in CRC patients. It is speculated that in Chinese CRC patients, there may be an interaction between the OGF–OGFR axis and P16 and P21, influencing the occurrence and progression of tumors.

Limitations

The acquisition of CRC tissue samples depends on many factors such as surgical resection, patient consent, surgical arrangement and hospital cooperation. In order to obtain the research results as soon as possible, only a certain number of samples can be collected within a limited time. Therefore, both sample processing and data analysis require cost input, and the increase of sample size will increase the research cost. Obtaining patient consent is a prerequisite for sample collection, but some patients are reluctant to participate due to privacy protection or trust issues in the study. At the same time, strict privacy regulations must be observed when handling patient samples, which also limits access to samples. Limitations of this study include its retrospective design and relatively small sample size. The protein and mRNA expression analyses were conducted on a limited cohort of CRC patients, which may introduce selection bias and reduce statistical power. All findings are therefore preliminary and require validation in larger, independent cohorts. Additionally, we did not perform functional experiments or mechanistic assays, so causality between OGFR and p16/p21 expression cannot be determined; we have thus avoided causal language. The precise mechanism of action of OGFR, P16, and P21 in CRC patients remains incompletely understood. Although the sample size of the 30 tissues in this paper is relatively small, reasonable experimental design, data analysis methods and other strategies can make up for the problem of insufficient sample size to some extent, and obtain valuable research results. Future research should encompass larger sample sizes and multiple centers to validate our findings and hypotheses. Furthermore, there is a pressing necessity to delve into the functions that these proteins undertake in initiating, accelerating the progression, and enabling the metastasis of CRC. This involves unraveling the regulatory processes of the OGF-OGFR signaling pathway, with the ultimate goal of developing more targeted therapeutic strategies for CRC patients.
