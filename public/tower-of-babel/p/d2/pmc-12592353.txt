The frequency, form, and function of self-talk in everyday life
Authors: Kathryn E Schertz, Ariana Orvell, Susannah Chandhok, Brian D Vickers, Jason S Moser, Ozlem Ayduk, Ethan Kross
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTalking to oneself is a common phenomenon; yet research on spontaneous self-talk overlooks nuances in the self-perspective people adopt when engaging in this process. Laboratory research suggests that distanced (vs. immersed) self-talk has regulatory benefits, but we know little about how often and to what effect people use distanced (or immersed) self-talk in daily life, and whether this varies based on individual differences. We conducted a two-week ecological momentary assessment study (12,966 surveys from 208 participants) to examine distanced and immersed self-talk use in response to four situation types: feeling critical of oneself, needing to prepare for what to say or do, wanting to feel better, or feeling pleased with oneself. Distanced self-talk was less common than immersed self-talk across all situation types, and neither were associated with trait-level emotional distress or narcissism. Distanced (vs. immersed) self-talk was associated with improved momentary affect when implemented in the context of needing to prepare what to say or do, but results did not indicate similar functionality in other situations. These findings shed light on how people intuitively adopt different perspectives in self-talk to navigate their daily lives and suggest that distanced self-talk may have emotion regulation benefits for preparatory situations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22647-2.Keywords: Self-talk, Emotion regulation, Distancing, Experience sampling, Ecological momentary assessmentSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

Engaging one’s inner voice is a common phenomenon1,2. Yet, research on spontaneous self-talk has largely overlooked nuances in the self-perspective that a person adopts when engaging in this process. An emerging body of laboratory research suggests that distanced self-talk, that is using one’s own name or second- and third-person pronouns to refer to one’s self, helps people shift away from their default immersed perspective to a more distanced one3, and is a functional tool that helps children and adults downregulate a range of negative emotions and align their attitudes and behaviors with their goals in self-regulatory contexts3–12. However, we know remarkably little about the basic phenomenology that characterizes this process in daily life: When and how frequently do people spontaneously use distanced self-talk outside the lab? When used in daily life, does distanced self-talk lead to similar benefits as those observed in controlled experiments? Are certain people more likely to use distanced self-talk than others, and does this depend on theoretically relevant individual differences, such as narcissism or people’s tendency to ruminate and worry? The goal of the present research was to examine these questions using ecological momentary assessment, the gold standard method for examining how psychological processes unfold in daily life over time13.

The study of how people “talk to themselves” has a long history in psychology. Piaget was among the first to write formally on this topic in modern psychology. After observing young children talking to themselves out loud, he speculated that this was a sign of unsophisticated cognitive development14. Vygotsky independently observed a similar phenomenon, but interpreted it differently – talking to themselves was a way for children to emulate the guidance and instructions they are used to hearing from their caregivers15. In Vygotsky’s view, self-talk represents an important developmental shift away from relying on others to regulate one’s behavior (i.e., “other-regulation”) to relying on the self (i.e., “self-regulation”). Vygotsky theorized that this self-talk eventually grows silent, serving as an inner voice for adults that helps guide self-regulation15.

Building on Vygotsky’s initial theory, research has robustly linked the activation of self-talk to situations that require self-regulation, including when individuals are engaging in rehearsal or actively planning what to say or do, i.e., preparatory contexts16–18. Research finds that both children19,20 and adults21,22 spontaneously use self-talk when engaging in tasks that are difficult, those that involve performance, and situations that elicit self-criticism, anxiety, or negative emotions (e.g.,16,23–25). The spontaneous and experimentally cued use of self-talk in these contexts has also been associated with improved performance17,20, suggesting that speech directed toward the self may be functional in some situations.

However, most existing work on spontaneous self-talk has not distinguished between the different self-perspectives a person can adopt when engaging in self-talk, and the distinct linguistic forms that accompany these perspectives. That is, a person could talk to themselves from their default, self-immersed perspective, using first-person singular pronouns, for example, “I can do this.” However, they could also talk to themselves as if they were addressing another person, from a more distanced perspective, using second- or third-person pronouns, for example, “Kate, you can do this.” Considering nuances in the self-perspective people take when spontaneously talking to themselves has implications for our understanding of this phenomenon and its functionality, as previous laboratory work has found that cueing distanced self-talk can promote positive outcomes in a variety of contexts. Distanced self-talk promotes problem solving behaviors and intentions4, wise reasoning in social conflicts26, goal pursuit in healthy eating5, challenge responses to active stressors27, and emotion regulation9,28, including for situations where a person is preparing for a demanding task3,9,28.

Several laboratory experiments have detailed the different linguistic perspectives that spontaneous self-talk can take when people engage in emotion regulation or self-control. One study found that people were more likely to use distanced self-talk—giving themselves commands with a second-person structure or in the second-person—in situations that required self-control compared to those that did not require self-control12. In work examining the linguistic underpinnings of emotion regulation, Nook and colleagues found that people who used fewer first-person singular pronouns (among other indices of psychological distance) in a reappraisal task had greater success reducing negative emotions8,29. Similarly, using distancing language when describing negative events in writing has been found to be associated with lower momentary stress30. Thus, it appears that different contexts, particularly those that involve self-regulation, may elicit a distanced perspective when a person talks to themselves31.

One open question is the extent to which the use of distanced self-talk itself is a stable individual difference. Overall self-talk has been measured as a trait using the self-talk scale16; however this scale does not assess the perspective a person adopts during self-talk. It may be the case that some people use distanced self-talk more than others, and/or that use of distanced vs. immersed self-talk is driven by individual differences.

Another related question is the relationship between self-talk and psychopathology, anxiety, and self-defeating cognitions (e.g.,32–34). Negative or self-critical self-talk, in particular, is correlated with depressive symptoms and worry, as well as psychopathology (e.g.,33,35–37). However, it is unknown whether individuals who are prone to depressive symptoms or anxiety are more or less likely to engage in self-talk overall, outside of negative and self-critical contexts, and what perspective they adopt when doing so. Previous research suggests that either could be the case. On the one hand, we could expect emotionally distressed individuals to use immersed self-talk more, and distanced self-talk less, relative to their less emotionally distressed counterparts. This prediction is based on research indicating that people with high levels of depressive symptomology and those prone to experiencing negative affect tend to be more immersive, as indicated by their higher use of first-person pronouns in written and spoken language38–40.

On the other hand, it is possible that emotionally distressed individuals may use distanced self-talk more and immersed self-talk less. This prediction is based on the assumption that emotionally distressed individuals may face a greater need to self-regulate in their daily lives, and may draw on distanced self-talk as a tool to help them accomplish this goal41–43. Regardless of whether emotionally distressed individuals use distanced or immersed self-talk to different degrees, the use of self-talk as a regulatory mechanism may lead to different outcomes for these individuals. Prior research demonstrates that, at times, individuals who are emotionally distressed benefit more from adopting a self-distanced perspective compared to their less distressed counterparts7,44–46. This suggests that these individuals may benefit more from distanced self-talk when they use it in daily life.

Another individual difference that may influence how much someone uses distanced self-talk is narcissism. Observations from popular culture reveal that high-status individuals, at times, refer to themselves in the third person. For example, LeBron James, Donald Trump, Bernie Sanders, and Bob Dole have all engaged in this practice in public discourse47,48. It is possible that high-status individuals’ tendency to refer to themselves in the third person is simply a way to distance their public persona from their private self or engage in self-regulation while deliberating about difficult decisions publicly. However, another possibility is that this practice may reflect grandiose perceptions of the self, a trait of narcissism. Further supporting a possible link between narcissism and the use of distanced self-talk is the fact that referring to oneself in the third person is atypical, and outside of what is normative for adults—something that narcissists are less sensitive to49. That is, research indicates that narcissists are more likely to violate social norms, and may even do so as a means to gain attention.

Current investigation

In the current study, we used ecological momentary assessment (EMA) to investigate (a) the stability and frequency with which people engage in distanced and immersed self-talk in daily life in distinct types of situations, (b) how using distanced or immersed self-talk relates to people’s emotional experience, and (c) and whether these patterns depended on individual differences including a person’s tendency to experience emotional distress or levels of narcissism. We focused on four meaningful contexts to examine these questions, building on existing literature that links self-regulatory contexts to the spontaneous use of self-talk. Each time we texted participants, we asked them to indicate whether they had experienced a situation in which they (a) were trying to feel better about something, (b) were actively planning what to do or say (preparatory), (c) were critical of themselves, or (d) felt pleased with themselves. The last situation, regarding whether participants felt pleased with themselves, was designed to serve as a comparison, non-self-regulatory context. If participants had experienced a situation, they indicated whether they had engaged in immersed or distanced self-talk, or no self-talk, during the situation. Given previous research suggesting that distanced self-talk, in particular, is beneficial in self-regulation contexts (e.g.,3), we expected that engaging in distanced self-talk—relative to immersed self-talk—in the contexts of trying to feel better, preparing for future actions, or being critical would lead to improvements in how people felt over time. We did not have predictions for how using self-talk in situations where people felt pleased with themselves would impact how people felt over time, as we assumed this context would not be accompanied by a motivation to regulate one’s emotions.

The study consisted of three phases. In Phase 1, participants completed a battery of baseline questionnaires (see Supplement for additional questionnaires not reported in the main text), received training on how to distinguish between different types of self-talk (i.e., immersed vs. distanced), and reviewed procedures for the EMA portion of the study (i.e., Phase 2) with a trained experimenter. During Phase 2, participants received text messages with a link to a short survey 5 times a day for 14 days. In each survey, they were asked whether they had experienced each of the four situations described above (critical; feel better; preparatory; pleased) and indicate if they had used immersed self-talk, distanced self-talk, or no type of self-talk in this situation. Notably, participants could check as many as applied (e.g., they could select that they engaged in both immersed and distanced self-talk for the same situation). In Phase 3, participants were debriefed and compensated.

Results

Frequency and stability of immersed and distanced self-talk in daily life

How often do people report using immersed and distanced self-talk in daily life?

We collected 12,966 surveys from 208 participants, reporting a total of 20,646 situations across the two-week experience sampling period. Participants reported experiencing at least one of the four situations we asked them about 78% of time. Overall, there were significant differences in people’s tendency to engage in distanced self-talk, immersed self-talk or no self-talk across all four types of situations, X2 (6, N = 20,646) = 752.35, p < .001. When people experienced one of the four situations that we asked about, they reported using immersed self-talk 43.2% of the time, distanced self-talk 14.5% of the time, and reported not using any type of self-talk 42.3% of the time. Follow-up pairwise comparisons with a Bonferroni correction indicated that people were significantly more likely to use immersed vs. distanced self-talk (padjusted < 0.001) in each of the four types of situations. They were also significantly more likely to not use self-talk as opposed to using distanced self-talk (padjusted < 0.001). There was no difference in people’s tendency to use immersed self-talk vs. no self-talk (padjusted = 0.15).

The two most frequent types of situations in which people used distanced self-talk were those where they were being critical of themselves (21%) and those where they were trying to make themselves feel better about something (20%). There were 38 (18%) participants who reported never using distanced self-talk and four (2%) participants who reported never using immersed self-talk. Among those groups, two participants overlapped, reporting never using either form of self-talk. Of the 168 participants who used both types of self-talk at least once, the majority of people (57%) used immersed self-talk more frequently than distanced self-talk, as indicated by a higher average daily proportion of immersed self-talk. Only 5% had a higher average daily proportion of distanced self-talk use compared to immersed self-talk use, and 38% had distanced and immersed self-talk averages that were not significantly different from each other. Individuals who used immersed self-talk more often also did so to a greater extent than those who used distanced self-talk the most. How stable are individuals in their tendency to use distanced or immersed self-talk over time?

To examine stability of self-talk use over time, we calculated the standard deviation of daily self-talk use over the 14 days of the study, for all situations. The mean daily standard deviation for distanced self-talk was 0.13, while for immersed self-talk it was 0.21. A paired t-test for the 168 participants who used both types of self-talk at least once showed that this difference was significant, t(167) = 8.60, p < .001, d = 0.88, meaning that use of distanced self-talk was more stable (i.e., less varying) than use of immersed self-talk at the daily level (see Fig. 3). When we break down daily stability by situation type, we see that for all situations, distanced self-talk remains more stable than immersed self-talk (see Fig. 4). Using Tukey HSD to compare across situations and within self-talk type, usage of distanced self-talk was more stable for preparatory (mean SD = 0.11) and pleased (mean SD = 0.13) situations compared to critical (mean SD = 0.22) and feel better (mean SD = 0.19) situations, ps < 0.001. Usage of immersed self-talk was more stable for pleased situations (mean SD = 0.28) compared to critical situations (mean SD = 0.33; p = .003); no other differences were significant (see all pairwise comparisons in Supplemental Table S3).

Implications of using distanced (vs. immersed) self-talk spontaneously on affect

Is engaging in distanced (vs. immersed) self-talk spontaneously associated with increases in positive affect?

Using a time-lagged analysis which controlled for affect at the previous time point, we found that during preparatory situations, engaging in distanced (vs. immersed self-talk) predicted an increase in positive affect over time, B = 1.53, SE = 0.49, t(4611.33) = 3.15, p = .002, 95% CI [0.58, 2.48], d = 0.09. Self-talk and individual differences

Is the tendency to engage in distanced or immersed self-talk associated with individual differences in emotional distress?

We did not observe a significant correlation between individual differences in emotional distress (assessed at baseline) and the tendency to engage in distanced (r = .07, p = .30) or immersed (r = .08, p = .28) self-talk. Examining the correlation between individual differences in distress and self-talk within each of the four types of situations separately did not yield any significant correlations for either distanced or immersed perspectives (ps = 0.15-0.76).

Do individual differences in emotional distress moderate the associations between self-talk and positive affect?

As in the analysis above, we used a time-lagged model controlling for affect at the previous time point and added an interaction term between emotional distress and self-talk perspective, to see whether emotional distress would moderate the relationship between distanced vs. immersed self-talk and positive affect. As shown in Table 2, individual differences in emotional distress did not moderate whether people benefited more (or less) from using distanced self-talk (vs. immersed self-talk), indicated by the non-significant interaction terms.

Is the tendency to engage in self-talk associated with individual differences in narcissism?

We did not observe a correlation between individual differences in narcissism and the tendency to engage in distanced self-talk overall (r = -.01, p = .89). There was also no significant correlation between the tendency to engage in immersed self-talk and narcissism (r = − .09, p = .24). Notably, we also did not observe a significant correlation between narcissism and using distanced self-talk in situations where people felt pleased with themselves (r = − .04, p = .55). There were no other significant correlations between individual differences in narcissism and distanced or immersed self-talk usage within each of the four types of situations and (ps = 0.10-0.87).

Discussion

Using ecological momentary assessment, we aimed to gain a deeper understanding of how and when people spontaneously engage in self-talk in their daily life. Our findings are in line with prior work suggesting that engaging one’s inner voice is a common phenomenon1,16. Indeed, people engaged in self-talk 61% of the time in the situations we asked about. Only 2 participants (1%) reported never using distanced or immersed self-talk across the two-week study period. These data are consistent with the idea that most people routinely engage in self-talk, though it is also acknowledged that some individuals report having no inner voice50, a condition recently coined as anendophasia51.

People were more likely to adopt a self-immersed perspective when engaging in self-talk, consistent with research indicating that this represents people’s default way of reflecting on the self52–54. However, our data suggest that people address themselves from a distance using their own name or the second person with relative frequency, as well: 14.5% of the time, overall, in the situations we asked about. Furthermore, in situations where a person was being critical of themselves or wanted to make themselves feel better, the tendency to adopt a distanced self-perspective using distanced self-talk was even higher, around 20%, a finding that future research could explore more deeply.

Although immersed self-talk was clearly the default mode across individuals and situations, there were some people who were more likely to use distanced self-talk regularly. However, the individuals with the highest use of distanced self-talk did not use it at the equivalent level as those with the highest use of immersed self-talk. With regards to stability of use, distanced self-talk use appeared more stable than immersed self-talk use, which suggests a more trait-like tendency. Within distanced self-talk, use was most stable in preparatory situations, which was also the only situation sampled in which this process predicted significant emotion regulatory benefits. Thus, although use of distanced self-talk was lower in these situations (particularly compared to critical and ‘feel better’ situations), its level of use was fairly consistent from day to day. Future work could use recurrence quantification analysis55 to further examine the patterns of consistency vs. fluctuation over time, as in56.

The spontaneous use of distanced (vs. immersed) self-talk had implications for how people felt over time. More specifically, engaging in distanced self-talk served an emotion regulation function when people used it to prepare what to do or say, where its use predicted feeling better than at the previous timepoint. These findings conceptually replicate laboratory work showing that using distanced self-talk to prepare for an upcoming stressful speech task has emotion regulation and self-regulatory benefits, leading people to feel less anxious and perform better on the task3,27. Yet, distanced self-talk did not serve an emotion regulation function when people reported using it to try to make themselves feel better, despite laboratory research suggesting it should be functional in this context. It is possible that laboratory manipulations, which cue people to engage with their deepest thoughts and feelings using distanced self-talk, allow for a more in-depth activation of self-distance, which does not occur when people spontaneously use distanced self-talk in daily life in these circumstances. Nonetheless, taken together, these findings suggest that distanced self-talk may be an effective tool in preparatory situations. Further, our analyses comparing distanced vs. immersed self-talk represent a particularly strong test of the functionality of distanced self-talk in daily life, given a large body of research suggesting that engaging in any form of inner speech (i.e., including immersed self-talk) can support self-regulatory outcomes.

In terms of how the usage of immersed and distanced self-talk relates to individual differences, the tendency to engage in distanced or immersed self-talk was not correlated with any of our measured individual difference metrics, including the two of primary interest – emotional distress and narcissism. The lack of relationship between distanced self-talk and narcissism suggests that distanced self-talk is not used more by individuals with grandiose self-perceptions. However, lay beliefs associating narcissism with distanced self-talk are likely derived from instances where high-status individuals talked about themselves in the third person, not necessarily to themselves. Thus, it is possible that the link between narcissism and self-perspective would only be revealed when other people are present (and thus serves as an attention-drawing mechanism by being outside the norm). Additionally, emotional distress did not moderate the influence of distanced self-talk on affect, suggesting that the efficacy of distanced self-talk as a regulation strategy was not related to an individual’s tendency to experience distress.

One question not cleanly answered by the current research is how distanced self-talk compares to not using any form of self-talk. The supplemental material reports results which compare the use of immersed self-talk, distanced self-talk, or both forms of self-talk together to experiencing a situation but not engaging in self-talk. These results suggest that, in situations where one is feeling critical of themselves, using no self-talk or using distanced self-talk alone is superior in terms of improving positive affect than using both immersed and distanced self-talk together. However, when distanced and immersed self-talk are used together, the temporal sequence may matter. For example, a person could say, “Oh, I’m scared…Kate, you’re fine.” or “Kate, what do you need to do today? I need to finish my response to reviewers and call my sister.” It is possible that shifting from an immersed to a distanced perspective may have different regulatory implications than the reverse. Capturing this nuance would require different methods of data collection, perhaps event-contingent reporting by participants when they engage in self-talk, or experimental designs. In situations where one is preparing, using no self-talk is superior to using immersed self-talk (see Supplemental Figure S6). An important limitation of these analyses is that participants who indicated that they experienced a situation, but did not use self-talk, could have used some other regulatory strategy instead (e.g., distraction) or could have done nothing. The interpretation of these effects is therefore ambiguous. Future research should compare the effects of engaging in distanced self-talk against not using any regulation and also compare the efficacy of distanced self-talk against other spontaneously used emotion regulation strategies.

Further research should also investigate how self-talk can be leveraged to improve emotional wellbeing in daily life, particularly in the context of preparatory situations. Although distanced self-talk was most useful in preparatory situations to improve momentary affect, these were also the situations where people were least likely to use it. Interestingly, however, distanced self-talk was also used most consistently—albeit at lower rates—in this context. Determining why it is used less often for preparation compared to the other situations examined may help design interventions to increase its use in these situations, given its efficacy. One possibility is that participants weren’t consciously aware that using distanced self-talk when preparing was leading to higher momentary affect, suggesting that teaching individuals about the benefits of this technique to raise awareness could in turn increase its use. Nonetheless, the relatively stable use of distanced self-talk suggests that it is activated habitually, and therefore automatically. In this vein, future research can explore the degree to which people deliberately employ distanced self-talk as a regulatory strategy, versus spontaneously engaging this form of self-talk when a goal is activated57.

An additional question for future research is to understand when and how people’s tendency to use distanced (or immersed) self-talk develops. For example, is the tendency to use distanced self-talk correlated between parents and children, suggesting that this strategy may be learned through observation? An alternative possibility is that distanced self-talk represents an activation of some external figure (e.g., a mother, father, or grandparent), as proposed by Vygotsky15, whose representation may be activate more positive or negative language. In this vein, our measures did not differentiate between whether the tone of self-talk was positively or negatively valanced (e.g., “Ariana, you can do this!” vs. “Ariana, you idiot!”). It is possible that the valence of distanced self-talk may be predicted by the quality of one’s attachment figures. Relatedly, it is possible that valence may interact with distanced self-talk to predict differential outcomes. Perhaps engaging in negatively valanced distanced self-talk is harmful. Alternatively, it is possible that distanced self-talk serves a motivating function and is not harmful—even when negatively valanced—because it is delivered from a distance, in contrast to engaging in negative self-talk from an immersed perspective, which could be associated with negative outcomes.

Cultural or linguistic features may be associated with the type of self-talk that people use and its regulatory implications. For example, the cultural factor of interdependence has been linked to use of distanced self-talk when reflecting on negative experiences58. In terms of linguistics, Japanese provides several options of first-person pronouns that vary by context, including formality59. The nuance involved in selecting a first-person pronoun could result in higher, or lower, rates of immersed self-talk among Japanese speakers. Another linguistic feature that may relate to the frequency or type of self-talk in daily life is ‘prodrop’, wherein subject pronouns can be omitted and are inferred through context60. It is possible that the mere act of dropping a first-person pronoun (even if implied) may serve a distancing function, which could have functional implications61.

In sum, these findings provide a window into how people engage in distanced and immersed self-talk when navigating their daily lives. They also suggest that the tendency to view the self from a distance represents a fairly common tendency and highlight how language may support this process.

Method

Participants

Two-hundred and twenty-two individuals (152 females, 62 males, 8 unreported; 56% White, 22% Asian, 8% Black/African American, 7% Hispanic, 6% Multi-racial) were recruited through the University subject pool (n = 40) and through flyers (n = 182) to participate in this study. Participants were largely undergraduates (31% freshman, 24% sophomore, 14% junior, 15% senior, 8% graduate student, 7% other). We did not collect age measurements due to an oversight in programming the demographics questions. Our sample size was determined by running as many participants as possible over the course of two semesters or until data from 200 participants were collected. Data was collected between April 2017 and February 2018. Subject pool participants were compensated up to two hours of credit for their participation. Paid participants were compensated up to $30 for their participation (i.e. $11 for completing Phase 1, $1 per day for 14 days, and a $5 bonus for answering at least 90% of the texts that they received over the course of the 14-day EMA period). Participants who completed 90% of the texts were also entered into a lottery for a $100 Visa gift card. This study was determined to be exempt from review by the University of Michigan Health Sciences and Behavioral Sciences Institutional Review Board. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Belmont Report and Declaration of Helsinki. All participants completed informed consent procedures before beginning the study.

Due to an error, a subset of participants (n = 46) received an incorrect version of the baseline survey. Because these scales were meant to measure stable, trait differences, we re-contacted these participants once the error was discovered and asked them to complete the correct survey remotely, after their participation in the study was complete. We offered participants compensation for their additional time in the form of a $5 Starbucks gift card; 36 participants completed the new survey, and this is the data we report. Survey version did not moderate any of the results.

Attrition and compliance. Participants responded to an average of 86% of text messages. Following prior work62,63, we excluded participants who responded to fewer than 40% of the surveys on a priori grounds. This exclusion criteria applied to nine participants, and an additional five participants did not complete the EMA phase, leaving us with 208 participants for the subsequent analyses.

Phase 1: background and training

Measures

Emotional distress composite. Participants completed the Perceived Stress Scale64 and the brooding subscale of the Ruminative Response Scale65. The Perceived Stress Scale contains 10 items assessing perceptions of stress within the past month (e.g. “In the past month, how often have you felt nervous or stressed?”; 0 – Never to 5 – Very often). Items were reverse scored, when appropriate, and averaged (α = 0.85, M = 2.82, SD = 0.67). The brooding subscale of the Ruminative Response Scale contains five items (e.g. “I think, what am I doing to deserve this;” 1- Almost never, 4 – Almost always; α = 0.72, M = 2.34, SD = 0.69). Examining all items together indicated high consistency (α = 0.86), so the scales were averaged to form a single composite of participants’ trait-like emotional distress (M = 2.58, SD = 0.59).

Narcissistic Personality Inventory - Short. Participants completed a 15-item version of the Narcissistic Personality Inventory66 (One item from the scale was not included due to an error in programming the survey). Participants were presented with 15 pairs of statements and were asked to choose which one they agreed with more (e.g. “I prefer to blend in with the crowd” vs. “I like to be the center of attention.”). Responses that endorsed the narcissistic response received a 1 whereas non-narcissistic responses received a 0; an average score was then created (α = 0.68, M = 0.29, SD = 0.19).

Training

After completing the baseline questionnaires, participants were prompted by the computer to take a short stretch break. Next, they were guided through a short training exercise that introduced them to the concept of self-talk, and its different types. They were told, “Researchers have determined that most people talk to themselves at times. People can talk to themselves in different ways; for example, they can talk to themselves internally, in their heads. Or they can talk to themselves externally, out loud. Sometimes people use first-person pronouns like “I” or “me” when talking to themselves…Other times, people can talk to themselves as if they were addressing themselves using their own name or “you” (for full script, see Supplementary Materials). Participants were instructed to practice using each type of self-talk (i.e., distanced and immersed) on their own, following two different prompts. After the computer-delivered training, the experimenter returned and asked the participant to provide an example of distanced and immersed self-talk to ensure comprehension.

Last, the experimenter reviewed the procedure for Phase 2, the ecological momentary assessment phase, with the participant. Participants were also emailed a summary sheet once they left the laboratory to reiterate the EMA protocol.

Phase 2: ecological momentary assessment

Overview. Most participants began the EMA phase 1–3 days after training. Several (n = 6) participants began the EMA phase later due to technology issues. Participants received 5 text messages between the hours of 10:00am and 10:00pm for a period of 14 days. Text messages included a link, which led participants to a short Qualtrics survey. Text messages were sent at random times with a minimum of at least 60 min between each one. Participants were instructed to answer the surveys as soon as possible after receiving them, but to skip a survey if they received the next text message before they had a chance to answer the previous one.

Items. Each survey contained six questions. The first two questions, which assessed affect and energy level, were presented in a fixed order (i.e. “How do you feel right now?” 1-Very negative, 100 – Very positive, M = 66.04, SD = 19.15; “How tired do you feel right now?” (1 – Very tired, 100 – Very energized, M = 52.76, SD = 22.38). These measures were positively correlated (r = .44, p < .001).

Next, participants were asked whether they had experienced several types of situations since the last text message, and how they handled them in regard to self-talk. As described above, participants were asked whether they had experienced a situation in which they (1) “were really critical of themselves” [critical], (2) “tried to figure out what to do or say” [preparatory], (3) “tried to make themselves feel better about something” [feel better], and (4) “were really pleased with themselves” [pleased]. For each of the four situations, participants were asked to indicate: “No- I did not experience this situation;” “Yes- but I did not talk to myself;” “Yes- and I talked to myself during or immediately after using ‘I’ or ‘me’;” “Yes- and I talked to myself during or immediately after using ‘you’ or my own name.” Participants were instructed to select as many options as applied.

Phase 3: debrief and compensation

After the EMA period was complete, participants returned to the lab where they were debriefed and compensated for their participation. In a later wave of data collection, participants (n = 55) completed the debriefing remotely.

Statistical analysis plan

We report three types of analyses to examine our key questions. For all analyses, we only included observations wherein participants reported experiencing one of the four situations, and reported using either distanced self-talk, immersed self-talk, or no self-talk (in other words, we excluded nonsensical responses e.g., where a participant indicated “No- I did not experience this situation” and “Yes- and I talked to myself during or immediately after using ‘I’ or ‘me’”; n = 983). We also excluded situation-level responses where participants indicated that they engaged in both immersed and distanced self-talk (n = 1,529); exploratory analyses including these responses are reported in the supplement. After applying these criteria, we retained 20,646 situations (M = 99.2; SD = 48.3).

First, we report descriptive statistics on the frequency with which participants engaged in immersed vs. distanced self-talk overall, as well as across the different types of situations. Daily proportion of self-talk use was calculated by dividing the number of situations a participant reported using each type of self-talk by the total number of situations they reported experiencing that day. This was then averaged over the 14 days to compute a single number per participant. We assessed stability of self-talk use by computing the standard deviation of these proportions of using distanced and immersed self-talk for each day across participants (for a similar approach to variability/stability see67.

Second, we report whether engaging in immersed or distanced self-talk has implications for how people feel over time using time-lagged, multilevel modeling to account for the nested data structure of observations at time points within participants. The multi-level models examined whether usage and type of self-talk between subsequent time points (i.e., between text messages, or between T0 and T1) during the four types of situations predicted affect (at T1), controlling for prior affect at (T0; please see the supplementary materials to see the full model equations). Following prior work62,68, we excluded between-day lags from the analysis (i.e., participants’ last ratings of affect on the previous day were not used to predict participants’ first ratings of affect the following morning). This resulted in 3272 critical situations, 4665 preparatory situations, 3826 feel better situations, and 3781 pleased situations. All level-1 covariates were group mean centered following centering recommendations for cross-sectional multilevel models from Enders & Tofighi69, to control for the relative within-cluster deviations. Multilevel analyses were conducted using the lme4 and lmerTest packages in R Version 4.4.170,71. Unstandardized regression weights are reported.

Third, we report between-subject correlations for the tendency to engage in immersed or distanced self-talk and the individual difference measures. We also added emotional distress as a moderator to the time-lagged model to examine if emotional distress interacts with distanced or immersed self-talk to influence affect over time.
