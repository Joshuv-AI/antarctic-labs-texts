Basolateral amygdala parvalbumin and cholecystokinin-expressing GABAergic neurons modulate depressive and anxiety-like behaviors
Authors: Muhammad Asim, Huajie Wang, Abdul Waris, Jufang He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe basolateral amygdala (BLA) is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of depression and anxiety-like behaviors. However, the specific contribution of individual BLA neurons to these behaviors remains poorly understood. Building on our previous study, which demonstrated increased activity in glutamatergic BLA neurons in response to aversive stimuli and that enhancing inhibition in the BLA can alleviate depressive-like behaviors, we investigated the role of individual BLA GABAergic neurons (BLAGABA) in depressive and anxiety-like phenotypes. To address this question, we employed a comprehensive array of techniques, including c-fos staining, fiber photometry recording, optogenetic and chemogenetic manipulation, and behavior analysis. Our findings indicate that BLAGABA neurons show decreased activity during tail suspension and after chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) during social interaction. High-frequency activation of BLAGABA neurons attenuated depressive and anxiety-like behaviors, while low-frequency activation had no effect. Fiber photometry recordings revealed increased activity in BLA GABAergic neurons expressing somatostatin (SST), parvalbumin (PV), and cholecystokinin (CCK) during footshock aversive stimuli. Moreover, we found increased activity in PV and SST neurons and decreased activity in CCK-GABA neurons in the BLA during tail suspension stress. However, after CSDS, BLAPV neurons displayed decreased activity, while SST and CCK neurons showed no changes during the social interaction test. Behavioral analysis demonstrated that chemogenetic inhibition of PV and CCK-GABA neurons induced depressive and anxiety-like behaviors. whereas SST neuron inhibition had no effect. Conversely, chemogenetic activation of BLAPV neurons alleviated depressive behaviors, and activation of BLACCK-GABA neurons alleviated at least partly both depressive and anxiety-like behaviors. This study provides compelling evidence that BLAPV neurons play a critical role in regulating depressive-like behaviors, and that BLACCK-GABA neurons are involved, at least in part, in modulating both depressive-like and anxiety-like behaviors in mice.Subject terms: Epigenetics and behaviour, Physiology, Molecular neuroscience

Introduction

According to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease report, affective states such as depression and anxiety disorders were identified as the primary contributors to disabilities on a global scale [1]. The incidence of depression and anxiety disorders witnessed a significant increase of approximately 25% due to the social and economic unrest caused by the COVID-19 pandemic [2]. The growing number of individuals experiencing depression and anxiety disorders is a cause for concern, as it indicates we are heading to another pandemic of poor mental health. Currently, cognitive behavioral therapy and pharmacological treatments are the two main therapeutic approaches employed for managing these disorders. While these treatments are generally effective, they often require weeks to months to exhibit treatment effects, and a significant proportion of patients exhibit resistance to treatment [3, 4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to gain a deeper understanding of the underlying pathophysiology of these disorders in order to identify new targets for treatment.

Traumatic or stressful life events are significant risk factors for the development of depression and anxiety disorders [5, 6]. In animal models, chronic stress has been shown to induce behaviors resembling depression and anxiety [7, 8]. Studies have provided growing evidence that stressful events can induce changes in synaptic plasticity, protein expression, and morphology in the basolateral amygdala (BLA), a brain region implicated in emotional processing [9–11]. Heightened activity in the amygdala has consistently been observed in individuals with depression and anxiety [12, 13]. Our previous research has demonstrated an increase in amygdala activity specifically in glutamatergic cells expressing the cholecystokinin B receptor (CCKBR), which has been associated with the induction of depressive-like behaviors [14]. Conversely, inhibiting glutamatergic or activating BLA GABAergic (BLAGABA) neurons has been found to alleviate chronic aversive stimuli-induced depressive and anxiety-like behaviors [8]. However, the specific effects of aversive stimuli on BLAGABA interneurons, including those expressing somatostatin (SST), parvalbumin (PV), and cholecystokinin (CCK), and how the modulation of the activity of these neurons influences depressive and anxiety-like behaviors, remain unclear.

In this study, we employed a combination of techniques including c-fos staining, fiber photometry recording, optogenetic manipulation or chemogenetic manipulation, and behavior analysis to investigate the role of BLAGABA neurons in modulating depressive and anxiety-like phenotypes. By utilizing c-fos staining, we were able to identify and visualize the activation of specific GABAergic neurons (SST, PV, and CCK) in the BLA following footshock aversive stimuli. Fiber photometry recording allowed us to monitor the activity of these neurons in real-time, providing insights into their dynamic changes during footshock, tail suspension, and social interaction with aggressive mice. To further dissect the functional role of BLAGABA neurons, we employed optogenetic or chemogenetic techniques, to selectively activate or inhibit BLAGABA neurons, we were able to explore their impact on depressive and anxiety-like behaviors. By integrating these experimental approaches, our study aimed to provide an understanding of how specific types of BLAGABA neurons contribute to the regulation of depressive and anxiety-like behaviors.

Methods & materials

Ethics and participants

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Animal Advisory Committee at the City University of Hong Kong (Kowloon, Hong Kong), known as the Animal Research Ethics Sub-Committee (AEC) (Reference # A307 & AN-STA-00000278). The study design and all methods complied with the applicable ethical guidelines and regulations. No human materials were utilized in this research.

Animals

For the various experiments including behavioral tests, immunohistochemistry, fiber photometry, optogenetics, and anatomy experiments, specific strains of male mice were utilized. These included C57BL/6J mice aged 8–12 weeks, CCK-ires-Cre (Ccktm1.1(Cre)Zjh/J) mice on a C57BL/6J background (referred to as CCK-Cre, obtained from The Jackson Laboratory), PV-ires-Cre (Pvalbtm1(cre)Arbr/J) mice on a C57BL/6J background (referred to as PV-Cre, obtained from The Jackson Laboratory), SST-ires-Cre (Ssttm2.1(cre)Zjh/J) mice on a C57BL/6J background (referred to as SST-Cre, obtained from The Jackson Laboratory), Vgat-ires-Cre (Slc32a1tm2(Cre)Lowl/J, Vgat-Cre, C57 background, The Jackson Laboratory), and Ai14 (B6;129S6-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm14(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J, C57 background) mice. The mice were housed in social groups of five per cage and maintained under standard housing conditions. The cages were furnished with corn cob litter, and the animals were kept in a temperature-controlled animal room at a constant temperature of 23 ± 1 °C. A 12-hour light/dark cycle was implemented, with lights on from 8:00 to 20:00 daily. The mice had free access to food and water. To ensure consistency, all behavioral experiments were conducted during the nighttime period (of animals). The sample size was selected based on our previous studies.

Viruses

The viruses utilized are as follows: AAV9-CAMKII-GCaMP6s (2.50 × 1013 vg/ml) was obtained from Addgene (MA, USA). AAV9-mDlx-GCaMP6s (1.23 × 1013 vg/ml) was purchased from Shanghai Taitool Bioscience, China. AAV9-mDlx-DIO-GCaMP6s (6.05 × 1012 vg/ml), AAV9-mDlx-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry (4.60 × 1012 vg/ml), AAV9-mDlx-DIO-hM3Dq-EGFP (5.54 × 1012 vg/ml), AA9-mDlx-DIO-Chronos-mCherry (5.77 × 1012 vg/ml), AA9-mDlx-DIO-ChrimsonR-mCherry (5.09 × 1012 vg/ml), AAV9-mDlx-DIO-mCherry (2.50 × 1012 vg/ml), and AAV9-mDlx-DIO-EGFP (5.24 × 1012 vg/ml) were purchased from BrainVTA company (Wuhan, China).

Stereotactic surgery

To perform the bilateral stereotaxic injection of viruses into the BLA, mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection). The injection coordinates were as follows, based on our previous study [8, 14]: BLA (anteroposterior [AP] = −1.60 mm, mediolateral [ML] = ± 3.37 mm, dorsoventral [DV] = −4.00 mm from bregma and dura mater). A total volume of 300 nl of the virus was infused into each side of the brain at a rate of 50 nl/min. After each injection, the needle was left in place for an additional 10 min before being gradually withdrawn. For recording the GCaMP signal using fiber photometry, mice were surgically implanted with an optical fiber cannula (6 mm length, numerical aperture [NA] 0.37) held in a ceramic ferrule (Inper company) positioned over the BLA (AP: −1.60 mm, ML: ±3.37 mm, DV: −3.85 mm from bregma and dura mater). For optogenetic stimulation, mice were implanted with bilateral optical fiber cannulas (6 mm length, NA 0.37) held in ceramic ferrules (Inper company) over the BLA (AP: −1.60 mm, ML: ±3.37 mm, DV: −3.70 mm from bregma and dura mater).

Fiber photometry

The fiber photometry recording procedure was conducted following the methods described in our previous studies [8, 14]. Briefly, mice were habituated to the fiber for 10 min before the start of the recording session. The footshock was conducted 3 weeks after virus injection on day 21, tail suspension on day 22, and a social interaction test was performed on day 23 pre-CSDS and on day 33 post-CSDS (Supplementary Fig. S1A).

For footshock (aversive stimuli)-induced calcium measurements, mice received footshocks of 0.2 mA intensity through a metal grid for 2 s. This was repeated for a total of three trials with a 1-minute interval between each trial. Data were analyzed for a 5 s baseline and compared with 2 s (0–2 s) footstock duration. Data were also analyzed for individual footshock responses to all three footshock trails.

For tail suspension (aversive stimuli)-induced activities, mice were suspended by their tails for 30 s by a single experimenter. This was repeated three times with a 1-minute interval between each tail suspension trial. Tail suspension data were analyzed 10 s before each trial of mice hanging and 30 s during hanging for three trials in all mice.

For social interaction (aversive stimuli)-induced activities, mice were placed into the social interaction chamber with a novel CD-1 mouse (aggressor) which was never exposed to experimental mice. Social interaction data were analyzed 5-sec before the mice entered the interaction zone around an aggressive mouse and 10-sec from the mouse entered to interaction zone during the 5-minute recording session. However, each social interaction bout ranges from 2 to 10 s, after that mice leave the interaction zone.

To investigate the frequency-dependent change in inhibition of BLAGABA onto BLACAMKII neurons. Fiber photometry was performed on day 21 before optogenetic activation of BLAGABA neurons, immediately after activation, and 24 h after activation (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Footshock calcium events data were analyzed for 5-sec before the footshock stimuli and 2-sec during the footshock stimuli in all three groups 80 Hz (ChrimsonR), 1 Hz (ChrimsonR), and 80 Hz (Control).

The fitted 405-nm signal was used to normalize the 473-nm signal using the following equation: ΔF/F0 = (473-nm signal - fitted 405-nm signal) / fitted 405-nm signal. This normalization method allows for the calculation of relative changes in fluorescence (ΔF/F0) by comparing the 473-nm signal to the fitted 405-nm signal.

Immunohistochemistry

As described previously [15], following anesthetizing the mice with a pentobarbital sodium overdose, they were transcardially perfused with 30 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by 30 ml of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS, 60 min after the footshock (0.5 mA 30 trails with an interval of 10 s). The brains were then carefully removed and fixed with 4% PFA overnight. Coronal brain sections with a thickness of 50 μm were obtained using a vibratome. The brain sections were washed with PBS and then blocked with 5% goat serum in PBST (0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 2 h to minimize non-specific binding. Subsequently, the slices were incubated with primary antibodies, such as anti-c-fos rabbit antibody (ab190289; 1:1000) or antibodies against PV (1:1000), SST (1:500), or CCK (1:500), for 24 h at 4 °C to enable specific protein detection. After washing the slices three times with PBS, they were incubated with secondary antibodies, such as Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500), Alexa Fluor 647 (1:500), or Alexa Fluor 594 (1:500), at room temperature for 2 h to visualize the target proteins. Following another three washes with PBS, the slices were incubated with DAPI (1:5000) for 5 min to stain the cell nuclei and then washed again three times with PBS to remove excess DAPI. Finally, the slices were carefully mounted onto slides, and images were captured using a Nikon confocal microscope to visualize the labeled proteins and nuclei. Data analysis was performed using Nie offline software to analyze the acquired images and extract relevant information for further investigation and interpretation of the results. The cells are counted all over the BLA and the average is taken for BLA slices taken from 3 mice.

Optogenetic manipulation

To investigate the effects of optogenetic activation of BLAGABA neurons on depressive-like behavior, C57 mice were injected in the BLA with either AAV9-mDlx-DIO-Chronos-mCherry or AAV9-mDlx-DIO-mCherry, along with AAV-mDlx-Cre. Three weeks after virus injection mice underwent chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) protocol followed by a social interaction test to assess the effect of optogenetic activation of BLAGABA neurons on social behavior later. For optogenetic activation of the BLA GABAergic neurons, mice were connected to an optical patch cord and placed on opposite sides of CD-1 aggressor mice using a divider. Blue light stimulation was delivered in a pattern of 0.25 s of light-on followed by 19.75 s of light-off for a total of 10 min. This stimulation paradigm was repeated for three consecutive days or administered daily during CSDS followed by a series of behavioral tests to compare the optogenetic manipulation on depressive-like phenotypes compared with control mice without optogenetic light-sensitive opsin (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Chemogenetic manipulation

To examine the impact of chemogenetic inhibition on depressive-like behavior, we injected AAV9-mDlx-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry or AAV9-mDlx-DIO-mCherry into the BLA of SST-Cre, CCK-Cre, and PV-Cre mice. After the first trial of physical defeat, mice were administered CNO (2 mg/kg), a pharmacological compound that activates the inhibitory hM4Di receptor (Supplementary Fig. S1D). For chemogenetic activation, mice were infused with AAV9-mDlx-DIO-hM3Dq-mCherry or AAV9-mDlx-DIO-EGFP into the BLA. After CSDS, mice were administered with CNO (2 mg/kg) for three consecutive days to alleviate the depressive or anxiety-like phenotype. CNO acts as an agonist for the hM3Dq receptor, leading to the activation of the targeted neurons and subsequent modulation of depressive-like behaviors (Supplementary Fig. S1E).

Behavioral assays

Chronic social defeat stress (CSDS)

CSDS was conducted as described previously [16]. Retired male breeder CD1 mice were assessed for aggression, followed by placing male C57BL6/J, SST-Cre, CCK-Cre, or PV-Cre mice as intruders in the home cage of aggressive CD1 resident mice for 5–10 min of physical defeat, repeated for 10 days. After the physical defeat, experimental mice were placed in sensory contact with the aggressor for 24 h using a partition. A social interaction test was performed 24 h after CSDS. Mice with abnormal behavior or severe wounds are excluded from the study.

Two-trial subthreshold social defeat stress (SSDS)

SSDS experiment was conducted following established protocols as described [8, 17]. The male C57BL6/J, SST-Cre, CCK-Cre, or PV-Cre mice were exposed to 10 min of physical defeat by being placed in the home cage of an aggressive CD1 mouse. After the initial physical stress, the experimental mice underwent 10 min of sensory stimuli on the opposite side of a CD1 mouse using a perforated Plexiglass wall. Subsequently, the mice experienced a second trial of physical defeat with a novel CD1 mouse. Behavioral tests to evaluate depressive-like behavior were conducted on subsequent days.

Social interaction test (SIT)

Social avoidance behavior was assessed using a two-trial SIT. In the first trial, mice were placed in an open-field chamber (44 × 44 × 44 cm3) containing an empty cage made of mesh wire and plastic sheets (10 × 6 × 8 cm3). The mice were tracked using Debut video-tracking software, and the time spent in the social interaction zone (8 cm around the cage) was measured for each mouse individually. This trial was labeled as the ‘No target’ condition. After a one-minute period in the home cage, a novel CD1 aggressor mouse was introduced into the mesh wire cage for the second trial, referred to as the ‘Target’ condition. The social interaction ratio was calculated as the ratio of time spent in the interaction zone with the target to the time spent in the interaction zone during the ‘No target’ condition.

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

Mice were habituated over two consecutive days to 50 ml tubes equipped with rubber stoppers and ballpoint steel sipper tubes in a two-bottle choice setup. One of the bottles was subsequently replaced with a 1% sucrose solution to assess sucrose preference. To avoid side preference, the positions of the bottles were periodically switched. Sucrose preference was calculated as the percentage of sucrose consumed relative to the total volume consumed (sucrose + alternative bottle) multiplied by 100. The total intake of sucrose over 24 h was measured to determine sucrose preference.

Tail-suspension test (TST)

Mice were suspended by their tails, positioned 1 cm from the tail tip, using adhesive tape approximately 50 cm above the surface to prevent contact. Plastic tubes were placed on the tails to prevent climbing or hanging. The mice were recorded using a video camera for 6 min, and the duration of immobility during this period was measured to assess despair-like behavior.

Open-field test (OFT)

Mice were placed individually in the central zone of an open-field arena (44 × 44 cm2) under dim lighting conditions during the dark phase for 10 min. The mice’s movements were recorded using a video camera and analyzed using Smart 3.0 software. Anxiety-like behavior was evaluated by measuring the time spent by the mice in the central zone of the arena.

Quantification and statistical analyses

The data are presented as means ± s.e.m. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 software, employing appropriate inferential methods as specified in Supplementary Table S1. Paired and unpaired t-tests were utilized for two-group comparisons of normally distributed data. One- and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for multiple groups, followed by the Bonferroni test for multiple comparisons. Behavioral data were analyzed blindly. Statistical significance was indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Result

BLAGABA neurons regulate the negative affective states

In this study, we aimed to investigate the impact of aversive stimuli on the activity of BLAGABA neurons. To accomplish this, we first employed footshock as an aversive stimulus and examined the activity of BLAGABA neurons of Vgat-Cre: Ai14 mice, which express red tdTomato in GABA neurons (Supplementary Fig. S2A). The overall number of c-fos-positive cells increased without any alteration in the total number of GABA-positive cells in the BLA (Supplementary Fig. S2C, D). Our findings revealed that mice exposed to footshock displayed a higher number of c-fos positive GABA neurons compared to control mice (only placed in a conditioning chamber without exposing footshock) (Supplementary Fig. S2B–E). To further explore, we employed fiber photometry techniques to monitor the real-time neural activity response of BLAGABA neurons during footshock, tail suspension, and social interaction, before and after CSDS. For this purpose, first, we confirmed the specificity of the mDlx promoter towards GABAergic neurons by injecting AAV-mDlx-GFP into the BLA of Vgat: Ai14 mice (which express tdTomato in GABA cells). We found that the mDlx promoter effectively labeled GABAergic cells, as indicated by a 98% colocalization between GFP+ cells and GABA cells (Supplementary Fig. S2F–H). Next, we injected the AAV-mDlx-GCAMP6s virus to selectively record the activity of BLAGABA neurons (Fig. 1A). The footshock stimuli increased the activity of BLAGABA neurons (Fig. 1B, C). However, the first footshock stimulus produced a stronger response compared to the second and third footshock stimuli (Supplementary Fig. S2I, J). During the tail suspension, we observed that BLAGABA activity was inhibited, as indicated by a decrease in calcium responses (Fig. 1D–G). Further data analysis showed that, compared to the baseline period (−10-0) seconds, the activity of BLAGABA neurons was not significantly changed during the (0–10) duration after the footshock stimulus. However, the BLAGABA neuron activity was significantly inhibited during the (10–20) and (20–30) second periods following the footshock (Supplementary Fig. S2K). Furthermore, our results demonstrated that BLAGABA neurons were rapidly activated during social interaction pre-CSDS. However, post-CSDS, their activity was significantly reduced, as indicated by decreased calcium responses during social interaction (Fig. 1H–L). Collectively, our findings suggest that different aversive stimuli may have different effects on BLAGABA neuron activity, depending on the stimuli nature, and duration of the stimuli. Specifically, chronic aversive stimuli appear to inhibit the activity of BLAGABA neurons.

In light of the observed decrease in BLAGABA activity in the presence of persistent aversive stimuli, which are known to trigger negative affective states such as depression and anxiety-like phenotypes [7, 8], we sought to investigate whether selectively activating BLAGABA neurons could alleviate these phenotypes. To achieve this, we injected the AAV-mDlx-Chronos-mCherry virus to selectively activate BLAGABA neurons (Fig. 2A). Three weeks after the virus injection, mice were exposed to CSDS, a well-established mouse model for inducing depressive and anxiety-like phenotypes [16, 18]. Mice received blue light stimulation at either low frequency (1 Hz) or high frequency (80 Hz) for three consecutive days, with each session lasting 10 min. Our results revealed that mice receiving 80 Hz activation of BLAGABA showed significant reductions in depressive and anxiety-like phenotypes, as evidenced by increased social interaction in the SIT (Fig. 2B, C), improved sucrose intake in the SPT (Fig. 2D), decreased immobile time in the TST (Fig. 2E), and increased center time in the OFT (Fig. 2F–H), compared to the control mCherry (80 Hz) group. Importantly, mice receiving 1 Hz activation did not show significant improvements in these phenotypes (Fig. 2B–H). Furthermore, we found that high-frequency activation of BLAGABA during CSDS increased the number of resilient mice (Supplementary Fig. S2A–G), suggesting a potential role for BLAGABA in promoting resilience to chronic aversive events.

Based on our previous study, which demonstrated increased activity of glutamatergic neurons in the BLA in response to acute and chronic aversive stimuli [8], we sought to investigate how 80 Hz or 1 Hz activation of BLAGABA neurons affects glutamatergic neuron activity in the BLA. To achieve this, we injected AAV-CAMKII-GCAMP6s along with either AAV-mDlx-ChrimsonR-mCherry or AAV-mDlx-mCherry viruses into the BLA and conducted fiber photometry recordings three weeks later (Fig. 2I–J). Our findings revealed that high-frequency activation (80 Hz) of BLAGABA neurons effectively inhibited the activity of glutamatergic neurons in response to footshock aversive stimuli (Fig. 2K, L). This inhibitory effect was observed 24 h after activation when compared to control virus-injected mice. However, the low-frequency (1 Hz) group or high-frequency (80 Hz) activation in the control group failed to produce significant inhibition of glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 2M–P). Importantly, these results are consistent with the observed behavioral phenotypes. These findings indicate that activation of BLAGABA neurons, particularly at high frequency, can effectively alleviate depressive and anxiety-like phenotypes induced by chronic aversive stimuli, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting BLAGABA in the treatment of mood disorders.

BLASST neurons do not modulate the negative affective states

To determine the subtype of BLAGABA neurons that modulate depressive and anxiety-like behavior, we focused on three distinct types of GABAergic interneurons expressing SST, PV, and CCK, respectively. Firstly, we examined the changes in BLASST neuron activity induced by footshock. Footshock increased the c-fos positive cells and did not affect the total number of SST neurons (Supplementary Fig. S4C, D). We observed that footshock increased the C-fos positive SST neurons in the BLA compared to non-stressed control mice (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B). To track real-time changes in neural activity in freely behaving mice, we utilized fiber photometry. We injected the AAV-mDlx-DIO-GCAMP6s virus into SST-Cre mice to selectively monitor the activity of BLASST neurons (Fig. 3A &Supplementary Fig. S4E). The footshock aversive stimulus elicited an increase in the activity of the BLASST neurons (Fig. 3B, C). Similar to the first footshock, the third footshock also demonstrated activation of the BLASST neurons but the difference was insignificant when compared with the baseline (Supplementary Fig. S4F, G). During tail suspension, we also observed an increase in BLASST activity, as indicated by an elevation in calcium responses (Fig. 3D, E). The activity of BLASST neurons was substantially elevated for the initial 10 s (0–10 s) of the tail suspension but then returned to baseline levels over the subsequent 20 s (10–30 s) (Supplementary Fig. S4H). Moreover, our results demonstrated that BLASST neurons were activated during social interaction before and after CSDS, as indicated by no significant change in calcium responses during social interaction before and after CSDS (Fig. 3F–H).

Subsequently, we aimed to investigate whether selectively inhibiting or activating BLASST neurons through chemogenetic methods could modulate depressive or anxiety-like phenotypes. To accomplish this, we injected either AAV-mDlx-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry or AAV-mDlx-DIO-mCherry viruses into SST-Cre mice to selectively inhibit BLASST neurons (Fig. 3I). Three weeks after the virus injection, the mice were exposed to SSDS, as previously described. After the first trial of physical defeat, the mice were injected with CNO. Surprisingly, chemogenetic inhibition of BLASST neurons did not have any effect on depressive or anxiety-like phenotypes, as evidenced by the lack of significant differences during SIT, SPT, TST, and OFT between the control and experimental groups (Fig. 3J–P). Afterward, we injected AAV-mDlx-DIO-hM3Dq-EGFP or AAV-mDlx-DIO-EGFP into SST-Cre mice to selectively activate BLASST neurons (Fig. 3Q). Three weeks after the viral injection, the mice were exposed to CSDS. Mice received CNO for three consecutive days during the sensory period, with each session lasting 10 min. Chemogenetic activation of SST neurons also failed to alleviate the depressive and anxiety-like phenotypes in mice, as indicated by no differences between the control and experimental groups in the tests assessing depressive and anxiety-like behavior (Fig. 3R, X).

BLAPV neurons modulate distinct negative affective states

To assess the functional role of BLAPV in modulating depressive or anxiety-like behavior we adopted the same protocol as mentioned above. We observed that footshock increased the activity of BLAPV neurons, as evidenced by an increase in C-fos positive PV neurons in the BLA compared to non-stressed control mice, without affecting the total number of PV neurons (Supplementary Fig. S5A–D). Subsequently, by selectively monitoring the activity of BLAPV neurons using the AAV-mDlx-DIO-GCAMP6s virus in PV-Cre mice (Fig. 4A & Supplementary Fig. S5E), we found that the footshock aversive stimulus rapidly increased the activity of BLAPV neurons (Fig. 4B, C). The first footshock elicited stronger activation of BLAPV neurons compared to the third footshock (Supplementary Fig. S5F, G). During the tail suspension, BLAPV activity was also increased, as indicated by elevated calcium responses (Fig. 4D, E). However, the BLAPV neuron activity was elevated for the initial 10 s (0–10 s) but then returned to baseline levels over the subsequent 20 s (10–30 s) of the tail suspension test (Supplementary Fig. S5F, G). Furthermore, our findings revealed that BLAPV neurons were rapidly activated during pre-CSDS social interaction, but their activity was significantly reduced post-CSDS, as indicated by decreased calcium responses during social interaction after CSDS (Fig. 4F–H).

To investigate the behavioral effects of BLAPV, we injected either AAV-mDlx-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry or AAV-mDlx-DIO-mCherry viruses into PV-Cre mice to selectively inhibit BLAPV neurons (Fig. 4I). After the first trial of physical defeat, the mice were injected with CNO. Surprisingly, chemogenetic inhibition of BLAPV neurons triggered depressive-like phenotypes, as evidenced by a decreased social interaction ratio and decreased sucrose preference compared to the control group (Fig. 4J–M). However, we did not observe any effect on anxiety-like behavior in the OFT (Fig. 4N–P). Subsequently, we injected AAV-mDlx-DIO-hM3Dq-EGFP or AAV-mDlx-DIO-EGFP into PV-Cre mice to selectively activate BLAPV neurons (Fig. 4Q). As assumed, chemogenetic activation of PV neurons alleviated depressive-like phenotypes in mice. This was indicated by a significant increase in social behavior, improved sucrose intake, and decreased immobile time, without any significant change in center time in the OFT between the control and experimental groups (Fig. 4R, X). These results demonstrate that BLAPV neuron activation is crucial for alleviating depressive-like phenotypes in mice.

BLACCK-GABA neurons modulate the negative affective states

Lastly, we investigated the role of BLACCK-GABA in modulating depressive or anxiety-like behavior. The footshock-induced increase was observed in the overall number of c-fos-positive cells and did not affect the total number of CCK-GABA neurons (Supplementary Fig. S6C, D). We also observed that footshock increased the C-fos positive CCK-GABA neurons in the BLA compared to non-stressed control mice (Supplementary Fig. S6A, B). Subsequently, we injected the AAV-mDlx-DIO-GCAMP6s virus into CCK-Cre mice to selectively monitor the activity of BLACCK-GABA neurons (Fig. 5A & Supplementary Fig. S6E). The footshock stimulus led to an increase in the activity of CCK-GABA neurons (Fig. 5B, C), which remained elevated at a consistent level between the first and third footshock trials (Supplementary Fig. S6F, G). During the tail suspension, we observed a decrease in BLACCK-GABA activity, as indicated by inhibited calcium responses (Fig. 5D, E). Compared to the baseline period of 10 sec (-10-0), the activity of CCK-GABA neurons was significantly inhibited during the initial 10 sec (0–10 s), but this inhibition was of higher statistical significance when compared to the final 10 sec (20–30 s) of the tail suspension test (Supplementary Fig. S6H). Furthermore, our results demonstrated that BLACCK-GABA neurons were slightly activated during pre-CSDS social interaction without any significant change post-CSDS, as indicated by no change in calcium responses during social interaction after CSDS (Fig. 5F–H).

To study the behavioral effects of BLACCK-GABA, we injected either AAV-mDlx-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry or AAV-mDlx-DIO-mCherry viruses into CCK-Cre mice to selectively inhibit BLACCK-GABA neurons (Fig. 5I). After the first trial of physical defeat, the mice were injected with CNO. Surprisingly, chemogenetic inhibition of BLACCK-GABA neurons selectively decreased the social interaction ratio and center time, without any significant change in sucrose intake and immobile duration (Fig. 5J–P). Subsequently, we injected AAV-mDlx-DIO-hM3Dq-EGFP or AAV-mDlx-DIO-EGFP into CCK-Cre mice to selectively activate BLACCK-GABA neurons (Fig. 5Q). Interestingly, chemogenetic activation of BLACCK-GABA neurons alleviated anxiety-like behavior, as indicated by a significant increase in center time in the OFT (Fig. 5V–X). There was no significant improvement in sucrose intake or social interaction ratio, but the immobile time was decreased (Fig. 5R–U). These results demonstrate that BLACCK-GABA neurons have the potential to modulate the negative affective states induced by aversive stimuli, particularly anxiety-like behaviors and at least partially depressive-like phenotypes.

Discussions

Given the pivotal role of the amygdala in affective states [13, 19, 20] and the limited efficacy of current treatments for depression and anxiety disorders [3, 4], further understanding of the pathophysiology of these disorders is necessary. In our study, we demonstrated that BLAGABA neurons are activated by acute footshock stimuli but show decreased activity in response to tail suspension and CSDS. High-frequency activation of BLAGABA selectively inhibits glutamatergic neurons and reduces depressive and anxiety-like symptoms. BLAGABA appears to regulate amygdala activity through three subtypes (SST, PV, and CCK) [21], which may modulate depression and anxiety. SST, PV, and CCK neurons exhibit increased activity during footshock stimuli, while tail suspension selectively increases SST and PV activity while decreasing CCK activity. Notably, PV activity is reduced after CSDS, while CCK and SST show insignificant differences. Activation of BLAPV neurons has an antidepressant-like effect, activation of BLACCK-GABA neurons has an anxiolytic and a partial antidepressant-like effect, and BLASST has no effect on depression or anxiety-like phenotypes, as observed in behavior tests.

In the local microcircuit of the BLA, our investigation revealed that BLAGABA interneurons exert inhibitory control over glutamatergic neuronal activity, consistent with previous findings [8, 22]. Notably, high-frequency activation of BLAGABA, but not low-frequency activation, effectively suppresses glutamatergic neuronal activity, suggesting the involvement of additional neuropeptidergic mechanisms. Prior studies have demonstrated that neuropeptide release requires high-frequency stimulation rather than low-frequency stimulation [23, 24]. Furthermore, our recent research demonstrated that high-frequency stimulation of neuropeptide CCK-expressing neurons enhances inhibitory processes in the auditory cortex [25]. Future studies can utilize advanced technologies, such as G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR)-based sensors [26], to detect the release of CCK and other neuropeptides during high-frequency activation of GABAergic neurons. These sensors enable precise monitoring of neuropeptide release dynamics, providing valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying the effects of high-frequency GABAergic neuron activation. Furthermore, our findings indicate that high-frequency activation of BLAGABA reduces depression and anxiety by effectively modulating negative affective states. This suggests that interneurons exert strong control over excitatory neuron activity, and disruptions in this interaction may result in hyperactivity and, consequently, anxiety and depression. Thus, our results support the association between increased amygdala activity and the presence of depression and anxiety [12, 13].

BLAPV neurons, which are the largest group of GABAergic neurons in the BLA, form synapses on the soma of glutamatergic projection neurons [27, 28]. This suggests that they have the ability to strongly inhibit the output of glutamatergic neurons. Previous research has demonstrated the involvement of BLAPV neurons in associative fear learning and memory formation [21, 29]. In this study, we investigated the role of BLAPV neurons in modulating anxiety and depressive-like phenotypes in mice. Interestingly, we observed that acute stress activates BLAPV neurons, while chronic stress leads to a decrease in BLAPV activity. This decrease in activity suggests that the reduced inhibition of glutamatergic projection neurons by BLAPV neurons may contribute to the development of depressive-like phenotypes. However, the specific reasons behind the inhibition of BLAPV neurons after CSDS remain unclear. Therefore, it is important to investigate the factors that cause this inhibition. Considering that the BLA receives input from various sources [8], it is very likely that the input from long-range GABAergic neurons onto BLAPV neurons is strengthened after CSDS. This strengthening of input may contribute to the inhibition of BLAPV neurons. Another potential mechanism is the involvement of local VIP GABAergic interneurons, which are known to inhibit BLAPV neurons and disinhibit glutamatergic neurons [30]. It is possible that these interneurons cause strong inhibition of BLAPV neurons following CSDS. BLAPV neurons are also characterized by their enrichment in the 5HT receptor [31], and the loss of these receptors can lead to a decrease in network PV inhibition [32, 33], which supports that this could be another potential mechanism underlying the inhibition of BLAPV neurons after CSDS. Moreover, BLAPV neurons were observed to colocalize with calbindin [34], and the activation of calbindin neurons has been associated with reward and the mitigation of depressive-like symptoms [35]. In line with these findings, we found that the activation of BLAPV neurons leads to the alleviation of depressive-like phenotypes.

BLASST neurons are 15% of the GABAergic neurons in the BLA, where they establish synapses on the dendrites of BLA glutamatergic projection neurons [21, 36]. Previous research has demonstrated that the activation of SST neurons in the brain produces anxiolytic effects [36, 37]. However, the specific impact of BLASST neurons on anxiety and depression has not been fully understood. It has been previously observed that exposure to anxiogenic environments leads to an increase in c-fos activity in BLASST neurons [38]. Similarly, our study exhibited that aversive footshock stimuli increased the number of c-fos positive SST neurons in the BLA. Furthermore, we observed an elevation in the activity of BLASST neurons during tail suspension stress. However, we did not find any significant changes in the activity of BLASST neurons before and after CSDS. Intriguingly, the activation or inhibition of BLASST neurons did not influence anxiety or depressive-like behaviors. This discrepancy could potentially be attributed to differences in the stress protocols employed. The findings of the current study suggest that the modulation of BLASST neuron activity, whether inhibited or activated, does not affect depressive or anxiety-like phenotypes atleast in the social defeat stress model of mice.

Although CCK-GABAergic neurons in the BLA are present in small numbers compared to PV interneurons, they are equally effective at inhibiting the activity of glutamatergic neurons by forming synapses at the soma [39, 40]. Previous research has demonstrated the crucial role of CCK in regulating anxiety [41–43]. Consistent with these findings, our study reveals that BLACCK-GABA neurons modulate anxiety-like and partially depressive-like behaviors in a social defeat mice model: chemogenetic inhibition triggers anxiety-like and social avoidance behaviors, while activation alleviates the anxiety and partially depressive phenotypes. Additionally, we observed an increase in the number of c-fos-positive BLACCK-GABA neurons following footshock stress. Interestingly, we found that the activity of BLACCK-GABA neurons decreased during tail suspension stress, without any changes during the social interaction test pre- and post-CSDS. A recent study showed that SST neurons inhibit CCK interneurons in the amygdala, and their activity is also modulated by acetylcholine [44]. Therefore, the decreased activity observed in CCK-GABA neurons during the tail suspension test may be attributed to the increased activity of SST neurons. Several studies have reported the expression of cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1R) on presynaptic CCK-GABA neurons [45, 46], and CB1R is known to play a critical role in presynaptic inhibition [47–49]. It is hypothesized that CB1R activation during tail suspension stress leads to the inhibition of presynaptic GABA or CCK release, resulting in the disinhibition of glutamatergic neurons and the induction of anxiety-like phenotypes. However, further investigations are needed to elucidate the detailed mechanism underlying the inhibition of CCK-GABA neurons during tail suspension stress. In our recent study, we discovered that CCK-GABA neurons induce inhibition in the auditory cortex via a novel receptor called GPR173 [25], a similar mechanism may be involved in the amygdala [50]. Therefore, it would be intriguing to explore the involvement of GPR173, a novel CCK receptor, in the regulation of anxiety-like phenotypes in the BLA. Overall, our findings demonstrate that BLAPV neurons modulate depressive phenotypes, while CCK-GABA neurons modulate anxiety-like and at least partially depressive-like phenotypes in mice.
