Differential Effects of Telehealth on Psychological Outcomes in Heart Failure: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
Authors: Firman Sugiharto, Yanny Trisyani, Aan Nuraeni, Khatijah Lim Abdullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure (HF) represents a significant global health challenge. Individuals with HF commonly experience psychological issues, including depression and anxiety, which negatively affect quality of life (QoL) and clinical outcomes. While telehealth has been identified as a promising approach for chronic disease management, its effectiveness in enhancing psychological outcomes in HF remains unclear.ObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of telehealth interventions in improving psychological outcomes among individuals with HF.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted across six electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, EBSCOhost, Springer Nature, Taylor & Francis, and Sage Journals). Methodological quality was evaluated with the JBI and RoB 2 tools. Pooled effects were reported as standardized mean differences (SMD) or mean differences (MD), applying fixed- or random-effects models based on heterogeneity (I2). Subgroup analyses were conducted by delivery model, intervention duration, and measurement tool. Publication bias was examined using funnel plots, Egger’s test, and Begg’s test.Results26 RCTs were included, with 17 (n = 4,796) entered the meta-analysis. Telehealth showed no significant effect on QoL (SMD=−0.31; p=0.25; I2=98%) or depression (SMD= −0.65; p=0.07; I2= 96%), but were associated with a significant reduction in anxiety (SMD= −0.91; p=0.009; I2 = 94%). Subgroup analyses indicated that mobile applications significantly improved QoL (SMD= −0.92; p=0.01), while phone-call support significantly reduced depression (SMD= −1.28; p=0.02). Significant reductions in depression were also observed when using HADS (p=0.006) and BDI (p=0.010), and with follow-up >6 months (p=0.03).ConclusionTelehealth interventions significantly reduced anxiety among individuals with HF but demonstrated limited effects on depression and QoL. The presence of very high heterogeneity across all outcomes reduces the certainty of these findings and necessitates cautious interpretation. The effectiveness of telehealth as a psychological intervention may depend on specific design and implementation factors.Keywords: anxiety, depression, heart failure, meta-analysis, quality of life, systematic review, telehealth

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents a global health issue, affecting around 64 million people worldwide.1 In the United States and Europe, it accounts for more than 1 million hospital admissions. Additionally, previous research has shown that the average 30-day readmission rate across 18 countries is approximately 13%.2 Beyond its clinical impact, HF also imposes a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems globally.3 This condition highlights the considerable challenges in managing HF, as it not only strains healthcare resources but also negatively impacts patients’ quality of life (QoL).

Psychological burden is one of the problems frequently encountered by HF patients.4 Many patients describe sadness or depression (44%), anxiety or fear of dying (33%), isolation, hopelessness and feeling like a burden.5 Depression is frequently observed among patients with HF and is strongly linked to reduced health-related quality of life (HRQoL).6–9 A substantial proportion of individuals with HF experience depression, with reported prevalence rates varying between 21.5% and 65% across different studies and populations.6,10,11 These symptoms are clinically important because they worsen functioning, self‑care and outcomes.

QoL is a multidimensional construct that includes physical, social, functional, and psychological domains.12 In HF, QoL is strongly influenced by patients’ psychological status, especially depression and anxiety.6,7 Patients with good QoL show higher adherence to therapy, greater motivation to perform self-care, and a better prognosis.13–16 On the other hand, patients who have a poor QoL are indicated to have poor self-care, so that patients experience an increase in physical and psychological symptoms.6,7 However, many studies have reported that depression in HF patients continues to increase. Depression also plays a role as the strongest predictor compared to other psychological (anxiety) and clinical variables in influencing the QoL of patients with heart failure.6,7,9,17,18 Therefore, management of depression and anxiety is an important component in the comprehensive management of patients with HF.

The management of depression in HF patients is considered less than optimal, and it is reported that there are still many challenges and obstacles faced.19,20 Treatment for depression in hospitals has generally been basic, such as providing brief education, simple counselling, pharmacotherapy or referrals if deemed necessary.19–21 Then, the effectiveness of pharmacological therapy is also limited because the use of certain antidepressants can interact with heart failure drugs, increase the risk of arrhythmia, and worsen the patient’s hemodynamics.19 In addition, various challenges are still faced, including difficulties in identifying depression due to overlapping symptoms, suboptimal routine screening, limited psychological interventions, and a lack of integration of multidisciplinary approaches.19,22–24 This condition causes depression to go often goes unidentified and even untreated, and many treatment needs remain unmet.25

In recent years, telehealth has emerged as an innovative approach in the management of patients with chronic diseases, including HF.26 Telehealth refers to the delivery of healthcare services and information using information and communication technologies, and includes several related but distinct modalities.27–29 Telemedicine is the remote provision of clinical services by healthcare professionals, while telenursing focuses on nurse-led remote care, education, monitoring, and coordination.30,31 Telemonitoring involves the remote transmission and clinical review of physiological or symptom data. Mobile health (mHealth) uses smartphones or mobile applications to deliver interventions, often including self-monitoring, reminders, and two-way communication.26,32,33 These modalities vary in intensity, interactivity, and personalization, and are therefore likely to have different effects on psychological outcomes.

In this review, psychological outcomes were operationally defined as patient-reported affective and HRQoL states, specifically depression, anxiety, and QoL, measured using validated self-report instruments. Differences in intervention type, program duration, population characteristics, and outcome measurement methods contribute to the inconsistent findings.26 Several systematic reviews have assessed telehealth interventions in HF, but they differ from this review in scope, focus, and analytical depth. Most previous syntheses addressed physical outcomes,34 self-care behaviours,35,36 and QoL.35 The most comparable meta-analysis focused solely on telenursing and QoL, pooling 11 RCTs (n = 2,032).35 In contrast, this review covers a broader range of telehealth modalities, including telemonitoring, mHealth, telecare, and hybrid models, and examines three psychological domains: depression, anxiety, and QoL, across a larger evidence base. Additionally, this review conducted predefined subgroup analyses by delivery model, measurement instrument, and intervention duration to determine when telehealth is most effective and to explore sources of heterogeneity. This review advances current evidence by clarifying whether and under what conditions telehealth improves psychological outcomes in HF.

This indicates a critical gap in the evidence regarding the effectiveness of telehealth interventions in addressing psychological burden among patients with HF. Therefore, a comprehensive evidence synthesis is needed to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of telehealth in improving psychological outcomes, particularly depression, anxiety, and QoL in patients with HF. Based on this rationale, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of telehealth interventions in improving psychological outcomes including depression, anxiety, and QoL among patients with HF.

Materials and Methods

Design

This study employed a systematic review approach, following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.37,38 This review has been registered in PROSPERO with the ID number CRD420251050361.

Eligibility Criteria

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, three independent reviewers screened and selected eligible studies following the PRISMA guidelines (see Figure 1). The PICOT framework guided the formulation of the research question and inclusion criteria. The population (P) targeted adults diagnosed with heart failure. The intervention (I) of interest was telehealth, including telemedicine and telenursing approaches. The comparator (C) was the standard of care. The outcomes (O) specifically emphasised psychological outcomes, including depression, anxiety, quality of life, and other related psychosocial measures. The type of study (T) included was limited to randomised controlled trials (RCTs).

PRISMA Flowchart.

Studies were included if they were available in full text, published in English, and employed an experimental design to assess the effectiveness of telehealth interventions in improving psychological outcomes among adults with heart failure. Studies were excluded if the full text could not be accessed, were written in languages other than English, or were categorised as secondary studies (eg., reviews). No limitations were applied to the publication year to capture a comprehensive range of relevant evidence.

Search Strategy

The literature search was conducted from database inception to 28 March 2026, with no restriction on publication year applied to capture the full body of relevant evidence. The identification of articles was conducted systematically through six main databases: EBSCOhost, PubMed, Scopus, Taylor and Francis, Springer Nature, and Sage Journal. The keywords used in the search process included:

(“heart failure” OR “cardiac failure” OR “congestive heart failure” OR CHF) AND (“telehealth” OR “telemedicine” OR “telenursing” OR “remote monitoring” OR “mhealth” OR “ehealth” OR “digital health”) AND (“psychological outcomes” OR depression OR anxiety OR “quality of life” OR “mental health” OR “self-efficacy” OR psychosocial).

The Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were utilized to refine or broaden the search results and to capture relevant literature across various databases effectively. More details of the search strategy are provided in Table S1. In addition to database searching, hand searching was also conducted. Hand searching is the manual process of reviewing the reference lists of relevant articles, journals, or conference proceedings to identify additional studies that may not have been retrieved from electronic databases. This approach was used to ensure a more comprehensive identification of eligible studies for inclusion in the review.

Study Selection and Quality Appraisal

The study selection process was conducted independently by two reviewers (FS and AN) using predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. In the preliminary stage, duplicate records were identified and eliminated using Mendeley reference management software. Subsequently, the titles, abstracts, and full-text articles were carefully assessed for relevance to the research objectives, using the established eligibility criteria. In the final phase, all authors performed an in-depth evaluation of the selected studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool to assess methodological quality.39

For the quality appraisal, randomised controlled trials were assessed using 13 specific criteria. Each criterion was rated on a scale of 4 possible responses: Yes, No, Not Applicable, and Unclear. A score of 1 was assigned to each “Yes” response, while the remaining options were scored 0. Studies with a total JBI score of less than 75% were excluded from the review. Any differences in scoring were resolved through discussion among the authors, although no disagreements arose regarding the inclusion of the selected studies.

Assessment of Risk of Bias in Included Studies

Two reviewers (FS and AN) independently assessed the Risk of Bias (RoB) for RCT studies included in this review analysis using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool. RCT studies consist of five RoB domains, including (1) randomisation process, (2) deviation from the intended intervention, (3) missing outcome data, (4) outcome measurement, and (5) selection of reported outcomes.40 RoB is defined as “high”, “low”, “some concern”, or “no information” for each domain. Discrepancies in the assessment results were then discussed, and a review by all authors determined the decision.

Data Extraction

The data extraction process was conducted independently by two reviewers (FS and YT), with support from a research assistant. To ensure the accuracy, consistency, and completeness of the extracted data, the results were subsequently verified by a third reviewer. Any discrepancies identified during this process were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved among all authors. A structured data extraction form was developed to collect relevant information from each included study systematically. The extracted data were then organised into two summary tables to facilitate comprehensive analysis. The Characteristics of Included Studies presents the general profile of each study, including study reference (author and year), country or setting, study design, participant characteristics (eg., type of heart failure, age, and proportion of male participants), sample size, duration of follow-up, intervention, control and result. This systematic extraction approach ensured a comprehensive synthesis of the evidence and supported both qualitative and quantitative analyses in subsequent stages of the review.

Data Analysis and Synthesis

Data synthesis was performed by extracting effect size measures, including mean difference (MD) or standardised mean difference (SMD), along with standard deviation (SD), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values for both intervention and control groups from each included study. Statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark). Qualitative analysis was also conducted using narrative synthesis on articles that were not analysed quantitatively and combined with pooled effects.

The pooled effects of telehealth interventions on psychological outcomes—such as depression, anxiety, and quality of life were calculated using MD or SMD with corresponding 95% CIs and p-values, based on comparisons between intervention and control groups. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed by visually examining forest plots (confidence interval overlap) and quantitatively using the I2 statistic. A fixed-effect model was applied when heterogeneity was low (I2 < 50%), whereas a random-effects model was used when heterogeneity was moderate to high (I2 ≥ 50%) to account for variability between studies.

To ensure consistency and reduce heterogeneity, several predefined data handling strategies were applied: (1) for studies with multiple intervention arms, only telehealth-related interventions were compared with usual care; (2) when outcomes were reported at multiple follow-up periods, data from the last post-intervention assessment were selected; (3) if different measurement tools were used for the same psychological outcome, the most frequently used instrument across studies was prioritized to enhance comparability and reduce methodological heterogeneity; and (4) if heterogeneity remained high, additional analyses were conducted using Jamovi version 2.3.21 to detect potential outliers and assess their impact on pooled estimates.

Subgroup Analysis

All subgroups were defined a priori and were prospectively documented in the registered protocol PROSPERO. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore differences in the effectiveness of telehealth interventions on psychological outcomes among patients with HF. These analyses aimed to identify specific conditions under which telehealth interventions produced stronger or weaker effects. The subgroup classifications were based on predefined criteria: (1) type of telehealth intervention, including telemonitoring and mobile health (mHealth) platforms; (2) duration of intervention, categorised as short-term (≤6 months) or long-term (>6 months), and (3) based on measurement tools to evaluate the influence of intervention length on outcome sustainability (Table 1).

Publication Bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot visualisation, forest plot interpretation, Egger’s test, Kendall’s tau rank correlation test, and a regression test (Z) in Jamovi version 2.4.8.0. A p-value >0.05 for funnel plot asymmetry was considered indicative of no significant publication bias. If no asymmetry was detected, all outcomes were included in the final analysis.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness and stability of the meta-analysis results. This process involved excluding studies with small sample sizes that could potentially act as outliers and disproportionately influence the pooled results. Sensitivity testing was specifically conducted for all outcomes to ensure the reliability of the findings.

Results

Study Selection

A total of 9,346 records were identified through database searching, including PubMed (n = 556), Springer Nature (n = 4,538), Scopus (n = 1,220), EBSCOhost (n = 766), Taylor and Francis (n = 1,975), and Sage Journal (n = 291). After removing 1,784 duplicate records, 7,562 records remained for screening. Following title and abstract screening, 7,514 records were excluded (Figure 1).

Subsequently, 48 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, all of which were successfully retrieved. Of these, 27 articles were excluded due to inappropriate study design (n = 4) and irrelevant outcomes (n = 23). Additionally, 5 records were identified through hand searching. All retrieved reports were assessed for eligibility, and none were excluded. In total, 26 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review, comprising 21 studies identified through database searching and 5 from hand searching. Among these, 17 studies were included in the quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis).

Quality Appraisal

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Randomised Controlled Trials (Table S2). Overall, the studies demonstrated a high level of methodological rigour. Most of the included trials achieved the maximum score of 13 out of 13, while a few studies scored slightly lower, ranging from 11 to 12.

Across all studies, key methodological domains were consistently fulfilled. All studies employed appropriate randomisation procedures, and the majority adequately reported allocation concealment. Baseline characteristics between intervention and control groups were generally comparable, indicating good internal validity. In addition, outcome measurements were conducted consistently and reliably across groups, and appropriate statistical analyses were applied in all studies. Follow-up was complete in most cases, and participants were analysed according to their original group allocation, suggesting adherence to the intention-to-treat principle. Blinding of outcome assessors was also well reported across studies, minimising detection bias. However, several studies reported unclear or absent blinding of participants, a common limitation in telehealth interventions due to the nature of the interventions themselves. In a few cases, allocation concealment was also reported as unclear.

Risk of Bias Results of Included Studies

The risk of bias assessment across the 26 included studies indicates an overall good methodological quality, with the majority of studies demonstrating a low risk of bias across most evaluated domains (D1–D5) (Figures 2 and 3). In Domain 1 (bias arising from the randomisation process), all studies were judged to have a low risk of bias, suggesting that appropriate and adequately reported randomisation procedures were consistently implemented. Similarly, Domains 3 (bias due to missing outcome data), 4 (bias in measurement of the outcome), and 5 (bias in selection of the reported result) were uniformly rated as low risk across all studies.

However, some concerns were identified in Domain 2 (bias due to deviations from intended interventions). Several studies, including Davoudi et al (2020),41 Dorsch et al (2021),42 Hale et al (2016),43 Kohler et al (2021),44 Piotrowicz et al (2016, 2024),45,46 Smolis-Bąk et al (2015),47 Villani et al (2015),48 and Völler et al (2022),49 were rated as having “some concerns” in this domain. This suggests potential issues with adherence to the intervention protocol or insufficient control over deviations, which may introduce performance bias and affect the estimated intervention effects.

Publication Bias

Publication bias was assessed using fail-safe N, Begg and Mazumdar rank correlation test, and regression-based tests (Egger’s test) across all outcomes (see Table 2). For the QoL outcome (n = 16), the Begg and Mazumdar test indicated no evidence of publication bias (r = 0.017; p = 0.965), which was further supported by Egger’s regression test (Z = −0.082; p = 0.935). For depression (n = 9), the Begg and Mazumdar test also demonstrated no indication of publication bias (r = −0.056; p = 0.919). Egger’s regression test yielded a non-significant result (Z = 1.527; p = 0.127), indicating no evidence of small-study effects. Similarly, for anxiety (n = 4), no evidence of publication bias was observed based on the Begg and Mazumdar test (r = 0.333; p = 1.000) and Egger’s regression test (Z = 0.945; p = 0.345).

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates across psychological outcomes by excluding studies with small sample sizes and those with dominant sample contributions (Figures S4–S9). For QoL, excluding small-sample studies did not materially change the findings.43,52,53 Before exclusion, the pooled effect was non-significant (SMD = 0.04; 95% CI: −0.48 to 0.55; p = 0.89) with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 97%, p < 0.00001). After exclusion, the results remained non-significant (SMD = −0.04; p = 0.89), and heterogeneity increased slightly (I2 = 98%; p < 0.00001), indicating that QoL outcomes remained stable and were not driven by smaller studies.

For depression and anxiety outcomes, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding Bakitas et al (2020),54 which had the largest sample size and substantial weight in the pooled estimates. For depression, the pooled effect decreased from a large effect size (SMD = −1.34; p < 0.001; I2 = 96%) to a moderate but still significant effect (SMD = −0.55; p < 0.001; I2 = 82%) after exclusion. Similarly, for anxiety, the pooled effect decreased from a significant effect (SMD = −1.03; p = 0.02; I2 = 94%) to a borderline significant effect (SMD = −0.69; p = 0.05; I2 = 80%). Despite reductions in effect size and heterogeneity, the direction of effect remained consistent across analyses. Overall, these findings indicate that the results are robust for depression, stable but non-significant for QoL, and moderately sensitive for anxiety, with heterogeneity remaining high across outcomes.

Characteristics of Participants

The included studies involved a heterogeneous population of patients with heart failure, with sample sizes ranging from small pilot trials of fewer than 20 participants52,53 to large-scale trials including more than 1,500 participants.58 Most participants were diagnosed with chronic heart failure and classified according to the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classes I to IV, although the majority of studies focused on patients with moderate severity (NYHA II–III).60,61,68

The mean age of participants ranged from approximately 44 years63 to over 72 years,68 with most studies reporting mean ages above 60 years.49,67 Male participants constituted the majority in most studies, often exceeding 60% of the sample,47,59,66 although some studies reported more balanced gender distributions.41,47,52,59,66

Several studies specifically targeted subgroups of patients, such as those with reduced ejection fraction61,68 and those with comorbid conditions including diabetes58 or depressive symptoms.44 This diversity reflects real-world clinical populations but may also contribute to heterogeneity in intervention effects.

Characteristics of Intervention

The interventions examined across the included studies were predominantly technology-based and varied in type, complexity, and delivery. Telemonitoring and remote patient management were among the most frequently implemented approaches using phone call support,47,54,70 mobile app,41,42,52,53,61,62,64–66 and special device.43,46,59,68

The duration of interventions ranged from ≤6 months,41–43,45,52–54,59,61,62,65,66,70 > 6 months,46,47,64,68 to 12 months49,56,58,60 with varying intensity and levels of healthcare provider involvement. Most studies compared these interventions with usual care, which generally consisted of standard clinical management without additional digital support.

These outcomes were selected based on their strong clinical relevance, as heart failure not only affects patients’ physical health but also has profound psychological and emotional impacts. More details on the extraction of mean and standard deviation data for each outcome are provided in Table S3. The entire forest plot overall analysis can be seen in Figures S10–S12.

Subgroup Based on Delivery Model

Quality of Life

Subgroup analysis based on delivery model (phone call support, mobile app, and special device) was conducted to evaluate whether different telehealth approaches influenced QoL outcomes. For the phone call support subgroup, the pooled analysis showed a non-significant effect (SMD = 0.54; 95% CI: −1.92 to 3.00; p = 0.67), with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 99%). Similarly, the special device subgroup demonstrated a non-significant effect (SMD = 0.14; 95% CI: −0.12 to 0.40; p = 0.29), with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 98%), suggesting no significant improvement in QoL.

In contrast, the mobile app subgroup demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in QoL (SMD = −0.92; 95% CI: −1.65 to −0.20; p = 0.01), although heterogeneity remained very high (I2 = 95%), indicating considerable variation in effect sizes across studies. When all delivery models were considered together, the overall findings suggest that telehealth interventions did not consistently improve QoL.

Depression

Subgroup analysis based on delivery model (phone call support, mobile app, and special device) was conducted to evaluate whether different telehealth approaches influenced depression outcomes. For the phone call support subgroup, the pooled analysis showed a statistically significant effect (SMD = −1.28; 95% CI: −2.34 to −0.22; p = 0.02), with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 97%), indicating a significant reduction in depression despite substantial variability across studies.

In contrast, the mobile app subgroup demonstrated a non-significant effect (SMD = −0.14; 95% CI: −0.43 to 0.14; p = 0.33), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), suggesting consistent but non-significant results across studies. Similarly, the special device subgroup also showed a non-significant effect (SMD = −0.11; 95% CI: −0.77 to 0.54; p = 0.74), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 51%), indicating no meaningful improvement in depression outcomes. When all delivery models were considered together, the findings suggest that telehealth interventions did not consistently reduce depression.

Discussion

This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of telehealth on psychological outcomes, including depression, anxiety, and QoL, in patients with heart failure. Overall, the findings of this study indicate that telehealth interventions have varying effects across psychological domains, with the most consistent results found in anxiety reduction.48,54,61,70 Meanwhile, the effects on depression tend to be borderline,43,45,47,48,52,54,61,62,70 and no significant effect was found on QoL.41–43,46,47,52–54,59,61,62,64–66,68,70 These findings indicate that telehealth has potential as a psychosocial intervention, but its effectiveness depends heavily on the type of outcome measured and the characteristics of the intervention used.

Comparative Analysis with Previous Studies

Compared with previous meta-analyses, this study demonstrates clear differences in the number of included studies, sample sizes, and the strength and consistency of the observed effects. The previous meta-analysis included 11 RCTs with a total of 2,032 participants and reported that telenursing interventions significantly improved QoL with a large effect size (SMD = 1.05; p = 0.03), although with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 98%).35 In addition, subgroup analysis in the study showed consistently significant results across various quality-of-life measurement instruments, such as the MLHFQ (I2 = 99%) and KCCQ (I2 = 71%), indicating that although the effect is strong, the variation between studies is substantial.

In contrast, this meta-analysis included a larger number of studies, including 17 RCTs with a total of 4,796 participants, compared to the previous meta-analysis. Furthermore, the scope of outcomes in this study was also broader and more specific, not only focusing on QoL, but also encompassing three main psychological domains: QoL, depression, and anxiety. In terms of results, the main difference is seen in the strength of the effect and statistical significance. Regarding QoL outcomes, this meta-analysis did not find a significant effect (p = 0.25; I2 = 98%), in contrast to the previous study, which showed a significant effect (p = 0.03; I2 = 98%).35

These findings suggest that when the scope of interventions is expanded from telenursing to the entire telehealth spectrum (including telemonitoring, mHealth, and a combination of digital interventions), the effects on QoL become more heterogeneous and less consistent. Telehealth is a broad concept encompassing all remote healthcare services delivered through information and communication technologies across multiple health professions.27–29 In contrast, telenursing is a specific subset of telehealth that focuses on nurse-led remote care, education, monitoring, and coordination.30,31 Furthermore, the main strength of this study is the subgroup analysis, which has not been widely explored in previous studies.35 This analysis showed that intervention characteristics significantly influenced telehealth effectiveness.

Impact of Telehealth on Anxiety, Depression, and Quality of Life

The effects of telehealth on psychological outcomes among patients with heart failure vary across domains, with the most consistent effect in anxiety, as demonstrated in this meta-analysis. The significant anxiety reduction (SMD = −0.91; p = 0.009; I2 = 94%) for telehealth effects indicated a moderate to large effect size despite high heterogeneity. Telehealth addresses anxiety in HF patients by providing continuous, proactive, and accessible care that reduces the uncertainty associated with chronic disease management.48,49,71 In the context of heart failure, anxiety is often triggered by the uncertainty of the disease condition, such as the unpredictable nature of symptom exacerbations, fear of sudden death, and the complexity of treatment regimens.72,73 Therefore, the presence of telehealth can increase patients’ sense of security and self-control.74,75

In contrast, the effect of telehealth on depression was weaker and not statistically significant (SMD = −0.65; 95% CI: −1.35 to 0.05; p = 0.07; I2 = 96%), although there was a trend toward improvement. A p-value approaching significance indicates a potential effect, but it is not yet strong enough overall. Very high heterogeneity (I2 = 96%) indicates substantial variation across studies, both in intervention design and in population characteristics. Furthermore, depression is a more complex and chronic condition than anxiety, so telehealth interventions, which typically focus on education and monitoring, may not be sufficient to achieve a significant impact without a more intensive psychological approach.

Another possible explanation for this differential effectiveness across the psychological outcomes may be due to theoretical perspectives.76 According to Bandura’s Self-Efficacy Theory, patients’ sense of control and self-confidence may be enhanced, and thus indirectly contribute to reduced psychological distress. From a nursing perspective, telehealth may be regarded as a form of person-centred care due to the continuous interaction, emotional support, and tailored interventions. These elements are particularly effective in addressing emotional responses such as anxiety.

On QoL outcomes, this meta-analysis showed no significant effect (SMD = −0.31; 95% CI: −0.83 to 0.22; p = 0.25; I2 = 98%), with very high heterogeneity. These results differ from several previous meta-analyses that reported significant effects on QoL. The insignificance of these results suggests that while telehealth may improve certain psychological aspects, such as anxiety, it is not sufficient to produce meaningful changes in multidimensional outcomes, such as quality of life. QoL in heart failure patients is influenced by various factors, including physical limitations, disease progression, and social factors, which cannot be fully modified through technology-based interventions.7,77–79

Interestingly, subgroup analyses in this study showed that telehealth effectiveness depended heavily on the intervention delivery model. For QoL outcomes, mobile app–based interventions demonstrated a significant effect (SMD = −0.92; p = 0.01), whereas phone call support and special device–based interventions did not show significant improvements (Table 5). In contrast, for depression outcomes, a significant effect was observed in the phone call support subgroup (SMD = −1.28; p = 0.02), while mobile app and special device interventions did not demonstrate significant effects (Table 5). Meanwhile, in depression, a significant effect was found in certain subgroups such as the use of the HADS instrument (p = 0.006),54,61,70 BDI (p = 0.010),45,47 and at intervention duration >6 months (p = 0.03).47,48 These findings suggest that although the overall effect of telehealth interventions may not always be significant, their effectiveness varies depending on the delivery model and targeted outcome, with mobile app interventions being more effective for improving QoL and phone call support showing greater benefits for reducing depression.

These subgroup findings should be considered preliminary and require further validation rather than being interpreted as definitive conclusions. This caution is warranted for several reasons: each subgroup included a limited number of studies, in some cases only two to four; the overall pooled effects for depression and QoL were non-significant; tests for subgroup differences were largely non-significant; and substantial heterogeneity remained within most subgroups. Therefore, the observed significant subgroup effects should be viewed as initial indications rather than evidence of broad telehealth effectiveness for depression or QoL, and they require confirmation in adequately powered randomized controlled trials.

Across all three outcomes, heterogeneity was very high (I2 = 94–98%); consequently, the random-effects model was applied throughout, and the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution and regarded as having limited certainty. To explore this heterogeneity, we conducted predefined subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses, which indicated that the variability was attributable in part to differences in delivery model, measurement instrument, and intervention duration rather than to the influence of individual studies.

Fundamental differences in the intensity, interactivity, and degree of personalisation of telehealth interventions may explain these subgroup findings. mHealth-based interventions demonstrated a significant effect on QoL, which may be attributed to their capacity to facilitate continuous engagement, real-time feedback, and patient-centred self-monitoring.41,42,52,53,61,62 Such features, including automated reminders, symptom tracking, and bidirectional communication with healthcare providers, are known to enhance patient activation and self-efficacy, both of which are critical determinants of improved HRQoL. In contrast, more passive telehealth modalities, such as conventional telemonitoring or one-way educational approaches, may lack sufficient responsiveness and engagement, thereby limiting their overall effectiveness.

In contrast, for depression outcomes, phone call–based interventions appeared to be more effective, which may be explained by the more direct, synchronous, and personalised nature of verbal communication.47,48,54,70 Phone call support allows for real-time emotional interaction, immediate feedback, and the development of therapeutic relationships between patients and healthcare providers.26 These elements are particularly important in addressing psychological conditions such as depression, where empathy, active listening, and tailored counselling play a crucial role. Unlike app-based or device-driven interventions, which may rely heavily on self-directed engagement, phone-based approaches provide a more humanised form of care, potentially increasing emotional support, adherence, and patient trust, thereby leading to better mental health outcomes.26

For depression outcomes, the observed significance in specific subgroups, particularly those using HADS and BDI, highlights the importance of measurement sensitivity and responsiveness to change, rather than merely instrument validity. BDI was more sensitive to change when measuring treatment outcome compared to the inventory of depressive symptoms.80 Previous studies have shown that the results from using the BDI are consistently positive, and the BDI is now known to correspond to more than 90% of clinical diagnoses for patients suffering from depression.81 Although instruments such as the PHQ are also widely used and well-validated for depression screening, they are often designed for case identification and severity classification, which may be less sensitive in detecting short-term changes following an intervention.82,83 Although HADS-D and PHQ-9 demonstrate comparable responsiveness to change, HADS may be more suitable in patients with chronic medical conditions such as heart failure, as it minimizes the influence of somatic symptoms that may overlap with physical illness.84 In contrast, PHQ-9 includes somatic items that may inflate depression scores and affect the interpretation of intervention effects. This variation may partially explain the heterogeneity observed across studies.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the presence of digital technology does not solely determine the effectiveness of telehealth interventions but is highly contingent upon intervention design characteristics, implementation intensity, duration, and the appropriateness of outcome measurement tools.26,85 The lack of statistical significance in the overall analysis should therefore be interpreted with caution, as it likely reflects substantial heterogeneity across studies rather than true ineffectiveness. When delivered through structured, theory-informed, and long-term intervention models, telehealth demonstrates considerable potential to improve psychological outcomes among patients with heart failure.

Implication for Practice

The findings of this meta-analysis have important implications for clinical practice, particularly in the management of psychological outcomes among patients with heart failure. Telehealth interventions demonstrated a significant effect in reducing anxiety (SMD = −0.91; p = 0.009), suggesting that telehealth can be effectively integrated as a supportive strategy for psychological stabilisation, especially in addressing acute emotional responses such as uncertainty and fear related to disease progression. Therefore, healthcare providers, particularly nurses and multidisciplinary teams, should consider incorporating telehealth into routine follow-up care to enhance continuous monitoring, communication, and emotional support.

However, the non-significant effects observed in depression (p = 0.07) and quality of life (p = 0.25) indicate that telehealth alone may not be sufficient to address more complex and chronic psychological conditions. This highlights the need for more structured, theory-based, and intensive telehealth interventions, such as integrating cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT), motivational interviewing, or psychosocial counselling into digital platforms. In addition, subgroup findings suggest that mHealth-based interventions and longer intervention duration (>6 months) may yield more meaningful benefits. Therefore, future clinical implementation should prioritise interactive, personalised, and long-term telehealth models to maximise effectiveness.

Strengths and Limitations of This Study

This study has several notable strengths. First, it included a relatively large number of randomised controlled trials (26 studies, of which 17 were included in the meta-analysis) and a substantial sample size (n = 4,796 participants), providing a more comprehensive and robust synthesis of the current evidence than previous reviews. Second, this study expanded the scope of analysis beyond quality of life by incorporating multiple psychological outcomes, including depression and anxiety, thereby offering a more holistic understanding of telehealth effectiveness in heart failure management. Third, the inclusion of subgroup analyses by intervention type, duration, and measurement tools is a key strength, as it provides deeper insights into factors influencing intervention effectiveness. Additionally, rigorous methodological approaches were applied, including quality appraisal using JBI and risk-of-bias assessment using RoB 2, along with a comprehensive evaluation of publication bias.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, a consistently high level of heterogeneity was observed across all outcomes (I2 ranging from 94% to 98%), indicating substantial variability in study design, intervention characteristics, and measurement methods, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the diversity of telehealth interventions (eg., telemonitoring, mHealth, hybrid models) may have contributed to inconsistencies in effect sizes. Third, the number of studies included for certain outcomes, particularly anxiety (n = 4), was relatively small, which may affect the stability of the pooled estimates. Fourth, variation in outcome measurement tools (eg., PHQ, HADS, BDI) may have introduced measurement bias and influenced the observed effects. Finally, the inability to blind participants in most telehealth interventions may have introduced performance bias, a common challenge in digital health research. Furthermore, the restriction to full-text articles published in English may have introduced language and publication bias and may have excluded relevant evidence reported in other languages; grey literature was not formally searched, although hand-searching of reference lists was undertaken to mitigate the risk of omitting eligible studies.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that telehealth interventions have differential effects across psychological outcomes in patients with HF. Telehealth significantly reduced anxiety, indicating its potential as a supportive intervention for managing anxiety symptoms in this population. However, its effects on depression and QoL remained limited and non-significant in the overall analyses, suggesting that these outcomes may require more intensive, structured, and long-term intervention approaches. These findings should nonetheless be interpreted with considerable caution, as very high heterogeneity was observed across all outcomes (I2 = 94–98%), substantially reducing the certainty of the pooled estimates and limiting the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn. Importantly, subgroup analyses indicated that telehealth effectiveness was highly dependent on intervention characteristics, including delivery model, duration, and measurement instrument, with mobile app–based interventions and longer intervention periods (>6 months) appearing to yield more favorable outcomes. However, these subgroup results should be regarded as preliminary. Overall, telehealth should be regarded as a complementary strategy rather than a standalone solution in the management of psychological outcomes among patients with HF. Future research should prioritize the development of standardized, theory-driven, and personalized telehealth interventions, the adoption of harmonized psychological assessment instruments, and the use of more uniform telehealth protocols to enhance comparability across trials and reduce the substantial heterogeneity that currently limits the strength of the evidence.
