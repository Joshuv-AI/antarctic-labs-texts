Continuous Intraoperative Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve Monitoring in Transcervical Inflatable Mediastinoscopic Esophagectomy
Authors: Luo Zhao, Zhijun Han, Yisheng Zhang, Zijia Liu, Jia He, Li Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTranscervical inflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy (TIME), with fewer incisions and without going through the thoracic cavity, may achieve the same results and lymph node dissection compared with Thoracoscopic assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (TAMIE). However, its small visual field increases recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) injury risk. Intraoperative nerve monitoring (IONM) reduces RLN paralysis in TAMIE, but few reports have used it in TIME. This paper introduces continuous IONM in TIME to protect RLN. A patient underwent TIME with continuous left RLN monitoring using an EMG endotracheal tube and APS electrode. Stimulation frequency was 1 Hz, with the alarm set at ≥ 50% EMG amplitude reduction or latency prolongation > 10%. The signals are within the normal range during the mediastinal surgery and neck anastomosis. The patient had a normal voice and symmetric vocal cord movement postoperatively. Continuous IONM in TIME may help identify and protect RLN, enabling more aggressive lymph node dissection, though it needs anesthesia cooperation to avoid muscle relaxants. It may be the best method for RLN protection in TIME in the future.Keywords: esophagectomy, inflatable mediastinoscopy, intraoperative recurrent laryngeal nerve monitoring

Thoracoscopic assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (TAMIE) combined with systematic lymph node dissection has become the standard surgical procedure for the treatment of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Dissection of the bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) lymph nodes may lead to RLN paralysis, resulting in complications such as hoarseness and pulmonary infection. Recently, transcervical inflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy (TIME), with fewer incisions and without going through the thoracic cavity, may achieve the same results as TAMIE and lymph node dissection. This may become the mainstream surgical approach in the future. However, due to small visual field exposure under TIME, the RLN, especially the left RLN, is prone to being pulled and touched by surgical instruments, increasing the probability of injury. Intraoperative nerve monitoring (IONM) has currently been applied in some medical centers and has been proven to reduce the incidence of RLN paralysis during TAMIE and increase the number of lymph nodes dissected [1]. Herein, we introduced the application continuous IONM of RLN in TIME to help us identify and protect the RLN.

Surgical Steps

A 7.0 or 8.0 mm single‐lumen reinforced endotracheal tube with integrated electrodes for monitoring laryngeal electromyography is used (NIM Contact Reinforced EMG Endotracheal tube, Medtronic, Jacksonville, FL, USA). During anesthesia induction, therefore, a short‐ to medium‐acting nondepolarizing muscle relaxant should be selected, with a 1× 95% effective dose (ED95) typically being sufficient for routine surgeries, although the dosage can be appropriately increased for procedures with a longer duration and greater scope. During the maintenance phase of anesthesia, additional doses are generally not required, or the muscle relaxant can be added only in small amounts if necessary, and the principle of minimum dose should be followed [2] The patient is placed in the supine position, and the operation starts with an oblique incision below the left neck.

Monitoring of Right RLN

The right RLN loops around the right subclavian artery and returns to the neck, with a relatively short course. Thus, intermittent IONM is recommended instead of continuous monitoring. First, we open the anterior cervical muscles to expose the right tracheoesophageal groove, as most RLNs travel upward along this groove. Therefore, a 3.0 mA current can be applied to initially locate the RLN before anatomical exposure. A crossover method can be used; that is, the initial mapping can be performed below the lower pole of the thyroid gland perpendicular to the trachea to identify the site with the maximum signal strength. Subsequently, mapping along this site in a direction parallel to the direction of the trachea is performed; thus, the RLN can be roughly mapped through two crossover probing paths near the tracheoesophageal groove [3]. After the initial localization, a 1.0 mA current is applied for precise localization and the right RLN lymphadenectomy will be performed under the protection of IOMN.

Monitoring of Left RLN

Subsequently, we dissected and freed the left vagus nerve for about 1 cm. Then, a 2 mm APS electrode (NIM‐Response 3.0 with the APS Electrode; Medtronic Inc., Jacksonville, FL, USA) is clipped onto this nerve. Next, the flange of the electrode is fixed to the carotid sheath with 3–0 absorbable sutures (Figure 1). The electromyography baseline for continuous intraoperative nerve monitoring (CIONM) is established at least 30 min after the last dose of neuromuscular blocking agent is administered. When the left vagus nerve is stimulated with a current of 1 mA, a minimum stimulated electromyogram of 100 μV is considered an acceptable baseline. The alarm threshold is set according to the manufacturer's recommendations as a 50% reduction in electromyogram amplitude and a latency prolongation of > 10%. Then, the anterior cervical muscles are opened, and the esophagus and the left RLN are freed. Under TIME, the esophagus is dissected caudally to the level of the inferior pulmonary vein, and the lymph nodes including station 2R, 2L, 4L, 7 and 8U are dissected (according to AJCC/UICC). The stimulation frequency is once per second throughout the freeing process. When the intraoperative electromyogram amplitude is reduced by 50% and the latency is prolonged by > 10%, there may be nerve injury, and the operation is immediately stopped to find the possible causes, such as excessive traction, nerve entrapment, etc. Then, the stomach is freed under laparoscopy, and the surrounding lymph nodes are dissected, a tubular stomach is created, and a jejunostomy is performed. The continuous monitoring baseline is re‐established, and then the tubular stomach is lifted to the neck for esophagogastric anastomosis. After the anastomosis is completed, the monitoring is terminated, and the monitoring electrodes are removed. The APS monitoring signals of the patient throughout the process are shown in Figure 2. The signals are within the normal range during the mediastinal surgery and neck anastomosis. The patient's voice was normal after the operation, and laryngoscopy showed symmetrical movement of the bilateral vocal cords. The patient was discharged on the 6th day after the operation. Discussion

More and more studies have shown that for squamous cell carcinoma, the incidence of lymph node metastasis in the bilateral RLN chain is relatively high, and extensive lymph node dissection is beneficial to its prognosis [4] However, RLN nerve injury caused by the dissection of lymph nodes beside the RLN not only leads to hoarseness; weak cough after the operation will impair sputum excretion and increase the chance of pulmonary complications. A poorly protected airway is likely to lead to aspiration pneumonia. Bilateral RLN paralysis can even impair airway patency, and a tracheostomy may be required. In the long run, the quality of life will be worse. There is a balance between pursuing lymph node clearance by dissecting RLN lymph nodes and the safety of the operation. Technologies that improve the safety of RLN lymph node dissection will be very useful.

Application of TIME can perform esophageal cancer resection and mediastinal lymph node dissection through the neck and laparoscopy [5] Since this surgical approach does not require going through the thoracic cavity, it reduces the interference with the respiratory and circulatory systems and has been popularized in recent years. A recent meta‐analysis found that compared with traditional TAMIE, TIME has advantages in terms of intraoperative blood loss, anastomotic leakage, chylothorax, postoperative bleeding, arrhythmia, postoperative complications, and in‐hospital mortality. The operation time, 3‐day drainage volume, postoperative hospital stays, number of lymph node dissections, and the rate of pulmonary complications in the TIME group are lower than those in the TAMIE group. However, the incidence of RLN injury in TIME is significantly higher (63/379 vs. 45/380) [6] Therefore, the protection of the RLN is particularly important in TIME. IONM has been reported in several studies on esophageal cancer and is considered to reduce nerve injury and increase the number of lymph node dissections. However, most of these studies mainly use intermittent nerve monitoring during surgery, and the early warning of nerve injury is still insufficient. In our case, we reported the successful use of CIONM in TIME. The intraoperative nerve signals were good, and the patient had no hoarseness after the operation, and the laryngoscopy result was normal. The right RLN is less likely to be injured because its anatomical path is more constant and its length is shorter. Therefore, the main purpose of using CIONM is to assist in the dissection of the left RLN. Continuous monitoring allows the surgeon to promptly detect potential operations that may damage the nerve, making the lymph node dissection more aggressive. Therefore, this technology may become an important means of nerve protection.

Of course, this technology still has some disadvantages. The most important is the cooperation of anesthesia to avoid the use of muscle relaxant drugs. As we can see, when muscle relaxant drugs are used during abdominal surgery, the amplitude decreases significantly and only recovers slowly 30–40 min later. In addition, nerve monitoring also has problems such as false positives and false negatives. However, this technology is still the best method to solve nerve injury in TIME surgery in the future.
