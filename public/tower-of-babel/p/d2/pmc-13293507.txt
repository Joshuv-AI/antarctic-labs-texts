Access to mental health and psychosocial support among migrants in transit in Colombia
Authors: M. Claire Greene, Diany Castellar, Yazmin Cadena Camargo, Nasim Sabounchi, Davíd Chavez, Andrea Armijos, Matina Kakalis, Carlos Gomez Restrepo, S. Patrick Kachur, José Miguel Uribe-Restrepo, Marcela Venegas, Camilo Ramirez, Annie G. Bonz, Mike Wessells, Milton L. Wainberg, Terry T.K. Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extended periods of transit have become a defining feature of migration, driven by restrictive externalization policies globally and under-resourced health and social protection systems in transit countries. Migrants in transit—individuals actively migrating between their place of origin and intended destination—are disproportionately exposed to violence, exploitation, and other adversities that increase their risk for mental health problems. Migrants in transit remain underserved by systems ill-equipped to support mobile populations. To investigate the factors influencing access to mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) among migrants in transit, this study used a community-participatory system dynamics approach in Colombia, a key transit country in the Americas. We facilitated 10 group model building workshops in migrant shelters situated along key migration routes to co-develop causal loop and stock-and-flow diagrams illustrating the dynamic processes shaping access to MHPSS. Results revealed three critical delays in the care continuum: barriers to MHPSS awareness, seeking help, and initiating MHPSS. These delays are driven by structural barriers such as legal status, mobility, discrimination, and costs, but may be mitigated through community outreach, leveraging social networks to disseminate information and provide direct support, increasing access to information, providing basic needs and services along migration routes, and improving inter-agency coordination. Findings underscore the urgent need for flexible, low-threshold MHPSS delivery models responsive to migrants’ mobility and realities. This study identifies actionable leverage points for multisectoral policy and program innovation to reduce inequities in access to MHPSS for migrants in transit, a growing but underserved population in Latin America and globally.

Introduction

Migration patterns in Latin America have experienced significant shifts in recent years, with individuals and families experiencing prolonged periods of transit (Herrera and Gómez, 2022; International Organization for Migration, 2024). The increased use of externalization policies by host countries, which prevent migrants from reaching their intended destinations, combined with the inability of health and social protection systems in transit countries to meet migrants' needs, contributes to prolonged migration journeys (Herrera and Gómez, 2022; Santi, 2022; Stock et al., 2019). Extended migration journeys are often characterized by uncertainty and exposure to adverse or potentially traumatic events, posing considerable risks to migrants’ health and wellbeing (UNICEF, 2023).

Migrants in transit - individuals actively migrating between their place of origin and destination - represent a large and understudied population with distinct health, protection, and social service needs (Infante et al., 2012; Schapendonk, 2012; Vukčević Marković et al., 2023). Despite their increasing visibility across global migration corridors, this population often falls between the cracks of humanitarian and health systems that are not designed to serve people on the move. Their marginalization poses both a pressing human rights concern and a public health challenge that transcends borders. Countries along the migration journey—including origin, transit, and destination states—rarely coordinate to safeguard the health and dignity of migrants in transit.

Colombia remains one of the primary countries of transit and destination for migrants within the region, particularly for those fleeing the economic and political crisis in Venezuela (Bitar, 2022). In 2025, an estimated 685,000 Venezuelan migrants are expected to transit through Colombia in addition to the 2.9 million who are residing in Colombia and the 1.9 million pendular migrants (i.e., people who move regularly or cyclically between two or more places) (GIFMM, 2025). Colombia is also becoming an increasingly common transit country for migrants from other countries of origin, including Ecuador, Haiti, China, and India, among others (Pan American Health Organization, 2025).

Against this backdrop, exposure to migration-related stressors such as violence, family separation, economic instability, human rights violations, and limited access to basic needs and services contributes to the risk of mental health problems (Miller and Rasmussen, 2017; Vukčević Marković et al., 2023). Migrants in transit are particularly vulnerable to these risks (Correa-Salazar et al., 2023; Infante et al., 2012; Schapendonk, 2012). Yet, due to their high levels of mobility, by definition, conducting research or providing services to understand and address their mental health needs is a challenge. Although limited, available research does indicate a high burden of mental health problems among migrants in Latin America, including Colombia, yet fewer studies have focused specifically on migrants in transit (Alarcon et al., 2022; Bojorquez-Chapela et al., 2024; Naranjo et al., 2023; Rocha-Jiménez et al., 2025).

Despite the evident need for mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) services, addressing the mental health needs of migrants in transit remains a formidable challenge (Jaramillo Contreras et al., 2024). Mobility, time constraints, competing survival priorities, and the nature and duration of available interventions all present barriers to engagement (Dickson and Bangpan, 2018; Infante et al., 2022; Walter et al., 2018). For example, many of the evidence-based, scalable MHPSS interventions implemented by humanitarian organizations require five to twelve weekly sessions (Bryant, 2023), which are unrealistic in highly mobile populations, such as migrants in transit. Among migrants in Latin America, those who have recently arrived in a locality with the intention to continue their migration journey are among the least likely to receive MHPSS and other humanitarian services, highlighting a critical gap in access to care (Greene, 2023; R4V, 2024). This gap represents a source of inequity, as those who may need the support the most are often the least likely to access it.

Moreover, multiple structural and systemic delays and barriers further complicate efforts to deliver timely and effective MHPSS among migrants in transit. Common barriers to accessing health and MHPSS for migrants in transit include direct and indirect costs, lack of health insurance, discrimination, limited system capacity and availability, and long wait times (GIFMM, 2023; Hussein and Nye, 2024; Infante et al., 2022). Migrants in transit often lack legal status that permits them to access mental health services through the national system of the country through which they are transiting (Bitar, 2022; Hussein and Nye, 2024; Jaramillo Contreras et al., 2024). In 2021, the Colombian government implemented policies offering temporary protected status to Venezuelan migrants who meet certain criteria, which grants migrants legal recognition without refugee status (Poveda-Clavijo and Mena, 2024). Temporary protected status facilitates access to health services, including MHPSS, offered through the national health system. However, migrants in transit, most of whom do not qualify or have not undergone the regularization process within Colombia, continue to face significant challenges in accessing services (GIFMM, 2023). Importantly, even when legal status permits access, the national health system in Colombia faces capacity limitations including lack of coordination, high demand and staff workloads, and limited resources (Alarcón Garavito et al., 2023).

Given the unprecedented scale and persistence of global displacement, the growing number of migrants in transit represents not only a regional concern for Latin America, but a global imperative for action. This population's invisibility in data systems and program designs creates service gaps that compound existing vulnerabilities. As the number of migrants in transit rises, so too must our commitment to generate context-specific evidence and implement adaptive models of care to ensure no one is left behind. A deeper understanding of the multilevel and interrelated factors influencing engagement in MHPSS services within this population is essential to inform such policies and programs that are both responsive and adaptable to the realities of migrant populations. Using group model building (Hovmand, 2014), a community-participatory systems modeling approach, this study aimed to characterize the relationships among structural, social, and individual-level determinants that shape access to MHPSS among migrants in transit, offering insights into strategies for improving access to care and reducing health disparities in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study setting and participants

This study was conducted in four migrant shelters in Bogotá and Ipiales, Colombia. Bogotá, the capital of Colombia, is a common transit and destination city for migrants. Ipiales is a small town on the border with Ecuador that serves as a transit point for many migrants(Bitar, 2022). Participating migrant shelters were financed and operated by a range of organizations including United Nations (UN) agencies, the Colombian Red Cross, and non-governmental or religious organizations. These short-stay shelters provide basic services (e.g., housing and food) at no cost for migrants for two to three days, on average (Bitar, 2022).

Procedures

We recruited adult (≥18 years) migrants staying in these four shelters and staff working within these shelters to participate in the study. The research coordinator introduced the study to migrants and staff in shelter common areas. Adult migrants and staff who were interested in participating were invited to attend a 1.5-h workshop held within a private room in the shelter. Interested individuals were given a study information sheet, which the research coordinator reviewed with them prior to obtaining written informed consent for their participation.

We conducted a series of 10 group model building workshops (7 with migrants, 3 with shelter staff) between November 2023 and March 2024. The objective of the group model building workshops was to identify factors that influence access to MHPSS for migrants in transit and to characterize the relationships among these factors. Group model building is a participatory approach that allows stakeholders to visualize complex problems, identify interconnections across multiple levels, and develop feedback loops that inform intervention strategies (Hovmand, 2014; Trani et al., 2016). This approach supports the development of a shared mental model among participants regarding the underlying explanatory structures and interdependencies that give rise to the problem in question.

Due to time constraints within shelter environments, each of the group model building workshops with migrants was divided into two parts that were carried out across two sessions with different groups of participants. We combined these two parts into single-session workshops for shelter staff. In the first workshop with migrants, participants identified key variables affecting access to MHPSS and began mapping the hypothesized causal relationships among them. In the second session, the initial diagram was refined to capture feedback loops. First, participants characterized the polarity of each relationship between variables. Positive polarity, indicated by ‘+,’ represents a direct relationship between the variables (i.e., an increase in one variable will lead to an increase in the other variable). Conversely, negative polarity, indicated by ‘−,’ represents an inverse relationship between the variables (i.e., an increase in one variable will lead to a decrease in the other variable). Participants then identified and described the processes represented by the feedback loops. Each feedback loop was defined as either reinforcing or balancing in nature. Reinforcing loops represent self-amplifying processes, where a change in one variable leads to a chain reaction that ultimately intensifies the change over time. Balancing loops represent stabilizing forces that counteract changes in the system, preventing uncontrolled escalation or amplification. At the end of this process, we conducted a group model building workshop with migrants to validate the final model (Fig. 1). Each workshop was facilitated by a study investigator with a dedicated notetaker documenting discussions.

All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards at Columbia University Irving Medical Center (AAAU3219) and Pontificia Universidad Javeriana (Number 15/2023). Formal memoranda of understanding (MOUs) were established with participating migrant shelters to ensure ethical engagement and safeguard participant rights. All participants provided informed consent before participation and all data were collected anonymously.

Analysis

Data from the workshops were analyzed according to community-based system dynamics principles and methods (Hovmand, 2014). This approach is designed to support theory building and identify plausible leverage points to most efficiently and effectively influence elements within the system through a consensus-based approach (Cruden et al., 2024). This process was iterative, collaborative, and involved real-time analysis during workshops as well as asynchronous refinement and synthesis between workshops by study investigators. During the workshops, participants discussed and debated the variables and relationships among those variables which resulted in a preliminary causal loop diagram at the end of each workshop cycle (parts 1 and 2).

After each cycle, we consolidated the individual causal loop diagrams with those developed in previous workshops to produce a single, combined causal loop diagram while retaining the original diagram from each workshop for validation and comparison. Initially, all variables and relationships were included in the combined causal loop diagram. Discrepant relationships identified during model construction were revisited in subsequent workshops using alternative diagram formulations. Facilitated discussion focused on the conditions under which each relationship might apply until consensus was achieved regarding the most appropriate and accurate representation. When consensus was not achieved, relationships were disaggregated, replaced, or intermediary variables were added to reflect multiple pathways rather than forcing agreement.

Variables that were retained in the final causal loop diagram were those that emerged as salient during the consensus-building discussions within the workshops. Salient variables and relationships were those that participants across workshops consistently identified as central mechanisms that shaped MHPSS access. Each of these variables was mapped onto the levels of Bronfenbrenner's ecological model that describes how individual factors interact with five environmental systems: the microsystem, the mesosystem, the exosystem, the macrosystem, and the chronosystem (Bronfenbrenner, 1976; Bronfenbrenner et al., 2009).

We reviewed the notes taken during workshops to ensure that the final causal loop diagram accurately reflected the discussions within workshops. Through this process, we identified that access was described in the workshops as a multidimensional process composed of several interrelated components and stages, namely learning of MHPSS, seeking help for MHPSS needs, receiving MHPSS, and discontinuing MHPSS. To account for this, we replaced the singular variable of ‘access’ in the causal loop diagram with a series of stocks and flows that better depicted the dynamic nature of MHPSS access for migrants in transit. The final diagram was presented in a final model validation workshop with migrants to ensure it accurately represented the salient variables and processes influencing access to MHPSS. Subject-matter and technical experts then reviewed the final stock and flow diagram. The stock and-flow diagram was programmed using Vensim software and validated in a final workshop with migrants (Ventana Systems, 2010).

Results

Sixty-eight migrants and seventeen shelter staff participated in group model building sessions (Table 1). Migrants were, on average, 31 years of age (SD = 9.0) and most were female (57.4%) and heads of household (83.8%). Nine percent reported living with a disability and three percent identified as Afro-descendant or Indigenous. All migrants who participated in the study were Venezuelan and most did not have a regularized legal status in Colombia (97.1%). In contrast, all shelter staff members were Colombian and most were female (88.2%). None of the staff members reported having a disability or identified as Afro-descendant or Indigenous. Less than half wereheads of their household (41.2%).

The flows between states of MHPSS awareness, help-seeking, service engagement, and emigration are influenced by a set of 21 multilevel determinants that explain delays in accessing MHPSS (Bronfenbrenner, 2005). Many determinants operated at the individual level, including knowledge about mental health and help-seeking, knowledge of migratory routes and associated risks, worry and migratory stress, coping skills, financial resources, time available to attend to needs, and overall mental health and psychosocial wellbeing (Table 2). Other determinants related to migrants' immediate relationships and environments (microsystem), such as family functioning, levels of social support, and the ability to meet basic needs amid competing priorities, as well as to interactions between these settings (mesosystem), including the availability of information and accessibility of technology. Determinants characterizing the humanitarian response (exosystem) included the number of humanitarian actors, the strength of the MHPSS response (in terms of accessibility, acceptability, availability, and quality), out-of-pocket costs for MHPSS services, inter-agency coordination, and pressure on social and public service systems. Finally, at the macrosystem level, broader societal norms, policies, laws, and structural conditions shaped migrants’ access to MHPSS, including stigma related to migration and mental health, the prevalence of violence, experiences of xenophobia and discrimination, and difficulties obtaining regularized legal status. These determinants can either facilitate or obstruct access to MHPSS and often interact within reinforcing (i.e., virtuous or vicious) and balancing (i.e., stabilizing) feedback loops. These flows and the feedback loops that influence rates of transition can be characterized into three primary delays that prevent migrants from accessing MHPSS services within a given setting. Table 3 provides an overview of these loops.

Delay 1. learning about MHPSS

Learning about MHPSS was largely related to the availability of timely and trusted information about MHPSS services. Greater knowledge about MHPSS and pathways to support enhances interest, help-seeking behaviors, and service initiation. Individuals who engage with MHPSS and develop coping skills, including the ability to mobilize social support networks, are more likely to share information about available services within their communities, thereby diffusing MHPSS-related information throughout their social networks. This process is self-perpetuating (i.e., a reinforcing feedback loop), where increased awareness about MHPSS fosters greater utilization of services, which in turn generates broader knowledge dissemination. Additionally, the availability of information through humanitarian actors and technology play crucial roles in increasing MHPSS awareness, further reinforcing help-seeking behaviors and service engagement among affected populations. Determinants of learning about MHPSS were primarily at the individual level (e.g., knowledge about mental health, coping skills, financial resources) as well as he microsystem and mesosystem levels (e. g., availability of information, social support, accessibility of technology).

Delay 2. help seeking

Delays in help-seeking were strongly related to the level of migration-related stressors and the competing demands and priorities that migrants face. As migrants acquire knowledge about migratory routes and associated risks, they become better equipped to anticipate challenges and navigate stressors, which can enhance their ability to address basic needs and competing priorities, thereby alleviating migratory stress. However, heightened worry and stress related to migration may also act as barriers to MHPSS help-seeking, as immediate survival concerns take precedence over mental health needs. When migrants do engage with MHPSS services, these interventions can strengthen coping skills, enhance social support networks, and improve access to critical information about migratory risks and resources, ultimately facilitating resilience and adaptive decision-making. The availability of accurate and timely information plays a crucial role in this feedback loop, empowering migrants to make informed choices about their journey while also influencing their willingness and capacity to seek psychosocial support. Similar to learning about MHPSS, the determinants of help-seeking were individual (e.g., knowledge about mental health, worry/migratory stress, coping skills, financial resources), microsystem (e.g., social support), and mesosystem characteristics (e.g., availability of information, accessibility of technology, and ability to address basic needs and competing priorities).

Delay 3. initiating MHPSS

Barriers to initiating MHPSS among those who had already sought support related primarily to determinants operating at the exosystem level (e.g., strength of the MHPSS response, number of humanitarian actors, inter-agency coordination, and out-of-pocket cost of MHPSS). The financial burden associated with out-of-pocket costs for MHPSS can serve as a significant barrier to initial engagement in care. Additionally, when MHPSS services require payment, ongoing utilization can diminish the affordability and overall strength of the MHPSS response, potentially limiting its long-term effectiveness and accessibility for displaced populations. However, when MHPSS services are accessible, acceptable, affordable, and well-integrated within humanitarian response systems, barriers such as distance to services or concerns about the appropriateness of services are reduced. A well-developed MHPSS response that enhances access to high-quality care can ultimately improve population-level mental health and psychosocial wellbeing, reinforcing the sustainability of the response by fostering greater community engagement and reducing long-term demand for MHPSS services.

Other determinants of access to MHPSS services

Three other processes (i.e., feedback loops) were central to activating the feedback loops that directly impact access to MHPSS. These processes include: 1) structural determinants, such as legal status and mobility, which was defined by individual, exosystem, and macrosystem determinants; 2) stigma, discrimination, and violence, defined by macrosystem determinants; and 3) socioeconomic resources, defined by individual and microsystem determinants. First, difficulties in obtaining regular migratory or legal status create significant barriers for migrants, limiting their ability to meet basic needs and often forcing them to relocate in search of better opportunities. This constant movement can reduce the time and resources available for addressing essential needs, including accessing MHPSS. Second, the compounded stigma associated with being a migrant and experiencing mental health challenges further heightens discrimination, isolation, and increases exposure to various forms of violence, including gender-based violence and community insecurity. These reinforcing cycles shape multiple factors that influence MHPSS access, including heightened worry and migratory stress, diminished social support, and limited opportunities for legal integration. Third, improved mental health and psychosocial wellbeing can serve as a protective process that strengthens family functioning, enhances social support networks, and increases financial and material stability, ultimately improving migrants’ ability to meet basic needs, navigate competing priorities, and mitigate migration-related stress.

Discussion

This study provides a systems-based understanding of the barriers and facilitators influencing access to MHPSS services among migrants in transit in Colombia. Through group model building workshops with migrants and shelter staff, we identified key factors shaping engagement with MHPSS and characterized their interrelationships using causal loop and stock-and-flow diagrams. Our findings reveal similarities to the barriers experienced in learning about, seeking, and receiving MHPSS and other forms of healthcare in humanitarian and low-resource contexts (Asgary et al., 2022; Lokot et al., 2022; Shah et al., 2020; Whitaker et al., 2021). These similarities underscore that delays in MHPSS access are not only individual-level challenges, but also reflections of broader systemic, social, and policy-level gaps, akin to maternal and child health, reproductive health, communicable disease, and non-communicable disease service failures in emergency and low-resource settings. In this analysis, the causal loops that determine the rate of advancing toward access to adequate MHPSS indicate promising entry points for intervention to improve service delivery for this highly mobile and hard-to-reach population.

First, our results emphasize the structural constraints that shape access to MHPSS including the lack of legal status and limited health and humanitarian system capacity, which drive high levels of mobility (Bitar, 2022). Migrants without regularized legal status face additional obstacles, including discrimination and exclusion from some social services and parts of the health systems, including mental health services. These findings align with prior research indicating that undocumented migrants and those with irregular migratory status experience significant health disparities due to social, legal, and institutional barriers to care (Infante et al., 2022). Moreover, competing survival priorities, such as securing food, shelter, and income-generating opportunities, limit the time and resources available for migrants to prioritize their mental health. Direct and indirect (e.g., transportation, time away from income-generating activities) costs associated with MHPSS can deter help-seeking and contribute to discontinuation, highlighting the economic precarity of transit migration (Hung et al., 2024), and emphasizing the need for financial and logistical support mechanisms to facilitate engagement with MHPSS (Woodward et al., 2023).

While structural barriers impede access, individual- and community-level factors can serve as enablers of MHPSS access and utilization. Knowledge about MHPSS, particularly when disseminated through social networks and when phrased in terminology that resonates with migrants’ lived experience, can promote engagement. However, limited access to information and misinformation about mental health can act as barriers to help-seeking. This aligns with prior research demonstrating that social networks play a critical role in shaping access to information and help-seeking behaviors among migrant populations (Díaz de León, 2022; Romero et al., 2024). Expanded community-based outreach and peer-based initiatives could strengthen knowledge about MHPSS and, thus, help-seeking over time (Alford and Otake, 2021; Dickson and Bangpan, 2018; Troup et al., 2021).

We also identified a bidirectional relationship between engagement with MHPSS and psychosocial wellbeing. Acquiring coping skills and reducing psychological distress further promote awareness of MHPSS and help-seeking for individuals and their social ties. However, when access to MHPSS is constrained, distress accumulates, reinforcing vicious cycles of untreated mental health problems that hinder long-term stability and integration. Strengthening early intervention strategies may be essential to breaking these negative cycles and fostering positive mental health trajectories among migrants in transit. Even among those who do access MHPSS services, the strength of those services – particularly the availability, quality, affordability, and accessibility – is essential. Long wait times, inadequate provider capacity, and a mismatch between services and migrant realities hinder engagement.

Traditional MHPSS interventions often require multiple sessions over several weeks, which is incompatible with the high mobility of migrants in transit. This highlights the need for flexible, low-threshold service delivery models such as single-session interventions (Schleider et al., 2025), self-help interventions (Augustinavicius et al., 2023; Greene et al., 2024a; Karyotaki et al., 2023; Tol et al., 2020), remote-delivered and digital MHPSS interventions (Carswell et al., 2018; Greene et al., 2024b; Harper Shehadeh et al., 2019; Sit et al., 2021), and community-based, peer-led models of support (Bolton et al., 2023; Chow et al., 2025). Evidence for these low-threshold interventions is still emerging in humanitarian settings and none have been evaluated among migrants in transit. Further research on the feasibility and effectiveness of these interventions among migrants in transit is needed. Recognizing that MHPSS needs vary across individuals and these low-threshold services may only be effective for a subset of migrants in transit with lower-severity MHPSS needs, the establishment of stepped care interventions may improve efficient resource allocation and ensure that individuals receive a level of care appropriate for their needs (Purgato et al., 2025). In addition, better inter-agency coordination can play a critical role in mitigating systemic and service delivery barriers (Romero et al., 2024). Strengthening collaboration between government agencies, humanitarian organizations, and local communities could enhance comprehensive and coordinated service delivery in transit and destination countries (such as Colombia) by ensuring that MHPSS programs are adaptable, responsive, and available along key migration routes.

Finally, the model revealed several opportunities for policy and program-level intervention. First, increasing access to information through coordination among humanitarian actors and diffusion via social networks may serve as an effective strategy to improve awareness of and help-seeking for MHPSS services. Second, inter-agency coordination is expected to strengthen MHPSS access by increasing access to reliable information, improving migrants’ ability to meet their basic needs, and strengthening the overall accessibility, acceptability, availability, and quality of MHPSS services. Third, reducing the indirect (e.g., cost of transportation, lost income due to missed days of work) and direct out-of-pocket costs to receiving MHPSS services may reduce barriers to initiating MHPSS and increase continued engagement in MHPSS services. Further research testing the effectiveness of low-threshold interventions (e.g., single-session or brief interventions, self-help interventions) and innovative models of implementation (e.g., digital and remote delivery, peer-led) is needed. Introducing low-threshold interventions as entry points into a stepped care system, ideally situated within national and humanitarian health systems, can ensure that migrants with varying levels of needs have better access to appropriate and adequate care. To achieve this, partnership with government and humanitarian actors, coordination with national and humanitarian systems, and building capacity of mental health and humanitarian providers are essential.

Strengths and limitations

The findings from this study are based on qualitative data from a limited number of shelters, which may not fully capture the diversity of migrant experiences across transit routes and contexts within and outside of Colombia and may limit the model's transferability. This study reached migrants who were staying in shelters which may have excluded the most vulnerable migrants in transit, potentially underestimating barriers faced by those navigating extreme instability. The consistency and convergence of perspectives across participants and workshops support the face validity of the resulting diagram within this study population and context.

Second, we utilized participatory methodologies to identify and characterize the determinants and processes that influence access to MHPSS through a collaborative approach with study participants (Vaughn and Jacquez, 2020). However, the high levels of mobility among migrants in transit, by definition, made it difficult to build rapport and relationships with individual migrants over time. To compensate for this limitation, we incorporated several other elements of participatory research that did not rely on continuity of individual participants across sessions. These components included starting each session with rapport-building activities, presenting findings from previous workshops to obtain feedback and validate the research team's interpretations, and structuring workshop activities to ensure that decision-making power was held primarily by study participants.

Finally, while the group model building provided rich insights into system dynamics, it was not intended to estimate population parameters or accurately quantify trends. Further studies should incorporate quantitative modeling to calibrate and validate these study findings while also exploring and simulating opportunities for intervention. Despite these limitations, to our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the multilevel barriers and facilitators of access to MHPSS among migrants in transit. These findings provide a critical foundation for future programs and policies to design and adapt MHPSS delivery to meet the needs of the growing population of migrants in transit globally.

Conclusion

Migrants in transit face significant structural, social, and psychological barriers to accessing MHPSS, perpetuating inequities in mental health care. Our systems science approach illustrates how virtuous and vicious cycles shape help-seeking behaviors, service engagement, and mental health outcomes, emphasizing the need for multilevel interventions that address both structural constraints and individual enablers. Addressing these challenges requires policy innovation, service adaptation, and strengthened inter-agency collaboration to ensure that MHPSS services are responsive to the realities of migrants in transit. By implementing flexible mental health interventions, humanitarian and public health systems can better support one of the most vulnerable, underserved, and rapidly growing migrant populations in Latin America and globally.
