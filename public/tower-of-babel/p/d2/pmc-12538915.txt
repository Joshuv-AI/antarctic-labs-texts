Improving quality in childcare facilities, the “Accompagne-moi…!” approach: a qualitative study
Authors: Charlotte Decroix, Anaïs Essilini, Sylvana Côté, François Alla, “Accompagne-moi...!” group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProvided they are accessible and of high quality, childcare centres may help in addressing social inequalities in health. The "Accompagne-moi…!” (AM) approach was developed to improve the quality of these centres in France. It aimed at improving professional practices to help children develop their social and emotional skills, and was evaluated in a pilot study which analysed its viability conditions in France.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted in 14 childcare centres of four deprived areas in France over the 2019–2022 period. Eight interviews were conducted with stakeholders who initiated the approach, followed by 33 semi-structured interviews with childcare professionals.ResultsThe childcare centres interviewed were characterised by involved professionals working in challenging conditions, which were exacerbated during the health crisis. AM had the potential to meet certain needs (e.g. professional training) and to produce positive effects (e.g. a less stressful environment). However, the challenges of deploying such an intervention in highly stressed environments resulted in some professionals reporting a negative experience, such as a feeling of failure or loneliness. The viability conditions of AM were therefore highlighted, such as the need for sufficient human resources, integration of the approach into childcare centre projects, collective ownership of the approach, and supportive management and organisation.ConclusionWhile it is essential to consider the emotions of the children in childcare centres, the results emphasise how the well-being of children must be considered alongside to the well-being of professionals. Although it needs to be adapted before being scaled up, the AM approach has the potential to contribute to high-quality early childhood education. Some structural determinants are essential to implement interventions such as AM without creating difficulties for professionals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24248-0.Keywords: Childcare centres, Quality, Early childhood, Social determinants of health, Education, Viable validity, Evaluation

Background

Social inequalities in health are a major issue for social justice worldwide [1]. France is no exception: the 2022 report on the health status of the French population demonstrated a social gradient in terms of life expectancy and determinants of health [2]. Tackling these inequalities involves considering all the social determinants of health, which are"the circumstances in which people grow, live, work, and age, and the systems put in place to deal with illness"[1]. Among the latter, the role of early childhood education is of paramount importance [3]. Formal childcare has the potential to help combat social health inequalities and may help to offset certain developmental differences observed in children [4, 5]. In this regard, high-quality early childhood education and care (ECEC) centres need to be affordable, including to families from the most disadvantaged socio-economic backgrounds [6–8]. The quality of these services offered will influence the positive, neutral or even detrimental effects of formal childcare on various aspects of early childhood development. [6, 8, 9].

In France, most children start mandatory pre-school at the age of three. Until then, ECEC and childminders are the two main forms of formal childcare for young children [10]. ECEC was the first choice for 87% of parents, whose children were mainly looked after in childcare centres in 2020. For 55% of them, this choice was motivated by the expected benefits for the child's development and socialisation [11]. ECEC represents the first and, for some children, the most important experience of group socialisation in the first 1,000 days of life. It is an ideal setting for children to express their social behaviour at an early age and to learn social skills with their peers. Young children often display so-called ‘disruptive’ behaviour, mainly because they have not yet acquired the necessary social skills to communicate their needs and frustrations. Toddlers often go through a period known as the ‘terrible twos’ [12–15], a difficult time for both parents and educators, as children have not yet acquired the self-control and social skills to interact with others in a socially acceptable way, such as expressing emotions, sharing and cooperating in a game [13, 16, 17]. Behaviour such as hitting, throwing tantrums and refusing to follow rules reach their peak between the ages of two to three. This type of immaturity is most evident in ECEC, where children have to interact with their peers and carers for several hours a day, and this behaviour is one of the main challenges of early childhood professionals [18–20]. A good-quality childcare environment is one in which interactions between children and adults are relaxed and stimulating, where the environment is predictable and conflicts are rare. The quality of childcare for young children is therefore a major issue for political decision-makers at both national and international levels [8, 21].

In France, the association Ensemble pour la Petite Enfance (EPE) has developed the "Accompagne-moi…!"(AM) approach to continue improving the quality of educational practices in ECEC. It is the French adaptation of two initiatives developed and evaluated in Quebec, Canada: the continuous improvement approach developed by the Association Québécoise des Centres de la Petite Enfance (AQCPE), and the Brindami initiative designed by the Centre de Psycho-Education du Québec (CPEQ), [22]. Merging these two into one and transposing it from one context to another may question its effectiveness. The AM approach incorporates Brindami into a process of continuous improvement of practices of childcare centre professionals. Unlike Quebec, where the education system is integrated (children aged 0 to 5 attend the same place), the French system is ‘separate’, i.e. formal childcare is available for children aged 0–3, followed by pre-school education [23]. Differences in organisation between France and Quebec (e.g. educator-child ratio, training of professionals, etc.) can affect the quality of ECEC and its effect on children's development [5, 8]. In France, ECEC is governed by the Code of Public Health and can be implemented by local authorities, private companies, associations or parents [10]. It involves a multidisciplinary team of professionals from various medical, social and educational backgrounds. Furthermore, initiatives such as AM are complex and entail several methodological challenges in their development and evaluation. This type of initiative involves several interacting components and the right context to produce the expected effects. It also focuses on a wide range of behaviours and requires expertise in those delivering the intervention [24–26].

Before considering the scaling up of AM, its viable validity within the French context needs to be assessed by conducting a pilot study on a limited number of ECEC centres which have implemented it [27]. Viable validity (or viability) concerns the incorporation of an intervention into the system in which it is to be implemented. The aim of this study is therefore to analyse the viability conditions of the AM approach in childcare centres in France.

Methods

We performed a qualitative study nested within a pilot study. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with: (i) the stakeholders who initiated the AM approach and (ii) the childcare centre professionals in which AM was deployed. The study follows the "Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research"(SRQR) reporting guidelines [see Additional file 1] [28].

Presenting the context of ECEC and the "Accompagne-moi…!"(AM) approach

In France, ECEC mostly caters for children between two and a half months and three years old. They range in size from 10 to 60 children. They may be run either publicly or privately. Public ECEC is managed by municipal authorities, while private ECEC can be part of a network of ECEC centres [10], which may be managed by a coordinator when in the same region. ECEC centres are composed of a management unit and an educational team. The management unit is responsible for the administrative side of operations, team management and the environment of the centre. The educational team is generally composed of at least one paediatric nurse, one early childhood educator and several childcare assistants. Days are organised around a pedagogical project by an early childhood educator in collaboration with the management team, who have a background in education. The paediatric nurses, who have medical training, are responsible for the health and hygiene of the children and the centres. The childcare assistants are involved in the activities and playtime, as well as care time such as meals and naps. They have a paramedical background [10, 29, 30].

AM represented a continuous improvement approach to the quality of education in ECEC centres. The AM approach consisted of five phases: gaining the support of management and educational teams, observation (self-observation and hetero-observation by the EPE team) and analysis of professional practices, designing an action plan to improve practices, implementing the action plan, and evaluating it. The participating ECEC centres received regular support sessions from the EPE team. The ‘Pilou-Filou’ tool was one of the activities proposed in the action plan. It aimed to support childcare professionals in helping children to develop their social skills at their own pace. It included: (i) a series of activities for children supervised by a trained professional, (ii) documents about the activities for parents, and (iii) suggestions for other activities for professionals.

Participants and setting

The ECEC centres were selected by EPE on the basis of various criteria, including social deprivation and the degree of motivation of the centres. The pilot study was initially scheduled for 2019–2020 in the Nord, Pas-de-Calais and Yvelines departments. Owing to the COVID-19 crisis in France, ECEC centres were closed from March 2020 to May 2020. The timeline and territories for deployment of AM were therefore adapted. The pilot study was implemented twice in the Nord, Pas-de-Calais and Yvelines departments. It was initiated in 2019–2020 and in 2020–2021, in 23 and 24 ECEC centres, respectively. During the second deployment in 2020–2021, the Gironde (4 ECEC centres) department was added, to include ECEC implementing the intervention as originally planned. The stakeholders were recruited among the people who initiated the approach and were identified by the team coordinating the approach.

Several childcare centres were included to form an initial sample among those implementing AM as part of the pilot study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: ECEC centre supported by various EPE representatives, centres with different statuses (municipal, network, association), different geographical areas (urban, rural - Nord, Pas-de-Calais, Yvelines, Gironde), with different team dynamics (well-established, recent teams) and different sizes (number of places and sections). For each centre, two to three professionals were recruited depending on their profile and the size of the centre. At least one professional from the management team and the educational team trained in the Pilou-Filou tool were interviewed. For the educational team, various profiles were sought: gender, experience and background (number of years of experience, years of service at the childcare centre, and professional diploma), role in the ECEC centre. Interviews were conducted with network coordinators when applicable. In two centres, interviews were also conducted with professionals who had not been trained in the tools. The inclusion criteria were as follows: experience, background, role in the ECEC centre, gender and interest in the approach. The inclusion criteria were based on their potential influence on the perceptions and experiences of the professionals. We drew on the literature [5, 8, 31, 32], the characteristics of the French system, and a discussion with the research team and the association behind ‘Accompagne-moi…!’ (EPE). Then, CD consulted all the management teams to obtain a description of the characteristics of the professionals in their childcare centres. The sample was selected by the research team, based on all the descriptions provided by the management teams and the targeted criteria.

Data collection

To conduct the semi-structured interviews, interview guides were designed by CD and FA [see Additional file 2]. For the stakeholders, the guide investigated how they felt about the origins of the project, its rationale and the adjustments made between the initiatives in Quebec and the AM approach in France. They were conducted in 2020 in person or by video conference, owing to the geographical distance for participants in Quebec and social distancing requirements related to COVID-19 for participants in France. For ECEC professionals, the guide explored: their experience of the AM approach, the context in which it was deployed, the perceived effects and the mechanisms responsible for producing these effects. Interviews were conducted from July to November 2021. They were all carried out in person on the professionals’ work premises.

All interviews were conducted in French by CD and were recorded with the participants'oral consent. Participation was voluntary and not compensated. Exploratory interviews were conducted with eight participants and lasted from 30 min to 1 h and 16 min. The sample is detailed in Table 1. Three interviews were held in person and five by video conference. Interviews with ECEC professionals were carried out in 14 ECEC centres in four French departments (Nord, Pas-de-Calais, Yvelines and Gironde). Two were run by two different local authorities, 10 were run by three different private networks, and one was run by an association (see Table 1).

Data analysis

The interviews were transcribed in full and anonymised. The analysis comprised two parts:

(i)Each interview was first analysed individually by either CD or AE, two specialists in public health trained in qualitative research. A thematic analysis [33] was then conducted by CD and AE. Each interview was coded with the analysis grid [see Additional file 3]. Each theme and subtheme was discussed and interpreted by both CD and AE.

Each interview was coded with the analysis grid [see Additional file 3]. Each theme and subtheme was discussed and interpreted by both CD and AE.

(ii)Using the integrative validity model developed in 2010 by Chen, CD then analysed the the viability conditions [26]. Viability concerns the degree to which stakeholders feel that an intervention fits into the system in which it is to be implemented. Viable validity refers to "stakeholders'views and experience regarding whether an intervention program is practical, affordable, suitable, evaluable and helpful in the real world". The ‘real-world’ refers to everyday practice in childcare centres. In this article, we considered four viability criteria, defined by Chen as follows: “practicality refers to whether implementers and staff in a community could adequately implement an intervention. Suitability refers to whether an intervention is capable of being coordinated or organized by local agencies and community-based organizations. Affordability refers to whether an implementing organization can pay for the cost of coordinating and implementing an intervention. (…) Helpfulness refers to whether stakeholders can notice and experience progress in alleviating or resolving a problem“ [34]. Results

ECEC: systems under pressure, exacerbated during the health crisis

A workplace with dedicated professionals working in challenging conditions

The interviews revealed a high level of dedication from professionals working in conditions described as difficult: salaries considered low, limited staff, considerable needs of training, and a high turnover.

“This approach [AM] is a means of recognizing professionals (...) their salaries are very low (...) there is no recognition of the value of early childhood professions” (stakeholder).

The statutory number of professionals per child, the number of professionals working on a daily basis and the policy of absence from work varied from one centre to another. Absence due to sick leave, maternity leave or non-replacement had repercussions for ECEC in both the short and long term: burnout, changes in staff roles and team dynamics. These consequences were exacerbated: (i) when the absences involved management staff and/or (ii) when the management team compensated for absences by no longer supporting the teams'projects or were overwhelmed by their responsibilities.

"There should have been two of us, and my coworker joined us in September, but she left at the end of September. Then, a new coworker arrived in March, but she didn't stay. So, in reality I worked the whole year alone."(early childhood educator).

The challenge of integrating new projects into an organisation under pressure

In some ECEC centres, human resource issues were cited as obstacles, both to implementing new initiatives and to working with children on a day-to-day basis. The challenge of taking professionals away from their work to join a team meeting, to reflect on their work or to carry out educational activities that require significant preparation was emphasised, for the AM approach as for other projects.

"I needed time, and there wasn't enough. Right now, once we leave the area, it impacts the whole group. There's barely enough of us” (Childcare assistant).

The interviews reflected the challenge of integrating projects such as AM in the centres. Two consequences were identified: (i) obstacles to the project were amplified by the slightest ‘grain of sand’ that messed up the working of an already stressed centre; (ii) professionals were put in a difficult position, which called into question the project's suitability and sustainability if the structural conditions were not met.

Challenges exacerbated by the health crisis

The interviews showed how the health crisis required considerable adjustments from professionals: the closing and reopening of ECEC centres, increased sick leave, need for special support for children, constant changes in working conditions as new measures were often introduced to prevent the spread of coronavirus (e.g. reorganisation of rooms, cleaning of toys, reducing parents’ access to the centre, isolation of contact cases). The health crisis highlighted the level of commitment of professionals, since the AM approach was implemented in spite of these circumstances. In some centres, the reactions of some professionals to the health crisis was a complicating factor, such as views on vaccination or the use of masks. While the Covid crisis exacerbated problems, the interviews revealed that they were pre-existent.

"We're starting to see a real shortage of professionals. When I started I had a hundred CVs on my desk. Now I'm having to hunt candidates down. There's a real shortage (...) this profession, working with young children, is no longer attractive. [Professionals] do it because it is their calling; it's in their bones and so they give it their all. We ask a lot of them"(Coordinator).

Integrating “Accompagne-moi…!” (AM) into these systems: viability of the approach

The AM approach's potential in the French context: meeting needs

The interviews highlighted the relevance of an approach aimed at promoting children's and professionals'emotional well-being by improving the quality of the environment in ECEC centres. In the exploratory study, the development of AM in France was accounted for by the need for ECEC staff to be trained, supported and given tools to help them provide appropriate care for children in everyday, group situations, such as sharing, waiting for their turn and following instructions. These needs were confirmed by interviews with coordinators, managers and educational teams.

"[Behind the development of "Accompagne-moi…!", there was a need] to change how we do things. With the idea of kindness, kindness from early childhood educators toward children, from early childhood educators toward families, from families toward their children, and kindness within support structures as well as professionals"(Stakeholder).

Professionals stressed the need to pay more attention to children's emotions in certain situations:

"When I arrived in February...emotions didn't exist. Children weren't allowed to cry. It was difficult"(Childcare assistant).

The responsibility of early childhood educators in initiating and driving the ECEC’s educational project within the entire team was highlighted, making it all the more important to support them. Finally, the benefits of the approach were underscored by: (i) early childhood educators'recognition of greater potential for supporting children in their social and emotional development; (ii) the need to re-examine the educational purpose of words and habits that have become routine.

“For me, it's a great tool to help them [education team] to approach children from a child's point of view and not to be in a situation where you have expectations about how the child should be like and to say to yourself, well, each of them is unique, and finally try, but that's hard work, to see them more as individuals than as a group” (Early childhood educator).

Perceived positive effects: calling for the approach to be integrated as support in childcare centres

The analysis showed that AM could become a cornerstone for various ECEC projects. The interviews highlighted the perceived effects of AM in helping young children acquire the social skills they need in their daily life in ECEC centres (mealtimes, playtimes). According to the context, other effects were reported by the management teams (e.g. supporting the improvement of professional practices), the professionals (e.g. reflection about day-to-day support provided to children), the team dynamics (e.g. helping to adopt a shared vocabulary), and even the structure as a whole (e.g. calmer atmosphere in ECEC centres). These effects were reported as having facilitated other projects.

"Today, I see'Accompagne-moi...!'as a flower with petals, with the centre, the practices and all the projects that revolve around it, such as sign language, the layout of our space, and the Pilou - Filou tool to help the children with their psychosocial skills"(Early childhood educator).

However, in some ECEC centres, professionals did not report the same experience. They considered the continuous improvement approach to quality and the Pilou-Filou tool as merely ‘additional’ projects that were not integrated into their centre’s own projects.

Experiences that question the viability conditions of the approach

The interviews showed that some professionals had difficulties or even a negative experience when implementing AM. Individually, professionals'difficulties were associated with feelings of isolation and/or failure amongst those who were unable to implement AM. These situations were caused by a lack of time, questions on how to implement the Pilou-Filou tool, difficulties in mastering the approach and convincing their colleagues about it. Individual negative experiences sometimes led to animosity within teams. Stress and frustration stemmed from the impact of implementing Pilou-Filou on the ECEC’s organisation, the impossibility of training the entire team in Pilou-Filou, and the perceived lack of involvement of some colleagues in AM.

"I admit that there were times when I was a bit - I'm not going to say gutted, but yes, I did feel a bit isolated and lost, and that kept me awake for a few nights (laughs) "(Early childhood educator).

Viability of the approach: substance and feasibility

The perceived helpfulness of AM was influenced by its perceived affordability, suitability and practicality. The results of the viability study revealed the difference between the approach's content and feasibility. In most ECEC centres, AM met a need (Fig. 1) with perceived positive effects. Some early childhood educators who had not been trained in Pilou-Filou said they were not interested in it, mainly due to the changes it induced in their practice rather than to doubts about its rationale. In terms of feasibility, the interviews highlighted the organisational difficulties involved in incorporating AM into ECEC projects and helping professionals to take ownership of it, both individually and as a group. In some situations, interest in the content of AM was overshadowed by the management and teams'perception of its feasibility.

Results by criteria

Figure 1 summarises the findings according to Chen's viability criteria. Concerning helpfulness, the pilot study highlighted the existence of needs (e.g. to attribute more importance to children’s emotions, to support ECEC professionals, to improve ECEC working environments). AM had the potential to meet those needs, according to the positive perceived effects described in the interviews. However, its helpfulness was questioned, as revealed by some negative experiences of the approach at both individual and team levels. Affordability was conditional on the financial resources of each ECEC centre to recruit enough professionals and the possibility of financing it over time. Suitability was influenced by the ECE’s ability to appropriate and run the approach, the human resources available, and the issue of incorporating AM into each ECEC centre’s own project. Practicality was mainly challenging during the first part of the implementation of AM in ECEC centres, owing to the time needed to take ownership of the approach and the need to get all team members involved.

Discussion

The results showed conflicting perceptions between the fulfilment of ECEC needs and the negative experience of some professionals. Although the approach met a need in most situations, some obstacles were reported in deploying and implementing AM. The results underscore the need to associate the approach with an educational project in each ECEC centre. In addition, this study showed that to improve children's well-being in facilities also meant addressing the well-being of professionals.

Needs consistent with the historical context of the development of childcare in France

From the demographic challenge of child survival at the end of the Second World War to the present day, formal childcare policy has been underpinned by several objectives: balancing work and family life, promoting gender equality, nurturing children in all aspects of their development, and tackling the intergenerational transmission of poverty [7, 31, 35]. The paradigm shift from ‘childminding’ to ‘childcare’ centres in the 1980 s reflected the desire to consider psychological and social issues alongside health concerns [35, 36]. Reflecting on this historical context, today's educational teams are staffed by professionals with varied training and knowledge, including backgrounds in medical sciences, social sciences and/or educational sciences, managed by different ministries with varying lengths of study. ECEC centres are organised around three types of stakeholders: the educational team, the management team and the early childhood educators. The training and duties of early childhood educator make them the key players in initiating and promoting educational projects in ECEC centres [35].

This context highlights the need for training ECEC and supporting early childhood educator in their important responsibilities as seen in this pilot study. This is even more important when there is disagreement between the director, i.e. the head of the management team, and the early childhood educator, i.e. the head of the educational team [29].

From evidence-based initiatives in Quebec to "Accompagne-moi…!" in France: continuity and adaptation

The AM approach is based on two initiatives that were integrated into routine practice and evaluated in Quebec using a holistic approach [22]. AM combines a continuous improvement approach with educational quality and an intervention to encourage the development of young children's psychosocial skills while respecting their own pace. The results show how the perceived quality of the educational practices of professionals was influenced by structural factors beyond their individual knowledge, skills and experience. This issue can be seen in terms of structural quality and process quality. The former covers the most distal quality indicators for childcare centres, which are often subject to regulation. They concern the human, physical and material resources available [6, 8]. The latter "concerns the more proximal processes of children’s everyday experience and involves the social, emotional, physical and instructional aspects of their interactions with staff and other children (peer interactions) while being involved in play, activities or routines"[8].

The AM approach sought to influence both process quality (the aim of the approach) and structural quality by training professionals. The results highlighted the influence of perceived structural quality indicators on improving process quality, in accordance with the international literature [6, 8, 37]. To be viable, initiatives such as AM require several conditions to be met, such as sufficient human resources and a supportive management and organisation. For example, the management team and the early childhood educator must be able to lead AM within the facility to ensure collective ownership of the process. In the French context, the study emphasised the importance of working conditions and organisational types on the process quality of ECEC centres [38]. Furthermore, various literature reviews have demonstrated the positive relationship between professional training and process quality [6, 37]. The results also demonstrated the interdependence of the structural characteristics of quality inherent in the AM pilot study, which illustrated the importance of having a supportive organisation and sufficient human and financial resources to allow professionals to practice the approach and reflect on it.

Strengths and limitations

This pilot study tested AM in 14 ECEC centres. The qualitative study explored its viability conditions by interviewing the stakeholders who initiated the project and the professionals who deployed it. Trustworthiness of the results was improved by triangulating the participants and by having two investigators analyse the data (CD and AE) [39]. A viability study is essential before scaling up an intervention, and these results highlighted the potential of AM to meet a need in France.

However, there are some limitations. In each ECEC centre, the professionals from the educational team were selected by their management team. Despite the selection criteria for these interviews, the participants were probably more interested and involved in the AM approach. The study also took place during the COVID-19 health crisis, requiring to modify the pilot. The main adjustments were the continuation of the AM approach in 2020–2021 and the addition of five new ECEC over that period, and the remote implementation of some phases that were initially designed as face-to-face sessions. The research methodology was regularly adjusted as the health crisis unfolded. The situation did not allow regular observations in the ECEC centres or physical interviews with the parents as planned initially.

Some of the study's conclusions need to be taken with caution, given the bias caused by the health crisis. Nevertheless, the continuation of the project despite the circumstances testifies to the commitment of everyone involved. Furthermore, the participants'comments showed that the health crisis was not solely responsible for the obstacles to the implementation of AM. Rather, they demonstrate the systemic difficulties present in the early education sector. These issues were highlighted in the 2023 report by the Inspection Générale des Affaires Sociales (IGAS) on the quality of care and prevention of abuse in ECEC in France [30]. Despite recent trends, major problems persist, generating inequalities between ECEC centres. Our results highlight several of these problems: the need for training, low social esteem of early childhood professionals, challenges in hiring more staff, and the feeling of not being able to give children the time that they need. Therefore, "early childhood care is a sector that tends to be idealised: we don't acknowledge the hardships or the risks in the field, to the detriment of both children and professionals. In this respect, the task force [carried out by the IGAS] considers that it is essential to change how we look at these services, to put them in their rightful place, that of caring for people who are extremely vulnerable and dependent, and to appreciate what this type of activity entails in terms of working conditions, the time needed to provide support, offering training, preventing risks, and carrying out assessments and inspections" [30].

Following up from the pilot study: prospects for research

Despite the positive effects it obtained, the results demonstrated the need to adjust the initiative and/or the context to improve the AM approach's viability. The seven 1.5- to 3.5-h restitution sessions with 68 participants gave rise to several suggestions, including the following: (i) to suggest changes in the approach to maintain the perceived positive effects while strengthening the viability conditions; (ii) to offer operational solutions without standardising the approach so that it can be integrated in the various environments, in accordance with the paradigm of complex interventions. Both the findings of the pilot study and its limitations call for further research. First, the approach has to re-evaluated: (i) to assess whether the changes help to preserve the perceived positive effects while preventing negative experiences; (ii) to unravel the mechanisms underpinning its effectiveness and how they are triggered, including for the Pilou-Filou tool; (iii) to explore parents'perceptions. Second, the conditions for the sustainability of the observed results requires further investigation. The results of this study allowed us to put forward hypotheses about the conditions for sustaining the results over time, It depends in particular on the team's collective ownership of the approach, the management team's commitment to it and the type of ECEC centre it is implemented in (public, private or associative). Third, the issue of both children's and professionals'well-being calls for research that goes beyond the framework of the AM approach. Several questions arise: what does it mean concretely if an ECEC centre claims to promote the well-being of children and professionals? What are the conditions that promote well-being? How are these conditions accounted for in France? What are the levers and obstacles to integrating these conditions into the French system?

Conclusion

This study examined the viability conditions of a process designed to improve the educational quality of early childhood education in childcare centres in France. The findings highlight the value of such a approach in light of the urgent need to take children's emotions into consideration. They also demonstrate how the well-being of children cannot be considered separately from that of the professionals who care for them every day. The results showed that their working environment needs to be adapted to allow them to deliver high-quality services. Therefore, there are structural prerequisites for introducing a continuous improvement approach that improves the quality of processes within childcare centres. While the commitment of our participants to using high-quality educational practices despite their challenging working conditions was clearly demonstrated, their low social recognition is a matter of concern. To ensure that their commitment to the approach does not wane, the overall structural quality of their working environment must be ensured in terms of the number of professionals involved, access to ongoing training, etc.

Despite its significant potential, the viability conditions of AM were not met. The professionals involved in its evaluation noted its positive effects when the conditions for its implementation were satisfactory, and the pilot study enabled it to be adapted to address the obstacles encountered. Additional research is needed on the overarching environment of ECECand how it affects the well-being of both children and professionals.
