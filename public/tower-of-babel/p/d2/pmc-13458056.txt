Emergence of fibrotic pericytes and their transcriptional regulation in pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Wen Gao, Ying-Wei Lan, Zicheng Deng, Enhong Li, Anusha Acharya, Jonathan Do, Xiaomei Xia, Nicole Marquez, Danli Lu, Ali Al Siraj, Nicholas E. Banovich, Angara Sureshbabu, Ross Bremner, Vladimir V. Kalinichenko, Tanya V. Kalin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple cell types have been implicated in pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis with pericytes emerging as a new focus due to their role in promoting fibrotic remodeling. Fibrotic environment changes normal pericyte functions, but transcriptional programs regulating pericyte transition towards fibrotic state remain unclear. Utilizing single cell RNA-sequencing of human pulmonary fibrotic lungs and mouse genetic models, we identified a unique cluster of fibrosis-associated pericytes which was not present in normal lungs and exhibited a distinctive transcriptional signature indicating transition of normal pericytes to a fibrotic state. FOXF1 was identified as one of the transcription factors decreased in fibrotic pericytes. Pericyte-specific deletion of Foxf1 increased severity of pulmonary fibrosis in bleomycin mouse model as demonstrated by reduced survival, impaired lung functions, increased body weight loss and increased fibrotic remodeling. Pericyte-specific overexpression of Foxf1 attenuated pulmonary fibrosis, reversed fibrotic changes and improved survival outcomes. Based on single cell RNA-sequencing and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing, FOXF1 transcriptionally regulates an extensive pericyte signaling network critical for pulmonary fibrosis. In vitro, FOXF1 transcriptionally activated ID3 which inhibited fibroblast activation through decreased secretion of IL8 and CXCL1 by pericytes. Altogether, FOXF1 prevents transition of pericytes to a fibrotic state, suggesting new therapeutic opportunities for treatment of pulmonary fibrosis.

Introduction

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is characterized by the aberrant deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), persistent activation of myofibroblasts, and the disruption of normal alveolar architecture and function. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is the progressive form of PF and represents the most common type of interstitial lung disease (ILD). The pathogenesis of IPF is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of environmental exposures, aging, genetic predisposition, epigenetic modifications, immunologic dysregulation, and gerontologic mechanisms1. Multiple cell types are involved in IPF pathogeneses, including epithelial cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts and immune cells. Chronic injury to alveolar epithelial cells plays a central role in the pathogenesis of IPF, followed by an abnormal repair that includes dis-regulated activation of fibroblasts, endothelial cells and recruitment of immune cells2–4. In addition to the well-documented roles of alveolar epithelial cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts and immune cells in pulmonary fibrosis, emerging evidence highlights the importance of lung pericytes in regulating pulmonary fibrogenesis5,6.

Pericytes are the perivascular cells that share a basement membrane with endothelial cells and contribute to vascular integrity. During pulmonary fibrosis, pericytes detach from the endothelium, leading to vascular leakage and contributing to the progression of fibrosis7. Pericytes have been shown to undergo phenotypic changed in the fibrotic environment, acquiring myofibroblast-like properties with increased collagen production8,9. While pericyte to myofibroblast-like changed have been observed across various tissues10,11, the transcriptional regulators governing this process during lung fibrotic remodeling remain unclear. Given the multifaceted role of pericytes in mediating tissue repair and shaping fibrotic microenvironment, elucidating the mechanisms by which pericytes contribute to fibrosis is essential and holds great promise for uncovering novel therapeutic targets and improving outcomes for patients with pulmonary fibrosis.

Our study aims to elucidate the transcriptional mechanisms by which pericytes regulate pulmonary fibrosis. Through transcriptional profiling of lung pericytes from donors and patients with pulmonary fibrosis, we have identified a subcluster of fibrosis-associated pericytes that is not present in normal lungs. We have also identified FOXF1 as a transcriptional regulator involved in the transition of normal pericytes to fibrosis-associated pericytes that support lung fibrogenesis. FOXF1 (Forkhead box F1) is a transcription factor expressed in lung mesenchyme during lung development12–15. Mutations in FOXF1 gene locus cause alveolar capillary dysplasia with misalignment of pulmonary veins (ACDMPV), a lethal congenital disorder of neonates and infants with significant fibrotic component16–19. FOXF1 is known to play a role in embryonic and neonatal angiogenesis by regulating pro-angiogenic genes in endothelial cells20–22, and has been shown to inhibit lung cancer progression while promoting tumor vessel normalization23. During lung fibrosis, FOXF1 regulates the reprogramming of normal endothelial cells into fibrosis-associated endothelial cells3 and prevents expansion of myofibroblasts24. While FOXF1 is expressed in lung pericytes, its role in pericytes remains unknown.

In this study, we utilized single-cell RNA sequencing and mouse genetic models to demonstrate that the loss of FOXF1 in pericytes leads to upregulation of pro-fibrotic signaling pathways, including the TGF-β pathway, extracellular matrix organization and actin cytoskeleton rearrangement. FOXF1 loss in pericytes significantly exacerbates the severity of lung fibrosis. Conversely, FOXF1 overexpression in pericytes attenuates pulmonary fibrosis by reducing fibrotic remodeling. These findings underscore the critical role of FOXF1 in modulating lung fibrogenesis and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for pulmonary fibrosis.

Results

Transcriptome analysis identified a unique sub-cluster of fibrotic pericytes in the lungs of patients with Pulmonary Fibrosis

To characterize the role of lung pericytes in PF, we first compared the transcriptome of lung pericytes from donors and patients with pulmonary fibrosis (PF) by analyzing publicly available scRNA-seq datasets25. In these datasets, the samples from PF patients were stratified into two groups, including with “less fibrosis” and “more fibrosis” based on the differentially affected areas of one lung used to prepare sequencing libraries25. Mesenchymal cells from the dataset were re-clustered and annotated using cell-type-specific marker genes provided by LungMap26 (Fig.1A and Supplementary Fig. 1A). Human pericytes were identified based on the expression of CSPG4, HIGD1B, COX4I2, KCNK3, and LAMC3 and visualized by immunostaining in donor and fibrotic lungs (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. 1A–D). A unique pericyte sub-cluster (Peri2) was detected that was present exclusively in both groups of PF lungs but was absent in donor lungs (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1E–G). Peri2 pericyte sub-cluster is characterized by a reduced expression of pericyte-specific markers and an increased expression of fibrosis-associated markers, including COL1A1, COL3A1, and ACTA2, as well as the pericyte activation marker RGS5 (Fig.1C). In contrast, Peri1 pericyte cluster was present in both donors and PF patients (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1E–G). To further elucidate the differences between Peri1 and Peri2 pericyte clusters, we performed KEGG pathway analysis. Unlike Peri1 subcluster, which was enriched in pathways associated with cell adhesion and metabolic activity, Peri2 pericytes showed enrichment in fibrosis-associated pathways, including TGF-beta signaling, extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 1D). Pseudotime analysis predicted a transition from Peri1 to Peri2, with colors representing inferred pseudotime values from early-stage (dark blue) to late-stage (yellow) (Fig. 1E), suggesting that pericytes undergo a trans-differentiation process under fibrotic conditions, with cell fate shifting toward a fibrotic state. Co-localization studies demonstrated the existence of NG2+aSMA+ cells in the fibrotic lungs, supporting scRNAseq data (Fig. 1F). The lack of MYH11 staining indicated that these cells were not smooth muscle cells (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Ligand-receptor interaction analysis using the CellChat computational method27 shows the crosstalk between pericytes and other lung cell types, especially alveolar fibroblasts and endothelial cells (Fig. 2A). Based on this analysis, Peri1 govern a “homeostatic support function” by being the dominant source of Angiopoietin 1 (ANGPT1), a critical homeostatic growth factor that promotes lung vascular stability, reduces capillary leakage, and is anti-inflammatory during lung injury (Fig. 2B–D). In contrast, Peri2 govern a “pathogenic activation function”, transitioning toward a reactive, pro-fibrotic state evidenced by increased Fibronectin 1 (FN1), Thrombospondin (THBS), NOTCH and Collagen signaling network, which collectively promote myofibroblast differentiation, excessive collagen deposition and vessels remodeling (Fig. 2B-C). The existence of the unique fibrotic subcluster of pericytes was confirmed in the bleomycin-treated mouse lungs

To determine whether the unique subcluster of fibrotic pericytes is also present in the pre-clinical murine model of PF, we analyzed publicly available scRNA-seq datasets of the bleomycin-induced PF mouse model (GSE132771)28. Mouse mural cells were sub-clustered and pericytes were identified based on the expression of Cspg4, Higd1b, and Pdgfrb (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 2A). Consistent with human data, the mouse Peri2 subcluster of pericytes was found in bleomycin-treated fibrotic lungs and was absent in the untreated normal murine lungs (Fig. 3B). Similar to human data, the mouse fibrotic Peri2 pericytes were characterized by increased expression of Rgs5 (Fig. 3C), decreased expression of pericyte markers (Higd1b, Cspg4 and Cox4i2) and increased expression of fibrosis-associated markers Col1a1, Col3a1, and Acta2 (Fig. 3D). KEGG pathway analysis showed that Peri2 pericytes were enriched in fibrosis-associated signaling pathways, including TGF-beta signaling, extracellular matrix and actin cytoskeleton regulation, whereas Peri1 was enriched in pathways related to adhesion and metabolism (Fig. 3E). To make sure that the mouse data is not limited to one dataset, we have also analyzed the different publicly available scRNA-seq datasets of bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis (GSE210341)2. Using the second data set, we verified the existence of the unique fibrotic cluster of Peri2 pericytes in PF lungs but not in the normal mouse lungs (Supplementary Fig. 2B). We have also verified that Peri2 pericytes had increased expression of fibrosis-associated markers and were enriched in fibrosis-associated signaling pathways, including TGF-beta signaling (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D). Pseudotime analysis predicted transition from Peri1 to Peri2 (Supplementary Fig. 2E), a finding consistent with human PF data (Fig. 1E). Also, consistent with human data, the CellChat analysis identified Peri1 as being the dominant source of Angiopoietin 1 (ANGPT1), while Peri2 decreasing ANGPT1 and increasing ANGPT2 to destabilize endothelial cells. In addition, Peri2 are identified as a source of pro-fibrotic TGFß, FGF and IGF signaling (Supplementary Fig. 3A–C), promoting myofibroblast differentiation, excessive collagen deposition and vessels remodeling (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Altogether, the pre-clinical murine model of PF confirmed the presence of the unique fibrotic cluster of Peri2 pericytes in PF lungs but not in the normal mouse lungs. Lineage-tracing reveals the transition of normal pericytes to a fibrotic phenotype in bleomycin-treated mouse lungs

Since scRNAseq and pseudotime analysis predicted that lung pericytes undergo a trans-differentiation process under fibrotic conditions with cell fate shifting toward a fibrotic state, we next used a lineage tracing approach to determine the fate of lung pericytes during their transition from normal to fibrotic phenotype. The NG2-CreER; Rosa26-LSL-tdTomato mice (abbreviated as NG2; LSL-tdT) were treated with Tamoxifen to label pericytes (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Efficient recombination was detected in approximately 81.6% of NG2+ cells following Tamoxifen treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4C). FACS analysis and immunostaining demonstrated the presence of tdTomato-positive cells in the mouse lung (Supplementary Fig. 4D, E). To assess the fate of NG2+ cells during fibrotic remodeling, Tamoxifen-treated NG2; LSL-tdT mice received two intratracheal administrations of bleomycin, and lungs were harvested on Day 21 (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 2F). FACS analysis revealed an increase in the percentage of tdTomato+ cells after bleomycin treatment compared to untreated control lungs (Fig. 3G). Co-immunostaining for NG2 and αSMA showed that the percentage of αSMA+/tdTomato+ cells increased, while the percentage of NG2+ / tdTomato+ cells decreased after bleomycin treatment (Fig. 3H, I). Using FACS-sorted tdTomato+ cells, we next demonstrated that mRNA levels of fibrosis-associated Col1a1, Col3a1, Acta2 genes were increased in tdTomato+ cells from bleomycin-treated lungs compared to untreated controls (Fig. 3J). These findings are consistent with the increased fibrotic markers found in fibrotic pericytes from human and mouse scRNA-seq analysis (Figs. 1C, 3D). Thus, using the lineage-tracing approach, we have shown directly that during fibrotic lung remodeling, normal pericytes undergo transition to a fibrotic state, characterized by the increase in fibrosis-associated markers Col1a1, Col3a1, and Acta2.

Next, we used mouse bleomycin PF scRNA-seq datasets to compare the transcriptome of Peri2 and Peri1 pericytes. Upregulated transcription factors in murine fibrotic Peri2 pericytes were Prrx2, Scx, Bnc2, Sox9 and Egr2, while downregulated transcription factors were Foxf1, Id1, Tbx3, and Heyl (Fig. 4A). FOXF1 is decreased in pericytes of human PF lungs

To analyze further the differential expression of FOXF1 in normal versus fibrotic pericytes, the FeaturePlot, DotPlot and ViolinPlot analyses were used to demonstrate that FOXF1 was highly expressed in Peri1 pericytes but was dramatically decreased in fibrotic Peri2 pericytes (Fig. 4B, C and Supplementary Fig. 5A). Moreover, the severity of PF further decreased FOXF1 levels in Peri2 pericytes, with a significant reduction observed in patients with more advanced fibrosis compared to those with less fibrosis (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Immunostaining for FOXF1, NG2, and αSMA showed high levels of FOXF1 protein in pericytes of donor lungs, but nearly undetectable levels of FOXF1 protein in pericytes within fibrotic αSMA-positive regions in PF lungs (Fig. 4D). FOXF1 protein was detected in approximately 60% of pericytes from donor lungs, but only 10% pericytes from patients with PF were positive for FOXF1 (Fig. 4E). Thus, both mRNA and protein of FOXF1 are significantly decreased in lung pericytes of patients with pulmonary fibrosis.

Foxf1 is decreased in pericytes of bleomycin-treated mouse lungs

Next, we determined whether a bleomycin mouse model of PF recapitulated the results obtained from human patients. Based on scRNAseq analysis of mouse lungs from control and bleomycin-treated mice, Foxf1 mRNA was decreased in fibrotic Peri2 pericytes compared to normal Peri1 pericytes (Fig. 4F, G), which is consistent with the data from human donor and PF lungs (Fig. 4B). The decrease in Foxf1 mRNA in bleomycin-treated pericytes was confirmed by qRT-PCR using FACS-sorted CD31-/CD45-/NG2+ lung pericytes from control and bleomycin-treated mice (Fig.4H). Next, immunostaining for FOXF1, NG2, and αSMA, was used to show that FOXF1 protein was highly expressed in the lung pericytes of control lungs but was nearly undetectable in the pericytes within fibrotic lesions of bleomycin-treated mice (Fig. 4I). FOXF1 protein was detected in approximately 60% of pericytes from control lungs, but only 20% of pericytes from bleomycin-treated lungs were positive for FOXF1 (Fig. 4I, left panel). Next, FACS analysis was used to quantify the number of FOXF1-positive pericytes, demonstrating that the number of FOXF1+/NG2+ pericytes is reduced in the lungs of bleomycin-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 6A–C). In addition, decreased expression of FOXF1 was verified at both mRNA and protein levels in tdTomato-labeled pericytes using immunofluorescence staining and qRT-PCR of FACS-sorted tdTomato-lineage traced pericytes (Fig. 4J, K). Thus, Foxf1 mRNA and protein levels were decreased in pericytes of bleomycin-treated fibrotic lungs, which is consistent with our findings in human lung pericytes from the lungs of PF patients.

Pericyte-specific deletion of Foxf1 increases the severity of pulmonary fibrosis in pre-clinical animal models

To determine whether FOXF1 in pericytes is important during fibrotic remodeling, we generated mice with pericyte-specific deletion of the Foxf1 by breeding NG2-CreER transgenic mice with Foxf1-floxed mice (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). Tamoxifen treatment decreased Foxf1 expression as shown by qRT-PCR using FACS-sorted CD31-/CD45-/NG2+ pericytes (Supplementary Fig. 7C). Tamoxifen-treated NG2-CreER; Foxf1 f/f mice (abbreviated as periFoxf1KO) had normal lung histology and function, as assessed by H&E staining, immunostaining for NG2 and CD31, and FlexiVent lung function tests (Supplementary Fig. 7D, E). To induce fibrosis, Tamoxifen-treated periFoxf1KO mice had received two doses of bleomycin and lungs were harvested at days 14 and 21 post-bleomycin injury (Supplementary Fig. 8A). Compared to controls, bleomycin-treated periFoxf1KO mice experienced reduced survival rates, profound body weight loss, and decreased lung functions (Fig. 5A–C and Supplementary Fig. 8B). Hydroxyproline was increased in bleomycin-treated periFoxf1KO mice compared to controls, indicating increased collagen depositions (Fig. 5D). Histological examination revealed progressive fibrotic remodeling in bleomycin-treated periFoxf1KO mice, with severity intensifying over time, as indicated by H&E, Sirius Red/Fast Green, Masson’s trichrome staining, and Ashcroft score (Fig. 5E, F). Immunostaining for αSMA showed time-dependent collagen deposition in bleomycin-treated periFoxf1KO mice (Fig. 5G). FACS-sorted pericytes from bleomycin-treated periFoxf1KO lungs exhibited increased pro-fibrotic Acta2, Col1a1, and Col3a1 mRNAs compared to control lungs (Fig. 5H–J). To verify that the protective role of FOXF1 in pericytes is not limited to one animal model, we have also used the model of silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice and the ex vivo mouse precision-cut lung slices (PCLS) treated with fibrosis-induced cocktail (Supplementary Fig. 10). In both models, deletion of FOXF1 in pericytes increases the severity of lung fibrosis as quantified by increased collagen depositions (Supplementary Fig. 10A–J). Collectively, all three pre-clinical models of pulmonary fibrosis demonstrate that the loss of FOXF1 in pericytes exacerbates pulmonary fibrosis.

Overexpression of Foxf1 in pericytes before bleomycin injury prevents pulmonary fibrosis

Since deletion of Foxf1 in pericytes exacerbates pulmonary fibrosis, we sought to investigate whether overexpression of Foxf1 in pericytes could mitigate the progression of lung fibrosis. To test this, we generated a pericyte-specific Foxf1 overexpression mouse model, NG2-CreER; Rosa26-LSL-rtTA; TetO7-HA-mFoxf1 (abbreviated as periFoxf1OE) (Supplementary Fig. 11A). Upon tamoxifen treatment, Cre-mediated recombination removes the LoxP-floxed stop codon, leading to the expression of rtTA in pericytes. In the presence of doxycycline(Dox), rtTA activates the TetO7-CMV promoter, which in turn drives the expression of the mouse Foxf1 transgene. Consequently, the combined administration of tamoxifen and doxycycline results in increased Foxf1 expression specifically in pericytes (Supplementary Fig. 11A). In the absence of bleomycin treatment, the combined administration of tamoxifen and doxycycline did not alter lung histology or the distribution of pericytes and endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 11B).

To induce pulmonary fibrosis, Tam- and Dox-treated periFoxf1OE were given two weekly IT administrations of bleomycin (Fig. 6A). Mice were harvested on day 21 following the first dose of bleomycin. Efficient overexpression of Foxf1 was shown by qRT-PCR using FACS-sorted lung pericytes (Fig. 6B). In the absence of bleomycin injury, Foxf1 in periFoxf1OE pericytes was approximately six-fold higher than in controls. While bleomycin treatment decreased Foxf1 mRNA in pericytes in both groups, Foxf1 expression levels were higher in periFoxf1OE pericytes (Fig. 6B). On day 21, bleomycin-treated periFoxf1OE mice showed remarkable improvements compared to controls, as evidenced by increased oxygen saturation in the peripheral blood (Fig. 6C) and decreased collagen depositions in the lung tissue based on quantitative hydroxyproline assay (Fig. 6D). Fibrotic lung remodeling was attenuated in bleomycin-treated periFoxf1OE mice, as shown by Ashcroft score assessment, Sirius Red/Fast Green and trichrome staining (Fig. 6E, F). In addition, FACS-sorted lung pericytes from bleomycin-treated periFoxf1OE lungs demonstrated lower expression of fibrosis-associated Col1a1, Col3a1, and Acta2 mRNAs compared to lung pericytes from bleomycin-treated control mice (Fig. 6G). Overexpression of Foxf1 in pericytes during already established fibrosis decreases progression of pulmonary fibrosis

Next, we tested whether increasing Foxf1 in pericytes during already established fibrosis will improve the outcomes. Control and periFoxf1OE mice were treated with Tamoxifen at days 8–12 after bleomycin treatment (Fig. 7A) when mice already had significant collagen depositions3. Pericyte-specific overexpression of Foxf1 during already established fibrosis was sufficient to alleviate pulmonary fibrosis and improve lung functions, as demonstrated by decreased body weight loss in periFoxf1OE mice (Fig. 7B) and increased oxygen saturation in the peripheral blood (Fig. 7C). Fibrotic lung remodeling was also decreased as demonstrated by decreased hydroxyproline levels, improved Ashcroft score, as well as Sirius Red/Fast Green, and trichrome staining (Fig. 7D–F). FACS-sorted pericytes from periFoxf1OE lungs had decreased Col1a1, Col3a1, and Acta2 mRNAs compared to controls (Fig. 7G). To identify genes regulated by FOXF1, we first determined differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Peri2 from bleomycin-treated periFoxf1KO mice compared to Peri2 from bleomycin-treated control mice (Fig. 8D). Several positive regulators of the TGF-β signaling pathway, including Tgfbr1, Tgfbr2, Tgfbr3, Acvrl1 were upregulated in the periFoxf1KO pericytes, while the negative regulator Smad7 was downregulated (Fig. 8E). Next, for human fibrotic lungs, where pericyte-specific FOXF1 knockout is not feasible, we instead compared FOXF1low fibrotic pericytes from patients with PF to FOXF1hi pericytes from donors. This allowed us to capture genes associated with reduced FOXF1 expression in the human fibrotic lung. We then cross-compared DEGs from mouse and human datasets to identify overlapped genes consistently regulated under fibrotic conditions in both human and mouse lungs, potentially reflecting conserved FOXF1 targets (Fig. 8F). The overlapped DEGs were subjected to KEGG pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, demonstrating enrichment in pro-fibrotic signaling pathways, especially TGF-β signaling (Fig. 8F and Supplementary Fig. 13A).

Direct transcriptional targets of FOXF1 in lung pericytes

To determine which of these conserved DEGs are direct transcriptional targets of FOXF1, we used a publicly available Foxf1 ChIP-seq dataset of mouse lung tissue to identify FOXF binding sites. Focusing on genes involved in the regulation of pro-fibrotic pericyte transition, we prioritized five candidate genes, Id3, Id1, Aplp2, Fkbp1a, and Acvrl1, based on strong FOXF1-binding signals in their promoter regions. FOXF1-binding sites in Id3, Id1, Aplp2, Fkbp1a promoter regions coincided with the H3K4me3 binding, indicating active gene transcription and absence of repressive H3K27me3 marks (Fig. 8G and Supplementary Fig. 13B). For Acvrl1, FOXF1 binding was associated with suppressive H3K27me3 marks and lack of active H3K4me3 (Fig. 8G). Thus, based on ChIP-seq data, FOXF1 activates transcription of Id3, Id1, Aplp2, Fkbp1a, but inhibits transcription of Acvrl1.

Since FOXF1 deficiency decreased Id3, Id1, Aplp2, and Fkbp1a and increased Acvrl1 mRNAs in both mouse Foxf1KO and human FOXF1low fibrotic pericytes (Supplementary Fig. 13C), we next determined whether Foxf1 overexpression could rescue these changes. Using FACS-sorted lung pericytes from bleomycin-treated periFoxf1OE and control mice, we have shown that pericyte-specific overexpression of Foxf1 increased expression of Id3 and Aplp2, and decreased expression of Acvrl1 compared to bleomycin-treated controls, regardless of whether Foxf1 was overexpressed prior to bleomycin injury (Fig. 8H) or after (Fig. 8I). Id1 and Fkbp1a mRNAs were not changed after Foxf1 overexpression, indicating that these genes are not the direct targets of Foxf1 (Supplementary Fig. 13D, E). These findings indicate that Foxf1 directly activates Id3 and Aplp2, while represses Acvrl1 in pericytes.

To further assess the FOXF1-dependent roles of ID3, APLP2 and ACVRL1 in pericyte functions, we have restored ID3 or APLP2 expression in the FOXF1-deficient human lung pericytes using expression plasmids in vitro (Fig. 9A). We have also inhibited ACVRL1 expression in pericytes using siACVRL1 (Fig. 9A). Restoration of either ID3 or APLP2, or depletion of ACVRL1 in FOXF1-deficient pericytes decreased fibrosis-associated ACTA2, COL1A1, and COL3A1 mRNAs, indicating that each gene individually contributed to the fibrotic phenotype of FOXF1-deficient pericytes (Fig. 9B). Since FOXF1 expression in endothelial cells is inhibited by hypoxia3,29, we have also tested if hypoxia suppresses FOXF1 in pericytes. FOXF1-deficient pericytes increase fibroblast activation in vitro

Since lung fibroblasts are responsible for collagen deposition in pulmonary fibrosis, and CellChat analysis predicted the extensive crosstalk between lung pericytes and fibroblasts (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3), we assessed the contribution of FOXF1-deficient lung pericytes to the activation of fibroblasts in vitro. Human lung fibroblasts were cultured in the presence of conditioned medium (CM) from control and FOXF1-deficient human lung pericytes (Supplementary Fig. 9A, B). CM from FOXF1-deficient lung pericytes increased migration, proliferation and expression of pro-fibrotic CTHRC1, COL1A1 and ACTA2 mRNAs in lung fibroblasts compared to fibroblasts cultured in CM from control pericytes (Supplementary Fig. 9C–F). Thus, FOXF1-deficient pericytes promoted a pro-fibrotic phenotype in lung fibroblasts in vitro.

We next tested whether pericyte-derived ID3, APLP2 and ACVRL1 function downstream of FOXF1 to activate lung fibroblasts. Human lung fibroblasts were cultured with conditioned medium from FOXF1-deficient lung pericytes with or without restored expression of ID3 or APLP2, or from FOXF1-deficient pericytes after ACVRL1 depletion (Fig. 9C–E). Migration and proliferation assays demonstrated that ID3, APLP2 and ACVRL1 in pericytes independently contribute to the regulation of pro-fibrotic behavior in lung fibroblasts (Fig. 9C–E). We also assessed the changes in pericytes’ conditioned medium after ID3 restoration using a human cytokine array. Restoration of ID3 in the FOXF1-deficient human lung pericytes decreased the secretion of pro-fibrotic mediators, such as IL-8 and CXCL1 (Fig. 9F–H). Thus, ID3 is one of the FOXF1-dependent regulators of pericyte homeostatic functions, acting in part through regulation of pro-fibrotic cytokines.

Altogether, these findings demonstrate that FOXF1 directly regulates a network of pericyte genes critical to the progression of pulmonary fibrosis and transition of pericytes to the fibrotic state.

Discussion

Pulmonary fibrosis is a complex, multicellular process involving well-documented contributions from epithelial cells30, endothelial cells3,31, fibroblasts32 and immune cells33. These cell types play critical roles in injury-induced aberrant activation of fibroblasts, fibrotic remodeling of the interstitium and inflammatory cell recruitment, all of which drive the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Recent studies have identified several unique cell populations associated with pulmonary fibrosis, including Sfrp1+ transitional fibroblasts implicated in matrix production34, distinct epithelial and mesenchymal cell types35, and a novel population of profibrotic alveolar macrophages36. Despite these advancements, the role of pericytes in pulmonary fibrosis has often been underestimated.

In several organ tissues, including the heart and kidney, emerging evidence suggests that pericytes can serve as a source of myofibroblasts37,38. Their potential to adopt a fibrotic phenotype has drawn attention to their possible involvement in fibrotic diseases. Recent studies have identified a specialized pericyte subtype in pulmonary capillaries during hypoxia-induced pulmonary hypertension39, however, its role in fibrotic lung remodeling is unclear. In the present study, we used scRNA-sequencing to identify a distinct pericyte population in the lung tissue of PF patients. These pericytes were exclusively observed in the lungs of PF patients and bleomycin-treated mice but were absent in normal donor lungs and untreated mice. This fibrosis-associated pericyte population exhibited a unique gene signature, including elevated expression of collagens and pro-fibrotic markers, suggesting their contribution to the promotion of a fibrotic microenvironment and fibrotic remodeling. In fibrotic lungs, differentiating fibrosis-associated pericytes from smooth muscle cells is challenging since both cell populations can acquire overlapping mesenchymal phenotypes and express common markers, including as α-SMA (ACTA2), TAGLN, and PDGFRβ during injury-induced remodeling. In addition, activated pericytes may transition toward myofibroblast-like states, while vascular smooth muscle cells can also change their transcriptional programs and cell state. Thus, the precise cell type identification dependent on combining lineage tracing, spatial localization, and multi-marker analysis rather than single-marker staining alone. In our current studies, we have used a lineage-tracing approach and pseudotime analysis of mouse and human scRNAseq datasets to demonstrate that Peri2 cells can derive from Peri1 cells. A limitation of our study is that Peri2 cells can potentially derive from smooth muscle cells, and this cannot be distinguished by immunostaining.

The significant contribution here is that we have identified a cluster of fibrotic pericytes (Peri2) that is uniquely present in fibrotic but not in normal lungs. We have also identified a transcriptional signature of fibrotic pericytes, and an evolutionary conserved signature of differentially expressed transcription factors in fibrotic pericytes from patients with pulmonary fibrosis and bleomycin-treated mice. Several of them have been already shown to be important in PF, including ID1, DUSP4, and HEYL40–42. Id1-deficient mice exhibited increased susceptibility to bleomycin-induced lung injury40. DUSP4 has been reported to attenuate cardiac fibrosis by suppressing the TGF-β/p38 signaling pathway41. Inhibition of NOTCH3 signaling in a HEYL-dependent manner has been found to mitigate meniscus fibrosis42. The anti-fibrotic roles of these transcription factors align with their observed downregulation in fibrotic pericytes in our study. Conversely, upregulated transcription factors in fibrotic pulmonary pericytes include PRRX1, FOSB, BNC2, and SOX9. These factors are recognized for their pro-fibrotic functions in promoting extracellular matrix (ECM) gene expression and myofibroblast differentiation43–46, consistent with their upregulation in fibrotic pericytes. Among the most significantly downregulated transcription factors in pericytes, FOXF1 emerged as a key candidate, with its downregulation observed in both PF patients and bleomycin-treated mice. Consequently, we used mouse genetic models to focus on elucidating the role of FOXF1 in fibrosis-associated changes in pericytes.

Previous studies have demonstrated that FOXF1 has anti-fibrotic functions in fibroblasts and endothelial cells24,47. For instance, FOXF1-dependent CDH2-CD11 switching in fibroblasts is essential for their transition to a pro-fibrotic phenotype24. FOXF1 has been shown to alleviate silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis by inhibiting the TGF-β/SMAD2/3 pathway, decreasing myofibroblast differentiation and ECM accumulation47. FOXF1 has also been implicated in regulating endothelial cell reprogramming into a pro-fibrotic phenotype3. In addition to lung endothelial cells and lung fibroblasts, FOXF1 is highly expressed in normal lung pericytes. Our results demonstrate that loss of FOXF1 in pericytes exacerbates pulmonary fibrosis, whereas pericyte-specific FOXF1 restoration mitigates fibrotic progression. Together with the previously published studies, our findings indicate that FOXF1 acts as an anti-fibrotic factor across multiple cell types involved in pulmonary fibrosis, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target in human IPF.

In the current work, we have also identified the network of FOXF1- regulated pericyte genes important for pulmonary fibrosis. Cross-comparison of human and mouse DEGs in fibrotic pericytes and KEGG/GO pathway analysis48 demonstrated that FOXF1-depleted pericytes were characterized by increased pro-fibrotic signaling pathways, including TGF-β signaling, extracellular matrix organization, actin cytoskeleton regulation, and cell differentiation. TGF-β signaling is known to promote mesenchymal cell differentiation, including myofibroblast differentiation, during the progression of pulmonary fibrosis49,50. Regulation of the actin cytoskeleton pathways are intricately linked to TGF-β signaling, contributing to pro-fibrotic phenotype and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition51. Using ChIP-seq analysis and in vivo Foxf1 overexpression experiments, we identified several direct transcriptional targets of FOXF1 in pericytes. ID3 and APLP2 are known for their anti-fibrotic activity and were downregulated in the fibrotic FOXF1-deficient pericytes. ID3, a member of the inhibitor of DNA binding (Id) family of transcription factors, has been shown to suppress COL1A2 promoter activity in TGF-β1-stimulated pulmonary myofibroblasts when overexpressed52. In this study, we found that Id3 was significantly downregulated in mouse Foxf1ko fibrotic pericytes and human FOXF1low fibrotic pericytes. APLP2, a type I transmembrane glycoprotein, inhibits TGF-β signaling and regulates ECM-receptor interactions. It inhibits TGF-β signaling by promoting degradation of the TGFBR253 and facilitates cell adhesion to fibronectin and collagen IV54. Acvrl1, was found to be upregulated in Foxf1KO group. This gene encodes a type I cell surface receptor that belongs to the TGF-β superfamily of ligands55. Our co-culture experiments with FOXF1-deficient pericytes were consistent with the hypothesis that ID3, APLP2 and ACVRL1 contribute to pericyte re-programming and increased fibroblast activation downstream of FOXF1.

In summary, FOXF1 exhibits an anti-fibrotic function in lung pericytes during pulmonary fibrosis. Loss of FOXF1exacerbates the severity of pulmonary fibrosis, characterized by increased collagen deposition, tissue remodeling, loss of lung function and increased mortality. FOXF1 is important in maintaining pericyte homeostasis and suppressing the pericyte transition to fibrotic phenotype. Further investigation into the precise molecular pathways downstream of FOXF1 could unveil promising therapeutic targets for the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis, offering new avenues for intervention in this devastating disease.

Methods

Ethical statement

The use of human lung tissue was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Arizona. Donor and human idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) lung tissue specimens were obtained from the tissue repository and explanted lungs at the University of Cincinnati Medical Center and St. Joseph’s Hospital in Arizona under the protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Cincinnati and St. Joseph’s Hospital Medical Center. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All samples were provided in a de-identified manner. All animal care and experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Arizona. Lung tissue sections were from both female and male human patients. For mouse experiments, both males and females were included in equal numbers.

Transgenic mice

Both male and female 8–10-week-old mice were used in this study. Homozygous Foxf1f/f mice were crossed with heterozygous NG2-CreER mice to generate NG2-CreER; Foxf1f/f mice (abbreviated as periFoxf1KO), enabling pericyte-specific depletion of Foxf1 upon Tamoxifen administration. Rosa26-LSL-tdTomato mice were crossed with NG2-CreER mice to generate NG2-CreER; Rosa26-LSL-tdTomato mice (abbreviated as NG2; LSL-tdT). To induce Cre recombinase activity, Tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were administered intraperitoneally (I.P.) into mice at a dose of 80 µg/g body weight per day for 5 consecutive days. To achieve pericyte-specific overexpression of Foxf1, TetO7-HA-mFoxf13 was crossed with NG2-CreER mice and Rosa26-LSL-rtTA mice (abbreviated as periFoxf1OE). For Foxf1 overexpression in pericytes, Tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were administered intraperitoneally (80 µg/g body weight per day) for 5 consecutive days, followed by maintenance on doxycycline-containing chow (Inotiv, Indianapolis, IN, USA) for the duration of the experiment. Mice are maintained in a specific pathogen–free animal facility under controlled environmental conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, temperature 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 40–60%) with free access to standard chow and water.

Bleomycin-induces and silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis models

To induce pulmonary fibrosis, mice were administered bleomycin sulfate (EMD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA) intratracheally (I.T.) at doses of 1.5 µ/kg or 2 µ/kg once per week for 2 weeks56. Mice were harvested on day 14 or day 21. Control mice received phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) intratracheally once per week for 2 weeks. Control groups included Foxf1f/f mice receiving tamoxifen/PBS and Foxf1f/f mice receiving tamoxifen/bleomycin treatments. For the silica-induced fibrosis mouse model, silica particles (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were washed in 1 N HCl at 100 °C for 2 h, rinsed thoroughly with sterile water, autoclaved for 1 h, then dried and suspended in PBS. The silica suspension was sonicated before administration. Mice were administered silica intratracheally (I.T.) at a dose of 200 mg/kg once and harvested on day 21.

Precision-cut lung slices (PCLS) generation

Lung lobes were inflated and embedded in 2% low-melting point agarose (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Billings, Montana, USA), and then sliced (300 μm thickness) using a vibrating microtome (Precisionary Instruments, Ashland, Massachusetts, USA). After slicing, PCLS were cultivated in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS,2% Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Gibco, Billings, Montana, USA). PCLS was treated with a cocktail consisting of 5 ng/ml TGFβ (Sino Biological, Beijing, China), 10 ng/ml PDGF-AB (Gibco, Billings, Montana, USA), 10 ng/ml TNFα (Proteintech, Rosemont, Illinois, USA), 5 µM LPA (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, Michigan, 48108) for 10 days to induce ex vivo fibrosis. Medium was changed 3 times.

Respiratory mechanics and oxygen saturation

Mice were anesthetized on day 14 or day 21 following the initial bleomycin treatment. Oxygen saturation (SpO₂) was measured using the MouseOX Plus pulse oximeter (STARR Life Sciences, Oakmont, PA, USA). A collar sensor was utilized to obtain signals from the carotid arteries of the mice. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using the MouseOX Plus 1.6.x software. In vivo respiratory mechanics were assessed using the FlexiVent system (Scireq, Montreal, QC, Canada). The collected data was analyzed with FlexiVent software version 7.5.

Immunofluorescent staining and histological staining

Lung paraffin sections were prepared for histological staining, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Sirius Red/Fast Green, immunohistochemistry, and Masson’s Trichrome staining57. Lung tissue sections were from both female and male patients. For mouse experiments, both males and females were included in equal numbers. Both frozen and paraffin lung sections were used for immunofluorescent staining. For paraffin sections, mouse lung tissues were perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and inflated with 10% formalin. The tissues were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin. For frozen sections, lung tissues were perfused with PBS, inflated with a 1:1 mixture of 30% sucrose and OCT, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h. Tissues were then transferred to 30% sucrose overnight before embedding in OCT. For immunofluorescent staining, lung sections were rehydrated in PBS (two washes, 5 min each) and permeabilized with PBST. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBST for 1 h, primary antibodies were applied (Supplementary Table T1). Slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C and Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488, 594, or 647) and DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were added. Images were acquired using the EVOS Imaging System, the Zeiss Axio Imager M2 upright widefield fluorescent microscope, and the Zeiss LSM710 laser scanning confocal microscope. For histological staining, paraffin slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated with xylene and a graded ethanol series of decreasing concentrations. Staining was performed following the protocols for H&E (ScyTek Laboratories, Logan, UT, USA), Sirius Red/Fast Green (Chondrex, Woodinville, WA, USA), and Masson’s Trichrome Staining Kit (ScyTek Laboratories, Logan, UT, USA).

Collagen quantification and fibrosis severity assessment

Quantification of lung collagen contents were performed using the hydroxyproline assay (Cell Biolabs, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The severity of fibrosis was assessed by the Ashcroft score58. The score was assigned from 0(no fibrosis) to 8(fibrous obliteration) based on the sections observed under an EVOS brightfield microscope.

qRT-PCR

RNA was extracted from FACS-sorted lung pericytes, including NG2+ cells from periFoxf1KO and periFoxf1OE mice or tdTomato+ cells from NG2; LSL-tdT mice, using the Quick-RNA Microprep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). The RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using either the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) or the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 3 system using Taqman probes or SYBR Green probes, as detailed in the supplementary table (Supplementary Table T2). GAPDH and 18S were used as internal controls.

Flow cytometry and flow sorting

Flow cytometry experiments on mouse lung tissue were performed as previously described59,60, using the antibodies listed in the supplementary table(Supplementary Table T3). Stained cells were analyzed with an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer, provided by the Flow Cytometry Core Facility at the University of Arizona. Pericytes were sorted using a BD FACS Symphony S6, available through the Flow Cytometry Core Facility at Arizona State University. Data analysis was conducted using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1).

10x Genomics single cell RNA-seq and data analysis

The publicly available human lung scRNA-seq datasets from GSE227136 was used that included lung tissue samples from 116 individuals, including 67 patients with pulmonary fibrosis (PF) and 49 unaffected donors25. The publicly available murine lung scRNA-seq datasets from GSE2103412 and GSE13277128 were used. The datasets include lung tissue samples collected at day 0 (non-bleomycin-treated), 7, 14, and 21(after bleomycin treatment).

Single-cell RNAseq libraries were generated from day 14 bleomycin-treated control and periFoxf1KO mice using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits v3.1 (Dual Index) (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol by the Genomics Core at Arizona State University. Three or four lungs in each group were combined to prepare sequencing libraries. To enrich for pericytes, hematopoietic cells (CD45+) were depleted from the cell suspensions using FACS-sorting. Approximately 11,000 cells were loaded for each sample, with a targeted cell recovery estimate of 7000 cells. The libraries were sequenced with NovaSeq X at a sequencing depth of 3.25 billion reads per sample provided by the TGen-Translational Genomics Research Institute. Cellranger mkfastq was used to extract sequence reads in FASTQ format. The cellranger count with mm10 reference genome (as provided by 10X) and the Cell Ranger software (v8.0.0, 10X Genomics) were used to generate gene-count matrices from the FASTQ files. Reads were processed by counting Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMI). Cells with unique feature counts less than 500 and more than 8000 were excluded from the analysis. Low-quality cells were removed based on the fraction of mitochondrial genes ( > 20%). Data filtering, normalization criteria and principal component analysis are described in the previous paper21.

To further ensure that clustering would not be impacted by batch effects or biological variability associated with mouse age or oxygen conditions, we performed the data integration method implemented by the Seurat software (version 4.4.0 in 4.4.2 environment) for SCTransform-normalized data. The data were log-normalized with a scale factor of 104. Variable features were determined by the FindVariableGenes function. FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions were used to integrate datasets. Next, highly variable features from the integrated matrix were used as input for linear dimensionality reduction via principal component analysis (PCA). Principal components (PCs) with standard deviation > 3.5 were used as input for unsupervised clustering with a resolution set to 0.2, using the nonlinear UMAP down dimensional algorithm.

For differential analysis of gene expression, pericytes were defined using the Idents function in the Seurat package. Next, the FindMarkers function was used with the logFC threshold set to 0.25 to identify genes downregulated or upregulated between samples. For molecular pathway analysis, the functional annotation clustering was performed using DAVID Bioinformatics Resources Ver.6.8. The KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway enrichment analysis was used to produce the annotation data. Functional clusters with Benjamini-corrected P- value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Cell lines, wound healing and proteome profiler human cytokine array

Human lung fibroblast (MRC5) and human lung pericyte cell lines, along with the corresponding culture medium kit, were commercially obtained. A cocktail of siRNAs was used to knockdown FOXF1 or ACVRL1 in human lung pericytes using Dharmafect transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Dharmacon, Lafayette, Colorado, USA). A cocktail of non-targeting siRNAs was used as a control. To create the human ID3 and APLP2 expression vectors, the vector backbone was amplified using Primestar Max polymerase (Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan) with primers in the supplementary table (Supplementary Table T4). Human pulmonary artery endothelial cells (HPAECs) RNA was extracted using the Quick-RNA micro prep kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was completed using Induro Reverse Transcriptase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. ID3 and APLP2 cDNA sequences were amplified with homology to the vector using Primestar Max polymerase and primers in the supplementary table (Supplementary Table T4). The vector and insert were purified using the Nucleospin Gel and Clean up kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified vector and inserted were incubated with Infusion snap assembly master mix following the manufacturer’s instructions (Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). ID3 and APLP2 mixes were then transformed into chemically competent Stellar cells (Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). Cell migration was evaluated using a wound healing assay, with the gap created by 2-well Culture-Inserts (Ibidi, Fitchburg, WI, USA). Proteome profiling was performed using the Proteome Profiler Human Cytokine Array Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphical data presentation were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Normally distributed variables between control and experimental groups were compared using a two-tailed Student’s t test, while non-normally distributed variables were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Data are presented as mean ± SEM (standard error of the mean), and a P- value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
