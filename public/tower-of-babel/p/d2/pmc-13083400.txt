Postbiotic approach to antibiotic resistance: antibacterial and antibiofilm potential of Lactobacillus salivarius supernatant
Authors: E Akgol, Elif Aydin, A Temel, Percin Renders D
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid rise in antibiotic resistance is making infections, particularly those caused by ESBL-positive and biofilm-forming Escherichia coli isolates, increasingly difficult to treat. Therefore, interest in alternative biotherapeutic approaches based on probiotic or postbiotic sources is increasing. Although there are many reports on the antibacterial and antibiofilm effects of probiotics in the current literature, the efficacy of Lactobacillus salivarius supernatant (LS-S) on ESBL-positive and biofilm-forming clinical E. coli isolates has not been studied sufficiently. The aim of this study was to evaluate the antibacterial and antibiofilm effects of LS-S against ESBL-positive E. coli isolates in vitro and to compare it with other probiotic-derived findings in the literature. Antibacterial activity was determined by modified disk diffusion and agar well diffusion methods, and growth inhibition was assessed over time using a microplate assay. Biofilm inhibition was measured using a quantitative biofilm assay, and the results were analyzed statistically. LS-S produced average inhibition zones of 16.0 ± 3.9 mm (disk) and 18.8 ± 3.3 mm (well) in ESBL-positive E. coli isolates. Microplate assay results showed that inhibition reached 20% at 3 h, 65–70% at 6–12 h, and 87% at 24 h. In biofilm analyses, biofilm formation was suppressed in all isolates, with inhibition exceeding 40% detected in 13 isolates (43.3%), and the highest efficacy was found to be 73.1%. The data obtained demonstrate that LS-S has the capacity to inhibit both planktonic growth and biofilm development. These findings demonstrate that postbiotic-based biotherapeutics are promising candidates for infection control in the era of antibiotic resistance and highlight the need for in vivo and translational studies for clinical applications.Keywords: Lactobacillus salivarius, ESBL-positive Escherichia coli, Antibacterial activity, Antibiofilm activity, Postbiotic

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) represent a significant health concern in women and are most commonly caused by Escherichia coli (E. coli). Among bacterial infections, UTIs rank as one of the most frequently diagnosed conditions across the world (Mahdavi et al. 2018, Magale et al. 2015). Failure to identify and manage UTIs in a timely manner can result in recurrent illness and irreversible renal impairment (Gupta et al. 2025). In cases of uncomplicated UTIs, treatment is generally straightforward since the causative agents are well known, with E. coli responsible for 80–90% of infections (Simões et al. 2020).

Widespread antibiotic use over recent decades have been extensively applied against pathogenic bacteria; however, their overuse has accelerated the emergence of antimicrobial resistance, which has now developed into a major health threat (Chiang et al. 2018). As antimicrobial resistance continues to rise, the threat posed by multidrug-resistant organisms has become a critical issue, with serious implications for patient safety and healthcare surveillance (Giraldi et al. 2019). Bacterial resistance to antibiotics has become an undeniable global concern. The lack of newly developed antibiotics intensifies the challenge of treating infectious diseases in the face of rising resistance. Presently, antimicrobial resistance causes roughly 700,000 deaths per year worldwide, and forecasts suggest this figure could escalate to 10 million by 2050 (Gharavi et al. 2021).

A major concern in this context is the emergence of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)– and carbapenemase-producing bacteria. ESBL-producing strains confer resistance to β-lactam antibiotics by hydrolyzing widely used agents such as penicillins, monobactams, and most cephalosporins, while typically sparing cephamycins and carbapenems (Jomehzadeh et al. 2022). These organisms are frequently multidrug resistant, further limiting therapeutic options. Compounding this challenge, recurrent urinary tract infections affect nearly one-third of patients, emphasizing the significant clinical burden of UTIs, which remain among the most common bacterial infections worldwide (Lindblom et al. 2018).

Multiple virulence determinants play a role in UTIs by this pathogen, and biofilm development is considered one of the most significant. Biofilms, which consist of organized clusters of microorganisms embedded on the bladder mucosa, enhance bacterial tolerance to antibiotics and disinfectants. They are implicated in approximately 60% of all human infections and often persist despite antimicrobial therapy (Garousi et al. 2022).

Lactic acid bacteria are a group of Gram-positive probiotics that lack spore-forming ability and respiration, producing lactic acid as the main metabolic product of carbohydrate fermentation. Lactobacillus species, in particular, are well-established probiotics and have recently been investigated for their antibacterial applications in human disease therapy (Shah et al. 2024). Probiotics are widely recognized for their antibacterial activity against pathogenic microorganisms. This activity is attributed to several mechanisms, including the production of organic acids that inhibit pathogen growth, competition for adhesion sites, and the reduction of bacterial adherence Soltani et al., (2022); Mahdavi et al., (2018). Studies have reported that Lactobacillus strains generate multiple bactericidal agents. The antibacterial efficacy of these organisms is largely attributed to the production of organic acids and bacteriocins (Mahdavi et al. 2018, Widyastuti et al. 2021, Mahdavi and Isazadeh 2019, Rather et al. 2015). In light of this background, the present study aimed to evaluate the antibacterial and antibiofilm activities of Lactobacillus salivarius ATCC 11,741 as a probiotic against ESBL producing clinical isolates of E. coli.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

Totally, thirty non-repetitive uropathogenic E. coli isolates were selected from urine samples of patients with urinary tract infections for this study. The bacterial isolates had been previously identified and confirmed as ESBL producing and biofilm-forming strains (Aydın et al. 2024). Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles and species-level identification of the strains were determined using the BD Phoenix™ M50 ID/AST system (Becton Dickinson, USA). ESBL production was verified by the double-disk synergy test (DDST) following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100-S25 guidelines. Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 and E. coli ATCC 25,922 were used as quality control strains for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29,212 served as a positive control for biofilm production. All bacterial isolates were stored in tryptic soy broth (supplemented with 40% glycerol) at − 20 °C until further use (Aydın et al. 2024).

Detection of biofilm formation in E. coli isolates

The biofilm capacity of ESBL E. coli strains (n = 30) was quantified with the spectrophotometric microplate method (Aydın et al. 2024). Initially, E. coli isolates were plated on Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA) medium (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and incubated at 37 °C for 20–24 h. Then bacterial suspensions were prepared in tryptic soy broth with 2.5% glucose (TSBG, Oxoid, UK) medium (3 mL) by direct colony suspension method. The suspensions were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard for inoculum. Following the addition of TSBG (180 µL) medium to wells of sterile, 96-well, flat-bottom microplates, the inoculum suspension (20 µL) for each strain were seeded. The microplates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to be able to form biofilm. The well contents were gently aspirated and washed with sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS, Oxoid, UK) to remove non-adherent cells. The microplates were allowed to air dry at room temperature. Methanol (200 µL) was added to wells to fix the microorganisms adhered and the microplates were incubated for 15 min. Then, methanol was aspirated and crystal violet (CV, 2%, 200 µL) solution was added to each well to stain. The well contents were disposed of after the microplates were incubated for 15 min at room temperature. The microplates were washed with tap water until the rinse water being colourless. After drying, 200 µL of 95% ethanol was added and waited for 10 min. Lastly, the optical density (OD) values of each well were measured at 570 nm, by using spectrophotometric microplate reader (CLARIOstar Plus Microplate Reader, BMG LabTech, Cary NC, USA). The OD of the wells including only TSBG medium was used as negative control. Three standard deviations over the mean OD of the negative controls were the definition of the cut-off optical density, or ODC. Each measurement was run in triplicate. GraphPad Prism 5.03 (San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analyses (Welch’s t-test and non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test), with p < 0.05 being deemed significant. The biofilm-forming capacity of isolates were categorized according to the following criteria: OD. ≤ ODC: no biofilm, ODC < OD. ≤ (2 × ODC): weak biofilm, (2 × ODC) < OD. ≤ (4 × ODC): moderate biofilm, and (4 × ODC) < OD.: strong biofilm (Temel and Erac 2022).

Isolation and propagation of L. salivarius

L. salivarius ATCC 11,741 strain was obtained in a lyophilized form and rehydrated in a sterile hydration capsule before subculturing. Initially, single-colony inoculation was performed on de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h under anaerobic conditions (5% CO₂) using an anaerobic jar with the BD GasPak™ EZ Anaerobe Container System (Becton Dickinson, USA) (Davoodabadi et al. 2015). The purified L. salivarius colonies were examined for morphological characteristics and further confirmed by Gram staining under a microscope (Fig. 1). The isolates were then transferred into broth medium supplemented with 16% glycerol and stored at − 20 °C. For subsequent experiments, pure cultures obtained from a single plate were used.

Typical colony morphology of L. salivarius ATCC 11,741 on de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar after incubation at 37 °C for 24–48 h

Isolation and preparation of supernatant from L. salivarius

L. salivarius colonies propagated on de MRS agar were suspended in 10 mL of sterile physiological saline, and the bacterial density was adjusted to 10⁶–10⁸ CFU/mL using a McFarland densitometer. Aliquots of these dilutions were inoculated onto fresh MRS agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h under anaerobic conditions (5% CO₂, anaerobic jar with GasPak system). Following incubation, colony counts were performed. Parallel inoculations were also made into MRS broth, which was further incubated at 37 °C for 2–4 h under anaerobic conditions to allow regrowth (Davoodabadi et al. 2015).

The preparation of L. salivarius ATCC 11,741 supernatant (LS-S) was carried out as described by Wang and Zeng (2022) with minor modifications (Wang and Zeng 2022). Briefly, bacterial cultures grown in MRS broth were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was collected. To denature and precipitate proteinaceous compounds, the supernatant was heated at 80 °C for 20 min in a water bath, followed by a second centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 15 min. The clarified fraction was then filtered through a sterile 0.22 μm pore-size filter and stored at + 4 °C until further use.

Determination of antibacterial activity of L. salivarius supernatant

The antimicrobial activities of the isolates were evaluated using a modified disk diffusion method. Previously obtained ESBL-positive E. coli isolates were streaked onto tryptic soy agar (TSA; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h to obtain fresh single colonies. In addition, bacterial growth inhibition was evaluated by microplate assay, where standardized bacterial suspensions were co-incubated with LS-S and monitored spectrophotometrically at 660 nm over a 24 h period.

Modified disk diffusion assay for antimicrobial activity

Freshly subcultured ESBL-positive E. coli isolates were adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to a 0.5 McFarland standard and evenly spread onto MHA plates using sterile cotton swabs. After inoculation, sterile blank antimicrobial susceptibility test disks (Oxoid, UK) were aseptically placed on the surface of the agar at equal distances from one another. Each disk was then loaded with 20 µl of the LS-S. As a negative control, sterile MRS broth was applied onto separate disks under the same conditions. The plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h, after which the diameter of the inhibition zones surrounding the disks was measured and recorded. All assays were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility of the results (Davoodabadi et al. 2015).

Agar well diffusion assay for antimicrobial activity

ESBL-positive E.coli isolates were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard and uniformly spread onto petri dishes containing 20 mL of MHA using sterile cotton swabs. Subsequently, 6–8 wells with a diameter of 6 mm were aseptically punched into the agar, and 20 µl of sterile MHA cooled to 45 °C was dispensed into the bottom of each well to prevent leakage. Each well was then loaded with 50 µl of the LS-S, while sterile MRS broth was used as the negative control. The plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h, after which the diameters of the inhibition zones around the wells were measured and recorded (El-Mokhtar et al. 2020).

Evaluation of bacterial growth ınhibition by microplate assay

The ESBL-producing E. coli isolates were adjusted to a final concentration of 10⁶–10⁸ CFU/mL using a McFarland densitometer. For each assay, 20 µl of the standardized bacterial suspension, 90 µl of Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and 90 µl of sterile filtered LS-S were inoculated into sterile 96-well microplates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the optical density (OD) at 660 nm every 3 h. As a negative control, 20 µl of bacterial suspension, 90 µl of fresh MHB, and 90 µl of freshly prepared MRS broth diluted with distilled water (20%) were added to the wells. As a positive control, bacterial suspension and MHB without LS-S were used. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility (Divyashree et al. 2021). The inhibition percentage was calculated using the formula: Inhibition (%)=((ODcontrol​−ODsample​)​/ODcontrol​) ×100.

Determination of antibiofilm activity of L. salivariussupernatant

The antibiofilm activities of LS-S (at sub-MIC) on biofilm formation of ESBL E. coli isolates were investigated by spectrophotometric microplate method. Firstly, 90 µl of TSBG medium, 20 µl of standardized bacterial suspension and 90 µl of sterile filtered LS-S (MIC/2) were added into the wells of sterile 96-well, flat bottom microplates, respectively. The microplates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to ensure biofilm formation. The biofilm-forming capacity of isolates in the presence and absence of supernatant was calculated by averaging the OD values measured in replicate runs. Control wells for bacterial growth (including bacterial suspension and TSB) and medium without LS-S were separately tested in each run.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (5.03). All experiments were conducted in at least three independent replicates. Differences between groups were assessed after testing for normality using parametric Welch’s t-test and non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance in the figures is indicated by asterisks: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001.

Among the isolates, 63.3% (n = 19) exhibited moderate biofilm formation, while 36.7% (n = 11) demonstrated strong biofilm-forming capacity (Table 1). The quality control strain, E. faecalis ATCC 29,212, also exhibited strong biofilm formation. These findings indicate that the majority of ESBL-producing E. coli isolates display at least moderate biofilm-forming ability, highlighting their potential to persist in clinical environments.

Antibacterial activity of L. salivarius supernatant

Antibacterial activity determined by modified disk diffusion assay

The antibacterial activity of LS-S was evaluated using the modified disk diffusion assay and was observed against all 30 ESBL-producing E. coli isolates. The mean inhibition zone diameter was 16.0 ± 3.9 mm (range: 9.8–22.6 mm). No inhibition was detected in the negative control, while the quality control strain E. coli ATCC 25,922 also exhibited an inhibition zone (Table 1).

When stratified by biofilm capacity, isolates with moderate biofilm formation showed a mean inhibition zone of 18.3 ± 2.7 mm (n = 19), whereas strong biofilm formers exhibited 12.2 ± 2.2 mm (n = 11). Statistical analysis demonstrated a significant difference between the groups (Welch’s t-test and Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.001), indicating that the antibacterial effect of LS-S is markedly stronger against isolates with moderate biofilm-forming capacity (Fig. 2A).

Antibacterial results by agar well diffusion assay

The antibacterial activity of LS-S was evaluated using the agar well diffusion assay and was observed against all 30 ESBL-producing E. coli isolates. The mean inhibition zone diameter was 18.8 ± 3.3 mm (range: 13.7–24.0 mm). No inhibition was detected in the negative control, while the quality control strain E. coli ATCC 25,922 also exhibited an inhibition zone (Table 1).

When stratified by biofilm capacity, isolates with moderate biofilm formation showed a mean inhibition zone of 20.6 ± 2.4 mm (n = 19), whereas strong biofilm formers exhibited 15.7 ± 2.0 mm (n = 11). Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference between the groups (Welch’s t-test and Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.001), indicating that the antibacterial effect of LS-S is stronger against isolates with moderate biofilm-forming capacity (Fig. 2B).

Time course of bacterial growth Inhibition by microplate assay

The growth inhibition induced by LS-S increased progressively over time in all isolates. No inhibition was observed at baseline (0 h), and the effect became evident in the subsequent time points. The mean inhibition percentage reached ~ 20% at 3 h, stabilized between 65 and 70% from 6 to 12 h, and rose to an average of 87% at 24 h. This trend was clearly illustrated in the mean ± SD line plot (Fig. 3A) and further supported by the distribution of isolates in the boxplot (Fig. 3B). The dotplot representation also confirmed that individual sample values (blue dots) consistently followed this pattern, with mean values (red lines) peaking at 24 h (Fig. 3C). Statistical analyses (Friedman test) revealed highly significant differences among the time points (p < 0.0001).

When evaluated according to biofilm capacity, both moderate (n = 19) and strong biofilm-forming isolates (n = 11) exhibited a highly similar inhibition profile throughout the experimental period (0–24 h). In both groups, mean inhibition percentages increased progressively from approximately 20% at 3 h to nearly 87% at 24 h, following a comparable time-dependent pattern of activity. At intermediate time points (6–12 h), inhibition stabilized at 65–70% in both groups, with overlapping standard deviations. Statistical analyses confirmed the absence of significant differences between the groups at any time point (Welch’s t-test and Mann–Whitney U test, p > 0.05). These findings indicate that the antibacterial effect of LS-S is time-dependent yet independent of the biofilm-forming capacity of the isolates, suggesting that the inhibitory potential of the supernatant is consistently maintained regardless of the isolates’ biofilm phenotype.

Antibiofilm activity of L. salivarius supernatant

The biofilm-forming capacities of E. coli isolates and the antibiofilm effects of LS-S were presented in Table 1. The analyses were based on OD measurements and calculated IRB values in accordance with the given formula above.

Significant reduction in biofilm formation was observed in all isolates upon exposure to the supernatant, although the magnitude of inhibition varied considerably across isolates.

Overall, 13 out of 30 isolates (43.3%) exhibited high antibiofilm activity, defined as IRB values above 40% (Fig. 4). In these isolates, inhibition rates ranged from 40.8% to 73.1%. The highest activity was recorded in isolate E6 (73.1%), followed by E11 (61.6%), E20 (60.5%), and E10 (58.9%), all of which demonstrated strong antibiofilm inhibition. Moderate antibiofilm activity (approximately 45–55%) was observed in isolates E5, E7, E13, E15, E17, E19, E22, and E25. In contrast, the remaining 17 isolates showed IRB values below 40%, indicating weaker antibiofilm responses. L. salivarius supernatant showed antibiofilm activity on both moderate and strong biofilm isolates. It was noticed that the percentage of inhibition was higher for isolates with strong biofilm capacity (50.92 ± 8.09%).

Particularly, isolates with higher IRB values reflect strong suppression of biofilm formation, whereas those with lower values demonstrate only partial inhibition.

Discussion

Probiotic-derived compounds’ antibacterial and antibiofilm properties against isolates of E. coli that produce ESBL and form biofilms highlight the potential of innovative biotherapeutic approaches for infection control in the age of rising antibiotic resistance.

The data obtained in our study showed that L. salivarius supernatant (LS-S) has potent antibacterial activity against ESBL-positive E. coli isolates. The mean inhibition zone was 16.0 ± 3.9 mm in the modified disk diffusion method and 18.8 ± 3.3 mm in agar well diffusion. Wider inhibition zones were observed, especially in isolates that formed moderate biofilms, suggesting that the antibacterial effect of LS-S may vary depending on the biofilm phenotype. Similarly, it has been reported in the literature that probiotic-derived supernatants form inhibition zones of various sizes in different pathogens. For example, zone diameters of 34.3 mm and 32.6 mm were recorded on E. coli by Leuconostoc citreum KCC-57 and KCC-58 strains isolated from rice plants, respectively (Muthusamy et al. 2023). In another study, LAB supernatants isolated from corn stalk silage formed zones > 8 mm in many pathogens, including E. coli (Li et al. 2015). The disappearance of the effect after protease application supported the proteinaceous (bacteriocin-like) nature of the antibacterial compounds. Furthermore, supernatants from species such as Pediococcus pentosaceus and Lactobacillus graminis have been reported to produce zones ranging from 4.33 to 17 mm (González-Pérez et al. 2019). These differences depend on the strain used, medium conditions, supernatant composition, and methodological approaches. Although the average zones of 16–19 mm obtained in our study are below the high values ​​reported in the literature (30 mm and above), significant inhibition was achieved even against clinically important ESBL-positive and biofilm-forming E. coli isolates, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of postbiotics. Soltani et al. (2022) reported that L. acidophilus and L. casei strains exhibited strong antibacterial and antibiofilm effects on antibiotic-resistant E. coli (Soltani et al. 2022). Similarly, Sharma et al. (2025) showed that Lactobacillus supernatants provided high inhibition on E. coli strains isolated from urinary tract infections (Sharma and Lee 2025). These findings support that probiotic supernatants are effective not only in vitro but also on clinically important pathogens. In addition, a study by Shahverdi et al. (2023) emphasized that L. paracasei strain exhibited strong antibacterial effects on E. coli and S. aureus both in vitro and in vivo models (Shahverdi et al. 2023). This is important evidence reinforcing the therapeutic potential of probiotics. Finally, Keeratikunakorn et al. (2023) demonstrated that cell-free supernatants obtained from Lactobacillus species suppressed many pathogens, including Gram-negative bacteria, by providing broad-spectrum inhibition (Keeratikunakorn et al. 2023). All these literature findings are consistent with the results presented in our study and highlight the importance of probiotic-derived supernatants as an alternative biotherapeutic approach against antibiotic resistance.

Our microplate assay results revealed that the L. salivarius supernatant exhibited a time-dependent antibacterial effect. In particular, low-level inhibition was observed at 3 h (around 20%), increasing to 65–70% at 6–12 h and reaching approximately 87% at 24 h. This finding suggests that LS-S does not initiate a rapid effect in suppressing bacterial growth, but rather provides an inhibition that strengthens over time. Furthermore, the lack of significant differences in growth inhibition between moderately and strongly biofilm-forming isolates suggests that the supernatant’s effect persists independently of biofilm capacity.

In the literature, inhibitory effects of probiotic supernatants have also been reported over time. For example, Jiang et al. (2023) reported that the supernatant of L. salivarius CGMCC20700 inhibited the growth of E. coli ATCC 3521 to a limited extent in the first 6 h but achieved strong suppression within 24 h of incubation. Similarly, Keeratikunakorn et al. (2023) suggested that various Lactobacillus supernatants produced significant inhibition on E. coli after 24 h, and this might be due to the combination of organic acids and proteinaceous components. Furthermore, Soltani et al. (2022) reported that L. acidophilus and L. casei supernatants exhibited an increasing suppressive effect over time in their growth curves, emphasizing that this effect might be due to the production of bacteriocins and organic acids. These findings suggest that the gradual inhibition pattern observed in our study may be related to the accumulation of probiotic-derived metabolites and the strengthening of their mechanisms of action over time.

Similar patterns of inhibition with increasing time have been reported in the existing literature. In experiments using cell-free supernatants obtained from lactic acid bacteria, the antibacterial effect observed was limited in the first hours, but inhibition gradually increased as the incubation period progressed (Drumond et al. 2023). Drumond et al. (2023) showed that supernatants of different Lactobacillus strains suppressed the growth of Gram-negative bacteria by only 15–25% in the early phase, but the inhibition level reached over 80% after 24 h of incubation. This increase was associated with the accumulation of organic acids (especially lactic acid), hydrogen peroxide, and proteinaceous compounds in the supernatant over time (Drumond et al. 2023). Similarly, genomic and phenotypic analyses performed on L. salivarius CGMCC20700 by Jiang et al. (2023) revealed that this species possesses gene clusters encoding bacteriocins and antimicrobial peptides (Jiang et al. 2023). This suggests that the observation of limited effect in the early period and the gradually increasing inhibition pattern in later hours may be related to the synthesis and release dynamics of metabolites.

In our study, LS-S suppressed biofilm formation in all ESBL-positive E. coli isolates, with over 40% inhibition achieved in 13 isolates (43.3%). The highest antibiofilm activity was observed in isolate E6, at 73.1%. These findings demonstrate that LS-S significantly inhibits not only planktonic growth but also the development of biofilm structures. Similar antibiofilm effects have been reported in the literature. Drumond et al. (2023) reported that cell-free supernatants obtained from four different Lactobacillus strains reduced Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilm formation by 40–80%, demonstrating that this effect was valid both in the initial biofilm phase (adhesion) and on previously formed biofilms (Drumond et al. 2023). This result suggests that probiotic-derived supernatants can be used not only for preventive but also for therapeutic purposes. In a review by Sharma and Lee (2025), it was stated that exopolysaccharides (EPS) produced by plant-derived Lactobacillus and Leuconostoc strains have antibiofilm activity, acting by disrupting pathogen adhesion and the integrity of the biofilm matrix (Sharma and Lee 2025, Kavitake et al. 2023).

Soltani et al. (2022) evaluated the antibiofilm activity of L. acidophilus and L. casei supernatants and reported that it significantly reduced biofilm formation in resistant E. coli strains (Soltani et al. 2022). Sharma et al. (2025) found biofilm inhibition by Lactobacillus strains secretome in E. coli strains isolated from urinary tract infections to be in the range of 35–60% and suggested that this might be related to the disruption of quorum sensing mechanisms (Sharma et al. 2025). Jaffar et al. (2016) also showed that Lactobacillus species suppress biofilm development by competing with pathogens for adhesion sites and reducing extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) synthesis. Collado and Sanz (2007) reported that some Lactobacillus strains inhibit biofilm formation through bacteriocin production.

Similarly, Srivastava et al. (2020) reported that L. plantarum supernatant reduced biofilm mass by 73% in a multi-species (S. mutans and C. albicans) biofilm model, which may be due to antimicrobial compounds (e.g., bacteriocin-like proteins or quorum sensing inhibitors) competing with the pathogen in these regions, providing updated evidence for the biofilm inhibition mechanism (Srivastava et al. 2020). In this context, the findings presented in our study indicate that the dual effect of LS-S against both planktonic cells and biofilm structure provides a scientific basis for the development of innovative postbiotic-based biotherapeutic strategies in the control of antibiotic resistance and biofilm-associated infections.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that LS-S exhibited potent antibacterial and antibiofilm effects against ESBL-positive and biofilm-forming E. coli isolates. Our findings suggest that postbiotics may not only be an alternative in combating antibiotic-resistant pathogens but also serve as potential synergistic agents when combined with antibiotics. The progressive inhibitory effect of supernatant and the suppression of biofilm formation offer significant clinical advantages. Furthermore, molecular identification of the active components, elucidation of their mechanisms of action, and validation in animal models are necessary to strengthen the translational potential of this approach. In conclusion, the obtained data suggest that LS-S is a promising biotherapeutic candidate for infection control in the era of antibiotic resistance.
