The Impostor Phenomenon1 (IP) describes self-perceptions of intellectual fraudulence despite external and/or objective feedback supporting one’s intellectual abilities [1]. Salari and colleagues [2] provided a meta-analysis of the IP among health service providers, aggregating data from 30 studies and 11,483 participants. One of their major aims was reporting the prevalence (i.e., the proportion of a population affected by a condition) of the IP. Therefore, they analyzed studies reporting prevalence rates. While we applaud the aspiration of Salari and colleagues in their work, we discuss why it is currently not suitable to interpret prevalence rates of the IP from a conceptual and methodological perspective.

Conceptual issues

Clance and Imes [3] identified the IP as a pattern of feelings, cognitions, and behaviors among their psychotherapy clients. Clance [1] clarified that the IP is not “a pathological disease” (p. 23). As noted by Salari et al. [2], the IP is not included in the International Classification of Diseases and The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. Instead, the IP is considered an individual difference variable that is best understood on a dimension from low to high expressions [1].2 Data on inter-individual variability follow an approximately normal distribution: thus, comparatively few show very low or very high IP expressions [5]. Accordingly, the literature does not conceptualize the IP as a categorical variable distinguishing between individuals who do and do not ‘have’ it; thus, attempts to estimate prevalence rates, describing how many individuals are ‘affected’, are misaligned with its theoretical framework.

When a large meta-analysis [2] shows a psychological construct like IP to affect between 62 and 70.6% of people, it might be considered a normative experience, and it demands critical scrutiny. If most people feel like a fraud, then it is probably a psychological trait, possibly tied to social comparisons, unrealistic standards, or other shared experiences. This aligns with the conceptualization of the IP being a continuous dimension and observations of its expression following a normal distribution.

Assessment-related issues

It can be argued that an assumption of a continuous dimension does not dismiss categorization and using cut-offs to distinguish between groups. For example, people differ in their fear of being laughed at (gelotophobia) on a dimension from no to high expressions, and cut-offs are available that allow the grouping of persons on a broader level (i.e., gelotophobes and non-gelotophobes) and narrower levels (e.g., no fear, borderline, elevated, extreme fear) [6]. Similarly, in medicine, biological markers such as the prostate-specific antigen range from low to high expressions, and there are cut-offs where levels are considered abnormal (e.g., ≥ 4.0 ng/mL) and indicate a potential cancer diagnosis that requires further diagnostics [7]. Accordingly, it might be feasible to translate such approaches to the IP, which is assessed through self-report questionnaires that provide individual scores. The Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS) [1] is the standard instrument and the majority (83.3%) of the studies included in Salari et al.’s [2] meta-analysis used the CIPS.

Although Clance [1] did not conceptualize the IP as a syndrome or categorical variable, she introduced cut-offs along with the CIPS that should distinguish between no, mild, moderate, and severe IP expressions. These cut-offs have rarely been used in the literature, studies having instead used dichotomization (impostors vs. non-impostors). Further approaches are summarized by Salari et al. [2]: “Some authors suggest using a score of 62 as a cutoff value on the CIPS scale, while others suggest using the mean CIPS score in the study sample to distinguish between IS and non-IS sufferers” (p. 13). This overview highlights a major flaw, namely that the location of the cut-offs is largely arbitrary and depends on the author and approach; broad empirical research supporting any of the cut-offs is missing. This is evident in the broad range of prevalence rates reported in Salari et al.’s [2] overview, with prevalences of between 27.5 and 94.0% based on CIPS scores. Heterogeneity tests show unusually high variation across studies, with I2 = 98.6, which is surprising when considering that the data stem from a comparatively homogeneous population.

As an extension of Salari et al.’s [2] finding of observed heterogeneity between data sets that are evaluated with different cut-offs, we can illustrate the effects of applying these arbitrary approaches to the same data sets. We used two comparatively large and openly available data sets (Ns = 1,124 and 670 [8]), and applied the available cut-off criteria, namely, Clance’s [1] four categories, dichotomization, the 62-points cut-off, the Halle-Wittenberg cut-off (58 points)3, and sample-based cut-offs (mean and median), respectively. Table 1 shows the prevalence rates. For Sample 1, prevalences of 11.6, 54.0, 49.4, 63.8, or 51.5% are estimated depending on the cut-off, whereas in Sample 2 8.1, 51.0, 45.5, 65.1, or 48.7% might be identified as “having IP.” Thus, the estimates depend on the choice of cut-offs. If the findings are generalized to a study population, the differences are substantial. For example, assuming a population of 83.6 million people, a change of 1% in prevalence rate translates to a further 836,000 individuals being classified as “having” the IP. Putting the conceptual issues aside, this shows that available CIPS cut-offs do not converge well, and thus pooling rates from studies using different cut-offs strongly biases the estimation and might explain Salari et al.’s observation that “The prevalence […] depends largely on the screening tools and threshold values used […]” (p. 13).

Finally, there are no robust data on the validity of the CIPS cut-offs. Without multi-methodological data from sufficiently large samples, the sensitivity and specificity, and thus the validity, are unknown. The lack of robust validity data forbids the use of cut-offs to provide estimates of (the conceptually flawed) prevalence rates of IP.

Conclusion and recommendations

Although it should not take the journal about a year to publish a short commentary, we applaud Salari and colleagues’ [2] efforts to provide an overview regarding the IP in the field of health service providers. However, we strongly recommend against the use of cut-offs and interpretations of prevalence rates regarding the IP. As outlined in this commentary, the IP is not conceptualized as a categorical construct and available cut-offs for the standard instrument (CIPS) are arbitrary and not validated. While Salari et al. note that “The most important limitation of the present study was the lack of prevalence reporting in some of the reviewed studies […]” (p. 13), we view this as encouraging for the field, as it suggests that the use of conceptually and methodologically flawed cut-offs remains the exception than the norm.

Despite our criticism of relying on cut-offs, we applaud efforts to aggregate data, as it contributes to knowledge of the field by testing the generalizability of findings. Considering the discussed issues, we recommend aligning the analytic and methodological approach with the available knowledge about IP and its assessment. We expect that relying on continuous data representing the continuum of IP experiences will contribute to the field.
