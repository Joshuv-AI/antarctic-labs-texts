Sex Differences in 90-Day Readmission and Mortality Trends in Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction: Insights From the National Readmissions Database
Authors: Usman Ahmed, Huma Hussain, Shirin Saeed, Adil Al-Karim Manji, Juan Valencia, Rayaan Yunus, Mark Robitaille, Guanqing Chen, Feroze Mahmood, Robina Matyal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesHeart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) accounts for nearly half of all heart failure hospitalizations and disproportionately affects women, who present with distinct risk profiles and pathophysiologies compared to men. Prior studies exploring sex differences have been limited by small sample sizes and have often focused on index hospitalizations. We aimed to examine sex differences in risk factors, causes of readmission, and mortality following HFpEF hospitalization using a large, nationally representative cohort.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study using the 2016–2019 National Readmissions Database. Adults hospitalized with a primary diagnosis of HFpEF were included. Patients were followed for 90-day readmissions, and multivariable logistic regression was used to identify predictors of readmission and readmission-related mortality, stratified by sex. The final sample included 353,536 patients (217,354 women and 136,182 men).ResultsWomen were older at admission, more likely to live in lower-income areas, and more often presented with uncomplicated hypertension, while men had a higher burden of ischemic heart disease. Advancing age was associated with increased risk of readmission in women. Women were more frequently readmitted with respiratory failure, diastolic heart failure, and atrial fibrillation, suggesting a greater burden of vascular stiffness and symptom severity. Chronic kidney disease and diabetes were key predictors of readmission and mortality in both sexes.ConclusionsHFpEF manifests with distinct sex-specific risk factors, clinical trajectories, and outcomes. These findings underscore the need for sex-informed, individualized treatment strategies and equitable resource allocation to reduce disparities and improve outcomes in HFpEF care.

INTRODUCTION

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is a complex syndrome driven by elevated filling pressures, accounting for 40–50% of heart failure hospitalizations.12) Despite advances in heart failure management, women comprise the majority of HFpEF cases (2:1 ratio).34) This is attributed to physiological and anatomical differences, along with a greater comorbidity burden—particularly hypertension and obesity—that contributes to age-related myocardial stiffening and left atrial enlargement.5) Conversely, HFpEF in men is often due to a higher burden of ischemic heart disease.6) Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, morbidity continues to be high in the female cohort. Given its high burden on healthcare systems, reducing hospital readmissions and improving long-term outcomes in HFpEF is a key focus of research. Sex differences in HFpEF are clinically important due to distinct pathophysiological mechanisms, comorbidity profiles, and therapeutic responses between men and women. While prior studies have explored sex differences in HFpEF outcomes, findings have been heterogeneous, limited by small sample sizes and have focused primarily on index hospitalizations.7) In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis using the 2016–2019 United States (U.S.) National Readmissions Database (NRD) to evaluate sex differences in HFpEF-related hospitalizations, readmissions, and mortality. By examining a large, nationally representative cohort, this study aims to provide insights into sex-specific HFpEF risk factors, causes of readmission, and post-hospitalization outcomes, ultimately informing more targeted management strategies for both men and women.

METHODS

Ethical statement

This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (Supplementary Table 1). Institutional Review Board approval was not required for this study as the datasets used are publicly available and utilize de-identified patient information. Analysis was completed according to the ethical criteria set by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project.

Data source

The NRD is the largest publicly available all-payer inpatient health care readmission database in the U.S. and includes discharge data for 49.3% of the non-institutionalized U.S. population. It provides sufficient data for analysis across hospital types and the study of readmission for relatively uncommon diagnoses or procedures. All patients with ≥1 inpatient hospitalization are assigned a unique linkage number allowing tracking of discharges across hospitals within a state throughout a calendar year. Each patient record in the NRD contains information on the patient’s diagnoses and procedures performed during the hospitalization based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision-Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes as well as Clinical Classification Software codes that group multiple ICD-10-CM codes for facilitated statistical analysis. Analysis using the NRD can be weighted to generate national estimates of hospital admissions in the U.S.8)

Study design

Adult male and female patients with a primary diagnosis of acute HFpEF between January 2016 and December 2019 were included. HFpEF was identified using ICD-10-CM codes I50.31 (acute diastolic heart failure) and I50.33 (acute-on-chronic diastolic heart failure), consistent with standard coding practices in administrative datasets. While definitions of HFpEF can vary, some using an ejection fraction (EF) threshold of >40% and others ≥50%, these codes are widely accepted as capturing patients with preserved EF in the absence of systolic dysfunction. Echocardiographic data were not available in the NRD; therefore, identification relied on clinician-coded diagnostic labels reflective of preserved EF at the time of hospitalization. Full details of all ICD-10-CM codes utilized for index diagnosis, baseline characteristics, and comorbidities are provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

The details of sample selection and cohort division are shown in Figure 1. Adult patients who were discharged alive during their initial hospitalization were included in the study because they were the only population at risk of readmission. Patients with hospitalizations between October and December were removed from the final sample to allow for 90-day follow-up. Patients under the age of 18 years (n=215), with missing length of stay (n=10), or with missing NRD_DaysToEvent (n=0) were excluded prior to analysis.

Study variables

Demographic and admission-specific hospital characteristics were provided as in-built variables in the NRD. These variables included age, sex (male or female), insurance status, median household income by zip code, hospital urban-rural designation, hospital teaching status, hospital bed size, elective admission, weekend admission, length of stay, and total hospitalization costs (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Comorbidities were generated as binary variables using ICD-10-CM codes in the secondary discharge diagnosis fields. A list of the clinically relevant comorbidities and their corresponding ICD-10 codes are provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

The primary outcome of interest was 90-day all-cause readmission according to the methodology described by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project. Patients are tracked across the time for readmissions using a linkage variable. Time to readmission was computed as the number of days from discharge date of index admission to readmission date.

We identified causes of readmission using ICD10-CM codes in the primary discharge diagnosis field of these readmissions. Causes of readmission were grouped into categories using the ICD 10 codes given in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS (version 29; IBM SPSS, Inc., Armonk, NY, USA). Discharge weight provided by NRD was used for all analyses to obtain national estimates. For descriptive analyses, we compared baseline patient and hospital characteristics of patients with HFpEF stratified by sex (male or female). The Shapiro-Wilk test for normality (p>0.05) was used to assess the distribution normality for continuous demographic variables. For normally distributed data, mean ± standard deviation was used to describe central tendencies, whereas median (interquartile range) was used to describe central tendencies for skewed data. Mann-Whitney U-test and independent samples t-test were utilized to assess differences in nonparametric and parametric distributions respectively. Pearson’s χ2 test was used for comparison of categorical variables.

A binary logistic multivariable regression model was used to identify risk predictors of 90-day readmission and readmission mortality for both sexes. Risk predictors included in our adjusted model are shown in Table 1. The data are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

RESULTS

Patient demographics and socioeconomic characteristics

A total of 353,536 patients with an index hospitalization for HFpEF were included, including 136,182 males (38.5%) and 217,354 females (61.5%).

Between 2016 and 2019, the frequency of weighted admissions for HFpEF decreased for both sexes. Female admissions consistently higher than males, starting at 146,000 in 2016 and decreasing to approximately 20,000 in 2019. Male admissions similarly declined from 130,000 in 2016 to around 18,000 in 2019.

Across all age groups, a greater number of females were admitted for HFpEF compared to males, with a higher median age at admission (79 years vs. 75 years, p<0.001) and a larger proportion of admissions in older age categories.

A greater proportion of females were insured by Medicare (84.3% vs. 78.0%; p<0.001), whereas males had higher rates of private insurance (10.7% vs. 7.3%; p<0.001). Females were more likely to reside in lower-income zip codes (29.8% in the 0–25th percentile vs. 28.2% for males; p<0.001).

Males had significantly higher rates of chronic kidney disease (CKD) (36.4% vs. 31.8%), tobacco use (35.8% vs. 24.6%), and prior myocardial infarction (11.3% vs. 8.6%) compared to females. They also had a markedly higher prevalence of prior coronary artery bypass grafting (17.1% vs. 8.6%; p<0.001). In contrast, females had higher rates of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (29.6% vs. 23.9%) and uncomplicated hypertension (36.5% vs. 31.3%).

Hospital-level characteristics revealed similar proportions of admissions to metropolitan teaching hospitals for both sexes. Similar lengths of hospital stay were reported for males and females (Table 2).

Causes, trends and risk predictors of 90-day readmissions

Cardiac causes were the leading contributors to 90-day readmissions for both males and females, comprising 42.8% and 42.5%, respectively. Heart failure alone accounted for over 3-quarters of these cardiac readmissions, with diastolic heart failure being the most frequent subtype, particularly among females (19.0% vs. 16.3% in males, p<0.001). Conversely, a larger proportion of males were readmitted with systolic heart failure. Atrial fibrillation (AF) or flutter was also a common cardiac cause of readmission, affecting 2.5% of males and 2.7% of females within 90 days of discharge. Pulmonary causes were the second most frequent reason for readmission, affecting 13.1% of males and 13.3% of females. Infections represented the third most common cause of readmission in 8.5% of males and 8.3% of females (Table 1).

The 90-day readmission rates showed a gradual decline from 2016 to 2019, with males consistently having higher rates than females each year. In 2016, the readmission rate was 40.1% for males and 39.6% for females, decreasing to 37.8% and 37.5%, respectively, by 2019 (Figure 2). Significant predictors of 90-day readmissions included age, socioeconomic status, and comorbid conditions. Among males, the likelihood of 90-day readmission decreased with advancing age (e.g., age >84 years: OR, 0.76, 95% CI, 0.64–0.89). In contrast, females showed an increasing trend in readmission risk with age (e.g., age >84 years: OR, 1.34, 95% CI, 1.16–1.54). Among males, the strongest predictors of readmission included COPD (OR, 1.27, 95% CI, 1.24–1.30), CKD (≥ stage 3) (OR, 1.20, 95% CI, 1.16–1.23) and diabetes with chronic complications (OR, 1.14, 95% CI, 1.11–1.17). For females, advanced age (>84 years) (OR, 1.34, 95% CI, 1.16–1.54), CKD (OR, 1.22, 95% CI, 1.19–1.25) and diabetes with chronic complications (OR, 1.20, 95% CI, 1.17–1.23) were the strongest predictors of 90-day readmission (Table 3, Figure 3).

Predictors of mortality on readmission

Two thousand two hundred sixty-nine males and 3,591 females experienced in-hospital mortality following a 90-day readmission following their index hospitalization. Among these deaths, 28.9% were due to cardiovascular causes, while 71.2% were attributed to non-cardiac causes. We also evaluated a composite outcome of in-hospital mortality during 90-day readmission and readmission due to heart failure. Overall, 19.34% of patients experienced this composite outcome, with similar rates observed between males (19.2%) and females (19.43%). Age (males: OR, 1.02, 95% CI, 1.01–1.04; females: OR, 1.03, 95% CI, 1.02–1.04) and prolonged hospital stay (males: OR, 1.04, 95% CI, 1.03–1.05; females: OR, 1.04, 95% CI, 1.04–1.05) were both significant predictors of mortality on readmission for both sexes. COPD (OR, 1.10, 95% CI, 1.01–1.20) and CKD (OR, 1.11, 95% CI, 1.01–1.23) were significant predictors of mortality on readmission for males and females, respectively. Males residing in the highest income quartile (76th–100th percentile) exhibited an increased incidence of mortality on readmission (OR, 1.18, 95% CI, 1.03–1.36) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Leveraging a large, nationally representative database, our study expands upon previous research emphasizing the predominance of HFpEF hospitalizations and readmissions among women, with advancing age and uncomplicated hypertension as major contributing factors. In contrast, men presented with HFpEF secondary to a history of coronary artery disease, demonstrating a higher incidence of readmission with systolic heart failure. These findings support the need for further investigation into sex-specific therapeutic strategies for HFpEF management.

Women exhibited a higher prevalence of uncomplicated hypertension, which may possibly contribute to the development of HFpEF, particularly when combined with age-related myocardial remodeling.910) Many of the cardiovascular changes characteristic of HFpEF appear more prominently in women than in men.1112) Women tend to develop concentric left ventricular remodeling in response to arterial hypertension.1314) These differences are likely driven by distinct physiological and anatomical factors, as well as the decline in estrogen levels after menopause.15) Data from smaller studies with a longer follow-up also allude to hypertension having a more pronounced impact on the risk of heart failure in women.916)

Increasing age is another major factor contributing to HFpEF hospitalizations in women, as evidenced by the substantial representation of older women in both clinical trials and community-based studies.1718) While age and sex are included in many existing HFpEF risk prediction models, our findings suggest that their impact may differ significantly between men and women. Our study demonstrated that advancing age (>55 years old) was significantly associated with higher readmission rates in women. Supporting this, recent data from a registry demonstrated that women with HFpEF had a higher adjusted risk of readmission compared to men, highlighting the substantial long-term morbidity burden among female patients with HFpEF.19) These findings highlights the complex interplay between aging, female sex, and vascular dysfunction—exacerbated by postmenopausal estrogen decline—in predisposing women to HFpEF.

Interestingly, we observed an inverse association between age and 90-day readmission risk in males, which appears counterintuitive given the expected increase in comorbidity with aging. However, this finding is consistent with previous literature. A post hoc analysis of the ASCEND-HF trial, which included over 7,000 patients with acute heart failure, reported that individuals under 45 years of age had the highest rates of 30-day HF readmissions.20) Similarly, Elbadawi et al.21) found lower odds of 30-day readmission in men aged ≥85 years. Such patterns may reflect survivorship bias, where older individuals represent a healthier subset, or may be influenced by reduced healthcare-seeking behavior and more conservative care strategies near the end of life. These factors may contribute to lower observed readmission rates in elderly men despite higher baseline risk.

The higher proportion of readmissions due to respiratory failure among women in our cohort suggests a greater severity of symptoms in female HFpEF patients, a trend also observed in a recent study.22) Women with HFpEF are more likely to experience pronounced symptoms such as dyspnea and exercise intolerance and are more frequently reported to have worse overall health status compared to men.323) Although women tend to have a longer life expectancy, they often endure a lower quality of life and increased morbidity due to persistent symptom burden and functional limitations.24) Furthermore, socioeconomic disparities appear to exacerbate these outcomes. Women were more likely to reside in lower-income zip codes, indicating greater financial instability and potential barriers to accessing timely and adequate healthcare.25) The association between higher income and increased mortality among males is counterintuitive and warrants further consideration. While women in our cohort were more likely to reside in lower-income zip codes, socioeconomic status may interact differently with outcomes across sexes due to differences in healthcare utilization, disease severity at presentation, or unmeasured psychosocial and behavioral factors. These factors—combined with heightened susceptibility to severe symptoms and increased risk of readmission—underscore the need for more equitable, sex-specific approaches to HFpEF management.

A greater proportion of women were readmitted for AF, which is consistent with previous research indicating worse outcomes in women with HFpEF and coexisting AF. Although AF is more prevalent in men, its clinical impact appears to be more severe in women. The TOPCAT trial, for instance, demonstrated a stronger association between AF and hospitalization risk in women with HFpEF compared to men.26) Epidemiological studies have further established a robust link between AF and HFpEF, with AF not only serving as a frequent comorbidity but also as a key predictor of HFpEF development.27) Left atrial dilation, resulting from concentric hypertrophy, and inflammation secondary to endothelial dysfunction28) are key contributors to the development of AF. Women are particularly susceptible to this pathological cascade, exhibiting a higher risk of developing AF in the setting of HFpEF and experiencing worse clinical outcomes when both conditions coexist.

Our finding that males had higher 90-day readmission rates than females, with both declining over time, aligns with some prior studies but contrasts with others reporting higher short-term risk in women. For example, a scoping review29) found that women experienced more frequent HF hospitalizations in the early period after diagnosis, though this pattern reversed over time, with men demonstrating higher long-term readmission risk. These differences may reflect variations in disease trajectory, comorbidities, and follow-up timing across studies.

While our study does not assess treatment effects directly, the findings highlight the potential value of early identification and management of high-risk patients to reduce rehospitalization and adverse outcomes in the HFpEF population, alongside implementing targeted AF management strategies, particularly for women. Women with HFpEF might benefit from early recognition of hypertension and targeted antihypertensive medications,30) whereas men may require more aggressive ischemic heart disease management. Importantly, many randomized controlled trials informing current guidelines either lack sex-stratified analyses or are underpowered to detect sex-specific differences, as trials are often designed around overall treatment effects. Inclusion criteria frequently favor younger, male participants, limiting the applicability of findings to older women with multiple comorbidities.31) These gaps in representation and analysis are especially critical considering that women account for over half of patients with HFpEF. A personalized, sex-informed approach to HFpEF therapy may help guide future research and improve understanding of sex-based differences in outcomes. This patient-specific approach should factor in sex-related differences in physiology, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, renal function, body composition, and comorbidity burden.3233) Sex-specific pathophysiological differences may contribute to the observed disparities in HFpEF outcomes. Women more frequently exhibit increased vascular stiffness, impaired ventricular-vascular coupling, and heightened inflammatory burden,34) all of which may contribute to symptom severity and increased readmission risk. These mechanisms suggest that therapies targeting endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, or arterial stiffness may be particularly beneficial in women. Our findings highlight the importance of readmission prevention strategies tailored to each sex. Given that age affects HFpEF progression and readmission risk differently in men and women, age and sex may be integrated together into clinical risk prediction models for HFpEF hospitalizations to improve patient stratification and personalized treatment.435) Healthcare resource allocation should address the higher burden of HFpEF in women by ensuring equitable access to treatment and follow-up care, particularly in the context of socioeconomic and financial barriers.

By examining a large, nationally representative cohort, this study provides insights into sex-specific HFpEF risk factors, causes of readmission, and post-hospitalization outcomes. Unlike prior studies limited to index hospitalizations or small sample sizes, this analysis captures 90-day trajectories using real-world data, enabling robust sex-stratified comparisons. It also incorporates socioeconomic and hospital-level factors, offering a more comprehensive understanding of disparities in HFpEF outcomes and guiding more targeted, equitable management strategies. Despite its strengths, this study has limitations that should be acknowledged. As a retrospective study, it cannot establish causation, only associations. The use of administrative data, while enabling analysis of a large and diverse cohort, lacks clinical granularity, including detailed echocardiographic parameters and medication adherence, which are crucial for understanding HFpEF pathophysiology and treatment efficacy. As such, we could not assess the impact of guideline-directed medical therapy on readmissions or mortality. Further research using clinical datasets or registry-linked pharmacy records is needed to evaluate treatment patterns and their relationship with outcomes in HFpEF populations. We also observed a higher frequency of HFpEF admissions in 2016 compared to subsequent years, a pattern for which no clear explanation was identified despite reviewing coding changes, healthcare policies, and treatment guidelines. This observation may reflect unmeasured factors and warrants further exploration in future studies. While several comparisons in this study reached statistical significance, some differences, particularly those with small absolute effect sizes, may not be clinically meaningful. The large sample size enhances statistical power but can also yield significant p values for differences of limited practical relevance. Therefore, findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution, and emphasis should be placed on effect sizes, clinical context, and consistency with existing literature. Additionally, the exclusion of out-of-hospital deaths may underestimate the true mortality burden associated with HFpEF. Future research employing large population prospective designs with long follow-up and incorporating detailed clinical, behavioral, and socioeconomic data will better elucidate the mechanisms driving these observed disparities. Ultimately, a targeted approach that accounts for sex differences in age-related pathophysiological changes will be essential in advancing personalized care and improving outcomes for patients with HFpEF.

In this large, nationally representative analysis of patients hospitalized with HFpEF, we observed notable sex-based differences in clinical characteristics, causes of readmission, and predictors of post-discharge outcomes. Women were more frequently readmitted with diastolic heart failure and pulmonary conditions, while men had higher rates of ischemic heart disease and systolic heart failure. Although overall 90-day readmission rates were similar between sexes, important distinctions emerged in readmission causes and risk factors. Additionally, over 19% of patients experienced the composite outcome of heart failure–related readmission or in-hospital mortality during readmission. These findings emphasize the need for personalized, sex-informed approaches to managing HFpEF and reducing rehospitalization risk.
