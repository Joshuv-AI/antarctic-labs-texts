Accurate Prediction of the Proppant Distribution in a Hydraulically Fractured Stage
Authors: Shabeeb Alajmei
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
One of the greatest challenges in stimulating a single hydraulic fracturing stage with multiple clusters is ensuring that the proppant is equally distributed across all clusters. In this paper, the Buckingham pi theorem was applied to implement a dimensional analysis to establish an empirical correlation. The experimental correlation was developed by acquiring and integrating the data of various independent variables, such as different proppant characteristics (i.e., size, density, and concentration), using a wide range of internal diameters of the horizontal wellbore and multiple perforation configurations, and utilizing many carrier fluids with different viscosities. This work presents a newly enhanced experimental correlation for the distribution of proppants by incorporating the effect of gravity on proppant particles. The correlation proved its reliability in forecasting the proppant distribution with an average percentage error of less than 10%. The developed correlation has the potential to serve as a tool for forecasting proppant distributions among multiple clusters in multistage hydraulic fracturing treatments in the field.

Introduction

The fracture conductivity is highly dependent on proppant placement inside the induced hydraulic fracture. Therefore, the enhancement of hydrocarbon production from stimulated reservoirs is directly linked to proppant placement. This can be achieved by evenly distributing the proppant between different perforation clusters within a single hydraulic fracturing stage. According to Miller et al.1 only 70% of completed perforations in unconventional formations account for the majority of production, whereas one-third of these perforations do not make any significant contribution to production. A comprehensive understanding of proppant distribution in a horizontal wellbore during hydraulic fracturing stimulation is essential for optimal production.

To address this issue, many studies have been conducted experimentally and numerically by investigating the transport and distribution behavior of the proppant in a horizontal wellbore and after it reaches the initiated fractures. It is crucial to understand the proppant placement inside the initiated hydraulic fracture. Therefore, many laboratory experiments have been conducted to simulate induced fractures and study the proppant behavior inside these fractures. The design of these experiments varied from a single fracture by Woodworth and Miskimins2 to complex fracture designs3–7 including a large-scale experiment by Brannon et al.8 Additionally, numerical simulation studies were conducted to understand the proppant transport and settling behavior inside the created fracture.9,10

Furthermore, the proppant distribution between multiple clusters inside the horizontal wellbore is no less important than that of the induced fracture. Consequently, several experimental and numerical investigations have been conducted on proppant transport and distribution among various perforation clusters. Crespo et al.11 experimentally investigated the distribution of proppants using a large-scale horizontal wellbore. The used apparatus comprises a 100 feet-long horizontal wellbore with an internal diameter of 4 in. The wellbore was completed with three perforation clusters separated equally by 15 ft. The outcome of this research was modeled using CFD, and several simulation iterations were considered by examining different injection rates, various proppant properties, and multiple fluid viscosities.12 Furthermore, the proppant distribution was investigated experimentally with smaller-scale horizontal wellbores with different pipe diameters and perforation designs.13–17 Zhang and Dunn-Norman18 analyzed the impact of altering the number of the perforation in a single cluster and its orientation on the proppant distribution.

Several crucial variables influence the transport and distribution of proppants within a horizontal wellbore. The parameters include the properties of the horizontal wellbore (i.e., internal diameter), the viscosity of the carrier fluid, the injection rate of the proppant-laden slurry, perforation design (specifically, the number of shots per foot and orientation), and proppant properties (specifically, density, concentration, and size). A proppant distribution term (PD) was established to evaluate the distribution of proppants among numerous perforation clusters. This investigation provides a technical definition of PD as the percentage variation between the supplied proppant concentration and the standard deviation of the proppant concentration determined at various cluster locations. As the standard deviation decreases, the PD improves and reaches a value of 100 for a uniform distribution among the numerous perforation clusters (i.e., optimal distribution).13,19 Alajmei19 proposed an experimental correlation for limited-entry perforation designs. However, this study includes a correlation that is suitable for predicting the PD of different perforation configurations, such as 3, 4, and 6 SPF configurations. In addition, the experimental correlation derived previously from limited-entry perforations does not consider the effect of gravity on proppant particles.

The present research offers an enhanced empirical correlation by incorporating the influence of gravity on proppant transport through a horizontal wellbore. The modeling of proppant behavior is accomplished through the utilization of various designs of perforation configurations, thereby broadening the range of the previously existing correlation.19 This optimization is achieved by considering various variables, including the horizontal wellbore internal diameter, carrier fluid viscosity, perforation orientation and configurations (i.e., number of shots per foot), slurry injection rate, and properties of the injected proppant. The current research demonstrates an experimental correlation that enhances the optimization of proppant placement and distribution for hydraulic fracturing applications.

Experimental Setup

The present study involved gathering data from previous research in the literature13,17,20 for the purpose of enhancing and strengthening the experimental correlation. The principal objective of these experimental tests is to evaluate the movement and distribution of proppants through different perforation arrangements.

The core component in all conducted tests is a 30 feet-long, transparent horizontal pipe that contains three separate sets of perforations.16 The perforation clusters exhibit a range of perforation designs, spanning from 1 to 6 SPF, and feature multiple phasing designs that vary from 0 to 180°. Each cluster is 1 foot in length, and its perforation diameter is designed to be 0.25 in. Two distinct horizontal wellbores with different internal diameters were utilized to investigate the distribution of proppant, specifically 1.5 and 2.5 in.

A tribladed propeller is employed for mixing the proppant and water within a 200 gallon container, as shown in Figure 1. Following a mixing period, a centrifugal pump is utilized to introduce the proppant-laden slurry into a horizontal wellbore. A variable frequency drive is used to regulate the operation of the pump. Furthermore, the pressure within the pipe was continuously monitored through the installation of various pressure gauges located prior to different perforation clusters. The determination of the slurry injection rate was achieved by installing a flow meter in the pipeline subsequent to the pump.

Correlation Development and Methodology

By gathering the experimental data in the literature and then applying the effect of gravity on the PDs in a horizontal wellbore, the correlation was established. Several parameters, such as the type, size, and concentration of the proppant, fluid viscosity, injection rate, and number of perforations and orientations, were used as independent variables in order to establish this relationship. The experimental correlation was developed using the data from a variety of different types of proppants, such as sand, which has a specific gravity of 2.65, and two types of ultralight weight (ULW) ceramics, which have specific gravities of 1.054 and 2.0. The particle sizes of the proppant used range from 100 mesh sand to 14/40 mesh, which are used with various proppant concentrations ranging from 0.07 to 4.17 ppg. This correlation utilized carrier fluid viscosities between 1 (fresh water) and 17 cP (water with different percentages of friction reducer). However, carrier fluids with friction reducers are considered shear-thinning non-Newtonian fluids. This implies that the apparent viscosity of the carrier fluids will be decreased due to the high shear during the fluid injection. The injection rates are between 0.001 and 0.005 m3/s.

The relationship is determined by studying how different variables influence the forecast of PD across several perforation clusters in a single hydraulic fracturing stage. Using the pi theorem developed by Buckingham,21 it was discovered that dimensional analysis is an effective technique for developing the correlation between a number of independent variables in order to predict the dependent variable (i.e., PD).

Independent variables were identified as the horizontal wellbore internal diameter, the injection rate of the slurry, the injected proppant characteristics (i.e., injected concentration, specific gravity, and the average diameter), the carrier fluid viscosity, and the perforation configurations (i.e., number of shots per foot and orientation). The viscosity of the fluid, the diameter of the pipe, and the gravity were chosen as the repeated variables that would be used in the process of developing the correlation. On the nonrepeating variables that were used were the other independent variables such as the injection rate, proppant’s characteristics (i.e., size, specific gravity, and injected concentration), number of holes open to flow, and the perforation’s alignment.

Despite the intuitive belief that increasing the viscosity of the fluid would result in a more uniform PD, this may not be the case. Rather, it is imperative to attain an optimal combination of numerous independent variables. The distribution of the proppant is subject to variation based on the interaction of many independent factors. The size and density of the proppants as well as the hole design have a significant impact on the PD. Larger proppant particles lead to higher settling at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore. This results in a poor distribution of the proppant by concentrating most of the injected proppant around the heel clusters. Enhancing the injection rate could potentially mitigate the impact of the gravity. As the injection rate increased, more turbulence associated with the high injection rate suspended the proppant and transported it further into the wellbore. This lessens the effect of gravity acting on the proppant and reduces the proppant settling at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore.

The experimental correlation indicated in eq 12 was derived from a comprehensive analysis of all combined data available in the literature. The established correlation integrates all variables that impact the PD, including acceleration due to gravity (g), injected concentration of the proppant (Cp), proppant diameter (Dp), density (ρp), injection rate (q), horizontal wellbore diameter (ID), carrier fluid viscosity (μ), and perforation design, which includes the number of perforations (N) and the arc length between the perforations (s).12

Discussion of the Results

The data predicted using eq 12 are plotted and compared to the actual PD computed using eq 3. The PD data are presented as percentages on the y axis versus the type of carrier fluid used in the case of sand experiments or the proppant size in the case of ULW ceramic, as well as the type of completed perforation on the x axis. Figure 2 shows the PD prediction of the 100 mesh sand at different injected proppant concentrations and various injection rates using fresh water as the carrier fluid. Overall, the predicted correlation matches the actual distribution of the proppant using the measured data in the laboratory. The correlation, in certain scenarios, resulted in an overestimation of the PD. This was particularly observed when the horizontal wellbore was completed with a single perforation per cluster and the perforation was placed at the lower side of the well. Additionally, this overestimation occurred when the injected proppant concentration ranged between 1 and 1.5 ppg. In contrast, the correlation underestimated the PD of the 100 mesh sand when completing the horizontal wellbore using 4 SPF and pumping the sand at lower injected proppant concentrations below 1 ppg at a high injection rate of 70 gpm. Furthermore, the predicted correlation underestimates the PD when completing the horizontal wellbore with 6 SPF and pumping the sand at a lower injection rate of 54 gpm. The correlation was able to accurately forecast the PD of the 100 mesh sand with a low average percentage error of 8% under different completion configurations while utilizing fresh water as the carrier fluid.

The graph depicted in Figure 3 illustrates the projected distribution of the 40/70 mesh sand, as influenced by varying concentrations of the injected proppant and different injection rates. The correlation exhibited a high degree of accuracy in predicting the PD of 40/70 mesh sand with an average percentage error of 10% across various completion configurations. In addition, the majority of experiments utilized fresh water as the carrier fluid, with the exception of a few tests that were conducted utilizing the 4 SPF completion design. The correlation yielded an underestimation of the PD. This condition was especially highlighted in cases where the horizontal wellbore was completed using a single-point entry and the perforation was located at the bottom of the well. On the contrary, completing the horizontal wellbore using 3 SPF resulted in an underestimation of the PD of the 40/70 mesh sand.

The correlation developed in this study demonstrated the capability of predicting the PD behavior across various perforation clusters in a horizontal wellbore. This is evidenced by the low percentage errors observed when predicting different types and sizes of proppants under varying injection parameters (i.e., proppant concentration and rate) using different viscosities of the carrier fluid.

Application of the Model

The proposed model helps optimize the PD in the laboratory. The experimental correlation can also be used as a foundation for predicting the PD in the field. Accurate prediction of PD leads to successful stimulation treatments. Therefore, the injected proppant will be placed into the induced fractures to sustain its productivity for production enhancement. In addition, the predictive model is cost-effective. In lieu of injecting the hydraulic fracturing treatment at high injection rates and accumulating proppants near the toe clusters, the treatment parameters should be optimized by using a predictive model to ensure a uniform PD.

Conclusions

Based on the established PD correlation, the following conclusions were drawn.1.The newly developed correlation was established by analyzing the experimental data available in the literature on PDs using a horizontal wellbore.2.The experimental correlation was generated using Buckingham’s pi theorem, and the influence of gravity on the PD across different perforation clusters was incorporated.3.The established correlation exhibits a high degree of accuracy, as demonstrated by very low percentage errors of less than 10% between the predicted and measured PD values.4.The resulting experimental correlation can be further used to develop a field correlation for forecasting the PD over a range of field completions.
