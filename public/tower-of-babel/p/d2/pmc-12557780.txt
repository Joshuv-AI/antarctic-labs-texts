Droplet outbursts from onion cutting
Authors: Zixuan Wu, Alireza Hooshanginejad, Weilun Wang, Chung-Yuen Hui, Sunghwan Jung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceTear induction during onion cutting has long vexed the culinary community. Although science has attributed the cause to enzymatic sulfur compounds, a mechanistic view of how pungent aerosols are sprayed out is lacking. Using high-speed visuals, we found that an onion does not release tear-triggering fluid in one burst. Instead, the blade first punches out a layer of high-speed mist, then slower fluids snap into drops. When the blade is blunter and faster, droplet number and launch energy increase sharply. The finding explains culinary intuition and suggests a practical fix: Keep knives sharp and cut gently to spray less, with implications for reducing kitchen splashing and preventing the spread of food-borne diseases via droplets and aerosols.

Onion has been an essential agricultural product on the kitchen and medicinal tables since the ancient Egyptian and Sumerian ages in 3000 B.C., or even earlier (1, 2). Ancient Egyptians worshiped onions believing that the concentric circles within symbolized eternity, and often placed them in the burial tomb of pharaohs (1–3). However, throughout the span of culinary history, users have long been troubled by the lacrymatory effects induced by onion cutting and slicing, as Shakespeare described the discomfort in Antony and Cleopatra: “Indeed the tears live in an onion that should water this sorrow.” When an onion is cut, the enzymatic reaction turns the sulfur compounds to propanethial S-oxide upon onion tissues rupture (4–6). When propanethial S-oxide reaches human eyes, the chemical activates the ciliary nerves in the cornea and causes tears (6). Kohman first suggested that in addition to such chemicals being volatile, tiny hovering droplets containing them during cutting may be the true culprit, though evidence was lacking at his time (7). However, there have not been any mechanistic reviews of droplet ejection in cutting onions supported by experimentation and visualizations.

Coupling of fluids to elasticity in complex soft composites has shown rich emergent dynamics (8–15). This applies also to fruits and vegetables. Smith et al. elucidated that microjet formations from the exocarps of citrus fruits depend on the mechanical properties of the soft shell (16). Box et al. have shown that even a simple protective layer has rendered nuanced stiffness changes for fruits (17). As biomimetic, soft material systems gain popularity for their often biocompatibility, adaptability, and complex functionality (18–20), understanding how soft composites fracture and liquid inclusion exits pressurized complex soft composites can facilitate engineering designs.

Beyond the scope of lachrymatory reduction and engineering concerns, onion cutting represents a common practice that lends well to the study of how kitchen cuts generate pathogenic splashes and contribute to the spreading of food-borne diseases. Atomized droplets are perfect carriers of viruses and bacteria, and there are many recent engineering designs aiming at reducing pathogenic splashes as the COVID pandemic has reasserted this notion (21–25). In the United States alone, there are 76 million cases of food-borne diseases annually, most notably food poisoning by bacterial agents like Salmonella and Campylobacter (26). Malpractices in food preparation such as inadequate cutting board cleaning and the splashing of pathogen-carrying droplets are thought to play a major role as well (27–29). Does the cutting conditions in processing raw foods, such as speed and blade sharpness, matter in spawning such infected droplets?

In this work, we tackled the above inquiries by using an experimental approach to investigate the combined mechanics of fluid fragmentation and soft tissue fracture at high spatial and temporal resolution, for the practice of onion cutting.

Experiments

We built a guillotine-style setup consisting of a vertical slider with a blade attached at the bottom to control the impact conditions between an onion hemisphere and a thin steel blade. The blade was released from a height H to achieve free-fall cutting as shown in Fig. 1A. The blade had a high aspect ratio, Lb/Wb≫1, where Lb and Wb are the length (on the order of 200 mm) and height of the blade, respectively. The height Wb was 5 mm to enable visualizations near the onion surface. The thickness of the blade is noted as Tb as shown in the cross-section in Fig. 1A, on the order of 1 to 2 mm. It illustrates the geometry of the cutting front, characterized by the tip width rb and the wedge angle α, rather than showing the entire cross-section of the blade. The blade sharpness was characterized by the edge width rb, measured using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), while other geometric parameters, including the wedge angle (α≈ 8 to 10°), were kept constant. The y^ axis is defined along the onion’s pole direction, i.e., from root to shoot. A cutting schematic is shown in Fig. 1B, where key variables are defined. The primary observational plane corresponds to the yz projections of the rupture. Droplet diameter/radius and velocity are denoted as Dd, Rd, and Vd, respectively. To minimize the yz projection error in the droplet speed, the depth of field was limited to 1 mm. For analysis, we approximate Vd using Vd,z, the yz projection of the velocity. Error estimation procedures are described in Materials and Methods. High-speed videos at 5,000 to 20,000 frames per second (fps) show that droplets are ejected from the fracture site immediately upon onion rupture, with an initial velocity of 1.0 to 40 m/s. This ejection rapidly fragments into hundreds of satellite droplets within 0 to 20 ms as shown in Fig. 1C. The full sequence is available in SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Movie S1.

As shown in Fig. 1C, the ejected liquid emerges as either distinct droplets or ligaments, followed by successive breakup processes. By analyzing the ejected liquid across all frames, we extract temporal data on the count and volume of both ligaments and droplets. Fig. 1D shows the ejection of accumulated drops and ligaments over time, revealing two distinct stages of droplet formation. The first stage is the initial violent rupture, akin to industrial atomization, in which the fluid stream is rapidly disintegrated into fine droplets (30). This stage exhibits a high drop generation rate of 11.5 ± 0.3 mm3/ms. The second stage results from ligament fragmentation, which begins with a gradual decay in ligament counts and volume as they break apart and get converted into droplets, leading to a lower but steady drop generation rate of 4.8 ± 0.1 mm3/ms.

Hydrodynamics instabilities during primary breakup from atomization to wind-induced fragmentation are observed, which is driven by interactions among capillary, inertial, and aerodynamic forces (31, 32). The onion juice is measured to be a shear thickening fluid in the typical cutting speed regime, with viscosity μd at 0.002 to 0.005 Pa·s, surface tension γd≃52.5 mN/m. Due to the high cellular water content, density ρd is approximated at ρd≃ρd, water=1,000 kg/m3. A detailed discussion of breakup processes and onion juice properties is provided in SI Appendix, section 1 and Fig. S2.

To analyze droplet trajectories and statistics, we used a custom particle tracking velocimetry tool (PTV), suitable for detecting small droplets and developing cross-frame linkages. Implementation details are provided in SI Appendix, section 2 and Algorithms 1 and 2. Tracking results are shown in Fig. 1E, where the ith droplet is linked to the jth trajectory, as labeled for a cutting sequence at t=7.5 to 15.9 ms. Straight linkages across multiple frames indicate reliable track reconstruction; see SI Appendix, Fig. S5 and Movie S2 for the full sequence. However, the current tracking scheme is still prone to errors at higher droplet densities and speeds during early-stage ejections.

With reliable PTV tracking and a clear understanding of ejected drop dynamics, we turn to examine the influence of two cutting parameters: cutting speed Ub and blade sharpness rb.

Statistics and Energetics

The overall ejection pattern is predominantly confined to a single plane, oriented nearly parallel to the blade’s penetration plane in the z^ direction as expected since onion is compressed vertically. The probability density function (PDF) of ejection angles θ as defined in Fig. 1B, is shown in Fig. 2A and consistently peaks around 10°. This slight deviation from a purely vertical release is mostly due to the wedge angle of the blade at approximately 8°. Mann–Whitney U tests are conducted to assess shifts in highly skewed distributions. No statistically significant differences in median ejection angle at the 5% significance level are found across different rb, Ub, or cutting orientations. Blade sharpness is the only parameter that causes a statistically significant shift in the ejection angle with P=0.017, possibly due to the sharpening process slightly altering the wedge condition. Details on the sharpening procedure are explained in Materials and Methods.

Pressure within the onion layer during blade penetration dictates the speed of rupture according to the scaling relation Pe∼ρdV02/2 (16, 35). This approximation assumes that the internal pressure does not significantly relax at the instant of fracture (see SI Appendix, section 1 for details). Fig. 2B shows that V0 decays with ejection time Te with a power law with exponent n=−1, regardless of the parameters. This decay profile over two orders of magnitude implies that fluid originating from deeper within the tissue experiences higher dissipation before reaching the surface. As a result, the fastest droplets are ejected during the initial rupture phase, posing the greatest risk of reaching the cutter’s eyes.

Fig. 2 C and D displays the PDFs for the ejected droplet diameter (Dd) and initial velocity (V0). To model these data, we used fits based on the Gamma distribution, chosen for reasons detailed in SI Appendix, section 1. Since some datasets showed a clear secondary peak around Dd≈ 0.4 mm after a primary peak at around 0.2 mm, a bimodal distribution was applied. Otherwise, a single Gamma distribution was used. The general form of the distribution for a variable x is[2]P(x=Dd,V0)=1baΓ(a)xa−1e−x/b.

The number of ejected droplets increases approximately fourfold as the cutting speed Ub increases from 0.44 to 2.03 m/s, and up to forty-fold as the blade sharpness rb increases from 0.91 to 13.3 μm, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S6. The fitting parameters for the bimodal distributions change in the ranges of a1=5 to 20 and b1=0.0070 to 0.033 mm for the primary peak. Mann–Whitney U tests for a no-difference null hypothesis shows statistically significant droplet sizes increments with increasing cutting speeds, with P≤0.015 comparing low to high cutting speeds.

However, the statistical results for Vd variations are less clear, which suggests that the pressurization and rupture in soft tissue are more dependent on material and blade properties. Blade edge width rb, on the other hand, shows a statistically significant positive correlation with both droplet size and speed. Compared to the sharpest blade (rb=0.91μm), P-value is from 0.1 to 0.2 for similarly sharp blades, showing no significant difference. However, for blades with rb>7μm, P-value drops below 0.01, which indicates a statistically significant increase in droplet size. Similarly, Vd scales positively with increasing rb with the P-value consistently below 0.01 compared with the sharpest blade.

The bimodal distributions of the droplet size is consistent with other fluid fragmentation processes where breakup of different fluid structures such as jetted ligaments contribute separate peaks to the final droplet distribution (36–38). This bimodal distribution with thick tails and occasional secondary peaks is more pronounced when using duller blades or higher cutting speeds, as these conditions enhance the formation of unstable ligaments. This is analogous to bag breakups, where distinct parts of the disintegrating fluid sheet (e.g. rim, nodes, bag structures) each contribute their own droplet distributions (37, 39). Here, the rupturing topology differs from that of a bag, and unstable ligament strands primarily constitute the secondary peak, while initial outbursts make up the primary. More detailed discussion is included in SI Appendix, section 1.

Velocity distributions, on the other hand, do not follow a previously reported Gaussian distribution for unsteady sheet fragmentation (40). The breakup dynamics here exhibit signatures of first and second wind-induced instabilities with violent shearing of the liquid film and highly irregular ligament shapes, which differ remarkably from the scenario of an impinging drop expansion with regular rim pinch-off (40).

To understand energy balance in the cutting process, we conduct energy analysis by dividing the blade’s input energy into the energy spent on cutting (fracture and dissipation) and the energy associated with drop ejections as Eb=mbUb2/2=Ecut+Ed,total, where Ed,total=∑mdVd2/2 represents the total kinetic energy of the ejected droplets. The average droplet energy, ⟨Ed⟩, is the total kinetic energy divided by the number of the ejected droplets. The averaged droplet energy positively correlates with the blade speed, as shown in Fig. 2E. The Inset also reveals a similar monotonic increase in Ed,total. Time-based color coding further suggests that the average drop energy is highest immediately following ejection and decays over time. Assuming an equal partitioning between cutting and drop ejection, one expects Ed,total∼Ub2. However, the creation of surfaces and energy dissipation in the onion tissue both depend on cutting speed, due to the viscoelastic nature of vegetables and fruits (41, 42). As a result, we observe a sublinear scaling of droplet energy with increasing Ub, reflecting the nonlinear increment of Ecut with cutting speed Ub.

As rb increases, the droplet energy increases monotonically, as shown in Fig. 2F. In particular, the total droplet energy Ed,total scales with rb superlinearly, mostly due to the increase in ejected volume, expected from the scaling relation of Ed,total∼rplastic3, where rplastic denotes the plastic fracture zone as the blade passes through the onion, which itself increases with the size of the blade rb.

However, the average Vd also increases with larger rb. The pressurization beneath the blade should inversely scale with rb instead, due to the reduced stress concentration and contact pressure. Nonetheless, Fig. 2 D and F show that droplet diameter Dd, velocity Vd, and average energy ⟨Ed⟩ all increase with rb, especially during the early ejection stage (Te< 1 ms) of the outermost layer of the onion. To elucidate blade–onion interactions, the following sections focus on how increasing rb affects tissue compression and model subsequent pressurization during the early stage of blade–onion contact. In this early stage, droplets experience minimal dissipation and have the highest potential for spreading.

Pressurization Mechanics

In this section, we elucidate and model how heterogeneous pressure builds up inside the onion composite during rupture with a focus on the first-layer compression of soft onion tissue. The compression mechanism can be approximated with a flat-punch indentation model, in which the narrow blade exhibits a vertical load along the z^ direction on the onion surface, treated as a semi-infinite flat body (43). The size of the resulting stress zone, i.e., the compressed region in the blade–onion contact, is denoted as L∗, with L∗≈0.1Lo≈0.3 mm, where Lo is the thickness of a single onion layer. Since L∗ is much smaller than both the onion’s radius of curvature and the blade length (Lb≃100 mm), the problem reduces effectively to a two-dimensional xz plane strain condition.

The key difference from a classic flat punch problem lies in the onion layer’s sandwiched structure, where a tough epidermis (onion skin) encases a much softer mesophyll interior. Upon compression, a stress zone develops within the mesophyll, where most droplets generate from. The epidermis, however, would not fracture until the tensile stress reaches its critical value σc for skin. At that moment, the mesophyll sustains high pressure that exceeds its own fracture stress, which in turn is relieved by rupture. The Upper panel schematics in Fig. 3A illustrate this process, with the corresponding visualization in the Lower panel. From the moment of contact tcontact, the onion skin undergoes significant displacement, stretching the epidermis, and storing energy into the underlying mesophyll. Just before rupture, the blade slows down as it deforms the skin, followed by a rapid breakthrough once the epidermis fails. This slow-down and spring-forward motion of the blade tip is evident in SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and in Movie S3. The onion layer is also observed to immediately rebound back after rupture, a signature of elastic energy release. Microscope images are included in the Upper panel Insets, demonstrating differences between mesophyll and epidermis. The epidermis exhibits aligned, elongated cell structures, in contrast to the more isotropic mesophyll. It is crucial to note that the skin here refers strictly to the thin epidermis of each layer, not the brown outermost dry layer that is commonly removed before kitchen cutting.

Instron testing with a cylindrical flat punch reveals that the peak force required to fracture skin-on onion samples is typically two to three times higher than that for skin-off samples, as shown in Fig. 3B. This indicates that more energy is needed to fracture the skin-on samples, confirming the mechanical reinforcement provided by the thin outer epidermis layer. We further note that the onset of fracture on the skin-on samples is clearly aligned with the peak force, whereas the skin-off samples fracture intermittently as the force increases in discrete steps. By examining the relatively linear portion of the F−δ curve, the elastic modulus E can be estimated as E=F(1−ν2)/(2aδ) (44), where a is the indentor/blade size and ν the Poisson ratio. This further demonstrates that skin-on samples exhibit a significantly higher elastic modulus of approximately 2.2 ± 0.36 MPa compared to 1.4 ± 0.3 MPa for skin-off samples, validating the role of the skin/epidermis in enhancing material stiffness. The measured values are consistent with the previously reported range of 2.0 to 5.4 MPa (skin-on samples) (45). The evidence supports that, at the moment of epidermal rupture and pressure release, the underlying mesophyll interior is sustaining internal stresses exceeding its fracture threshold.

Using digital image correlation (DIC), we further extract strain distributions on the xz cross-section to visualize stress concentrations beneath and around the blade tip. A horizontal profile across the ϵz field (Fig. 3C) shows a localized stress zone approximately 0.3 Lo in width. Time-lapse data show the progressive growth of ϵz up to the moment of fracture at t=1.75 ms. Fig. 3D–F present the time-lapse maps of Lagrangian strains ϵz, ϵxz, and ϵx on the xz plane, respectively. In Fig. 3D, the regions with positive z^ tensile strain appear directly above the negatively compressive strain zone in t=5.00 to 6.75 ms, indicating that central compression due to the blade induces stretching in the surrounding regions. Fig. 3E shows the development of two oppositely signed shear strain regions on either side of the cutting direction as expected. Fig. 3F shows that red regions in ϵx show horizontal stretching within the stress zone due to compression, which suggests a Poisson ratio of ν=−ϵx/ϵz=0.3 to 0.4, consistent with previously reported 0.26 to 0.45 (45). The complete DIC movie is available in Movie S4. An unexpected region of negative ϵx appears directly beneath the blade, overlapping with the negative ϵz region. This indicates that the material directly below the blade tip experiences collapse in both vertical and horizontal directions, i.e., a signature of prematurely ruptured tissues prior to skin fracture. It causes some errors that limit the rigor in DIC analysis. For details on the stress zone analysis via DIC, see SI Appendix, section 3 and Fig. S8.

Connecting the Initial Droplet Velocity to Blade Sharpness

In this section, we develop a simplified model to relate the blade tip width rb, the spatial average compressive strain in the vertical direction ϵ, and initial droplet velocity V0. At the moment of rupture, the critical fracture stress and force on the skin can be written as σc,skin∝Fc/rbLc assuming linearity. This indicates Fc∝σc,skinrbLc as illustrated in Fig. 4A, where Lc is the blade–tissue contact length in the y^ direction. However, Instron data in Fig. 4B show a power law of Fc∼rbl with l≃ 0.82 indicated by the red curve. This deviation suggests that the force–width relationship is more complex than the linear model predicts. In particular, the pressurization within the stress zone likely exceeds the mesophyll’s fracture stress, but the pressure distribution is not known a priori and differs from the material critical fracture stress σc,skin at the blade tip. This distribution is highly dependent on rb and directly determines the initial velocity of ejected droplets. Due to the difficulty in resolving the full pressure profile, we approximate the average strain in the hemispherical stress zone as ϵc∝δc/L∗, where δc is the critical indentation depth at rupture, which can be extracted from experiments (see extraction details in SI Appendix, section 3 and Fig. S8), and L∗ is the characteristic length of the stress zone.

The relationship between blade tip width rb and critical indentation depth δc is then extracted empirically. As expected, the linearity assumption does not hold for plastic deformation of cellular materials at fracture. Fig. 4C shows the force–displacement F-δ relationships from quasi-static (∼10−5 m/s) to typical cutting speeds (10−1 to 100 m/s), using both needle (N) and blade (B) indentors. The data normalized by values at the moment of fracture show a power-law relationship of F¯∼δ¯m, where overbars denote normalized variables and the exponent m ranges from 1 to 3/2. This scaling leads to δc∼Fc1/m∼rbl/m with l/m≃0.53 to 0.82. Fig. 4D presents the measured indentation depth δc as a function of rb, confirming the deeper indentation before fracture for larger blade widths. The parametric relationship is validated in Fig. 4E, showing strong agreement with the experimental data.

Given ϵc∼rbl/m, one can establish an expression for the cellular pressure Pcell. A superlinear relationship between average compressive strain and Pcell is suggested as ϵn∼Pcell with n≃ 1.9 to 2.3 (46) for vegetable tissues. Combining these relations, we express the pressure at fracture as Pcell∼rbnl/m∼ρV2, and the corresponding initial droplet velocity as V0∼rbnl/2m with nl/2m≃0.52 to 0.94. By singling out the very early-stage data (i.e., droplets ejected within the first 0.5 ms), we extracted experimental values that align more closely with the lower end of this predicted range, as shown in Fig. 4F.

Discussion

In this work, we investigated the mechanism of droplet generation during onion cutting under different conditions, primarily cutting speed and blade sharpness. Using our custom-developed particle visualization and tracking techniques, we revealed that onion cutting with unsharpened blades generates liquid droplets through a two-step process: an initial outburst driven by internal pressurization, followed by a slower fragmentation of ligaments in air. The emerging onion droplets are concentrated within a nearly vertical plane, tilted approximately 8° from the blade surface. Exit velocities decay over time, reflecting the increased resistance encountered by fluid originating from deeper onion layers. Both blade cutting speed Ub and blade tip width rb effectively increase the number and average energy of ejected droplets.

Focusing on droplet ejection from the first onion layer, which exhibits the higher initial velocities, we developed high-speed DIC procedures to extract the temporal evolution of the cross-sectional strain maps during onion cutting. These maps reveal the formation of a localized stress zone immediately beneath the blade tip. The strain maps also suggest a shielding effect made by the tough onion epidermis that allows the softer mesophyll beneath to accumulate excessive stress before rupture. This effect enables blunter blades to indent deeper, causing greater pressurization before fracture. A scaling analysis based on this mechanism explains well the observation of the early-stage droplet velocity Vd increasing with blade tip width rb, following the relationship V0∼rbnl/2m with nl/2m=0.52 to 0.94. Finally, we developed a simplified model representing the onion composite layer as a membrane supported by a spring foundation. Numerical solutions with empirical boundary conditions predict the fracture forces across different blade widths rb, with results in great agreement with independently measured Instron data.

We also evaluated common strategies for reducing droplet ejection using blunter blades with rb≈10 to 12 μm, along with the PTV and indentation profile techniques in order to assess the effects of cutting orientation and temperature. However, these effects were difficult to isolate due to substantial sample variations. Both cutting orientations produced similar droplet velocities, normalized by the mean speed from pole-to-pole condition as Vd¯=Vd/⟨Vd,P⟩. The median Vd¯ from equator cuts was slightly higher than that from pole-to-pole cuts. The critical indentation profiles were almost identical. These findings are summarized in SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B. Previous studies have shown that indenting plant tissues along the longitudinal cell direction (pole direction in onion epidermis) requires lower fracture energy than transverse directions (48, 49), which may explain the marginal increase in droplet speed from equator cuts. However, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.6 for Vd¯ from Kolmogorov–Smirnov test).

A common perception is that chilling onions prior to cutting reduces enzymatic activity and associated irritation (50). To investigate whether cooling also alters the mechanical response during cutting, we refrigerated onions at 1 °C for 12 h and compared their cuts with room-temperature samples. Similarly, statistical analysis of droplet velocity distributions showed no significant difference between the two conditions with P=0.4. The corresponding fracture profiles were nearly identical, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and D. However, we observed a noticeably larger volume of droplet ejection from the chilled samples. Previous studies have reported increased elasticity and brittleness of plant tissues at lower temperatures (48, 51), which may have expanded the fracture region, even though the critical epidermal fracture threshold remained unchanged. Further controlled experiments are needed to rigorously evaluate the mechanical effects of temperature on onion cutting and droplet generation. Certainly, many other natural factors have also escaped consideration in the current study and remain to be studied in the future, such as onion variety and layer thickness, though thicker layers typically produce more voluminous and energetic splashes from our empirical observations.

Food-borne pathogens in kitchens are known to spread through droplet splashes during washing and direct surface contact (28). A study of commercial kitchens reported that 33 to 100% of cutting and washing regions were contaminated with Campylobacter (28), a common cause of diarrhea. The current work reveals an alternate route for bacterial agents to spread via fracture from raw food cutting: inside fragmented food droplets. This is supported by previous work showing contamination levels decrease with distance from cutting boards during chicken chopping (29). Ejected droplets can come into direct contact with contaminated blades or carry surface-borne pathogens as they leave the food surface. The corresponding Stokes numbers of the droplets can be calculated as St=τUb/L with the characteristic time for droplets defined as τ=ρdDd2/18μa, on the order of 10−2 to 100; L is taken as the size of the onion and μa is the air dynamic viscosity at 1.8 × 10−5 Pa·s. While heavier droplets with larger sizes follow their inertial pathways, lighter droplets are readily suspended and can be transported by ambient air currents, thereby posing a potential risk for airborne transmission.

The current findings reveal the importance of blade sharpening during raw food cutting practices, as sharper blades reduce not only the number of droplets but also their speed and kinetic energy. This is particularly relevant for fruits and vegetables, which can carry food-borne pathogens such as Salmonella (52), and typically possess tough epidermal layers allowing excessive internal compression before fracture.

In summary, motivated by the mechanics underlying onion tear induction and pathogen-laden droplet ejection during cutting, we developed customized PTV and DIC experimental procedures to visualize, quantify, and link droplet generation to cellular compression in onions. These observations were supported by theoretical models that accurately capture independently measured fracture forces. Our findings demonstrate that blunter blades increase both the speed and number of ejected droplets, providing experimental validation for the widely held belief that sharpening knives reduces onion-induced tearing. Beyond comfort, this practice also plays a critical role in minimizing the spread of airborne pathogens in kitchens, particularly when cutting vegetables with tough outer layers capable of storing significant elastic energy prior to rupture.

Materials and Methods

Sample Preparation and Characterizations.

Steel blades were purchased from Jsumo Co. and cut to dimensions of 5 mm (width) by 200 mm (length). To sharpen the blades, we used 3M aluminum oxide abrasive films with 0.3 to 30 μm grits. The sharpness is then tuned by first holding the blade vertical and translating it on top of the film horizontally. The blade was rotated to match the wedge angle and polished by moving it along the wedge surface. The sharpness of the blade was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (Zeiss LEO 1550 FESEM) at the Cornell Center for Materials Science (CCMR). The blade was attached to the measurement stage with the cutting edge oriented toward the electron beam. 7 to 10 measurements were taken for each blade across the sample.

Onions were always freshly sourced from local vendors and experimented within two weeks. The species Allium cepa L. was chosen for consistency. The outermost dry layer (brown skin) was removed, which is typically not used in culinary applications. The onion was then separated into either two halves for regular PTV measurements or four quarters for high-speed DIC experiments.

For PIV tests on speed variations with 3 conditions, we conducted 3 spatially separated cuts on the same onion half to reduce sample variations. Adhesive tape was used to secure the onion and to cover previous cut openings to limit fluid ejection to the active cutting zone. For blade sharpness variations, tests were conducted on different onions to collect averaged results across samples. The overall ejection speed statistics shown in Fig. 2 were obtained from PTV measurements. For early-stage ejection speeds before 0.5 ms in Fig. 4F, we addressed potential early-time PTV errors caused by dense droplet ensembles from videos at a higher frame rate, 20,000 fps. The velocities of all droplets within the first 0.5 ms (first 10 frames) were then manually measured.

We take care of the blade samples by cleaning and sealing the blades in zip-lock bags with desiccant packs to avoid moisture corrosion. All the PTV trials are conducted within a tight set of time frames in 2 to 3 wk; and the results from different trials over the weeks are consistent, without significant shifts in means. We have conducted some PTV trials after running through DIC trials as well, and the results are consistent with the previous results. Therefore, we conclude that wearing effect is insignificant here for the sample statistics. Also, it is worth noting that most of the cutting here is vertical puncture, which typically is less susceptible to wearing than slicing motions. Even if there is a small amount of wear, it should be uniformly applied across all blades and should not affect the arguments drawn from PTV or DIC above.

Error Estimation with yz Projection.

The angular distribution of droplet ejection θ shows that 80% of the ejection data fall within a 20% error margin for velocity Vd when using the yz projection. This means that for slower droplets below 8 m/s, which may have errors above 30% displacing the worst-case-scenario 1-mm depth of field at 8,000 fps, most of the error is still limited to 20%. These results validate the use of the yz projection for characterizing the statistical properties of the droplet ensemble.

Segmentation Analysis from Ejection.

High-speed video recordings were subjected to segmentation analysis to differentiate between external structures (onion and blade), ligaments, and droplets. Classification was based on geometric features such as size, circularity, and eccentricity, along with spatial filters. Additional boundary-based recognition techniques were employed to identify droplets exiting the frame, enabling accurate measurement of cumulative droplet count and volume.

High-Speed Digital Image Correlation (DIC).

High-speed digital image correlation was conducted with the following steps: speckle patterning, high-speed videography, and DIC analysis. A quarter section of an onion was used for each trial. To create a suitable speckle pattern, the exposed cross-section was coated with black spray paint (Rust-Oleum commercial brand) applied from a horizontal distance of 30 to 40 cm, followed by 30 min of drying. High-speed imaging was performed using Photron FASTCAM Nova and SA-Z series cameras, operating at frame rates ranging from 8,000 to 20,000 fps. The resulting videos were segmented into individual frames and analyzed using Ncorr, an open-source 2D DIC program in MATLAB. The reference frame was selected as the last frame prior to blade–onion contact. Subset radii for correlation were set between 10 to 15 pixels, corresponding to approximately 200 to 400 μm depending on resolution, and were selected to match the scale of onion cells. Seed propagation and step analysis were enabled to accommodate large deformations. For strain calculation, a strain radius of 7 to 15 pixels was empirically chosen to minimize error, as recommended in the program manual. The resulting strain maps were exported for further analysis and visualization.
