How to minimize complications during and after periacetabular osteotomy
Authors: Ta-Wei Tai, Adrian E Gonzalez-Bravo, Sergio F Guarin Perez, Diego J Restrepo, Rafael J Sierra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPeriacetabular osteotomy (PAO) is a complex procedure for treating hip dysplasia by reorienting the acetabulum to slow osteoarthritis progression. This study aimed to assess the types and rates of complications after PAO and provide recommendations for prevention.MethodsA total of 715 consecutive patients underwent unilateral PAO by a single surgeon between December 2006 and January 2024, with at least one year of follow-up. Indications included hip dysplasia, acetabular retroversion, and protrusio. Hip arthroscopy was performed concurrently in 325 cases. Demographic factors, perioperative data, and complications – graded using the modified Clavien-Dindo system – were analyzed. Risk factors and learning curves were also assessed.ResultsOf the 715 patients, 121 (16.6%) experienced 144 complications. There were 68 grade I, 52 grade II, 23 grade III, one grade IV, and 0 grade V complications. The 23 grade III complications requiring reoperations included four revision PAOs for overcorrection, three fixations for posterior column fracture, four neurolysis procedures for symptomatic lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) dysesthesias, four heterotopic bone excisions, and eight wound debridements. All type I and II complications were treated without sequelae. The addition of hip arthroscopy did not increase complication rates. Age < 20 years was associated with a lower risk of complications (odds ratio (OR) = 0.53, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.84, p = 0.008), whereas smoking history (OR = 1.72, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.87, p = 0.040) and correction of both acetabular dysplasia and retroversion (OR = 2.46, 95% CI 1.08 to 5.24, p = 0.024) were linked to an increased risk of complications. The operating time and incidence of complications decreased with increasing experience.ConclusionPAO is an effective procedure with an acceptable complication rate. Risk factors should be discussed during preoperative counselling. While patient optimization may help to reduce complications, precise intraoperative technique remains critical for minimizing risk.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2025;6(10):2022–2031.Keywords: Periacetabular osteotomy, Hip dysplasia, Complications, Learning curve, Surgical outcomes, periacetabular osteotomy, osteotomies, nerves, revision surgery, Hip arthroscopy, single surgeon, excisions, acetabular retroversion, acetabular dysplasia

Introduction

Periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) is a surgical intervention intended to reposition the acetabulum that has been shown to decrease the progression of hip osteoarthritis in patients with hip dysplasia.1 By improving femoral head coverage and redistributing mechanical loads across the joint surface, PAO can reduce abnormal loads across the joint. It has been refined over the past few decades, with the introduction of minimally invasive techniques and muscle-sparing procedures.2-5 It is commonly accepted that PAO leads to reduced pain and improved function after surgery.6,7 Recently, PAO has been performed alongside hip arthroscopy to address and repair existing chondrolabral damage.8

PAO effectively treats hip dysplasia, but involves technical complexity and a prolonged learning curve. Complications include wound problems, nerve or vascular injuries, heterotopic ossification, delayed or nonunion, deep infection, intra-articular penetration, posterior column fractures, and – rarely – death. Reported complication rates for PAO vary widely, ranging from 6% to 37%,6,7,9 based on small cohort series with inconsistent definitions of complications. An early multicentre study reported a major complication rate of 5.9%, even in the hands of experienced surgeons.10 Reducing the learning curve and minimizing complications remain significant challenges, particularly for low-volume surgeons.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the types, rates, and risk factors for complications associated with PAO performed by a single surgeon (RJS). Additionally, we aim to provide insights into strategies for managing and minimizing complications. These recommendations should contribute to refining surgical techniques to optimize patient outcomes by helping surgeons minimize the risks associated with PAO.

Methods

Patient cohort

This research is a retrospective case series of 715 consecutive patients who underwent PAO for various forms of hip pathology (606 dysplasia, 72 acetabular retroversion, 33 combined dysplasia and retroversion, and four protrusio acetabuli) between 1 December 2006 and 31 January 2024, and had at least one year of follow-up. Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to commencing the study. There were 617 females and 98 males. The mean age was 24.5 years (12 to 48) (Table I). Among the cohort, 446 (62.4%) were adults aged ≥ 20 years, and 269 (37.6%) were teenage patients. All patients were followed for at least one year. The mean follow-up was 7.7 years (1 to 15.6). Eligible patients had radiological evidence of acetabular dysplasia, defined by a lateral centre-edge angle of less than 20°, and presented with clinical symptoms of hip pain, acetabular retroversion, or protrusio with a limited range of motion in the hip.

Data collection

Data, including preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative information, were collected retrospectively from medical records. Preoperative data included demographic details (age, sex, BMI) and relevant medical history (prior to hip surgery, comorbidities, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade). Intraoperative data included the duration of surgery, intraoperative complications, and any associated procedures, such as the management of chondrolabral damage and femoral head-neck junction abnormalities (Table I). All postoperative complications encountered during the first year were recorded.

Procedures for PAO

All procedures were performed by a single surgeon (RJS) specializing in young hip conditions. Patients were positioned supine on a radiolucent table for fluoroscopic guidance. Intraoperative hip internal rotation at 90° flexion was recorded before osteotomy. An abductor-sparing, modified Smith-Petersen approach was used via a longitudinal incision centred over the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS). The Smith-Petersen interval was entered laterally by incising over the tensor fascia lata to protect the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LCFN). The sartorius was sharply detached from the ASIS without performing an ASIS osteotomy.

The operative leg was flexed over a bump except during correction verification. Retractors were carefully placed within elevated tissues – inside the sciatic notch and around the pubic bone. Posterior column osteotomy was performed under fluoroscopy using a 60° modified false-profile view, with an inside-to-anterior connection joining the posterior and ischial osteotomies. Complete removal of posterior-lateral iliac bone ensured smooth osteotomy progression without forceful fragment mobilization or extra osteotomy passes (Figures 1a and 1b). Fragment mobility was verified using a Schanz pin and bone hook (Figure 1c). The acetabular fragment was repositioned by assessing radiological landmarks (teardrop, pubic, and ischial osteotomies) and parameters (flat sourcil, appropriate version).

a) Illustration highlighting the importance of confirming the completion of the iliac posterior-lateral osteotomy before advancing to the posterior column osteotomy. b) Incomplete posterior-lateral iliac osteotomy can impede propagation toward the ischial osteotomy, potentially requiring forceful mobilization of the acetabular fragment or additional osteotomy passes. c) Illustration demonstrating the use of a Schanz pin and sharp bone hook to mobilize the acetabular fragment. The Schanz pin is positioned under fluoroscopic guidance to ensure alignment parallel to the ischial osteotomy and prevent unintended cutting into the iliac osteotomy. Mobilization of the fragment with the Schanz pin follows a counterclockwise rotation for the right hip and a clockwise rotation for the left hip. A sharp bone hook is used at the pubic osteotomy site to facilitate fragment mobilization and prevent hinged correction, particularly when resistance to rotation occurs.

Before August 2013, open arthrotomy was performed to evaluate the femoral head-neck junction and to treat labral conditions. After 2013, selective hip arthroscopy replaced open arthrotomy for significant femoral head-neck abnormalities or labral pathology, providing superior intra-articular access and rectus-sparing advantages. Arthroscopy was concomitantly performed in 325 patients (45.5%): 242 labral repairs, 152 osteochondroplasties, and 206 acetabular chondroplasties. A total of 17 patients underwent open surgical dislocation, and 19 had proximal femoral derotational osteotomies (Table I).

Postoperative care

After surgery, all patients’ hips were flexed at 30° until they were examined postoperatively. If quadriceps weakness is present, the knee should be flexed at all times while in bed until full recovery is achieved. Crutch training and a physical therapy consultation are provided preoperatively, with an emphasis on avoiding falls. Weightbearing is limited to toe touching for the first four weeks, followed by progressive weightbearing as tolerated. Guided physical therapy begins three weeks after surgery to assist in the recovery process. Smoking cessation is strongly encouraged to promote better healing outcomes. For patients with increased pannus, incisional wound vacuum-assisted closure (wound VAC) is used to enhance wound healing.

Classification and definitions of complications

The complications were categorized via the modified Clavien-Dindo grading system,12,13 which was adapted and validated specifically for hip preservation surgery and has been applied to PAO.14-16 Grade I complications did not require treatment and did not alter the normal postoperative course. Grade II complications required pharmacological treatment or close outpatient monitoring. Grade III complications required surgical or other invasive interventions. Grade IV complications were life-threatening or posed a risk of permanent disability. Grade V complications directly caused death.

Motor or sensory deficits involving the femoral, sciatic, or obturator nerves were recorded as complications. Lumbar plexopathy, presenting as multiple motor palsies and sensory deficits in the ipsilateral limb after surgery, was also documented. Temporary numbness or decreased sensation over the lateral thigh due to LCFN neuropraxia was common after PAO and was not considered a complication. However, prolonged LCFN dysesthesia lasting more than six months and requiring close observation, medical treatment, or local injection was considered a complication and was recorded. Nonunion of the osteotomy site was defined as the absence of radiological evidence of bone healing one year after PAO. Heterotopic ossification (HO) was considered a complication only if it caused pain, had a limited range of motion, or required excision. Hardware removal was performed only if the patient was symptomatic. The incidence of hardware removal has been reported previously and was not considered a complication in this study because it does not affect the final outcomes.17

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographic details and complication rates. Continuous variables are presented as the means with SDs, whereas categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons between patients with and without complications were conducted via independent-samples t test for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Risk analysis was performed via logistic regression. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to confirm the difference in median operation time. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare complications among the early- and late-case groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were performed via BlueSky Statistics software version 10.3.4 (USA).

Results

We identified 121 patients (16.6%) who experienced 144 complications following PAO. Patients with complications were more likely to be older (26.6 years (SD 8.1) vs 24.0 years (SD 8.5), p = 0.002). The analysis of complication rates across different age groups revealed significant differences (p = 0.001). When patients aged under 20 years were used as the reference group, those aged 20 to 29 years had a significantly greater risk of complications, with an odds ratio (OR) of 2.01 (95% CI 1.22 to 3.37, p = 0.007). Similarly, patients aged 30 to 39 years presented an even greater risk, with an OR of 2.82 (95% CI 1.67 to 4.84, p < 0.001). Patients aged 40 years and above had an OR of 1.86 (95% CI 0.75 to 4.24, p = 0.156), which did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to the small sample size in this group. Additionally, patients with complications were more likely to be obese (p = 0.042), have hypothyroidism (p = 0.014), and have a history of smoking (p = 0.009). The complication rates did not differ between PAO alone and PAO with concomitant arthroscopic procedures (p = 0.548) (Table I).

The complications included 67 bone-related, 36 nerve-related, 28 wound-related, and 13 other complications (Supplementary Table i). In total, 68 complications were classified as Clavien-Dindo grade I, 52 as grade II, 23 as grade III, and one as grade IV. There were no grade V complications. Grade III and IV complications accounted for 16.7% of all complications, whereas 83.3% were grades I or II (Table II). No patient experienced more than one grade III or IV complication.

Grade II complications required additional evaluations, testing, or prescription medications, but did not necessitate surgical intervention. Bone-related complications were the most common, identified in 56 patients (7.8%), and included posterior column stress fractures, pubic ramus fractures, subluxation of the pubic symphysis, heterotopic ossification with limited range of motion, leg length discrepancies, and radiological nonunion of the pubic osteotomy. All pubic nonunions were asymptomatic. Nerve-related complications were observed in 32 patients (4.5%), primarily involving LCFN dysesthesia, as well as femoral nerve palsy, obturator nerve palsy, and lumbar plexopathy. This included nine cases of femoral nerve palsy, all of which resolved without issues. There were no cases of sciatic nerve palsy and one case of obturator nerve neuropraxia. There were three patients with lumbar plexopathy, which was presumed to be inflammatory, who were treated with sural nerve biopsy and oral steroids. In total, 18 LFCN injuries were treated with medical management or local injection, whereas the other three had tolerable symptoms. Wound-related complications, including delayed healing, haematoma formation, superficial infection, and cellulitis, occurred in 20 patients (2.8%). Other complications included psoas tendinitis, deep vein thrombosis, and dyspareunia.

Grade III complications required surgical intervention. Bone-related complications were identified in 11 patients (1.5%), including four with overcorrection necessitating revision surgery, three with posterior column fractures requiring additional fixation, and four with heterotopic ossification requiring excision. Nerve-related complications were observed in four patients (0.6%), including three patients with prolonged LCFN dysesthesia requiring decompression surgery and one patient with femoral nerve palsy due to slight overcorrection and anterior translation of the fragment. This patient underwent revision surgery to reposition the fragment and recovered femoral nerve function within two weeks. Wound-related complications occurred in eight patients (1.1%), including deep infections or delayed wound healing that required debridement under local or general anesthesia. There was no total hip arthroplasty that occurred within two years due to surgical complications, such as intra-articular osteotomy or fractures. One patient experienced a grade IV complication of pulmonary embolism, accounting for 0.1% of the treated hips. There were no grade V complications.

We conducted a risk analysis of complications following PAO via logistic regression (Table III). According to the univariable analysis, age under 20 years demonstrated a protective effect against complications (OR 0.44, p < 0.001). Obesity (OR 1.70, p = 0.044), hypothyroidism (OR 2.59, p = 0.018), smoking (OR 1.94, p = 0.010), and combined dysplasia and retroversion (OR 2.81, p = 0.036) were identified as risk factors. In the multivariable analysis, age under 20 years (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.83, p = 0.007) remained protective against complications, whereas smoking history (OR 2.46, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.87, p = 0.024) and combined dysplasia and retroversion (OR 2.46, 95% CI: 1.05 to 5.12, p = 0.024) were associated with an increased risk of complications. Obesity and hypothyroidism did not remain significant in the multivariable model, suggesting that their associations may be influenced by other confounding factors.

The analysis of median operating times across case groups demonstrated a significant reduction in operating time as case numbers increased, indicative of a learning curve effect (Figure 2c). The Kruskal-Wallis test revealed statistically significant differences among the groups (p < 0.001), with pairwise comparisons showing that the early case groups (e.g. first 200 cases) had significantly longer median operating times than the latter case groups did. The IQR in earlier groups also tended to be broader, reflecting greater variability in operating time during the early stages of experience.

a) The median operating time for periacetabular osteotomy (PAO), along with the IQR, significantly decreased across the patient groups, as demonstrated by the Kruskal-Wallis test (p < 0.001). b) The trend of complications in periacetabular osteotomy across patient groups. The total number of complications (black bars), Clavien-Dindo grade III and IV complications (grey bars), and cases of overcorrection or undercorrection (white bars) are shown for each case group. A significant decrease in the total number of complications was observed with increasing case numbers, as demonstrated by Fisher’s exact test (p = 0.004). Additionally, there is a noticeable trend towards a reduction in overcorrection or undercorrection with increased experience.

The analysis of complications associated with PAO across case groups revealed a significant reduction in the total number of complications as surgical experience increased (Figure 2b). Fisher’s exact test revealed a statistically significant difference in the overall complication rates among the patient groups (p = 0.004). Notably, the highest complication rates were observed in the earliest group (first 200 cases), while the rates progressively decreased in subsequent groups. A similar trend was observed for cases of overcorrection or undercorrection, with fewer occurrences in the latter case groups. These findings suggest that cumulative surgical experience plays a critical role in reducing complications and improving outcomes in PAO patients. Surgical pearls and technical considerations derived from intraoperative experience were included to facilitate the prevention of procedure-related complications (Table IV).

Discussion

In our series of 715 PAOs performed by a single surgeon specializing in young hip conditions, we observed an overall complication rate of 16.9%, including a 3.4% rate of grade III and IV complications. The risk of complications among adult patients is nearly double that among younger patients. Importantly, there were no cases of permanent major nerve injury, complications requiring salvage THA, or mortality during the first year after surgery. As surgeons gain more experience, the complication rates tend to decrease. We also share practical insights gained from experience to help prevent various complications.

A previous multicentre cohort study involving 205 unilateral PAOs reported 30 (15%) complications during the first postoperative follow-up, following additional 42 complications at the second follow-up visit, with a major (grade III or above) complication rate of 5.9%.10 Our series showed a lower major complication rate of 3.4%, highlighting outcomes from a single experienced surgeon after 18 years performing this complex procedure. Another study reported major complications in 37% of 60 PAOs but noted these did not affect final outcomes.18 Our findings align with prior reports, suggesting most complications are manageable.

We identified eight cases of symptomatic overcorrection and one case of undercorrection. To avoid overcorrection or undercorrection, fluoroscopy should be used at each step for verification. It is crucial to ensure that the pubic symphysis and coccyx are aligned to account for rotation and pelvic tilt, enabling a true anteroposterior view of the pelvis under fluoroscopy. Aim for a flat sourcil with appropriate lateral correction, taking care to medialize the hip centre as needed. After correction, the range of motion of the hip was assessed to prevent unacceptable impingement. This approach helps achieve proper balance and alignment during the procedure, reducing the risk of complications associated with overcorrection or undercorrection.

In our series, we observed 14 cases of posterior column fractures. Posterior column fractures often result in delayed healing or nonunion at osteotomy sites, particularly at the ischial site. An underlying posterior column fracture should always be considered in cases of ischial delayed union. The prevention of posterior column fractures requires careful attention to the osteotomy procedure. Fluoroscopy was used to verify the direction and depth of the Mast osteotome. The ischial osteotomy on the ischium was centred, and alignment was confirmed with fluoroscopy before a 60° modified false profile view was obtained. The osteotome was impacted under fluoroscopy, avoiding passing the tip of the osteotome past the mid-portion of the ischium to prevent overpenetration. To connect the posterior column to the ischial osteotomy, a straight posterior 19 mm osteotome was gradually advanced parallel to the posterior column, and once the straight osteotome passed the equator of the femoral head, it was switched to a curved osteotome to connect the posterior column to the ischial osteotomy in a curved fashion. Additionally, implementing fall prevention strategies after surgery is important to avoid further complications.

We did not encounter any cases of intra-articular osteotomy. However, this complication can result in severe consequences and should be meticulously avoided. To prevent intra-articular osteotomy, it is essential to define the location and trajectory of the osteotomies precisely before execution. Detailed planning ensures that osteotomy is performed accurately, thus avoiding the risk of inadvertently damaging the joint surface or intra-articular structures.

The prevention of heterotopic ossification involves the use of medications to reduce the degree of risk. A total of 162 mg of aspirin was prescribed daily for thromboprophylaxis and heterotopic ossification prevention. For males or high-risk patients, a cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitor is prescribed for at least two weeks post-surgery. However, three of the four patients who required heterotopic ossification excision in our series were female. Therefore, preventing the formation of heterotopic bone may be considered for all patients and is currently employed. To prevent other minor complications, such as psoas tendinitis, internal snapping, or dyspareunia, a large pubic fragment can be avoided by not performing the osteotomy too medially. The ideal site for pubic osteotomy is just medial to the eminence. Kalhor et al19 also suggested filling large gaps at the pubic osteotomy site to minimize the risk of femoral nerve entrapment or psoas tendon snapping. We have not used this technique.

Nerve-related complications are a significant concern following PAO, as both patients and surgeons experience anxiety due to the uncertainty of recovery. Predicting whether or when nerve function will fully recover from palsy is challenging. Nerve injuries after PAOs are not uncommon, particularly during the surgeon’s early learning curve. A systematic review of PAOs reported an incidence of 8.2% for LCFN dysesthesia; 0.88% for transient nerve palsy of the femoral, sciatic, or peroneal nerves; and 0.05% for permanent major nerve palsy.6 The prevalence of nerve-related complications can drop to as low as 2% with experienced surgeons.20

We found nine cases of femoral nerve palsy in this series, all of which fortunately resolved over time. Femoral nerve injuries typically result from anteverted correction of the acetabular fragment and may occur several days or weeks after PAO. To prevent femoral nerve palsy, the fragment should not be anteriorized. The fragment should be in posterior contact with the posterior column, and verified through direct palpation of the posterior osteotomy and review of false profile fluoroscopic images. A cadaveric study also revealed that the femoral nerve develops a bifurcation of ascending and descending branches to innervate the rectus femoris muscle. The nerve tends to be stretched around the bifurcation during medial retraction of the soft-tissues.21 We flex the hip to 30° to release tension on the femoral nerve while retracting the medial soft-tissue for ischial and pubic osteotomies, and we aim to minimize the duration of retraction. After surgery, the hip and knee were flexed to 30° until femoral nerve function was assessed. If there was any weakness, the knee was flexed while in bed until normal nerve function returned. The return of function occurs quickly if it is detected. If femoral nerve palsy is missed, several months may be needed for recovery to occur.

Obturator nerve palsy typically results in weakness of adduction in the affected limb. This temporary injury is likely related to the pubic osteotomy step. In this series, only one patient developed obturator nerve palsy, which resolved within four months without additional intervention. This injury was caused by a repeat osteotomy to mobilize the fragment due to an initial incomplete pubic osteotomy. The obturator neurovascular bundle runs approximately 5 mm behind the superior ramus through the obturator foramen.22 When blunt retractors are placed subperiosteally to expose the pubic osteotomy site, care should be taken to avoid placing the retractors too deeply. In our series, no sciatic nerve palsy was observed. We avoid overpenetration of the ischium during the initial and final ischial cuts, as the sciatic nerve runs approximately 1 cm lateral and posterior to the ischial osteotomy site.22 Lumbar plexopathy is an inflammatory neuropathy caused by postoperative local swelling and inflammation. If global weakness is observed in nerves not typically at risk, lumbar plexopathy should be considered.23 The recommended treatment includes steroids.

Numbness over the lateral thigh area due to LCFN neuropraxia is extremely common, with the prevalence of LCFN injuries reaching 30%.24 Fortunately, most cases are minor. However, some patients experience prolonged shooting pain in this area which, although not serious, can be quite bothersome. We reported 24 cases (3.4%) of LCFN dysesthesia, of which 18 required only medical treatment or local injection, while three eventually underwent surgical decompression or neurectomy. Preventing LFCN dysesthesia involves careful surgical techniques. The Heuter approach should be used to protect the LFCN within the Smith-Petersen interval during initial exposure. A knife was used to release the sartorius to prevent thermal injury. Performing an osteotomy of the ASIS to detach the sartorius and provide protective layers can be considered to balance the risk of additional screw fixation and nonunion of the ASIS osteotomy versus soft-tissue reattachment.19,22 The LFCN should be carefully identified during reattachment of the soft-tissue to the ASIS, as patients with LFCN issues often have the nerve trapped under the reattached inguinal ligament.

Wound infections or delayed healing led to eight reoperations in our series. Weight management should be recommended prior to surgery, as being overweight is a known risk factor for various postoperative complications, including surgical site infection.25 In this study, we found that the prevalence of smoking was significantly greater in the complication group than in the non-complication group. As smoking was an independent risk factor in our multivariable analysis, we strongly recommend smoking cessation, as smoking adversely affects bone and wound healing.26-28

Among 33 patients with combined dysplasia and retroversion, six complications occurred: two infections, one symptomatic HO, one femoral nerve palsy, one overcorrection causing posterior overcoverage, and one leg length discrepancy. These patients may have a higher complication risk compared to those with dysplasia alone, suggesting increased complexity when treating combined deformities. Further studies are needed to clarify this relationship.

This study has several limitations. First, it is based on a series performed by a single experienced surgeon, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other surgical settings, particularly those involving less experienced surgeons. Surgeons in the early stages of PAO may experience higher complication rates (Figure 2b). Second, as a retrospective chart review, the incidence of complications may be underestimated due to the inherent limitations of retrospective data collection. Additionally, the lack of a standardized prospective protocol for identifying complications may lead to inaccuracies in assessing their severity. However, with the use of the Clavien-Dindo grading system, which categorizes complications on the basis of the need for reoperation, we are confident that nearly all major complications have been appropriately captured. Third, the race and ethnicity of the patients were not analyzed in this study, which may also affect the generalizability of the results.

Although the surgical technique of PAO is technically challenging and has a steep learning curve, it is generally a safe procedure for correcting hip dysplasia. The overall and grade III/IV complication rates following PAO are 16.7% and 3.4%, respectively. Patients younger than 20 years may have a lower risk of complications. Smoking history and combined correction of acetabular dysplasia and retroversion increase the risk of complications. Additionally, the risk of complications tends to decrease as surgeons gain more experience.

Take home message

- Periacetabular osteotomy is effective for hip preservation, but carries a risk of complications.

- Awareness of potential complication types, recognition of risk factors, and meticulous surgical technique can help improve patient safety and outcomes.
