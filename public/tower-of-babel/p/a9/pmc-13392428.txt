Systematic review and meta-analysis of machine learning models predicting massive hemorrhage protocol in trauma
Authors: Gemma Postill, Anglin Dent, Richard Cheng, Anton Nikouline, Teruko Kishibe, Melissa McGowan, Jethro C C Kwong, Brodie Nolan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTimely activation of massive hemorrhage protocols (MHP) is critical to prevent exsanguination and improve survival in trauma patients. Current clinical tools for predicting MHP need have limited sensitivity and moderate specificity. Machine learning (ML) models may enhance early recognition of life-threatening hemorrhage. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies that developed or validated ML models to predict MHP need in patients with traumatic injuries (PROSPERO CRD42024574891). Databases searched included MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and LILACS. Risk of bias was assessed with PROBAST, a funnel plot and Deeks’ statistical test, and the GRADE checklist. Of the 4542 abstracts screened, 21 studies (50 ML models) were included. The ML model types with the highest pooled AUROC were random forest (0.89, 95% CI 0.85–0.93), neural networks (0.88, 95% CI 0.83–0.93), and XGBoost Models (0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.93), all of which commonly outperforming their non-ML comparator. Despite superior discrimination, most ML models lacked robust subgroup performance evaluation, external validation, or feasibility for clinical deployment. The median APPRAISE-AI methodologic quality score was 51 out of 100 (Interquartile range [IQR]: 46, 55). Further, model reporting quality (TRIPOD + AI) was moderate; no study achieved full points across all 27 reporting items. Risk of bias was frequently high or unclear. Thus, pooled performance metrics should be interpreted with caution, as they may be influenced by methodological weaknesses in the underlying studies. These findings underscore the need for more rigorously designed and transparently reported ML studies in trauma care. Realizing the potential of ML for supporting MHP delivery in trauma care will require further collaborative, clinically grounded model development with robust validation, transparent reporting, and a focus on real-world deployment to translate predictive performance into improved patient outcomes.PROSPERO Registration Number: CRD42024574891.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49472-5.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Massive hemorrhage protocols (MHPs) refer to the standardized rapid provision of blood components and products for the resuscitation of bleeding patients1,2. Providing appropriate and timely transfusion therapy to critically injured patients is an essential aspect of trauma resuscitation, given hemorrhage is responsible for almost half of deaths from traumatic injuries globally3–5. Balancing early activation and more efficient delivery of standardized components and blood products with the potential waste of unused blood product and administrative efforts, is thus a priority in cost-effective, quality care delivery6.

Owing to the importance of timely and appropriate MHP activation, clinical tools to predict need for MHP have been developed7; these include the Assessment of Blood Components (ABC)8, Trauma Associated Severe Hemorrhage (TASH)9, and the McLaughlin score10. However, the predictive performance of these tests in civilian trauma settings is moderate11–15. More recently, to reduce the cognitive load these scores can place on clinicians and improve predictive accuracy, machine learning (ML) models have been developed to predict MHP16,17. ML is a subset of artificial intelligence (AI) that leverages self-learning computer systems to perform tasks (e.g., prediction) without explicit instructions, relying instead on patterns and inferences in the data.

Two prior systematic reviews on the use of ML for MHP prediction indicated their potential16,17. However, neither review evaluated the technical elements of model development, model performance, nor model reporting quality using standardized measures. Additionally, these studies used different, non-exhaustive terminology to capture ML models. These gaps leave clinicians and researchers without clear, evidence-based guidance on which approaches are high performing and methodologically sound, and thus, have potential for clinical use.

The recent development and validation of the APPRAISE-AI tool18 and TRIPOD + AI checklist19 presents an opportunity for harmonized evaluation of the methodological and reporting quality of ML models. Our objectives were thus to (1) characterize the performance and reporting of ML models predicting MHP newly developed tools for model assessment, and (2) explore whether differences in methodological quality or reporting standards influence the observed results. To do so, we conducted a systematic review and subsequently a meta-analysis and quality evaluation of included studies. Our summary can guide further ML approaches for MHP prediction, as well as elsewhere in trauma, ultimately expediting the translation of ML-based tools to the clinical setting.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO CRD42024574891) in collaboration with an interdisciplinary team with expertise in ML, trauma care, and epidemiology. Methodologically, this study was designed following The Cochrane Handbook20. The study is reported according to the Transparent Reporting of Multivariable Prediction Models for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis: Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (TRIPOD-SRMA) recommendations (Supplemental 1)21.

Search strategy

In collaboration with a health information specialist (TK), we designed and conducted a systematic search of studies using ML for MHP prediction between January 1, 2004 to March 24, 2025 (Supplemental 2). The search included the following databases: MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), Web of Science (Clarivate), the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Ovid), and LILACS. Due to significant advances in medical practice and technology, we limited the search to 2004, as older research would not be applicable to current definitions of AI and modern trauma care. Compared to prior reviews16,17, we employed broader, more rigorous search criteria recognizing the various terminology used to reflect ML methodology (e.g., supervised learning, reinforcement learning, and neural networks). We also spoke to topic experts and manually searched ClinicalTrials.gov and reference lists of the eligible studies to identify additional studies for inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included studies evaluating use of ML for MHP prediction (model outcome) in trauma care. We included studies regardless of their setting (prehospital vs. in-hospital), patient populations (e.g., military and civilian), MHP definition, and study type (e.g., both observational studies and trials). We excluded studies that were not primary literature, predicted only need for blood product (vs. MHP), or only developed logistic or linear regression models as our objective was to evaluate quality and performance of newer ML models – specifically, those for which APPRAISE-AI and TRIPOD + AI were developed. We included studies regardless of whether and how they compared their ML models to non-ML benchmarks (e.g., non-ML models and clinical benchmarks).

Study selection and data extraction

Titles and abstracts of articles identified in the search were independently screened in duplicate for eligibility using the Covidence systematic review software by four reviewers (AD, GP, JK, and RC)22. All disagreements in article screening were resolved by an additional reviewer (BN). The full texts were then independently screened in duplicate (AD, GP, and RC), with disagreements again resolved by an additional reviewer (BN).

Data was extracted in duplicate by AD and GP using an extraction template and consensus verification when uncertainty arose. Extracted data included general study information (e.g., author, title, year, country), MHP definition, dataset characteristics (e.g., timeframe, size, inclusion of older adults) and ML model(s) developed. We assessed inclusion of older adults as these individuals and their presentations of shock differ from younger adults23; given these differences, assessing whether developed ML models perform equally well on this subpopulation is clinically important.

Study outcomes

Our study outcomes included (1) model performance and (2) model quality. Model performance metrics assessed included sensitivity (recall), specificity, area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUROC) and standard error. Model quality was evaluated with APPRAISE-AI (Supplemental Table S1) and the TRIPOD + AI checklist (Supplemental Table S2).

APPRAISE-AI is a scoring instrument for evaluating the methodological and reporting quality of AI studies for clinical decision support (Supplemental Table S1)18. This instrument encompasses 24 items across 6 categories (clinical relevance, data quality, methodological conduct, robustness of results, reporting quality, and opportunities for reproducibility and clinical validation.), with a total score of 100 points (< 40 indicates low quality, 40–59 moderate quality, ≥ 60 high quality). TRIPOD + AI is a 15-item (27-subitem, with 52 checklist boxes) checklist to guide transparent reporting of studies developing, validating, or extending (updating) a prediction model (Supplemental Table S2)19. Unlike APPRAISE-AI, the TRIPOD + AI checklist was not developed for quantitative comparison of included studies. Thus, to compare the reporting quality on both systems, we assigned 1-point for the presence of each checklist item on the TRIPOD + AI checklist (max score = 52). Scores were assessed independently by two reviewers (GP and AD), with a final consensus rating established through discussion and collective review. Scores were tabulated with Microsoft Excel version 16.89.123.

Data analysis

Studies were summarized descriptively, comparing ML algorithm type, training/testing dataset characteristics, model features, study characteristics, MHP definitions, and performance outcomes measured. For model quality, the consensus APPRAISE-AI and TRIPOD + AI scores were tabulated by item and domain.

Subgroup meta-analysis of model discrimination measures (area under the receiver operating curve [AUROC], sensitivity, and specificity) was conducted for each ML model algorithm type (XGBoost, decision tree, random forest, neural networks, support vector machine, and adaptive boosting) as well as for comparator algorithms (logistic regression models and ABC Score) used in 2 + studies. Bayesian (N = 2) models did not have sufficient power for inclusion in the meta-analysis. To account for considerable between-study heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used to pool effect sizes. The restricted maximum likelihood estimator24 was used to calculate the heterogeneity variance τ2. Prediction intervals were included to encompass the likely range of pooled AUROC in future studies while accounting for between-study heterogeneity. For meta-analyses of sensitivity and specificity, a random-effects bivariate model was applied. Paired forest plots highlighting the sensitivity and specificity for each ML model algorithm type were generated, in addition to Summary Receiver Operating Characteristic (SROC) curves. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed through visual inspection of the SROC curves. Meta-analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1. Additionally, qualitative subgroup analysis of model discrimination, as measured by AUROC, stratified by outcome measure and separately clinical timepoint (pre-hospital vs in-hospital), was also performed.

The correlation between (1) APPRAISE-AI and TRIPOD + AI scoring systems, (2) APPRAISE-AI Score and model discrimination (measured with AUROC), (3) TRIPOD + AI Score and model discrimination (measured with AUROC), (4) study quality and publication date for each metric were assessed through calculation of their Spearman correlation coefficient. As a sensitivity analysis we also characterized the relationship between model quality and model performance including only studies’ best models and stratifying the algorithm subtype. Correlations were assessed using Python version 3.13.9.

Risk of bias and confidence in cumulative evidence

Two reviewers (AD and GP) independently assessed the risk of bias and applicability for each of the included studies using the prediction model risk of bias (Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool [PROBAST]) assessment, with disagreements resolved by consensus24. Risk of bias was categorized as “high”, “unclear”, or “low” across the participant, predictor, outcome, and analysis domains. Applicability was categorized as “high”, “unclear”, or “low” concern across the participant, predictor, and outcome domains. A funnel plot was used to assess bias overtime27. Deeks’ statistical test for assessing funnel plot asymmetry, the optimal approach for Diagnostic Test Accuracy Systematic reviews, was used. Confidence in cumulative evidence was reported using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) checklist25.

Results

Study characteristics

We identified 5296 studies (Fig. 1). After the removal of duplicates, we reviewed 5245 title and abstracts, 9 full texts, and included 21 studies (Fig. 1)26–46. The studies analyzed were published between 2005 to 2024, with the majority conducted in 2023 (n = 7, 33%) and 2024 (n = 6, 29%) (Table 1). Collectively, these studies included nearly 1.7 million individuals, though the inclusion of older adults was only clearly identified in 52% of studies (Supplemental Table S3). Median outcome (MHP activation) prevalence was 9% (range: between 2–66%) across the 19 studies reporting outcome prevalence (Table 1). The majority were ML derivation studies without an external validation (n = 16, 76%); four studies included an external retrospective validation, and one study included a prospective, silent validation (Table 1).

Model characteristics

Across 21 studies, 50 ML algorithms were captured. Common ML model types included random forest (n = 9, 21%), neural networks (N = 8, 19%), and XGBoost (n = 6, 14%) (Supplemental 2). Logistic regression algorithms (n = 7) and the ABC Score (n = 5) were commonly used benchmark measures to compare ML model performance. Features commonly included in models were hemodynamic, resuscitation, and neurological indicators (Supplemental Table S4). Label (outcome) definitions varied across study (Table 1); commonly used outcomes were 10 + units of pack red blood cells (PRBC) within 24 h and 3 + units of PRBCs in 1-h. Models were primarily designed for use in initial hospital resuscitation (n = 12, 57%) and prehospital settings (n = 7, 33%). Of the included studies, 12 (57%) included methods of explainability (Supplemental Table S4).

Model performance

All ML algorithm types demonstrated a pooled AUROC > 0.80, indicating strong discrimination (Fig. 2, Supplemental Figure S1). The ML model types with the highest pooled AUROC were random forest (0.89, 95% Confidence Intervals [CI] 0.85–0.93), neural networks (0.88, 95% CI 0.83–0.93), and XGBoost Models (0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.93). The pooled AUROC of ML models demonstrated superior performance compared to logistic regression and the ABC score (Fig. 2). Likewise, pooled sensitivity and specificity of the ABC score (sensitivity: 0.70, 95% 0.64–0.77; specificity: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.46–0.68) was lowest while that of the XGBoost models (sensitivity: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.82–0.90; specificity: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.81–0.83) and neural networks (sensitivity: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.82–0.90; specificity: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.72–0.87) was the highest (Supplemental Figure S2).

Each point represents the reported AUROC for an individual study, with horizontal lines indicating corresponding confidence intervals. Pooled estimates for each model type are shown as diamonds, derived using a random-effects meta-analysis to account for between-study heterogeneity. Model categories include ABC score, adaptive boosting, decision trees, logistic regression, neural networks, random forests, support vector machines, and XGBoost. Considerable variability in performance is observed both within and across model types, reflecting differences in study populations, predictor variables, outcome definitions, and validation approaches. Pooled estimates should therefore be interpreted as descriptive summaries rather than definitive comparisons of model performance.

Model performance varied substantially within and across outcome definitions. Models predicting early transfusion requirements (e.g., ≥ 3 units PRBC within 1 h) as well as traditional definitions of massive transfusion (e.g., ≥ 10 units within 24 h) demonstrated moderate to high discrimination, with model performance clustering by study (Supplemental Figure S3). Additionally, model performance was consistently high across both prehospital and in-hospital settings; in-hospital models tended to show slightly higher and more consistent performance, likely reflecting greater data availability and later-stage prediction (Supplemental Figure S4).

Model quality

Across studies, the median consensus APPRAISE-AI score was 51 (Interquartile range [IQR]: 46, 55), reflecting overall moderate quality; only one study was rated high-quality (Supplemental Table S5)26. Mean domain scores were highest for clinical relevance (median: 100%, IQR: 75–100%) and study reporting quality (median: 83%, IQR: 83–100%), and lowest for reproducibility (median: 25%, IQR: 20–35%), robustness of results (median: 35%, IQR: 20–40%), and methodological conduct (median: 35%, IQR: 30–55%) (Fig. 3, Supplemental Table S6). No studies achieved full points for source of data, sample size calculation, explanation of hyperparameter tuning, error analysis, and code transparency (Fig. 4). Only three studies (14%) reported model calibration (Fig. 4). Study methodologic quality did not show a trend towards improvement overtime (Supplemental Figure S5).

Within the TRIPOD + AI checklist, the median score across studies was 37 of 52 items (IQR: 35, 39) (Supplemental Table S7–S8). No study achieved full points for reporting across all 27 items (Fig. 5). Several items were consistently well-reported across studies, including background, data sources, participant characteristics, outcomes, predictors, ethical approval, evaluation methodology, and study limitations (Fig. 5, Supplemental Table S7). Reporting was notably poor for key AI-specific items such as model output, fairness, and model specification. Study reporting quality did not show a trend towards improvement overtime (Supplemental Figure S6). A weak, non-significant positive association was observed between the APPRAISE-AI and TRIPOD + AI scores (r = 0.25, p-value = 0.251, Fig. 3B, Supplemental Table S8), emphasizing that the constructs captured in each differ, with a greater emphasis on methodologic quality captured in the APPRAISE-AI tool.

Relationship between study quality and model performance

Across included models, a weak, non-significant negative correlation (Spearman ρ = − 0.27, p = 0.068) was observed between the models’ methodological quality (APPRAISE-AI) and discrimination (Fig. 5). A non-significant correlation was also observed when only the best performing model of each study was included (Supplemental Figure S7) and when stratifying by model subtype (Supplemental Figure S8). Model reporting quality (TRIPOD + AI) was negatively associated with AUROC (ρ = − 0.53, p = < 0.001) (Fig. 5). Similar trends were observed when including only the best performing model of studies (Supplemental Figure S7) and when stratifying by model subtype analyses by model subtype (Supplemental Figure S9), though correlations on these analyses were non-significant.

Risk of bias and applicability

Most included studies were determined to be at unclear risk of bias, owing to limited details regarding data complexities (e.g. censoring) and a lack of comparison to results from a multivariable analysis (Fig. 6). Regarding selection of participants, risk of bias was low as all studies used large cohort studies for their training and testing data and almost all of them reported detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria. Similarly, the risk of bias introduced by predictors was low among most included studies as all predictors used in the development of the models had been defined and assessed without knowledge of outcome data. However, in some studies not all predictors would be available at the time of intended use; studies were downgraded when this was identified. Almost all studies were at low risk of bias due to outcome measurement, studies primarily reported objective, standardized, and measurable MHP outcomes. Many of the included studies were determined to be at unclear risk of bias due to analysis, with a minority demonstrating a high risk of bias, owing to limited details regarding data complexities (e.g. censoring) and a lack of comparison to results from a multivariable analysis. Applicability of the majority of studies was low risk, with the exception of five studies which were considered high risk and moderate risk of non-applicable findings28,31,39,42,46. We concluded this systematic review presents evidence with a low certainty, given the observational nature of the included studies. Assessment of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness of evidence, imprecision of results, and publication bias did not result in additional downgrading (Supplemental Table S9) (Fig. 7).

Risk of bias assessment via ROBAST.

The generated funnel plot for the assessment of publication bias consisted of the pooled AUROC from 45 algorithms with available AUROC metrics generated from the 21 included papers (Supplemental Figure S10). On appearance, the funnel plot appeared moderately symmetrical, with most studies concentrating near the top peak of the funnel. Deek’s statistical test for asymmetry revealed the funnel plot to be symmetrical (Deek’s test p-value = 0. 495). In combination with the extensive and broad literature search strategy, this review was concluded to be at a low risk of influence from publication bias.

Discussion

From the 5245 abstracts screened, we identified 21 studies (capturing 50 ML models) predicting MHP in the prehospital or in-hospital setting. Features included in models were largely hemodynamic and neurologic indicators. When pooled by algorithm type, all ML algorithm types all showed strong discrimination, as measured by pooled AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity. Random forest and XGBoost models particularly offered notably strong performance complemented with interpretability. All studies comparing ML model predictive performance to the ABC score showed improved performance. However, ML model quality, as measured with APPRAISE-AI, was moderate overall. Studies commonly lacked sample size calculations, external validation, bias or subgroups assessment, and code or data sharing, ultimately limiting the validity and generalizability of models. Though our findings demonstrate potential value in using ML to predict MHP, they also identify the need for improved quality of research using ML for the prediction of MHP; such is essential to properly understand its potential for improved clinical prediction.

Prior systematic reviews have also highlighted the potential of ML to improve clinical decision-making across topics from triage to long-term outcomes in trauma47,48. Specific to ML for predicting MHP, two prior reviews exist16,17. Peng et al.16 identified 11 ML studies on transfusion and 8 on hemorrhage detection in care for traumatic injuries. The authors noted that few studies adequately validated algorithms and most use mortality as the prediction outcome, which is less clinically useful when making acute decisions for coagulopathy management. Oakley et al. 2024 identified 17 of 25 ML models for blood transfusion prediction in trauma with overall strong performance (AUROC > 0.8), through only four were externally validated17. However, neither review included used ML-specific approaches to evaluate model performance or reporting quality. Our study extends on these reviews by including new studies not previously included28,30,34,36,37,46, comparing ML model performance to traditional clinical tools (e.g., logistic regression and ABC), and more robustly evaluating ML model methodologic and study reporting quality with AI-specific tools (e.g., with APPRAISE-AI). In doing so, we highlight that, while promising, current ML models predicting MHP are only of moderate quality, limiting our ability to ascertain the full potential of ML for predicting MHP in trauma.

Using APPRAISE-AI we identified particularly weak domains of model quality: methodological conduct, robustness of results, and reproducibility. Within these domains, many studies lost points due to lack of complete reporting of methods (vs. poor conduct); for example, studies reported conducting hyperparameter tuning but failed to report what hyperparameters were tuned, how they were tuned, and what metric was used to optimize model performance. These findings reflected broader issues with the clinical ML literature49,50. Incomplete reporting makes it unclear whether methods were appropriately applied. Thus, there is need to improve both the methodological conduct and reporting quality.

In using two different newly developed tools for evaluating the quality of ML models (APPRIASE-AI and TRIPOD + AI), this study additionally offers insights into the comparability of such tools. We found a weak positive correlation between APPRIASE-AI and TRIPOD + AI scoring, observed likely in part due to differences in their structuring. While both are designed to assess the quality of ML models, TRIPOD + AI has greater emphasis on reporting while APPRAISE-AI more directly evaluates model quality alongside reporting. Differences in the construct measured (reporting versus methodologic quality) may also explain the differences in the strength of the negative correlation observed between model reporting score (TRIPOD + AI or APPRAISE-AI) and performance was observed. Methodological rigor (captured by APPRAISE-AI) may lead to more conservative and potentially less optimistic performance estimate, resulting in a weaker association with performance. For both, the observation of a negative trend between scores and AUROC may reflect heterogeneity in study design, reporting practices, and potential overestimation of performance in lower-quality studies.

Importantly, strong model performance does not necessarily translate into clinical utility in trauma resuscitation51. For MHP prediction, utility is inherently time-sensitive, requiring predictions to be available early enough to influence activation of transfusion protocols and mobilization of resources. Models that rely on variables obtained later in care may demonstrate strong discrimination but have limited real-world applicability. Further, for real-world applicability, use of clinically meaningful outcomes (e.g., those that are actionable and have the potential to improve care) is also needed. Our review highlighted variability in the definitions of MHPs, reflecting the current inter-center heterogeneity in MHP definition; future work should further explore the performance ability of a specific ML algorithm across definitions of MHPs.

Beyond high quality models, successful implementation also depends on integration into clinical workflows (e.g., embedding models within prehospital systems, emergency department triage, or electronic health record interfaces) to enable real-time, low-friction use52–54. Understanding the output format preferred by clinicians and how clinicians interpret and act upon model outputs, is a valuable first step to ensure that ML tools effectively support, rather than disrupt, care55. Elsewhere in healthcare, models that have been implemented into care proactively address barriers, including interoperability challenges, clinician trust and interpretability concerns, and alert fatigue53,54,56,57. Prospective evaluation studies (e.g., silent deployment and pragmatic trials) are therefore needed to assess model performance under real-world conditions as well as to determine whether they improve clinical decision-making, resource utilization, and patient outcomes when embedded in care pathways35. The relative lack of robust external validation is a major limitation of the current evidence base.

Based on the limitations of the evidence found in our review, we summarized the priorities for future research and clinical translation of ML tools for MHP prediction as identified by our review in Box 2; many of these priorities are applicable in the broader AI for healthcare landscape. Despite the excitement around AI/ML, first and foremost, improved methodological reporting and justification is needed. For example, missing data management is important to document and justify, given missingness of resuscitation and registry data is non-random58. Second, robust evaluation of ML model performance (including both discrimination and calibration measures) across subgroups is crucial for enabling equitable outcomes59. ML models can have biased performance across such subgroups, even when variables such as income, race, or ethnicity are not included in the model60. Subgroup performance evaluation can be done using a data-drive framework61, across pre-specified groups that pertain to the population studied49,62,63. or with an intersectionality lens to ensure differences in model performance by subgroup are not masked with single variable stratification64. Subgroup analyses by age are particularly important in trauma where known disparities in trauma outcomes exist (e.g., those with language or cultural barriers65, older adults66,67, and vulnerably housed populations68). However, to evaluate performance across these subgroups, data on these characteristics must be available within the dataset and sufficient sample size of each group within the testing data. Further, consideration of deployment from the onset (e.g., including only clinically available features) as well as prospective evaluation trials are needed to evaluate the ability of models to maintain performance in light of dataset shift and other deployment challenges, and to actually improve patient outcomes once integrated into clinical workflows69.

Limitations

Our study has limitations. First, there was significant heterogeneity across studies in how models were developed and the MHP definition used, which limits our meta-analysis and thus, our ability to interpret one model type as superior to another. As such, pooled performance estimates should be interpreted as descriptive summaries of reported model performance rather than evidence of permanent superiority between model types. Second, due to limited power, a subgroup meta-analysis analysis could not be performed to evaluate the impact of prehospital versus in-hospital prediction on pooled AUROC. Third, model quality as operationalized by its APPRAISE-AI score reflects a combination of model/study quality and reporting quality, analogous to other non-ML guidelines (ex. The Reporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data—RECORD—Statement70). Models may thus have similar scores but different qualities. For example, points are given equally for any hyperparameter tuning strategy, though the various strategies that exist are not equally rigorous18. Fourth, while APPRAISE-AI and TRIPOD + AI are landmark tools in that they provide a standardized approach to quantitative evaluate ML study quality with a technical lens, both employ a one-size fits all approach. In doing so, they overlook the nuanced reporting requirements that ML model types. For example, reporting “XGBoost version 2.2.1 was used” provides sufficient description of the overall model architecture, whilst denoting that “a neural network was implemented with tensorflow version 2.2.1” does not convey the same reproducibility. Finally, variation in AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity between and within classes of model types is reflective of both abilities of models as well as underlying differences in training procedures (i.e., optimizing on one metrics over the others); interpretation of pooled results requires consideration of this. Additional research is needed to identify optimal approaches for developing and reporting specific to ML model type as well as clinical question (i.e., how outputs and inputs are defined varies by field).

Conclusion

Our comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 studies (50 ML models) using ML to predict MHP in acute trauma care demonstrated that ML models may improve the prediction of MHP need compared to existing clinical references. However, there was substantive heterogeneity in how models were developed and overall model quality was only moderate, limiting our ability to rigorously conclude on the potential effectiveness of ML techniques for predicting MHP. Thus, further research is needed to identify and externally validated optimal ML methodologies for predicting need for MHP in trauma. More broadly, original ML research and ML-focused systematic reviews should integrate tools such as APPRAISE-AI and TRIPOD + AI, to improve the reproducibility of clinical ML studies and the translation of developed model to the clinical setting.
