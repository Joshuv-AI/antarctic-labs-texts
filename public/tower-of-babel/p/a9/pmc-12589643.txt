Negative and positive childhood experiences with regard to PTSD and subjective well-being in the national sample of Poles
Authors: Marcin Rzeszutek, Magdalena Grabowska, Małgorzata Dragan, Maja Lis-Turlejska, Katarzyna Schier, Joanna Kowalkowska, Małgorzata Styśko-Kunkowska, Ewa Malinowska, Tomasz Osiński, Kamilla Bargiel-Matusiewicz, Barbara Sosińska, Maria Ibisz, Saadat Almazova, Marcin Bagiński, Szymon Szumiał
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to explore the distinct and cumulative impacts of various types of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and positive childhood experiences (PCEs) on the occurrence of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and subjective well-being (SWB) in a Polish citizens. A nationally representative sample of Polish adults (n = 2,231) participated in this study. ACEs were evaluated using the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACE-Q), while PCEs were assessed using the Benevolent Childhood Experiences Scale. The level of PTSD was measured via the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 and lifetime traumatic events with the use of the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5. SWB indicators were assessed using the Satisfaction with Life Scale and the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule. Network analysis showed that not all but rather specific ACEs (sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect and mental illness in the household) were related to the risk of PTSD in adulthood. In contrast, PCEs were associated mostly with SWB levels in the participants. Moreover, we found an extraordinarily elevated PTSD level in Poland (23.8% probable PTSD diagnosis in the entire sample). Our study highlights the need to broaden childhood adversity research to include PCEs as an important target for promoting resilience in adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22431-2.Keywords: Adverse childhood experiences, Positive childhood experiences, Post-traumatic stress disorder, Well-being, Network analysisSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

Over the past three decades, interdisciplinary studies on adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)—defined as negative or potentially traumatic life events occurring during childhood and adolescence—have revealed their destructive impact on various health outcomes in adulthood (see reviews by1,2). Several authors have observed that ACEs are a significant risk factor for poor overall well-being in adulthood3 as well as various mental health problems such as affective disorders4, personality disorders5, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD;6,7). Moreover, other authors found that ACEs predicted problems with substance abuse8, risk of chronic illnesses (e.g., obesity, cancer, or cardiovascular disease9–11, and even premature death12). Regarding its pervasive influence on the multidimensional aspects of functioning in adulthood, ACEs are a major but mostly unaddressed threat to public health1,13.

In contrast, much less attention has been paid to the role of positive childhood experiences (PCEs), described as experiences before age 18 that are generally beneficial for individual development in adulthood (e.g., positive relationships with parents and other adults, beneficial household routines, having good neighbors, etc.;14,15). Consistent with the social ecological theory on human development16, there is increasing evidence that PCEs may act as a source of resilience that buffers the deleterious effects of ACEs in adulthood (see17). It was observed that PCEs are associated with reduced risk of depressive and anxiety disorders18, more beneficial psychosocial functioning19, lowered risk of chronic diseases in adulthood20, and also higher subjective well-being (for a review, see21).

Although PCEs and ACEs may co-occur during a patient’s lifespan, to date, most of the research has focused on ACEs only without considering the role of PCEs17. A few studies have analyzed the simultaneous effect of ACEs and PCEs and have demonstrated that PCEs may neutralize the destructive effect of ACEs on depression22 and PTSD in adulthood14. What is still not entirely understood is to what degree ACEs and PCEs constitute an independent set of childhood experiences in predicting various health and well-being patterns in adulthood1,17.

The aforementioned research gaps predominantly stem from the methodological shortcomings of contemporary studies on ACEs and PCEs. Specifically, most authors adopted a cumulative approach, aggregating individual ACEs or PCEs to generate a continuous score. This approach neglects the fact that various childhood experiences may not be relevant to everyone17. Other authors used latent class models to identify ACEs/PCEs profiles in a given population and explore which applies to a particular person23. While this approach may improve our understanding of the unique impact of ACEs or PCEs, it still prevents us from understanding how various childhood experiences are interrelated in predicting adult functioning. In light of these measurement limitations, recent studies have employed network analysis to model the intricate relationships between individual childhood adversities and various adult health outcomes concurrently23,24. Nevertheless, the simultaneous implementation of network analysis in ACEs and PCEs remains rare25. However, it is only through this approach that one can fully grasp the complex nature of ACEs and PCEs in order to create personalized interventions for adults with a history of childhood trauma.

The current study

While PTSD is a disorder closely related to traumatic experiences and their potential consequences26, subjective well-being (SWB) may be a general indicator of psychological health based on people’s evaluations of their own life satisfaction and affect balance27. However, the population of SWB is substantially related to cultural factors28,29. Therefore, it should be analyzed from a contextual perspective. In the case of Poland, the ongoing war in neighboring Ukraine is certainly such factor. However, studies that take these issues into account are rare30 and practically non-existent with regard to such an important risk/protective factor as childhood experiences. Therefore, when verifying the our research questions, we were particularly interested in determining whether the various types as well as the number adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), positive childhood experiences (PCEs), or the number of lifetime traumatic events was the most important factor influencing the level of PTSD and SWB in adulthood. Due to the scarcity of studies that analyse ACEs and PCEs using this specific methodology, our study is primarily exploratory; therefore, no direct hypotheses have been formulated.

In light of the aforementioned research gaps in the literature, our study had two aims. Firstly, we aimed to explore the distinct impacts of various types of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and positive childhood experiences (PCEs) on post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms and subjective well-being (SWB), as operationalized in terms of life satisfaction and the presence of positive and negative affect (PA/NA), in a national sample of Poles, while controlling for the number of lifetime traumatic events and demographic data among participants. Secondly, we aimed to investigate the cumulative impact of ACEs and PCEs on the intensity of PTSD and the level of SWB in participants.

Methods

Participants and procedure

This study was conducted on a representative sample of 2,231 Poles aged 18–99 (M = 48.10; SD = 15.93), 1,194 females aged 18–99 (M = 48.25; SD = 15.85), and 1,037 males aged 18–99 (M = 47.93; SD = 16.02). The majority of participants, 1,383 subjects (62.0%), lived in a city, and 848 (38.0%) lived in a village. Forty-nine participants (2.2%) completed primary education, 247 participants (11.1%) completed vocational education, 920 participants (41.2%) completed secondary education, and 1,015 participants (45.5%) completed higher education. The majority of participants, 1,437 (64.4%), were professionally active.

We cooperated with the Polish research panel Ariadna, which has a database of over 200,000 registered users. The panel holds a valid Interviewer Quality Control Programme (PKJPA) certificate, confirming its high standard of research services. Participants were recruited from the general Polish population aged 18 and above through various channels, including email invitations, advertisements, phone calls, and direct contact. Participation in the research was voluntary, and respondents were rewarded with points in the Ariadna panel’s loyalty program. After completing the verification process, individuals registered on the Ariadna panel received encrypted and personalized invitations to their registered email addresses to participate in the study. As compensation for participation, participants were awarded tokens of the quantity corresponding to the complexity and length of the survey. These accumulated tokens could be redeemed for various gifts (e.g., e-books, household appliances, or cosmetics). Prizes were delivered via courier, requiring recipients to provide their personal details and addresses; this also functioned as an additional verification measure for panelists.

Based on the panelists, a random quota sample was selected for the study. The sample was representative of the Polish population in terms of gender, age, education, and size of place of residence. The sample structure was carefully adjusted to match the demographic composition of the Polish population. Representativeness was ensured by controlling each of these criteria across 50 weighted demographic cells. The weights were calculated according to these four variables, and the participants were randomly selected within each cell to meet the predefined demographic quotas. Finally, the project was approved by the ethics committee of the Faculty of Psychology, University of Warsaw. All the described methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects of this study.

Measures

Adverse childhood experiences

The Polish adaptation of the ACE-Q31 was used to evaluate the participants’ exposure to ACEs. This measure is a 10-item scale with items such as “Did you live with anyone who was depressed, mentally ill, or attempted suicide?” or “Did you often feel no one in your family loved you or thought you were important or special?”2. Participants were asked to respond to each item using a binary (Yes = 1, No = 0) format. The total score was calculated by summing all “Yes” responses, with higher scores reflecting increased exposure to childhood adversities. In this study, the ACE-Q showed satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.79).

Positive childhood experiences

The Polish translation of the Benevolent Childhood Experiences Scale was used to assess the PCEs in our sample14. The scale consists of 10 items with a dichotomous (Yes = 1, No = 0) response format14. It focuses on aspects such as perceived physical and emotional safety and relationships with adults and peers before the age of 18. Participants were asked to answer questions such as: “Did you have at least one caregiver with whom you felt safe?” or “Did you have at least one teacher who cared about you?” Each affirmative response adds to the total BCE score, where higher scores indicate a higher incidence of PCEs. The internal consistency of the BCE scale in this study was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.73).

Post-traumatic stress disorder

To assess the level of PTSD symptoms, the Polish adaptation of the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5)32 was used. The PCL-5 is a 20-question survey that assesses the symptoms of PTSD based on the DSM-5 criteria for this disorder33. It evaluates PTSD symptom severity on a Likert scale from 0 to 4, also providing the total score regarding the probable PTSD diagnosis according to the DSM-5. The internal consistency in this study was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.97).

In addition, we controlled for the potential lifetime prevalence of other traumatic events among participants with the aid of the Polish adaptation of the Life Events Checklist for the DSM-5 (LEC-5)34. The LEC-5 is a 17-item questionnaire that evaluates diverse types of potentially traumatic events across the lifespan, including natural disasters, accidents, exposure to war, physical and sexual assault, and life-threatening illnesses.

Subjective well-being

The cognitive component of subjective well-being (SWB) was assessed using the Polish version of the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS)35. The SWLS is a widely used 5-item measure designed to assess global cognitive judgments of one’s life satisfaction36. Participants responded to items such as “In most ways, my life is close to my ideal” or “I am satisfied with my life” on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Scores were then summed across the five items, yielding a total score from 5 to 35, with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. The internal consistency of this scale was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.92).

The affective component of SWB was measured using the Polish version of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule37. This measure is a 20-item scale that assesses both positive affect (PA) and negative affect (NA)38. Respondents rated the extent to which they experienced each emotion over the past four weeks using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very slightly or not at all) to 5 (extremely). The PA and NA scores were then calculated separately, with the higher values indicating higher levels of either positive or negative emotions. Here, the internal consistency was high for both subscales (α = 0.88 for PA; α = 0.85 for NA).

Data analysis

First, the demographic data were analysed to provide an accurate description of the current sample. Next, descriptive statistics were calculated for the analysed interval variables. The distributions of the types of ACEs, PCEs and lifetime traumatic events were analysed. Finally, network analysis was conducted using the bootnet and qgraph packages in the R Statistics 4.4.3 software environment. Network analysis is derived from complex systems theory and is used to analyse patterns of statistical associations in various scientific disciplines39. This method enables system components (network nodes) and their mutual relations (links between nodes) to be identified graphically. In clinical psychology, for instance, it can help determine whether symptoms of mental disorders constitute a syndrome with causal relationships among them23. Using this technique, symptoms can be represented as nodes and their associations as edges, providing a simple way to present complex psychopathological problems. In this type of analysis, the nodes represent variables and the edges represent the associations between them. The analysis is based on a partial correlation matrix, which involves calculating the relationships between the analysed variables while controlling for all included variables. Consequently, it can be used to evaluate the relationship between any two included variables, while accounting for the exclusion of all other variables.

As we anticipated that specific ACEs and PCEs would be related, we incorporated both types of childhood experience into a single network model for analysis. This enabled us to consider ACEs when calculating the associations between PCEs, PTSD and SWB, and vice versa. During the analysis, a graph is provided that depicts the relationships, showing both the strength of the relationships and the proportion of variance explained. Each node on the graph represents a variable and each line connecting nodes represents a relationship between those variables when controlling for all the others. The stronger the relationship, the thicker the line. In addition, the colours of the lines are informative. Blue lines indicate positive relationships, while red lines indicate negative ones. Each node in the network has a border that can be filled in more or less. The proportion of the node that is filled reflects the percentage of variance explained by the variable that the node represents. For network analysis, we used stepwise estimation and the extended Bayesian information criterion procedure to select the tuning parameter. We required at least one estimate to be non-zero in order to set the edge to be present. The advantage of network analysis in our particular study that it can incorporate many dependent variables into a single statistical model23. Since network analysis is based on partial correlations, the associations between dependent variables and all other variables are calculated by taking all variables into account. This gave us the opportunity to understand the links between the structures of the independent and dependent variables (see Fig. 1), which was what we needed to address the aims of our study.

The network graph for relationships between analyzed variables.

On the other hand, regression analysis allowed us to investigate the cumulative impact of ACEs and PCEs on participants’ PTSD and SWB levels. We performed a hierarchical linear regression analysis. In the first block, the models included the number of lifetime traumatic events, and in the second block, they included the number of ACEs and PCEs. Thus, we were able to calculate the relationships between the cumulative numbers of ACEs and PCEs while controlling for the number of lifetime traumatic events.

Network analysis

To address the complex patterns of the studied variables, a network analysis was performed. We analyzed the number of lifetime traumatic events and demographic data, i.e. participants’ sex and age, as controlled variables. Specific ACE and PCE events were included in the analysis as explanatory variables. The network acquired is depicted in Fig. 1 whereas the proportion of explained variance for each node can be found in Table S4 in the supplementary materials. The thickness of the line shows the relationship between the line variables. The thicker the line, the stronger the relationship with the blue line for a positive correlation and the red line for a negative correlation.

Higher levels of PTSD symptoms were strongly associated with higher levels of NA. A higher SWL, on the other hand, was strongly associated with PA. The age of the participants was negatively related to NA. The number of lifetime traumatic events was positively associated with PTSD symptoms. Sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect were positively associated with PTSD symptoms. Mental illness in the household was also linked to PTSD. Verbal abuse was associated with a lower SWL.

Regarding PCEs, liked school and caring teacher were co-occurring positive events. Liked school was positively related to SWL and caring teacher, with PA. Comforting beliefs, fun activities, and predictable routines were also positively related to PA, while comforting beliefs, self-acceptance, and predictable routines were positively related to the level of SWL.

Emotional neglect and fun activities were negatively related to each other, being mutually exclusive in most cases. Emotional neglect was associated with higher levels of PTSD symptoms and fun activities with higher PA. Similarly, mental illness in the household and predictable routines were associated negatively, and mental illness in the household was associated positively with higher PTSD symptoms. Predictable routines were linked to higher levels of PA and SWL. The participants who experienced an emotional neglect also experienced a lack of fun activities and, as a result, higher levels of PTSD symptoms and lower PA. The participants with the experience of mental illness in the household also experienced a lack of predictable routines and, as a result, suffered from higher levels of PTSD symptoms and lower levels of PA and SWL.

Discussion

The results of this study showed that not all but rather specific ACEs were related to the risk of PTSD in adulthood. Specifically, network analysis found that sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, and mental illness in household most strongly predicted PTSD in our sample, after controlling for lifetime exposure to other traumatic events in adulthood. We find this result extremely important from a clinical perspective. The declaration of sexual abuse is often minimized due to shame and anxiety40; for many years, emotional neglect was a “neglected” problem, both in clinical psychology and in the area of research41.

At the same time, not concrete kinds of PCEs, but rather a lack of them at all, was mostly associated with PTSD levels among study participants. In addition, regression analysis revealed that for the severity of PTSD symptoms, the most significant risk factor was exposure to ACEs, followed by experiences of other traumatic events in adulthood and finally deficits in experience of PCEs. On the one hand, our findings are consistent with previous studies linking various ACEs to an elevated risk of PTSD in adulthood (e.g6,7,42). In particular, ACE may predict PTSD levels uniquely, even after accounting for other traumatic events, which showed its strong and long-lasting impact on mental health in adulthood6.

Until now, only24, following the network analysis, observed that not all ACEs matter the same for PTSD risk in adults, indicating sexual abuse as the most significant of them. However, the above-mentioned studies were conducted mostly in small clinical groups, which calls for more research on representative samples1. The results of our study, conducted in a national Polish sample, broadened the list of ACEs most related to PTSD risk and showed the necessity to move beyond the cumulative and single adversity approach in ACE research43. It seems that not all ACEs contribute in the same way to psychopathology risk in adulthood, which supports the perspective showing developmental mechanisms linking childhood adversity and adult psychopathology. Namely, the Dimensional Model of Adversity and Psychopathology (DMAP)44 proposes two interrelated dimensions—threat and deprivation—that describe a wide variety of adverse experiences in childhood.

Threat deals with experiences of harm or threat of harm (e.g., sexual abuse), and deprivation is the absence of desired resources from the environment (e.g., an absence of good caregiver interactions, as in emotional neglect). These dimensions may be found in various ACEs but in varying degrees. For instance, threat is a core element of sexual abuse, whereas deprivation is a main characteristic of various forms of neglect or the general lack of a functional family environment. This may somehow explain why PTSD symptoms were not associated with any particular PCEs, but deprivation of them in total. Overall, the results of our study may provide empirical contributions to the dimensional models of adversity and psychopathology, which may be useful for creating personally tailored psychological interventions for adults with a history of ACEs1,43.

We noticed an intriguing relationship between PCEs and the dimensions of SWB among our participants. First, network analysis indicated that such PCEs, such as liked school and caring teacher, often co-occurred with each other, with liked school being positively related to the level of life satisfaction and caring teacher being positively related to the levels of PA. Comforting beliefs, fun activities, and predictable routines were also positively related to levels of PA, and comforting beliefs, self-acceptance, and predictable routines were positively related to life satisfaction. These results suggest that all of these factors are associated with more frequent experiences of positive emotions in adult life.

Second, and what might be even more interesting, lower levels of life satisfaction were linked primarily to the lack of positive experiences. Life satisfaction is a cognitive element of SWB, and ACEs or exposure to lifetime traumatic events were almost insignificant in predicting this aspect in our participants. Likewise, a lower level of PA was also associated with a lack of PCEs and had almost nothing common with ACEs or other traumatic events in our sample. In contrast, the level of negative affect was linked mostly to the frequency of ACEs but had no relationship with the presence of PCEs or lifetime trauma prevalence.

Overall, it seems that our results provided additional evidence for the independence of PCEs from ACEs—that PCEs could predict various adult outcomes separately from childhood adversity17. Moreover, our findings may shed new light on the tripartite model of SWB regarding the mutual relationships between its cognitive (life satisfaction) and affective components (PA/NA)27,45,46. Numerous authors have found that those SWB elements are interrelated but also constitute independent constructs, especially given their time, stability, and underlying predictors47. With respect to this latter issue, PCEs should be included as another significant factor associated with life satisfaction and emotional well-being in adulthood.

On the other hand, it seems that elevated negative affectivity in adulthood stems mostly from ACEs; here, PCEs are limited in buffering their destructive impact48. This provides another argument for the separation of PA from NA constructs in the discussion of the paths of human development49. Thus, we found that psychoeducation to support schools and parents in developing children’s and adolescents’ self-acceptance to be alternative pathways to adult well-being. Additional pathways include comforting beliefs, predictable routines, and enhanced activities that make students happy and perceive teachers as caring adults (for a review of some school-based interventions, see50).

Finally, the results of our study provided additional evidence for the extraordinary elevated level of PTSD in Poland (see 23.8% probable PTSD diagnosis in the whole sample). It is even higher compared to the previous national Polish study on PTSD prevalence, which showed a level of 18.8%51. These estimates exceed not only current US studies (compare 4.7% to 3.8% PTSD level)52 but also against all European countries (compare 0.4% to 3.8%)53,54. Also intriguing is the fact that the lifetime prevalence of traumatic events in our sample, estimated at 63%, is not especially higher than in the above-mentioned studies.

In order to interpret this significant discrepancy, versatile socioeconomic, cultural, or historical country characteristics should be considered55,56. Discussing all or even some of them is beyond the scope of this article. However, recent studies have observed the role of the massive human losses experienced by Poles during World War II (WWII), as well as the repression experienced during the subsequent Communist regime57; all indicate the potential, transgenerational transmission of trauma from that period58. Nevertheless, this hypothesis warrants further empirical investigation.

On the other hand, one should underscore the ongoing global conflict happening near the Polish border, i.e., Russian aggression against Ukraine. The outbreak of a full-scale war in 2022, followed by the massive influx of Ukrainian migrants to Poland, significantly affected the psychological well-being and mental condition of Polish society by exposing them to the details of the war59. Previous global crises related to the COVID-19 pandemic also led to elevated rates of various mental health problems on a global scale, including in many regions of Europe53. Thus, it may be that both situational factors, the previous pandemic, and the current tense situation resulting from the war in Ukraine could translate into the current high level of PTSD in Poland.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the mutual assessment of ACEs and PCEs in a nationally representative sample within an innovative methodological framework (i.e., the network analysis). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in Poland and one of the first in the literature25 that simultaneously investigates PCEs and ACEs in the context of SWB in a national, representative sample. However, a few limitations should be mentioned. First, we used a self-report and retrospective assessment of study variables, including ACEs, PCEs, and PTSD, instead of some form of clinician-administered interviews. Nevertheless, this kind of measurement would have been very difficult in an epidemiological study from an organizational point of view. Second, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes the drawing of causal conclusions on the link between study variables. Third, we did not control for potential previous treatments or past trauma-focused psychotherapy in our participants with a history of childhood trauma. Lastly, we did not specifically measure the possible influence of the current situation on Polish society resulting from Russian aggression against Ukraine.

Conclusion

Despite the above-mentioned limitations, our study makes an important contribution to research on the adverse and PCEs and their long-lasting psychological consequences in adulthood. It seems that while ACEs are mostly associated with the risk of psychopathology in the form of PTSD, PCEs are predominantly related to well-being in adults. However, not all ACEs and PCEs contribute in the same way to these dimensions of mental health and well-being. This calls for moving beyond the cumulative and single adversity approaches in this research area. Overall, there is a need to broaden the childhood adversity research on PCEs, which should be treated as independent experiences as well as an important target for promoting resilience in adults. The use of network analysis allowed us to find a group of variables stemming from childhood experiences that could be of special importance in the development of PTSD, thus enabling clinicians to develop optimal treatment strategies.
