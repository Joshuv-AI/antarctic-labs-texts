Vibration assessment of transfemoral implant stability: a longitudinal case series
Authors: Mostafa Mohamed, Eric Beaudry, Ahmed Shehata, Dylan Brenneis, Lindsey Westover, Jacqueline Hebert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe success of osseointegrated implants (OI) for individuals with transfemoral amputation (TFA) depends on the quality of the bone-implant interface (BII), which is difficult to track clinically. Vibration methods assess the biomechanical properties of the BII non-invasively and quantitatively. This study implements a vibration-based approach that quantifies BII stiffness (k) for press-fit transfemoral osseointegrated implants . The primary objective is to assess the initial validity of using k to determine BII condition as individuals progress through rehabilitation and and after transition to unaided prosthesis use.MethodsImplant stability measurements of four participants with TFA were longitudinally collected prospectively over a 2-year follow-up period. The measurements covered post-operative rehabilitation from static loading up to and including unaided prosthesis walking. Vibration signals were analyzed using a custom-developed application that estimates k by comparing the clinically acquired vibration signal to a one-dimensional finite element model of the bone-implant system. The longitudinal changes in k were tracked, and the Mann Whitney U-test was used within each participant to compare the Early (after completion of static loading), Intermediate and Late (post-year 1) osseointegration phases. Complications and X-rays were tracked as per clinical standard.ResultsLongitudinal k measurements demonstrated high repeatability and sensitivity. Within participants, maximum to minimum k ratio ranged from 2.8 to 88 times, indicating high sensitivity to potential BII changes (such as mechanical properties and/or bone-implant contact ratio) during rehabilitation. Observed patterns, such as reduced k during loading followed by higher readings and plateaus later in rehabilitation, align with expectations of BII healing. At the individual level, Early and Late healing showed a significant difference (p<0.001), with higher k in the Late phase, indicating greater implant stability and sensitivity towards clinically meaningful healing.ConclusionsThe proposed measurement method shows promising potential for non-invasively tracking implant stability for individuals with TFA. The changes in k were aligned with: expected bone-implant healing timelines, previously reported vibration assessments for other OI systems, implant survival and rehabilitation stages. The small sample size limits population level inference and generalizability of findings. Larger clinical cohorts are needed to enable population level comparisons and establish clinically relevant predictive k stability/instability thresholds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07056-z.

Introduction

Direct skeletal attachment of prostheses through osseointegrated implants (OI) can provide greater comfort and superior mobility compared to traditional socket prosthesis for persons with lower limb amputation [1–5]. Osseointegration, essential to long-term functional and structural integrity of the implant [6, 7], depends on primary (mechanical) stability from surgical insertion and secondary (biological) stability from continuous bone formation and remodeling around the implant [7, 8]. The bone-implant interface (BII) is a critical site in the system and is susceptible to failure [7, 9]. Reported complications of OI in individuals with TFA can arise from infection, falls, improper loading, and lack of osseointegration [10–12]. Grade 1 or 2 infections, which are typically superficial, can often be managed with oral antibiotics, whereas higher-grade infections (grades 3 or 4) may progress to deep bone and BII infection, leading to septic loosening [10–12]. Falls and improper loading may also result in periprosthetic fractures [10–12]. In addition, poor osseointegration and insufficient bone-implant contact may promote fibrous tissue formation and contribute to aseptic loosening [10–12]. Rehabilitation protocols play a critical role in promoting successful osseointegration. Standardized protocols have been developed for different OI systems [13]. Despite differences in surgical approach (two-stage versus one-stage) and implant design (screw-fixated versus press-fit), most protocols follow a similar loading sequence [13]. Following the final surgery, the patient remains initially immobile, after which progressive static loading is introduced up to a specified duration or percentage of body weight, typically 50% [13]. This is followed by assisted gait training and gradual progression to unassisted mobility. During rehabilitation, progression may follow standardized timelines or be adjusted based on pain tolerance, bone condition, and other clinical factors [13]. Recent animal studies suggest that osseointegration trends, characterized by an early response following implantation followed by a longer integration phase, are consistent with these rehabilitation protocols [14]. Despite advancements in rehabilitation protocols, there remains a lack of clinically available, non-invasive, non-ionizing quantitative metrics that can directly assess BII stability and help guide loading progression and patient-specific rehabilitation protocols. Therefore, longitudinal monitoring and quantitative assessment of BII properties is critical for preventing implant failure, guiding rehabilitation, and understanding osseointegration biomechanics [7, 9, 15].

Radiography is the most common diagnostic tool for implant stability assessment [15]. In older implant systems with longer follow-up histories, such as cementless hip arthroplasty, which serves as a useful analogue for press-fit OI transfemoral systems, radiolucent lines over 50% of the BII indicate instability [16]. More specifically, for patients with TFA and OI implants, conventional X-ray studies demonstrated remodelling around the implant including changes in density, longitudinal bone coverage and cortical thickness [17, 18]. However, these studies primarily characterized morphological and structural remodelling rather providing a direct quantitative measurement of mechanical stability, and they did not establish validated stability thresholds [17, 18]. In some studies, statistically significant changes in cortical thickness could not be established between follow-up time points, despite cortical remodeling greater than 1 mm being observed in all implants [18]. Building on this, Ahmed et al. proposed an X-ray-based interface scoring system that incorporates multiple radiographic features, including bone integration around the collar and interface radiolucency [12]. The method showed promising potential in a clinical series, with lower scores observed in explanted systems [12]. However, as noted by the authors, the approach was evaluated in a small and multivariate clinical dataset and remains subject to the inherent limitations of radiographic assessment, so further validation in larger studies is needed before it can be regarded as a formal standard [12].

Bone mineral density (BMD) measurements have also been used to evaluate bone remodeling and surgical outcomes following OI implantation. However, a recent review noted that postoperative BMD changes were not strongly correlated with OI surgery success or periprosthetic fracture risk, and that reported findings remain highly variable, with studies showing insignificant changes, reductions, or increases in BMD [19]. The authors therefore emphasized the need for larger studies and standardized BMD measurement protocols before BMD can be reliably interpreted as an objective marker of implant stability [19]. Implant migration measured by radiostereometric analysis (RSA) can also offer a quantitative stability metric [20]. Persistent migration can be associated with aseptic loosening of cementless arthroplasty, but current evidence is insufficient to draw thresholds for failure prediction [21, 22]. More specifically, RSA of the osseointegrated prostheses for the rehabilitation of amputees (OPRA; Integrum, Sweden) has demonstrated larger migration for unstable implants [20]. Despite wide clinical usage, radiography exposes individuals to radiation, lacks standardized quantitative metrics and thresholds, is prone to subjective human interpretation and sensitivity to factors related to image acquisition and test setup, and lacks sensitivity to early failure detection [15]. Although preliminary and promising radiographic scoring methods and migration metrics have been proposed [12], there is currently no formally established quantitative radiographic gold standard for assessing BII integrity after osseointegration to serve as a cross reference.

Vibration analysis offers a non-invasive, quantitative alternative for measuring BII integrity and is well-established for dental implants [15, 23]. The underlying principle involves extracting frequencies and mapping them to the BII structural stiffness (k); higher frequencies indicate greater k and higher stability [7, 15]. It was shown, however, for dental implants and bone anchored hearing aids that the frequency cannot serve as an absolute stability metric across patients with different implant sizes and designs due to its dependence on implant geometry [24–27]. The Advanced System for Implant Stability Testing (ASIST) addresses this limitation by coupling the vibration analysis with a mathematical model that directly estimates k [25, 28, 29].

Benchtop experiments of the OPRA system demonstrated the feasibility of vibration analysis for predicting implant stability and highlighted its sensitivity to the BII condition [30–33]. In a longitudinal case study (for one patient), the frequency was initially high (indicating high primary stability), dropped during early loading, and then gradually rose to a plateau that exceeded pre-loading levels [33]. To date, this remains the only clinical investigation of vibration analysis for the screw-fixated OPRA system. To the author’s best knowledge, vibration-based implant stability assessment of other systems such as the press-fit Osseointegrated Prosthetic Limb (OPL; Permedica, Italy) has not been considered in-vivo with participants yet. Furthermore, direct extraction of bone-implant stiffness by determining k using a validated mathematical model has not been investigated in clinical cases involving individuals with TFA.

In previous computational and in-vitro work, the ASIST approach was applied to the OPL system by coupling vibration measurements with a representative one-dimensional finite element (1DFE) mathematical model [34, 35]. The percutaneous distal portion of the implant was excited longitudinally (axially), and the measured axial acceleration response was fitted to the model to directly estimate k [34, 35]. The approach was validated against higher fidelity three-dimensional FE models and benchtop experiments but has not been validated in clinical participants yet [34, 35].

Here, we present the first pilot results demonstrating the clinical applicability of the ASIST in a two-year longitudinal prospective observational study of four participants with press-fit OPL systems. The primary research question is whether the method can detect longitudinal changes in the BII during healing and progressive weight bearing in individual cases. It is hypothesized that the measurements will exhibit distinguishable longitudinal patterns and changes in k, and that statistical inference will demonstrate significant differences between clinically relevant rehabilitation phases within individual participants (where the null hypothesis assumes equal distributions or medians between any two rehabilitation phases: Early (upon completion of static loading), Intermediate and Late (post-year 1). Comparisons of the results with the expected biological timeline of osseointegration, published literature of similar vibration systems, and clinically defined rehabilitation stages are used to contextualise these findings in the absence of a quantitative radiographic or clinical gold standard. Given the absence of a clinical gold standard for detecting BII healing, this work provides crucial first evidence of a potential non-invasive method of tracking longitudinal changes in the BII. This work also informs future larger case series and larger clinical studies needed to assess whether these findings generalize to a wider population of individuals with TFA.

Method

Participants

Longitudinal measurements of implant stability were conducted for four participants with unilateral transfemoral amputations. All participants received titanium Type A OPL implant system with a GV1 connector (Fixus BV, The Netherlands) [2]. The demographics of the participants at the time of surgery are listed in Table 1. Bone mineral density (BMD) was measured using dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), which uses low dose X-rays to assess BMD in the hip and spine. The Z-score was reported based on comparison with reference values from individuals of the same age, sex, and body size. In the present study, the BMD Z-score was included as an indicator of overall bone quality and degree of osteopenia, but it does not provide information on the BII and was not correlated with the measured k. All participants underwent single-stage surgery [4] performed by the same surgeon. Rehabilitation progressed through (1) static loading, (2) prosthetic fitting, (3) daily prosthetic use with two crutches for 6 weeks, (4) prosthetic use with one crutch for 6 weeks, and (5) unaided mobility. During static loading, the femur was axially loaded from 5 kg to 50% body weight. Loading was performed in a standing position within parallel bars and was monitored by the physiotherapist using a scale and an adjustable support surface. Prosthetic fitting was then performed after reaching 50% body weight. Loading onset and rate depended on several factors including, overall bone quality evaluation (BMD), operative factors, and post-operative factors such as pain tolerance as described by Muderis et al. [4].

Participants underwent regular radiographic assessments of the femur as per clinical standard of care: at every gait transition (start of loading, prosthetic fitting, and when progressing off gait aids), and every six months post-operatively. The X-rays were reviewed by both the radiologist and the orthopaedic surgeon for qualitative assessment of BII integrity. Adverse events were tracked and extracted from the clinical record.

Data collection and signal analysis

Procedures were approved by the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board (Pro00109990) and all participants provided written informed consent. Testing was conducted at the University of Alberta Hospital, Kaye Clinic and the Glenrose Rehabilitation Hospital. During testing, participants were positioned supine with the prosthesis disconnected and were instructed to relax their muscles (Fig. 1). Axial/longitudinal vibrations were applied to the percutaneous connector using the Periotest® (Simens, Germany). The corresponding acceleration was recorded using an accelerometer attached to the connector using a double-sided tape, the effect of accelerometer mounting was investigated in our previous work [35]. Each session involved ten trials. In each trial, the Periotest® generated 16 controlled impulses (strikes) within a 4 s window. The first strike was used to trigger the acquisition, and the axial acceleration response of the OI system was recorded for the remaining 15 strikes. These 15 recorded strikes were then temporally aligned and averaged to obtain one representative signal for that trial, from which a single k value was determined. Accordingly, each session yielded up to ten k readings. Signal acquisition and analysis using the 1DFE application followed previously described procedures [34, 35]. The 1DFE application, including its user interface and signal processing workflow, was developed in-house and is independent of the Periotest® [34]. The Periotest® was used only as the excitation device to generate controlled impulses. The 1DFE application predicts the BII stiffness k by comparing the measured acceleration signal with the response predicted by a mathematical model of the bone–implant system [34]. The model is a simplified one-dimensional representation that uses bar elements to lump the stiffness and inertial properties of the system while still accounting for its complex geometry. This simplified representation was validated using 3D finite element simulations and benchtop experiments [34, 35]. In this model, the BII stiffness k is represented by linear grounded axial springs with unknown stiffness magnitude, whereas the remaining system parameters (such as implant geometry, impact rod size, and impact speed) are known a priori and specified by the user before running the analysis. The application predicts k by iteratively matching the measured and model-predicted acceleration signals through an optimization routine that minimizes the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{4}$$\end{document} norm (a distance metric between the two signals) [34, 35]. In clinical use, the operator selects the implant geometry and impact rod size, sets an initial estimate for k, and triggers the optimization. The application then reports the final estimated k and a similarity metric that quantifies the agreement between the measured and predicted signals. This approach has been shown experimentally and numerically to distinguish between different interface conditions, demonstrating order-of-magnitude changes in k between silicone rubber (low), wax (intermediate), and superglue (high) interfaces [34, 35]. The full details of the signal processing (including application inputs, setting initial k estimate, and assessing signal quality) are presented in Online Appendix A.

The first measurement was taken as soon as feasible post-operatively based on clinical status (within 0–7 days post-surgery). Sessions were scheduled three times per week for three weeks, monthly for the following six months and every three months up to two years. Frequency was adjusted for participant availability, and complications were tracked separately in a parallel study.

Longitudinal and statistical analysis of the bone-implant interface stiffness

BII stiffness (k) was longitudinally tracked for each participant. Each session resulted in ten k value predictions. Within session outliers were identified using the 1.5 interquartile range (IQR) criterion, or Tuckey‘s rule, where a k value was considered an outlier if it falls below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Q}_{1}-1.5IQR$$\end{document} or exceeded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Q}_{3}+1.5IQR$$\end{document}. Tuckey’s rule was chosen as the session k values were not Normally distributed. After outlier removal, the remaining number of k values per session were 7.8± 1.4, 8.30± 1.9, 9.1 ± 1.3 and 8.8± 0.93 for participants 1,2,3 and 4, respectively. To visualize the longitudinal changes across sessions, bar charts were used to show session medians with IQR whiskers, participants’ rehabilitation stages (static loading, fitting, 2 crutches, 1 crutch and unaided walking) were color coded. Repeatability was evaluated using the ratio of IQR to median for each session. As an estimate of sensitivity, the maximum to minimum k median ratio was reported. Furthermore, a scatter plot with an overlaid locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) was used to demonstrate global trend of k over time. A LOESS smoothing span of 0.76 was selected, as it minimized the root mean square deviations of residuals.

Individual comparisons between Early, Intermediate, and Late healing were performed using separate Mann–Whitney U-tests (α =0.05/3, Bonferroni correction =3) for each participant. Early healing included post-operative measurements up to and including the last day of static loading. Intermediate healing extended from the start of prosthetic fitting up to and including day 364 post-surgery. Late healing was all the measurements from day 365 onwards. The phases were dictated by clinically significant rehabilitation stages and osseointegration timelines [8, 37]. Given the limited sample size, no population-level inferences were drawn; participants were analyzed as individual cases.

For Participant 1 (Fig. 2a), k ranged from 76 to 1100 N/µm throughout rehabilitation. Immediately after implantation, k was ∼ 120 N/µm, followed by a decline at the start of static loading (∼ 76 N/µm). A sharp increase (~ 510 N/µm) was observed towards the end of loading (day 17), followed by another drop (~ 150N/µm) around prosthetic fitting. k remained within this range until day 286 where it exceeded 1000 N/µm. Between days 353–383, k dropped to 500-600 N/µm. During this time, the participant was diagnosed with a deep soft tissue infection (adjacent to the femur, not involving the implant), which was treated with oral antibiotics. Afterwards, k increased and plateaued around 1000 N/µm from days 490–854. A fall on day 935 subjected the implant to a large axial load and was associated with pain and a non-weight bearing period, and this event coincided with a drop in measurements to 630 N/µm on day 977. The LOESS trend line indicated that k exhibited minor fluctuations up to day 100, then steadily rose up to day 300 and plateaued thereafter.

For Participant 2 (Fig. 2b), k initially started around 540 N/µm and declined to 90 N/µm mid-loading. Thirty days later, k exceeded 3000 N/µm. Afterwards, k plateaued and remained within 2200-5000 N/µm. The LOESS line showed an increase in the BII stiffness up to day 100 followed by a plateau. This participant had no complications.

Participant 4’s k (Fig. 2d) ranged from 73 to 6500 N/µm throughout rehabilitation. It rose from 450 N/µm (day 5) to 2900 N/µm (day 7) and then 3800 N/µm (day 33). Loading onset was delayed to day 32 due to osteoporosis and due to the surgeon’s assessment of bone quality of the distal cortex during implant insertion. During loading, k dropped to 440-570 N/µm, followed by another drop during prosthetic fitting (~ 99 N/µm). By day 98, an increase in k to 5400 N/μm was observed. However, 20 days later, k dropped to 73 N/µm and then steadily rose to 6400 N/µm by day 224, plateauing within a higher range (5000-6400 N/µm) afterwards. The LOESS line showed a relatively high initial k, a decline during loading and the 2 crutches phase, a steady rise during the single crutch phase, and plateauing by the early stages of unaided mobility. Discussion

This study quantified the temporal variation of the k, representing BII healing, using vibration analysis in four press-fit OI cases followed longitudinally. For screw-fixated implants, osseointegration progresses temporally from: (1) fibrin clotting and inflammation (days 1–2), progressive bone mineralization (2 weeks), and (3) maturation to lamellar bone (12 weeks) [38, 39]. Since OPL systems use press-fit fixation [2], cementless arthroplasty offers a more appropriate biological reference. Human retrievals of OI hip implants showed that at 3 weeks, despite good primary stability and timely loading, only early woven bone was observed with no transformation to host lamellar bone [37]. By 3 months, newly mineralized bone covered ≥ 80% of the implant surface, and additional retrievals demonstrated continued remodelling and maturation to lamellar bone over 2–3 years [37]. A more recent case report of a retrieved acetabular cup reported evidence of bone ingrowth 37 days after surgery [40], further supporting the early osseointegration timeline described in human retrieval studies [37]. Accordingly, osseointegration of press-fit implants progresses from early signs by 3 weeks, substantial mineralization by 3 months and lamellar remodelling over 2–3 years [8, 37].

Changes in k aligned with press-fit osseointegration timelines and clinical events. Significant differences were observed for Early-Late (Early: End of static loading ~ 3–4 weeks; Late ≥ 1 year) and Intermediate-Late in all participants (Fig. 3). Early/Intermediate medians were consistently lower than Late, indicating sensitivity to progression from woven to mineralized to mature lamellar bone. Greater variability in the Intermediate phase was expected due to the wide biomechanical changes in loading. However, the high repeatability suggests that observed trends reflect BII changes.

Although k is a vibration-based metric, substantial evidence from benchtop experiments and animal studies have correlated vibration characteristics to the BII condition [34, 41, 42]. In previous work, the BII condition was varied using different materials: silicone rubber (LOW), paraffin wax (INTERMEDIATE), and cyanoacrylate (HIGH) mimicking fibrous tissue, immature bone and mature bone, respectively [34, 35]. Corresponding k thresholds were defined as LOW (k<10 N/µm), INTERMEDIATE (10≤ k<1000 N/µm) and HIGH (k≥ 1000 N/µm) [34, 35]. For ease of interpretation, a dotted band was added to Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 to show the transition between the INTERMEDIATE and HIGH categories.

In the present case-series, no participant fell in the LOW range, consistent with adequate initial primary stability, implant survival and the absence of major bone-implant complications (as indicated by X-rays). Participant 1 experienced a soft tissue infection (day 353) and an axial impact with subsequent loading-related pain (day 935). Such events can affect the participants’ mobility due to pain and could potentially degrade the bone condition from lack of activity but would not necessarily lead to major BII complications. Therefore, in both events, k dipped from the HIGH range to the INTERMEDIATE range but did not enter the LOW range or return to immediate postoperative values, which suggests potential sensitivity of the current approach to events that can affect the bone condition. Furthermore, Participant 3 underwent two soft-tissue revision surgeries with no noticeable change in k and no noticeable bone-implant adverse effects from radiographs, indicating robustness of the measurement to soft-tissue procedures relative to the BII and further suggests that measurements based on axial vibration signals are more sensitive to the BII than to the surrounding soft tissues [35].

The wide range of k within participants also supports the sensitivity of the method to the BII condition. Participant 3 experienced the least variation (maximum/minimum ratio of 2.5), probably attributed to higher primary stability, while Participant 4 experienced the highest variation (maximum/minimum ratio of 88). Our previous work showed that this approach, particularly when relying on axial signals, is insensitive to the boundary condition [35]. This inherently means that the measured k is sensitive to the interfacial condition and less influenced by surrounding factors such as the properties of the surrounding tissues and connections between the implant components. Furthermore, repeatability was high, as the IQR/median ratio for the majority of the testing sessions was below the accepted coefficient of variation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le 10\%$$\end{document}) in biomechanics [43].

The results of this study align with prior clinical vibration studies [29, 33]. Shao et al. investigated the transfemoral OPRA system in one patient and reported a sizable drop in the resonant frequency during loading [33]. This may suggest that the interface is initially weakened by loading [33]. However, as indicated by Wolff’s law, continued loading may later promote bone ingrowth and improve the mechanical properties of the BII [33]. Westover et al. reported several implant stability patterns, including a drop in readings during loading followed by a plateau of stable readings or consistently stable scores throughout healing for bone-anchored hearing aids, and attributed these differences to the initial bone condition and primary stability [29]. In our investigation, three of four participants showed prominent drops in k during loading followed by higher values during unaided prosthesis use. This primary pattern, applicable to Participants 1, 2 and 4, involved intermediate to high early (primary) k, followed by a sizable drop during static loading, and then high values during unaided mobility that exceeded initial post-surgery readings. The second pattern, seen with Participant 3, showed two relatively stable phases with high early k followed by slightly higher k. Participant 3 began with a higher post-implantation k (850 N/µm) compared to Participant 1 (120 N/µm), 2 (540 N/µm) and 4 (450 N/µm). Therefore, no Early-Intermediate significance was observed suggesting that higher primary stability may have led to a more gradual remodelling.

Another observation is related to the time of the decrease in k during loading. Participant 1 experienced the decrease during the earlier phases of static loading, while Participant 2 (towards the end of static loading) and Participant 4 (during prosthetic fitting) experienced the decrease later (Fig. 2). This observation can be attributed to the participants initial BMD measurements (Table 1), where Participants 2 and 4 (with lower BMD) experienced a slower response to loading compared to Participant 1. This observation is important to consider as most benchtop experiments are done with simulations of healthy bone, whereas clinical cases often have osteopenia, which may affect the BII healing.

The primary limitations of this investigation are the small sample size and lack of fully developed radiographic standard against which to compare. Previously reported radiographic methods detected changes in cortical thickness but could not demonstrate statistical significance [18], and radiostereometric migration analysis has not established concrete stability thresholds [20]. The emerging radiographic scoring method proposed by Ahmed et al. shows strong promise and can set grounds for cross-comparison for future studies [12]. Additionally, previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of directly measuring three-dimensional loads applied to transfemoral OI using instrumented pylons [44, 45], which may enable future cross-correlation analyses between implant loading, measured k values, and BII adaptation. Despite the lack of a gold standard, several observations support the clinical relevance of our findings for TFA settings: the measured k followed expected osseointegration timelines, all implants survived, the longitudinal patterns and loading behaviour were consistent with trends reported in the literature, the measurement system has been previously validated experimentally and numerically and is consistent with other vibrations-based methods, and the measurements were sensitive to clinically significant rehabilitation stages and events. Because no implant failures occurred, there is insufficient clinical evidence to establish k thresholds that predict implant instability. Population-level inference was not possible in this study due to the small cohort. For future studies, it is recommended to re-evaluate these findings in larger cohorts to determine the consistency of the loading patterns with the measured k and to further investigate possible influencing factors such as loading rate, loading-k correlations, and cross- comparisons with the emerging quantitative radiographic evaluation methods.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study supports the proposed vibration-based approach potential as a sensitive and reliable tool for assessing BII condition and lays the groundwork for wider clinical validation. The method distinguished between clinically meaningful rehabilitation stages in all participants, with higher observed k by the end of rehabilitation, a finding consistent with implant survival. Longitudinal patterns aligned with the literature. Future work should involve larger cohorts to allow population-level inference, investigating loading-k correlations, cross-comparison with newly developed radiographic scoring methods and attempting to define predictive k failure thresholds.
