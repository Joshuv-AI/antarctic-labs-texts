The expectation-perception gap in primary healthcare quality: a study of Iranian older adults
Authors: Pooya Pakzad, Nahid Yazdi, Mohammad Hassan Naji, Neda Mohammadi, Seyedeh Ameneh Motalebi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePrimary healthcare (PHC) is essential for an effective national health system. Evaluating the quality of PHC services is crucial for designing targeted interventions that can maintain and improve the health of older populations. This study aims to assess the expectation-perception gap in the quality of health services provided to older adults in both urban and rural comprehensive health centers throughout Qazvin Province, Iran.MethodsThis cross-sectional descriptive-analytical study was conducted on 557 older adults residing in Qazvin Province, Iran. A multi-stage sampling method was used to select the older adults. Data were collected using a demographic checklist and the Service Quality Model (SERVQUAL) scale over three months from August to October 2024. Questionnaires were completed through face-to-face interviews at urban and rural comprehensive health centers of Qazvin Province, Iran. Data analysis was performed using a paired t-test and a multiple regression model.ResultsThe study included a total of 557 older adults, with a mean age of 67.97 ± 6.56 years. The results showed a negative gap across all six dimensions of the SERVQUAL scale. The largest mean gap was found in the tangibles (− 3.11) and the “responsiveness” (-2.46) dimensions, while the smallest gap appeared in the reliability dimension (− 1.91). Older adults living closer to healthcare centers (β = -0.35, 95% CI: -0.52, -0.32, P < 0.001), Farsi-speaking elders compared to other ethnic groups (β = -0.12, 95% CI:-2.28, -0.44, p = 0.004), and those with good (β = -0.13, 95% CI:-6.00, -1.37, p = 0.002) and bad (β = -0.09, 95% CI:-6.77, -0.57, p = 0.020) economic status compared with those with moderate economic status reported lower expectations for PHC service quality. In contrast, employed older adults had lower expectations than housewives (β = -0.09, 95% CI:-7.83, -0.42, p = 0.029).ConclusionThe study identified significant gaps between older adults’ expectations and perceptions of PHC quality across all the SERVQUAL dimensions, particularly in the tangibles and responsiveness. To address these gaps, it is recommended to upgrade healthcare facilities and equipment, enhance staff training in responsive and patient-centered communication, establish routine feedback mechanisms with a focus on socially and economically vulnerable groups, and implement culturally tailored outreach strategies for ethnic minorities.Keywords: Aged, Primary healthcare, Expectation, Perception

Introduction

Aging is an inevitable biological process that affects all living organisms, including humans [1]. According to projections by the World Health Organization (WHO), the proportion of people aged 60 years and older is expected to rise from approximately 12% in 2015 to 22% by 2050 [2]. This demographic shift is particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries, with more than half of the projected increase in older adults occurring in Asia [3]. Iran is no exception to this global trend and is undergoing a rapid demographic transition toward an older population [4]. The most recent national census, conducted in 2016, indicated that approximately 8.4 million Iranians, representing 6% of the total population, were aged 65 years or older [5]. In this context, strengthening primary healthcare (PHC) systems to address the complex and multidimensional health needs of older adults has emerged as a critical national priority [2, 6].

PHC serves as the first point of contact between individuals and the health system when seeking health services. These facilities provide a spectrum of services across different levels, including outpatient care, environmental health, disease prevention, and individual and family health promotion [7]. In Iran, the PHC system is structured around two main types of facilities: Rural Health Houses, staffed by community health workers (Behvarz in Persian) who provide basic preventive and curative services to populations in villages, and Urban Comprehensive Health Centers, which serve urban populations and are staffed by multidisciplinary teams including physicians, nurses, and midwives [8]. These PHC networks form the backbone of Iran’s public health infrastructure and are intended to provide accessible, continuous, and comprehensive care particularly for vulnerable groups [9].

The increasing number of older adults is placing a greater strain on healthcare systems, family dynamics, and community resources [10]. Declining mortality rates and increased life expectancy have led to a growing number of individuals living with chronic diseases, disabilities, and functional limitations [11, 12]. Notably, approximately 75% of individuals over the age of 60 are affected by at least one chronic condition, while nearly half suffer from two or more comorbidities [13]. Chronic conditions increase the need for geriatric care, which includes home-based services, hospitalization, and long-term institutional care [14]. Hospital-centered care systems are often inefficient and costly for managing the complex health and social challenges of an aging population [15]. In contrast, well-functioning PHC systems particularly those incorporating geriatric competencies can effectively address service delivery gaps by providing accessible, coordinated, and person-centered care [16]. Low-income countries face challenges due to insufficient funding, fragmented funding sources, and inequitable allocation of resources [17, 18]. Under-resourcing, political constraints [17, 19], and weak integration pose significant challenges in allocating financial resources to PHC in middle-income countries [20]. Research has identified several challenges affecting the PHC in Iran, including the limited involvement of the private sector in service delivery, overall resource shortages, inequitable distribution of available resources, and an incomplete or underdeveloped health information system [21].

The WHO has launched the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) initiative, offering a comprehensive approach that emphasizes functional ability, early identification of decline, and person-centered care planning [22]. This holistic approach not only enhances the quality of care but also improves the overall quality of life for older individuals [23, 24]. Although Iran’s PHC system has successfully improved health indicators such as expanding vaccination programs, reducing maternal and infant mortality, and controlling communicable diseases, it remains under-equipped to address the multidimensional needs of older adults [25–27]. Recent evaluations indicate that the implementation of ICOPE has not yet translated into measurable improvements in perceived service quality among older Iranians [28, 29], highlighting the need to first address foundational weaknesses in PHC delivery.

Improving the quality of PHC services is essential for encouraging older adults to actively engage in their care [30]. A critical step is assessing the gap between their expectations and actual experiences of PHC. A robust framework for such an assessment is the SERVQUAL model, which measures service quality across five dimensions: tangibles, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy. While several Iranian studies have examined service quality gaps in hospitals [31–34], few have focused specifically on older adults within the PHC context [28]. Addressing this gap is vital for designing age-responsive, equitable, and effective PHC services. Therefore, this study therefore aims to (1) assess the gap between expectations and perceptions of PHC service quality among a sample of Iranian older adults across the six dimensions of the SERVQUAL model, and (2) identify key sociodemographic determinants of older adults’ expectations of PHC quality.

Method

This cross-sectional analytical study is based on survey data collected to assess the expectation–perception gap in PHC service quality among older adults in Qazvin Province, Iran. A cross-sectional design was appropriate because it enables efficient measurement of both expectations and perceptions within a defined population at a single point in time, which aligns with the SERVQUAL model’s requirement to capture these constructs concurrently for gap analysis.

A multi-stage sampling method was employed to select older participants. Qazvin Province comprises six counties: Qazvin, Abyek, Avaj, Takestan, Bouin Zahra, and Alborz. These counties served as the primary strata. Within each county, residency status (urban vs. rural) defined secondary sub-strata. From each sub-stratum, 1–10 comprehensive PHC centers were randomly selected as clusters, resulting in a total of 39 centers. The number of participants drawn from each center was proportional to the center’s registered older adult population, ensuring representativeness. Within each selected center, eligible older adults were randomly sampled from health registries using simple random sampling via a computer-generated list. Selected older adults were contacted and invited to visit the PHC center based on their region of residence at a specific time, if they were willing to participate in the study. During the phone call, potential participants were provided with an explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures. Participants needed to be 60 years or older, have a health record at a PHC center, and be willing to participate. Those with a diagnosed mental illness or any physical or cognitive conditions that could hinder effective communication or the ability to visit the PHC center and complete the questionnaire were excluded from the study. Of the 787 individuals initially contacted, 557 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 70.80%. Non-response was primarily due to the inability to reach participants after three contact attempts, the inability to visit the PHC center unaccompanied, physical accessibility barriers such as the lack of elevators or the presence of stairs at the PHC center, recent consultation with a specialist physician within the past month, or exclusion during screening because of cognitive impairment or severe physical disability that precluded reliable participation.

The sample size (n) for this study was calculated using the following formula:

Using a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a margin of error (e) of 0.07, and an estimated population variance (δ²) of 0.62 from a previous similar study [24], the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 485 participants. Accounting for a 15% non-response rate, the final target sample size was set at 557.

Instruments

Demographic information checklist, and the SERVQUAL scale were used for collecting the data.

Age, gender, education level, living arrangements, place of residence, marital status, source of income, type of underlying health conditions, number of care visits in the past year, distance from the home to the PHC center, and kind of access to the PHC center.

The Service Quality Model (SERVQUAL) scale, developed by Parasuraman et al. [35], is a well-established instrument used to assess service quality based on the perceptions and expectations of service recipients [35]. We used the latest translated and standardized version of the SERVQUAL scale, as employed by Cheraghi et al. [28]. It comprises 56 items, with 28 items focusing on expectations and 28 on perceptions. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high), yielding a total score between 28 and 130 This instrument evaluates service quality across six dimensions: tangibles (5 items) that assesses the appearance of physical facilities, equipment, and personnel; reliability (5 items) that measures the ability to consistently deliver services accurately and dependably, provide correct information, and demonstrate a willingness to educate clients; responsiveness (5 items) that assesses staff willingness and promptness in delivering services and addressing clients’ concerns; assurance (5 items) gauges the knowledge, competence, and professionalism of staff and the organization in building trust and confidence among service users; empathy (4 items) focuses on understanding, closeness, and provision of personalized attention to service recipients; and access (4 items) evaluates the availability and accessibility of services, staff, and equipment. The SERVQUAL scale measures services in two distinct sections: the service recipient’s expectations and their perceptions of the service. Older participants were asked to respond to items on two separate occasions. Before receiving care, they provided their ideal expectations for service quality. After receiving care, their opinions were gathered regarding their actual perceptions of the service quality they experienced [36]. The service quality gap was calculated by subtracting perception scores from expectation scores. A positive score indicated that services exceeded expectations, a negative score reflected unmet expectations and thus a quality gap, while a score of zero suggested that service delivery met patient expectations. Studies have employed the SERVQUAL model to evaluate healthcare service quality in different Iranian settings, including healthcare centers [28, 37] and hospitals [32, 33]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for older adults’ perceptions ranged from 0.75 to 0.84, and for their expectations, it ranged from 0.69 to 0.82.

Data were collected over a three-month period from August to October 2024 via face-to-face interviews conducted by trained health professionals using standardized questionnaires with eligible older adults. This process commenced after providing detailed explanations about the study and obtaining informed consent from all participants. To prepare for data collection, an educational training session was held for the health professionals. This session ensured that they were proficient in interview techniques, data collection procedures, and the proper administration of the questionnaire, which contributed to the overall reliability of the study’s findings.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R statistical software, Version 4.5.1. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. To assess the difference between older adults’ expectations and perceptions of PHC service quality, a paired-sample t-test was performed. The SERVQUAL gap score, computed as: Expectation Score – Perception Score for each dimension and overall. Consistent with SERVQUAL conventions [35], a negative gap indicates unmet expectations (perceived quality < expected quality), which signifies a service quality deficit. To identify predictors of PHC quality expectations, we employed linear mixed-effects and PHC centers were specified as random intercepts in all models. First, univariate regression analyses were performed to examine the association between each predictor variable and PHC quality expectations of older adults. Variables that showed a statistically significant association (p < 0.05) in the univariate analyses were then included in the subsequent multivariate linear regression model. The dependent variable was the older adults’ expectation score and independent variables included sociodemographic and healthcare-access factors: age, distance to PHC center, place of residence, marital status, ethnicity, level of education, employment status, and economic status.

Model diagnostics were assessed to ensure the appropriateness of the linear mixed-effects model. Multicollinearity among the independent variables was examined using variance inflation factors (VIFs); all VIF values were below 2.5 (range: 1.04–1.81), indicating no problematic multicollinearity. The conditional R² (variance explained by both fixed and random effects) was 0.31, while the marginal R² (variance explained by fixed effects alone) was 0.21. Normality assumptions were evaluated using Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The study included a total of 557 older adults, with a mean age of 67.97 ± 6.56 years (range: 60–103). The average distance of older participants’ homes from PHC centers was 13.75 ± 9.99 KM. Most participants resided in urban areas (n = 416, 74.7%). Nearly half of the sample was from Qazvin city (n = 271, 48.7%), followed by Takestan (n = 81, 14.5%), Buin Zahra (n = 65, 11.7%), Abyek (n = 35, 6.3%), and Avaj (n = 24, 4.3%). In summary, this study identified significant gaps between older adults’ expectations and perceptions of PHC quality across all SERVQUAL dimensions, most notably in tangibles and responsiveness. Furthermore, key demographic factors including proximity to a health center, ethnicity, employment, and economic status, were significantly associated with the level of service quality expected by the older adults. These findings highlight specific service dimensions and user profiles that should be prioritized to better align PHC delivery with the expectations of older adults.

Discussion

This study found a significant negative gap between older adults’ expectations and their perceptions of PHC services across all six dimensions of the SERVQUAL model. This finding aligns with the majority of previous research conducted specifically in PHC settings [28, 38, 39]. For instance, studies by Cheraghi et al. [28] in Iran and Jonkisz et al. [39] in Poland similarly concluded that PHC services provided to older adults failed to meet their expectations in any dimension of the SERVQUAL model [28, 39]. This consistent gap across various contexts indicates systemic challenges within PHC systems instead of isolated issues. The main causal pathway likely involves a combination of structural constraints, such as resource limitations, and process-related inefficiencies, like service delivery models [25, 40].

The results indicated that the most substantial gaps in PHC service quality were observed in the tangibles and responsiveness dimensions. The physical aspects, such as the infrastructure and facilities of PHC centers, are essential for improving service quality for older adults [41]. The gap in the responsiveness dimension may indicate slow service delivery and a reduced willingness among staff to address the concerns of service recipients. These findings are consistent with other previous studies, such as that of Cheraghi et al. [28] in Iran, who identified high and significant gaps in tangibles in PHC services. Aghamolaei et al. [31] also reported a high and significant gap in the responsiveness dimension on hospital service users [31]. To enhance service quality from the perspective of older adults, it is important to empower staff, provide quicker assistance, and improve communication. Given that the level of responsiveness can significantly affect service quality for older adults, creating opportunities to enhance responsive skills is crucial [42]. However, Kashfi et al. (2019) found that the largest gap was in the empathy dimension, followed by responsiveness [29]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the sample of studies, as Kashfi et al. research included individuals over the age of 18, potentially reflecting different priority concerns across the adult lifespan.

Among all dimensions of service quality, reliability showed the smallest gap between the expectations and perception of older adults on PHC quality. This suggests that PHC services are meeting expectations for reliability more effectively than they do for other aspects of service quality. However, it is important to note that, despite this smaller discrepancy, reliability still does not meet the high-quality standards expected by the older adults. These findings support previous research by Kashfi et al. (2019) and Papanikolaou et al. [43], which also identified reliability as having the narrowest service quality gap [29, 43]. In contrast, Sharifi found that the smallest gap was in the tangibles dimension [24]. These differing results may reflect variations in cultural contexts or the characteristics of study populations across the various research settings.

A significant finding was that ethnicity predicts older adults’ expectations regarding the quality of PHC services. Specifically, Farsi-speaking elders reported higher expectations than other ethnic groups. This finding corresponds with Verhagen (2014) research on the varying patterns of healthcare utilization among different ethnic groups [44]. Farsi-speaking elders may have acquired significant health literacy due to their better linguistic connection with television, radio, and print media. A study suggests that older adults with higher health literacy utilize health services more frequently [45]. Additionally, language barriers can lead to dissatisfaction for both the service recipient and the healthcare provider [46]. Therefore, Farsi-speaking older adults are likely to report greater satisfaction, subsequently leading to elevated expectations.

The current study findings indicated that economic status significantly predicts the expectations of PHC services. Specifically, older adults with moderate economic status reported higher expectations than their peers in either bad or good financial situations. This finding contrasts with Jonkisz et al. [39], who reported no significant association between economic status and health service expectations. Variations in study design, measurement tools, or sample characteristics could account for this discrepancy. Older adults with low socioeconomic status often encounter structural barriers that restrict their access to PHC services [47–49]. This limited engagement may lead them to lower expectations of care. In contrast, individuals with high economic status often avoid public PHC services, instead choosing private clinics that are viewed as more efficient and respectful [50]. This decreased reliance on public PHC may lead to disengagement and subsequently lower expectations of these services.

The results of the present study suggest that employment status significantly influences older adults’ expectations of PHC services, with employed individuals reporting lower expectations compared to housewives. This finding may relate to differences in healthcare engagement, available time, and perceived health needs between the two groups. Employed older adults often experience time constraints that make it less likely for them to visit PHC centers. As a result, their expectations may be lower due to limited interaction with PHC services. Additionally, their healthcare needs may be met through occupational health services or private providers, which can further diminish their expectations of public PHC services. Additionally, this result may be partly explained by the prominent role of women particularly housewives as family caregivers in Iran [51], which increases their contact with PHC services. These interactions can result in higher expectations regarding service availability, provider responsiveness, and overall quality of care.

An additional finding highlighted the role of geographical proximity, demonstrating that older adults living nearer to PHC centers reported higher expectations of healthcare service quality. This suggests that physical access is a foundational determinant of both engagement and expectation formation. Significant accessibility barriers, particularly long travel distances, hinder utilization of PHC services [52]. Older adults residing in remote areas often face compounded challenges, including limited public transportation, high out-of-pocket travel costs, and physical mobility constraints [53, 54]. Over time, reduced engagement with the PHC system may result in diminished expectations among older adults, not as an indicator of satisfaction, but as a form of adaptive resignation to systemic access barriers.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to infer causal relationships. Longitudinal cohort studies are therefore recommended in future research to better elucidate causal pathways and track the evolution of expectations and health service utilization among older adults. Another limitation of this study is the reliance on self-reported data from older participants, which may be subject to response bias due to factors such as memory recall issues and social desirability. To minimize this bias, clear explanations about the study’s purpose were provided, and participants were assured of the confidentiality of their responses. Also, the assessment of older individuals’ expectations was based solely on quantitative instruments, which may not fully capture their health service needs and preferences. To gain a more comprehensive and meaningful understanding, future research should incorporate qualitative methods that allow older participants to articulate their perspectives in their own words. A further potential limitation is that the sampling approach may have underrepresented the most vulnerable older adults specifically those who were homebound or had severe functional impairments. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings to these older people may be limited.

Conclusions

This study revealed a significant gap between older adults’ expectations and perceptions across all SERVQUAL dimensions, with the largest disparities observed in “tangibles” and “responsiveness.” To address these gaps, practical solutions include modernizing health center infrastructure and equipment, training staff in communication and responsiveness skills, and establishing continuous quality monitoring systems. These findings directly inform Iran’s PHC reform initiatives and the implementation of the ICOPE framework by identifying service quality gaps that need to be addressed to create truly age-friendly primary care services. The study provides a baseline for evaluating future quality improvement interventions aimed at enhancing elderly care within Iran’s healthcare system.

Implications for practice

Based on the significant gaps in service quality for older adults identified in this study, several targeted interventions are recommended to strengthen the Iranian PHC system. First, implementing mandatory, specialized training programs for PHC staff is essential to enhance geriatric communication skills, responsiveness, and empathy. Second, strategic investment is needed to upgrade the physical infrastructure and medical equipment of PHC centers to meet the accessibility and comfort needs of an aging population. Third, developing and institutionalizing comprehensive, evidence-based geriatric care standards within the national PHC framework would ensure consistent, high-quality service delivery. These concrete steps address the core gaps in tangibles and responsiveness directly revealed by the SERVQUAL analysis and are critical for building a PHC system capable of meeting the expectations of older adults. Furthermore, successfully implementing these recommendations would provide essential operational support for the rollout and sustainability of national healthy aging initiatives, such as the ICOPE program in Iran.
