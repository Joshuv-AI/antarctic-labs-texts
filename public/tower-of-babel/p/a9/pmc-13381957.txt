Spatial overlap of palm trees and triatomine distribution areas in Brazil
Authors: Felipe de Oliveira, Ane Luíse Quinze Dias de Faro de Oliveira, Raphael Testai, Matheus Pinheiro Ferreira, Cleber Galvão, Diogo Souza Bezerra Rocha, Ana Maria Jansen, Samanta Cristina das Chagas Xavier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrypanosoma cruzi transmission occurs in the sylvatic environment, requiring a profound understanding of the interaction between triatomines and their natural ecotopes. This study investigated the spatial association between four triatomine species (Rhodnius robustus, Rhodnius pictipes, Rhodnius neglectus, Rhodnius nasutus) and palm trees (Arecaceae) in Brazil. We employed analyses of ecological niche overlap (Schoener’s D Index), spatial correlation (Spearman), and an assessment of vector presence across palm richness strata.Two distinct ecological profiles were identified. R. robustus and R. pictipes exhibited a high dependence on diverse environments, with strong spatial correlations (up to 0.647) and a presence concentrated (up to 86.41%) in strata with high palm richness. Attalea aculeata and Mauritia flexuosa emerged as their primary ecotopes (D up to 0.9161). Conversely R. neglectus and R. nasutus showed greater plasticity, with weaker correlations and significant presence in low-richness areas. Notably, Copernicia prunifera was a highly specific ecotope for R. nasutus (D = 0.9106) in the Caatinga.The integration of findings on niche overlap, spatial correlation, and distribution by palm richness provides critical insights into vector dynamics and the persistence of the T. cruzi sylvatic cycle. Introduction

American trypanosomiasis, caused by Trypanosoma cruzi (Chagas), is primarily a sylvatic enzootic disease involving several species of mammals and triatomines. These cycles can result in human infection in different scenarios1.Understanding the peculiarities of local landscape ecology and the biotic and abiotic variables that modulate this complex transmission system is essential for defining and establishing prophylaxis and control measures for the human disease2. In the current epidemiological context of Chagas disease, knowledge of environmental variables is particularly relevant, as vectorial-oral transmission has become the main route of human infection in Brazil, especially in the Amazon region, where outbreaks are frequently associated with the consumption of food contaminated with T. cruzi3. This scenario has gained prominence following the control of Triatoma infestans, historically responsible for domiciliary contaminative vector transmission, leading to the re-emergence of oral transmission as the predominant pathway of infection in the country. In this context, infections may also occur through contact with wild triatomine bugs, either by direct exposure in natural environments or by the invasion of these insects into residential areas4.

The control of vector-borne transmission of T. cruzi remains a complex challenge, requiring a reorientation of public health approaches5. Although primary vectors have been eliminated in several regions, the persistence of native species well adapted to both natural and artificial ecotopes highlights the need for broader and more sensitive surveillance strategies capable of detecting the presence of triatomines in wild and peridomestic environments6. In this context, rather than pursuing eradication, it is essential to prioritize continuous control measures and the implementation of active surveillance systems7.

One of the major challenges of current surveillance is accurately recognizing natural triatomine ecotopes. These environments include rock piles, palm trees, animal refuges, they provide suitable habitats for several triatomine species and often overlap with peridomestic and domestic areas. This facilitates the invasion of human One of the major challenges of current surveillance is accurately identifying the habitats of wild triatomines. These habitats, including rock piles, palm trees, and animal shelters, provide suitable conditions for several species. When located near human dwellings, these ecotopes allow vectors to invade domestic environments8.The complexity and diversity of these ecotopes hinder early detection and control, especially since many native species can maintain stable colonies in sylvatic and peridomestic settings9. Recent spatial analyses have shown that areas with higher indices of natural triatomine infection are strongly associated with regions of increased transmission risk. This reinforces the need for sensitive, integrative surveillance strategies that monitor natural ecotopes10.

In this context, the role of palm trees is prominent, as they are frequently associated with species of the genus Rhodnius, which are vectors of T. cruzi11. The presence and individual attributes of these palm trees have been shown to be determining factors in triatomine infestation, serving as useful indicators for identifying areas with transmission risk12. Specific characteristics of these ecotopes can thus act as ecological indicators of the presence of enzootic parasite cycles.

Mapping the geographical distribution areas of palm trees represents a promising strategy for monitoring T. cruzi transmission, especially considering the association of palms with sylvatic triatomines13–15.This approach allows for the recognition of environmental variables and the natural ecotopes of triatomines, thereby facilitating an understanding of the environmental conditions that favor the distribution of triatomines in these habitats16.

In this context, geotechnologies have enabled the production of eco-epidemiological analyses of T. cruzi transmission in nature17. Ecological Niche Modelling (ENM), is a technique that estimates and analyses the potential distribution area of a species (its Fundamental Niche) by extrapolating the environmental characteristics of known occurrence data points.These results produce models representing areas that are environmentally suitable for maintaining the species18,19. Ecological niche modelling, understood as correlative modelling of a species, requires two main types of data: biotic variables, including georeferenced occurrence records, species interactions, and dispersal capacity, and abiotic variables that represent environmental conditions potentially shaping the species ecological requirements18. The data must then be processed using machine learning algorithms to estimate areas with environmental conditions favourable to the presence of a given species20,21.

This study aims to perform ecological niche modeling of natural triatomine ecotopes. The objective was to identify areas of overlap between species and the environmental conditions that influence their potential distribution. This information will contribute to surveillance and control strategies based on spatial evidence.

Methods

Acquisition of climatic and environmental variables

The climatic variables were obtained from the WorldClim image database. (https://www.worldclim.org/) at a resolution of 30 arc-seconds (~ 1 km²), 19 bioclimatic variables, all in raster format (GeoTIFF), were acquired.The wind speed and water vapor pressure variables were obtained from WorldClim as monthly layers22. The 12 monthly rasters were then used to calculate the annual minimum and maximum values on a pixels basis for each variable. Then, the annual range was computed as the difference between these values to generate a single raster layer representing the intra-annual variability of each variable.

The landscape variables were obtained from the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform (https://earthengine.google.com/). The variables used included the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), derived from the MODIS sensor onboard the Terra satellite, using the dataset (MODIS/061/MOD13A2) at a 1000 m resolution, in raster format (GeoTIFF). Land Surface Temperature (LST) was obtained from the dataset (MOD11A1 V6.1) with a spatial resolution of 1000 m, and Topographic Diversity was derived from the dataset (CSP/ERGo/1_0/Global/SRTM_topoDiversity) from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM). All images were resampled to 30 arc-seconds (~ 1 km²). The NDVI, EVI, and LST variables were produced by calculating the median pixel values of the images from 2000 to 2022.

The topographic variables were obtained from the AMBDATA database (http://www.dpi.inpe.br/Ambdata/index.php), which is produced by the National Institute for Space Research (INPE). The variables used included Altitude, Slope, and Aspect, generated from Shuttle Radar Topographic Mission (SRTM) data. All images were resampled to a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds (~ 1 km²). The selection of variables for the Ecological Niche Modeling (ENM) was performed using Spearman’s correlation among them, with variables selected that had a correlation coefficient within the range (−0.6 ≤ α ≤ 0.6).

Acquisition of database of occurrences

Palms from the Arecaceae family were selected. First, a literature review was conducted to identify the main palm genera described as natural ecotopes for triatomines. A total of 10 species were selected: Acrocomia aculeata (604 occurrences), Astrocaryum aculeatum (270 occurrences), Attalea maripa (251 occurrences), Attalea phalerata (273 occurrences), Attalea speciosa (107 occurrences), Copernicia prunifera (383 occurrences), Mauritia flexuosa (538 occurrences), Oenocarpus bataua (219 occurrences), Syagrus coronata (160 occurrences), and Syagrus oleracea (129 occurrences). Occurrence data for the selected palm species were compiled from available databases, covering the period from 1990 to 2023.

Occurrence data for triatomines were collected from the Specieslink and GBIF databases and the DataTri database, which includes bibliographic data (Ceccarelli et al., 2018). The selected species were: Rhodnius nasutus (608 occurrences), Rhodnius neglectus (736 occurrences), Rhodnius robustus (279 occurrences), Rhodnius pictipes (188 occurrences). Triatomine occurrence records covered the period from 1980 to 2023.

Ecological niche modeling

In the first preprocessing step, using RStudio, the following filters were applied to the database: a filter to remove points with duplicate coordinates, a filter to retain only one occurrence per pixel, a filter to remove points in pixels without radiometric information, and a geographic filter to select occurrence points located within the Brazil (Table 1).

To generate pseudo-absences in areas with low or no environmental suitability, an inclusion buffer (area α, with a radius equal to the species displacement capacity of 10 km) and an exclusion buffer (area β, where points will not be generated) were considered. Points were then generated in the area between the inclusion and exclusion buffers (α – β). The Bioclim modeling algorithm was used to assess environmental suitability at presence points22,23. Areas where 90% of the highest suitability values for the presence points were located were considered as presence areas, while those with less than 10% suitability were treated as absence areas. This approach was adopted to select pseudo-absences in areas with low environmental suitability, reducing the likelihood of including false absences in regions that may be suitable for the species24–26. Pseudo-absence points were then generated within the defined range, with 1.000 pseudo-absence points produced in the geographic space for the evaluated species24–26.

For the ecological niche models, the modleR package in R27, developed by the research team at the Rio de Janeiro Botanical Garden, was used. Three machine learning algorithms were employed: Maxent, Random Forests, and Support Vector Machines. To test the accuracy of the models, the database was divided into five partitions, with 20% of the occurrence points used to evaluate model performance and 80% for training the algorithm. The k-fold cross-validation methodology was applied, with 10 iterations for each algorithm. The models were evaluated using the TSS (True Skill Statistics), with a threshold of TSS ≥ 0.7 to select models with good performance.

The overlap between suitability areas of species was quantified using Schoener’s D. This metric ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates no overlap between the two distributions, and 1 indicates complete overlap. The analysis was performed both at the national scale (Brazil) and separately for each Brazilian biome, by spatially partitioning the suitability maps according to biome boundaries. This approach allowed the assessment of overlap patterns at different spatial scales.

Correlation analysis between triatomine and palm suitability areas

Spatial associations between palm tree species richness (a continuous variable) and triatomine presence/absence (a dichotomous variable) were investigated using the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for each vector species.

This approach quantifies the strength and direction of monotonic relationships between variables, making it suitable for nonparametric and spatially structured data. All raster files were reprojected and resampled to the same 1 km spatial resolution, with national coverage. A palm richness raster, with continuous values ranging from 0 to 10 (presence per pixel), was used as the independent variable. For the triatomines, four binary presence/absence models (1 = probable presence, 0 = absence), previously generated by ecological niche modeling, were used.

The analyses were conducted exclusively on valid pixels, excluding areas with NoData or null values in both the triatomine and palm layers. Only pixels where both layers had valid data were analyzed. The correlation between the presence of the four triatomine species and palm richness was evaluated using the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, considering the entire extent of Brazil.

Analysis of spatial overlap between palm tree richness and triatomines

To investigate this spatial association further, we conducted an analysis of the relative presence of triatomines in different palm richness level using binary predictive models for the species R. robustus, R. pictipes, R. neglectus, and R. nasutus. Palm richness was categorized into four discrete strata: Class 0: absence of palms (richness = 0); Class 1: low richness (1–3 species); Class 2: medium richness (4–6 species); and Class 3: high richness (7–10 species). This approach enabled the calculation of the percentage of triatomine species present in each richness level based on the total number of pixels in the level, as well as the species triatomine presence distribution relative to the total number of species palms in the national territory.

Results and Discussion

Selection of variables

The selected variables were BIO 8 Mean Temperature of Wettest Quarter, BIO13 = Precipitation of Wettest Month, BIO 15 Precipitation Seasonality (Coefficient of Variation), BIO18 = Precipitation of Warmest Quarter, Land Surface Temperature (LST) wind speed and slope. Spearman correlation analysis was performed among all predictors, retaining only those with low correlation to reduce multicollinearity and avoid redundancy in the models. This procedure also helps prevent overfitting and improves model stability. The selected variables were maintained based on their ecological relevance in defining environmental conditions that influence species distributions, particularly those related to water availability, seasonal variability, and thermal regimes, which are critical for species survival, reproduction, and establishment. Topographic variables such as slope capture habitat heterogeneity, while variables such as land surface temperature (LST) and wind speed represent microclimatic conditions not fully described by the bioclimatic predictors. Climatic variables from WorldClim (1970–2000) represent long-term averages widely used as proxies for current conditions, while remote sensing variables (2000–2022) reflect more recent environmental dynamics. This approach is common in ecological niche modeling and allows the integration of stable climatic patterns with recent landscape changes. (Fig. 1). After applying spatial filters to the occurrence points in RStudio, the data underwent a validation process and were carefully prepared for use in the species distribution modeling (Fig. 2; Table 1).

Performance of the algorithms

The algorithms Maxent, Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine (SVM) showed consistent results, with all True Skill Statistic (TSS) values exceeding the cutoff point of 0.7, indicating high accuracy in the modeling. For each species, 30 partitions were generated 10 for each algorithm which were used to build a robust ensemble model of the potential distribution of ecotopes and triatomines.

As shown in (Table 2), the TSS values across species and algorithms ranged from moderate to high. Palm species such as S. coronata, A. aculeatum, and M. flexuosa exhibited consistently high TSS scores across all models. Among triatomines, R. robustus, R. pictipes, and R. nasutus showed strong predictive performance with TSS values frequently above 0.90. These results confirm the reliability and robustness of the modeling approaches employed in estimating the potential distribution of both ecotopes and vectors (Figs. 4 and 5).

Analysis of ecological niche modeling overlap areas

Schoener’s D index analysis revealed different patterns of ecological overlap between palm and triatomine species. The index values ranged from 0.0755 to 0.8899, indicating low to high ecological niche overlap among the species combinations (Fig. 3).

Niche overlap index of ecotopes (palms) and triatomine species, distributed in Brazil. Schoener’s D measures niche overlap between species, ranging from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (complete overlap).

Among the palm trees, a high overlap was observed between A. speciosa and A. phalerata (D = 0.838), as well as between M. flexuosa and A. aculeata (D = 0.8899). This suggests that these species share similar environmental conditions and, potentially, similar ecological functions in terms of habitat availability for vectors29. C. prunifera presented the lowest overlap indices with the other palms, which indicates a more restricted or distinct niche (Fig. 3).

Within the triatomine group, a high similarity between R. pictipes and R. robustus (D = 0.7341) was notable, in addition to the overlap between R. nasutus and R. neglectus (D = 0.7683). This suggests that these vector species may co-occur in similar environments, implying potential competitive interactions or the sharing of food and shelter sources29.

Among the highlighted pairs, C. prunifera showed high overlap with R. nasutus (D = 0.6247), corroborating previous records of this palm’s association with sylvatic and peridomestic populations of this vector, especially in semi-arid and Caatinga environments. Similarly, M. flexuosa presented high overlap values with R. robustus (D = 0.6717), which is consistent with studies that document the frequent presence of colonies of this species in palm groves of floodplain areas and humid forests31.

The high overlap observed between some palms of the genus Attalea, such as A. speciosa and A. phalerata, and triatomines like R. pictipes and R. neglectus also suggests a relevant niche sharing. These results point to the existence of ecological convergence zones between plant hosts and vectors, where the presence of palm trees can sustain vector populations, functioning as shelter, support for feeding, and reproduction sites29,30.

Amazon biome

The buriti palm M. flexuosa showed high overlap with R. robustus (D = 0.9145) and R. pictipes (D = 0.7297), reinforcing the strong evidence of an ecological association between this humid-area palm and triatomine species widely found in Amazonian environments12. Similarly, other palm species stood out in their interactions with vectors. The macaúba palm A. aculeata, for instance, demonstrated high overlap with R. robustus (D = 0.806) and R. pictipes (D = 0.7153), while A. maripa presented high overlap with R. pictipes (D = 0.8632) and R. robustus (D = 0.7371). A. speciosa also revealed expressive values with R. pictipes (D = 0.8235) and R. robustus (D = 0.7716) (Fig. 5).These results indicate that various species of the genus Attalea, along with M. flexuosa, share ecological niches with triatomines that are widely distributed and epidemiologically relevant in the region2(Figs. 4 and 6).

The high affinity of these Rhodnius species for M. flexuosa is biologically plausible due to the presence of associated fauna (birds, rodents) that serve as food sources for the vectors, and the palm’s structure which provides ideal shelter12,30. The vast extent and abundance of buriti groves in the Amazon make these areas crucial hotspots for maintaining the enzootic cycles of T. cruzi11,31. Similarly, the ubiquity of these Attalea palms in the Amazon, combined with high niche overlap, suggests that environmental degradation or deforestation may lead to the fragmentation of these habitats, potentially forcing vectors to seek resources in environments close to human dwellings, increasing the risk of domestic or peridomestic transmission14,32,33.

On the other hand, some interactions exhibited more moderate overlaps. The carnaúba palm C. prunifera, while generally showing lower values with typical Amazonian Rhodnius species, maintained moderate values with R. nasutus (D = 0.5057) and R. neglectus (D = 0.521)29. This may reflect a more restricted affinity of these triatomines to dry biomes like the Caatinga and transition zones, which are less represented in the Amazon, or areas of overlap of their ecological niches in specific contexts. The presence of these values in the Amazon can be explained by sampling in transition areas with other biomes, or by the plasticity of these species in utilizing less-than-ideal resources when available11,12,30.

Caatinga biome

The carnaúba palm C. prunifera showed a high overlap with R. nasutus (D = 0.8663), one of the triatomine species most commonly associated with this biome. This strong association is a key finding, confirming the importance of the carnaúba palm as a primary ecotope for R. nasutus in the Caatinga. R. neglectus also presented a notable overlap with C. prunifera (D = 0.4307) (Fig. 5), indicating that, although R. nasutus is the main vector in carnaúba palms, R. neglectus can also utilize it to some extent29(Figs. 4 and 6).

This high overlap is highly consistent with the literature, which recognizes the carnaúba palm as the main ecotope for R. nasutus in the semi-arid biome34,35. The architecture of the carnaúba palm, with its moisture-retaining leaves and multiple crevices and shelters, is ideal for the survival of R. nasutus in an environment with large thermal and water variations29. The moderate overlap of C. prunifera with R. neglectus (D = 0.4307) is also a relevant finding, demonstrating ecological plasticity or the exploration of less competitive niches, which may be more common in transition areas between the Caatinga and the Cerrado36(Figs. 4 and 6).

Other palms present in the matrix, such as the macaúba palm A. aculeata and the guariroba palm (S. oleracea), which have their distribution more associated with biomes like the Cerrado, also showed important interactions in the Caatinga. The macaúba palm revealed high overlap with R. neglectus (D = 0.5321) and R. nasutus (D = 0.279), reflecting the possible occurrences of these palms in transition areas or in pockets of higher humidity within the Caatinga29.

The guariroba palm, in turn, showed a significant overlap with R. neglectus (D = 0.2212) and R. nasutus (D = 0.1096). The presence of these palms, with lower overlaps in the Caatinga, reflects their more marginal distribution in this biome, limiting interaction with local triatomines, but indicating that, where present, they can still serve as secondary ecotopes37.

In contrast, triatomine species with niches more strongly associated with the Amazon, such as R. robustus and R. pictipes, showed consistently very low overlaps with Caatinga palms. R. robustus and R. pictipes exhibited minimal values with M. flexuosa (D = 0.0343 and D = 0.1171, respectively), a humid-environment palm that is rarer in the Caatinga, and also with C. prunifera (D = 0.0068 and D = 0.0122, respectively). These very low indices corroborate the niche specificity of these species for more humid and structurally different environments, reinforcing the clear biogeographical and ecological segregation relative to Caatinga triatomines. The climatic conditions and ecotope availability in the semi-arid region are incompatible with the niche requirements of these species that are more adapted to humid, forested environments29,38.

Cerrado biome

The guariroba palm S. oleracea emerged as a palm of high relevance, showing a very high overlap with R. neglectus (D = 0.7706), one of the most important and widely distributed triatomine species in the Cerrado. This interaction is fundamental, as R. neglectus is frequently found in palms in this biome and can invade the domestic environment39. The macaúba palm A. aculeata also demonstrated a high overlap with R. neglectus (D = 0.9211) (Fig. 5), one of the highest values observed, underscoring its importance as a key ecotope for this vector species38. This finding is crucial because R. neglectus is recognized as the main sylvatic vector in the Cerrado, with a high capacity to colonize domestic and peridomestic environments, especially in areas where these palms are abundant39. The structure of Attalea aculeata, with its large canopies and ability to shelter bird and mammal nests, creates an ideal microenvironment for R. neglectus colonies9.

Other typical Cerrado palms, such as the buriti palm M. flexuosa, which characterizes the “veredas” (wetlands), exhibited a notable overlap with R. neglectus (D = 0.9036) and R. nasutus (D = 0.813). Although R. nasutus is more commonly associated with the Caatinga, its overlap with M. flexuosa in the Cerrado suggests a considerable plasticity of this species and the importance of transition areas between biomes. Furthermore, Attalea speciosa also showed high overlap with R. neglectus (D = 0.855) and R. nasutus (D = 0.8681), indicating that various Attalea species are widely used by these vectors in the biome. In the Cerrado, buriti “veredas” and areas with Attalea offer conditions that may favor the presence and colonization by R. nasutus, this is especially true in locations with some influence from more semiarid elements38. This suggests that surveillance should cover a broader spectrum of ecotopes and vector species in areas of contact between biomes.

This low overlap reiterates the niche specificity of these species for Amazonian ecotopes and the lower relevance of typical Cerrado palms for their populations, demonstrating a clear spatial and habitat segregation. This low overlap is an expected result, validating the niche specificity of these species for more humid and structurally different environments, such as the Amazon29.

The maintenance of a landscape matrix with these native Cerrado palms, which are often removed for agricultural expansion, is vital for the preservation of sylvatic cycles and for preventing an increase in vector domiciliation.

Atlantic forest biome

The guariroba palm S. oleracea and the macaúba palm A. aculeata, palms with wide distribution in Atlantic Forest fragments and transition areas, showed significant overlap with R. neglectus (D = 0.5582 and D = 0.4958, respectively) (Fig. 5). Although these values may be moderate compared to overlaps observed in other biomes, they indicate that these palms continue to offer resources and shelter for R. neglectus in the Atlantic Forest environment (Figs. 4 and 6). A. speciosa also revealed considerable overlap with R. neglectus (D = 0.5693). Given the continuous degradation and the proximity of forest fragments to urban and rural areas, these interactions represent a potential risk of Chagas disease transmission to humans, either through domiciliary invasion by triatomines41,42.

The presence of R. nasutus, more typically associated with dry biomes, showed overlap with some palms. Interestingly, the carnaúba palm C. prunifera presented a moderate overlap with R. nasutus (D = 0.3074), which can be explained by the punctual occurrence of this palm in transition zones or altered areas of the Atlantic Forest that approach Caatinga or Cerrado conditions, as well as the plasticity of R. nasutus. This highlights the importance of considering ecotones and interactions at multiple geographical scales.

Triatomine species with niches more associated with the Amazon, such as R. robustus and R. pictipes, generally showed lower overlaps with most of the palms analyzed in the Atlantic Forest. However, it is interesting to note that R. robustus showed slightly higher values with the ouricuri palm S. coronata (D = 0.0127) and the patauá palm O. bataua (D = 0.3218), while R. pictipes demonstrated overlap with A. marijpa (D = 0.1073) and the patauá palm (D = 0.1724). Although these values are still low in a general context, they may indicate very localized of occurrence or adaptation in specific micro-habitats of the Atlantic Forest, or the presence of these species in interface areas with other biomes. It can be inferred that the connectivity between forest fragments and the presence of ecological corridors could facilitate the dispersal of vectors between biomes43. Surveillance in the Atlantic Forest, therefore, must be comprehensive, considering not only the most common species, but also the possibility of occurrence of species from adjacent areas.

Pantanal biome

The macaúba palm A. aculeata proved to be a palm of central relevance, showing high overlaps with R. robustus (D = 0.6736) and R. pictipes (D = 0.7062). These results are particularly significant, as R. robustus and R. pictipes, although more commonly associated with the Amazon, can also be found in some areas of the Pantanal and in other transition biomes12. Other Attalea species, such as A. speciosa, also revealed considerable overlap with R. robustus (D = 0.7518) and R. pictipes (D = 0.7481), reinforcing the importance of the genus Attalea as an ecotope for these vector species in the Pantanal (Fig. 5). The dense canopies of the macaúba palm provide protection against floods and predators, in addition to being frequently inhabited by mammals and birds, which are potential blood sources for triatomines44.

The overlap of the ouricuri palm S. coronata with R. robustus (D = 0.5443) and R. pictipes (D = 0.5386) is notable, which suggests the importance of this palm in some vector interactions in the Pantanal, even though it is more common in the Northeast. Furthermore, the patauá palm O. bataua, despite being a palm more typical of the Amazon, showed considerable overlap with R. robustus (D = 0.6044) and R. pictipes (D = 0.5942), which may indicate the presence of transitional environments or specific micro-habitats in the Pantanal that support this association. “Cordilheiras” (mountain ridges) and higher areas that are not flooded can, however, serve as important refuges for triatomine populations during flood periods45.

On the other hand, R. neglectus and R. nasutus, species more characteristic of the adjacent Cerrado and Caatinga, showed lower overlaps with most of the predominantly Pantanal palms. R. neglectus presented a moderate overlap value with A. aculeata (D = 0.2917) and R. nasutus with A. aculeata (D = 0.5021). This may reflect a lower adaptability of these species to the flooded environments of the Pantanal or a preference for drier46.

Pampa biome

The typical palms from other biomes present in the analysis, such as the guariroba palm S. oleracea and the macaúba palm A. aculeata, which may occur in transition areas or in specific fragments of the Pampa, demonstrated moderate overlap with R. neglectus (D = 0.7134 and D = 0.7214, respectively) (Fig. 5). These values suggest that, even in a biome where they are not the dominant species, these palms can serve as ecotopes for R. neglectus in specific niches (Figs. 4 and 6). Although the matrix used presents these values, the natural occurrence and density of these palms in the Pampa are very restricted, concentrating in transition areas with the Cerrado (in the north of Rio Grande do Sul) or in specific plantations. Thus, the overlap, where it occurs, would be more punctual and not representative of a generalized pattern for the biome.

On the other hand, Rhodnius species more typically associated with hot and humid biomes, such as R. robustus and R. pictipes, showed consistently low overlaps with most of the listed palms39. For example, R. robustus showed minimal values with M. flexuosa (D = 0.0247) and Syagrus oleracea (D = 0.013), and R. pictipes also with reduced values. This low overlap corroborates their lower occurrence and adaptation to the colder environments and with less diversity of large-sized palms characteristic of the Pampa, reinforcing niche segregation in relation to the predominant conditions in this biome. The colder climate of Southern Brazil and the scarcity of palms that provide the adequate niche act as biogeographical barriers for the colonization and establishment of these triatomine populations. The results obtained demonstrate that, even in a biome like the Pampa, with a lower abundance of large-sized palms compared to other Brazilian regions11.

Correlation between triatomine presence and palm richness

The correlation between the presence of the four triatomine species and palm richness was evaluated using the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, considering the entire extent of Brazil. The results indicate different degrees of spatial association between the analyzed species and palm diversity.

R. pictipes showed the highest positive correlation (ρ = 0.647), indicating a strong association with areas of higher palm richness. This result suggests that R. pictipes may significantly depend on palm diversity for its distribution, possibly using them as habitat or a resource source. R. robustus showed a moderate correlation (ρ = 0.456), also indicating a considerable relationship with palm richness, but less intense than R. pictipes. The species R. neglectus (ρ = 0.299) and R. nasutus (ρ = 0.212) presented weaker correlations, which may indicate that these triatomines have greater ecological plasticity or use other types of environments besides palms13. It is important to note that all coefficients had statistically significant p-values (p < 0.001), reinforcing the robustness of the detected associations (Fig. 7).

These findings of correlation with palm richness directly complement the high niche overlaps observed (Fig. 8). Palms such as A. aculeata, M. flexuosa, and species of the genus Attalea such as A. speciosa and A. maripa consistently demonstrated the highest overlaps with R. robustus and R. pictipes in various regions, especially in the Amazon and Pantanal29. The strong correlation of R. pictipes and R. robustus with palm richness highlights the ecological importance of these plant species, as they provide shelter, stable microclimatic conditions higher humidity and thermal buffering, and support a high diversity of vertebrate hosts that serve as food sources for the vectors, thereby creating favorable conditions for their maintenance12.In contrast, the lower correlation of R. neglectus and R. nasutus with palm richness suggests a reduced dependence on high palm diversity. These species exhibit greater ecological plasticity, allowing them to exploit a wider range of palm species, including S. oleracea and Copernicia prunifera, as well as other ecotopes across different biomes, such as the Cerrado, Atlantic Forest, and Caatinga29,47. The high overlap of R. nasutus with the carnaúba palm in the Caatinga (D = 0.9106) is a clear example of this regional specificity which, although it does not translate into a strong correlation with general palm richness at a national level, is crucial for local dynamics.

The spatial association between palm richness and triatomine occurrence, assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation, should be interpreted with caution. When applied to spatial raster data, this approach is influenced by spatial autocorrelation, which may inflate correlation values and violate the assumption of independence among observations. Therefore, the results are best understood as indicative of broad-scale spatial patterns rather than evidence of causal relationships. Even so, this method remains useful in exploratory contexts, supporting the identification of general trends of co-occurrence between environmental and biological variables.

Spatial overlap between palm richness and triatomines

The results of this analysis revealed distinct patterns for each triatomine species in relation to palm richness. For R. robustus, the percentage of presence per stratum increased significantly with palm richness: 31.16% in low richness, 46.26% in medium richness, and 82.98% in high richness. The distribution of its presences also showed an increasing concentration, with 14.83% in low richness, 28.33% in medium, and 56.84% in high palm richness. Similarly, R. pictipes exhibited a pattern of strong dependence on high richness, with percentages of presence per stratum of 9.71% (low), 39.40% (medium), and 86.41% (high), and a distribution of presences of 5.26% (low), 27.44% (medium), and 67.31% (high). These data reinforce that the species R. robustus and R. pictipes are strongly associated with environments with greater palm diversity, confirming the findings of high niche overlap with palms such as Attalea and Mauritia (Fig. 9).

Presence and distribution triatomine species by richness palms. Rhodnius species and palm tree richness. Colors: light green = Rhodnius. robustus, golden yellow = Rhodnius pictipes, turquoise = Rhodnius neglectus, dark blue = Rhodnius nasutus.

For R. neglectus, the percentage of presence per richness stratum was 18.75% in low richness, rising to 59.18% in medium richness and 55.03% in high richness. The distribution of its presences also reflected this trend, with 10.77% in low, 43.74% in medium, and 45.49% in high richness. This pattern indicates that, although R. neglectus benefits from greater palm richness, it maintains a considerable proportion of its presences in medium richness strata, which is consistent with its wide distribution and ability to use various ecotopes in biomes such as the Cerrado and Atlantic Forest, where palm diversity can be more variable.

R. nasutus presented a slightly different pattern, with 35.58% of presence in low richness, 51.57% in medium, and 59.12% in high palm richness. The distribution of its presences was 19.02% in low, 35.48% in medium, and 45.50% in high richness.The higher percentage of presence and distribution in low richness strata, compared to the other Rhodnius species, highlights the adaptation of R. nasutus to environments like the Caatinga, which naturally have a lower diversity of palms, but where specific species like the carnaúba palm C. prunifera are crucial and are widely used by this vector12,28. This flexibility of R. nasutus in exploring environments with lower palm richness, but with the presence of specific ecotopes, differentiates it from the other Rhodnius species analyzed.

In summary, the results obtained in this study consolidate the understanding that palms are indispensable structural and functional ecological components for the maintenance of sylvatic triatomine populations throughout Brazil. The ecological niche overlap between these plants and the vectors of T. cruzi, together with the observed correlation with palm richness and the more detailed analysis of presence in different richness strata, are critical factors in determining the spatial dynamics of vectors and the persistence of the sylvatic cycle of Chagas disease29.

The persistence of high overlaps in biomes such as the Amazon and Cerrado, where deforestation and agricultural expansion are intense, suggests a continuous risk of human-vector contact. This deepened knowledge is fundamental for the development of entomological surveillance and vector control strategies that are comprehensive and adapted to the particularities of each region. Such strategies should allow for the mapping of risk areas, the prioritization of interventions in specific palm groves, and health education for rural communities, aiming to mitigate the risk of human infection throughout the national territory12.

Although the association between palms and triatomines is more strongly recognized for species of the genus Rhodnius, these ecotopes should not be interpreted in a restrictive way. Palm infestation by triatomines in the Americas involves multiple genera, including Rhodnius, Panstrongylus, and Triatoma, indicating that, in addition to more specialized species, opportunistic species are also capable of exploiting palms as shelters, feeding sites, or secondary ecotopes11. This perspective expands the ecological importance of palms for surveillance, as these landscape structures may support a more diverse vector fauna, including outside the Amazon region.

Examples from different regions reinforce this interpretation. In the Atlantic Forest, Panstrongylus tibiamaculatus has been associated with sylvatic environments and urban notifications, especially in areas where forest fragments, palms, and human residences occur within the same environmental matrix48. In southern Brazil, outbreaks of oral T. cruzi transmission, such as those reported in Navegantes, Santa Catarina, also demonstrate that natural ecotopes and wild reservoirs may have epidemiological relevance outside the Amazon domain49. Furthermore, records of R. neglectus in exotic or ornamental palms, such as Roystonea oleracea, indicate that urbanization and landscaping practices may create new ecological contexts for vector colonization42,50. Therefore, the overlap observed between triatomines and palms reinforces that eco-epidemiological surveillance should consider not only the presence of Arecaceae, but also palm species composition, structural characteristics, origin, landscape, and proximity for human habitation.

Conclusions

This study provides insight into niche overlap patterns, spatial correlation, and preference for palm tree richness ranges, which is essential for developing more effective entomological surveillance and vector control strategies. These strategies must be comprehensive and adapted to the particularities of each biome and vector species, allowing for the mapping of risk areas, the prioritization of interventions in specific palm groves, and health education for rural communities. Understanding these ecological interactions is an essential pillar for mitigating the risk of human infection throughout the national territory, contributing to the surveillance of Chagas disease in Brazil.
