Genetic diversity and spatial genetic structure of Caucasian apple (Malus orientalis Uglitzk.) populations based on microsatellite markers for conservation strategy
Authors: Hamid Bina, Farrokh Ghahremaninejad, Shahin Zarre, Fatemeh Moein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn important tree in Eurasia, Malus orientalis Uglitzk. (Caucasian apple) provides ecological and economic benefits. To evaluate the genetic diversity of this species, we sampled 167 individuals from 20 populations across the Zagros Mountains (6 populations) and Hyrcanian forests (14 populations). We assessed six quantitative morphological traits and employed 26 microsatellite (SSR) markers, which revealed a total of 455 alleles in the wild apple populations, all of which were polymorphic. The markers revealed mean values for genetic diversity indices, including allelic richness (Ar = 0.21), private alleles (Ap = 0.65), expected heterozygosity (HE = 0.74), and observed heterozygosity (HO = 0.71). Our analyses identified three major genetic clusters and two significant genetic barriers among the studied populations. Estimations of gene flow indicated limited connectivity among M. orientalis populations in the Caucasus region, suggesting habitat fragmentation as a key factor shaping genetic structure. Based on genetic diversity assessments, two areas within the Hyrcanian forest (Siamarzkoh region, Tokestan region, Abesk region, Asalem region) and one in the Zagros forest (Sepedkoh region) were identified as priority regions for conservation efforts to protect this valuable species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-025-02474-9.Keywords: Conservation unit, SSR, Hyrcanian forest, Iran, Population genetics, Zagros mountains

Introduction

As a Southwest Asian country, Iran exhibits remarkable variation in climate, topography, and soil, which fosters exceptional biodiversity. The country is home to approximately 8,000 flowering plant species, belonging to 167 families and 1,200 genera, including around 1,700 endemic species [1]. These species are distributed across four major ecological zones, Hyrcanian, Zagros, Irano-Turanian, and Khalij-Oman, each characterized by distinct physiographic and climatic conditions [2]. The genus Malus Mill. (Rosaceae) comprises 25 to 47 species, which are among the most economically significant crops globally [3]. These species are widely distributed across North America, East Asia, Europe, and the Caucasus [4]. Malus orientalis Uglitzk., commonly known as Caucasian apple, plays an important role in the local diets of rural and peri-urban communities [3, 5]. It has been cultivated in the Caucasus region since ancient times because its fruits are particularly valued for their outstanding nutritional content [6]. Species of M. orientalis also considered one of the ancestors of M. domestica (Suckow) Borkh [4, 6]. Wild Malus species provide food and shelter for the wildlife while also is used in genomic screening studies to identify genes for breeding purposes [3, 4]. Therefore, their conservation is essential for maintaining ecosystem stability and agricultural resilience [7]. In Iran, the habitat of this species extends across the ancient Silk Road trade routes, specifically in the Hyrcanian and Zagros forests [4]. These mountain regions are heterogeneous in terms of topography, vegetation, and climate [2].

The Zagros and Hyrcanian forests of Iran provide important habitats for M. orientalis, each with distinct ecological characteristics [2, 4]. The Zagros forests, Iran’s largest oak-dominated semi-arid ecosystem, host drought-tolerant species such as Quercus brantii Lindl. and Pistacia atlantica Desf., alongside heterogeneous landscapes of steppe vegetation and forest remnants [8]. In contrast, the temperate Hyrcanian forests in northern Iran are a biodiversity hotspot, supporting diverse tree and shrub communities that create habitat heterogeneity [9]. These ecological features influence the distribution and genetic structure of M. orientalis populations [4, 10, 11]. However, wild fruit trees in both regions are increasingly threatened by overharvesting, land-use changes, and climate-induced stress, highlighting the need for effective conservation and management strategies [12].

Habitat fragmentation has influenced the distribution and connectivity of M. orientalis in Iran, but its impact should be considered alongside historical factors such as trade and human activity along the Silk Road. While fragmentation may reduce gene flow and increase isolation, the Silk Road historically facilitated connections among populations [13]. Thus, both environmental barriers and historical human activities have shaped the current genetic patterns of wild apple populations [14]. Gene flow facilitates the exchange of genetic variability between distant populations; by increasing dispersal, it can decrease the rate of species extinction [15]. To identify high-priority populations for conservation, molecular markers are frequently employed to analyze spatial genetic structure, aiding in the recognition of populations with high genetic diversity, unique alleles, or limited gene flow, which makes them particularly important to prioritize for conservation [16, 17]. Analyzing spatial genetic structure offers insights into dispersal mechanisms and biogeographic patterns of species [18]. Numerous population genetics studies have use analyses such as population structure, contemporary and historical gene flow (Nm), genetic differentiation (FST), Nei’s genetic distance, and migration rates often utilizing coalescent-based approaches to characterize intraspecific diversity [11, 19, 20]. Comparing genetic differentiation among populations assists in defining conservation units and supporting selective pressures across geographic regions [11, 19, 20]. Various molecular markers, including ISSR, RFLP, and SSR, have been used to illustrate species’ spatial genetic structure and demographic history [4–6, 21]. These markers enable restricted inference of evolutionary processes and help identify wild populations with high conservation value based on genetic parameters [4–6, 21]. Among these, microsatellites (simple sequence repeats, SSRs) are particularly favored in conservation genetics, offering high-resolution data for delineating management units and assessing genetic diversity within wild populations [22]. Compared to other marker types, microsatellites exhibit superior allelic diversity and versatility, making them especially useful for germplasm characterization and population genetic analyses [22]. Within the genus Malus, the use of SSR markers is widespread, particularly in studies of evolutionary history, domestication, and population genetic structure [4–6, 11].

Previous studies among Malus species have shown high variation in morphological traits, including leaves [23–25]. Since the genus Malus is highly diverse, the variation in morphological characters has always been of interest to researchers [23–25]. Leaf morphology provides important insights into the genetic identity and evolutionary history of plants. Although many studies describe leaf traits and perform clustering, systematic quantitative analyses remain limited. This study examines the leaf macrostructures of M. orientalis using PCA and ANOVA to assess phenotypic variation, taxonomic significance, and evolutionary relationships among populations, providing information relevant for conservation and management [23–25].

We aimed to characterize microsatellite polymorphism within M. orientalis populations along the historical Silk Road trade routes, with a focus on Iranian germplasm. We further expected to quantify gene flow patterns and genetic diversity, and to identify conservation units essential for maintaining the genetic resources of M. orientalis.

Materials and methods

Plant sample collection

The data used in this study were obtained from a previous research conducted by HB on the genetic diversity and domestication of M. orientalis in the Caucasus region [4]. In this study, 167 M. orientalis samples were analyzed, collected from two main regions in Iran: the Zagros Forest (35 samples) and the Hyrcanian Forest, which includes Western Hyrcanian (5 samples), Central Hyrcanian (60 samples), and Eastern Hyrcanian (67 samples) (Table 1).

Morphological parameters studied

All plant specimens were morphologically validated at the Department of Plant Sciences, Faculty of Biological Sciences, Kharazmi University. Voucher specimens were deposited in the Kharazmi University Herbarium (herbarium code: T) under accession numbers 25,760–25,927 (https://bs.khu.ac.ir/page/7775/herbarium). All M. orientalis sampling sites in Iran used in the in the present study were located outside protected areas and national park boundaries; therefore, no collection permits were required under current environmental regulations in the country.

Six quantitative leaf morphological traits the most informative organ for population delimitation in M. orientalis were analyzed from 167 samples (Fig. 1). These traits included lamina area, lamina length, petiole length, maximum lamina width and maximum leaf width at 0.1 and 0.9 of lamina length [26]. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 14.00 [27], For each trait, normality of the data was first checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was tested with Levene’s test. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was then applied to evaluate significant differences among populations. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on standardized trait data using the ClustVis website [28, 29].

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was isolated from dried leaf samples of Caucasian apple using the NucleoSpin Plant II kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For microsatellite analysis, we performed multiplex PCR amplification using a Qiagen multiplex PCR kit (26 SSR markers) (Table S1) [4, 6, 30]. The reliability of these markers for population genetics has been well documented in a previous research [6].

Genetic diversity parameters

In this study, parameters of genetic diversity for each microsatellite locus were calculated via FSTAT v2.9.3 [31]. ADZE software (https://rosenberglab.stanford.edu/adze.html) was employed to estimate the population genetic diversity, including allelic richness (Ar) and private allele (Ap), using standardized sample sizes of 2 gene copies (ADZE = 2), assuming the individuals to be diploid in the Caucasian apple populations [32]. The number of different alleles (Na), number of effective alleles (Ne), expected heterozygosity (He) and observed heterozygosity (Ho), within-population inbreeding coefficient FIS and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium were calculated with GENEPOP 4.2 [33, 34]. Statistical significance of pairwise FST values was tested using 10,000 permutations in Arlequin [35].

The inverse distance weighted (IDW) interpolation function implemented in the Geographic Information System (GIS) software ArcGIS 9.3 [36] was used to estimate the geographic patterns of HO and HE for all our 20 populations of the Caucasian apple. We explored the relationships among clusters via principal component analysis (PCA) with the adegenet package in the R environment [37]. Analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) was performed via GENALEX v6.5 [38] to infer the genetic differentiation between populations from Hyrcanian forest and Zagros forest. Barrier analysis, which is based on the [39] maximum difference algorithm, was conducted to define significant genetic breaks and spatial differences between populations. Genetic barriers were assessed via BARRIER 2.2 [40].

Genetic structure and gene flow analysis

We assessed the genetic structure of the Caucasian apple via STRUCTURE v2.3.4 software [41]. This tool was used to examine range-wide genetic clustering among the sampled populations. Ten independent runs were performed for each K from 2 to 10 with 200,000 burn-in steps followed by 500,000 Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) steps. The STRUCTURE output was analyzed and visualized by using StructureSelector [42]. To infer historical gene flow (Nm) patterns, MIGRATE-N v3.6 [43] was used to estimate the effective population sizes (θ) and mutation-scaled immigration (M) among the groups identified by STRUCTURE. Additionally, we used the Bayesian method implemented in BayesAss (BA3) to estimate current gene flow between populations [44]. Estimates gained by this method are based on migration over the last few generations. We selected the best run for allele frequencies and posterior probability densities of inbreeding coefficients with different random seeds among three independent runs. The Bayesian deviance was calculated to determine the best run using a script applied in R [44]. To obtain a correct acceptance rate, the values of the parameters ranged between 20% and 40% [45]. The inbreeding coefficient was set to 0.35, and the migration rate was set to 0.75 for the final analysis. For allele frequencies, the default setting of mixing parameters was used. The analysis was conducted with 106 burn-in and 107 MCMC iterations with every 1,000th iteration sampled. The results were visualized via QGIS 2.18 ‘Las Palmas’ [46]. CIRCUITSCAPE software [47] was used to evaluate the influence of topographic complexity as a resistance factor affecting gene flow among populations. This software models populations as nodes connected by resistors, based on the principles of electrical circuit theory and the connectivity-resistance approach. While altitude alone may not fully capture topographic complexity, we used a digital elevation model (DEM) to derive a resistance surface for the habitats of M. orientalis in Iran [48].

Unit of conservation selection

To determine the conservation priority of the Caucasian apple, we used reserve selection analysis via DIVA-GIS software. The reserve selection analysis algorithm, which is based on allele frequency in each population, determines the minimum number of geographic units necessary to maintain genetic diversity [49]. The populations with the highest allelic richness were given first priority for conservation, and the priorities of other populations were selected on the basis of the first population [49].

Results

Genetic diversity of Caucasian apple populations

In this study, 26 microsatellite loci were analyzed, revealing a total of 455 alleles across the wild apple populations. All loci were polymorphic among the Caucasian apple populations. The number of alleles per locus ranged from 7 (CH02c03b) to 29 (CH04e03) (Table S1), with an average of 17.5 alleles per locus (Table S2). The average number of alleles per population was 5.22, with a range from 2.07 in the Doroud region (Zagros forest, Lorestan Province) to 7.5 in the Vatna region (Hyrcanian forest, Gorgan Province). The mean gene flow (Nm) was estimated at 0.6, indicating a moderate level of genetic exchange among populations (Table S3).

The Vaz and Sangdeh regions (Mazandaran Province) of the Hyrcanian forests exhibited the highest observed heterozygosity (HO = 0.85) and expected heterozygosity (HE = 0.83), whereas the Doroud and Nozhyan (Lorestan Province) regions of the Zagros forests showed the lowest Ho (0.44) and He (0.65) values. Overall, heterozygosity was lower in the Zagros forest populations compared to those in the Hyrcanian forests (Table 2). The mean of inbreeding coefficient (FIS) for Caucasian wild apple in Iran ranged from − 0.83 to 0.11, with the average FIS in Zagros forest populations being negative (Table 2). Furthermore, populations in the Hyrcanian forests displayed greater mean allelic richness (1.74) than those in the Zagros forests (1.72). However, the mean number of private alleles was higher in Zagros populations (0.22) compared to Hyrcanian populations (0.20) (Fig. 2).

Population structure and genetic differentiation

The STRUCTURE analysis revealed that the Caucasian apple samples exhibited high levels of heterozygosity and identified five genetically distinct clusters corresponding to five main geographic regions. The most apparent population structure was observed at K = 5, which provided the clearest geographical pattern. This K value delineated five distinct clusters, each associated with a specific geographic region, and no new groups emerged beyond K = 5 (Fig. 3a).

At K = 3, populations from Nozhyan (Zagros forest, Kordestan province), Doroud (Zagros forest, Lorestan province), and Sepedkoh (Zagros forest, Lorestan province) (purple group) were separated from the others, while apple populations from Marivan, Saghez, and Buflo (Zagros forest, Kordestan province) clustered with the Hyrcanian populations (blue group). Gene flow between the habitats of M. orientalis

To estimate gene flow, populations of the Caucasian apple were classified by geographical region. Our findings illustrate some gene flow among populations. Gorgan populations (Hyrcnian forest) were the predominant source of exogenous allelic variants, and strong gene flow was observed from Gorgan into Mazandaran, Gilan (Hyrcnian forest), and Kordestan populations (Zagros Forest) (Nm values from Gorgan into Mazandaran, Gilan, and Kordestan provinces were 0.172, 0.24, and 0.185, respectively). We observed minor gene flow from Gilan’s population into Lorestan’s population (Zagros Forest) (Nm = 0.008) as well as minor gene flow from Kordestan’s population into Mazandaran’s population (Nm = 0.05). Little or no gene flow between Zagros populations (Lorestan Province and Kordestan Province, Ne = 0.01) was detected, suggesting that M. orientalis populations in Zagros are genetically isolated from each other (Fig. 3b, Table S4).

Pairwise FST analysis

According to the pairwise FST analysis, most apple populations from the Hyrcanian and Zagros forests showed very low genetic differentiation, with FST values ranging from 0.005 to 0.30 (Fig. 3c). The FST between Marivan (Zagros forest, Lorestan Province) and Vatna (Hyrcanian forest, Gorgan Province) was very low (FST = 0.005) and not statistically significant. Moderate levels of genetic differentiation were observed between some western (Vatna region and Siamarzkoh region rom the Hyrcanian forest, belong to Gorgan Province) and central populations of the Hyrcanian forest (Gaznasara region and Vaz region from the Hyrcanian forest, Mazandaran Province), with FST values above 0.25 and statistically significant (P < 0.001). These results indicate moderate genetic differentiation among habitats of M. orientalis in the western and central parts of the Hyrcanian forest (Fig. 3c).

The patterns of PCA observed were largely consistent across the axis combinations. PC1 and PC2 accounted for 5.71% and 4.32% of the total genetic variance, respectively. The populations of the Zagros forest were divided into two groups via PCA; however, populations from northern Zagros overlapped with those from the central part of the Hyrcanian forest. The populations from the west of the Hyrcanian forest have mixed with the populations from the central area of the forest the most (Fig. 4).

Identification conservation units of Caucasian apple

To determine the minimum geographic units necessary to conserve the genetic diversity of Caucasian apple (M. orientalis) across its two primary regions, the Hyrcanian and Zagros forests, we prioritized populations based on genetic diversity and allelic uniqueness. In the Hyrcanian forest, the populations of Toskestan, Siamarzkoh, Abesk (Gorgan province), and Asalem (Gilan province) were designated as first-priority conservation units due to their high genetic diversity and presence of unique alleles. Sangdehsari and Vaz (Mazandaran province) were classified as second-priority units. Within the Zagros forest, Sepedkoh (Lorestan province) emerged as the first-priority conservation unit, while Buflo, Sagez and Nozhyan (Kordeatan province) were assigned second-priority status. Remaining areas (indicated in orange and green in Fig. 5) were categorized as lower-priority regions (Fig. 6).

Discussion

M. orientalis is one of the main ancestors of cultivated apples in the Caucasian region [4, 6]. This species is important for ecosystems and can be used in apple breeding to improve traits like disease and drought resistance, as well as to meet future challenges [4, 11]. Therefore, it is important to protect its germplasm from serious threats such as habitat fragmentation and other potential risks. Our results identified four key areas in the Hyrcanian forest and one in the Zagros forest for conserving M. orientalis genetic diversity, suggesting that conservation efforts in the Hyrcanian forest may need extra attention.

Our study found a geographic barrier among populations in the Zagros forests (Sepedkoh region), which was identified as a conservation priority. In the Hyrcanian forest, the Siamarzkoh region is another priority area and is also along a barrier separating populations. Creating ecological corridors could help maintain gene flow and keep these populations connected [50].

Genetic diversity within populations of M. orientalis

Our results show that some M. orientalis populations in the Hyrcanian (Asalem and Gorgan) and Zagros (Doroud and Nozhyan) forests are genetically different and diverse. Previous studies using SSR markers have demonstrated a close genetic relationship between Iranian wild apple populations and those in Central Asia (Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan) [4]. This genetic affinity suggests shared ancestry, likely resulting from historical dispersal events [5, 6, 51]. Given the geographic proximity of these regions to ancient Silk Road trade routes, these findings support the hypothesis that the Silk Road served as a key corridor for apple migration, potentially facilitated by either human-mediated transport or natural dispersal processes [10].

In conservation genetics, allelic richness is one of the important parameter, and it can be used to forecast historical bottleneck populations [52–54]. The average allelic richness value in Iran (6.2 ± 0.04) is lower than that reported in Europe and China [5]. This reduction may be related to historical factors such as repeated bottlenecks, smaller effective population sizes, and long-term habitat fragmentation in Iran. In addition, anthropogenic pressures, including land-use change, deforestation, and overgrazing, may have accelerated the loss of genetic diversity. A decrease in allelic richness can reduce effective population size, making wild apple populations more vulnerable to unfavorable conditions such as climate change, disease outbreaks, drought, and dehydration [54, 55]. Another important parameter is expected heterozygosity, which is a good indicator of genetic diversity [56]. The mean HE for Caucasian apple in Iran was 0.65, which was lower than the HE for M. orientalis in Europe and China (0.80), indicating that population dynamics may not be uniform in the different growing regions of M. orientalis [57, 58]. The observed variation in genetic diversity can be attributed to the distinct distribution of M. orientalis across two ecologically divergent regions in Iran: the Hyrcanian forest and the Zagros forest. These regions exhibit marked differences in climate and vegetation, which likely promoted divergent selection pressures and gene flow low but still ongoing gene flow exist but is slightly restricted between populations. This ecological segregation may have contributed to the non-uniform genetic structure and diversity patterns observed in these populations [59]. The The FIS parameter reflects deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium within populations. A negative value indicates an excess of heterozygosity [60]. The FIS value was significantly lower in Iranian wild apple (-0.81 ± 0.02) than in European wild apple (0.04 ± 0.06). This difference maybe suggests a strong excess of heterozygotes in Iranian populations compared to European populations. The gene pool of various Malus species has been strongly influenced by human impact since ancient times because apple fruits were already important crops in the era of the Sumerians [61]. Thus, it is difficult to recognize whether natural populations of M. orientalis were shaped more by natural processes or by long-term human influence.

Gene flow

Gene flow and migration have a significant impact on spatial genetic structure and limit local adaptation [62]. The gene flow analysis showed notable migration from eastern Hyrcanian populations toward the western Hyrcanian and northern Zagros populations, with strong Nm values, suggesting historical connectivity or recent migration across these regions [63]. However, gene flow between Zagros populations was extremely low, implying genetic isolation among populations in this region [63]. These patterns of gene flow do not completely align with the barriers identified by Circuitscape and Monmonier’s algorithm. This apparent contradiction may be explained by the nature of the analyses: gene flow estimates reflect directional migration and historical connectivity, while barrier analyses identify current resistance to gene exchange based on genetic distances [6, 64]. Therefore, while past gene flow may have occurred across these regions, recent landscape fragmentation or geographic barriers may now limit gene exchange.

The central topography of Iran and the poorly connected northern and southern Zagros regions likely contribute to the current genetic isolation, especially in Lorestan, which shows little admixture. Overall, our results suggest that gene flow has historically played an important role in shaping the genetic patterns of M. orientalis, especially across continuous habitats. However, recent environmental and topographical barriers have reduced connectivity in some regions. In the past, wild apple trade along the Silk Road likely helped to overcome these barriers, although they now limit gene flow.These findings are consistent with the view that Iran served as a historical corridor for apple gene flow from Central Asia to Europe via the Silk Road, while also experiencing regional isolation that maintains population differentiation [6, 64].

Our results suggest that historical connectivity, including past dispersal along the Silk Road, contributed to shaping the genetic diversity and admixture patterns of M. orientalis. In contrast, recent landscape fragmentation and topographical barriers appear to limit contemporary gene flow, particularly in isolated regions such as Lorestan. These findings indicate that maintaining or improving connectivity could be important for the long-term genetic stability of populations. Potential management measures may include the protection of continuous habitats, restoration of habitat corridors, and careful consideration of assisted gene flow between isolated populations to support genetic diversity and adaptive potential, while avoiding unintended disruption of local genetic structure [50].

Spatial genetic structure of M. orientalis

The previous studies on M. orientalis in Eerope reported no significant association between geographic location and genetic structure [5, 11], whereas our findings indicate reveal a moderate spatial genetic differentiation between wild apple populations in the Hyrcanian and Zagros forests (4,78). This is supported by both STRUCTURE and PCA results, which revealed distinct genetic clusters generally aligning with geographic regions. The whole Iranian range of the species was divided into three clusters via STRUCTURE analysis, which differs from the results of previous studies [5, 10, 11]. One of these variations may be the response of Iranian wild apples in the Hyrcanian and Zagros forests to population size and dispersal [65].

Although our results indicate that M. orientalis populations in the Hyrcanian and Zagros forests generally form two distinct gene pools, the population from Gilan (northwest Hyrcanian Forest) shows genetic similarity to Zagros populations. This is notable because the Gilan population is geographically distant from other Hyrcanian populations in the central and western Hyrcanian forest and, based on geographic distance, is closer to the Zagros forest than other M. orientalis populations in the Hyrcanian forest. In addetion, Previous studies suggest these populations share a common ancestor, which could explain this observed similarity. Wild apple populations from Lorestan were strongly separated from both Hyrcanian and northern Zagros populations. The isolation of this area is supported by the results of migration analysis, as well as CIRCUITSCAPE and PCA. The distinctiveness of this area, which is difficult to assess due to topographic barriers, may indicate that a unique gene pool exists, which should be protected with high priority [66].

STRUCTURE analysis revealed broad-scale clustering patterns, whereas Monmonier’s algorithm identified localized genetic barriers in the Hyrcanian and Zagros forests. One of the reasons of this difference maybe becaue, as STRUCTURE detects genome-wide patterns assuming Hardy–Weinberg and linkage equilibrium, while Monmonier’s method identifies sharp spatial genetic discontinuities without relying on these assumptions [67, 68]. Using both approaches can improve our understanding of population structure and help identify multiple causes of observed differences. STRUCTURE highlights overall population structure and broad genetic clusters, whereas Monmonier detects fine-scale barriers that may locally influence gene flow. For example, the Nozhyan population (Zagros forest, Lorestan province) forms a separate genetic group in STRUCTURE (K = 3), while small barriers detected in the Hyrcanian forest by Monmonier suggest subtle genetic differentiation that could reflect early local adaptation or environmental heterogeneity [69]. These two methods provide complementary insights into both broad and fine-scale patterns of genetic structure, contributing to a better understanding of connectivity and population differentiation [70].

Conclusion

Species of M. orientalis contributes to the genetic background of cultivated apples in the region and represents a valuable source of genetic diversity for future breeding programs. Our analyses revealed broad and fine-scale patterns of genetic structure, highlighting historical connectivity along continuous habitats and localized barriers in the Hyrcanian and Zagros forests. These findings indicate that some populations could be considered in conservation planning, particularly those showing unique genetic signatures or located near geographic barriers. Maintaining habitat connectivity and protecting these populations could help preserve genetic diversity and adaptive potential, supporting the long-term viability of M. orientalis.
