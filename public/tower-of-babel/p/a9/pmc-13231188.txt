Toll-Like Receptors in Diabetes: Immunometabolic Mechanisms and Emerging Precision Therapeutic Strategies
Authors: Rui Song, Siyu Yang, Yaling Chen, Jing Ren, Saber Khederzadeh, Ou Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past few years (2020–2025), our understanding of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) in diabetes has evolved significantly, transitioning from their traditional role as innate immune sentinels to central integrators within the immunometabolic hub. Aberrant TLR activation, often triggered by metabolic damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), contributes fundamentally to pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and systemic insulin resistance. To provide a comprehensive synthesis of these developments, we performed a targeted literature search across PubMed and Web of Science, prioritizing high-impact studies published between 2020 and 2025. This review explores the complex, often non-classical, mechanisms driving this pathology, particularly highlighting the emerging “Trojan horse” pathway—an exosomal stealth delivery system wherein gut-derived vesicles covertly transport inflammatory ligands directly into the endosomal compartments of β-cells, bypassing traditional surface receptor surveillance. Furthermore, recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics and Mendelian randomization have unraveled the cellular heterogeneity of the β-cell immune response and established causal genetic links to metaflammation. Building on these mechanistic insights, we propose a preliminary framework for precision medicine that incorporates targeted nanotherapeutic delivery and polygenic risk stratification. Ultimately, we propose that the future management of metabolic diseases must rely not on indiscriminate immunosuppression, but on the careful and precise recalibration of the immunometabolic thermostat.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) has evolved from a simple metabolic research object to the most representative chronic epidemic in the 21st century. According to the latest forecast of the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), there are more than 537 million adults with diabetes in the world, and this number is rising with an unstoppable trend, and it is expected to exceed 780 million by 2045.1 This epidemic outbreak, driven by rapid urbanization and obesity environment in low-and middle-income areas, has brought huge economic burden-global medical expenditure is estimated to exceed $1 trillion every year. Although there are a variety of hypoglycemic drugs to choose from (from metformin to sodium-glucose cotransporter 2(SGLT2) inhibitors), there are still a considerable number of patients who cannot achieve continuous glycemic control or prevent serious complications. This clinical reality highlights a key concept limitation: the current treatment strategy still focuses on “glycemia”, aiming at symptoms (hyperglycemia) rather than potential molecular drivers. More and more evidence show that there is systemic disorder in the interaction between metabolic homeostasis and innate immune system. It suggests that diabetes is essentially an “immunometabolic syndrome”.

The intersection of immunity and metabolism is not an accidental phenomenon, but rather an evolutionarily conserved necessity. In lower organisms such as Drosophila, a single organ, the fat body, performs the dual functions of nutrient storage and host defense.2 Mammals have compartmentalized these tasks into specialized tissues (adipose and lymphoid), but the underlying signaling architecture remains strictly conserved. However, this ancient efficiency has become a liability in the modern obesogenic environment. Under conditions of nutrient overload, saturated fatty acids (eg, palmitate) structurally mimic the lipid A moiety of bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS). Consequently, TLR2 and TLR4 function as promiscuous sensors, misinterpreting dietary excess as a mass bacterial invasion.3 This “evolutionary mismatch” hijacks the host defense machinery, converting a mechanism designed for transient pathogen clearance into a state of chronic, sterile inflammation—“metaflammation”—that actively antagonizes insulin signaling pathways.4,5

While the classical role of TLRs in recognizing exogenous pathogens via the MyD88-dependent axis is a well-established paradigm, their non-canonical activation by endogenous metabolic ligands represents a more recently elucidated, and highly complex, frontier in immunometabolism. Central to this pathogenesis is the pancreatic β-cell, which recent evidence (2020–2025) has redefined from a passive victim of glucotoxicity to an active architect of its own demise. β-cells constitutively express a functional repertoire of TLRs (TLR2, TLR3, TLR4, TLR9) that, upon activation, trigger a deleterious cascade involving NF-κB nuclear translocation and interferon regulatory factor (IRF) activation.6 The traditional model mainly focuses on apoptosis induced by soluble cytokines, while current research has revealed a more hidden result: dedifferentiation. Under continuous inflammatory stress, β-cells will start a reverse program to return to progenitor-like, non-secretory phenotype, thus evading the recognition of the immune system.7 This mechanism may explain why the reduction in the number of β-cells in the early stage of type 2 diabetes is usually reversible, indicating that the TLR signaling pathway plays a crucial role in determining the fate of cells.

More complicated, some non-classical signal transduction mechanisms have been discovered recently, which bypass the traditional receptor dynamics. Traditionally, the activation of Toll-like receptors (TLR) requires direct contact between surface receptors and circulating ligands. However, this dogma fails to explain why inflammation still spreads to the pancreas when there are potent lipopolysaccharide (LPS) neutralizing proteins in the blood. Recent studies (including that of Alen et al) put forward the Trojan horse hypothesis.8 The model holds that the imbalance of intestinal flora will lead to the dysfunction of intestinal epithelial cells, which will wrap TLR ligands and pathogenic MicroRNA in exosomes. These lipid bilayer vesicles, like Trojan horse-like delivery systems, can protect the inflammatory factors they carry from immune surveillance of the circulatory system. When these extracellular vesicles reach the pancreas, they will directly fuse with β cells and release intracellular substances, thus combining with TLR in vivo. This unique cell-free communication mechanism makes surface receptor antagonists fail in this specific pathway, which provides a powerful explanation for the limited efficacy of previous anti-inflammatory experiments. Unlike the established cell-surface receptor dynamics, this exosome-mediated “Trojan Horse” pathway is an emerging concept. While it elegantly explains the failure of surface-level antagonists, the precise in vivo kinetics of endosomal TLR activation by exosomal cargo remains a nascent area requiring rigorous longitudinal validation.

Toll-like receptor (TLR) signal transduction has far more influence than pancreas. It integrates signals from remote organs, so the “gut-pancreas axis” embodies this systematic connection. In diabetic patients, dysbacteriosis-characterized by the loss of microbial diversity and the decrease of mucin-degrading bacteria-can damage the intestinal epithelial barrier. This “intestinal leakage” phenomenon allows bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS) to cross the portal vein circulation, which is called metabolic endotoxinemia.9 This gut-derived LPS first encounters hepatic TLR4, triggering liver inflammation and insulin resistance, before disseminating to the pancreas to prime islet macrophages. Furthermore, recent viral pandemics have added another layer of complexity. SARS-CoV-2, capable of binding ACE2 receptors on β-cells, has been shown to synergize with TLR4 pathways, potentially accelerating the onset of new-onset diabetes in susceptible individuals.10,11 Therefore, TLRs as an integration hub, transform microbial, viral, and dietary signals into a unified inflammatory response, thus damaging metabolic health.

Despite these solid mechanisms, the clinical transformation of TLR biology is still the Achilles heel in this field. Early attempts to use broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory agents or nonspecific TLR inhibitors in diabetes mellitus have not yielded clear results, which is mainly due to the “dichotomous nature” of TLR signaling. Although chronic overactivation is destructive, basic TLR signaling is very important for tissue repair, autophagy, and anti-infection defense.12 Indiscriminate inhibition risks severe immunosuppression and a side effect unacceptable for a chronic condition like diabetes. This stagnation requires us to shift from a rough drug blocking to a paradigm shift to precise regulation. This review aims to fill this gap by integrating major discoveries between 2020 and 2025. Unlike the traditional review that only focuses on a single mechanism, we have integrated the emerging multi-model research methods and put forward a new research roadmap. We deeply studied how single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) can analyze the immune heterogeneity of β cells, and revealed which specific subsets drive the inflammatory response.13 We also explored how nanotechnology can accurately deliver siRNA-loaded nanoparticles to islet macrophages, thus avoiding systemic side effects.14 In addition, we clarified the causal genetic relationship between TLR gene polymorphism and diabetic complications through Mendelian randomization (MR) and polygenic risk score (PRS) mechanisms.

By integrating these multi-dimensional insights, this review aims to provide a robust framework for understanding the “Janus-faced” role of Toll-like receptors in diabetes—balancing their essential physiological vigilance against their pathological contribution to chronic inflammation. To ensure a comprehensive and up-to-date synthesis, we performed a targeted literature search across PubMed and Web of Science, prioritizing high-impact, peer-reviewed studies published between 2020 and 2025. By focusing on recent breakthroughs in exosomal “Trojan horse” pathways, single-cell heterogeneity, and Mendelian randomization, this work seeks to accurately map the TLR-mediated immunometabolic landscape. Ultimately, we propose that the future of diabetes management lies in transitioning from broad, indiscriminate anti-inflammatory approaches toward precision recalibration of the immune thermostat, potentially facilitating true disease modification.

The TLR Architecture

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) represent the phylogenetic conservative interface between host environment and innate immune system. In humans, 10 functional TLRs (TLR(TLR1–TLR10) are not randomly distributed, but are strategically compartmentalized to optimize pathogen identification. Surface-expressed receptors (TLR1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 10) mainly play a role by detecting microbial lipids and protein (such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and flagellin) in extracellular microenvironment.15 Conversely, endosomal receptors (TLR3, 7, 8, 9) function as intracellular sensors, detecting nucleic acids derived from viral internalization or, critically in the context of tissue damage, host-derived DNA fragments.16 This subcellular localization dictates distinct pathological trajectories in diabetes: while cell-surface TLR4 is a primary responder to circulating saturated fatty acids in Type 2 Diabetes (T2D), endosomal TLR3 and TLR9 are increasingly implicated in the viral or autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells in Type 1 Diabetes (T1D).17,18 Structurally, all TLRs share a modular architecture comprising leucine-rich repeats (LRRs) for ligand binding and a cytosolic Toll/IL-1 receptor (TIR) domain. Signal initiation is contingent upon ligand-induced receptor dimerization, which creates a molecular scaffold for the recruitment of downstream adaptors, thereby converting structural conformational changes into intracellular kinase cascades.19

The difference of two key adaptor proteins, MyD88 and TRIF, regulates most of the complexity of TLR signal transduction. Except for TLR3, all TLRs adopt the classic MyD88-dependent pathway, which functions as a “rapid response” module. When activated, TAK1 complex will be ubiquitinated by TRAF6 and IRAK kinase family, which will lead to nuclear translocation of AP-1 and NF-κB. Under the metabolic background, this pathway usually maintains a chronic and low-grade inflammatory microenvironment (such as IL-1β and TNF-α), which will gradually damage the islet function instead of causing the typical chaotic reaction of acute sepsis.20,21 In contrast, the TRIF-dependent pathway (only in TLR3 and endocytosis TLR4) mediates the “delayed response” centered on the production of interferon regulatory factor (IRF3/7) and type I interferon. TLR4 is unique in that it is the only receptor that can activate both pathways simultaneously. This dual signal transduction ability makes TLR4 the core node of metabolic inflammation, enabling it to simultaneously drive NF-κB-mediated cytokine release and interferon-mediated immune regulation, and this synergistic effect significantly amplifies β-cell stress.22,23

In the microenvironment of diabetes, the physiological mechanism of TLR activation is often subverted by a phenomenon called “metabolic hijacking”. In this process, hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia not only exist as inducing conditions, but also directly act as agonists. A lot of evidence shows that the saturated fatty acids in circulation (especially palmitic acid) simulate the lipid A part of LPS in structure. However, this interaction is complicated: palmitic acid needs the participation of carrier protein fetuin A(Fetuin-A) and co-receptor CD14/MD-2 to induce TLR4 aggregation and then translocation to lipid rafts.24 This nutrient sensing mechanism will trigger “sterile inflammation”, a unique pathological process driven by metabolic injury-related molecular patterns (DAMPs) rather than infectious pathogens. Furthermore, hyperglycemia exacerbates this cycle by promoting the release of Advanced Glycation End-products (AGEs) and High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1), which act as endogenous ligands for TLR2/4.25 The sustained engagement of these receptors leads to the constitutive activation of JNK, which phosphorylates insulin receptor substrates (IRS-1/2) on inhibitory serine residues. This molecular uncoupling directly impedes downstream PI3K-Akt signaling, establishing a key mechanistic basis of insulin resistance.26,27

Advances in systems biology have revealed that TLR dysregulation extends beyond simple receptor-ligand binding to encompass crucial post-translational and epigenetic modifications, contributing to the “metabolic memory” observed clinically. For instance, hyperglycemia-induced N-glycosylation of TLR4 stabilizes the receptor on the cell membrane and impairs its lysosomal degradation, thereby lowering the activation threshold.28,29 At the epigenetic level, persistent histone acetylation at pro-inflammatory gene promoters allows for sustained inflammatory expression even after glycemic control is restored.30–32 Furthermore, the failure of negative regulatory mechanisms, such as those mediated by miR-146a, often leaves the signaling network in a state of constitutive activation. The resulting cellular heterogeneity and specific “immune-active” subsets within the islet, as revealed by recent single-cell transcriptomics, will be discussed in detail in the following section regarding β-cell fate.33

TLR-Driven Inflammation and β-Cell Fate Reprogramming

In the pathogenesis of diabetes, the Toll-like receptor (TLR) family functions not merely as passive observers of hyperglycemia but as central effectors driving the progressive failure of the pancreatic islet. Specifically, TLR2 and TLR4 have emerged as critical molecular hubs that integrate intracellular stress signals with the immune microenvironment. This section dissects the mechanisms by which these receptors accelerate diabetic progression, moving from ligand recognition and signaling cascades to the newly elucidated “Trojan Horse” exosomal transport and cellular heterogeneity.

Abnormal recognition of metabolic ligands is a necessary condition to start sterile inflammation in diabetes. Although Toll-like receptor 4(TLR4) can recognize bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS), saturated fatty acids (SFAs) are considered to be the main inducement for the activation of TLR4 in diabetic microenvironment. However, the study of structural biology challenges the view that SFAs can be directly combined with TLR4. On the contrary, there is evidence that SFAs such as palmitate need hepatocyte factor fetuin-a secreted by the liver as an endogenous binding molecule to activate TLR4.

Fetuin-A binds free fatty acids to form a soluble complex that interfaces with TLR4 and MD-2, inducing receptor dimerization on the β-cell membrane.34 This mechanism elucidates how systemic dyslipidemia is translated into localized receptor activation. However, it is crucial to note that much of the mechanistic evidence supporting this Fetuin-A/TLR4 interaction is derived from murine models and in vitro assays. Future translational studies are required to critically evaluate the stoichiometric dynamics of these complexes in human diabetic cohorts. Furthermore, this inflammatory loop is amplified by endogenous Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs). Hyperglycemia-induced High-Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1) and Islet Amyloid Polypeptide (IAPP) oligomers—prevalent in Type 2 Diabetes—directly engage TLR2 heterodimers.35 Consequently, protein misfolding within the islet is biochemically translated into inflammatory signaling, establishing a positive feedback loop that exacerbates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress.

As established in the preceding architectural discussion, the activation of the MyD88-dependent pathway instigates a dual pathogenic trajectory in the β-cell. Instead of a transient immune response, this pathway creates a detrimental crosstalk with insulin signaling. Specifically, the activation of JNK kinase phosphorylates the Insulin Receptor Substrate-1 (IRS-1) at the inhibitory Ser307 residue, directly impeding physiological PI3K-Akt survival signaling and providing a molecular basis for inflammation-induced insulin resistance.36 Furthermore, as the “priming” signal for the NLRP3 inflammasome, TLR signaling facilitates the proteolytic cleavage and release of mature IL-1β. In the state of chronic TLR activation, the ultimate fate of β-cells extends beyond simple apoptosis, they enter a state of “functional degradation”. Recent evidence highlights that the synergistic effect of TLR-NF-κB axis and Unfolded Protein Response (UPR) forces cells to balance function against survival. Epigenetic analyses reveal that this persistent stress silences key identity transcription factors.38 Consequently, β cells dedifferentiate, reverting to a hormone-negative state similar to progenitor cells, failing to secrete insulin while evading death (Figure 1). In addition, some stressed cells adopt a Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP), transforming the islet into a reservoir of chronic inflammation. This mechanism helps explain the clinical paradox: despite a sharp decline in insulin secretion, the absolute number of β-cells in early diabetic patients remains relatively stable.

Recent studies have challenged the dogma that TLR activation is strictly confined to the cell surface, and proposed a Trojan horse model of organ-to-organ communication that bypasses the traditional receptor dynamics.8 In this non-classical pathway, intestinal epithelial cells damaged by flora imbalance encapsulate TLR ligands and pathogenic microRNA in exosomes. With the advent of single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics, our understanding of islet pathology has reached a fine analytical level. These advanced technologies identify a specific subgroup of “immune hub” β cells, which proves that β cells are not a single group. These cells are often located near microvessels, and continue to overexpress TLR4 and CXCL10.39 In islets of Langerhans, there are “pacemaker” cell groups that sense the danger signals of circulatory metabolism and warn the nearby “silent” cells. This heterogeneity suggests that the failure of previous clinical trials using systemic inhibitors may be due to the lack of specific targeting. In the future, precise therapy must be aimed at selectively targeting these “pacing points” or specific downstream effectors (such as NLRP3 and JNK) to break the cycle of metabolic inflammation without damaging the host’s systemic immune defense.40

The Gut-Immune-Metabolic Nexus: TLRs as Regulators of Metaflammation

The tripartite interaction among intestinal microflora, innate immunity, and host metabolism constitutes the physiological framework of modern diabetic pathophysiology. Beyond the purely “glucose-centered” point of view, the current evidence not only locates the intestinal epithelium as the surface of nutrient absorption, but also as the main immune interface for environmental perception. Within this microenvironment, Toll-like receptors (specifically TLR2, TLR4, and TLR5) function as molecular integrators. They are tasked with the dual mandate of tolerating commensal symbionts while rapidly detecting metabolic stress signals (DAMPs) derived from dietary overload. This distinct recognition capability establishes TLRs as central switches within the gut-liver-pancreas axis, determining the tipping point between homeostatic tolerance and pathogenic metaflammation.41

In the natural history of Type 2 Diabetes (T2D), the compromise of intestinal barrier function serves as a precipitating event for systemic inflammation, a condition termed metabolic endotoxemia. Mechanistic studies by Jobe et al demonstrate that hyperglycemia-induced downregulation of tight junction proteins increases paracellular permeability, facilitating the translocation of Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from the luminal compartment into the portal circulation.42 Upon reaching the pancreas, circulating LPS engages the TLR4-MyD88-NF-κB axis on the β-cell surface, inducing IL-1β secretion and impairing Glucose-Stimulated Insulin Secretion (GSIS). This process is causally reversible: interventions utilizing polysaccharides (eg, from Ganoderma lucidum) to reshape the microbiota composition—specifically reducing the abundance of endotoxin-producing Proteobacteria—have been shown to lower the systemic LPS load. This effectively abrogates TLR4-driven hepatic lipid deposition and islet inflammation, verifying the Gut-LPS-TLR4 axis as a critical molecular bridge linking dysbiosis to systemic insulin resistance.43

Contrary to the pathogenic role of TLR4 in metabolic stress, TLR5, which recognizes bacterial flagellin, plays an indispensable role in maintaining mucosal homeostasis. Research by Carvalho et al shows that TLR5 is the “guardian” of symbiotic microorganisms. The signal transduction defect of TLR5 will lead to spontaneous transition to dysregulated and pro-inflammatory microbial groups, accompanied by impaired mucosal integrity and the appearance of metabolic syndrome phenotype in mouse models.44 In addition, in the context of Type 1 Diabetes (T1D), the signal transduction defect of TLR5 will destroy the tolerance education of intestinal immune system, and lead to the abnormal activation of self-reactive T cells against islet antigen in gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT). This evidence demonstrates that TLR family has functional dichotomy: TLR5 maintains the defense barrier, while TLR4 activation signals its breakthrough. This loss of balance is an early sign of the progress of diabetes.

Recent breakthroughs have expanded the “gut-pancreas axis” into a multi-organ communication network mediated by extracellular vesicles. Building on the “Trojan horse” model, Alen et al demonstrated that under lipotoxic stress, hepatocytes release exosomes enriched with TLR4 agonists and specific microRNAs.8 These vesicles act as systemic vehicles, delivering inflammatory cargo directly to distal pancreatic macrophages and β-cells, bypassing traditional circulatory neutralization. This mechanism provides a robust molecular explanation for why non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) frequently accelerates β-cell failure. Conversely, homeostatic signaling is maintained by healthy microbiota-derived vesicles carrying anti-inflammatory miR-146a, which negatively regulates TLR pathways.45 Thus, the gut-liver-pancreas axis is governed by a delicate balance of vesicle-mediated TLR activation and suppression.

Microorganisms not only affect host immunity through pathogenic ligands, but also through metabolic by-products, especially short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as butyrate. SCFAs are produced by symbionts such as fecal Escherichia coli, which constitute the natural negative regulatory loop of TLR signal. Based on these mechanisms, targeting the “gut-TLR axis” represents the frontier of diabetes treatment. Although the intervention of specific probiotics has shown the prospect of enhancing barrier function, the variability of patient response highlights the complexity of host–microorganism interaction. Dietary patterns rich in polyphenols, such as Mediterranean diet, have been proved to interfere with the recruitment of TLR4 to lipid rafts, thus increasing the threshold of inflammatory activation.47 However, given the individual heterogeneity in TLR gene polymorphisms and baseline microbiota, a “one-size-fits-all” anti-inflammatory diet is elusive. Future therapeutic strategies will likely rely on integrating metagenomic and immunogenetic data to develop personalized nutrition protocols aimed at restoring symbiotic balance and fundamentally interrupting TLR-driven metaflammation.48

TLRs as Guardians of Autoimmunity: The T1D Paradigm

Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) is fundamentally characterized not merely by the terminal loss of β-cell mass, but by the collapse of immune tolerance orchestrated by the innate immune system. Within this pathological framework, the Toll-like receptor (TLR) family functions as a critical “molecular translator,” converting environmental triggers—such as viral signatures and tissue damage—into potent licensing signals that break peripheral tolerance to islet antigens.49

Enteroviruses, particularly Coxsackievirus B (CVB), are established environmental precipitants of T1D. Mechanistic insights by Chen et al elucidate that this association is driven by the engagement of endosomal TLR3 (sensing double-stranded RNA) and TLR7 (sensing single-stranded RNA).50 Viral invasion of the islet not only initiates a clearance response, but also lead to serious collateral pathological damage. Interferon type I (IFN-α/β) reaction driven by Toll-like receptor (TLR) will promote the high expression of MHC molecules on the surface of β cells. This “molecular marker” makes β cells highly visible to self-reactive CD8+ T cells, thus transforming the cell’s own antiviral defense mechanism into an autoimmune target. Crucially, the latest research reveals the mechanism of persistent inflammation: TLR signal has been proved to abnormally activate Hippo-YAP pathway in pancreatic exocrine tissue. This signal axis forms a pro-inflammatory microenvironment through paracrine mediators, which can maintain the local inflammatory state even after the viral load is removed.51,52

When the initial injury occurs, the driving mechanism of inflammation changes from exogenous pathogen to endogenous driving factor. The necrotic β cells release high mobility group protein 1(HMGB1) and its own DNA, which act on TLR2 and TLR4 as potent injury-related molecular patterns (DAMPs). At this time, β cells change from passive targets to active participants in self-destruction. Stimulated by TLR2/4, β cells actively secrete chemokine CXCL10, which is the key ligand of CXCR3+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs).53 This establishes a chemotactic gradient that guides autoreactive T-cells into the islet core, creating a self-amplifying loop of “cytotoxicity → DAMPs release → TLR activation → further infiltration”.54,55

The ultimate consequence of aberrant TLR signaling is the functional reprogramming of Antigen-Presenting Cells (APCs). In pancreatic draining lymph nodes, Dendritic Cells (DCs) stimulated by TLR3/4 ligands undergo rapid maturation, upregulating co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/86) and secreting IL-12. This cytokine environment will force the naive CD4+ T-cells to differentiate into pro-inflammatory Th1 lineage, thus disrupting the balance of regulatory T cells /Th1 cells.56 The research of Spinelli et al provided verification that the unique’ “TLR-driven interferon transcription characteristics” could be detected in peripheral blood months or even years before serological conversion. This shows that TLR disorder is the initial event of autoimmunity, not the secondary consequence.57

This pathological model is supported by genetic evidence. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) found that the polymorphism of TLR gene cluster is an important risk factor for type 1 diabetes mellitus, indicating that genetic variation of receptor sensitivity can adjust the threshold of autoimmune activation.58 However, there is a dynamic interaction between genetic factors and environment. As mentioned above, Dopkins et al confirmed that TLR5 dysfunction will lead to the collapse of mucosal tolerance.59 This’ intestinal-pancreatic axis’ interaction indicates that intestinal sensory deficit may indirectly accelerate the occurrence of pancreatic autoimmunity by allowing leakage of microbial antigens that structurally mimic islet proteins.60

The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic has provided a clinical validation of the TLR-mediated autoimmunity hypothesis. Beyond its role in acute infection, SARS-CoV-2 RNA has been shown to overactivate the TLR3/7 pathways within pancreatic macrophages and β-cells.61 In a hyperglycemic environment, this activation synergistically accelerates β-cell apoptosis and may trigger a breach in immune tolerance through molecular mimicry. These findings highlight TLRs as the core intersection between post-viral sequelae and the accelerated onset of new-onset diabetes in susceptible individuals, necessitating therapeutic strategies that can selectively decouple viral defense from autoimmune destruction.62

Toll-like receptors are at the intersection of host defense and autoimmune destruction. They are not only the guardians of viral infection, but also the constructors of the pathogenesis of type 1 diabetes. Such duality brings severe treatment challenges: indiscriminate blocking may damage antiviral immunity, while laissez-faire will lead to autoimmune progress. The strategy in the future must shift to “pathway-specific regulation”-selectively decoupling the NF-κB pathway and interferon pathway in β cells by using small molecular inhibitors or nano-drugs, so as to maintain a delicate balance between tissue protection and immune function.

Targeting the TLR Axis from Molecules to Microbes: New Therapeutic Frontiers

In view of the key role of Toll-like receptors in regulating metabolic inflammation, the therapeutic strategy has evolved from extensive immunosuppression to targeted molecular intervention. This section expounds the present situation of transformation research, and divides the intervention measures into three categories: direct pharmacological blocking, upstream ecological remodeling and new generation precision delivery system.

The main strategy involves destroying the initiation of inflammatory cascade at the receptor interface. Synthetic antagonists, such as the lipid A analog Eritoran (E5564), function by competitively binding to the MD-2 co-receptor, thereby preventing LPS-induced TLR4 dimerization. While originally developed for sepsis, studies by Park et al demonstrated its efficacy in a metabolic context: Eritoran treatment significantly attenuated NF-κB nuclear translocation in β-cells, preserving insulin secretory capacity under lipotoxic stress.63 Parallel efforts target downstream adaptors; novel MyD88 inhibitors (eg, TJ-M2010-5) have successfully uncoupled the receptor from its kinase cascade, mitigating the “cytokine storm” within the islet microenvironment.64

However, the clinical application of synthetic inhibitors is often limited by off-target toxicity and the inherent risks of systemic immunosuppression. Therefore, natural bioactive compounds have become an attractive alternative because of their good safety characteristics and pleiotropic mechanism. Structural biological analysis shows that polyphenols such as curcumin and resveratrol not only have antioxidant effect, but also can be physically embedded in TLR4/MD-2 complex, and inhibit receptor dimerization through steric hindrance.65 In addition, berberine, an isoquinoline alkaloid, shows a dual mechanism of action: it can improve insulin sensitivity and inhibit the expression of TLR4 in adipocytes and hepatocytes, thus effectively inhibiting systemic inflammatory response.66

A more systematic approach is aimed at the “diet-microorganism-immune axis” to reduce the load of upstream inflammatory ligands. Its goal is to contain metabolic endotoxemia before it involves peripheral tissues. Landmark work by Zhao et al elucidated that polysaccharides derived from medicinal fungi (eg, Ganoderma lucidum) exert their effects not by entering the circulation, but by modulating the intestinal niche. These prebiotics selectively suppress endotoxin-producing Proteobacteria while expanding mucin-degrading symbionts, thereby restoring barrier integrity and reducing the systemic LPS burden.67 This “ecological restoration” strategy mirrors the effects of established probiotic therapies (Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, Bifidobacterium longum), which physically reinforce tight junctions. Moreover, dietary interventions such as the Mediterranean diet leverage epigenetic mechanisms: the production of Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs) like butyrate functions as a natural Histone Deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, epigenetically silencing the transcriptional activity of the TLR4/NF-κB axis in host immune cells.68

Based on the in-depth understanding of the mechanism of extracellular vesicles (EVs) promoting disease progression, future research aims to use engineered exosomes as a new delivery carrier for precise treatment.69 By encapsulating therapeutic loads such as TLR4-siRNA or miR-146a mimetics in these lipid vesicles and modifying their surface proteins to achieve organ-specific targeting, therapeutic molecules can be selectively delivered to inflammatory β cells or glomerular podocytes in theory.8 This strategy, known as “therapeutic Trojan Horse”, is expected to be used to treat diseases such as diabetic nephropathy by repairing the function of damaged organs and minimizing the adverse effects on the whole immune system. Although clinical trial data have not been published so far, this technology has shown remarkable potential in proof-of-concept studies and in vitro/animal models.

While preclinical studies show potential, no TLR-specific therapy has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of diabetes. In addition, the heterogeneity of patients-manifested by TLR4 polymorphism and different microbial baseline-indicates that “one size fits all” single therapy is doomed to failure. The future lies in combination medicine and stratified medicine. It is worth noting that the recently approved anti-CD3 antibody Teplizumab, although designed to delay the progression of type 1 diabetes, has been proved to down-regulate the TLR pathway, which indicates that the curative effect often depends on multimodal immune regulation.70 Future clinical trials must integrate multiple omics analysis to identify patient subgroups driven by specific inflammatory phenotypes. Only through this layered strategy can we achieve the ultimate goal: not to block the immune system indiscriminately, but to accurately regulate the immune balance of diabetic patients. Frontier multimodal approach: redefining the accuracy of TLR biology. The transformation of Toll-like receptor (TLR) biology from laboratory to clinical needs to cross the technical gap between molecular mechanism and clinical heterogeneity. This section evaluates how emerging multimodal technologies-from nanoscale engineering to causal inference-reshape our ability to regulate, locate, and predict TLR-driven pathology.

The historical limitation of TLR therapeutic drugs lies in the inability to decouple beneficial host defense from harmful metabolic inflammation. Nanotechnology provides solutions through environmentally responsive delivery systems. The first use of pH/glucose-dual-sensitive chitosan nanoparticles. While originally designed for insulin delivery, this “smart cargo” concept has been adapted to encapsulate TLR modulators (eg, TAK-242), enabling their release exclusively within the acidic, hyperglycemic niche of inflamed islets.71 Further innovation targets the “Liver-Pancreas Axis”. Yao et al developed “interceptor nanomedicines” designed to scavenge circulating exosomes. By neutralizing TLR4-agonist-loaded vesicles released from lipotoxic hepatocytes, these nanoparticles effectively sever the inter-organ inflammatory relay at its source.72 In addition, functional nano-exosomes loaded with miR-146a have regenerative potential: they can simultaneously inhibit NF-κB pathway and reactivate autophagy flow, thus promoting the survival of β cells.73

Although nanotechnology provides a delivery vector, single cell genomics technology determines the target. Traditional batch sequencing masks the key differences between “silent” cells and “active” cells. While early transcriptomic insights into islet pathology heavily relied on resources like the NOD mouse islet transcriptome atlas, the field has recently been propelled by human validation data from international consortia such as the Human Pancreas Analysis Program (HPAP).74 The integration of single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics completely changed this resolution, allowing for the translation of rodent findings into human contexts. Wang et al utilized spatial mapping to identify the abnormal upregulation of TLR3/7 specifically in retinal microglia, spatially linking innate immunity to neurovascular damage in diabetic retinopathy.75 At the systemic level, Li et al revealed that chronic inflammation is not driven by the whole monocyte pool, but by a unique subgroup of’ “TLR4 overexpression/depletion”. These cells are closely related to serum IL-1β and insulin resistance, and are identified as the real driving factors of meta-inflammation.76 In islets, trajectory analysis combined with CRISPR screening has begun to locate “inflammatory pacemaker” cells. Targeting these hyperactive nodes, rather than the whole tissue, represents a strategy to preserve global immune competence while ablating local pathology.77 Furthermore, recent integrative omics have elucidated the Hippo-YAP/TLR4 axis in exocrine-endocrine crosstalk, providing a novel molecular framework for virus-induced autoimmunity.78

The evolution of Polygenic Risk Scores (PRS) marks a shift from metabolic risk assessment to “immunometabolic Stratification.” Leveraging large-scale genetic and longitudinal datasets from global consortia such as TEDDY (The Environmental Determinants of Diabetes in the Young) and DIAMOND, researchers are now capable of moving beyond small-scale observations to robustly map TLR-related genetic risk in human cohorts.79,80 Traditional models, focused on insulin secretion loci, often miss the inflammatory component of disease risk. Recent iterations now integrate variants within the TLR gene cluster to calculate an individual’s “Inflammatory Susceptibility Score”. Guo et al’ s research (2025) shows that the gene–diet interaction is nonlinear in East Asian population: if individuals in the tenth highest PRS eat high-fat diet, their risk of type 2 diabetes will increase by 2.3 times, and this susceptibility is mediated by endotoxemia driven by TLR4.81 By integrating functional polymorphism, this stratification mechanism has been further improved, and patients can be divided into “inflammatory endotypes” with unique cytokine profiles.82 The new generation of dynamic PRS combined with artificial intelligence algorithm aims to predict the risk trajectory, which is expected to guide the preventive use of immunomodulators for genetically high-risk groups to achieve primary prevention.83 Although observational studies suggest correlation, Mendelian Randomization (MR) provides the “causal anchors.” By utilizing genetic variants as unconfounded instrumental variables, MR has dissected the complex web of immuno-metabolism. Zhou et al provided pivotal evidence using multi-variable MR, revealing that TLR4 signaling mediates the causal effect of gut dysbiosis (specifically Bifidobacterium depletion) on T2D risk.84 This establishes the “Microbiota-TLR-Inflammation” axis as a causal driver, not just a correlation. Cross-organ analyses by Rosoff et al further linked PCSK9-regulated lipid metabolism to TLR4 activation, providing a genetic basis for lipotoxicity.85 Crucially, MR studies in Diabetic Nephropathy have confirmed TLR4 as a causal driver of renal fibrosis, validating it as a viable therapeutic target rather than a mere biomarker.86 Current efforts are focused on an “Integrated MR-Single-Cell” framework, aiming to map these causal effects to specific cell types, predicting that targeted intervention could reverse up to 20% of early-stage autoimmune diabetes cases.87

Discussion

The evolution of TLR research in diabetes marks a definitive transition from a linear innate immune model to a multifaceted immunometabolic interface. While early preclinical successes with broad TLR4 inhibitors failed to translate into clinical efficacy, this discrepancy likely stems from a lack of spatiotemporal precision rather than target invalidity.88–90 Consequently, the field is pivoting toward a “pathway calibration” paradigm that respects the inherent duality of TLR signaling—where the same receptor maintains mucosal barrier integrity while simultaneously driving systemic insulin resistance.91,92 Future success hinges on defining the “critical threshold” where protective autophagy shifts to apoptotic stress, requiring the development of biased ligands capable of dampening pathological peaks without compromising essential host defense.

A critical conceptual leap in this recalibration is the identification of the “Trojan Horse” exosomal pathway.8 By bypassing traditional surface receptor surveillance, this hidden communication channel provides a robust molecular explanation for the persistence of β-cell failure despite systemic anti-inflammatory treatments. This shift necessitates a move toward intracellularly targeted nanomedicines that can neutralize ligands within the endosomal compartment, effectively severing the inter-organ inflammatory relay at its source. Furthermore, the integration of scRNA-seq and Mendelian Randomization (MR) has dismantled the assumption of uniform tissue responses, suggesting that inflammatory endotypes, rather than generalized hyperglycemia, should drive patient stratification.93,94

Looking forward, the clinical goal is to move beyond narrow “anti-inflammatory” attempts toward a comprehensive, multimodal framework of precision medicine. This framework must not only leverage organ-specific nanotherapies and risk stratification to reset the “immunometabolic thermostat” but also encompass the broader systemic nature of chronic disease.95,96 Just as molecular medicine is becoming increasingly integrated, clinical evidence from other domains—such as recent randomized controlled trials demonstrating the efficacy of combining jaw exercises with cervical stabilization for chronic neck pain—highlights how multimodal rehabilitation and adjunct functional strategies can significantly enhance therapeutic outcomes by addressing the systemic inflammatory sequelae often overlooked in single-target approaches.97

Ultimately, the next decade of diabetes management lies in the synergy between high-resolution molecular targeting and holistic patient-centered care. By integrating these diverse clinical perspectives with targeted TLR modulation, we can move closer to true disease modification that restores physiological homeostasis at its molecular and systemic roots.

Conclusion

In summary, the diabetic pancreatic β-cell is not merely a passive victim of metabolic stress but an active participant orchestrated by TLR-driven immunometabolic signaling. This review synthesizes three major conceptual pillars that are reshaping our understanding of this pathology. First, the exosomal “Trojan horse” pathway provides a paradigm-shifting mechanism for organ cross-talk (the gut-pancreas axis), highlighting how circulating inflammatory cargo bypasses systemic surveillance to trigger intracellular endosomal TLRs. Second, enabled by advanced technologies such as single-cell genomics and human transcriptome atlases (eg, HPAP), we have unmasked the cellular heterogeneity of the islet, identifying specific “immune-hub” β-cells that initiate localized metaflammation. Finally, these mechanistic insights demand a new clinical roadmap. The future of diabetes management must pivot from indiscriminate, broad-spectrum TLR blockade—which risks severe immunosuppression—toward precision pathway calibration. By integrating human genetic data (eg, TEDDY/DIAMOND) with targeted nanodelivery systems, future therapeutics should aim to selectively uncouple metabolic stress from destructive downstream effectors (such as JNK and NLRP3) in specific cell subsets. This precision stratification holds the potential to finally translate TLR biology into true disease-modifying therapies for diabetes.
