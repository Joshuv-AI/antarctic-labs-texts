Older Adults, Anti‐Amyloid Therapy, and Frailty: What Oncology Can Teach Us
Authors: Miguel Germán Borda, Kevin O'Hara‐Veintimilla, Dag Aarsland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnti‐amyloid therapies, such as lecanemab or donanemab, represent the first disease‐modifying treatments approved for early Alzheimer's disease (AD) in individuals with confirmed amyloid pathology. Their implementation in routine care raises important challenges, particularly in older adults with heterogeneous functional reserve and multimorbidity. We address the role of frailty in refining clinical decision‐making for anti‐amyloid therapies.MethodsThis short communication presents a conceptual discussion informed by geriatric oncology, where frailty assessment and comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) are routinely used to individualize treatment in heterogeneous older populations. We describe how similar principles may be applied to anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies once regulatory eligibility has been established, and outline a frailty‐informed conceptual framework to support clinical decision‐making in routine care.ResultsThis conceptual analysis proposes a stepwise, frailty‐informed clinical framework that integrates regulatory eligibility assessment with brief frailty screening and targeted comprehensive geriatric assessment. The framework defines differentiated clinical pathways for robust, pre‐frail, and frail individuals, linking frailty status to specific decisions regarding treatment initiation, need for prehabilitation, intensity of monitoring, and consideration of treatment deferral. By embedding frailty assessment within routine clinical workflows, the framework operationalizes evaluation of physiological reserve, anticipates treatment burden and monitoring feasibility, and provides a structured approach to individualized risk–benefit appraisal for anti‐amyloid therapies.ConclusionsFrailty‐informed frameworks may offer a pragmatic and ethically grounded approach to support real‐world implementation of anti‐amyloid therapies, guiding treatment selection as well as longitudinal decisions on monitoring, continuation, and reassessment over time.

Anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibody therapies, as disease‐modifying treatments (DMTs), have recently received regulatory approval in the European Union for patients with early Alzheimer's disease (AD) and confirmed amyloid pathology [1]. This approval represents a major therapeutic milestone, marking the first disease‐modifying treatment to demonstrate a clinically meaningful slowing of cognitive decline in this population. However, translating trial results into routine clinical practice poses substantial challenges. Identifying appropriate candidates—particularly among older adults, who constitute the majority of patients with AD—remains complex due to advanced age, multimorbidity, heterogeneous functional reserve, and the need for intensive monitoring. These factors complicate risk–benefit assessment beyond the regulatory eligibility criteria applied in clinical trials, highlighting the need for more refined approaches to patient selection in real‐world settings.

Frailty is highly prevalent in AD, affecting approximately 19% of individuals with mild disease and between 24.3% and 98.9% of those with more advanced stages [2]. In people living with dementia, frailty is consistently associated with accelerated cognitive and functional decline, increased hospitalization, and higher mortality rates [3]. Importantly, frailty captures vulnerability that is not fully explained by chronological age, diagnosis, or comorbidity counts alone. On this basis, frailty could plausibly influence the benefit–risk balance of anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibody therapies, potentially affecting vulnerability to treatment‐related adverse events such as amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), tolerance to repeated infusions and monitoring, and the capacity for recovery following complications.

However, current evidence regarding anti‐amyloid therapies in older adults or highly comorbid patients is limited and therefore calls for individualized, multidisciplinary risk–benefit assessment [4].

In this context, geriatric oncology provides a strong precedent for incorporating frailty into treatment decision‐making. In cancer care, comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA)—a multidimensional evaluation of functional, cognitive, nutritional, and psychosocial domains—operationalizes frailty and is used to guide individualized treatment. Frailty independently predicts toxicity, hospitalization, treatment discontinuation, and survival, often reclassifying 30%–50% of patients initially deemed fit [4]. CGA‐guided interventions reduced grade 3–5 toxicities from 71% to 51% and decreased falls from 21% to 12%, highlighting the feasibility of tailoring therapy to resilience.

The robustness of this approach is supported by population‐level evidence. A 2025 systematic review and meta‐analysis of 58 studies found that frailty—assessed with tools such as the Geriatric‐8 and the Vulnerable Elders Survey‐13—was associated with higher risks of mortality (hazard ratio 1.68, 95% CI 1.41–2.00), toxicity (odds ratio 1.83, 95% CI 1.24–2.68), treatment intolerance (odds ratio 1.68, 95% CI 1.32–2.12), and hospitalization (odds ratio 1.94, 95% CI 1.32–2.83). Across tumor types and settings, frailty screening consistently informs shared decision‐making and risk–benefit assessment [5].

Experience from oncology indicates that routine frailty assessment enhances patient selection, promotes prehabilitation, individualizes treatment intensity, and improves outcomes while reducing harm [6, 7]. A similar approach could strengthen therapeutic decisions for anti‐amyloid therapies. Incorporating structured frailty evaluations within a multidisciplinary “neurogeriatrics” framework—including neurology, geriatrics, nutrition, rehabilitation, and radiology—may help anticipate complications, personalize management, and align treatment with patient resilience and vulnerability.

Frailty‐Informed Framework for Anti‐Amyloid Therapies Administration

From a practical perspective, frailty assessment can be incorporated into decision‐making for new anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibody therapies, such as lecanemab or donanemab, through a stepwise and scalable clinical framework that complements existing regulatory eligibility criteria. In this approach, standard requirements for treatment initiation—including confirmation of early AD, amyloid positivity, genetic risk stratification, baseline neuroimaging, and the ability to comply with MRI monitoring—remain mandatory prerequisites [8]. Frailty evaluation is introduced only after regulatory eligibility has been established, ensuring that assessment of vulnerability refines, rather than delays or complicates, access to therapy. Initial frailty stratification may rely on brief screening instruments that are feasible within routine outpatient service or memory clinic workflows and can be administered by trained nursing or clinical staff. Tools such as the FRAIL scale allow rapid categorization of individuals as robust, pre‐frail, or frail, providing an early signal of physiological reserve and tolerance to potential treatment‐related stressors [9]. This first step enables risk stratification without imposing the burden of the CGA on all candidates.

In robust individuals, frailty screening does not add further procedural complexity, and treatment may proceed through standard shared decision‐making pathways with conventional safety monitoring. In contrast, pre‐frail individuals represent an intermediate‐risk group in whom vulnerability may be present but potentially modifiable. For these patients, targeted CGA focused on physical performance, functional status, nutritional state, comorbidity burden, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and social support can help distinguish reversible contributors to risk from more fixed limitations [10, 11]. Specific recommendations for frailty assessment tools in people living with dementia are discussed elsewhere [2]. Integration of these findings into multidisciplinary discussions—including neurology, geriatrics, and allied health professionals—supports individualized appraisal of treatment suitability and the identification of prehabilitation strategies aimed at optimizing resilience prior to or alongside treatment initiation [12].

Patients classified as frail constitute a high‐risk subgroup in whom the balance between potential benefit and harm is most uncertain. In this context, mandatory CGA and team‐based review may identify markers of limited physiological reserve, high treatment burden, or barriers to safe monitoring that substantially reduce the likelihood of net clinical benefit. For such individuals, deferring or withholding anti‐amyloid therapies in favor of symptom‐oriented and supportive care may better align with patient‐centered outcomes, with the option of reassessment if clinical status meaningfully improves.

Importantly, frailty is not a static attribute. Longitudinal reassessment is essential, particularly following adverse events such as amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities, the emergence of new comorbidities, or accelerated functional decline. Re‐evaluating frailty domains over time can inform decisions regarding continuation, temporary interruption, or discontinuation of therapy, reinforcing frailty as a dynamic guide to clinical judgment rather than a one‐time exclusion criterion.

Similarities and Differences Between Amyloid Therapies and Oncology

Although anti‐amyloid therapies for AD and oncological treatments differ fundamentally, geriatric oncology offers a relevant conceptual framework for risk stratification and individualized decision‐making in older adults. In both fields, chronological age alone has proven to be an inadequate predictor of treatment tolerance and clinical outcomes, prompting the systematic incorporation of frailty as a key determinant of the benefit–risk balance [3, 6] (Table 1).

Nevertheless, important differences between the two therapeutic domains must be acknowledged. Anti‐amyloid therapies and cancer treatments differ substantially in their mechanisms of action, therapeutic goals, and toxicity profiles. Unlike many cancer treatments, which often confer immediate benefits that may be curative or clearly palliative, anti‐amyloid therapies aim to modify the trajectory of a chronic neurodegenerative disease, with modest and delayed clinical effects [1, 13]. In addition, adverse events associated with anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies, such as ARIA, arise from distinct pathophysiological mechanisms compared with the systemic toxicities typically observed with cytotoxic or targeted cancer therapies. Taken together, these differences limit the direct transferability of predictive models developed in oncology to amyloid‐directed treatments.

Despite these distinctions, the conceptual parallel lies in the shared need to assess physiological reserve, recovery capacity, and vulnerability to potentially stressful interventions.

In the context of DMTs, oncology does not provide direct evidence regarding anti‐amyloid therapy efficacy or safety; however, it offers a well‐established model for structuring clinical decision‐making in heterogeneous and vulnerable populations. Evidence from oncological therapies demonstrates that systematic frailty assessment, supported by practical and scalable tools, enhances patient selection, improves safety, and promotes individualized care. Applying similar principles may support a safer, more personalized, and ethically robust use of anti‐amyloid therapies in older adults. As access to disease‐modifying therapies expands, including through international initiatives aimed at improving health system readiness and equitable implementation such as Access to Alzheimer's Disease (ACCESS‐AD) [14], frailty‐informed clinical frameworks may help ensure that broader availability is accompanied by appropriate patient selection, safe use, and alignment with patient‐centered goals, rather than treatment decisions based solely on regulatory eligibility or chronological age.
