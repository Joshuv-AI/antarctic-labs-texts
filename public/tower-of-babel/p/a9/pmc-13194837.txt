Eicosadienoic acid enhances antibody production in CHO cells by prolonging culture longevity and attenuating apoptosis
Authors: Xinxin Pei, Xiaoyi Liu, Rui Wang, Tengfei Ma, Buchang Zhang, Changzhi Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFatty acids are key nutrients exerting multiple functions in cells. This study aimed to investigate the effect of fatty acids on antibody expression in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells. Among different fatty acids, cis, cis − 11, 14-eicosadienoic acid (EDA, C20:2, 11,14-cis) was screened to exert the strongest effect to boost antibody yield in CHO-IgG cells tested. In batch and fed-batch culture processes, the antibody yield was promoted by 48% and 59% with supplementation of 10 µM of EDA respectively. Meanwhile, the culture duration was significantly prolonged while the apoptosis ratios were suppressed by EDA. The addition of EDA also promoted the yield of other antibodies transiently expressed in CHO-K1 cells, including Rituximab, Herceptin, and one anti-uPAR antibody. These findings revealed the potential of EDA in promoting antibody expression in CHO cells, shedding light on the application of functional fatty acids in therapeutic antibody manufacturing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13568-026-02050-4.Keywords: Eicosadienoic acid, CHO cells, Antibody expression

Introduction

Antibody therapeutics play a pivotal role in the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of multiple diseases (Gruell et al. 2022). Over the past decades, monoclonal antibodies have consistently accounted for approximately half of the annual sales revenue from the top ten global pharmaceutical products (Grilo and Mantalaris 2019). The global monoclonal antibody market is projected to exceed 237.64 billion dollars by 2030 (Aboul-Ella et al. 2024). The structural complexity of antibody molecules, characterized by the large molecular weight, intricate three-dimensional conformations, and essential post-translational modifications, necessitates the host cells capable of producing exogenous proteins with intact spatial configurations and functional modifications. Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells have emerged as the predominant system for therapeutic protein manufacturing, responsible for producing most clinically approved biologics, due to their great ability to adapt to various environments and resistance to disadvantage conditions (Tzani et al. 2024; Zeh et al. 2024).

Despite their industrial dominance, CHO cells still face limitations in serum-free culture systems, particularly with impaired viability under high-density growth conditions. Challenges including metabolic byproduct accumulation (lactate, ammonium), shear stress, osmotic pressure fluctuations, and dissolved oxygen variations collectively induce apoptotic cascades that constrain antibody titers in bioreactors (Alhuthali et al. 2021; Buchsteiner et al. 2018; Pereira et al. 2018; Schulze et al. 2021). Consequently, CHO cell engineering focuses on strategies, such as screening for high-producing, stress-resistant cell lines, optimizing expression vector systems, and refining media formulations and feeding protocols (Ha et al. 2022; O’Flaherty et al. 2020). Previously, we also provided that engineering CHO cells with miR-106b/CYLD, or mutant PTEN could significantly promote antibody production (Lu et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2019a, b; Zhou et al. 2021). However, genetic engineering approaches, while promising, also have limitations: modifying apoptosis-related genes often adversely affects growth kinetics, prolonging the time of clonal selection; overexpression of functional genes competes for cellular translational resources; and gene editing carries risks of off-target effects (Kalkan et al. 2023). Complementary to cellular engineering, exogenous supplementation has gained traction as a viable strategy for productivity enhancement (Ladiwala et al. 2024; Priem et al. 2025; Ritacco et al. 2018). For instance, a previous screening of phenolic antioxidants identified several candidates capable of decoupling growth from production (Toronjo Urquiza et al. 2019). Among these, resveratrol was shown to transiently arrest cells in S-phase and nearly double specific IgG productivity, while catechins improved final antibody titers by up to 1.5-fold and reduced acidic charge variants (Toronjo-Urquiza et al. 2020a, b). Similarly, the antioxidant baicalein enhances the production efficiency of recombinant antibodies by reducing oxidative stress in CHO cell cultures (Ha et al. 2018). Adding valproic acid, an HDAC inhibitor, in the late period of CHO cell fed-batch culture enhanced the antibody titer by 20% (Yang et al. 2014). Similar to valproic acid, the HDAC1 inhibitor valeric acid, when administered in a 2 L bioreactor, enhanced the monoclonal antibody production in rCHO cells by inducing G1 phase arrest (Park et al. 2016). Ectoine increased monoclonal antibody production by both enhancing cell viability and extending the culture period (Jarusintanakorn et al. 2024).Therefore, screening more functional and low-cost natural additives is promising for CHO cell engineering.

Fatty acids are key nutrients, essential components of cell membranes, energy sources via β-oxidation, and important signaling molecules involved in diverse cellular processes (Lu et al. 2025; Smolkova and Gotvaldova 2025; Wang et al. 2025). Recently, increasing evidence have emerged to provide the link between fatty acids and antibody expression. Injecting medium-chain fatty acid emulsions in animals significantly enhances the activity of immune cells, promoting immunoglobulin secretion (De Keyser et al. 2019). Another study indicates that relatively cold stress triggers the alteration of lipid abundance and saturation in CHO cells, particularly increasing the level of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs), which correlates with enhanced recombinant protein secretion (Masterton et al. 2010; Yoon et al. 2006). Expression of several secretory biopharmaceuticals was reported to be boosted in sterol regulatory element-binding transcription factor 1 (SREBF1) or stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) engineered CHO cells (Budge et al. 2020a, b). These findings suggest UFAs play a vital role in recombinant protein expression in CHO cells. However, it remains unclear which fatty acid supplementation could efficiently promote antibody production in CHO cells.

Herein, the function of different fatty acids on antibody production in CHO cells was investigated. Among fatty acids tested, cis, cis − 11, 14-eicosadienoic acid (EDA, C20:2, 11,14-cis) showed the most significant promotion effect on antibody expression. The further application of supplementation in batch and fed-batch culture processes and its potential mechanisms were also explored.

Materials and methods

Cell line and cell maintenance

The CHO-K1, CHO-IgG stably expressing anti-EGFR antibodies, and A549 cells used in this study were cultured as previously described (Lu et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2019a, b, 2022; Zhou et al. 2021). In brief, CHO-K1 cells were cultured in F12K medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 ℃ under 5% CO2. CHO-IgG cells were maintained in CD CHO medium (Gibco) containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin, at 37 ℃ under 8% CO2 and shaking at 125 rpm. In batch culture, CHO-IgG cells were seeded at 5 × 105/mL in shake flasks containing 30 mL of medium, with methionine sulfoxide (Sigma, USA) added at a final concentration of 60 µM. In fed-batch culture, cells were seeded similarly, with an initial supplement of 15% feed medium (CHO CD EfficientFeed™ B) and methionine sulfoxide (60 µM final concentration). Additional CHO CD EfficientFeed™ B was supplied every three days. For both cultures, 1 mL of cell suspension was collected every two days. Part of the sample was used to assess cell viability, and the remainder was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min to collect supernatant for concentration determination via ELISA. Sampling ceased when cell viability dropped to 0%. EDA and other fatty acids (Sigma) were dissolved in ethanol at indicated concentrations before being added to the culture medium. Vehicle concentration was constant across conditions, and that precipitation or phase separation was not observed.

Cell proliferation and apoptosis assays

Cell proliferation and apoptosis assays were assessed as previously described (Xu et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2021).

Cell transfection

Plasmids were transfected into CHO-K1 cells using Lipo8000 (Beyotime, China). Briefly, cells were seeded into 6-well plates one day in advance to achieve approximately 60% confluence on the day of transfection. Transfection was then performed with Lipo8000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Lei et al. 2024).

Antibody quantity and quality assays

Relevant antibody concentrations were determined using a sandwich ELISA. Anti-EGFR antibodies derived from the CHO-IgG cells were collected and purified. Then, antibody integrity was assessed by SDS-PAGE and size-exclusion chromatography high-performance liquid chromatography (SEC-HPLC). The affinity between antibodies and EGFR on A549 cell membrane was measured by flow cytometry. These methods were all performed as previously described (Xu et al. 2019a).

Fatty acid detection via GC-MS

To measure the speed of EDA exhaustion in culture medium and the changes of fatty acid profile in CHO cells, culture medium and CHO cells were collected and subjected to GC-MS assay as previously described (Xu et al. 2019b, 2022).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses in all experiments were performed as previously described (Lu et al. 2018). In brief, all data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 and Microsoft Excel 2021. Comparisons between two groups were evaluated by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, while multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Screening functional fatty acids accelerating antibody expression in CHO-IgG cells

To screen the potential fatty acids for boosting antibody expression, 26 types of fatty acids, with different lengths of carbon chain and degrees of saturation, were dissolved with ethanol and directly added into the culture medium to compare antibody expression (Fig. 1A). Based on preliminary toxicity testing (cell viabilit95%, Fig. S1), 10 and 20 µM concentrations were selected for further screening. As shown in Fig. 1B, caprylic acid (C8:0), myristic acid (C14:0), pentadecanoic acid (C15:1, 10-cis), stearic acid (C18:0), oleic acid (C18:1, 9-cis), and EDA (C20:2, 11, 14-cis) were found to boost the antibody yield of CHO-IgG cells in six-well plates, with 10 µM of EDA showing the most significant effect (antibody concentration was promoted by 1.4-fold compared to the control group). Further, we found that antibody yield increased progressively with rising EDA concentrations up to 10 µM, after which a declining trend was observed (Fig. 1C). Meanwhile, no significant change was observed in viable cell density (VCD) (Fig. 1D) or cell viability (Fig. 1E). Taken together, our data showed that supplementation with 10 µM of EDA yielded the most substantial improvement in antibody production.

B Assessment of antibody expression in CHO-IgG cells after treatment with different fatty acids. C–E Antibody expression (C), VCD (D), and cell viability (E) of CHO-IgG cells cultured in 6-well plates and treated with different concentrations of EDA. ns, not significant. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

To date, little is known about the role and function of EDA in cells. It can be internalized and metabolized by RAW264.7 macrophages, thereby modulating the inflammatory responses (Huang et al. 2011; Pereira et al. 2014). Clinical studies have revealed its association with an increased risk of prostate cancer, greater severity of moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis in males, and a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes (Forouhi et al. 2016; Lin et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2023). However, its correlation with antibody production in CHO cells was previously unknown. Therefore, we focused on the detailed function of EDA in CHO cells and antibody expression.

EDA promotes antibody yield in batch culture

Next, the potential of EDA on antibody production was tested under batch culture conditions. EDA concentration in the medium decreased by approximately 96% from day 2 to day 14 (Fig. S2), indicating effective cellular uptake. Unexpectedly, the administration of EDA decreased the VCD of CHO-IgG cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A). Although the antibody titer and Qp were slightly enhanced in certain EDA treated groups (Fig. 2B and C), the impaired VCD is a disadvantage for EDA application in CHO cell engineering. To overcome this shortcoming, the timepoint of EDA addition was optimized in the exponential period in batch culture process. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, the VCD and viability of CHO-IgG cells were not affected by EDA addition on day 8. Conversely, the administration of EDA prolonged the VCD and cell viability in the decline period, subsequently extending the total culture period from 20 days up to 25 days. As expected, the antibody titer in EDA treated group was promoted by 48% compared to the control group (from 0.98 up to 1.42 g/L) (Fig. 2F). Moreover, in batch culture, day 8 was identified as the optimal time point for EDA addition based on antibody yield and Qp (Fig. S3A and B). These data implied that the administration of EDA in the exponential period of batch culture could enhance CHO cell performance and antibody expression.

EDA promotes antibody yield in fed-batch culture

Given the similar ELISA results for 8 µM and 10 µM of EDA in preliminary shake flask screening (Fig. 2B), both concentrations were tested in fed-batch culture with EDA supplementation starting on day 6, a similar timepoint of the exponential period in fed-batch culture. Results showed that both 8 µM and 10 µM of EDA delayed the downtrend of cell viability in the decline period when compared to the control group (Fig. 3A and B). The culture periods were extended from 20 days up to 22 days and 24 days in 8 µM and 10 µM of EDA treated groups, respectively. Subsequently, the final harvest yields were 1.12 g/L in the control group, 1.59 g/L and ~ 1.78 g/L in 8 and 10 µM of EDA treated groups, corresponding to increases of 27% and 59% respectively (Fig. 3C). Similarly, in fed-batch culture, day 6 was identified as the optimal time point for EDA addition based on antibody yield and Qp (Fig. S3C and D). Taken together, these data also confirmed that EDA supplementation initiated from the exponential period of fed batch culture could facilitate antibody production.

The impact of EDA supplementation on antibody quality

To evaluate the quality of anti-EGFR antibodies produced by CHO-IgG cells with or without EDA supplementation, antibody integrity was firstly analyzed by reducing and non-reducing SDS-PAGE. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, results showed that EDA did not alter antibody composition and assembly. SEC-HPLC analysis showed that EDA did not affect antibody purity (Fig. 4C and D). Flow cytometry assay also revealed that antibodies in the two groups showed nearly identical binding curves between antibody and EGFR molecules on A549 cell membrane, indicating that there was no significant difference in antibody affinity (Fig. 4E). Taken together, no differences were detected by SDS-PAGE, SEC-HPLC, and binding assays.

EDA suppresses apoptosis in cultured CHO cells

Apoptosis is the most common type of cell death in CHO cells during the culture process, hampering cell proliferation at high density and antibody expression (Catalan-Tatjer et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). In previous shake flask experiments, EDA consistently exhibited properties of delayed growth and extended culture duration in both batch and fed-batch culture processes. This drove us to hypothesize that EDA might interfere with the occurrence of apoptosis, particularly in the decline period of CHO cell culture. Results in our apoptosis assays confirmed that EDA supplementation significantly suppressed both early and late apoptosis in CHO cells (Fig. 5A and B). Statistical analysis revealed that early apoptosis decreased from 15.16 to 10.76%, and late apoptosis decreased from 14.36 to 10.08%. Overall, EDA reduced the total apoptosis ratio in CHO-IgG cells by 23% (Fig. 5C), which likely constitutes one potential mechanism underlying its productivity enhancement.

EDA suppresses apoptosis in CHO-IgG cells. A and B CHO-IgG cells were treated with vehicle (A) or EDA (10 µM) (B) for 14 days, and the level of apoptosis was evaluated by Annexin V/PI double staining under batch culture conditions. C Quantitative analysis of apoptosis ratios. ***p < 0.001

EDA promotes transient antibody expression in CHO-K1 cells

The potential of EDA on transient antibody production was measured in CHO-K1 cells. Three plasmid kits encoding Rituximab, Herceptin, and one anti-uPAR antibody were harnessed here as previously described (Xu et al. 2019a). CHO-K1 cells were transfected with these plasmid kits, followed by the incubation with or without EDA. As shown in Fig. 6A and C, EDA supplementation increased the concentrations of Rituximab, anti-uPAR antibody, and Herceptin on day 5 by 30.6%, 35.8%, and 37.4%, respectively. These data indicated that EDA supplementation might be potential for more antibody expression in CHO cells.

Discussion

Optimizing the efficiency of recombinant protein expression in mammalian cell culture systems remains a critical pursuit in biopharmaceutical research. Maximizing therapeutic protein yields necessitates multi-faceted strategies encompassing host cell engineering, vector optimization, and medium or process development (Combe and Sokolenko 2021; Fischer et al. 2015). Chemical supplementation to modulate the culture environment has emerged as a particularly attractive approach in CHO cells due to its operational simplicity, compatibility with existing processes, and lower technical barriers compared to genetic modification. Fatty acids, key nutrients and structural components, participate in numerous cellular processes (Priem et al. 2025; Szeliova et al. 2020). In this study, we screened and identified that EDA was a potent stimulus for antibody expression among different fatty acids. This represents the first report of the role of EDA in antibody expression in CHO cells. In our fed-batch culture process, EDA administration was shown to achieve a remarkable 59% promotion in antibody yield, shedding light on its potential application in CHO cell engineering.

Fatty acids may influence cellular metabolism and fate through multiple pathways (Lu et al. 2025). In CHO cells under high-intensity protein expression, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) may serve as an energy source, enhance membrane fluidity, and alleviate ER stress (Ali et al. 2018; Ali and Szabó 2023; Budge et al. 2020a; Kel et al. 2005; Poursharifi et al. 2025; Toncan et al. 2025). These effects may collectively contribute to the mechanism by which EDA exerts its function. However, not all PUFAs in our screening exhibited functional activity, suggesting that the effects are structure-dependent. Furthermore, apoptosis is the most common type of cell death in CHO cells during high cell density culture (Zhang et al. 2024). Our results confirmed that EDA significantly suppressed both early and late apoptosis in CHO-IgG cells, reducing the overall apoptosis rate by 23%. This aligns with previous reports where compounds like baicalein and BiP inducer X (BIX) enhanced CHO productivity via apoptosis inhibition (Ha et al. 2018, 2019). Therefore, apoptosis reduction is a contributing mechanism here.

To further distinguish the drivers of enhanced antibody production, we analyzed IVCD and Qp under different culture modes. In batch culture, EDA increased both IVCD and Qp, indicating contributions from extended longevity and enhanced per-cell productivity (Fig. S4A and B). In fed-batch culture, however, Qp increased significantly while IVCD remained largely unchanged, suggesting that productivity enhancement was primarily Qp-driven (Fig. S4C and D). These findings demonstrate that the drivers of EDA-mediated antibody production vary depending on the culture process. Notably, a study has identified a distinct cell size increase phase in CHO fed-batch cultures, during which specific productivity increased linearly with cell volume alongside improved metabolic efficiency (Pan et al. 2017). This offers a complementary interpretation for our EDA findings, suggesting potential involvement of cell volume changes. Future studies including cell size measurements could help clarify the underlying mechanism.

One previous report suggested that EDA might exert the most inhibitory effect in eicosanoic acid (C20:0) analogs with different saturation, against inosine 5’-monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH), an enzyme catalyzes an essential step in de novo biosynthesis of guanine nucleotides for cell proliferation (Mizushina et al. 2007). Various strategies to decouple cell growth from recombinant protein production in CHO cells have been extensively reported, including cell cycle arrest, chemical additives, and mild hypothermia (Galli et al. 2024; Majumdar et al. 2025; Ryan et al. 2026; Sunley and Butler 2010). These approaches redirect cellular resources from proliferation toward product synthesis by modulating cell cycle progression or metabolic activity. This binding between EDA and IMPDH may lead to the inhibition of cell proliferation, consistent with our partial results in the exponential period of culture (Fig. 2A). By restricting nucleotide availability, EDA-mediated IMPDH inhibition may favor productivity under culture conditions relevant to antibody production. However, other analogs of eicosanoic acid did not perform the similar effect in promoting antibody expression (Fig. 1B). Therefore, the mechanism of EDA needs to be further studied in the future.

There are still some limitations in our study. Only one stably expressed cell line was used, and the mechanistic investigation remains at the level of phenotypic correlation. The transfection efficiency, cell growth, and viability in transient expression experiments were also not monitored. Meanwhile, other fatty acids, including caprylic acid and oleic acid, may also exert potential promotion effects on CHO-IgG cells; their detailed functions need to be further studied under varying conditions in the future. While the present study was conducted in shake flask systems to establish proof-of-concept, future validation in controlled bioreactor setups would further substantiate the potential of EDA supplementation in biopharmaceutical manufacturing.

In summary, this study is the first to demonstrate that EDA exerts a remarkable productivity-enhancing effect in CHO cells, primarily through the inhibition of apoptosis and prolonged culture duration, shedding light on the potential of functional fatty acids in therapeutic antibody manufacturing.
