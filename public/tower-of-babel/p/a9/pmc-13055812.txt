Access Challenges for Septal Reduction Therapy Among Individuals With Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Anjali T Owens, Patricia Schuler, Jenny Jiang, Xu Han, Ervant Maksabedian Hernandez, Amy Anderson, Yiyu Fang, Andrea Steffens, Anandkumar Dubey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn symptomatic patients with treatment‐refractory obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, septal reduction therapy (SRT) is recommended to relieve obstruction. This study examined the challenges associated with SRT access in the United States.MethodsCross‐sectional study of commercially insured individuals or Medicare Advantage with Part D members captured in the Optum Research Database from May 2012 to April 2022. Patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy who underwent SRT were identified based on diagnosis/procedure codes. Treatment center procedural volume was defined based on tertiles. SRT access was assessed by the distance patients traveled to care and the proportion of patients treated at hypertrophic cardiomyopathy centers of excellence (COE) by region. Cohorts of patients with coronary artery disease who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting were compared with the SRT cohort.ResultsOverall, 1864 patients who underwent SRT, 235 652 with percutaneous coronary intervention, and 69 236 with coronary artery bypass grafting were included. In the SRT cohort, 368 patients (19.7%) underwent procedures at COE. A greater proportion of patients who traveled long distances for care (≥50 miles) underwent SRT at COE (35.7%) or high‐volume facilities (43.8%) than those who traveled short distances (0–<10 miles; 13.3% and 28.1%, respectively). Mean distance to care was longer for the SRT cohort than the percutaneous coronary intervention/coronary artery bypass grafting cohorts. Among US regions, the proportion of patients who underwent SRT at standard centers versus COE was highest in the South (87.5% versus 12.5%).ConclusionsPatients traveled long distances for SRT at COE/high‐volume centers. Most patients received SRT at standard centers rather than at COE.Keywords: access to health care, alcohol septal ablation, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, septal myectomy, septal reduction therapySubject Categories: Cardiomyopathy, Disparities

What Are the Clinical Implications?

There are geographical disparities in accessing septal reduction therapy performed at a HCM center of excellence or high procedural volume center among patients with obstructive HCM compared with access to percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting procedures among patients with coronary artery disease.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a chronic myocardial disease defined by left ventricular hypertrophy that cannot be explained by another condition. 1 Approximately 70% of patients with HCM have the obstructive subtype of the disease, which is caused by septal hypertrophy that narrows the left ventricular outflow tract or anatomic alterations of the mitral valve and apparatus that lead to systolic anterior motion. 2 , 3

Historical guidelines from the American Heart Association (AHA)/American College of Cardiology (ACC; 2011) and the European Society of Cardiology (2014) recommended use of beta blockers as a first‐line therapy for management of symptomatic obstructive HCM and use of nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers in patients who are intolerant to or have contraindications to beta blockers. 4 , 5 The most recent guidelines from the AHA/ACC (2024) and European Society of Cardiology (2023) recommend the use of cardiac myosin inhibitors in adult patients who have persistent symptoms attributable to left ventricular outflow tract obstruction despite therapy with beta blockers or calcium channel blockers. Alternatively, disopyramide treatment (in combination with an atrioventricular nodal blocking agent) or septal reduction therapy (SRT) performed at experienced centers is recommended. 1 , 6

Recommendation of SRT by septal myectomy (SM) or alcohol septal ablation (ASA) relies on stringent selection criteria being met, including for patients to be severely symptomatic (New York Heart Association classes III–IV) and unresponsive to maximally tolerated pharmacological therapy and for the procedure to be performed by a multidisciplinary team with demonstrated excellence in clinical outcomes for SRT, in facilities that undertake a high volume of SRT. This forms the basis of the definition of a “comprehensive” HCM center provided by the guidelines. 1 , 6 , 7 In this context, SM and ASA are effective procedures to relieve left ventricular outflow tract obstruction and reduce symptoms, with low associated mortality. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 However, SRT outcomes vary widely based on procedure volume and clinician experience at treatment centers; previous studies that stratified US hospitals based on SRT volume determined that procedures performed at low procedural volume centers were associated with statistically significantly higher morbidity and mortality than procedures performed at high procedural volume centers. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 A minority of US health care facilities meet the AHA/ACC criteria for a high procedural volume comprehensive HCM center, which may necessitate patients having to travel a long distance to be adequately treated. 3 , 17 Therefore, patients who require SRT may face challenges to access high‐quality care if they live in a geographic region with limited nearby facilities. Furthermore, there is a substantial knowledge gap regarding whether there is equitable access to SRT among patients with obstructive HCM because SRT procedures are performed relatively infrequently. 18

The purpose of this study was to examine the challenges associated with access to SRT among individuals with obstructive HCM. To achieve this, the demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of a cohort of patients with diagnosed obstructive HCM and treated with SRT (SM and/or ASA) were assessed, and the geographical distance between the patient's location and the center where they underwent the procedure was analyzed. The findings were put into context by comparing the results from the SRT cohort with reference cohorts of patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).

METHODS

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the study sponsor upon reasonable request. The study sponsor's policy on data sharing may be found at https://www.bms.com/researchers‐and‐partners/independent‐research/data‐sharing‐request‐process.html.

Study Design and Patients

This was a retrospective cross‐sectional study of individuals in the United States with full commercial health insurance or Medicare Advantage with Part D (MAPD) health plan membership whose data were captured within the Optum Research Database. Data extracted from the Optum Research Database included administrative claims data and enrollment information. Additional data, including socioeconomic characteristics and social determinants of health (SDoH) data that are not available in typical claims databases, were linked from additional Optum data sources using unique patient identification numbers. Patients with missing socioeconomic characteristics or SDoH data remained in the study population. The study period ranged from May 1, 2012 to April 30, 2022. Therefore, treatment management during the study was performed in accordance with the 2011 and 2020 versions of the AHA/ACC guidelines. 3 , 5

The study was conducted in accordance with the International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology Guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices and applicable regulatory requirements. This study was reviewed by an institutional review board and granted an institutional review board exemption under 45 CFR § 46.104(d)(4). Owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of deidentified data, informed consent from patients was not required.

The study population comprised patients with obstructive HCM who underwent SRT during the study period. The index date was assigned to the first date of an inpatient visit with a procedure code for SRT. To be included in the final SRT cohort, patients met all of the following criteria: aged 18 years or older at the index date; at least 1 medical claim with an International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐9‐CM) or International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) diagnosis code for obstructive HCM, other HCM, or unspecified cardiomyopathy during the study period; at least 1 medical claim with a procedure code for SRT (including SM and ASA) during the study period; no medical claims with a diagnosis code for Fabry disease or amyloidosis during the study period; and no missing age, sex, insurance type, or geographical region data. To contextualize the findings derived from the SRT cohort, the characteristics of a comparator population of patients with CAD who underwent PCI or CABG during the same study period as the SRT cohort were additionally described and compared with the SRT cohort. The index date was assigned to a random date with an inpatient or outpatient visit with a procedure code for PCI or a random date with an inpatient visit with a procedure code for CABG, respectively. To be included in the final PCI or CABG cohorts, patients met all of the following criteria: aged 18 years or older at the index date; at least 1 medical claim with an ICD‐9‐CM or ICD‐10‐CM diagnosis code for CAD in any position during the study period; at least 1 medical claim with a procedure code for PCI or CABG in any position during the study period; and no missing age, sex, insurance type, or geographical region data. Patients with CAD who underwent both PCI and CABG during the study period could be included in both PCI and CABG study cohorts.

Study Assessments

The patient demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of the SRT, PCI, and CABG cohorts were assessed and compared across groups. The study measured the SDoH domains of financial security and transportation access using indices based on predictive models. The models used a combination of individual‐level consumer data and publicly available data by geography to predict the likelihood of an individual having unmet needs in each of the SDoH domains. Optum uses proprietary steps to review and validate a model for each SDoH domain. Model development included least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression to reduce dimensionality, an Extreme Gradient Boosting model to select for the most important features, and a correlation analysis to identify multicollinearity. 19 Each model was validated on unseen data and the area under the curve was reviewed for sensitivity and specificity levels. The SDoH indices are scaled from 0 to 9, with 0 indicating the highest level of unmet need—or patients having more insecurity—and 9 indicating the lowest risk of insecurity. In this study, patients were further categorized into tertiles of high unmet need (0–3), moderate level of unmet need (4–6), and no unmet need (≥7). The transportation access measure was developed and is presented for patients aged at least 65 years only.

The addresses of treatment centers where patients underwent SRT procedures were captured to allow for the quantification of the number of patients who were treated at an “HCM center of excellence” or a high procedural volume center. A HCM center of excellence is a designation granted by the Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy Association (HCMA) to facilities that provide “high‐quality care to patients with HCM in accordance with guidelines” and are “dedicated to treatment and research in the area of HCM.” 20 A high procedural volume center was defined as a facility with a high throughput of SRT procedures. The HCMA considers volume of care as a criterion for assessing if a center is one of excellence, but not all HCM centers of excellence necessarily have a high procedural volume and vice versa.

To determine the SRT procedure volume of the treatment center in which each patient received treatment, the count of SRT procedure claims per unique center in the SRT cohort was captured, and thresholds for low, medium, and high procedural volume levels were established based on tertiles of the data (procedures during the period: low, 1–2; medium, 3–7; high, 8–37). Patient and treatment center addresses for the SRT, PCI, and CABG cohorts were geocoded to determine the distance to care, as measured by the driving distance in miles from the patient's address to the treatment provider. Geospatial coding was performed on random samples of 20 000 patients in the PCI and CABG cohorts owing to the large sample sizes. Bivariate comparisons of patients with and without geospatial analyses were used to confirm the representativeness of the random samples.

The geographical distribution of patient access to HCM centers of excellence was determined by capturing the US state in which each patient was enrolled in a health plan. US states were categorized into 4 geographical regions in accordance with the designations defined by the US Census Bureau. 21

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analyses of each study cohort were performed separately. Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Bivariate comparisons between study populations were performed with a Z‐test using robust SEs in an ordinary least squares regression for continuous variables, and with a Rao–Scott chi‐square test for categorical variables.

RESULTS

Patient Selection

In total, 447 576 patients in the Optum Research Database had at least 1 medical claim with a diagnosis code for obstructive HCM, other HCM, or unspecified cardiomyopathy during the study period. Of these patients, 2736 (0.6%) had at least 1 medical claim with a procedure code for SRT, and, after applying the remaining criteria, 1864 were included in the SRT (SM and ASA) study cohort (Figure 1). In the SRT cohort, 884 patients (47.4%) had a claim for SM, 976 patients (52.4%) had a claim for ASA, and 4 patients (0.2%) had claims for SM and ASA. Of the 3 591 562 patients in the Optum Research Database with at least 1 medical claim with a diagnosis code for CAD during the study period, 235 652 met the criteria to be included in the PCI cohort and 69 236 met the criteria to be included in the CABG cohort.

Patient and Treatment Center Characteristics

Patient characteristics of the SRT, PCI, and CABG cohorts are presented in Table 1. The mean±SD age of the SRT cohort was 67.2 (11.1) years and the mean±SD ages of the PCI and CABG cohorts were 68.5 (10.6) and 68.6 (9.0) years, respectively. The SRT cohort included a statistically significantly greater proportion of female individuals than the PCI and CABG cohorts (46.5% versus 34.4% and 27.2%, respectively; P<0.001 for both comparisons). Distance to Care

Among the SRT cohort with available distance to care data (n=1822), 32.9% of patients (n=600) traveled 0 to <10 miles, 40.8% (n=743) traveled 10 to <50 miles, and 26.3% (n=479) traveled at least 50 miles for care (Table 3 and Figure 2A). The proportion of patients who underwent SRT while living in rural areas versus those who lived in urban areas increased with greater distance to care (0–<10 miles, 0% versus 100%; 10–<50 miles, 3.1% versus 96.9%; ≥50 miles, 13.2% versus 86.8%) (Table 3). Patients who traveled at least 50 miles were treated most commonly at a high procedural volume center (43.8%), whereas patients who traveled <10 miles were most commonly treated at a medium procedural volume center (37.3%). Of the individuals in the SRT cohort who underwent an SRT procedure at a HCMA‐defined HCM center of excellence and who had available distance to care data (n=363), 22.0% (n=80) traveled 0 to <10 miles, 30.9% (n=112) traveled 10 to <50 miles, and 47.1% (n=171) traveled at least 50 miles for care (Table 3). Among the SRT cohort with available distance to care data, a higher proportion of patients who traveled at least 50 miles to reach their health care facility had their procedure performed at a center of excellence (35.7%) than patients who traveled <10 miles (13.3%; P<0.001).

Geographical Access to Care

Analysis of the number and proportion of SRT procedures performed by geographical region and by HCMA‐defined HCM center of excellence designation and treatment center procedural volume is presented in Table 5. Patients underwent SRT most commonly in the South (43.2%), followed by the Midwest (27.4%), the Northeast (20.3%), and the West (9.1%). Across all regions, more patients had their SRT procedure performed at a standard center rather than at a HCM center of excellence. Among patients treated in the South, 12.5% received SRT at a HCM center of excellence compared with 25.3% of patients treated in the Midwest, 20.6% of patients treated in the Northeast, and 35.3% of those treated in the West. Among patients treated in the Midwest, 41.0% received SRT at a high procedural volume center compared with 36.4% of those treated in the Northeast, 28.9% of patients treated in the South, and 27.1% of patients treated in the West.

DISCUSSION

When performed by experienced, multidisciplinary teams at comprehensive treatment centers, SRTs are guideline‐recommended procedures to reduce left ventricular outflow tract obstruction in severely symptomatic patients with obstructive HCM who are refractory to maximally tolerated pharmacological therapy. 1 , 3 , 5 , 6 A cross‐sectional database study of US hospital data previously demonstrated that hospitalization length of stay, 30‐day readmission rates, and costs associated with SRT were statistically significantly lower in treatment centers with high procedural volume than in those with low procedural volume. 22 However, the level of access that a broad population of SRT‐eligible patients with obstructive HCM have to SRT in experienced, high procedural volume centers in the US health care system has not previously been examined. Using data from the Optum Research Database, this is the first analysis to inform understanding of SRT access in the United States by providing a detailed assessment of demographics, socioeconomic characteristics, distance to care, and treatment center types among patients who have undergone SRT. The findings of this study demonstrated that there are potential challenges related to the accessibility of SRT procedures among US patients with obstructive HCM, as indicated by a longer mean distance to care than for patients undergoing PCI and CABG, and the majority of patients receiving treatment at centers that are not HCMA‐defined centers of excellence.

To assess the challenges associated with access to SRT, a cohort of patients with obstructive HCM who underwent SRT was identified and compared with reference cohorts of patients with CAD who underwent PCI or CABG. These comparator groups were chosen based on the relatively high prevalence of CAD in the US population and revascularization procedures being widely accessible to traditionally underserved populations in the United States. 23 SRT is a more specialized, low‐volume procedure compared with PCI and CABG, which are among the most commonly performed cardiac procedures. Compared with the PCI and CABG cohorts, there was a similar proportion of patients in the SRT cohort who were fully commercially insured and a similar distribution of household incomes, possibly indicative of the 3 populations having a similar composition of socioeconomic backgrounds. Notable differences in characteristics that were observed between the SRT cohort and the PCI and CABG cohorts included the SRT cohort comprising a higher proportion of female and non‐Hispanic White individuals than the other cohorts. However, it should be noted that owing to differences in procedure frequency, the SRT cohort was substantially smaller than the PCI and CABG cohorts. Therefore, statistically significant differences in characteristics between cohorts should be viewed in the context of the respective population sample sizes.

Overall, approximately four fifths (80.3%) of patients did not have their procedure performed at a HCM center of excellence. Although the center of excellence designation from the HCMA and the comprehensive HCM center definition from the European Society of Cardiology and AHA/ACC guidelines are different, facilities must provide high‐quality care in accordance with the guidelines to obtain center of excellence designation. 3 , 6 A statistically significantly greater proportion of patients who traveled a long distance for care (≥50 miles) were treated at a high procedural volume treatment center or HCMA‐defined HCM center of excellence than patients who traveled a short distance (0–<10 miles), thus suggesting that treatment in compliance with guideline recommendations resulted in long travel distances for a substantial number of patients. Furthermore, patients who underwent SRT traveled a greater mean distance to care than those who underwent PCI or CABG. Interestingly, the median distance to care for the SRT cohort was substantially reduced compared with the mean distance to care. This finding indicated that the SRT cohort included outlier patients who had to travel a notably long distance to access care. One possible reason for this observation could be that, per guideline recommendations, the outlier patients were referred to a distant comprehensive HCM center. 17

Geospatial analysis indicated that among the patients included in the SRT cohort, SRT procedures were performed most commonly in the South and least commonly in the West. However, patients in the West and Midwest were more likely to receive SRT in a HCMA‐defined HCM center of excellence than patients in the South. The findings of this analysis serve to demonstrate that there is a disparity in access to HCM centers of excellence or high procedural volume centers across the United States and identifies clear areas of unmet need. Disparities in procedure‐related complications and mortality following SRT when stratifying by race, ethnicity, or US geographical region have also been identified previously. 24 , 25 Therefore, in the absence of a nearby HCM center of excellence or high procedural volume treatment center, SRT‐eligible patients with obstructive HCM may benefit from alternative treatment approaches to SRT. In the phase 3, multicenter, randomized, double‐blind VALOR‐HCM (A Study to Evaluate Mavacamten in Adults With Symptomatic Obstructive HCM Who Are Eligible for Septal Reduction Therapy) study (NCT04349072), the efficacy and safety of the cardiac myosin inhibitor mavacamten was assessed in patients with obstructive HCM and New York Heart Association class III to IV symptoms who were referred for SRT. Mavacamten was shown to statistically significantly reduce the proportion of patients who were eligible for SRT or who decided to proceed with SRT after 16 weeks of treatment. 26 At week 128, most patients in both the original mavacamten group and those who crossed over from placebo to mavacamten treatment at week 16 were no longer eligible for SRT, 27 suggesting that long‐term mavacamten treatment may mitigate the need for SRT.

A notable strength of this study was the use of data from the Optum Research Database, which provided a robust and valuable resource in which to describe and characterize a large cohort of patients who underwent SRT over a 10‐year period. Geospatial mapping of patient distance to care additionally provided a unique insight into the discrepancies in the level of access to SRT by geographical region in the United States. However, the findings of this study should also be discussed in the context of its limitations. The Optum Research Database is a medical claims database; limitations associated with such a source include the possibility of erroneously inputted diagnosis and procedure codes. Similarly, socioeconomic information captured from individual‐ and population‐level data sources is associated with limitations such as the potential for inaccuracies in the assignment of socioeconomic categorizations. The SDoH findings were derived from models that were reviewed and validated using undisclosed proprietary steps, thus limiting the reproducibility of the findings. Transportation access data were available only for patients aged at least 65 years. Furthermore, between 39% and 62% of data were missing for SDoH domains; because it is difficult to predict the influence that this missingness could have on the results, financial security and transportation access results should be interpreted with caution. The study period analyzed encompassed the period during which medical coding transitioned fm using ICD‐9‐CM to ICD‐10‐CM (October 2015). Given the increased granularity and improved specificity of ICD‐10‐CM codes compared with ICD‐9‐CM codes, it is anticipated that instances of misclassification, if any, would be more frequent in patients whose index date occurred before October 2015 than in those identified after the transition date. Because continuous enrollment was not required for inclusion in the study, it is possible that some patients with Fabry disease or amyloidosis were included in the final SRT population. Nevertheless, the proportion of patients with either condition included in the study was anticipated to be low. The Optum Research Database contains data from a minority of patients who are enrolled in commercial health insurance or have MAPD health plan membership in the United States; therefore, not all SRT procedures performed in the study period among these insured populations were captured. Furthermore, because only patients with full commercial health insurance or a MAPD health plan were eligible for inclusion, it is possible that the findings of this study are not applicable to uninsured patients or to those with Medicaid insurance. The total number of SRT procedures identified in the Optum Research Database for the defined population was relatively low; as such, the procedural volume tertile cutoffs used in this analysis were based on study procedures performed over the 10‐year study period, as opposed to procedures performed per year. Identifying SRT procedure volume in a claims database is also challenging because facilities may have multiple providers and provider identifiers. Therefore, it is possible that the reported procedure volumes were underestimated. Finally, HCM centers of excellence were defined based on HCMA guidelines from 2021. Therefore, it is possible that centers that were considered to be a center of excellence in 2021 were not considered to be centers of excellence earlier in the study and vice versa.

CONCLUSIONS

To conclude, the results of this study suggest that US patients with obstructive HCM who undergo SRT may be required to travel a long distance for care at a HCMA‐defined HCM center of excellence or high procedural volume treatment center. Furthermore, access to care at HCM centers of excellence varies by geographical region.
