Neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio for preoperative risk stratification of complicated appendicitis in children: a retrospective study
Authors: Ming Liu, Zaihui Tang, Yunpeng Gou, Ping Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePreoperative risk assessment of complicated appendicitis in children holds substantial clinical importance. This study aimed to evaluate the association between the neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR) and the occurrence of complicated appendicitis in pediatric patients.MethodsA total of 814 pediatric patients who underwent laparoscopic appendectomy in a single-centre retrospective cohort design were enrolled in this study. The primary outcome was defined as the occurrence of complicated appendicitis. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis, complemented by stratified and threshold effect analyses. The regression model was adjusted for potential confounders including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), duration of symptoms, vomiting, C-reactive protein (CRP), and appendiceal fecalith. The discriminative performance of NPAR and other inflammatory markers for complicated appendicitis was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The ROC analyses were conducted as univariable analyses for each biomarker.ResultsAfter adjusting for potential confounders, NPAR demonstrated a non-linear association with the risk of complicated appendicitis, with an identified threshold value of 19.53. For NPAR values below this threshold, each unit increase was significantly associated with an elevated risk of complicated appendicitis (OR = 1.46, 95%CI: 1.27–1.68, P < 0.001), whereas above the threshold, no significant association was observed (P > 0.05). The ROC curve analysis revealed that NPAR exhibited good univariable discrimination in identifying complicated appendicitis (AUC = 0.811, 95%CI: 0.777–0.844).ConclusionNPAR shows promise as a readily accessible biomarker derived from routine blood tests for risk stratification of complicated appendicitis in pediatric patients. The identified non-linear association pattern provides valuable insights for clinical risk assessment. Internal validation confirmed the stability of our model, and these findings warrant further validation in prospective multicenter studies before broad clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04390-0.

Introduction

Acute appendicitis persists as one of the most prevalent surgical emergencies in the pediatric population [1], with approximately 30% of cases progressing to complicated appendicitis, manifested by perforation, abscess formation, or peritonitis [2, 3]. Preoperative risk assessment of complicated appendicitis is essential for minimizing hospital stay duration, reducing surgical site infection risk, and decreasing readmission rates [4]. However, accurate preoperative risk stratification remains challenging in pediatric patients, attributed to their limited ability to articulate symptoms and the rapid progression of the condition, despite the availability of advanced imaging modalities such as ultrasound and computed tomography [1]. These challenges underscore the critical need for readily accessible biomarkers that can be derived from routine laboratory tests to facilitate preoperative risk stratification.

Previous studies [5–7] have established the prognostic value of inflammatory markers, including white cell count (WBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), in identifying complicated appendicitis in pediatric patients, highlighting the fundamental role of inflammatory response in disease progression. The neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR), an emerging inflammatory marker, incorporates key parameters that reflect both systemic inflammation and nutritional-immune status [8], potentially providing superior inflammatory assessment compared to conventional markers [9]. Mounting evidence has demonstrated the clinical utility of NPAR in the risk assessment and prognostication of various pathological conditions, including atrial fibrillation, sepsis, and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis [10–12]. However, its discriminative performance in pediatric complicated appendicitis remains to be elucidated.

This study aims to evaluate the discriminative performance of NPAR for risk stratification of complicated appendicitis in pediatric patients undergoing laparoscopic appendectomy. The findings are expected to provide clinicians with a simple and readily accessible risk stratification tool derived from routine admission blood tests, potentially optimizing preoperative risk assessment, reducing reliance on advanced imaging modalities, and facilitating targeted intervention strategies for high-risk patients.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This retrospective cohort study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for cohort studies. The study population consisted of pediatric patients who underwent laparoscopic appendectomy at the Department of Pediatric Surgery between January 2021 and December 2022. This retrospective analysis was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Suining Central Hospital (approval number: KYLLKS20240079, approval date: June 21, 2024). Written surgical informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all patients.

The inclusion criteria encompassed patients aged under 18 years with pathologically confirmed acute appendicitis and complete clinical documentation. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) prior antibiotic administration before hospital presentation; (2) periappendiceal abscess with prolonged symptomatology requiring initial conservative management (antibiotics or percutaneous drainage) followed by interval appendectomy; (3) concurrent systemic disorders or severe medical conditions; (4) histopathologically normal appendix; (5) presence of concurrent specific infections; and (6) alternative intraoperative diagnoses (including gynecological disorders, Meckel’s diverticulum, intestinal volvulus, or other acute abdominal pathologies).

Rationale for excluding conservatively managed periappendiceal abscess: Patients with periappendiceal abscess requiring initial conservative management typically present with prolonged symptom duration (> 5–7 days after onset), resulting in substantially altered inflammatory dynamics and pathological states compared with those undergoing immediate surgery. Moreover, antibiotics and/or percutaneous drainage may modify admission biomarker levels, reducing comparability. To ensure homogeneity in disease acuity and allow consistent evaluation of admission biomarkers in the context of immediate surgical decision-making, we excluded patients managed with interval appendectomy. This approach helps maintain temporal consistency between biomarker measurement and surgical intervention, which is important for the intended clinical application of preoperative severity triage in this specific context.

Acute appendicitis is conventionally classified into uncomplicated and complicated forms. Uncomplicated appendicitis is characterized by localized inflammatory changes without evidence of necrosis or perforation, whereas complicated appendicitis manifests with gangrenous changes or perforation, potentially accompanied by abscess formation, phlegmon, or peritoneal fluid collection [1, 13, 14]. In this study, cases of congestive and suppurative appendicitis were categorized as uncomplicated, while those exhibiting gangrenous changes or perforation were designated as complicated appendicitis. To ensure terminological clarity and avoid the confusion sometimes seen in literature where “complicated appendicitis” and “perforated appendicitis” are used interchangeably, we use “complicated appendicitis” for the composite outcome and reserve “perforation” for contexts specifically describing the perforation subset or related analyses.

Variables

The primary outcome measure was the presence of complicated appendicitis. The primary exposure variable was the NPAR, calculated from initial blood samples obtained upon hospital admission. The NPAR was computed using the following formula [10, 15, 16]: NPAR = Neutrophil percentage (%) × 100/Albumin (g/dL), where neutrophil percentage denotes the proportion of neutrophils within the total white blood cell population. This formulation, including the × 100 factor, was adopted for consistency with prior studies and to place the index on a more interpretable scale; it represents a linear rescaling that does not affect statistical associations or predictive performance, only the numeric expression of thresholds. Alternative formulations without the multiplication by 100 have been reported [17]. Albumin was uniformly expressed in g/dL.

Other variables encompassed demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, laboratory parameters, and surgical findings. Demographic data included age, gender, and body mass index (BMI). Clinical variables comprised duration of symptoms prior to admission, presence of gastrointestinal manifestations (vomiting and diarrhea), signs of peritoneal irritation (tenderness, rebound tenderness, and muscle guarding), and admission body temperature. Laboratory parameters, derived from admission blood specimens, included white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, lymphocyte percentage, CRP, NLR, and serum albumin. Surgical variables incorporated operative duration, presence of appendiceal fecalith (documented through preoperative imaging, intraoperative observation, or histopathological examination), and surgical drain placement. Drain insertion was implemented based on intraoperative findings, including gangrenous changes, perforation, or significant peritoneal contamination, with subsequent removal upon achieving minimal, serosanguineous drainage.

Statistical analysis

Given the retrospective study design and the inclusion criterion mandating “complete clinical documentation”, a complete-case analysis was performed. This criterion was applied during patient selection to ensure that all variables used in the statistical models (including NPAR, demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters) were 100% complete in the final analytic cohort. Therefore, no data imputation was necessary. To ensure the robustness of our findings and address potential concerns regarding influential observations, we performed systematic influence diagnostics using Cook’s distance. Observations with Cook’s distance values exceeding the threshold of 4/(n-k-1), where n is the sample size and k is the number of predictors, were identified as potentially influential. Sensitivity analyses were subsequently conducted by comparing model estimates with and without these influential observations to evaluate the stability of the primary results.

As this was a retrospective study, sample size was determined by available data rather than prospective calculation. Power analysis indicated adequate power (> 95%) to detect clinically meaningful associations (OR ≥ 1.3) in the primary analysis with α = 0.05. Power for subgroup analyses varied by sample size and is reported with corresponding results.

Study participants were stratified into two groups based on appendicitis type: uncomplicated and complicated appendicitis. Due to non-normal distribution, continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage) and compared using the chi-square test.

The association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression analysis. The strength of associations was quantified by odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Potential confounding variables were selected based on clinical relevance and previous literature [5, 18–21]. Three sequential models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2 (adjusted for age and gender); and Model 3 (fully adjusted for age, gender, BMI, duration of symptoms, vomiting, CRP, and appendiceal fecalith).

To examine the robustness of associations between NPAR and complicated appendicitis, subgroup analyses were conducted across gender, appendiceal fecalith, and vomiting categories using the fully adjusted model (Model 3). The heterogeneity of these associations across subgroups was evaluated through interaction tests, with statistical significance determined by interaction P-values. The potential non-linear association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis risk was visualized using smoothed curve fitting with full covariate adjustment. Piecewise linear logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed to identify threshold effects in this association. All statistical analyses were conducted using the “mgcv” package for spline and segmented analyses. The breakpoint was identified using a data-driven approach that maximizes the log-likelihood function across candidate threshold values ranging from the 5th to 95th percentiles of NPAR distribution. No thresholds were pre-registered prior to conducting the analyses. A two-segment piecewise linear model was fitted, with different slopes before and after the breakpoint (K), using the formula: logit(P) = β₀ + β₁×NPAR + β₂×(NPAR-K)⁺, where (NPAR-K)⁺ = max(NPAR-K, 0). The optimal breakpoint was determined by comparing log-likelihood values across all candidate points. Model comparison between the piecewise linear model and a simple linear model was performed using likelihood ratio tests. A two-sided alpha level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. The 95% confidence interval for the breakpoint was estimated using 1000 bootstrap resamples, with the optimal breakpoint re-estimated in each bootstrap sample using the same log-likelihood maximization procedure.

We evaluated the discriminative performance of multiple inflammatory markers for complicated appendicitis using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The biomarkers assessed included NPAR, CRP, absolute neutrophil count (ANC), NLR, and WBC. The discriminative ability of each marker was quantified by the area under the curve (AUC). Pairwise comparisons of correlated AUCs were conducted using DeLong’s test. We determined optimal cutoff values using Youden’s index and calculated their corresponding sensitivity and specificity.

Our statistical approach distinguished between confirmatory and exploratory analyses. For the five primary biomarker AUC comparisons (NPAR, CRP, ANC, NLR, WBC), we applied Bonferroni correction to control the family-wise error rate, with the significance threshold adjusted to α = 0.01. Subgroup analyses (by gender, age groups, appendiceal fecalith, and vomiting status) were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating; as such, they were not subjected to formal multiplicity correction, and their results should be interpreted accordingly. The pairwise AUC comparisons using DeLong’s test were pre-specified to evaluate specific hypotheses of clinical interest (e.g., NPAR + CRP vs. CRP alone) rather than all possible pairwise combinations.

To address potential overfitting and assess the stability of our findings, we performed comprehensive internal validation using bootstrap resampling with 1000 replicates. This validation included: (1) stability assessment of the ROC-derived optimal cutoff through bootstrap mean and 95% confidence intervals; (2) optimism correction for model performance by training models on bootstrap samples and validating on the original dataset, with corrected AUC calculated as apparent AUC minus mean optimism; and (3) evaluation of threshold effect breakpoint stability using bootstrap median and 95% confidence interval, building upon the previously described bootstrap procedure for breakpoint confidence interval estimation.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.0) and Empower Stats (http://www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions, Inc, CA, USA). Statistical significance was determined by a two-sided p-value less than 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study population

Based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 815 patients were initially enrolled. One patient was excluded due to an extreme NPAR value > 100 resulting from an implausibly low albumin level (0.87 g/dL), suggesting potential data entry error. Thus, 814 patients were included in the final analysis. As shown in Table 1, of the 814 patients, 158 (19.41%) were diagnosed with complicated appendicitis and 656 (80.59%) with uncomplicated appendicitis. Patients with complicated appendicitis demonstrated significantly higher NPAR values (median: 18.85 vs. 15.91, P < 0.001), were more frequently male (70.89% vs. 62.50%, P = 0.048), and younger (median: 7.00 vs. 8.00 years, P < 0.001). Notably, patients with complicated appendicitis had similar median symptom duration (24.00 h in both groups) but different interquartile ranges, with complicated cases showing longer symptom duration distribution (interquartile range: 24.00–48.00 h vs. 10.00–24.00 h, P < 0.001). These patients also showed higher prevalence of vomiting (66.46% vs. 46.49%, P < 0.001) and peritoneal irritation (91.14% vs. 61.13%, P < 0.001). Laboratory findings revealed markedly elevated inflammatory markers in complicated cases, including WBC count, neutrophil ratio, NLR, and CRP levels (all P < 0.001), while albumin levels were significantly lower (4.48 vs. 4.66 g/dL, P < 0.001). Surgical characteristics showed that complicated appendicitis was associated with longer operative time (60.00 vs. 40.00 min), higher rates of appendiceal fecalith (74.05% vs. 17.99%), and more frequent drainage tube placement (84.18% vs. 13.57%) (all P < 0.001).

Association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis

As presented in Table 2, NPAR exhibited a robust positive association with complicated appendicitis across all regression models. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each unit increase in NPAR was associated with a 27% increased risk of complicated appendicitis (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.15–1.39, P < 0.001). In tertile analysis, using the lower tertile (T1) as reference, both middle tertile (T2: OR = 3.87, 95% CI: 1.58–9.44, P = 0.003) and upper tertile (T3: OR = 8.59, 95% CI: 3.61–20.46, P < 0.001) demonstrated progressively increased risk of complicated appendicitis. The trend across NPAR tertiles was statistically significant (P for trend < 0.001). Additionally, we performed a sensitivity analysis that included the patient with NPAR > 100. The results of this analysis, presented in Supplementary Table 1, revealed that even with the inclusion of this extreme value, NPAR maintained a positive association with complicated appendicitis across all models, with significant associations observed in Model 1 and Model 2, while the association in Model 3 remained positive and approached significance.

Subgroup analyses

Stratified analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of the association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis across key subgroups (Table 3). Age-stratified analysis revealed varying associations across pediatric populations: the association was not significant in children < 5 years (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.88–1.24, P = 0.607), but remained significant in the 5–12 years group (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.20–1.57, P < 0.001) and the 12–18 years group (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.04–1.66, P = 0.023). The positive association also persisted across gender, appendiceal fecalith status, and presence of vomiting (all P < 0.05). The interaction effect approached statistical significance for age groups (P for interaction = 0.051), while no significant effect modification was observed between NPAR and these clinical characteristics (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Non-linear analysis

As illustrated in Fig. 1, fully adjusted smooth curve fitting revealed a non-linear association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis. Piecewise linear logistic regression identified a breakpoint at NPAR = 19.53 (95% CI: 13.16–20.27; Table 4), with the threshold effect clearly demonstrated in the logit(p) versus NPAR plot (Supplementary Figure S1). The model showed good calibration across NPAR quintiles, with predicted probabilities closely matching observed proportions in each stratum (Supplementary Figure S2). Below the breakpoint (NPAR < 19.53), each unit increase in NPAR was associated with a 46% higher risk of complicated appendicitis (OR: 1.46, 95% CI: 1.27–1.68; P < 0.001). However, above the breakpoint (NPAR > 19.53), the association was no longer statistically significant (OR: 0.83, 95% CI: 0.64–1.08; P = 0.161). The threshold effect was statistically significant as demonstrated by the log-likelihood ratio test comparing the piecewise linear model with a simple linear model (P = 0.001).

ROC curve analysis

The ROC curve analysis for classifying complicated appendicitis revealed that NPAR demonstrated good discriminative performance with an AUC of 0.811 (95% CI: 0.777–0.844), ranking second among the assessed inflammatory markers (Fig. 2; Table 5). At its optimal cutoff value of 16.78, NPAR exhibited the highest sensitivity (0.886) among all markers, albeit with moderate specificity (0.608). At the optimal cutoff value of 16.78, the positive predictive value (PPV) for NPAR was 35.26%, and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 95.51%. In comparison, CRP showed the highest overall classification accuracy (AUC 0.847, 95% CI: 0.809–0.884) with balanced specificity (0.814) and sensitivity (0.791) at a cutoff of 15.86 mg/L. A formal comparison indicated no statistically significant difference between the AUCs of NPAR and CRP (DeLong P = 0.105). The remaining inflammatory markers demonstrated lower discriminative performance: ANC (AUC 0.766, 95% CI: 0.727–0.806), NLR (AUC 0.751, 95% CI: 0.714–0.788), and WBC (AUC 0.743, 95% CI: 0.699–0.787). A sensitivity analysis applying Bonferroni correction for the five primary biomarker comparisons (significance threshold adjusted to α = 0.01) confirmed that all markers remained statistically significant (all adjusted P < 0.001), indicating robust discriminative performance not attributable to multiple testing. In addition, a combined NPAR + CRP model further improved discrimination (AUC 0.885, 95% CI: 0.857–0.912), significantly outperforming CRP alone (DeLong P = 0.003) and yielding a more balanced operating profile (sensitivity 0.823, specificity 0.806) at the data-driven threshold.

Systematic influence diagnostics and sensitivity analysis

Systematic influence diagnostics identified 63 observations (7.74%) with Cook’s distance exceeding 4/(n-k-1). Sensitivity analysis excluding these observations (n = 751) showed strengthened associations: fully adjusted OR increased from 1.27 (95% CI: 1.15–1.39) to 2.11 (95% CI: 1.54–2.90) and AUC improved from 0.811 to 0.879 (Supplementary Tables 2–4). Despite these quantitative differences, statistical significance (P < 0.001) and overall patterns remained consistent. These 63 observations were retained in the primary analysis as they represented clinically valid data. The strengthened associations in sensitivity analysis indicate that our primary results provide conservative estimates of NPAR’s predictive utility.

Bootstrap internal validation results

Internal validation using bootstrap resampling (1000 replicates) demonstrated excellent stability for the ROC-derived optimal NPAR cutoff (bootstrap mean: 17.34, 95% CI: 16.78–18.01). The AUC for NPAR alone was 0.811 (bootstrap mean: 0.811, 95% CI: 0.778–0.844), while the optimism-corrected AUC for the combined NPAR + CRP model was 0.884 (95% CI: 0.860–0.914). The threshold effect breakpoint showed greater variability (bootstrap median: 18.36, IQR: 16.00–19.50, 95% CI: 13.08–20.38), though the original value of 19.53 fell within the confidence interval. Optimism correction revealed minimal overfitting in both models (optimism: 0.0004 and 0.0003, respectively).

Discussion

In this large-scale study encompassing 814 consecutive pediatric patients, we investigated the association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis. After rigorous adjustment for potential confounders, NPAR exhibited a significant association with the risk of complicated appendicitis (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.15–1.39). Of particular interest, we observed a threshold effect at NPAR values below 19.53, where each unit increment corresponded to a 46% elevated risk of complicated appendicitis (OR: 1.46, 95% CI: 1.27–1.68). Additionally, NPAR demonstrated good univariable discrimination (AUC = 0.811, 95% CI: 0.777–0.844), suggesting its potential as a valuable biomarker for risk stratification of complicated appendicitis in clinical settings.

The marked association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis likely reflects dual pathophysiological mechanisms. First, neutrophils serve as key effector cells in the innate immune system, orchestrating responses to bacterial infection and tissue necrosis [22]. In complicated appendicitis, localized inflammation initiates neutrophil recruitment and activation, culminating in elevated peripheral neutrophil percentages [23, 24]. Second, inflammatory mediators modulate albumin homeostasis through multiple pathways [25]: inhibiting hepatic albumin synthesis, enhancing vascular permeability that facilitates albumin extravasation, and accelerating albumin catabolism in extravascular compartments. These alterations in albumin metabolism appear to contribute significantly to acute appendicitis pathophysiology [26]. Thus, NPAR, by incorporating these two distinct yet mechanistically linked parameters, enables a more comprehensive evaluation of disease severity.

Our findings demonstrate that NPAR, a novel inflammatory marker, exhibits discriminative ability comparable to established markers for predicting complicated appendicitis. Notably, NPAR demonstrated the highest sensitivity (0.886) among all markers evaluated, suggesting its particular value in screening for complicated appendicitis. Most importantly, the combination of NPAR with CRP demonstrated synergistic discriminative value, achieving significantly superior discriminative performance (AUC 0.885, P = 0.003 vs. CRP alone) with balanced sensitivity (0.823) and specificity (0.806). This suggests that NPAR complements rather than replaces CRP in the diagnostic workflow. These results substantiate NPAR’s clinical utility as a valuable biomarker in the risk stratification algorithm for complicated appendicitis, particularly as part of a multi-marker strategy.

Further statistical analyses revealed deeper insights into NPAR’s discriminative characteristics. The optimal cut-off value of 16.78 derived from ROC curve analysis provides high sensitivity (0.886) but moderate specificity (0.608). Notably, our threshold effect analysis revealed a non-linear association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis, with a breakpoint at 19.53. The difference between these two critical values reflects distinct statistical perspectives: the ROC threshold optimizes discriminative performance for clinical screening, while the breakpoint reveals the biological pattern of risk association. This observed non-linear association might be associated with the pathophysiological progression of acute appendicitis. During early inflammation, neutrophil counts increase while albumin levels may decrease as observed in adult inflammatory conditions [27], potentially resulting in a proportional elevation of NPAR. In our study, this elevation correlated with disease severity, though the specific albumin dynamics in pediatric appendicitis require further investigation. As inflammation advances beyond a certain threshold, it is possible that neutrophil function may not fully compensate despite continued numerical increases, leading to a potential plateau in their antimicrobial effectiveness relative to disease severity [28]. Further studies are needed to clarify the relationship between neutrophil numbers and function in such contexts. As inflammation progresses beyond a critical threshold, neutrophils expand numerically yet develop activation-induced dysfunction driven by metabolic reprogramming. In severe inflammatory states such as sepsis, chemotaxis and tissue homing are impaired; phagocytosis and oxidative burst weaken due to disrupted glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathway flux with inadequate NADPH supply; and mitochondrial defects impede directed migration and the maintenance of late reactive oxygen species. These metabolism-linked defects decouple neutrophil quantity from antimicrobial effectiveness [29]. Consequently, higher NPAR, an inflammation indicator, no longer translates into proportionally greater antimicrobial impact, and the incremental risk plateaus at higher ranges beyond the breakpoint. These findings suggest that clinicians should exercise particular vigilance with patients exhibiting NPAR values between 16.78 and 19.53, as this range may represent a potential window where the risk of complicated appendicitis increases most rapidly. The stability of the key thresholds underpinning this algorithm (cutoff: 16.78; breakpoint: 19.53) was supported by our internal bootstrap validation, which confirmed the reproducibility of the cutoff and positioned the breakpoint within a plausible range of population values.

To provide explicit guidance for clinical decision-making, we posit that the NPAR should be integrated as a complementary tool within a multi-marker strategy, not as a replacement for established markers like CRP. Based on our findings, we propose an actionable clinical pathway wherein patients with a low NPAR below 16.78 can be managed with routine observation, leveraging its high negative predictive value of 95.51% to defer immediate imaging. Those in the critical NPAR zone between 16.78 and 19.53 warrant heightened vigilance, including surgical consultation and urgent imaging. For diagnostically challenging cases within this critical zone, we recommend employing the combined NPAR and CRP model, as this combination demonstrates superior discriminative power with an area under the curve of 0.885; concurrent elevation of both markers provides robust decision support for urgent intervention. For patients with an NPAR exceeding 19.53, immediate surgical referral is justified. It is crucial to note that this algorithm’s utility is age-dependent, as NPAR showed no significant association with complicated appendicitis in children under five years, necessitating greater reliance on clinical assessment and imaging in this younger subgroup. This structured, stepped approach that uses NPAR for initial screening and the multi-marker panel for confirmatory assessment provides a rational framework for risk-adapted management, though it requires prospective validation to confirm its impact on patient-centered outcomes.

Our study shares methodological similarities with prior research [30] investigating the neutrophil-albumin ratio (NAR) in diagnosing acute appendicitis and predicting perforation, particularly in study design and the analysis of inflammatory markers. However, notable differences in findings emerged. Specifically, our study identified a strong association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis in children with good univariable discrimination (AUC = 0.811), whereas the previous study [30] failed to validate NAR’s discriminative value for perforation. These discrepancies may arise from several factors: first, physiological distinctions between children and adults in immune responses and disease progression; second, our more rigorous methodological approach, including non-linear analysis revealing threshold effects (NPAR = 19.53) and comprehensive adjustment for confounders; and finally, the larger sample size (814 cases) combined with standardized diagnostic criteria, which bolstered the reliability of our results. These findings suggest that the predictive utility of NPAR may vary by age group, highlighting the need for future multicenter prospective studies to further substantiate these results.

Our findings carry substantial clinical implications. Primarily, the established NPAR threshold (19.53) offers an objective criterion for early detection of complicated appendicitis in pediatric patients, facilitating evidence-based clinical decision-making. Of particular significance, within the NPAR range below 19.53, each unit increment demonstrated a marked association with complicated appendicitis risk, and this non-linear relationship enables more refined risk stratification. ROC curve analysis further validated NPAR’s robust discriminative utility. Additionally, stratified analyses confirmed NPAR’s consistent discriminative capability across gender subgroups and various clinical manifestations, strengthening its credibility as a reliable biomarker. Considering NPAR’s advantages - simplicity, immediate derivation from routine admission blood tests, widespread availability, and absence of radiation exposure - we propose its integration into pediatric appendicitis assessment protocols, especially in primary care settings, to aid in identifying high-risk cases requiring prompt referral. Future investigations could evaluate NPAR’s synergistic potential with other inflammatory markers to develop more comprehensive predictive models.

Several limitations warrant consideration in our study. To directly address potential concerns regarding model stability, we performed comprehensive internal validation using bootstrap resampling, which confirmed the robustness of the NPAR cutoff and model performance. Furthermore, while we addressed multiplicity for the primary biomarker comparisons through Bonferroni correction, the exploratory subgroup analyses were not formally corrected. This increases the risk of Type I error for these specific findings, which should therefore be considered as generating hypotheses for future validation rather than providing definitive evidence. Also, some subgroup analyses, particularly in the youngest age group (< 5 years), were underpowered. The non-significant findings in these subgroups should therefore be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no association. However, the retrospective, single-center nature of our investigation, conducted exclusively within Chinese pediatric populations, still necessitates external validation across diverse demographic and healthcare settings to ensure generalizability. Additionally, the COVID-era timeframe of the study may have altered care pathways, potentially impacting patient management and outcomes, thus further limiting the applicability of our findings. Ethnic biological differences and healthcare-system variation (e.g., referral patterns and initial workup) may influence discriminative performance; thus, the NPAR cutoff (16.78) and breakpoint (19.53) derived from our dataset may not directly translate, underscoring the need for external validation in diverse pediatric settings. Moreover, the retrospective nature of our study introduces potential selection and information biases inherent to this design. Selection bias could arise from requiring complete laboratory documentation; information bias may stem from variability in the symptom-onset–to–testing interval. To mitigate these, we used consecutive enrollment, analyzed the first admission blood sample under standardized procedures, and adjusted for key covariates, though residual confounding (such as hydration status, pre-hospital antipyretics/analgesics, nutritional status, immune responses, and prior outpatient antibiotic use not consistently recorded) may have influenced our results. Furthermore, our study protocol excluded children with periappendicular abscess initially managed conservatively and later undergoing interval appendectomy. Although this exclusion was intended to preserve temporal consistency for biomarker assessment in immediate-surgery settings, it introduces a risk of spectrum bias, which may limit generalizability to the full clinical spectrum of complicated appendicitis and could modestly inflate discrimination estimates. Future studies should assess NPAR performance in cohorts that explicitly include conservatively managed presentations to address this spectrum gap. Additionally, as a retrospective discriminative accuracy study, we did not evaluate whether incorporating NPAR into clinical decision-making algorithms improves patient outcomes, such as reducing unnecessary imaging studies, shortening time to surgery, or decreasing complication rates. Lastly, the study design precluded evaluation of NPAR’s temporal variations in relation to disease progression. These limitations underscore the necessity for future multicenter, prospective investigations to validate our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our investigation establishes a non-linear association between NPAR and complicated appendicitis in pediatric patients, identifying a critical threshold value of 19.53. Below this threshold, we observed a marked positive association, whereas no substantial association was evident above this value. Internal validation via bootstrap resampling supported the stability of our model and the key NPAR cutoff value. NPAR, as an accessible biomarker derived from routine blood tests, shows promise as a potential tool for risk stratification of complicated appendicitis. However, given the retrospective, single-center design, our findings warrant validation in further prospective multicenter studies are required to validate these findings before broad clinical implementation.
