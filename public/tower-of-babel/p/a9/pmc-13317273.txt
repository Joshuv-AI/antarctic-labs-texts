Comment on “an interpretable machine learning tool for predicting perioperative cardiac events in patients scheduled for hip fracture surgery: insights from the multicenter LUSHIP study”
Authors: Parthasaradhi Mayasala, G Sunil Kumar, Lalband Neelu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Azzolina et al. [1]. presented an interpretable machine learning model for predicting perioperative major adverse cardiac events (MACE) in elderly patients undergoing hip fracture surgery. Accurate preoperative risk stratification remains a persistent challenge in this vulnerable population, and the integration of clinical variables with lung ultrasound data represents a valuable attempt to enhance individualized perioperative assessment.

A notable strength of the study is the use of a multicenter dataset and the development of an ensemble model that combines a gradient boosting machine and elastic net generalized linear models. The reported discrimination, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.86, indicates a meaningful improvement compared with the Revised Cardiac Risk Index alone [2]. The integration of the lung ultrasound score as a bedside biomarker is particularly relevant, as point-of-care imaging has gained increasing attention for its potential to identify subclinical cardiopulmonary dysfunction before surgery [3]. Therefore, incorporating such dynamic physiologic indicators into predictive algorithms may offer a more patient-specific assessment than traditional risk indices.

Despite these strengths, several aspects merit further consideration when interpreting the model’s clinical applicability. First, the dataset included patients exclusively from Italian institutions, which may limit its external validity across healthcare systems with different patient demographics, perioperative pathways, and surgical timing practices. Hip fracture management varies considerably between regions, particularly regarding delays to surgery, anesthetic strategies, and perioperative monitoring protocols. External validation in geographically diverse populations would therefore be essential to determine whether the model maintains its predictive performance beyond the original cohort.

Second, although the study emphasizes interpretability through the use of local interpretable model-agnostic explanations and the variable importance via personalized odds ratio framework, the clinical translation of these outputs requires careful contextualization. Visualization techniques may clarify variable influence within the model; however, they do not necessarily confirm causal relationships between predictors and outcomes [4]. For instance, the lung ultrasound score and Revised Cardiac Risk Index likely reflect overlapping physiological domains related to cardiopulmonary reserve. Understanding how these variables interact within the predictive framework would help clinicians interpret risk estimates more effectively.

Another point relates to model calibration. Although the reported discrimination is strong, the calibration slope greater than one suggests a tendency toward overconfident predictions. In perioperative decision-making, accurate calibration is particularly important because risk estimates may influence choices, such as intensive monitoring, cardiology consultation, or perioperative optimization strategies. Future studies could evaluate recalibration methods or threshold adjustments to improve the alignment between predicted and observed event rates.

The implementation of a web-based Shiny application is an innovative component of the study, offering clinicians the possibility of real-time risk estimation. Nevertheless, the successful adoption of clinical decision support tools depends not only on predictive performance but also on workflow integration and clinician trust [5]. Prospective evaluation in routine perioperative settings would be valuable to determine whether the tool meaningfully influences clinical decisions, resource allocation, or postoperative outcomes.

Despite these considerations, the work by Azzolina et al. represents an important step toward the practical application of interpretable machine learning in perioperative medicine. By combining clinical risk indices with bedside ultrasound data and providing transparent predictive outputs, the proposed model contributes to the ongoing effort to personalize risk stratification in elderly surgical patients. Further validation and implementation studies will help clarify its role in improving perioperative management in this high-risk population.
