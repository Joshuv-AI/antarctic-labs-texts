Lineage-specific expansion of luciferin-binding protein genes associated with the evolution of bioluminescence in Scleralcyonacea
Authors: Chenlan Zhang, Shichao Wei, Lulu Yang, Xianan Fu, Weijian Guo, Zhongwen Ding, Xinyuan Wang, Baowei Zhang, Wenliang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBioluminescence serves important ecological functions in prey capture, predator avoidance, and mate attraction. To investigate the genomic basis of bioluminescence evolution in marine cnidarians, we focused on the order Scleralcyonacea, which possesses a bioluminescent system involving the core gene families LBP, LUC, and GFP. We generated a chromosome-level genome assembly for the sea pen (Cavernularia habereri). Phylogenomic analyses identified three independent bioluminescent lineages within Scleralcyonacea, including one ancestral origin in sea pens and two independent origins in deep-sea lineages. Among the three core gene families, only LBP copy number expansion was strongly associated with bioluminescence phenotype and showed elevated expression in luminous species. Furthermore, LBP expansions generated phylogenetically distinct clusters or tandem duplicates under positive selection, suggesting potential neofunctionalization. These findings suggested that lineage-specific LBP expansion likely contributed to the evolution of bioluminescence in Scleralcyonacea, providing broader insights into the evolution of bioluminescence in marine organisms.Keywords: bioluminescence, Cavernularia habereri, adaptive evolution, genomes, luciferin-binding protein

Introduction

Bioluminescence is a form of chemiluminescence in which living organisms produce light through biochemical reactions.1 These reactions typically involve the oxidation of small-molecule luciferin substrates catalyzed by luciferases or enzyme-like proteins. Bioluminescence is a widespread adaptive trait that serves various ecological functions, including prey capture, predator avoidance, and mate attraction.2,3,4,5 It has evolved independently at least 40 times across the tree of life.1 To date, approximately ten biochemically distinct luciferins and seven luciferase gene families with different chemical and evolutionary origins have been described.6,7 The marine environment, characterized by relative physical stability and consistently low-light conditions, has been particularly conducive to the evolution of bioluminescence systems.1 Fossil and molecular evidence suggest that a significant diversification of marine bioluminescence occurred during the Cambrian period, about 540 million years ago (Ma).8,9 The diversity of marine bioluminescent mechanisms reflects their adaptation to different ecological environments and offers multiple avenues to elucidate the molecular basis of phenotypic evolution and environmental adaptation. However, the evolutionary origins and diversification of bioluminescence in marine species remain largely unresolved.

Marine bioluminescent systems exhibit remarkable diversity, and the storage of luciferin substrates represents a key regulatory step in light emission.10,11 For instance, certain fishes concentrate luciferin within specialized light-emitting organs,12 whereas jellyfish stabilize luciferin in a peroxy-coelenterazine state through aequorin.12,13 Among marine bioluminescent organisms, Scleralcyonacea is an octocoral lineage comprising 32 genera, with its major bioluminescent clades distributed across the families Virgulariidae, Pennatulidae, Veretillidae, Coralliidae, and Chrysogorgiidae, and spanning an exceptionally broad ecological range from shallow coastal waters to depths of several thousand meters (Table S1). It has a distinctive coelenterazine-based bioluminescence system triggered by external stimuli, such as mechanical perturbation or chemical cues.14 During this process, external stimuli open calcium ion channels in the cell membrane, leading to a rapid influx of Ca2+ and a transient rise in cytosolic calcium concentration.15 This triggers the calcium-sensitive luciferin-binding protein (LBP) to release its bound luciferin, coelenterazine (CTZ), which is then oxidized by luciferase to produce an electronically excited oxyluciferin intermediate that emits blue light at a peak wavelength of approximately 480–490 nm. In some species (e.g., Pennatula phosphorea), this process further involves green fluorescent protein (GFP), which is associated with a spectral shift toward greener wavelengths, potentially mediated by energy-transfer processes. Hence, LBP, LUC, and GFP constitute key molecular modules of bioluminescence in Scleralcyonacea and provide a tractable framework for investigating the evolution and diversification of light-production systems in this group.

Comparative genomics provides a powerful framework for investigating the genomic basis of phenotypic evolution, including gene sequence mutations, copy number changes, regulatory divergence, and structural variation. Among these mechanisms, changes in gene copy number represent a fundamental mechanism for the acquisition or enhancement of phenotypic traits.16,17,18,19 However, high-quality genomic resources for Scleralcyonacea remain limited. To date, nineteen genomes have been published, seven of which belong to three recognized bioluminescent coral lineages (Table S2).20,21,22,23 Moreover, a suitable experimental model species that is amenable to transcriptomic sampling, is required. Cavernularia habereri, a member of the bioluminescent Pennatulacea, exhibits light emission upon mechanical stimulation,24 consistent with observations in closely related sea pen species. This species is also suitable for experimental study due to its relatively large population size and wide distribution along the coasts of the Yellow Sea and East China Sea in China, as well as in Japan and the Indo-Pacific region.

In this study, we first generated a chromosome-level genome assembly for C. habereri. By integrating this new assembly with existing genomic datasets, we reconstructed the phylogenetic relationships of Scleralcyonacea to elucidate the evolutionary history of bioluminescent lineages. We further investigated the copy variation, genomic organization, and expression patterns of LBP, LUC, and GFP genes to identify key genetic changes associated with bioluminescence in Scleralcyonacea. This finding will provide a valuable genomic resource and offer new insights into the evolutionary patterns underlying the acquisition of bioluminescence across distinct lineages within Scleralcyonacea.

Results

Characterization of bioluminescence in C. habereri

Although C. habereri is widely recognized as a bioluminescent species, its precise emission dynamics remain largely uncharacterized. Using field-collected specimens, we demonstrated that mechanical stimulation in the dark instantaneously triggers a localized green luminescence (Figure 1A and Video S1). This signal is spatially restricted to the site of physical contact and decays rapidly following each event. Interestingly, repeated stimulation resulted in a progressive attenuation of emission intensity, implying the localized depletion of luminescent substrates.

To determine whether this luminescent mechanism shares homology with the Renilla bioluminescent system, we supplemented C. habereri cell suspensions with coelenterazine (CTZ), the classical luciferin substrate for Renilla luciferase. The addition of CTZ triggered green light emission, confirming a CTZ-dependent luciferase reaction. Spectral analysis of the emitted light revealed a peak at 520–540 nm (Figure 1B), matching the characteristic emission range of GFP proteins. Together, these results suggest that the final bioluminescent output is not solely generated by the primary luciferase reaction but is rather modulated by a coupled GFP, which shifts the emitted light to longer wavelengths.

Genome assembly and annotations

We collected muscle tissue for genome sequencing and skin tissue for transcriptome sequencing following stimulation-induced bioluminescence. For genome assembly, we generated a total of 26.26 Gigabyte (Gb) HiFi, 50.09 Gb Hi-C, and 27.20 Gb PE library sequencing data (Table S3). Using PacBio HiFi sequencing, we generated a contig-level genome assembly of C. habereri, the initial HiFi contig assembly comprised 1,243 contigs with a contig N50 of 3.59 Mb. Hi-C scaffolding anchored 96.55% of the sequences onto fourteen pseudo-chromosomes (Figure S1), with lengths ranging from 60.34 Mb to 11.10 Mb (Figure 1C; Table S4). The final chromosome-level genome assembly is 481.91 Mb, with a scaffold N50 of 40.23 Mb. This assembly size is highly concordant with the K-mer-estimated genome size of 487.28 Mb (k = 17) (Tables S5 and S6). Assembly integrity assessment achieved a BUSCO completeness score of 96.1%, comprising 95.3% complete single-copy orthologs, 0.8% duplicated genes, 2.7% fragmented sequences, and 1.2% missing core eukaryotic genes, meeting stringent genome assembly benchmarks for marine invertebrate species (Figure 1D; Table S7).

For gene annotation, a total of 20,440 protein-coding genes were predicted, of which 19,207 (93.97%) genes were functionally annotated (Table S8). Gene structure analysis revealed 3,903 (19.1%) single-exon genes, with exon number frequency decreasing sharply for multi-exon genes (Figure S2A). Gene lengths showed remarkable variation, with a median of 4,831.5 bp and an exceptional maximum length of 1,053,502 bp, following a right-skewed distribution (mean = 13,040 bp; Figure S2B). We performed a comprehensive annotation of repetitive sequences, revealing that approximately 62.87% of the C. habereri genome is composed of repetitive elements. Among these, DNA transposons were the most abundant, accounting for 30.28% of the genome. Long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) made up 10.06%, followed by long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons at 6.37%, and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), which comprised 1.16% of the genome (Table S9).

Phylogenomic analysis and divergence time estimation

The whole-genome alignment of 16 genomes (14 Scleralcyonacea and 2 outgroups) (Table S2) aligned to the assembled C. habereri genome, retaining only syntenic blocks shared across all species. We identified 815 conserved homologous regions shared among these species, collectively spanning approximately 6.9 Mb of aligned genomic sequence. We further constructed a whole genomic alignment tree by maximum likelihood method, with all nodes exhibiting bootstrap values exceeding 90%, indicating strong statistical confidence in the inferred phylogenetic relationships (Figure 2). All five sea pen species (families Virgulariidae, Pennatulidae, and Veretillidae) clustered into a single clade, consistent with their shared bioluminescent traits. Ancestral state reconstruction further indicated the common ancestor of sea pens was bioluminescent (Figure S3). These results suggest a single origin of bioluminescence within this lineage. In contrast, two deep-sea bioluminescent species, Paragorgia papillata and Chrysogorgia sp., were placed in distinct phylogenetic lineages. Their most recent common ancestors were likely non-bioluminescent, supporting independent origins of bioluminescence in these deep-sea taxa (Figure S3). Notably, the divergence of sea pens from other Scleralcyonacea was estimated at approximately 235.66 Ma, a period that broadly corresponds to relatively low global ocean temperatures based on paleotemperature reconstructions25 (Figure 2). Similarly, the divergence times of the two deep-sea bioluminescent lineages (P. papillata and Chrysogorgia sp.) were estimated at 76.12 Ma and 71.54 Ma, respectively, well after the Cenomanian-Turonian thermal maximum and within the Late Cretaceous cooling trend,26 suggesting that these bioluminescent lineages emerged during a relatively cooler paleoceanographic interval.

Expansion and neofunctionalization of the LBP gene family in Scleralcyonacea

We retrieved sequences of the three core bioluminescence-related genes (LBP, LUC, and GFP) across the genomes of 16 Scleralcyonacea species. This search yielded a total of 77 LBP, 40 LUC, and 23 GFP genes. Phylogenetic reconstructions and conserved domain analysis of the sequences showed strong concordance for each family, thereby corroborating the reliability of these identifications (Figure S4). Following these results, we found that LUC was consistently present in two copies in non-luminescent species and two to five copies in luminescent species. The GFP generally exhibits limited copy number with zero or a single copy in non-luminescent species, and zero to four copies in luminescent species (Figure 3A). Interestingly, some bioluminescent species have higher copy numbers of LUC and GFP genes than non-bioluminescent species (e.g., Veretillum cynomorium contained five LUC genes and four GFP genes). However, no clear pattern of gene expansion was observed among species with respect to bioluminescence, suggesting that changes in LUC and GFP may not directly underlie the evolution of the bioluminescent phenotype. By contrast, the copies of LBP have a large variation range in species, from zero to 18 copies in all species, and zero to three in non-bioluminescent species and four to 18 (P. papillata) in bioluminescent species. Moreover, LBP copy number was significantly associated with the bioluminescent phenotype (χ2 = 9.65, df = 1, p = 0.0019) (Figure S5).

Phylogenetic analysis of LBP gene copies further revealed the expansion patterns among the three bioluminescent lineages (Figure 3B). In the shallow-water sea pen and Chrysogorgia sp., multiple LBP copies were segregated into distinct phylogenetic clusters: clusters 1 and 3 for the sea pen, and clusters 2 and 3 for Chrysogorgia sp. These genes were distributed across multiple chromosomes (Figure 3C). Notably, all LBP copies within cluster 3 belonged exclusively to bioluminescent species, suggesting that neofunctionalization followed the expansion into cluster 3 and was linked to the evolution of bioluminescence. This pattern is further corroborated by non-bioluminescent species such as Briareum asbestinum, which retains six LBP copies restricted solely to cluster 1. In contrast to LBP, we did not detect similarly clear bioluminescence-specific expansion patterns in the other two gene families, nor did we observe a distinct derived cluster specifically associated with luminous taxa (Figure S6).

In contrast, the 18 LBP copies in P. papillata were entirely clustered within cluster 2 but diverged into two subclusters arranged in tandem across three contigs (Figures 3B and 3C; Table S11). Among these expanded copies, four in subcluster 2 exhibited Ka/Ks values greater than 1, while 12 sequences showed Ka/Ks > 0.8 compared to the non-luminescent relative Corallium rubrum, indicating signatures of positive selection (Figure 3D). In contrast, both GFP and LUC genes showed consistently low Ka/Ks values (Ka/Ks < 1) across all species, suggesting strong purifying selection (Table S10). In addition, we assessed the overall three-dimensional fold structures of LBP proteins using AlphaFold and found that all predicted LBP structures showed similarity to the R. muelleri reference, with TM-scores greater than 0.5, indicating conserved global folding (Figure S7). Taken together, these results demonstrate that LBP expanded from a conserved ancestral sequence, and its subsequent neofunctionalization across these three lineages represents the key driver of bioluminescent evolution in Scleralcyonacea.

Expression patterns of core CTZ bioluminescence genes

Despite the expansion of LBP in bioluminescent lineages, it remained unclear whether these copies are functionally expressed in light-emitting tissues, predominantly the skin. To address this, we analyzed transcriptomic data across a luminescent (C. habereri) and two non-luminescent (D. gigantea and P. clavata) species. In the luminescent species, the overall expression of LBP genes was markedly higher than that in the non-luminescent species D. gigantea (Figure 4A). Furthermore, LUC and GFP genes exhibited comparable expression levels across both luminescent and non-luminescent species (Figure 4B). Together, these findings demonstrate that only the expanded LBP gene family displays a high-expression bias in the bioluminescent species.

Discussion

In this study, we generated a chromosome-level genome assembly of C. habereri, with genome size 481.91 Mb and scaffold N50 = 40.23 Mb. Compared with previously published genomes of other Scleralcyonacea species, which generally have scaffold N50 values ranging from several hundred kilobases to 22 Mb, our assembly exhibits one of the highest levels of quality. This high-quality genome not only provides new insights into the evolution of bioluminescence in Scleralcyonacea, but also serves as a valuable resource for advancing research on the biology, ecology, and evolution of marine invertebrates.

In many bioluminescent systems, the origin of light emission is associated with the evolution of key components of the bioluminescent machinery. For example, firefly luciferase genes are hypothesized to have originated through duplication and subsequent neofunctionalization of ancestral acyl-CoA synthetase genes.27 Similarly, we found that the expansion and neofunctionalization of the LBP gene family were associated with the evolution of the luminescent phenotype. Such mechanisms, wherein gene duplication coupled with functional innovation drives the evolution of adaptive traits, have been widely documented in animal evolution, such as the neofunctionalization of duplicated P450 genes that drives insecticide resistance in the brown planthopper.28 Furthermore, while all LBP genes in Scleralcyonacea share a common ancestry, their expansion occurred independently across distinct lineages through divergent genomic trajectories. In sea pens and Chrysogorgia sp., LBP underwent segregation into two phylogenetically distinct clusters; in contrast, the expansion in P. papillata was characterized by tandem duplication coupled with positive selection. Consequently, this pattern reflects a parallel evolutionary response to intense selective pressures, likely driven by analogous anti-predator challenges across their respective environments.

At a broader evolutionary scale, our results showed that bioluminescence in Scleralcyonacea likely originated independently three times, suggesting that bioluminescence is not a phylogenetically fixed feature of this clade, but rather a trait repeatedly favored under particular ecological conditions. The bioluminescence observed in sea pens appears to have originated from a single ancestral event, consistent with previous findings.21,29 Species in this lineage exhibit large population sizes, high species diversity, and diverse ecological niches, suggesting that bioluminescence may have contributed to their adaptive radiation.30,31 Notably, both the origin of the sea pen lineage and the emergence of the two deep-sea bioluminescent lineages were associated with periods of major paleoceanographic change. In particular, P. papillata and Chrysogorgia sp., which inhabit deep-sea environments characterized by darkness and low nutrient availability, diverged from their closest relatives during the Late Cretaceous (ca. 77-70 Ma), when sea-level rise and post-OAE3 deep-ocean re-oxygenation profoundly reshaped deep-sea environments.32,33 Previous evidence that ocean cooling was linked to coral shifts from shallow to deeper habitats raises the possibility that long-term paleoceanographic change helped create ecological conditions favoring the evolution of bioluminescence for predation and anti-predation.

A remaining limitation of this study is that the biochemical activities of different LBP clusters remain untested. Although cluster 3 groups with experimentally validated Renilla LBP homologs, we can currently only show that all copies in C. habereri are transcriptionally active and that this species possesses an endogenous CTZ-dependent luminescence system. Future work should directly assay representative copies from different clusters, particularly in non-luminous taxa, to determine whether they retain similar biochemical activity or have functionally diverged.

Limitations of the study

Candidate LBP genes showing signatures of neofunctionalization warrant further biochemical characterization and functional validation to clarify their evolutionary roles in light production and regulation. In addition, many bioluminescent Scleralcyonacea lineages were not included in this study due to the limited availability of high-quality genomic resources. A more comprehensive understanding of bioluminescence evolution in Scleralcyonacea will require broader taxonomic sampling, particularly from deep-sea octocoral lineages. Moreover, because sea pen bioluminescence is triggered by mechanical stimulation, future studies investigating mechanosensory perception and downstream signaling pathways may provide deeper insights into how bioluminescence evolved in the context of ecological adaptation in marine cnidarians.

Experimental model and study participant details

C. habereri colonies were collected from Qingdao, Shandong Province (36.059395° N, 120.328330° E) and Xiamen, Fujian Province (24.496006° N, 118.188121° E), China. Samples consisted of fleshy stems and branching secondary polyps used for genome sequencing and transcriptome analyses. The sex of sampled colonies was not determined because reproductive structures were not examined during collection and sex cannot be reliably identified based on external morphology. The exact age and developmental stage of individual colonies were unknown. All specimens appeared healthy at the time of collection and had not been subjected to any previous experimental procedures prior to sampling.

Method details

Sample collection and genomic DNA extraction

Four specimens of C. habereri were collected from coastal intertidal zones in Qingdao, Shandong Province (36.059395° N, 120.328330° E) and Xiamen, Fujian Province (24.496006° N, 118.188121° E), China. After recording the morphological data, the samples were stored at -80°C. Fleshy stems and branching secondary hydroids were isolated for DNA extraction. The FastPure® Blood/Cell/Tissue/Bacteria DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) was used for the extraction of whole genomic DNA. Post-extraction DNA integrity was assessed via electrophoretic analysis on a 1.0% agarose gel, while DNA concentration and purity were quantified concurrently using a NanoDrop™ One/OneC microvolume UV-vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).66

Library preparation and genomic DNA sequencing

For Illumina platform sequencing, DNA specimens were initially subjected to Covaris-based random fragmentation. Size selection (200 to 400 bp) was performed using the Agencourt AMPure XP-Medium kit (Beckman Coulter, USA), followed by library preparation with the VAHTS ssDNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme Biotech, China) to construct paired-end (PE) libraries.67 Post-fragmentation size selection was reconfirmed through Agencourt AMPure XP-Medium-mediated purification. The workflow encompassed sequential enzymatic processing including end repair, 3'-adenylation, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. Purified PCR products were heat denatured and circularized by the splint oligo sequence, sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000 second-generation sequencing platform (MGI Tech, China).68

For HiFi sequencing, genomic DNA samples were first randomly fragmented using Covaris g-TUBE, followed by enrichment and purification of large DNA fragments through magnetic beads. The resulting DNA fragment underwent processes of damage repair and end repair, after which stem-loop sequencing adapters were ligated to both ends. Following adapter ligation, exonucleases were employed to remove any residual fragments that did not successfully ligate to the adapters. The final library product was combined with sequencing enzymes to form a sequencing template, which was then subjected to real-time single-molecule sequencing in the nanopores of the sequencer.

Hi-C library preparation and sequencing

For Hi-C sequencing, samples were cross-linked with formaldehyde to fix the chromatin, followed by cell lysis and chromatin digestion. Biotin labeling was applied to the ends, which were then ligated to form circular DNA molecules. After de-crosslinking and purification, the ligated fragments were enriched to construct the Hi-C library. Following quality control, the library was subjected to high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq/MGI-2000 platform to obtain Hi-C data for subsequent assembly and analysis.

RNA library preparation and sequencing

In the context of transcriptome RNA sequencing, we performed local mechanical stimulation on the fleshy stem tissues of the samples to induce a bioluminescent response. Subsequently, the luminescent tissues were collected and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen to prevent RNA degradation. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method, followed by rRNA removal through magnetic beads to enrich mRNA, which was then reverse transcribed to synthesize cDNA. The resulting cDNA library underwent quality control and was sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq platform with paired-end sequencing.

Genome size estimation and genome assembly

To estimate the genome size and heterozygosity rate of the C. habereri, the k-mer frequencies were calculated for those genomic short reads with GCE v1.0.2. Genome assembly and refinement were performed using Hifiasm v0.14.2137 with default parameters to generate contig-level assemblies. The primary assembly underwent systematic quality optimization, including contiguity metric calculation (N50) and GC-depth profiling. And then, the redundant sequences in the assembled genome were removed by Purge_dups v1.2.538, leveraging read depth consistency and synteny breakpoint analysis. Genome assembly quality control was systematically conducted using BUSCO v5.4.639 with the metazoa_odb10 database to evaluate both sequencing data quality and assembly completeness.

The Hi-C experiment was carried out following a previously published protocol.69 Processed sequencing data were initially analyzed through the Juice pipeline v1.041 for quality filtering and adapter removal. Final genome assembly refinement involved manual curation of chromosomal connections and orientation validation using Juicebox Assembly Tools v1.11.042, with visualization-based adjustments to resolve topological ambiguities.

Genome annotation

Repeats in the genome were annotated by both de novo and homology methods. For the de novo prediction, RepeatModeler v1.0.1143 and LTR-FINDER v4.09.144 were applied to construct independent repeat libraries, which were subsequently combined and aligned to the final genome assembly with RepeatMasker v4.1.445. For the homology-based analysis, RepeatMasker v4.1.4 was used to predict TEs based on the known TE protein database and RepBase library v21.1246. In addition, Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) v4.07b43 was employed to detect tandem repeats. Finally, overlapping repeats from different methods were integrated to generate the final nonredundant repeat repertoire.

To annotate gene structures, we integrated ab initio predictions, homology-based annotation, and transcriptome evidence. The ab initio predictions were conducted using three independent algorithms: Augustus v3.3.148, GenScan45 and GlimmerHMM.46 These algorithms were utilized for species-specific predictions, probabilistic model identification, and splice site/exon boundary predictions, respectively. Homology predictions were performed using GeMoMa, referencing four phylogenetically related species: R. muelleri (data source: http://rmue.reefgenomics.org/download/), R. reniformis (GCA_900177555.1), Virgularia gustaviana (GCA_025770025.1), and Pteroeides caledonicum (GCA_026546875.1). Transcriptome-assisted annotation was performed by aligning RNA-seq reads to the C. habereri genome using HISAT2 v2.2.151, followed by gene model refinement and training with PASA v2.4.152, and final evidence integration using EVidenceModeler (EVM) v1.1.153 to produce a high-confidence, non-redundant gene set. Finally, functional annotation was performed by comparing the predicted proteins against multiple authoritative databases, including NCBI nr, SwissProt,50 KEGG,51 Gene Ontology and InterPro,52 thereby assigning biological functions to the predicted proteins. Circos v.0.69.857 was used to visualize the genome features.

Phylogenetic reconstruction of Octocorallia

The molecular phylogenetic study by McFadden et al. (2022) classified octocorals into the orders Malacalcyonacea and Scleralcyonacea.70 To elucidate the evolutionary relationships within Scleralcyonacea, we obtained genomic data for all 21 species from NCBI for BUSCO quality assessment and two Malacalcyonacea species as outgroups. Using the newly assembled genome of C. habereri as a reference sequence, we performed whole-genome alignments with LastZ (optimized parameters: HSP threshold = 2400, linear gap penalty mode). We merged all pairwise alignment results to generate a genome-wide multiple sequence alignment. A stringent filtering procedure was then applied: species exhibiting extensive non-syntenic regions, repetitive sequences, or low-complexity regions were first removed. Subsequently, genomes with low alignment coverage were excluded. Following this curation process, a total of 15 high-quality species (Table S2) were retained for downstream analyses. We combined gene annotation file of C. habereri with coordinates of fourfold degenerate sites (4D sites) to extract evolutionarily conserved regions.71,72,73,74

Maximum likelihood analysis of the 4dtv dataset was conducted using RAxML v8.2.1240,54 with 1000 bootstrap replicates, discarding trees with low consistency to obtain the optimal topology. To calibrate the phylogenetic framework, we constructed an independent evolutionary tree based on mitochondrial whole genomes, designating the order Malacalcyonacea as the outgroup. Bioluminescence data were collected through literature review and analysis of species audiovisual records (Table S12). The fossil-based evolutionary tree was independently used to estimate divergence times with MCMCTree,55 incorporating three fossil calibration points: (i) the divergence time between the orders Alcyonacea and Scleractinia estimated at 250-350 Ma65; (ii) the divergence between the genera Corallium and Hemicorallium within the family Coralliidae estimated at 4.25-6.29 Ma, and their divergence from the genus Paragorgia estimated at 6.83-11.28 Ma.

Ancestral reconstruction of ecological traits

We integrated global depth records of each species using previously published literature28,72,73,74,75 and data from the Coral Trait Database (https://www.coraltraits.org/), after removing anomalous observations. It is generally accepted that a depth of approximately 200 m marks the lower limit of the euphotic zone, below which light intensity is insufficient to sustain effective photosynthesis (Table S12).

Based on this ecological threshold, we quantitatively characterized the bioluminescence traits and ecological niches of 16 coral species and categorized them into shallow-water (< 200 m) and deep-water (> 200 m) groups. Bioluminescent phenotypes were assigned based on available ecological and experimental evidence. For taxa with uncertain or variable luminescence, such as the genus Paragorgia, we adopted a conservative classification strategy, as not all species exhibit bioluminescence under all conditions.

To further investigate the evolutionary history of ecological niches and bioluminescent traits, we performed ancestral state reconstruction based on the species phylogeny inferred in this study, incorporating both depth and bioluminescence phenotypes. Both depth category (shallow vs. deep) and bioluminescence (presence/absence) were treated as discrete characters. Ancestral states were reconstructed using the Bayesian Binary MCMC (BBM) method implemented in RASP 467. Default parameters were used unless otherwise specified. The results were visualized on the phylogenetic tree to infer the probable states of ancestral nodes and to identify potential transitions in habitat depth and bioluminescent traits across lineages.

Comparative copy number analysis of LBP, LUC, and GFP gene families

In this study, we utilized a chromosome-level genome assembly of C. habereri, along with publicly available genomes of other octocorals, to systematically investigate the evolutionary dynamics of bioluminescence-related gene families within Octocorallia. Our integrative computational framework combines orthology inference, structural annotation, and phylogenetic validation to address gene family expansion. Protein sequences were clustered using OrthoFinder v2.5.468 From the resulting orthogroups (OGs), we identified bioluminescence-related gene families, among which conserved LBP sequences were selected as the basis for downstream analyses.

To identify LBP homologs across Scleralcyonacea, we first constructed a pan-taxonomic reference database. A total of 148 candidate sequences were extracted from the target orthogroup and combined with 237 experimentally validated LBP sequences retrieved from NCBI RefSeq. Redundant sequences were removed using CD-HIT59 (identity cutoff = 0.95), generating a non-redundant LBP reference set. This consolidated database was subsequently aligned to the genomes of 16 coral species using Miniprot v0.770 (parameters: -c 0.95, -G 20k), enabling accurate mapping of candidate gene families across taxa, with raw alignments filtered through a Perl pipeline enforcing stringent criteria (>95% identity, >80% query coverage, and strand-specific exon overlap validated through GFF3-guided boundary parsing). Candidate genes underwent dual verification through InterProScan52 domain profiling (requiring ≥1 LPS-binding domain, IPR001645) and maximum-likelihood phylogenetic reconstruction (IQ-TREE2 with ModelFinder+), where bootstrap support (SH-aLRT >80%) confirmed evolutionary congruence with reference LBPs. Parallel analyses were systematically applied to GFPs and luciferase families using identical computational thresholds, enabling direct cross-family comparisons.

Sequence divergence and structural comparison of bioluminescence-related proteins

To assess sequence divergence among the three core bioluminescence-related gene families (LBP, LUC, and GFP), coding sequences from the three candidate gene sets were analyzed separately. Pairwise nonsynonymous substitution rates (Ka) and synonymous substitution rates (Ks) were calculated for each gene pair using MEGA.61 Ka/Ks ratios were then derived to evaluate selective pressure, with values >1 interpreted as indicative of putative positive selection, values <1 as consistent with purifying selection, and values close to 1 as suggestive of neutral evolution. The resulting Ka/Ks values were visualized in R, and gene pairs with Ka/Ks >1 were specifically highlighted.

To further assess the structural integrity of candidate LBP sequences, conserved domain analysis was performed prior to downstream evolutionary analyses. All candidate LBP protein sequences were queried against the NCBI Conserved Domain Database using CD-Search62 to identify characteristic functional domains. Sequences lacking essential conserved domains or showing incomplete domain architectures were excluded from further analysis as likely pseudogenes or annotation artifacts. The remaining high-confidence sequences were subsequently used for phylogenetic reconstruction to examine evolutionary relationships among LBP copies and to identify clades potentially associated with independent origins of bioluminescence.

Subsequently, we extracted the LBP sequences from three luminescent groups and utilized the AlphaFold Server to predict their three-dimensional structures. Structural comparisons were performed using ChimeraX63 software. To evaluate structural conservation, we compared the AlphaFold-predicted64 models of LBP proteins from three species with the experimentally resolved coelenterazine-binding protein from R. muelleri (PDB: 2HPS), and calculated Template Modeling scores (TM-scores). All predicted structures exhibited TM-scores > 0.5, indicating overall topological similarity to the reference structure.76 The three-dimensional structure of CTZ (PubChem CID: 135445694) was imported for visualization and analysis of the ligand-binding sites.

Gene expression analysis

To compare the transcriptional activity of the three core bioluminescence-related gene families (LBP, LUC, and GFP) between luminous and non-luminous octocorals, we generated RNA-seq data from six skin tissues of C. habereri and combined them with publicly available RNA-seq datasets from two D. gigantea samples (SRR8293935 and SRR8293936) and 14 Paramuricea clavata samples (SRR19977426–SRR19977439) (Table S13). After quality filtering, clean reads were aligned to the corresponding reference genome using HISAT2 v2.2.1. Alignment files were converted and sorted with SAMtools.65 Gene-level read counts were quantified with featureCounts and normalized as FPKM values. We then examined the expression of candidate genes belonging to the LBP, LUC and GFP families to assess whether differences in gene copy number were accompanied by differences in transcriptional activity between luminous and non-luminous taxa. All expression data were visualized in R using ggplot2.

Emission spectrum measurement

To assess endogenous luminescence activity, tissue-derived cell suspensions of C. habereri were prepared by enzymatic dissociation using collagenase digestion, followed by resuspension in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The resulting cell suspensions were used immediately for luminescence assays. Coelenterazine substrate (Renilla luciferase substrate, Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit, Promega) was added to the cell suspension to initiate the luminescence reaction. All measurements were performed under dark conditions to minimize background signal. After brief mixing, luminescence signals were recorded using a Thermo Scientific™ Varioskan™ LUX multimode microplate reader. Emission spectra were measured over a broad wavelength range to characterize the luminescence profile. Cell suspensions without substrate addition were used as negative controls to account for background signal.
