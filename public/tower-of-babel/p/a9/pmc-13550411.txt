Metabolite profiling and enhanced antimicrobial and nematicidal activities of zinc oxide nanoparticle-biofortified Pleurotus mushrooms
Authors: Leema, Shivanshu Garg, Himanshu Punetha, Sunny Sharma, Shivender Thakur, Deepanjali Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the present investigation, the second most widely consumed edible mushrooms of Pleurotus species, namely P. djamor, P. florida and P. sajor-caju, were biofortified with zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) and screened for bioactives with Gas Chromatography- Mass Spectrometer (GC–MS), governing potent biological activities (antioxidant, antibacterial, anti-fungal and nematicidal), checked subsequently. The fruiting body extracts of Pleurotus spp. control (0 ppm) and ZnONPs treated (20 ppm) were found to have distinct chemical compositions in GC–MS analysis, but certain compounds were also found common to both extracts. All three fortified ZnONP extracts exhibited significantly greater biological activity than controls, including antibacterial activity against Erwinia amylovora, Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli and Xanthomonas oryzae, anti-fungal against Fusarium oxysporium and Sclerotinia sclerotiorum and nematicidal activity against Meloidogyne incognitia. ZnONP biofortification generally increases the relative abundance of shared bioactive metabolites, such as squalene, methyl palmitate, methyl linoleate, and ergosterol derivatives, in a species-specific manner, which plausibly explains the enhanced biological activities observed in treated extracts. GC–MS analysis revealed distinct but partially overlapping chemical profiles between control and 20 ppm ZnONP-treated extracts of all three Pleurotus species. These findings demonstrate that ZnONP biofortification enhances the nutritional and health promoting value of Pleurotus spp. and augmetns their potential as a plant protectant, establishing dual benfits for the valorization of edible oyster mushrooms.

Introduction

Mushrooms are being used in the pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, and food nutrition industries. These sectors use mushrooms and mushroom extracts in pharmaceutical products, nutritional food items, and cosmetics to create medications for a range of illnesses1. After Agaricus bisporus, Pleurotus is considered to be the second most commonly farmed and distributed edible fungus worldwide. Pleurotus mushrooms contain a number of beneficial nutritional and physiological properties. The Pleurotus genus has a wide range of relevant characteristics that have been established in numerous research, demonstrating that they function as desirable low-cost industrial instruments that alleviate the burden of ecological concerns2. The oyster mushroom is a broad and varied genus of cultivated mushrooms with important medicinal and commercial uses. Oyster fungus is prevalent in both tropical and temperate climates worldwide3. Notably, P. sajor-caju contains polysaccharides, including a linear ß-D glucan and exo-polysaccharide, which give it anti-neoplastic and anti-inflammatory properties4. In addition, P. sajor-caju demonstrated potent antiviral properties against types 2 and 7 of the adenovirus5.

Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) have emerged as a promising agent for agricultural biofertifiation. As, Zn is a micronutrient that is required by humans, animals and plants6. Also, its nanoform (1–100 nm) offers enhaced bioavaiablity and uptake compared to conventional supplements of Zn7. Additionally, as agriculture deals with a number of unforeseen problems, like decreased crop output from biotic and abiotic stressors like pollution and nutrient shortage, nanotechnology has become a promising tool for precision agriculture8.

The current study constitutes an expansion of an earlier investigation9 where the ZnONPs-derived biofortification (for nutritional enhancement) in P. djamor and P. florida was experimentally proved. Previous studies have largely focused on productivity, while detailed metabolite profiling and systematic evaluation in ZnONP mediated biofortified Pleurotus spp. still remain underexplored. Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the metabolite profile through GC–MS of control and ZnONP fortified P. djamor, P. florida, and P. sajor-caju. Also, their antioxidant, antibacterial, anti-fungal and nematicidal activities were evaluated and compared, which widen the scope of futuristic research aiming at the biofortification and biological activities potential of ZnONPs in various mushroom species that are utilised for edible purposes.

Materials and methods

The present investigation was conducted to investigate the effect of ZnONPs on Pleurotus spp. (P. djamor, P. florida and P. sajor-caju), believed to be originated in temperate and sub-tropical forests of Europe, Noth America, and Asia. Different concentrations were tested at the time of spawning of Pleurotus spp. The research work was carried out in the Department of Biochemistry, College of Basic Science and Humanities (biochemical experimentation) & Mushroom Research and Training Centre (MRTC) (mushroom culturing), G.B. Pant University of Agriculture & Technology (GBPUA&T), Pantnagar. The previous work focused on nutrient and productivity changes, whereas detailed metabolite profiling and broad-spectrum bioactivities (including nematicidal) in ZnONPs biofortified Pleurotus spp. remain underexplored. Also, only 20 ppm concentration of the extract was shown to have greatest impact on the yield of the oyster musroom9, so this was chosen for the futuristic study here in the current research work.

Mushroom cultivation

Both fresh and dry wheat straw were utilized as substrates in this investigation. The substrate was chemically treated prior to spawning in order to avoid microbiological infestation. In a 200 L plastic drum, 10 kg of wheat straw was completely immersed in 90 L of water for treatment. Separately, 10 L of water was used to dissolve 7.5 g of Bavistin (50% WP) and 40 mL of formaldehyde (40% v/v). The mixture was then equally poured over the soaked straw and thoroughly stirred. After that, a polythene covering was used to seal the drum, and it was left alone for sixteen hours. Following treatment, the straw was taken out, and extra water was poured onto a sterile surface until the moisture level was around 70%. The modified substrate was then used for spawning.

Spawning and bag filling of Pleurotus spp.

Following the conventional procedure, the prepared wheat straw (about 2.0%) was completely combined with the commercial spawn. The spawned substrate (substrate after mixing the spawn) was placed in 60 × 45 cm polythene bags weighing 1.5 kg each, and the bags were securely sealed. Eight to ten tiny holes were cut at a specific spacing after the polythene bag was filled and sealed to allow for the free diffusion of gases and heat produced inside. For about 15 days, the bags were hanging in a dark incubation room with a temperature of 25–28 °C and a humidity of 70–80% until the entire straw was firmly linked by mycelia (spawn run) and the moisture content was kept at 70%.

Bag opening

Following the completion of the spawn run, the polythene sheet was carefully removed using a sharp knife. Water was then sprayed twice a day to maintain the right humidity for fruiting body development. Temperature and relative humidity were kept at 24–28 °C and 75–85%, respectively, during cropping. The total yield was calculated as the fresh weight of mushrooms harvested up to fifth flush per 1.5 kg of dry substrate for the three mushroom species used in 5 replicate bags per treatment for each of the three species studied here.

Application method of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs)

The ZnONPs were purchased from Sisco Research Laboratories, Pvt. Ltd. India. The purity of nanoparticles was 99.99% and the APS was 30 nm. The material was obtained as crystalline colorless powder of molecular weight 81.38 g/mol. It was soluble in water. Zinc oxide nanoparticles typically exhibit a zeta potential in the range of − 20 to − 30 mV in aqueous solutions at neutral pH. The three Pleurotus spp. were subjected to 20 ppm concentration of ZnONPs, procured from Sigma-Aldrich. Different concentrations of the working solutions were sprayed at the time of spawning and bag filling at 20 ml per 1.5 kg substrate and three replicates of each treatment were taken for this experiment. Spraying was done in the morning hour with the help of an atomizer sprayer. Similar conditions were followed for untreated (control) oyster mushrooms, which were sprayed with distilled water. Different concentrations of were made by properly mixing and diluting the stock solution with distilled water.

Metabolite profiling of Pleurotus spp. by gas chromatography-mass spectroscopy (GC–MS)

The fruiting bodies of Pleurotus species were collected, washed, and dried in a hot air oven at 40–50 °C until they reached a consistent weight. Using a lab grinder, the dried fruiting bodies were reduced to a fine powder. 10 g of the powdered material and 100 mL of methanol (1:10 w/v) were combined for extraction, and the mixture was macerated and sonicated for 24–48 h at room temperature with sporadic shaking. To guarantee the highest yield, this procedure was carried out three times. Particulate matter was eliminated by filtering the combined extracts with Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The fruiting bodies of Pleurotus species were collected, washed, and dried in a hot air oven at 40–50 °C until they reached a consistent weight. The dried extracts, ZnONPs treatment groups, and control groups were reconstituted in methanol at a concentration of 20 ppm for GC–MS analysis. 20 mL of methanol was mixed with 2 g of anhydrous Na₂SO₄, thoroughly shaken, and left to settle in order to eliminate any remaining moisture. Before being injected into the GC–MS instrument, a 5 mL aliquot of the clear supernatant was decanted and filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. To remove moisture, 2 g Na2SO4 was added to 20 mL of methanol and after vigorous shaking, the mixture was allowed to settle down. After that 5 mL methanol was decanted from this mixture. Small amounts of extracts were dissolved in methanol, after which it was filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper. and the solution was retained to be used for GC–MS analysis. A GCMS-QP 2010 Plus instrument was used to do the GC–MS profiling. With a column flow rate of 1.21 mL/min, total flow of 30.8 mL/min, input pressure of 69.0 kPa, purge flow of 3.0 mL/min, and split ratio of 22.0, helium was utilized as the carrier gas at a constant linear velocity of 39.9 cm/sec. Split mode was used to inject a 1 μL sample volume at 260 °C in the injector. Initially set at 50 °C, the oven temperature was programmed to climb at a rate of 3 °C per minute to 210 °C, where it was held isothermally for two minutes. After that, it was raised to 280 °C at a rate of 6 °C per min, held isothermally for two minutes, and then maintained at 280 °C for 11 min. Data were collected in full scan mode across a mass range of 40–600 m/z while the mass spectrometer was running in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV with an ion source temperature of 200 °C and an interface temperature of 280 °C. Using computer matching of mass spectra against the NIST Mass Spectrometry (NIST-MS) library and the Flavors and Fragrances of Natural and Synthetic Compounds (FFNSC) Wiley library, compounds were identified by comparing their Kovats retention indices on a DB-5 column with published literature values. A similarity match threshold of ≥ 80% was deemed acceptable for tentative identification.

Biological activities

Antioxidant activities

The same extract was used for antioxidant assays, antimicrobial assays, and nematicidal assays. The DPPH (2, 2-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging activity10, reducing power activity11,12, metal chelating activity13 and egg albumin test for in-vitro antiinflammatory action14 were performed.

Antibacterial activity

Using the disc diffusion method as described by Gashaw et al.15, the antibacterial activity of oyster mushroom extract against plant and animal pathogenic bacterial strains was tested against plant pathogenic bacteria viz. Erwinia amylovora, Xanthomonas oryzae, Escherichia coli and Bacillus subtilis. The two different concentrations as 1% and 2% were prepared to determine the antibacterial activity. Streptomycin was used as a positive control and nutrient agar was used as growth media in the study. The bacterial strains were obtained from the Department of Plant Pathology (College of Agriculture), GBPUA&T, Pantnagar. The disc diffusion method of antimicrobial activity was carried out by following the previously reported method. A loopful inoculum of pure culture of test bacterium was inoculated to a sterile conical flask containing nutrient broth and was kept for 24 h incubation. Conical flask were placed in incubator shaker until the absorbance reached between 0.008 and 0.1 measured with the help of spectrophotometer at 625 nm then 100 μL of each cell suspension with the help of micropipette was pipetted onto nutrient agar plate anduniformly spread into plates by using plate technique. The 30 μl of prepared solution of methanolic extract was impregnated into a 5 mm sterile disc and the disc was subjected to air-drying. After that disc was placed into the agar plate and left for incubation for 24 h at 37 °C. The antibacterial activity was evaluated by measuring the diameter of zone formed around the disc.

Antifungal activity

Two phytopathogenic fungi S. sclerotiorum and F. oxysporium obtained from the Department of Plant Pathology, were maintained and grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium in order to study antifungal activity of various extracts of oyster mushroom Grover et al.16. The PDA of Hi Media was used as a growth medium. Autoclaved PDA was cooled to 450C and added 20 ml PDA in each sterilized petri plates in laminar flow and kept undisturbed as such till it got solidified. The poisoned food protocol developed by Grover and Moore was followed to test the methanolic extract of mushrooms against selected fungi. Sterilized petri plates of 90 mm diameter were used for pouring the medium. In each petri plate about 20 ml sterilized melted medium supplemented with different concentrations of mushroom samples was aseptically poured near a burner flame in a sterilized laminar floor chamber. The medium in the plates were centrally inoculated by placing a 5 mm mycelia disc which was cut from the margin of 7 days old culture of the test fungus. The plates were sealed with parafilm immediately. Inoculated petri plates were incubated at 26 ± 10 in a BOD incubator. Under the same circumstances, a control dish without extract was equipped. The growth on the control plate was measured up to the edge of the plate. Each fungus strain’s percentage of radial growth inhibition was calculated concerning the control. Around the petri dish, there were millimeter-sized clear zone of mycelia growth inhibition, which were used to identify antifungal activity. The standard fungicide used was bavistin.

Nematicidal activity

The M. incognita eggs were collected from infected roots of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum). The infected roots were obtained from the Crop Research Center, GBPUA&T, Pantnagar, and maintained in a glasshouse at a temperature of 25 + 2 °C. Plants roots with galls were collected and washed with water to remove soil from diseased roots. Mature egg masses were hand-picked and cultured in distilled water at a temperature of 25 °C. Freshly hatched second-stage larvae (j2) were used for further trials. Within 48 h of hatching, about 100 s-stage juveniles per ml of water were taken into gridded petri dishes. Different oyster mushroom concentrations (0.5, 0.75, and 1.0%) in 1.0% Tween-20 water solution were used as treatments. The procedures were carried out three times, in random order. The juveniles in 1% Tween-20 water solution served as the control group. At 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, nematodes were observed for mortality assay using a stereo binocular microscope at 4 × magnification. Statistical analysis

Experimental results were the means of three replicates measurements. The mean values and standard deviation were calculated by Duncan test. The results are presented in the form of graphs and tables were subjected to ANOVA at 5% level of significance (p < 0.05) using SPSS16.00 and CRD using OPSTAT.

GC–MS analysis revealed that the chemical composition of fruiting body extracts of P. djamor control and 20 ppm was found to be different, but certain compounds were commonly found in both extracts, such as 3-cyclopentylpropionic acid, 2-dimethylaminoethyl ester, 5,8,22-ergostatrienol, Methyl linoleate, Methyl stearate, Methyl-trans-oleate, n-Propyl linoleate, Squalene and Methyl palmitate. The result showed that at 20 ppm concentration of ZnONPs, the % peak area was increased except for 5, 8, 22-ergostatrienol, responsible for various biological activities. In the case of P. florida chemical composition of the fruiting body extract at control and 20 ppm was observed to be different. The results demonstrated that the percentage peak area of certain chemicals was increased at 20 ppm concentration of ZnONPs, such as Ergosta-5, 8, 22-trien-3.beta.-ol, Ergosta-5, 7, 9 (11), 22-tetraen-3-ol, (3.beta.22E), Squalene, 3-cyclopentylpropionic acid, 2-dimethylaminoethyl ester, Carbonic acid and 2-dimethylaminoethyl isobutyl ester. The % peak area of some of the compounds was decreased (Nonadecane, Methyl 14-methylpentadecanoate, Linoleic acid, methyl ester, Methyl stearate, Monomethyl monobutyl “capped” tetraethylene glycol, 1-Monolinolein, Methyl 2-hydroxy-tetracosanoate). Similarly, the chemical composition of fruiting body extracts of Pleurotus sajor-caju at control and 20 ppm was found to be different. Result of GCMS analysis showed that some compounds were commonly found in both the extracts, such as Myristic acid, Carbonic acid, 2-dimethylaminoethyl 2-methoxyethyl ester, Methyl palmitate, Methyl linoleate, Methyl elaidate, 3-cyclopentylpropionic acid, 2-dimethylaminoethyl ester, Fumaric acid, 2-dimethylaminoethyl nonyl ester and Squalene. At a 20 ppm concentration of ZnONPs, the % peak area of these compounds was increased.

By comparing the detected peaks’ mass spectra to the NIST Mass Spectrometry (NIST-MS) library and the Flavors and Fragrances of Natural and Synthetic Compounds (FFNSC) Wiley library, compounds were identified. A minimum similarity index threshold of ≥ 80% was deemed acceptable for tentative identification. A second level of confirmation was obtained by calculating the Kovats retention indices (RI) of the discovered compounds based on retention periods relative to a homologous series of n-alkanes and comparing them with documented RI values reported in the literature for a DB-5 column. Positive identification was only provided for substances that satisfied both the retention index match criteria and the mass spectral similarity requirement. Without the use of internal or external standards, the discovered compounds were semi-quantified by computing the relative percentage abundance of each compound as the ratio of its individual peak area to the total ion chromatogram (TIC) peak area, expressed as a percentage. The relative percentage composition (%) of all discovered substances was used to report the results.

Discussion

The variations in phenolic and fatty acid contents found by GC–MS analysis can likely account for the heterogeneity in antioxidant activity seen among Pleurotus spp treated with ZnONPs. Well-known free radical scavengers, phenolic substances including phenol, hydroquinone, and derivatives of cinnamic acid directly enhance antioxidant capacity by means of electron transfer and hydrogen donation. Similarly, it has been noted that unsaturated fatty acids, especially linoleic and oleic acid, have antioxidant qualities via lowering lipid peroxidation and stabilizing free radicals. Consequently, it is anticipated that species or treatments with higher relative abundances of these unsaturated fatty acids and bioactive phenolics in GC–MS profiling will exhibit correspondingly higher antioxidant activity, supporting the observed differences across treatment groups11,12,33.

Certain bioactive compounds found in mushroom extracts are responsible for the antibacterial, antifungal, nematicidal, and antioxidant properties of Pleurotus spp. It has been well documented that fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs), especially linoleic acid and oleic acid, interfere with the fluidity and permeability of bacterial and fungal cell membranes, resulting in both antibacterial and antifungal effects. By competing with membrane ergosterol production pathways, ergosterol and its derivatives, characteristic sterols of fungal origin, have shown antifungal action. They also have a strong ability to scavenge free radicals, which contributes to antioxidant activity. In addition to their well-established antioxidant and antimicrobial qualities, terpenoids, including triterpenoids like ganoderic acid-like compounds found in oyster mushrooms, have been shown to exhibit nematicidal activity by impairing nematode cuticle integrity and neuromuscular function34–36. The broad-spectrum bioactivities seen in P. djamor, P. florida, and P. sajor-caju in the current investigation are explained by these metabolites taken together.

It has been noticed that at a concentration of 20 ppm, the ZnONPs produced the highest yield for P. djamor, with 723.27 ± 2.02 g in 2020 and 738.28 ± 3.44 g in 2021. For P. florida, the application of 20 ppm ZnONPs significantly enhanced yield to 787.45 ± 2.21 g in 2020 and 802.45 ± 2.50 g in 20219.

The broad-spectrum bioactivities observed in Pleurotus spp extracts in the present study can be plausibly linked to specific metabolite classes detected by GC–MS37,38, though it must be acknowledged that GC–MS profiling here is largely qualitative and relative, and the mechanistic connections proposed remain to be confirmed by targeted biochemical investigations. Mohamed and Farghaly39 identified 56 metabolites in P. ostreatus including acids, alkanes, esters, alcohols, fatty acids, terpenoids, and phenols, underscoring the metabolic richness of Pleurotus species as a baseline. A central hypothesis emerging from the present data is that ZnONP biofortification at 20 ppm selectively upregulates secondary metabolite biosynthesis, thereby enhancing bioactivities beyond baseline levels inherent to untreated mushroom extracts, as consistently evidenced by superior performance of T1 and T2 treatments over their respective controls across all bioassays.

In terms of antioxidant activity, constitutively produced phenolics like ergosterol, hydroquinone, linoleic acid, and derivatives of cinnamic acid, which donate hydrogen atoms and electrons to neutralize reactive oxygen species, are well-attributed to Pleurotus species’ baseline capacity to scavenge free radicals11,12,33. The increased antioxidant activity seen in ZnONP-treated extracts is in line with the findings of Ogidi et al.40, who reported 96.3% DPPH scavenging in Zn-fortified P. pulmonarius, and Stastny et al.41, who discovered notable variations in radical scavenging among Pleurotus spp. extracts, with P. flabellatus methanolic extract having the highest concentrations of ergosterol, ergothioneine, and mannitol. The idea that zinc supplementation promotes ergosterol and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis is supported by the fact that Lentinus edodes showed the strongest superoxide radical-scavenging activity in ethanolic extract and Agaricus bisporus var. subrufescens showed significant reducing power and antioxidant capacity in both chemical and cellular assays42. Matos et al.43 confirmed that ZnONPs themselves have intrinsic free radical scavenging potential, representing an additive ZnONP-specific contribution separable from baseline Pleurotus antioxidant activity. Dobrucka et al.44 and Jan et al.45 reported that the metal-chelating enhancement associated with ZnONPs using natural chelating agents in ZnONP synthesis further increases antioxidant capacity through a mechanism different from direct radical scavenging. By preventing oxidative stress-induced chronic inflammation, anti-inflammatory bioactive compounds found in oyster mushrooms, such as methyl elaidate, methyl stearate, linoleic acid methyl ester, and methyl petroselinate, further enhance antioxidant activity46.

According to Sarma et al.47, phenolic compounds like protocatechuic acid, syringic acid, caffeic acid, cinnamic acid, and vanillic acid are important antimicrobial constituents, and FAMEs like linoleic acid methyl ester, methyl elaidate, and methyl stearate are responsible for the baseline antimicrobial potential of Pleurotus extracts. ZnONPs may provide an independent antibacterial mechanism, as evidenced by the much larger inhibition zones seen for ZnONP-treated extracts, especially T2PF against Escherichia coli (28.53 ± 0.50 mm) surpassing streptomycin (28.13 ± 1.03 mm). ZnONPs significantly inhibited both Gram-positive (Klebsiella pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus) and Gram-negative (E. coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa) bacteria, according to Kamal et al.48. Surface-generated reactive oxygen species (ROS) destroyed bacterial cell walls and membranes, which was exacerbated by electrostatic interactions between positively charged ZnONP particles and negatively charged bacterial surfaces49. As a result, the antibacterial activity seen here most likely reflects a synergistic dual mechanism: ROS-mediated bactericidal activity introduced by ZnONP biofortification amplifies the baseline FAME and phenolic-mediated membrane disruption inherent to Pleurotus spp, and ZnONP application further enhances the overall antibacterial potential of oyster mushrooms across various biomedical contexts50.

In addition to germacrene D-4-ol, methyl palmitate, linoleic acid methyl ester, 6-octadecenoic acid, tetradecane, and methyl stearate, which were previously reported as antifungal by Sarac et al.24, Butt et al.51, Suresh et al.29, Satmi and Hossain30, Rangsinth et al.32, Aziz et al.52, and Lu et al.53. Although quantitative sterol profiling is still needed to confirm this, the hypothesis is that ZnONP treatment increases ergosterol pathway metabolite concentrations through zinc-mediated enzyme activation in sterol biosynthesis, thereby enhancing antifungal potency above baseline Pleurotus levels.

In the current study, Pleurotus extracts exhibited concentration-dependent effects in their antibacterial and antifungal properties, with larger dosages producing superior inhibition. T2PSC was the most effective antibacterial agent, X. oryzae was the most vulnerable pathogen, and P. florida even demonstrated competitive efficacy against E. coli by outperforming conventional streptomycin. On the other hand, antifungal activity regularly failed to completely inhibit the chemical control Bavistin, despite showing promise with T2PD as the best performer and S. sclerotiorum as the more sensitive fungus. While antibacterial efficacy was more widely concentration-driven, antifungal studies also demonstrated selective pathogen targeting. Both activities differed among species.

The most remarkable discovery regarding nematicidal activity was the near-complete larval immobility of 95.10 ± 0.00% attained by T2PSC against M. incognita J2 larvae at 96 h, significantly surpassing P. djamor (53.33%) and P. florida (31.50%), indicating significant species-dependent variation in nematicidal metabolite composition. Methyl elaidate, methyl stearate, and methyl palmitate, reported as nematicidal by Lu et al.53, Abubakar and Majinda22, and Adebayo et al.54, are thought to interfere with nematode cuticle integrity and neuromuscular signaling, which accounts for the baseline nematicidal activity present in Pleurotus species. These results are in line with earlier studies showing the nematicidal activity of extracts derived from mushrooms, such as the hydroalcoholic Neolentinus ponderosus extract exhibiting nematicidal action against Haemonchus contortus larvae in vivo and in vitro55 and the spent mushroom compost of L. edodes that significantly reduced intact larvae of Panagrellus spp. over 24–72 h of exposure56. The rapidly declining LC50 values of T2PSC from 0.73% at 24 h to 0.69% at 96 h demonstrate consistent time-dependent potency, whereas the comparatively steady LC50 values of P. florida treatments (0.72–0.78%) indicate a less potent but persistent baseline activity. Crucially, although the better performance of treated extracts over control extracts suggests ZnONP-induced enhancement of nematicidal activity, the precise effect of ZnONP biofortification on nematicidal metabolite profiles is still unknown in published literature9, indicating a substantial knowledge gap that calls for focused metabolomic research.

Conclusion

The results suggested that ZnONPs are a potential antioxidant, antibacterial, anti-fungal and nematicidal agent in oyster mushrooms. GC–MS analysis revealed the chemical composition of fruiting body extracts of Pleurotus spp. at control and 20 ppm was observed to be different. At a 20 ppm concentration of ZnONPs, the % peak area of some of the compounds increased, which were commonly found in extracts and were responsible for various biological activities. The effect of ZnONPs on biological activity was concentration-dependent. Whereas, the maximum activity was observed for the treatments at high concentrations of the extract, which suggested that high concentrations can be used for potential pharmacological applications. Fruiting body extracts of oyster mushrooms were observed to be enriched with bioactive compounds that possessed potential therapeutic activities. Future studies should focus on testing oyster mushrooms effectiveness against various diseases to position the bioactive compounds as promising herbal alternatives to chemically synthesised drugs and conducting in vivo trials to assess their therapeutic properties. In order to reduce the negative consequences of nutrient deficiency, future study should also concentrate on investigating the mechanisms behind various nutritional interactions.
