First joint report on the prevalence and genotyping of Cryptosporidium spp., Giardia duodenalis, and Enterocytozoon bieneusi among hospitalized children in Inner Mongolia, China
Authors: Zhiyu Wang, Wei Gao, Li Zhao, Yonghong Liu, Yuan Gao, Haifeng Yuan, Xuewen Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCryptosporidium spp., Giardia duodenalis, and Enterocytozoon bieneusi are zoonotic intestinal parasitic protozoa that occur globally and can cause varying degrees of diarrhea, especially in children. In immunocompromised individuals, these protozoa may lead to persistent diarrhea and even death. Most studies worldwide have focused on the occurrence of these protozoa in animals, while few have investigated the prevalence and genotyping of these three protozoans among hospitalized children in Inner Mongolia, China.MethodsDNA was extracted from fresh fecal samples of hospitalized children in Inner Mongolia. Nested polymerase chain reaction, genome sequencing, and sequence analysis were performed to evaluate the prevalence and genotyping of the above-mentioned three protozoa.ResultsAmong hospitalized children in Inner Mongolia, the prevalence of Cryptosporidium spp., G. duodenalis, and E. bieneusi infections was 0.84% (1/119), 30.25% (36/119), and 21.85% (26/119), respectively. G. duodenalis and E. bieneusi, as well as Cryptosporidium spp. and G. duodenalis, have mixed infections. The prevalence of E. bieneusi and G. duodenalis was numerically higher in non-diarrheic samples (27.27%, 21/77; 33.77%, 26/77) than in diarrheic samples (11.9%, 5/42; 23.81%, 10/42), and no association was found between protozoan occurrence and diarrhea. Cryptosporidium spp. was identified as C. bovis; G. duodenalis was identified as assemblage AI (1) and assemblage E (33); and E. bieneusi was identified as genotype J (11), BEB4 (2), and new genotypes NMGC1–9, all belonging to group 2.ConclusionsTo the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to jointly report prevalence and genotyping of Cryptosporidium spp., G. duodenalis, and E. bieneusi among hospitalized children in Inner Mongolia. Moreover, a mixed infection of two protozoa was detected; C. bovis and E. bieneusi were detected in hospitalized children in Inner Mongolia; a mixed infection of G. duodenalis assemblages AI and E was observed in humans; and E. bieneusi genotype J was identified as the dominant genotype in the evaluated population.Keywords: Cryptosporidium spp., Giardia duodenalis, Enterocytozoon bieneusi, Prevalence, Genotype, Children

Introduction

Cryptosporidium spp., Giardia duodenalis, and Enterocytozoon bieneusi are common gastrointestinal pathogens in children worldwide, especially in developing countries [1–4]. They are transmissible via the fecal–oral route through food, water, or direct contact, leading to outbreaks and epidemics of significant clinical and public health importance [2, 5, 6]. Cryptosporidium spp. is the second leading cause of diarrhea after rotavirus in children aged < 5 years in low-resource countries [7], whereas G. duodenalis is one of the top 10 human health-threatening parasites [8]. Cryptosporidium spp. and G. duodenalis are the fifth and eleventh most important food- and waterborne parasites, respectively [9]. E. bieneusi is classified as a class B biodefense pathogen and a waterborne microbial pathogen [10].

Cryptosporidium spp. is a complex zoonotic pathogen [11]. Currently, at least 44 valid Cryptosporidium spp. and approximately 120 genotypes have been described worldwide; of these, 29 species have been reported to infect mammals, and at least 19 species and 4 genotypes have been reported in humans. Cryptosporidium hominis and Cryptosporidium parvum are the primary species responsible for most human infections [12]. Genetic analyses using molecular typing have revealed that G. duodenalis comprises at least eight genetically distinct but morphologically identical lineages (A–H), with assemblages A and B infecting various hosts and vertebrates and thereby posing a high zoonotic risk [13]. E. bieneusi has been classified into 13 phylogenetic clusters (groups 1–13) using internal transcribed spacer (ITS) typing [14], and > 850 distinct genotypes have been identified, with > 90% of genotypes belonging to groups 1 and 2 [15, 16]. Group 1 contains most of the genotypes identified in humans. To date, more than 126 genotypes have been discovered solely in humans, with genotypes D, EbpC, and IV being the most common, and 58 genotypes have been identified as zoonotic [16, 17].

Cryptosporidium spp [1]., G. duodenalis [13], and E. bieneusi [18] are globally widespread, with pooled prevalence in China of 2.97% [19], 0.6% [20], and 5.8% [21], respectively. In Inner Mongolia, only a few studies have reported on protozoan infections, Cryptosporidium spp., with infection rates of 3.57% in outpatient children with diarrhea, 4.19% in children from pastoral areas [19], and 12.15% in pediatric patients with diarrhea [22]. In addition, C. bovis (one case) and G. duodenis (five cases) were detected in ranch workers [23], and a prevalence of 0.4% for Giardia duodenalis was reported in local children [24]. To date, no study has reported on E. bieneusi infections in humans in Inner Mongolia. This study aimed to assess and analyze the prevalence and genotypes of these three intestinal parasites among hospitalized children in Inner Mongolia, to evaluate mixed infections, and to develop targeted prevention and control strategies.

Materials and methods

Study areas and sample collection

Fecal samples were collected from all hospitalized children (n = 119) aged 1 month to 17 years at the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region People’s Hospital in Hohhot (Central Inner Mongolia) between May and July 2021. Fresh fecal samples were stored in dry, sterile, disposable stool specimen collection bottles, marked with relevant information, and stored at 4 °C. All study participants consented to the study, and all methods employed were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Routine fecal microscopy examination

Each fecal sample was stirred with distilled water, filtered, and centrifuged (3000×g for 3 min). The precipitate was stirred with a saturated sucrose solution and centrifuged (3000×g for 5 min). After centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred to a glass slide, and protozoan oocysts were examined under a light microscope.

DNA extraction and polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Genomic DNA was extracted from the fecal samples of the enrolled children using E.Z.N.A® Stool DNA Kit (Omega Biotek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions in a biosafety cabinet. The extracted DNA was stored at -20 °C. The SSU rRNA gene of Cryptosporidium spp [25]. and G. duodenalis [26] and the ITS gene of E. bieneusi [27] were amplified via nested PCR using Premix Taq™ (TaKaRa Taq™ Version 2.0 Plus Dye, TaKaRa, Beijing, China), with the extracted DNA as a template. Three types of protozoan DNA preserved by the Pathology Teaching and Research Department of Veterinary Medicine, Inner Mongolia Agricultural University were used as positive controls. Positive PCR products were commercially sequenced by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The Cryptosporidium spp. SSU rRNA-positive amplification products were analyzed using restriction enzymes (Ssp I and Mbo II; TaKaRa, Beijing, China) [28]. PCR products were digested into specific bands of different sizes using restriction endonuclease, and Cryptosporidium spp. were identified by comparing the specific bands with the restriction map. Restriction fragment length polymorphism results were combined with the SSU rRNA bidirectional sequencing results to confirm the DNA carrying C. parvum. Nested PCR of gp60 was performed using published primer [25], and the sequence analysis of the amplified product was used to determine the subtype of C. parvum [29]. For G. duodenalis, SSU rRNA-positive DNA was amplified for bg [30], gdh [31], and tpi [32] using nested PCR. PCR products were sequenced to determine G. duodenalis assemblages. Sequence analysis

The samples were sequenced in both directions, and positive sequences were corrected using SeqMan software and compared with reference sequences downloaded from GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) using MEGA 11.0 software (http://www.megasoftware.net/). The nucleotide sequences obtained in this study were analyzed using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool online platform. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the neighbor-joining algorithm based on a matrix of evolutionary distances calculated using the Kimura two-parameter model in MEGA 11.0 software. Cluster robustness was assessed using 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Univariate analyses, including Pearson’s chi-square test, chi-square for trend, Fisher’s exact test, and R×C chi-square test, were used to compare the prevalence of the three parasitic infections in groups according to age, sex, and disease background. Odds ratios (ORs; with 95% confidence intervals [CIs]) were calculated to examine the associations between participant characteristics and parasitic infections, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cryptosporidium spp. infection status

PCR amplification of the SSU rRNA gene of Cryptosporidium spp. revealed only 1 positive sample out of the 119 samples from a 12-year-old urban boy with nondiarrhea. The prevalence of Cryptosporidium spp. was 0.84% (1/119).

G. duodenalis infection status

PCR amplification of the SSU rRNA gene of G. duodenalis revealed that 36 out of the 119 stool samples were positive, yielding an overall prevalence of 30.25% (36/119). The prevalence of G. duodenalis was higher in children with nondiarrhea (33.77%, 26/77) than in those with diarrhea (23.81%, 10/42); however, the difference was not significant (OR = 0.613, 95% CI: 0.261–1.438, P = 0.258) (Table 2).

The prevalence of G. duodenalis was the highest in children aged 0–2 years (33.85%, 22/65), followed by 7–12 years (30%, 3/10) and 3–6 years (27.5%, 11/40), with no insignificant intergroup differences (χ2 = 2.277, P > 0.05). Urban children had a higher prevalence (32.63%, 31/95) than rural children (20.83%, 5/24), yet the difference was non-significant (OR = 1.841, 95% CI: 0.629–5.390, P = 0.261). Furthermore, female children showed slightly higher prevalence (33.33%, 14/42) than males (28.57%, 22/77), with no significant difference (OR = 0.800, 95% CI: 0.356–1.798, P = 0.589) (Table 2).

E. bieneusi infection status

PCR amplification of the ITS region of E. bieneusi from the 119 samples revealed 26 positive samples, indicating an overall prevalence of 21.85% (26/119). The prevalence of E. bieneusi was numerically higher in non-diarrheic samples (27.27%, 21/77) than in diarrheic samples (11.90%, 5/42), but not statistically significant (OR = 0.360, 95% CI: 0.125–1.040, P = 0.053) (Table 2).

E. bieneusi prevalence was highest in children aged 7–12 years (30%, 3/10), followed by 3–6 years (22.5%, 9/40) and 0–2 years (21.54%, 14/65), with no significant intergroup difference (χ2 = 1.521, P > 0.05). Urban children had a higher prevalence (23.16%, 22/95) than rural children (16.67%, 4/24), and the difference was non-significant (OR = 1.507, 95% CI: 0.466–4.877, P = 0.492). The prevalence was 16.67% (7/42) in females and 24.68% (19/77) in male, with no significant difference (OR = 1.638, 95% CI: 0.625–4.290, P = 0.312) (Table 2).

Cryptosporidium spp., G. duodenalis, and E. bieneusi coinfection status

All 119 samples were tested for Cryptosporidium spp., G. duodenalis, and E. bieneusi, and 9 samples were found to have mixed infections of G. duodenalis and E. bieneusi, whereas 1 sample had a mixed infection with Cryptosporidium spp. and G. duodenalis, based on a single isolate. All 119 samples tested negative for routine stool egg detection.

Discussion

Cryptosporidium spp

This study only detected a single positive case of Cryptosporidium spp., corresponding to a prevalence of 0.84% (1/119). This prevalence was lower than that reported in previous studies from this region [19, 22] and lower than that reported in most other Chinese provinces, with only Henan Province and Taiwan Province showing lower prevalence (Table 5). Internationally, the infection rate in this study was lower than that reported in African (2.6%–21.3%) and Central and South American (3.2%–31.5%) countries but higher than that reported in some European (0.1%–14.1%) and North American (0.3%–4.3%) regions [33]. These differences may be attributed to factors such as sample size, sampling time, regional and climatic differences, sanitary conditions, host factors, and parasite virulence.

Prevalence rates of Cryptosporidium spp., Giardia duodenalis, and Enterocytozoon bieneusi in China

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the positive sample obtained in this study belonged to C. bovis. There have been reports of ranch workers infected with C. bovis in Inner Mongolia [23]. Previously, C. bovis was considered to be endemic only to cattle and other bovid species, particularly the dominant calf species [28]. However, in 2010, C. bovis was detected in the fecal samples of some cows and dairy farm workers at several farms in Bangladesh and India, representing the first-ever cases of C. bovis infection in humans [39]. Subsequently, in 2012, C. bovis infections were identified in a 3-year-old child and a 23-year-old adult who consumed raw milk and had close contact with calves in Australia [40]. A child (< 6 years old) in Ismailia, Egypt, was coinfected with C. parvum and C. bovis [41]. These findings suggest that the host range of C. bovis is not as narrow as previously thought, and it may have zoonotic potential. However, sequence analysis of the gp60 locus in C. bovis infections in humans remains lacking [42], and its gene polymorphism is underexplored. Therefore, this study cannot conduct gp60 subtype analysis on C. bovis. Future studies should focus on identifying new genetic loci for large-scale dynamic studies of C. bovis in humans.

G. duodenalis

In this study, G. duodenalis prevalence was 30.25% (36/119), the highest among the three parasites studied. This infection rate was significantly higher than those reported from other Chinese provinces (Table 5). In contrast, most developed countries report significantly lower prevalence rates (0.4%–7.6%) [43], whereas certain developing countries, such as Egypt (34.6%), Sahrawi (34.2%), and Uganda (40.7%), show even higher prevalence levels [43]. Various factors, including ecological, environmental, and demographic conditions, particularly host immunity status, may account for the observed differences in prevalence.

Epidemiological investigation showed that children aged 0–2 years had the highest prevalence of G. duodenalis, followed by those aged 7–12 and 3–6 years. However, the differences were not statistically significant, likely owing to the small sample size or improved sanitation conditions. Previous studies have shown that age is an influencing factor for G. duodenalis infection, with one study reporting a high rate of this parasitic infection in children aged 7–12 years [44]. Younger children may be more vulnerable owing to immature immune systems, which is consistent with our results. Furthermore, G. duodenalis induces changes in intestinal microvilli, thereby decreasing the crypt-to-villus ratio, causing enzyme defects in the brush border, and leading to malabsorption and diarrhea. It also affects children’s growth and development [45–47]. In addition, in this study, diarrhea was not prominent in children with G. duodenalis infection. This warrants further investigation, as our sample size was insufficient to establish its association.

Genotyping via SSU rRNA analysis revealed that all G. duodenalis sequences belonged to assemblage E, which was the dominant assemblage. Unfortunately, only one credible sequence was obtained at the tpi loci, which limited the performance of multilocus genotyping. Phylogenetic analysis identified this sequence as assemblage A, revealing that it belonged to the assemblage AI. This is the first time that mixed infections of assemblage E and AI have been identified in humans. Assemblage E occurs mainly in cloven-hoofed animals, and it is also the dominant genotype of cattle, sheep, and other animals in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, especially calves [48]. But assemblage E has also been reported in humans [49, 50], suggesting that it has a zoonotic transmission ability [47, 51]. Assemblage AI is predominantly found in artiodactyls, indicating a potential for livestock-to-human transmission [43]. We emphasize here that in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, additional epidemiological studies and continuous surveillance of domestic animals are needed to clarify the transmission dynamics and public health significance of G. duodenalis.

E. bieneusi

In this study, we first discovered E. bieneusi infection among children in Inner Mongolia, with a prevalence of 21.85%. The prevalence of E. bieneusi in Inner Mongolia was higher than that reported in most provinces and regions in China (Table 5). It was also higher than that reported in some foreign countries, including Korea (0.1%) [52] and Thailand (3.3%) [53], but similar to that in Changchun, China (22.5%) [38]. These differences in prevalence may be attributed to variations in disease backgrounds, host immune levels, and the economic and living conditions of the tested individuals.

Epidemiological investigation showed that in this study, children aged 7–12 years old had the highest prevalence of E. bieneusi, followed by those aged 3–6 years old and 0–2 years old. The age distribution is consistent with previous findings in Zhengzhou, China [34], but inconsistent with those from Uganda [54]. In addition, the differences in prevalence in our results were not statistically significant, while the reports from Zhengzhou were significant [34]. These differences may be related to the small sample size, socioeconomic status of the studied areas, immune status of the hosts, parasite virulence, and sensitivity of the detection tools. E. bieneusi is the most common microsporidia causing intestinal diseases. The infection pathogenesis is associated with excessive intestinal epithelial cell death, partial villus atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and changes in some digestive enzyme activities, resulting in malabsorption, diarrhea, and weight loss [55, 56]. However, in the present study, diarrhea was not prominent in children with E. bieneusi infection. Does this parasite induce immune imbalance by inhibiting, modulating, and transforming the host’s immune response following infection [57], thereby evading immune attack and promoting its survival?

In total, 11 genotypes were identified based on E. bieneusi ITS sequence analysis, including 2 known genotypes (J and BEB4) and 9 novel genotypes (NMGC1–9), which were classified as group 2. Among them, genotype J was the dominant genotype; this finding was not consistent with a previous study reporting that genotypes D, EbpC, and Type IV were the most common infection genotypes in China [37]. These results suggest that E. bieneusi infection in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region has unique genetic characteristics. Genotypes J and BEB4 were initially thought to be bovine-specific. However, they remain the most frequently reported genotypes infecting cattle worldwide and have subsequently been found in humans [18, 34, 58, 59]. Group 2 genotypes are typically zoonotic [60] and mostly include bovine-specific genotypes [61]. The above findings suggest that E. bieneusi has some zoonotic potential and that cattle are an important reservoir. In the present study, nine new E. bieneusi genotypes that infect humans were identified, suggesting that E. bieneusi in Inner Mongolia has high genetic diversity and unique genetic characteristics. Future studies should focus on continuous surveillance and source tracing of E. bieneusi infection in endemic areas in both humans and livestock to better understand its epidemiological characteristics.

However, the relatively small sample size (n = 119) limits the generalizability of these findings, underscoring the need for larger multicenter studies to better evaluate infection patterns and associate risk factors.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to jointly report the prevalence and genotyping of Cryptosporidium spp., G. duodenalis, and E. bieneusi among hospitalized children in Inner Mongolia. For the first time, we detected mixed infections involving two protozoa; C. bovis and E. bieneusi were detected in hospitalized children in Inner Mongolia; mixed infections of G. duodenalis assemblages AI and E were observed in humans; and E. bieneusi genotype J was identified as the dominant genotype in this study. Our findings suggest that the population structure and genotypes of three protozoa are complex and diverse; however, our study has significant limitations due to a small sample size. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies involving human and animal specimens from the same epidemic areas to better understand the sources of human infection and improve prevention and control strategies for three protozoan infections.
