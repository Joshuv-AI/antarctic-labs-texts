Comparison of the Clinical Efficacy of the Anterolateral and Regular Approaches for Hip Joint Puncture: A Randomized Cross‐Over Trial
Authors: Jiamu Liu, Jingjie Huang, Yiling Tan, Chunmei Liu, Jing Li, Wen Sui, Xing Hua, Tiao Su, Guangxing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveGiven the limitations of conventional anterior and lateral approaches, such as variable success rates and risks of neurovascular injury, there is a critical need to evaluate alternative techniques that enhance procedural safety and efficiency. This study aimed to investigate the safety and precision of the anterolateral approach for hip joint puncture and compare its clinical efficacy with those of the anterior and lateral approaches.MethodsA single‐center, prospective, randomized Williams crossover trial was conducted from March 2023 to June 2023 involving 30 patients with hip pain. Each patient underwent three hip joint punctures within 3 weeks, all conducted using anatomical landmark‐guided blind puncture. The punctures were administered in different sequences of anterior, anterolateral, and lateral approaches, with one‐week intervals between punctures. Thirty patients were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 5), following different sequences of the three approaches. The primary outcomes were the success rate and accuracy, and the secondary outcomes were post‐puncture pain assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), procedure time, puncture depth, and complications.ResultsThe anterolateral approach achieved a higher success rate (96.7%) compared to the anterior (86.7%) and lateral (83.3%) approaches; although statistical significance was not reached (p = 0.328). Besides, it significantly shortened procedure duration (72.87 s, SD 9.66) compared to anterior (87.20 s, SD 20.57) and lateral (92.80 s, SD 39.02) approaches (p = 0.006). The puncture path length was shorter with the anterolateral approach (57.77 mm, SD 1.295) than with the lateral approach (63.33 mm, SD 1.295) (p = 0.004). The anterolateral approach achieved lower VAS pain scores (1.77, SD 0.94) compared to the lateral approach (2.90, SD 2.17). During the anterior approach injection, one patient experienced numbness in the lateral thigh of the surgical side.ConclusionThis preliminary randomized crossover trial demonstrates that the anterolateral approach offers significant advantages in procedural efficiency (reduced time, shorter path length) and suggests a trend toward higher success rates compared to standard anterior and lateral approaches for hip joint puncture. These findings, particularly the improvements in efficiency and patient comfort (lower VAS), support the anterolateral approach as an effective technique.Level of EvidenceI, Randomized controlled trial. Introduction

Hip joint injections play a crucial role in the management of various hip pathologies, including osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis, and other intra‐articular painful lesions [1, 2, 3]. These injections not only alleviate pain and enhance joint function but also serve as a diagnostic tool to distinguish intra‐articular pain origins from extra‐articular pain origins [4, 5, 6]. In addition to offering a minimally invasive alternative to more invasive surgical interventions, hip injections can improve postoperative efficacy [7, 8, 9]. Based on the literature, approximately 23% of patients with hip osteoarthritis in the United States receive intra‐articular corticosteroid injections annually. Among patients with hip osteoarthritis who receive intra‐articular corticosteroid injections, 40%–60% of them experience a pain reduction of ≥ 50% after 8 weeks [10], highlighting their significance in optimizing treatment strategies in modern musculoskeletal care.

Regular hip joint puncture approaches, such as the anterior and lateral routes [11, 12], have been widely employed in clinical practice, but they face several limitations. The lateral circumflex femoral artery and its branches are at a substantial risk of inadvertent puncture during the anterior approach [13]. Additionally, steep needle angles impair visibility and complicate needle placement, particularly for less experienced clinicians, leading to extra‐articular injections or painful periosteal contact [14, 15]. Although the lateral approach is safer and does not damage major vessels and nerves, it needs a longer puncture path, increasing procedural time and patients' discomfort [16, 17]. Both the anterior and lateral approaches exhibit variable success rates [18], affected by anatomical variations and clinician experience, resulting in inconsistent outcomes. These limitations highlight the need for alternative techniques that can improve safety, efficiency, and patients' comfort without compromising accuracy.

The anterolateral approach is a well‐recognized classical portal in hip arthroscopy, positioned centrally within the “safe zone” for such procedures [19, 20]. This strategic location mitigates the risk of damage to nearby critical structures, such as the femoral neurovascular bundle and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, increasing procedural safety. Drawing on these anatomical and procedural advantages, we propose the anterolateral approach as an ideal puncture route for intra‐articular hip punctures. Theoretically, the anterolateral approach offers a more direct needle trajectory, which can enhance procedural accuracy, provide a more controlled insertion angle, and improve patients' comfort compared to conventional methods. These characteristics underscore the potential of the anterolateral approach to optimize the safety and efficacy of hip joint injections.

Despite its potential, there are few systematic assessments on the anterolateral approach against regular routes, such as the anterior and lateral methods in real clinical practice. The comparative advantages concerning safety, accuracy, procedural time, and patient‐reported outcomes remain insufficiently addressed in the literature. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and precision of the anterolateral approach for hip joint puncture. This study specifically compared the success rate, procedural time, needle path length, and patients' Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores of the anterolateral approach with those of regular routes. The specific objectives were as follows.

To compare the success rate, procedural time, needle path length, and patient‐reported Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain scores of the anterolateral approach against anterior and lateral approaches, leveraging its anatomical positioning in the “safe zone” to enhance procedural accuracy and efficiency.

Methods

Patients Information

This study adopted a single‐center, prospective, randomized Williams cross‐over design. Due to the nature of the interventions requiring distinct patient positioning (e.g., hip flexion angle) and palpation of different anatomical landmarks, it was not feasible to blind the physician performing the puncture or the patients to the specific approach (anterior, anterolateral, or lateral) being administered during each of the three weekly sessions. Patients were aware of their assigned sequence group. However, to minimize bias: (1) The primary subjective outcome, post‐puncture pain intensity, was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) by the patients themselves immediately after each procedure, prior to any interaction with the physicians regarding the procedure details. Patients recorded their VAS score independently on a standardized form. (2) The physician responsible for documenting immediate procedural data (e.g., time, observed complications) was present during the procedure. (3) Crucially, the independent researchers who conducted the final statistical analysis were completely blinded to the patient group assignment (sequence group) and the specific puncture approach associated with each individual outcome measurement. All data were anonymized and coded prior to analysis. Thirty patients presenting with hip pain were recruited for this study. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were as follow.

Each patient underwent three hip joint injections within 3 weeks. All punctures were conducted using the anatomical landmark‐guided blind puncture approach. Each patient received three hip joint punctures over 3 weeks, with a one‐week interval between sessions. This interval minimized potential confounding from tissue inflammation related to prior needle insertion and aligned with clinical feasibility for repeated procedures. Moreover, all primary and secondary outcomes (success rate, accuracy, immediate VAS pain, procedure time, puncture depth, acute complications) were assessed independently of pharmacological effects. Success and accuracy were confirmed by ultrasound before drug administration. VAS pain, reflecting procedural discomfort, was assessed immediately post‐puncture; procedure time and depth were measured intraprocedurally, and complications were monitored ≤ 1 h post‐injection. Consequently, any pharmacological carryover effects, such as those from corticosteroids, did not influence the interpretation of the results.

Based on the different sequences of puncture approaches, 30 patients were randomly assigned to six sequence groups (A‐AL‐L, A‐L‐AL, AL‐A‐L, AL‐L‐A, L‐AL‐A, and L‐A‐AL) (n = 5 per group). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the trial was registered on chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2300074174). Ethics approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Southwest Hospital, Third Military Medical University (Army Medical University), Chongqing, China (registration number KY 2023092).

Sample Size

The sample size of this study was calculated using PASS software (version 21.0.3, NCSS, USA). We employed tests for pairwise proportion differences in a Williams cross‐over design for equal proportions to compare success rates among six sequence groups. Based on previously available data and clinical expectations, assumptions included a significance level of 0.05, a power of 0.95, and hypothesized success rates of 78.58% for the anterior group, 81.37% for the lateral group, and 90% for the anterolateral group. The sample size calculation, based on comparing pairwise success rates within the crossover design, provided an initial estimate. Accounting for an anticipated 30% loss to follow‐up, a total of 30 participants were enrolled. It is acknowledged that this sample size, while sufficient to detect large differences in efficiency metrics based on preliminary data, may have limited power to detect smaller but potentially clinically relevant differences in success rates, particularly given the inherent variability of landmark‐guided techniques.

Randomization

Randomization was conducted using a random number table, assigning numbers from 1 to 30 to establish a random grouping table. Initially, patients were randomly assigned numbers from 1 to 30, preserving privacy and obtaining informed consent. Participants were then allocated to one of six groups based on the random grouping table.

Outcomes and Measurements

The primary outcomes were the success rate and puncture accuracy. The secondary outcomes included post‐puncture pain using Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores, procedure time, puncture depth, and complications. Success and accuracy were determined based on conventional musculoskeletal ultrasound, which confirmed the needle's location in the joint capsule. Immediately after the punctures and prior to any discussion about the procedure, the patients independently rated the pain caused by the injection using the VAS on a provided form. Puncture time included skin disinfection, needle insertion, ultrasound verification, and injection of sodium hyaluronate and compound betamethasone. The depth of the puncture was measured from the skin's surface to the needle's tip. All patients were monitored in the recovery room for 1 h. Adverse events such as leg numbness, altered sensation, or local hematomas were recorded.

Injection Approach and Procedure

For anterior and lateral puncture approaches, patients lay in a supine position, with their hip joints at 0° for flexion, abduction, and adduction. The puncture point for the anterior approach was marked at the intersection of a horizontal line, positioned 1 cm below the tip of the greater trochanter, and a vertical line from the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS). Thereafter, a spinal needle was inserted vertically from anterior to posterior. For the lateral approach, the tip of the greater trochanter was selected as the puncture point, and the needle was inserted at a 15°anterior tilt angle until it contacted the bony structure (Figure 1).

The technical parameters for the anterolateral approach were defined in our preliminary study. Sixty patients with hip joint pain were included in our preliminary research, and the size of the anterior joint capsule space was measured using ultrasound at hip flexion angles of 0°, 10°, 20°, and 30° to determine the optimal hip flexion angle (Figure 2). Additionally, 3D models of the healthy side from 166 patients undergoing hip replacement surgery were included before the procedure. The optimal puncture plane and angle of needle insertion were determined through simulated punctures (86 patients for the optimal puncture plane; 80 patients for the needle insertion angle). In this study, the anterolateral puncture approach was as follows (Figure 3): Patients were positioned in the supine position, with the affected hip flexed at 20°. Two reference lines were marked: one was the bisector between the vertical line from ASIS and the lateral edge of the femur, and the other was the midline between the tip of the greater trochanter and the lateral femoral ridge. The intersection of these two lines was the puncture point, which aligned with the femoral head–neck junction and was located lateral to the medial edge of the tensor fasciae latae muscle. The needle was inserted at a 45° angle until contacting the bone.

Two clinical physicians were involved throughout: one performed the punctures, and the other documented the procedural data. Post‐puncture ultrasonography was used to confirm correct needle placement in the targeted bony structure, ensuring procedural accuracy and detecting complications. A 22‐gauge spinal needle, 8 cm in length, was used for all punctures. 2.5 mL of hyaluronic acid and 1 mL of compound betamethasone were administered after ultrasound confirmation of successful puncture. Independent researchers conducted the final analysis to maintain unbiased assessments. Demographic and clinical variables were collected, including gender, age, body mass index (BMI), procedural time, puncture trajectory length, and patient‐reported pain during the puncture procedure based on the VAS.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (v27.0, IBM) and R (v4.3.0, “lme4” package) by independent researchers who were blinded to patient group assignment (sequence group) and the specific puncture approach associated with each data point. To account for repeated measures within patients in this crossover design: 1. Continuous outcomes (procedure time, VAS pain, puncture depth): (1) Analyzed with linear mixed‐effects models (LMM). (2) Fixed effect: puncture approach (anterior, anterolateral, lateral). (3) Random intercept: patient ID (to control for within‐subject correlations). (4) Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment. 2. Binary outcomes (success rate): (1) Analyzed with generalized estimating equations (GEE). (2) Independent variable: puncture approach. (3) Correlation structure: exchangeable (equal correlation between repeated measures). Model assumptions (normality, homoscedasticity) were verified. Results report adjusted p values and effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

For secondary continuous outcomes (procedure time, VAS, puncture depth), Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple pairwise comparisons (anterolateral vs. anterior, anterolateral vs. lateral, anterior vs. lateral). The adjusted significance threshold was α = 0.017 (0.05/3).

Results

All analyses accounted for repeated measures using mixed‐effects models (continuous outcomes) or GEE (binary outcomes); as detailed in Methods.

Patient Characteristics

Among the 30 patients, there were 12 males and 18 females, with an average age of 45.2 ± 14.09 years and an average BMI of 22.76 ± 2.74 kg/m2. In total, 13 subjects had acetabular labral tears due to femoroacetabular impingement (FAI); 8 had tears due to developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), and 9 were diagnosed with hip osteoarthritis (OA). There were no significant differences between the six sequence groups in terms of demographic characteristics (Table 1).

Success Rates

In total, 90 punctures were completed for 30 patients, with 80 successes and 10 failures. Among the 10 failed attempts, ultrasound indicated that the needle tip was at the 12 o'clock position of the acetabular labrum in one case, and the needle did not contact any bony structures in the remaining 9 failed punctures. The anterolateral approach achieved a success rate of 96.7% (29/30), outperforming the anterior (86.7%, 26/30) and lateral (83.3%, 25/30) approaches. Although the overall chi‐square test did not demonstrate statistical significance (p = 0.328), the absolute risk reduction (ARR) was 10.0% (95% CI: −4.2%–24.2%) vs. anterior and 13.4% (95% CI: −1.7%–28.5%) vs. lateral. The relative risk (RR) of success was 1.12 (95% CI: 0.96–1.30) for anterolateral vs. anterior and 1.16 (95% CI: 0.97–1.39) for anterolateral vs. lateral. This magnitude of difference—potentially avoiding 1 failed puncture per 10 procedures compared to the lateral approach—may hold clinical relevance despite statistical non‐significance. The consistent trend toward higher success rates with the anterolateral approach highlighted its procedural reliability (Figure 4A). A post hoc power analysis indicated that the power to detect the observed difference in success rate between the anterolateral (96.7%) and anterior (86.7%) approaches was approximately 31.5%, and between anterolateral and lateral (83.3%) was approximately 45.5% (alpha = 0.05, Chi‐square test). While these values fall below the conventional 80% threshold, the consistent trend favoring the anterolateral approach suggests a potential clinical advantage warranting further investigation in larger trials.

Puncture Path Length

The anterolateral approach resulted in a significantly shorter puncture path length (57.77 ± 1.295 mm) compared to the lateral (63.33 ± 1.295 mm) and anterior (55.53 ± 1.295 mm) approaches (p = 0.000). Post hoc analysis indicated a notably shorter path for the anterolateral compared to the lateral approach (p = 0.004), facilitating a smoother procedure (Figure 4B).

Procedure Time

Linear mixed‐effects models confirmed significantly shorter procedure time for the anterolateral approach (mean = 72.87 s, SE = 1.76) vs. anterior (87.20 s, SE = 3.75; β = −14.33 s, 95% CI: −26.92 to −1.74, p = 0.022) and lateral (92.80 s, SE = 7.12; β = −19.93 s, 95% CI: −32.51 to −7.35, p = 0.001). The reduced operation time reflects the efficiency of the optimized anterolateral parameters, and significant differences were observed between the anterolateral and anterior (p = 0.021) and lateral (p = 0.002) approaches (Figure 4C). After Bonferroni correction (α = 0.017), the anterolateral approach remained significantly faster than both anterior (p = 0.021 < 0.017) and lateral approaches (p = 0.002 < 0.017).

VAS Pain Scores

Pain intensity during the procedures was lower with the anterolateral approach (1.77 ± 0.94), compared to the anterior (2.33 ± 1.90) and lateral (2.90 ± 2.17) approaches. Although the overall differences were not significant (p = 0.051), post hoc tests revealed a significant decrease in pain intensity in the anterolateral approach compared to the lateral approach (p = 0.015), suggesting an improved patient experience (Figure 4D). For VAS scores, the anterolateral vs. lateral difference was no longer significant after correction (original p = 0.015 → adjusted p = 0.045 > 0.017).

Complications

One patient experienced numbness on the anterolateral aspect of the thigh during puncture using the anterior approach, which radiated to the same‐side knee joint. The numbness resolved 30 min after completing the injection. No complications were observed in other patients.

Discussion

Summary of Main Findings

This randomized crossover trial demonstrates that the anterolateral approach for hip joint puncture offers significant advantages in procedural efficiency compared to standard anterior and lateral approaches. Key findings include a higher success rate (96.7% vs. 86.7% anterior, 83.3% lateral), significantly shorter procedure time (mean 72.87 s vs. 87.20s anterior, 92.80s lateral; p < 0.017), and reduced puncture path length (57.77 vs. 63.33 mm lateral; p = 0.004). The anterolateral approach also showed lower patient‐reported VAS pain scores (1.77) compared to the lateral approach (2.90) and was associated with fewer neurovascular complications (0 cases vs. 1 LFCN injury with anterior approach). These results collectively suggest enhanced procedural efficiency, patient comfort, and safety with the anterolateral technique.

Comparison of Efficacy Outcomes

While the higher success rate of the anterolateral approach (96.7%) did not reach statistical significance compared to anterior (86.7%) and lateral (83.3%) approaches (p = 0.328), the effect sizes suggest potential clinical relevance. The 13.4% absolute increase in success rate over the lateral approach (number needed to treat [NNT] = 7.5) implies that for every 8 procedures, one additional successful puncture may be achieved using the anterolateral approach—reducing repeat attempts, patient discomfort, and resource use. Similarly, the 10% advantage over the anterior approach (NNT = 10) aligns with literature reporting clinically meaningful thresholds for procedural success [21, 22]. Combined with its significant reductions in procedure time (mean difference: 19.9 s vs. lateral; p < 0.017), the anterolateral approach demonstrates compelling efficiency gains.

Anatomical and Technical Advantages of the Anterolateral Approach

The superior performance of the anterolateral approach stems from its unique anatomical advantages: The puncture site lies lateral to the tensor fasciae latae's medial border, avoiding the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN). This is critical given Rudin et al. [13] classification of LFCN variants (sartorius‐type: 36%; posterior‐type: 32%). In contrast, the anterior approach risks sartorius‐type LFCN injury (1 numbness case in our study), while the lateral approach jeopardizes posterior‐type LFCN [23]. Hip flexion at 20° expands the anterior capsular space by 42% (our ultrasound data, Figure 2B), enabling a shorter (57.8 vs. lateral 63.3 mm, p = 0.004) and more perpendicular needle path (45°). This contrasts with the steep angles (> 60°) required in anterior approaches, which impair needle control [14]. Targeting the femoral head–neck junction centralizes the needle within the “safe zone” for capsular access [19], minimizing labral contact (0% vs. lateral approach's 10% labral contact in our study) (Table 2).

Safety Profile and Clinical Relevance

The complex anatomical location of the hip joint significantly undermines the safety and precision of intra‐articular injections [25, 26, 27]. When selecting a puncture site from the anterior to the lateral aspects of the hip, it is crucial to minimize the risk of damage to the femoral and sciatic nerves while carefully considering the trajectory of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) [28, 29]. Our clinical results align with and extend Mikula et al. [29] cadaveric findings. They reported a mean LFCN distance of 23.4 ± 6.1 mm for anterolateral punctures (vs. 19.3 ± 7.9 mm for mid‐anterior), corroborating our zero nerve injuries. While Mikula emphasized angle variability as a limitation, our 3D simulation‐guided 45° insertion standardized needle trajectory, achieving 96.7% success vs. their 92%. This highlights the value of preoperative planning in clinical practice. Cadaveric studies cannot assess real‐time efficiency metrics (e.g., procedure time). Our data demonstrate the anterolateral approach's operational superiority in living patients, reducing time by 19.9 s vs. lateral approach (p < 0.001). The anterolateral approach has a greater safety advantage due to its distance from the LFCN, while the mid‐anterior approach relies on the surgeon's experience in preventing nerve injury (the average distance from the needle tip to the LFCN is 19.3 ± 7.9 mm, range: 11.8–30 mm). Iatrogenic injury to the LFCN can lead to adverse outcomes, such as sensory disturbances, including burning sensations, pain, and paraesthesia in the anterolateral thigh region [30, 31].

The 3.3% LFCN injury rate with our anterior approach aligns with clinical literature (Table 3), but risk is modifiable through: (1) Anatomical Awareness: Sartorius‐type LFCN (36% prevalence [13]) crosses the anterior approach's target zone (Figure 1A). In such variants, the nerve lies within 5 mm of the needle path [26], explaining our patient's transient thigh numbness. (2) BMI‐Dependent Risk: Cadaveric studies show adipose tissue obscures LFCN landmarks in BMI > 25 patients [28]. Our cohort (mean BMI 22.76) had lower risk; populations with higher BMI may experience > 5% injury rates. (3) Technical Refinements: Lateralizing the needle insertion point by 10 mm reduces LFCN contact risk by 83% [28]. However, this increases femoral nerve proximity, creating a “safety trade‐off”. In contrast, the anterolateral approach's 0% complication rate reflects its inherent safety: The puncture site (lateral to TFL medial border) avoids all LFCN variants (Figure 3B); 3D modeling confirms a > 20 mm buffer zone from neurovascular structures [27]. This makes it the optimal choice for high‐risk patients (obesity, variant anatomy).

Mei‐Dan et al. [12] investigated the precision and safety of the anterior approach in 55 patients, reporting a high success rate of 93%, with 51 successful punctures and no complications. On the contrary, our findings indicated a slightly lower success rate (86.7%) for the anterior approach. Although this method offers the shortest puncture path, one patient experienced an adverse reaction due to LFCN injury [34], emphasizing the need for avoiding the medial border of the tensor fasciae latae, particularly given the presence of the sartorius‐type LFCN in 36% of patients [13]. Furthermore, inserting the needle in the lateral margin of the sartorius muscle imposes significant risks due to its proximity to the femoral artery, vein, and nerve, leading to potential neurovascular injury [23]. This concern has been consistently reported by Leopold et al. [23] who conducted a cadaveric study with 30 hip joints from 15 cadavers. They found that 27% of punctures led to femoral nerve damage, with 60% of needles positioned within 5 mm of the nerve. Leopold et al. concluded that the lateral approach offers better neurovascular safety than the anterior approach.

Despite various adaptations of the lateral approach targeting the superior aspect of the greater trochanter [24], this region is frequently associated with posterior‐type LFCN, which can be observed in 32% of patients, posing a persistent risk of nerve damage [32, 35]. Our study indicated that the lateral approach, compared to anterior and anterolateral techniques, increases the path length and procedure duration. Additionally, variability in femoral neck‐shaft angles among patients elevated the risks associated with the lateral approach, potentially damaging the acetabular labrum or worsening existing labral injuries. Ultrasound assessments in three patients confirmed that the needle tip inadvertently reached the 12 o'clock position on the acetabular labrum, increasing patients' discomfort and VAS pain scores. Yaron Bar Ziv et al. [16] used methylene blue dye injection to assess puncture accuracy during surgery, which increases concerns about its efficacy, as it cannot reliably indicate the exact position of the needle tip, potentially obscuring labral injuries that might still be detectable during surgery.

Our findings suggest that the anterolateral approach significantly reduces the risk of injury to non‐fan‐type LFCNs. By targeting the midpoint between the ASIS vertical line and the anterior femoral edge as the entry point, the anterolateral approach ensures that the puncture site is positioned lateral to the medial border of the tensor fasciae latae. This strategic positioning minimizes the risk of nerve injury and improves localization during the procedure, thereby improving overall efficiency. Establishing the puncture plane with precision is essential to standardize the procedure and mitigate the effect of individual anatomical variations on outcomes. In our study, most procedural failures associated with the anterior and lateral approaches were linked to such anatomical differences.

The use of 3D data models to simulate puncture positioning helped identify an optimal needle insertion angle of 45° within the puncture plane, facilitating accurate targeting. This technique was applied for 30 patients undergoing the anterolateral approach, resulting in successful needle placement in the anterior joint capsule space at the femoral head–neck junction in 29 patients, with only one failure due to deviation during needle insertion.

Methodological Considerations and Future Directions

Senn et al. defined the cross‐over design as a design in which each subject receives all treatments, aiming to study differences among the treatment methods [36, 37]. A major advantage of randomized crossover trials, a specific type of randomized controlled trial, is that they assess the effects of different interventions on the same patients, thus reducing differences between subjects [38, 39]. The prerequisite of a cross‐over trial design is that each treatment must meet the assumption of no carry‐over effect, meaning the effects of the first treatment should not persist in the second stage [40]. Cross‐over trials include a washout period to fulfill this assumption. This study aimed to investigate the success rate, precision, immediate pain, puncture time, and length of the puncture path between the three different puncture approaches. None of the observed indicators showed carry‐over effects. Additionally, a one‐week washout period was scheduled between each puncture, satisfying the requirements of a cross‐over trial design. While a one‐week washout period may be insufficient to eliminate carry‐over effects from corticosteroids if evaluating long‐term therapeutic outcomes it is fully adequate for this study's objectives. Our endpoints measured immediate procedural characteristics (e.g., success, time, depth, acute pain), which are unaffected by prior drug exposure. The absence of pharmacological confounding is further ensured by: (a) Ultrasound confirmation of needle placement before drug injection; (b) VAS assessment within minutes post‐puncture; (c) real‐time recording of time/depth.

Limitations and Strengths

Although the crossover trial design can minimize differences between subjects, there may be an interaction between treatment sequence and intervention. The primary limitation of this study is the sample size. While the randomized crossover design enhanced efficiency and within‐patient comparisons, the total number of procedures (n = 90) provided robust data on procedural metrics; the number of patients (N = 30, n = 5 per sequence group) limited the statistical power to detect differences in success rates, as confirmed by our post hoc power analysis. This study should therefore be interpreted as providing preliminary evidence and strong trends, particularly regarding success rate. The significant findings on procedure time and path length demonstrate adequate power for those efficiency outcomes. Future larger multicenter randomized trials, potentially using parallel‐group designs focused specifically on comparing success/safety rates between the anterolateral and one standard approach (e.g., lateral), are needed for definitive confirmation. The single‐center design also limits generalizability.

This study has notable strengths in methodology and study design. The randomized Williams crossover design, with each patient receiving all three puncture approaches (anterolateral, anterior, and lateral) in randomized sequences, minimized inter‐subject variability and enhanced the reliability of within‐patient comparisons. The use of anatomical landmark‐guided blind puncture, combined with ultrasound confirmation of needle placement, ensured procedural consistency and accurate outcome assessment. In addition, the study was focused on multiple clinically relevant outcomes, including success rate, procedural time, puncture path length, and patient‐reported VAS pain scores. These outcomes provided a comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy and safety of the anterolateral approach. The incorporation of 3D modeling and ultrasound data to optimize the anterolateral approach's parameters further strengthened this study's methodology. In summary, these strengths enable the study to offer valuable insights for clinical practice and future research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the comparative analysis showed that compared to the anterior and lateral approaches, the anterolateral approach can significantly improve procedural efficiency and patients' comfort. These findings have important clinical implications, suggesting that the anterolateral approach may improve patients' experience during hip joint puncture. Future studies should validate these techniques through larger RCTs and potentially standardize the available protocols for hip joint interventions.
