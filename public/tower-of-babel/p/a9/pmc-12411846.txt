Sex Differences in Ketogenic Diet Response Reveal Gonadal Hormone Interaction With FGF21 in Mice
Authors: Yuan Zhang, Jesse D Cochran, Rhonda A Souvenir, Warren Tai, Ruiyou Xia, Brendan S Gladwin, Natalie B Barton, Renata O Pereira, E Dale Abel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough indicated as adjunctive therapy for seizure disorders, ketogenic diets (KDs) have gained popularity for weight loss and mitigating the metabolic risks associated with severe obesity. However, efficacy, durability, and long-term consequences are incompletely understood. In preclinical models, most studies have included only male mice, precluding an understanding of sex-specific responses to KD. In this study, we investigated sex differences in response to a high-fat, low carbohydrate, low-protein KD using male and female C57BL/6J mice. Despite equivalent circulating levels of β-hydroxybutyrate, male mice exhibited weight loss characterized by loss of fat mass and lean mass in concert with increased energy expenditure. In contrast, female mice exhibited increased fat mass and body weight on the KD. Male mice manifested increased insulin sensitivity, without reducing glucose excursions during glucose tolerance testing, in concert with decreased glucose-stimulated insulin release. In contrast, females developed glucose intolerance and insulin resistance relative to control females. Following oophorectomy, female mice lost weight on KD but remained glucose intolerant. Orchidectomy in male mice reversed weight loss in KD males. Circulating fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) concentrations were increased in males but not females on KD and correlated with increased FGF21 expression in brown adipose tissue. These findings demonstrate that the metabolic effects of KD are sex-specific and suggest that gonadal hormones modulate the adaptive response to ketogenic diets via FGF21 signaling.

Obesity and diabetes represent critical health challenges in contemporary society. Over the past 2 decades, obesity rates in US adults have surged from 31% to 42%. Disturbingly, projections indicate that nearly half of all Americans could be classified as obese by 2030 [1, 2]. Simultaneously, obesity-related diseases—including diabetes, cardiovascular disorders, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)—have seen a parallel increase. The economic burden of obesity is also considerable, inflating annual medical care costs in the United States by a staggering $315.8 billion [2, 3]. Thus, effective prevention strategies and targeted treatments are urgently needed.

Numerous dietary approaches have been proposed to enhance health outcomes. Among these, the ketogenic diet (KD) has gained some popularity for managing obesity and diabetes. Originally introduced in the early 1900s as a therapeutic intervention for epilepsy, KD has experienced a resurgence in recent years due to its potential benefits in other conditions, including obesity, diabetes, and certain cancers [4-6]. Additionally, studies have revealed that KD may extend lifespan and enhance cognitive function in rodent models [7, 8]. However, recent research has also highlighted potential adverse effects, such as increased aging markers in rodent kidneys and hearts [9].

The KD, by mimicking a state of caloric restriction, may induce the expression of fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), a hepatokine that orchestrates adaptive responses to metabolic stress [10]. Primarily secreted by the liver as a stress hormone, FGF21 plays a pivotal role in regulating systemic metabolic homeostasis. This hormone enhances carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, modulates energy expenditure and macronutrient preferences, and contributes to weight loss, largely through its ability to improve glucose and lipid homeostasis. FGF21 expression and secretion are dynamically regulated by diverse physiological and environmental factors, including metabolic state, dietary composition, hormonal signaling, and nutrient availability. Central to its regulation is peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor α (PPARα), a transcription factor critical for hepatic FGF21 induction during fasting and ketosis [10, 11]. Nutritional interventions—such as high-carbohydrate diets, low-protein regimens, or methionine restriction—also robustly stimulate hepatic FGF21 production [12, 13]. Furthermore, hormonal mediators like glucagon, insulin, and thyroid hormone directly modulate FGF21 transcription and release, underscoring its integrative role in metabolic adaptation [13-15].

Despite an abundant literature in preclinical models supporting beneficial effects of KD, most of these studies have exclusively used male mice. With this limitation, there remains a need to ascertain whether the findings of these prior studies can be extrapolated to both sexes. This study sought to investigate the sex-specific effects of KD on metabolic and physiological outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Unless stated otherwise, studies were performed in wild-type C57Bl/6J mice that were procured from Jackson Laboratories and selectively bred for the study. After weaning (at age 21 days), mice were randomly assigned to either a normal control diet (CD) or a KD and were maintained on this diet for 15-weeks. The CD consisted of Teklad Global Diet 2920X (16% fat, 24% protein, and 60% carbohydrates by caloric intake), while the KD was based on AIN-76A-Modified from Bio-Serv (93% fat, 5% protein, and 2% carbohydrates). After 12 week of KD feeding, a subset of mice was placed in the Promethion system (Sable Systems International) to measure changes in energy expenditure and locomotor activity for 5 days as previously described [16]. Prior to being humanely killed, animals were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane. Heart, skeletal muscle, liver, and brown adipose tissue (BAT) were removed, weighed, and then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Serum samples were prepared as described in “Serum Sample Preparation and Analysis.” A portion of liver tissue was preserved for histology, while the rest and the interscapular BAT depot were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. FGF21 homozygous germline knockout (KO) mice, kindly provided by Dr Matthew Potthoff, were used in a survival study, with KD feeding initiated at age 8 weeks.

Ovariectomy and Castration Surgery Procedures

Following weaning at age 3 weeks, female wild-type C57Bl/6J mice were randomly assigned to ovariectomy (OVX) or sham groups, while male mice (mixed background) were assigned to castration (CAS) or sham groups. During surgery, mice were initially anesthetized based on body weight. Bilateral medial incisions on the dorsal side allowed access to the ovaries. OVX mice had their ovaries excised, while sham mice had their ovaries exposed without removal. The fallopian tubes were returned to their original position, and incisions were sutured. For CAS surgery, the scrotal area was shaved and aseptically prepared. A midline scrotal incision (∼1 cm) was made, and both testes were exteriorized and removed. Remaining tissue was returned to the scrotum, and the incision was closed with sutures. Post surgery, mice received 100 μL of saline and 2 boluses of buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg) for pain management. Ketogenic diet feeding began between 5 to 7 days after surgery, depending on individual recovery status.

Glucose and Insulin Tolerance Test

Glucose and insulin tolerance tests were performed in the same mice, as described previously [17]. A glucose tolerance test was performed first and the interval between the 2 tests was 1 week. In brief, after a 6-hour fast, mice were administered glucose (1 mg D-glucose/g body weight) intraperitoneally. Blood glucose levels were measured at specific intervals (0 minutes, 5 minutes, 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 45 minutes, 60 minutes, and 120 minutes) after injection. The blood for the glucose tolerance test and insulin tolerance test was collected by massaging the tail-vein after a tail-clip. The first drop was discarded, and the second drop was placed directly onto the glucose test strip that was inserted in the glucometer. Blood glucose was determined using a glucometer (Glucometer Elite; Bayer). Glucose tolerance test data were quantified by calculating area under the glucose curve. Insulin tolerance test was performed after a 2-hour fast by injecting insulin (0.5 mU/g body weight; Humulin, Eli Lilly) intraperitoneally. Blood glucose levels were monitored at 0 minutes, 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 45 minutes, 60 minutes, and 90 minutes post injection, and concentrations were normalized to baseline. Insulin tolerance test data were quantified by calculating area under the glucose curve.

Body Composition Analysis by Nuclear Magnetic Resonance

Conscious low-resolution nuclear magnetic resonance imaging was used to measure body composition of each mouse biweekly using the Bruker minispec NF-90 (Bruker) housed in the Metabolic Phenotyping Core at the University of Iowa. Body fat and lean body mass were derived as previously described [18].

Serum Sample Preparation and Analysis

Blood samples were collected via tail bleeding with the aid of heparin-coated capillary tubes. A 200-μL pipette was then used to expel the blood on the open end of the capillary tube into an Eppendorf tube and allowed to clot for 15 minutes at room temperature. Samples were centrifuged for 10 minutes at 15 000 revolutions per minute, and the supernatant (serum) was collected in a new set of Eppendorf tubes. Serum samples were frozen at −80 °C until use. On the day of analysis, samples were thawed on ice and prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions. Circulating insulin, ketone, and FGF21 were determined by commercial assay kits (CrystalChem 90080, Sigma-Aldrich MAK041 and BioVendor R&D RD291108200R) following manufacturers' instructions. The intra-assay and interassay coefficients of variation for the β-hydroxybutyrate (β-HB) assay were 3.68% to 4.05% and 3.03% to 3.13%, respectively. The mouse insulin and FGF21 kits are based on enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. These kits have intra-assay and interassay coefficients of variation of less than 10%.

RNA Isolation and Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was isolated from liver and BAT using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocols. RNA concentration was quantified with the Nanodrop spectrophotometer. Complementary DNA libraries were prepared from total RNA using the Applied Biosystems High-Capacity Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems 4368814). In detail, the time and temperature for real-time reaction was 25 °C for 10 minutes, 37 °C for 120 minutes, 85 °C for 5 minutes, and hold at 4 °C. Transcript expression was then collected using the standard curve method with Power SYBR green PCR master mix (Applied Biosystems 4367759) in a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System, 384 well (Applied Biosystems). CopS6 was used as a housekeeping normalizer gene. Primer sequences are specified in Table 1.

Histology

Following humane killing, samples were fixed in a 10% zinc formalin solution overnight. Subsequently, a progressive dehydration process using gradient ethanol solutions was carried out. The samples were then embedded in paraffin to be cut in 5-μm sections. Slides were stained with Masson Trichrome using standard procedures. Imaging of Trichrome stained sections were obtained using an Aperio Ariol Digital Slide Scanner (Leica) and analyzed using NIH ImageJ software.

Statistical Analysis

All data values reported are means ± SEM. All 4-group analyses were subjected to 2-way analysis of variance following the Fisher least significant difference comparisons. P values less than or equal to .05 were considered statistically significant. All residuals from 2-way analysis of variance were examined using Q-Q plots. The linearity in Q-Q plots was used to confirm normal distribution. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Software (version 10.4.1 for Mac, www.graphpad.com). Metabolic cage data collected from the Promethion system were analyzed using analysis of covariance in CalR [19].

Results

Differential Metabolic Phenotypes in Male and Female Mice in Response to Ketogenic Diet Feeding

Male and female C57Bl/6J mice were randomly assigned to normal CD or KD groups, starting at age 4 weeks. Circulating concentrations of the ketone β-HB were slightly higher in females on CD relative to males, but were markedly increased by KD in both sexes, achieving similar concentrations in male and female mice (Fig. 1A). During the 12 weeks of feeding, male mice on KD gained less weight than those on CD, while female mice on KD gained more weight (Fig. 1B-1D). To discern whether these weight changes were due to changes in fat or lean mass, we noninvasively assessed body composition by nuclear magnetic resonance every 2 weeks. Notably, there were no significant differences in lean mass between female CD and KD groups but male mice on KD exhibited reduced lean mass (Fig. 1E). Male mice in the KD group had reduced fat mass relative to those in the CD group, whereas female mice in the KD group exhibited increased fat mass (Fig. 1F). Glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity tests performed 8 weeks after KD feeding revealed sex differences in glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity. In male mice KD feeding was associated with a modest worsening of glucose tolerance despite increased insulin sensitivity relative to male CD mice (Fig. 1G-1J). In contrast, glucose tolerance was impaired to a greater extent in females in concert with impaired insulin sensitivity (Fig. 1G-1J). Baseline and glucose-stimulated insulin concentrations were decreased in male mice by KD feeding but were increased in female mice in response to KD feeding (Fig. 1K and 1L). Insulin signaling in skeletal muscle was assessed in a separate cohort of mice fed a CD and KD for 18 weeks. On the CD, insulin marginally increased protein kinase B phosphorylation in male and female mice. In contrast, on the KD, there was a difference in insulin stimulated protein kinase B phosphorylation fold change, which was approximately 2-fold greater in males relative to the fold change observed in KD females (Supplementary Fig. 1) [20]. Intriguingly, KD induced hepatic fibrosis in male and female mice (Fig. 1M and 1N).

After 12 weeks of KD feeding, a subset of mice was assessed in the Promethion metabolic system to evaluate energy expenditure, water intake, and locomotor activity. Food intake measurements was precluded given the formulation of the KD chow as a paste. Analysis of covariance revealed a significant increase in energy expenditure in the Male_KD group (Fig. 1O), potentially contributing to their reduced body weight. VO₂ and VCO₂ were also elevated in Male_KD mice compared to Male_CD mice, while Female_KD mice exhibited higher VO₂ than Female_CD controls (Supplementary Fig. 2A and 2B) [20]. Female_KD and Male_KD groups both had significantly lower respiratory quotient values than their respective CD controls (Supplementary Fig. 2C) [20], consistent with increased lipid utilization. Physical activity was also significantly elevated in Male_KD mice relative to Male_CD mice (Supplementary Fig. 2D) [20]. Collectively, these results demonstrate sex-specific metabolic adaptations to KD, characterized by enhanced energy expenditure and lipid utilization, particularly in male mice.

Oophorectomized Female Mice Show Similar Ketogenic Diet–Induced Metabolic Phenotypes as Male Mice

To investigate whether sex differences were mediated by differences in gonadal hormones, oophorectomy was performed in female mice at age 3 weeks. KD feeding was initiated 1 week later and lasted for 15 weeks. At the time of death, uterus weight was significantly decreased in OVX females (Fig. 2A and 2B), consistent with loss of estrogen. β-HB concentrations were increased to a similar extent following KD in sham and OVX females after 15 weeks of KD feeding (Fig. 2C). On CD, OVX females gained more weight and accrued greater fat mass than sham operated mice (Fig. 2D-2F). On KD the degree of weight gain and fat mass accrual in OVX mice was attenuated relative to OVX CD mice (see Fig. 2D-2F). Importantly, OVX KD mice did not gain more weight than sham KD mice (see Fig. 2D-2F). No significant difference in lean mass was observed (Fig. 2G). Glucose tolerance tests performed 8 weeks after feeding revealed impaired glucose tolerance in OVX female mice on KD compared to OVX female on CD (Fig. 2H and 2I). To ascertain potential contributions of male sex hormones on the metabolic response to KD feeding, another cohort of male mice on a mixed genetic background were subjected to orchidectomy (CAS). After 15 weeks of KD feeding, the CAS males gained more weight than the CAS males on CD chow (Supplementary Fig. 3A) [20]. Glucose tolerance test performed 8 weeks after feeding revealed worsening glucose tolerance in CAS male mice on KD compared to CAS male on CD (Supplementary Fig. 3B and 3C) [20]. These data mirror the metabolic responses of female mice to the KD, underscoring a role for gonadal hormones in modulating KD responses.

Sex-Dependent Fibroblast Growth Factor 21 Response to Ketogenic Diet in Male and Female Mice

FGF21, a stress-induced hormone, is known to increase following KD feeding. In our study, male and female mice both exhibited elevated FGF21 serum levels after 15 weeks of KD (Fig. 3A). However, the increase in FGF21 was more pronounced in males than females in response to KD (see Fig. 3A). To investigate potential sources of FGF21, messenger RNA expression was determined in the liver and BAT, which represent important sources of circulating FGF21 [18, 21]. There were no differences in hepatic FGF21 between male and female mice on CD or KD (Fig. 3B). However, FGF21 was increased to a greater extent in the BAT of males on KD relative to KD females (Fig. 3C). Following OVX, BAT expression of FGF21 was further augmented by KD feeding (Fig. 3D). These data suggest that BAT could be the source of increased FGF21 in response to KD, and its regulation in this context is sexually dimorphic in an estrogen-dependent manner. To further assess if FGF21 mediated the sex-dependent differences in body weight and metabolic responses to KD, experiments were repeated in mice with germline deletion of FGF21. Unexpectedly, male and female germline FGF21 KO mice could not tolerate chronic KD feeding experiments and manifested early mortality (Fig. 3E), suggesting an essential role for FGF21 in the adaptation to chronic KD feeding.

Discussion

The KD has gained popularity in recent years due to its reported effects on weight loss, blood glucose maintenance, and its potential benefits on increasing longevity and improving cognitive function [5, 7, 8]. However, most preclinical studies reporting these benefits have been limited by the exclusive use of male mice [22, 23]. In this study, we compared the metabolic consequences of KD both in male and female mice. In contrast to the weight loss in male mice, female mice fed a high-fat, low-carbohydrate, low-protein KD gained more weight than those fed a CD, and no improvement in insulin sensitivity was observed, suggesting that circulating estrogens antagonized the potential metabolic benefits of KD feeding. Conversely, OVX female mice exhibited a similar phenotype as male mice when fed a KD, further supporting an essential role for estrogen in mediating sexually dimorphic responses to KD in mice. In males, the attenuation of the phenotype following orchidectomy further implicates gonadal hormones in metabolic adaptation to KD. The finding of increased liver fibrosis following chronic KD feeding was unexpected and was not modulated by sex. This finding raises concern that KD diets could induce liver injury. We did not elucidate the mechanisms for liver injury by KD, which will be the focus of future studies. Although recent studies also suggested KD feeding could increase aging markers in the heart and kidney [9], we did not observe significant changes in heart function in either male or female mice after chronic KD feeding in this model although heart weights were increased relative to CD mice in females (Supplementary Fig. 4) [20].

The sex differences observed may be due to differences in the modulation of FGF21. FGF21 is a stress hormone induced by diverse mitochondrial and metabolic stress [10, 18, 23-25]. FGF21 promotes ketogenesis in the liver during fasting or low-carbohydrate conditions. Additionally, FGF21 administration enhances adiponectin production and secretion via PPARγ in adipose tissue, amplifying PPARγ's insulin-sensitizing effects and stimulating white adipose tissue browning. FGF21 further increases energy expenditure by activating BAT and inducing beiging of white adipose tissue [26, 27].

We observed that high circulating FGF21 levels correlated with greater body weight loss. Several published studies have shown that pharmacological FGF21 administration may mediate sex-dependent metabolic phenotypes [28-30]. In our study, the KD-induced weight loss correlated with higher circulating FGF21 levels in male mice relative to female mice. Although previous studies on normal diet revealed higher circulating FGF21 concentrations in females that were attributed to the stimulation FGF21 release by estradiol in the liver under fasting conditions [31], we observed increased FGF21 in male mice under KD feeding status. Thus, KD may override any potential effect of estrogen on hepatic FGF21 generation.

Other studies have revealed that the relationship between sex and the metabolic response to FGF21 is complex and context dependent. For example, in OVX obese female mice fed a high-sugar, high-fat diet, FGF21 regulated certain metabolic parameters (eg, body weight, adipose weight, and glucose tolerance) independently of estradiol [32]. Another study identified FGF21 as a mediator of sex-dependent responses to dietary changes [33]. While KD increased FGF21 both in male and female mice, the whole-body metabolic regulation likely involves additional factors that are mediated in part by gonadal hormones. Future studies will be needed to dissect mechanisms for the crosstalk between gonadal hormones and FGF21 such as sexually dimorphic roles in the maintenance of muscle mass and adipose tissue expansion under the conditions used in this study.

While FGF21 is primarily secreted by the liver, it is also expressed and often secreted by other tissues, including BAT and skeletal muscle in response to fasting, feeding, protein restriction, and cold exposure [10, 11, 18, 21, 24, 25, 34, 35]. FGF21 enhances insulin sensitivity by promoting glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue to improve glucose tolerance. In the present study, BAT-associated FGF21 correlated with circulating FGF21 levels. Despite clear evidence of improved insulin sensitivity in male mice on KD, glucose tolerance was not improved. Indeed, the insulin response to glucose was blunted, raising the possibility that KD induced β-cell dysfunction.

Beyond energy expenditure and regulation of glucose homeostasis, FGF21 modulates satiety particularly for sugars and alcohol and regulates food and water intake [36-40]. Interestingly, KD has not been shown to significantly raise FGF21 levels in humans, unlike other dietary conditions such as starvation, high sugar intake, or dietary protein restriction [11, 24, 25, 34, 35]. However, the KDs used in human studies may be low carbohydrate and protein restricted without a concomitant increase in fat content, as is the case in our mouse studies. We observed that whole-body FGF21 KO mice failed to survive on a chronic KD, which is likely due to reduced thirst-drive and ketoacidosis, as previous studies reported an essential role for FGF21-regulated water intake in response to short-term KD and alcohol intake [36].

In summary, our study demonstrates that the metabolic effects of KD feeding in mice are modulated by gonadal hormones and occur potentially on the basis of differential induction of FGF21 in BAT. We also show that FGF21 is critical in mice for adaptation to chronic KD feeding. These findings, along with previous research, highlight the complex effects of KD, which include both potential benefits but toxic effects such as hepatic dysfunction and β-cell dysfunction. We also show that these complex effects are influenced by other factors such as circulating gonadal hormones. These considerations could be further confounded by the effect of age, duration of diet, and the existence of other comorbidities [4-6, 9, 25]. These factors should be carefully considered prior to widespread adoption of KD as a dietary intervention for obesity and metabolic disorders. Though we showed that KD feeding increased energy expenditure in males, the limitation of this study is that we were unable to quantify food intake due to the formulation of KD as a paste. Future studies will include food intake measurements with an improved feeding device to account for potential effects of caloric restriction. Our data collected on the mixed genetic background male mice support a potential regulatory role of male gonadal hormones in KD responses, although we cannot rule out the potential contribution of endogenous estrogens that are unmasked when testosterone deficiency is induced in males. Additionally, future studies comparing the effect of a KD in both sexes of other mammalian laboratory species such as rabbits, guinea pigs, or large mammals will be necessary to validate the generalizability of these findings.
