A One Health perspective on the 2025 chikungunya outbreak in Guangdong province, China
Authors: Cheng Guo, Nischay Mishra, Abdel Aouacheria, Cong Tuan Pham, Ernest Konadu Asiedu, Yan Long, George Fu Gao, Jiahai Lu
Published: 2025

GLOBAL CONTEXT OF CHIKUNGUNYA

As of August 12, 2025, Guangdong province has reported over 10,000 confirmed chikungunya cases within just one month, with most cases concentrated in Foshan city. This rapid and unexpected resurgence has drawn significant attention from health authorities both within China and internationally, raising substantial public health concerns.

Chikungunya is a mosquito-borne viral disease caused by the chikungunya virus (CHIKV), an RNA virus in the Alphavirus genus of the Togaviridae family [1]. It typically takes 2–12 days for the onset of symptoms in infected individuals after a bite from the infectious mosquitoes, typically characterized by sudden high fever and joint pain. Although the case fatality rate of chikungunya is below 0.1%, its sequelae—particularly persistent arthralgia—pose a significant burden [2]. Approximately 30%–40% of those infected develop chronic arthritis, with symptoms lasting weeks or even months, resulting in reduced productivity and imposing high social and economic burdens.

Since CHIKV was first identified in Tanzania in 1952, autochthonous transmission has been reported in 119 countries across the world, placing about 2.8 billion people at risk, primarily in areas south of the Tropic of Cancer [3]. In 2025, the global chikungunya cases had already surpassed 220,000, with notable outbreaks reported in America, Africa, and Asia [4]. Europe also faces risks of local transmission, exemplified by recent outbreaks in southern France, particularly in the Montpellier region. Concurrently, the current Guangdong outbreak marks the largest in China since the first local outbreak in Dongguan, another city in Guangdong province, in 2010 [5]. These developments together signal a potential change in the global pattern of Chikungunya spread, underscoring the urgent need for enhanced surveillance in unexplained febrile cases/outbreaks, mosquito population monitoring, and effective vector-control strategies, especially in regions like Europe and East Asia.

Complex drivers of transmission

Unlike a typical zoonotic disease, such as the Nipah virus, with a probable origin from bats that spilled over into humans, chikungunya's transmission involves more intricate ecological dynamics (Figure 1). As the primary vector, Aedes aegypti facilitates CHIKV transmission through its daytime biting behavior and adaptation to urban environments with abundant breeding sites. In contrast to Africa, where sylvatic cycles are maintained by nonhuman primates, urban settings in China lack such reservoirs, making human viremia the main source of infection for mosquitoes. Vertical transmission in mosquitoes, though less efficient, plays a role in sustaining CHIKV during interepidemic periods. More importantly, environmental factors, including warm temperatures, high humidity, and urbanization, exacerbated by climate change, create ideal breeding conditions for mosquitoes. Socioeconomic challenges, such as low herd immunity to chikungunya, limited healthcare access, poor sanitation, and low public awareness, further increase transmission risks. Lastly, although two chikungunya vaccines have been approved, the vaccines are predominantly manufactured and deployed by developed countries, with limited vaccine availability in endemic regions, like Southeast Asia and Africa. The One Health imperative

The One Health approach promotes collaboration across human, animal, plant, and environmental health sectors to improve global health outcomes. In 2022, the Quadripartite Alliance (comprising Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations [FAO], United Nations Environment Programme [UNEP], World Organisation for Animal Health [WOAH], and World Health Organization [WHO]) established a collaborative framework to address emerging health threats at the human–animal–plant–environment interface across multiple governance levels. It represents a major step forward in implementing One Health globally. Regionally, Europe has made progress through sectoral programs, research networks, and thematic action plans [6]. In parallel, China has established the One Health framework through initiatives such as the 697th Xiangshan Science Conference consensus and other related protocols [[7], [8], [9]]. These efforts highlight the need for cross-regional collaboration, enhanced public health infrastructure, the establishment of regional surveillance networks, and shared information and resources. Together, they provide a strong foundation for a science-based and comprehensive response to public health challenges.

Strategic recommendations

These years, Guangdong province has experienced increases in imported and locally transmitted mosquito-borne diseases, notably the dengue outbreak in 2024 and the current chikungunya outbreak in 2025. To effectively curb their transmission, a comprehensive One Health approach is crucial. It involves active surveillance and diagnosis, climate-informed vector monitoring and control, vaccine deployment, and regional collaboration. Firstly, developing a surveillance system that monitors disease cases, mosquito populations, and environmental factors is critical. An ample supply of multiplexed molecular and serological diagnostics enables accurate and rapid case identification, with health facilities reporting to the local Centers for Disease Control. Secondly, integrated vector management (IVM) for Aedes mosquitoes remains the primary measure for chikungunya containment during this outbreak. This includes cleaning water containers, proper waste disposal, larvicide use, and innovative methods like Wolbachia symbiosis and sterile insect techniques [10]. Collaboration with environmental and sanitation agencies is also vital for effective IVM. Thirdly, prioritizing vaccine approval and stockpiling for high-risk groups, travelers, and vulnerable populations is crucial despite the challenges [11]. Lastly, building cross-border One Health platforms across China and neighboring or interconnected countries will facilitate knowledge sharing, eliminate sectoral silos, and enhance international coordination to combat arboviral threats. What unfolds today in China holds direct relevance for Europe, as researchers across the Mediterranean are now investigating patterns of vector-borne emergence that were once the domain of studies in Asia, Africa, and Latin America.

Lessons learned

Guangdong's unique geographical, climatic, and demographic features offer valuable insights into the complex interactions between ecosystems and emerging arboviral diseases. While the current outbreak in Guangdong appears to be largely contained, with declining cases in core areas, the risk of imported chikungunya cases remains high due to extensive international travel and the ongoing global spread. It underscores the multifaceted nature of chikungunya transmission and highlights the difficulty of fully breaking transmission chains. However, compared to regions where chikungunya outbreaks have stabilized, Guangdong's One Health interventions have demonstrated notable success as well, showing their potential to disrupt transmission in large urban areas. In summary, we believe that sustainable prevention and control require a comprehensive One Health approach that integrates environmental management, vector control, community involvement, and international cooperation. By overcoming disciplinary and administrative barriers, a unified health ecosystem can be built to provide resilient protection against chikungunya and other emerging infectious diseases.
