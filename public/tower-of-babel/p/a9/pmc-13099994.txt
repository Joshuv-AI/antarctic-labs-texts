Green space suitability assessment for sustainable urban development using geospatial technology in Eka Tafo, Ethiopia
Authors: Dechasa Diriba, Ebassa Dugasa Leta, Degu Demise, Melion Kasahun, Shankar Karuppannan, Negedie Abrha Gebreslassie, Tesfaye Lemma, Mesay Mengistu Feyisa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban green spaces offer several economic, social, and health benefits to residents. This study was conducted to identify the appropriate locations of green spaces in the Eka Tafo Sub-City using geospatial technologies, namely Geographic Information System (GIS) and Remote Sensing (RS). Eight parameters were evaluated: slope, normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), proximity to rivers, elevation, land use, soil type, proximity to highways, and population density. The Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) was used to assign weights to each factor. The green space suitability map was generated by integrating all the thematic layers in ArcGIS. The green space map identifies five suitability zones: highly suitable, suitable, moderately suitable, poorly suitable, and unsuitable. The findings showed that approximately 30.7% of the study area was classified as suitable to highly suitable for green space, whereas 40.7% was considered unsuitable to poorly suitable. These results enable policymakers to determine the open green spaces that enhance the environmental quality of urban areas and the living standards of residents.Keywords: AHP, GIS, Eka Tafo, Remote sensing, Urban green spaceSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Urbanization has significantly changed global landscapes, with over 50% of the world’s population currently living in cities, expected to reach 6.3 billion by 20501, with sub-Saharan African countries experiencing the fastest growth rates. The UN-Habitat2 is concerned that urbanization in Ethiopia is increasing at an annual rate of over 5%, mainly due to rural-to-urban migration. Rapid urbanization requires a sacrifice of the environment, particularly the decrease in urban green areas3, which is crucial for reducing urban heat islands, increasing resilience to climate change, and promoting the well-being of the population.

In addition to their visible aesthetics, urban green spaces offer many social, economic, and health advantages to residents. Pasanen, et al.4 noted that individuals residing close to parks or forests are less stressed than those residing in places with few greeneries. Similarly, Park, et al.5 indicated that individuals who visit parks during the day experience reduced stress. Environmentally, Urban green spaces improve air quality6, support biodiversity conservation by increasing native and rare flora and fauna within city environments7, and reduce surrounding air temperature, radiant heat, and overall heat stress, particularly during hot, dry periods8.

Economically, urban green spaces empower the jobless by creating employment opportunities, diversifying income sources, and reducing poverty9. Moreover, urban green spaces attract tourists, stimulating the local economy through visitor spending on amenities such as entrance fees, guided tours and food services10. Despite these advantages, rapid urbanization11, poor planning, economic pressures, and lack of stakeholder involvement tend to cause the depletion or inefficient management of green spaces12. The rapid increase in population and the growth of people in demand for land to construct housing facilities have also led to a decrease in the size of the remaining open spaces, which may jeopardize their accessibility13 or availability14,15. In addition, urban planners do not pay much attention to green spaces in the preliminary planning stage, which contributes to their further degradation16. The current reduction in the size of urban green areas highlights the importance of innovative and evidence-based approaches to assist in making better decisions.

Geospatial technologies, particularly Geographic Information Systems (GIS), integrated with Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA), offer powerful solutions for the integration of numerous spatial, environmental, and socio-economic elements into urban green space planning17–19. Through these approaches, planners can examine issues such as land use, slope, access, or population density in a clear, systematic manner to identify ideal sites for urban green spaces. Through these spatially explicit methods, cities can balance competing demands in land use, prioritize areas where green infrastructure should be placed, and contribute to long-term environmental sustainability17. Recently, GIS-based MCDA has been applied in various fields, including selecting suitable sites for agriculture20,21, solar farms22, disease risk modeling23,24, and urban green space suitability analysis18,25–27.

One of the sub-cities of the newly developed Sheger city is Eka Tafo, which is experiencing a high level of urbanization due to housing demand and rural-to-urban migration. The expansion of sub-cities has come at the expense of farmland and open spaces, with previously available urban green areas facing reductions due to the expansion of built-up infrastructure. The lack of a comprehensive planning strategy has also worsened the decrease in the quality and quantity of urban green areas. As a solution to this urgent dilemma in the study area, this study combines both environmental and social aspects by using GIS and MCDA to determine sites most suitable for the development of urban green space or intervention according to accessibility, population needs, and environmental suitability criteria. This study presents a replicable GIS-MCDA model that can facilitate data-driven urban green space planning in fast-growing regions. It provides practical advice to local policymakers to prioritize green spaces in terms of access, population demand, and environmental appropriateness, and to highlight the prospects of the local economic value of tourism and green employment.

Description of the study area

One of the sub-cities in Sheger City is Eka Tafo, which is located between latitudes 9°00′N and 9°08′50″N and longitudes 38°48′E and 38°56′E (Fig. 1). It is situated approximately 25 km northeast of the center of Addis Ababa. Eka Tafo is defined by a flat evolving rolling plateau, with slopes that range from 0° to 48.5°, which is its topographic feature. The area elevation is between 2,278 and 3,151 m above sea level (m a.s.l.). Eka Tafo has a climate that is categorized as Dega-Woinadega with an average annual temperature of about 15.6 °c.

Study area map. The map was created using ArcGIS version 10.8 (https://www.esri.com/en-us/arcgis/products/arcgis-desktop/overview).

High rainfall is experienced in the study area during the rainy season, which occurs between June and September, and the dry season occurs between November and January. Four major land-use classes exist in the area: built-up areas, agricultural, forest, and open land. Among these, built-up areas cover approximately 29.7% of the region, whereas forestland, agricultural land, and open land cover approximately 15.5%, 35%, and 19.8% of the Eka Tafo sub-city, respectively. The study area is currently experiencing rapid urbanization and expansion owing to its accessibility to Addis Ababa, making it a strategic hub for industries, rural settlements, and real estate development. Consequently, the population of the area is increasing at an unprecedented rate.

Materials and methods

Datasets

In this study, primary and secondary data were collected and utilized. For instance, Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data were downloaded from the US Geological Survey “website (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/) to prepare distance-to-river, elevation, and slope data in ArcGIS. Soil data were acquired from the Ministry of Water, Irrigation, and Energy to prepare a soil-type map. A Landsat 9 Image (30-meter resolution) for January 2025 was acquired “from the US Geological Survey website (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/) to” create NDVI and LULC maps of the research area. Road data were digitized from high-resolution imagery in Google Earth Pro to create a proximity-to-roads map. Population data were downloaded from the Ethio-GIS “map server website (https://www.ethiogis-mapserver.org/) to” produce the population density map (Table 1).

Data processing and analysis

To determine green space areas for development in the study area, eight main controlling factors were selected based on expert opinion and previous studies18,28,29. These factors include proximity to rivers, population density, LULC, NDVI, slope, proximity to roads, elevation, and soil type. These factors were represented by thematic layers made from the processed and gathered datasets.

A DEM was processed in ArcGIS 10.8 using sink-filling and extraction procedures to generate elevation, slope, and distance-to-river maps of the study area. Seven Landsat 9 spectral bands (bands 1–7) were layer-stacked in ERDAS Imagine software to create the LULC map for the study area. The stacked image was then clipped to the study area and subjected to haze reduction to minimize atmospheric scattering and enhance image quality for further analysis in ERDAS Imagine 2015. In ERDAS Imagine, supervised classification was used to classify land use in the study area. The LULC map showed four major classes: open land, built-up areas, farmland, and forestland. The classification was validated and achieved excellent accuracy, making it suitable for subsequent analysis. Landsat 9 bands 5 (near infrared) and 4 (red) were used in ERDAS Imagine 2015 to create the NDVI layer. The NDVI of the area was calculated using Eq. (1).

Proximity maps of roads and rivers were created using the Euclidean distance available in ArcGIS. Likewise, population density and soil type maps were created using ArcGIS based on population and soil data, respectively.

Each selected factor received a weight depending on its impact on urban green space (UGS) identification. The current study employed a GIS-based multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) approach, which involves the assignment of weights to every factor, an imperative process for indicating the relative emphasis of each criterion in the development of urban green spaces17,29. To this effect, the most popular MCDM technique, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), has been employed to assign weights to each factor. Here, information on all factors was contrasted against each other using a 1–9 scale developed by Saaty30. Table 2 presents the Pairwise Comparison Matrix (PCM) of all factors.

Results

Analysis of UGS controlling factors

The selection and identification of factors are essential for urban green space (UGS) analysis. In this regard, eight factors, such as population density, river proximity, NDVI, LULC, slope, proximity to road elevation, and soil type, were chosen by experts and from literatures. These factors were assigned corresponding weights and grouped into sub-factors based on their relative significance in determining the appropriate areas of green spaces (Tables 3 and 4). The reliability of the weighted values was measured and found acceptable.

Population density

Population density is a key parameter for identifying green spaces in urban regions. In this study, a population density map was generated using ArcGIS, with values ranging from 50.6 to 2842.0 persons/km²(Fig. 3a; Table 4). The map was reclassified into five categories: 50.6–269.6, 269.6–598.0, 598.0–1035.8, 1035.8–1692.6, and 1692.6–2842.0, which cover 57.0 km² (55.8%), 22.4 km² (22.0%), 14.0 km² (13.7%), 5.2 km² (5.1%), and 3.4 km² (3.3%) of the study area, respectively. This indicates that 8.6 km² (8.4%) of the study area has a population density that is suitable to highly suitable for green spaces, while approximately 79.4 km² (77.8%) is unsuitable to poorly suitable (Table 4). On the other hand, approximately 14.0 km² (13.7%) of the study area was classified as moderately appropriate for green spaces.

Reclassified thematic maps of: (a) population density, b) NDVI, c) LULC, d) slope). (Maps are created using ArcGIS version 10.8, https://www.esri.com/en-us/arcgis/products/arcgis-desktop/overview).

NDVI

NDVI is a key environmental factor used for mapping green spaces. The NDVI values of the study area ranged from − 0.06 to 0.48 and were divided into five categories using the natural break classification method in ArcGIS software (Table 4). The highest category in the region, with NDVI values between 0.27 and 0.48, covered an area of 10.7 km² and indicated high suitability for green space development (Fig. 3b). Although NDVI values theoretically range between − 1 and + 1, the observed values in Eka Tafo are lower due to sparse vegetation and the predominance of built-up surfaces. Therefore, within this local NDVI range, the values of 0.27–0.48 represent the highest NDVI class, indicating areas with the densest and most suitable vegetation for green space development. Areas with NDVI values of 0.12–0.16 and − 0.06–0.12 are classified as poorly suitable and unsuitable for green space development, respectively.

LULC

LULC also influences the analysis of UGS suitability. The LULC of the Eka Tafo Sub City based on Landsat 9 was categorized into four groups, namely, built-up areas (29.7%), forestland (15.5%), agricultural land (35.0%), and open land (19.8%) (Table 4). These LULC classes were re-classified and given the appropriate ranks in their relative significance in determining more suitable green space zones. Accordingly, the highest rank value of 5 was assigned to open land, indicating high suitability, whereas built-up areas were considered unsuitable for green space development and were assigned the lowest rank (Fig. 3c). Forestland in the study area is mainly distributed in the northwestern and northern parts, where the population density is low, the slopes are steep, and accessibility is limited due to the greater distance from roads. Consequently, forestland was classified as having low suitability for green space development in this study.

Slope

The slope is also a crucial factor in assessing the appropriateness of the UGS. The slope of the research area was determined using a 30-meter DEM. The slope angles ranges from 0 to 48.5 and were reclassified into five categories: 24.1–48.5 (unsuitable), 16.3–24.1 (poorly suitable), 10.0–16.3 (moderately suitable), 4.7–10.0 (suitable), and 0–4.7 (highly suitable) (Table 4). The lower-angle class (0–4.7), covering the central, southwestern, or eastern parts of the study area, was assigned the highest rank for green space development (Fig. 3d).

Proximity to road

Proximity to roads is a key factor in green space development. This guarantees accessibility and maximizes the benefits of UGS29,31. In this study, areas within a 477.8-m radius of the road are considered highly appropriate for green space development (Fig. 4a; Table 4), while areas within 477.8–1132.5 m and 1132.5–1999.7 m are classified as suitable and moderately suitable, respectively. Conversely, areas more than 1999.7 m from the road, which account for 17.5% of the study area, fall into unsuitable to poorly suitable categories (Table 4).

Proximity to the river

Proximity to the river is a crucial factor in evaluating the suitability of the UGS in the study area. Rivers promote vegetation growth and support recreational activities in urban areas. In this study, river proximity ranged from 0 to 2600.8 m and was classified into five categories (Table 4). The highest rank was assigned to areas within a 234.5-m radius of the river network, covering 35% of the study area, and this class was considered highly appropriate for green space (Fig. 4b). However, the areas that are more than 1407.5 m in distance from the river, which form only 3.2% of the area under study, are not suitable for UGS development.

Elevation

Elevation also influences the choice of appropriate UGS sites. The elevation of the study area ranged from 2,278 to 3,152 m above sea level (Table 4). This elevation was divided into five classes: 2919–3152 m (unsuitable), 2759–2919 m (poorly suitable), 2608–2759 m (moderately suitable), 2488–2608 m (suitable) and 2278–2488 m (highly suitable) (Fig. 4c; Table 4). The suitable and highly suitable classes encompassed 64 km² (62.4%) of the study area due to their lower elevations.

Soil type

Soil type is a key factor in assessing the suitability of a site for UGS development. In the study area, five soil types were identified: orthic Solonchaks (5.8%), calcic Xerosols (2.7%), pellic Vertisols (84.2%), chromic Luvisols, and chromic Cambisols (7.3%) (Table 4). Among these, chromic Luvisols and chromic Cambisols are highly appropriate for green space development (Fig. 4d) because of their high natural fertility and clay content28.

Suitable green space area of Eka Tafo Sub-City

In this study, the green space suitability map of Eka Tafo was prepared by integrating eight main controlling factors (population density, proximity to roads, NDVI, LULC, slope, elevation, proximity to rivers, and soil type). Appropriate normalized weights were assigned to each factor in the AHP, indicating that population density, proximity to rivers, and NDVI were the most important factors, whereas soil type was the least significant. Each factor was reclassified into sub-factors based on its suitability for green spaces (Table 4). Finally, the reclassified thematic layers were combined using the WOA tool in ArcGIS software. Consequently, five suitability categories were identified in the study area: unsuitable, poorly suitable, moderately suitable, suitable, and highly suitable (Fig. 5). Approximately 9.2 km² (8.8%) and 22.9 km² (21.9%) of the study area are classified as highly suitable and suitable for green spaces, respectively. Moderately suitable areas cover approximately 30.0 km² (28.6%) of the Eka Tafo sub-city. Conversely, approximately 17.5 km² (16.7%) and 25.2 km² (24.0%) of the sub-city are classified as unsuitable and poorly suitable for green spaces, respectively (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, geospatial technology methods were employed, including GIS and remote sensing, integrated with a Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) approach to evaluate the suitability of green space areas in the Eka Tafo sub-city. To accomplish this, eight different criteria were identified and selected based on expert opinions and the literature29,32. Several regulating factors, such as population density, proximity to roads, NDVI, LULC, slope, elevation, proximity to rivers, and soil type, were used to map green space areas. According to Moisa, et al.29, population density is a key parameter for identifying green spaces in urban areas. NDVI values range between − 1 and + 1, where + 1 signifies highly vegetated areas and − 1 indicates water bodies or regions with minimal to no vegetation33. Negative NDVI values are entirely unsuitable for green space development, whereas values above 0.1 are considered suitable for green spaces29,32.

Land-use type is also an important factor in identifying suitable green spaces. Among the LULC types, open land, agricultural land, and forestland are considered appropriate for green space development32,34. Conversely, built-up areas are regarded as poorly suitable to unsuitable because of the higher costs required to convert them into open spaces29. Low-slope areas are highly suitable for green space development18,31,35,36, whereas higher slope angles are less suitable for green space development because they require landscape modifications37. Proximity to rivers and roads is highly influential in identifying suitable green spaces. Road accessibility boosts social interaction among urban residents17, and land near rivers and other water bodies is highly suitable for green space development18,29.

The produced green space suitability map classified the study area into five suitability zones, ranging from unsuitable to highly suitable. Highly suitable and suitable green space areas are frequently found in the central and southwestern parts of the study area. These areas are closely linked to high population density, proximity to roads, gentle slopes, and proximity to rivers. As depicted in Fig. 5, built-up areas, agricultural lands, and open land are also categorized as highly suitable to suitable for green spaces. Although built-up areas were initially deemed unsuitable for green spaces during the overlay analysis, many of these areas were eventually classified as highly suitable due to their high population density and proximity to roads, which reflects the strong demand and potential social benefits of green area provision. However, the implementation of green spaces in these densely built-up areas is difficult because of the existing infrastructure and high land acquisition costs. In many cases, immediate large-scale development of green spaces in such areas is not practicable without significant land-use reallocation and financial investment. Thus, built-up areas classified as highly suitable reflect high demand rather than immediate feasibility for green space development.

In contrast, approximately 17.5 km² (16.7%) and 25.2 km² (24.0%) of the sub-city are classified as unsuitable and poorly suitable for green spaces, respectively (Table 5). These zones are primarily characterized by sparsely populated areas, very steep slopes, higher elevations, and greater distances from roads and rivers than other zones. Forestland and soil types, such as orthic solonchaks and calcic xerosols, also define these areas. Although the northern and northwestern parts of the sub-city showed high NDVI values, which are usually associated with green vegetation and were initially considered highly suitable for green spaces, these areas were ultimately categorized as unsuitable or poorly suitable (Fig. 5). This is due to their sparse populations and steep slopes19,25. This trade-off reflects the study’s emphasis on allocating green spaces based on their potential to maximize social and functional benefits by prioritizing areas that serve dense and growing populations rather than focusing solely on the conservation of existing but largely inaccessible vegetation. Consequently, areas with high NDVI but low accessibility and limited population coverage were assigned lower suitability scores, reinforcing a demand-driven and accessibility-oriented approach to urban green space planning.

The findings of this study can assist urban planners and decision-makers by providing a foundation for green space planning in the Eka Tafo sub-city. Additionally, the results align with those of similar studies conducted in other regions, including Addis Ababa, Ethiopia29, Kigali City, Rwanda19, and Jimma City, Oromia, Ethiopia25.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study employed GIS and remote sensing techniques to evaluate the suitability of green spaces in the Eka Tafo sub-city. Eight factors were examined: population density, proximity to roads, NDVI, LULC, slope, elevation, proximity to rivers, and soil type. Among these, population density and proximity to roads were identified as the most influential factors in determining suitable areas. The results identified five categories of suitability: unsuitable, poorly suitable, moderately suitable, suitable, and highly suitable. The findings indicate that 30.7% of the area is classified as suitable to highly suitable for green space, which aligns with national guidelines suggesting that a portion of urban land should be reserved for green spaces. In contrast, approximately 40.7% of the area is considered unsuitable to poorly suitable, indicating environmental, accessibility, and physical constraints that limit green space development in the region. This implies that stakeholders and policymakers should prioritize zones that are suitable or highly suitable for green space development to maximize environmental, social, and health benefits. However, for sustainable decision-making, it is crucial to note that both proposed and existing land-use plans must be taken into account to achieve long-term urban development goals.

This study can serve as a valuable tool for guiding green space planning and intervention efforts in the Eka Tafo sub-city. Additionally, it provides important insights for future UGS research in Ethiopia and beyond, illustrating the potential of geospatial analysis in developing sustainable urban green infrastructure. Although the findings of this study provide a valuable initial assessment, it has certain limitations that future studies can address. One such limitation is the reliance on the traditional AHP to assign weights to each factor. Although commonly used, AHP can introduce bias because of the subjectivity involved in pairwise comparisons. Therefore, we strongly recommend that future researchers consider intermediate MCDA approaches, such as the Analytic Network Process (ANP) or Fuzzy AHP, to reduce subjectivity. Additionally, more advanced techniques, such as machine learning and artificial intelligence, are recommended for future studies to enhance the objectivity and accuracy of results.

Another limitation is the absence of historical green space data, which hindered the validation of the current findings. Future research should focus on incorporating validation methods by collaborating with stakeholders and relevant authorities to verify whether the proposed green space areas exist within the study area. Additionally, we recommend that future studies include a wider range of socioeconomic indicators, such as community preferences, income levels, and cultural patterns of green space use to produce more inclusive and context-specific results.
