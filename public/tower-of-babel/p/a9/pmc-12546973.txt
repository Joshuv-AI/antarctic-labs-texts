Analysis of neuronal activity impairment in iPSC-derived neurons from idiopathic autism patients
Authors: Andras Kern, Viktor Román, Tamás I Orbán, Ildikó Pál, József Nagy, György Lévay, Balázs Lendvai, Zsolt Némethy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnderstanding the molecular mechanisms underlying autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is crucial to develop effective interventions. Here, we utilized induced pluripotent stem cells differentiated into neurons (iPSC-Ns) from idiopathic ASD and control individuals (CTRL) to investigate the molecular basis of ASD. ASD-iPSC-Ns exhibited functional alterations displaying less calcium transients compared to CTRL. Different expression levels of microRNAs involved in neurogenesis and neuronal functioning might underlie these differences. ASD-iPSC-N neuronal networks showed impaired synaptic neurotransmission and connectivity (decreased [Ca2+]i waves and glutamatergic transmission), as demonstrated by a chemogenetic approach utilizing designer receptor exclusively activated by designer drug (DREADD) in co-cultures of iPSC-Ns and rat cortical neurons. Such functional alterations have profound implications for neuronal network formation and function, contributing to the core pathogenic features of ASD. Therefore, utilization of ASD-iPSC-Ns offers a unique opportunity to study the molecular mechanism in a relevant human cellular context. Our findings highlight the significance of neuronal activity and synaptic dysfunction in ASD pathogenesis.Subject areas: molecular biology, neuroscience, cell biology

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a heterogenous neurodevelopmental brain disorder affecting various aspects of social interaction and communication, accompanied by restricted and repetitive behaviors. Current studies revealed that genetic background plays a fundamental role in the development of ASD, and it is among the most heritable diseases.1 Genetic changes in ASD are manifested at the system level already during early development. Indeed, human genetic studies identified more than 1,000 risk gene variants associated with ASD.2 The widespread knowledge on ASD candidate genes facilitated the development of various animal models for the better understanding of the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. However, their limited translational value to human neuronal development is reflected by the suboptimal clinical efficacy of the candidate compounds previously developed upon the results of these animal ASD models.3,4 To overcome these in vivo challenges and study the early-onset conditions in ASD, somatic cells bearing the genetic background and retaining partial epigenetic memory of the original human individual samples can be reprogrammed to human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and terminally differentiated to neuronal cultures (iPSC-Ns).5,6,7,8 This approach allows us to model neurodevelopmental disturbances in disease-relevant cell types at the cellular and molecular levels. Functional analysis of iPSC-Ns from ASD individuals (ASD-iPSC-Ns) revealed defects in neuronal activity (e.g., altered microRNA [miRNA] expression profiles relevant to neuronal functioning,9 reductions in neuronal firing, disrupted postsynaptic currents, imbalance in excitatory/inhibitory tone, and impaired network activity).10,11,12,13,14 Monitoring neuronal activity in iPSC-N-based in vitro platforms, therefore, represents a new approach to understand and characterize the fundamental molecular pathomechanisms in ASD, which, in turn, may facilitate the development of novel drug-based therapies.

ASD is classified into syndromic and idiopathic forms. Syndromic ASD is typically associated with mutations in a single gene or chromosomal abnormalities, while the idiopathic form of ASD applies to individuals with unknown etiology.15 Recent findings in human iPSC-based models from idiopathic individuals with certain ASD-related neurodevelopmental conditions include altered neuronal and synapse development, as well as reduced spontaneous and synchronized neuronal activity.10,16,17 iPSC-derived neuronal models of non-syndromic idiopathic forms of ASD revealed several further functional alterations, including decreased calcium transients, impaired neuronal maturation, reduction in synaptic density,18,19 as well as functional impairments in neurotransmission.20 Modeling idiopathic ASD in iPSC-derived excitatory neurons using NGN2-based induction and CRISPR-Cas9 gene inactivation showed reduced spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC) frequency and reductions in action potential amplitude, mean firing rate, and network activity.21 Using transcriptomic analysis of cortical organoids from boys with idiopathic ASD compared to their unaffected fathers, an imbalance in excitatory neurons was demonstrated.22 However, these functional alterations found in ASD using iPSC-derived neurons cannot be considered uniform, involving both hypoactive and hyperactive changes.23 Therefore, it remains to be fully understood how these diverse phenotypes contribute to this heterogenous neuropsychiatric disorder.

In the present study, we used iPSC-N cultures from idiopathic ASD individuals with unknown etiology who were considered clinically homogeneous (Table S1). This clinical homogeneity was partly achieved by the exclusion of severe intellectual disabilities as well as known syndromes or malformations (micro- or macrocephaly) and by selecting donors from the same sex- and age range with no dysmorphic findings or other neurologic deficits. Using functional assays, we characterized the neuronal activity of ASD-iPSC-Ns and found marked differences in miRNA expression profiles as well as the neuronal network activity compared to iPSC-Ns derived from control individuals (CTRL-iPSC-Ns). ASD-iPSC-N samples exhibited not only changes in miRNAs regulating neurogenesis and neuronal functioning but also revealed functional alterations at the neuronal network level: compared to CTRL-iPSC-Ns, a decrease in both the overall intracellular calcium oscillations and synaptic neurotransmission was detected. Uncovering the molecular mechanisms that drive these functional alterations may help us to understand how the symptoms of ASD develop and may further support the identification of potential therapeutic targets for ASD. Targeted interventions aimed at restoring proper calcium signaling and synaptic neurotransmission could hold promise for developing novel treatment strategies for individuals affected by ASD.

Results

Comparison of miRNA expression profiles of ASD vs. control samples in NPCs

As it was demonstrated that abnormal expression of miRNAs in autistic samples may contribute to the molecular mechanism of autism,24,25 molecular profiles of iPSC-derived neuronal progenitor cells (NPCs) from autistic individuals were compared to controls at the level of miRNA expression. We used iPSCs generated from four individuals diagnosed with ASD (Table S1; Figure S2) and from two non-related neurotypical subjects26 (see STAR Methods for detailed description of the cell lines). The iPSCs were directed toward NPCs followed by terminal neuronal differentiation (see STAR Methods). First, we identified several miRNAs, which showed at least 4-fold overexpression in autistic samples compared to controls using miRNA Taqman low density array (TLDA) cards (see STAR Methods). Expression levels of the identified miRNAs by TLDA cards were further validated in NPCs using quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) assay (Figure S1A). We found that four miRNAs showed higher expression in the ASD-derived NPCs compared to the CTRL samples: hsa-let-7e-5p, hsa-miR-135b-5p, hsa-miR-16-5p, and hsa-miR-27b-3p (Figure S1A). All four miRNAs were clustered using the miRPath functional analysis27 and were closely linked to neuronal, Ca2+, and cAMP signaling, as well as stem cell development pathways (Figures 1A and 1B, using DIANA-miRPath v.4.0). Pathway enrichment analysis also confirmed previous results that ASD-miRNAs are significantly involved in pathways associated with cancer, mTOR pathways, cell proliferation, and cell cycle9,25,28,29 (Figures 1A and 1B, using DIANA-miRPath v.4.0). This clustering shows the enrichment of biological processes and pathways related to ASD: these processes are primarily involved in neuronal development, synaptic function, signaling, and cell structure—features that are critical in ASD pathophysiology. The key pathways implicated in ASD included axon guidance, Wnt signaling, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, and mTOR signaling pathway as well as regulation of actin cytoskeleton (Figures 1A and 1B, using DIANA-miRPath v.4.0 tool). Furthermore, gene target network analysis revealed that all four miRNAs regulate ASD, neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative, and neuropsychiatric conditions (Figure 1C).9,18,20,21 The full list of pathophysiological conditions regulated by the four miRNAs is presented in Table S2. In summary, by analyzing miRNA networks,30 we found that all four miRNAs are interconnected by regulating certain gene sets or pathways (Figure S1B) of neurogenesis and neuronal functioning (common signal transduction routes that channel into Ca2+, cAMP, and various kinase signaling pathways). It is worth mentioning that these results were all obtained in NPCs, as the studies at the level of terminally differentiated neurons were not feasible due to large variations, and analysis of the four miRNAs in ASD neurons resulted in no significant change compared to controls (Figure S1C). Our expression profile of four miRNAs in NPC samples showed that they influence pathways of neuronal functioning, are key regulators of early neurodevelopment, and can influence subsequent neuronal differentiation and function.31,32 Therefore, we continued the direct investigations on neuronal activity in iPSC-derived neuronal cultures.

Characterization of neuronal activity of control and ASD-derived iPSC-Ns

In the next set of experiments, we examined whether the expression differences found in NPCs manifest themselves in the neuronal activity using iPSC-derived neurons. We previously demonstrated that the differentiation protocol used in the present study leads to active, spiking, iPSC-derived neurons that constitute a neuronal network with an elaborate neurite morphology26 (Figures S3A and S3B). It has also been previously demonstrated that ASD-iPSC-Ns show reduced neuronal activity and functional connectivity compared to CTRL-iPSC-Ns.11,12,13,17 To compare neuronal activity of ASD- vs. CTRL-iPSC-Ns, we first measured the generation of spontaneous intracellular calcium transients ([Ca2+]i) utilizing the GCaMP6s sensor (Figures 2A and 2B), a genetically encoded fluorescent Ca2+ indicator.33 [Ca2+]i spikes faithfully correlate with the frequency for overall neuronal activity in iPSC-derived neurons and therefore they are generally considered as readouts for cell activity11,34 (Figures S3A and S3B). We detected significantly less spontaneous [Ca2+]i transients in ASD-iPSC-Ns compared to CTRL-iPSC-Ns (29.8% ± 0.7% of ASD vs. 100% ± 1.3% of CTRL-iPSC-Ns, respectively; p < 0.001), revealing impaired neuronal activity of ASD-iPSC-Ns (Figure 2C). We performed quality control experiments to test whether the reduced neuronal activity was not due to impaired differentiation of iPSC-derived cells. RT-qPCR analysis showed no significant differences in the expression of differentiation and maturation markers between control and ASD samples for both NPCs (PAX6, NES, DCX, and NEUROD2) and iPSC-derived neurons (MAP2, SYP, SYNGAP, GRIN2B, and RBFOX1) (Figure S2B). We have assessed the amounts of astrocytes by RT-qPCR analysis of GFAP expression and found no significant differences between control and ASD neuronal cultures, indicating comparable amounts of astrocytes in both conditions (Figure S2C).

Impaired neuronal connectivity and glutamate neurotransmission in ASD-iPSC-Ns

To estimate the role of synaptic transmission in the general activity of the cells, we used a chemogenetic approach by co-culturing iPSC-Ns expressing GCaMP6s with primary rat cortical neurons expressing DREADD (designer receptor exclusively activated by designer drug), an appropriate tool to identify circuitry and cellular connectivity. Previously it was demonstrated that co-culturing human iPSC-Ns with mouse primary neurons resulted in synaptic connectivity and synaptic transmission between the two cell types.35 To induce neuronal activity, we utilized hM3Dq, a DREADD coupled to Gαq, which can induce neuronal burst firing as a result of increasing [Ca2+]i.36,37 Activation of hM3Dq-containing neurons in co-cultures using the DREADD agonist clozapine-N-oxide (CNO, 10 nM) resulted in the induction of [Ca2+]i waves in the iPSC-derived neurons (Figure 3A), revealing functional synaptic connectivity and increase in the synaptic input to iPSC-Ns. We also confirmed in control experiments that when cells did not form connections or in the absence of hM3Dq expression, CNO treatment did not induce [Ca2+]i changes in iPSC-Ns (Figures S3C and S3D). In co-cultures of CTRL- vs. ASP-iPSC-Ns with rat primary cortical hM3Dq neurons, we found impaired neuronal transmission in ASD-iPSC-N cells as compared to CTRL-iPSC-Ns (14% ± 4% in ASD-iPSC-Ns vs. 100% ± 3% in CTRL-iPSC-Ns; p < 0.001), indicating aberrant synaptic connectivity in ASD-iPSC-Ns (Figure 3A). These findings suggest that synaptic functioning is compromised in ASD-iPSC-Ns.

We further examined how glutamatergic neurotransmission may contribute to changes in neuronal network connectivity. To study the glutamate-dependent synaptic neurotransmission, we applied a chemogenetic approach using rat primary cortical neurons expressing a genetically encoded glutamate sensor (iGluSn)38 in co-cultures with iPSC-Ns expressing hM3Dq. Upon the application of CNO, we detected activation of iGluSn, uncovering synaptic glutamate transmission in the co-cultures (Figure 3B). In control experiments, we confirmed that the operation of iGluSn was dependent on glutamate release and CNO in itself had no effect on sensor activity (Figures S3E and S3F). By comparing co-cultures containing iPSC-derived neurons derived from CTRL- or ASD individuals, we detected significantly less glutamatergic synaptic transmission in ASD- vs. the CTRL-iPSC-Ns (34% ± 6% of ASD-iPSC-Ns vs. 100% ± 4.6% of CTRL-iPSC-Ns; p < 0.001; Figure 3B). Our data show that glutamatergic neuronal transmission is specifically reduced in neurons derived from ASD individuals, strengthening our view that reduced functional neuronal network connectivity and synaptic transmission may stand behind the impaired cell activity in ASD-iPSCs. To examine synapse formation, CTRL- and ASD-iPSC-Ns were fixed and immunolabeled for the neuronal markers MAP2 and Synapsin1 (Figure S4A), and the number of Synapsin1-positive spots close to the MAP2-positive neuronal surface was quantified in confocal z stack images in 3D rendering (Figure S4B). In our studies, ASD-iPSC-Ns exhibited 50% significantly fewer Synapsin1-positive synaptic puncta compared to CTRL-iPSC-Ns (Figure S4C), revealing the dysregulation of synaptic formation in the ASD-iPSC-N cultures.

Actin dynamics is compromised in ASD-iPSC-Ns

Among the validated miRNA changes, we found species that interact with the MAPK and mTOR pathways (Figures 1A and 1B), which, in turn, can influence the actin cytoskeleton of the cells.39,40,41 In addition, dysregulation of spine actin dynamics leads to altered dendritic spine functioning, which was found in various neurological disorders, including ASD.42 In a recent study, it was demonstrated that the dynamics of actin polymerization were impaired in idiopathic ASD stem cells,43 and a role of defective actin filaments in dendritic spines and synapse formation in ASD pathogenesis has been reported.42,44 To estimate actin dynamics in our ASD-iPSC-N cultures, we applied the Dendra2-LifeAct sensor45 to monitor real-time actin dynamics in spines of iPSC-Ns (it combines an actin-binding peptide sequence [LifeAct]46 and a photoconvertible fluorescent protein Dendra2).47 LifeAct has been extensively used for live imaging in neurons48,49,50,51: The Dendra2-LifeAct construct is designed to bind reversibly to the F-actin cytoskeleton and can be used to examine actin dynamics due to its photoconversion from a green-to-red fluorescent form upon illumination at 405 nm (Figure 4A). Following the photoactivation of a small area of dendritic spine base with a brief 405 nm pulse, the local population of Dendra2-LifeAct is converted into the red form of the fluorophore and the actin accumulation is measured by the red fluorescence intensity in the dendritic spine heads of CTRL-iPSC-Ns and ASD-iPSC-Ns (Figure 4A). In our experiments, we found a significantly higher accumulation of actin in CTRL-iPSC-Ns compared to ASD-iPSC-Ns (100 ± 2 vs. 41.4 ± 3, respectively; Figures 4B and 4C). When we examined the dynamics of actin accumulation in spines, we found that CTRL-iPSC-Ns reached half-maximum of red fluorescence intensity in a shorter time period compared to ASD-iPSC-Ns (12.2 ± 1.6 s vs. 44.2 ± 1.2 s, respectively, Figures 4B and 4D), indicative of impaired actin dynamics in ASD-iPSC-Ns. These data demonstrate the dysregulation of synaptic formation in ASD-iPSC-N cultures and suggest that actin dynamics are impaired in ASD individuals, which may lead to abnormalities in synaptic functions (as most glutamatergic nerve terminals make synapses on spines).

Discussion

In this study, we first identified differences between the miRNA expression in ASD and CTRL NPCs and then developed a relevant human iPSC-derived neuronal model to study idiopathic ASD by examining the functional characteristics and connectivity of ASD-iPSC-Ns. This approach represents a complementary strategy to link genetic alterations manifested in development of the disease to molecular mechanisms and behavioral phenotypes found in ASD.

As approximately 70% of known miRNAs52 are expressed in the brain,53 miRNAs are often implicated in the development and proper functioning of the central nervous system (CNS). Despite the uncertainties about the pathogenesis of ASD, comparison of postmortem samples from ASD subjects with normally developed controls reveal significantly altered miRNA expression profiles typically involved in neuronal development and synaptogenesis (e.g., neurexin or SHANK3).54 Furthermore, disturbances of the miRNA post-transcriptional regulatory network have recently been proposed as one possible mechanism behind the pathophysiology of ASD. In iPSC-based model systems, miR-1290 was downregulated from donors with idiopathic autism, which, in turn, may target ASD-related genes (MECP2, DCX, DLG3, NLG3, and CNTNAP2).55,56,57 Furthermore, a paternally inherited miR-873-5p showed altered binding affinity for several autism risk genes including NRXN2, SHANK3, ARID1B, and CNTNAP2.58,59 Comparison of miRNA profiles of ASD- vs. control-derived iPSCs, therefore, offers a viable approach to characterize and understand the mechanisms underlying the pathophysiology of autism.

In our experiments, hsa-let-7e-5p, hsa-miR-135b-5p, hsa-miR-16-5p, and hsa-miR-27b-3p demonstrated higher expression in ASD-derived NPCs than in CTRL. All these miRNAs are connected to neurogenesis, neuronal survival, as well as the development of certain CNS pathologies. For example, expression of miR-135b-5p was significantly decreased in children with temporal lobe epilepsy and in an epileptic rat neuronal model,60 while its downregulation was shown to exert antidepressant effects.61 On the other hand, elevated miR-135b-5p relieved neuronal injury and inflammation in post-stroke cognitive impairment,62 while preventing oxygen-glucose deprivation and reoxygenation-induced neuronal injury.63 A possible mechanism of the regulation of cell proliferation and apoptosis can be the miR-135b-5p/GPNMB axis in a Parkinson’s disease cell model.64 On the other hand, hsa-miR-27b-3p showed pro-neuroinflammatory features65: its expression is clearly elevated in several disorders associated with neuroinflammation, i.e., in multiple sclerosis66 and Alzheimer’s disease.67 miR-27b downregulates the expression of the ASD-related engrailed-2 gene,68 while its inhibition enhances neurogenesis in a mouse ischemic stroke model.69 Circulating hsa-let-7e-5p was proposed as a possible biomarker in the diagnosis of major depression and bipolar disorders, as it was upregulated in plasma of individuals.70 Expression of hsa-miR-16-5p (CTRL) was downregulated in the saliva of ASD- vs. neurologically unaffected children.71 Although, there is no clear role of these miRNAs in ASD, their involvement in neuroinflammatory processes and psychiatric/neurologic conditions seems to be obvious. Here, we also used bioinformatic analysis tools to understand how the differentially expressed miRNAs contribute to ASD pathophysiology through the regulation of miRNA-targeted genes associated with ASD. Using functional annotations of four miRNAs, we found regulatory roles of differentially expressed miRNAs in ASD by targeting genes with established neurodevelopmental relevance (Figure S1B; Table S4, using experimentally validated data of miRTargetLink tool), including MEF2C,72,73 BCL2,74 SMAD2,75 SMAD5,75 BACE1,76 NCSTN,77 SLC6A4,78,79 BDNF,76,80 and MET.81 These genes are implicated in synaptic plasticity, apoptosis, neurogenesis, and neurotransmitter signaling, processes known to be disrupted in ASD.

We also developed an in vitro approach by using human iPSC-derived neurons from individuals with idiopathic ASD to investigate the molecular and cellular mechanisms involved in the development of ASD. We functionally characterized the idiopathic ASD-iPSC-Ns and compared them to healthy control iPSC-Ns and found marked differences in ASD neurons including reduced neuronal activity, synaptic connectivity, and neuronal signal transmission. Abnormal synaptogenesis and neuronal network formation were previously reported in postmortem brain tissue studies of ASD.82,83 Defects in synaptic proteins result in the development of ASD via alterations in synaptic structure/function and neural circuits, suggesting that “synaptopathy” may constitute an important component of ASD. To examine alterations of synaptic function in ASD-derived samples, we measured [Ca2+]i transients using the GCaMP6 sensor as a general readout of neuronal activity33 and detected a significant decrease in spontaneous neuronal activity in neurons derived from ASD individuals compared to control samples. GCaMP6 is widely used for calcium imaging to study neuronal activity, and in this study, we focused on the magnitude of [Ca2+]i transients as a measure of the robustness of [Ca2+]i signaling. Our analysis revealed a significantly lower spontaneous [Ca2+]i transients in ASD-derived neuronal networks compared to controls. Decreases in spontaneous calcium transients and spontaneous spikes have been previously reported in idiopathic iPSC-N models derived from ASD individuals.11,17,84 This decrease is of crucial importance, because spontaneous neuronal activity plays an important role during the development of neuronal circuits in the brain.85,86 In addition, spontaneous changes in the [Ca2+]i signaling are involved in actin cytoskeleton rearrangement of synaptic spine development and functioning.87,88 Our analysis of spines demonstrated a significant reduction of actin cytoskeleton function in ASD samples, which correlates with our functional observation concerning the reduced spontaneous neuronal activity.

A consistent feature in neurons from ASD individuals is the abnormal dendritic structure and alterations in spine morphology83,89,90—features that lead to aberrant neuronal brain connectivity.91,92,93 In our study, we performed direct neuronal connectivity analysis using a chemogenetic approach by applying a DREADD construct (hM3Dq). Chemogenetics is a tool to analyze neuronal connectivity of circuits by manipulating the activity of neurons expressing DREADD in the presence of the synthetic drug CNO.36,37 It has been reported that iPSC-Ns can form functional neuronal connections with mammalian primary neurons. Therefore, in this study, we used co-cultures of iPSC-Ns expressing the GCaMP6s sensor with rat primary cortical neurons expressing hM3Dq DREADD to manipulate the iPSC-N cell network and examine the neuronal connectivity. We confirmed synaptic dysfunctions in ASD by showing impaired neuronal connectivity in ASD neuronal co-culture samples compared to controls. Our data support other recent results demonstrating that a significant reduction in the amount of spontaneous activity leads to aberrant functional neuronal connectivity in ASD detected by iPSC-based neuronal model systems.12,17,21

Previous studies using ASD iPSC-N model systems have documented the dysfunction in synaptic transmission, resulting in a major defect in excitatory synaptic transmission as well as a shift in the excitatory/inhibitory balance.10,12,94,95 Several studies using iPSC-derived neurons detected perturbations in glutamatergic synapses in ASD samples, showing reduced vesicular glutamate transporter (VGLUT-1),17,19 consistent with a decreased excitatory glutamatergic synapse affecting synaptic transmission14,17 and reduced glutamate release and spontaneous activity.84 In our study, we examined the excitatory glutamatergic neurotransmission by utilizing a chemogenetic approach with a combination of a glutamate biosensor (iGluSn).38 To monitor glutamate release, we set up a co-culture including iPSC-Ns expressing DREADD-hM3Dq and rat primary cortical neurons expressing iGluSn. This approach allowed us to monitor glutamate release from iPSC-N to better model and understand the aberrant neurotransmission observed in ASD. In the presence of CNO, we were able to detect activation of iGluSn, confirming glutamate neurotransmission in the co-culture. In our study, we showed that idiopathic ASD-derived neurons display less glutamate response, supporting previous results of impairment in excitatory glutamate neurotransmission in ASD.10,12,17 Our finding demonstrates a reduced glutamate response in ASD-derived neurons measured by the iGluSnFR sensor, and this reduction may be influenced by several factors, including potential differences in pre-synaptic release properties and decreased synaptic density observed in ASD-derived neuronal networks (Figure S4). Further experiments are needed to determine the precise mechanism of contribution of these factors to the observed glutamate dynamics.

In conclusion, we established a cellular model of ASD using iPSC-Ns derived from ASD individuals. Our model was supported by the identification of a unique set of miRNAs showing alterations in their expression levels that could be linked to general cell activity and to Ca2+ and actin dynamics: the readout of the functional investigations presented in this paper. After differentiation of these cells toward neurons, marked changes were observed when the transmission properties were studied using various chemogenetic approaches. It is clear that there is an impaired transmission in ASD-derived neurons compared to control cells that can be linked to altered dynamics of dendritic spines and glutamate inputs, all connected to synaptic transmission. These data heavily support the idea that the impaired neuronal transmission is a prominent feature of the human ASD.

Limitations of the study

Here, we report the application of iPSC-derived neurons from idiopathic autistic individuals to characterize the molecular mechanisms underlining the ASD pathology. ASD is a highly heterogenous disease with diverse genetic and environmental contributors. In our iPSC-N model, we primarily captured the genetic manifestations underlining ASD, simplifying the broader environmental and epigenetic factors that can also contribute to the disorder. This means that while genetic influences can be captured in our study, the model may not replicate the full spectrum of ASD pathology.

Since the genetic background of the iPSC donors may influence the phenotypic and functional characteristics of the derived neurons, broader applicability of the results can be limited, and our findings from iPSC-Ns may not generalize well to a larger, more diverse population of individuals with ASD.

Our study focused on the characterization of the molecular mechanism underlying ASD, including changes in miRNA expression, alterations in neuronal network, and synaptic transmission. However, other potential biological processes and pathways (such as oxidative stress, inflammation, or other signaling pathways) are also involved in ASD and could be further studied.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patients and iPSC cells

The ASD donors were recruited by the Autism Foundation’s Outpatient Clinic, Budapest, Hungary, and the study was approved by the Research-Ethics Committee of Heim Pál Children’s Hospital (permission number KUT-83/2013). Written informed consent had been obtained from the legal guardians before the subjects entered the study. The children met the diagnostic criteria of autism spectrum disorder96,97 and Autism Diagnostic Interview Revised.98 Detailed clinical characterization of the donors for ASD diagnosis is shown in Table S1 (except for two clones of the same iPSC cell line derived from a subject with Kleefstra syndrome; characterization of these cells was described in detail in the previous publication26). Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (iPSCs) were derived from the subjects by reprogramming peripheral blood mononuclear cells using an integration-free method as published previously.99 The pluripotent and differentiation potential of the iPSCs is described in Supplementary Materials (Figure S2). The presence of Oct3/4, Nanog, SSEA4 and Alkaline Phosphatase (AP) labeling is an indication of pluripotency, while GATA4, Brachyury and TUBB3 markers show the differentiation capacity of the iPSCs toward the endoderm, mesoderm and ectoderm lineages, respectively. Donors named ASD1 and ASD2 contributed one iPSC line each, while ASD3 and ASD4 contributed two clones (ASD3/1, ASD3/2, ASD4/1, ASD4/2) each to the present study. The following iPSC lines of neurotypical subjects published previously in Nagy et al.26 were used: CTRL1/1, CTRL1/2, CTRL2/1, and CTRL2/2. Only a subset of miRNA experiments was supplemented with two more neurotypical controls: CTRL3 (1115S3) and CTRL4 (1125S7). We have also performed quality control experiments to determine whether the reduced activity due to an impaired differentiation of the iPSC-derived cells. For quality control assessment, we have performed qRT-PCR experiments (Figure S2B) to define whether we can find any difference in the markers of differentiation and maturation of the iPSC-derived cells in control vs. ASD individuals. For NPCs, we examined markers of neuronal development (PAX6 and NES), and early neuronal differentiation (DCX and NEUROD2). For iPSC derived neurons we tested markers of differentiation and functionality into mature neurons: MAP2, SYP (synaptophysin, marker for synaptic vesicles), SYNGAP, GRIN2B and RBFOX1 (nuclear factor regulating expression of genetic networks). We have found no significant changes in the gene expression of differentiation and maturation markers of controls compared to ASD samples (Figure S2B).

Method details

Cell culture

iPSCs, NPCs and cell lines

hiPSCs and Neural Precursor Cells (NPCs) were generated and obtained from Biotelentum as described previously.26 For terminal neuronal differentiation, NPCs were lifted from the surface using Accutase and seeded into poly-l-ornithine- and laminin-coated 96-well ibidi plates at a density of 45,000 cells per cm2. The neuronal culture was maintained either in Neuronal Maintenance Plus (NMplus) medium (1:1 mixture of DMEM/F12 medium and Neurobasal Plus Medium supplemented with N-2 and B-27, Non-Essential Amino Acids, Antibiotics, 10 ng/mL BDNF and GDNF) or in BrainPhys medium (BrainPhys Neuronal Medium supplemented with N-2 and B-27, 1 μg/mL Laminin, Antibiotics, 10 ng/mL BNDF and GDNF, 1 mM Dibutyryl-cAMP, 200 nM Ascorbic Acid). The neurons were maintained up to two months with half-media changes carried out twice a week. To perform robust and reproducible functional comparison we differentiated control and ASD neurons in parallel using identical conditions. The protocol we followed here for iPSC-neuronal differentiation used dual SMAD inhibition26,100 which results neuronal cells and GFAP-expressing astrocytes.100 In co-culture experiments, rat primary cortical neurons were added to 6 weeks old iPSC-N cultures in NMplus medium for 2 weeks of differentiation, with a human -to -rat neuron ratio 1:1 to facilitate synaptic interaction. 293T cells were obtained from ATCC (# CRL-3216) and were grown in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 μg/mL of penicillin/streptomycin, 500 μg/mL Geneticin in a humidified atmosphere at 37°C and 95% air/5% CO2.

Primary cortical neurons

Cortical tissue from E16-E17 rats (Toxi-Coop Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) were dissected in Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) supplemented with sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Sceintific), glucose (0.1% wt/vol) and HEPES (10 mM). Animal maintenance and experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All procedures using animals were approved by the local ethics committee (Institutional Animal Welfare Committee of Gedeon Richter Plc.) and conformed to the rules and principles of the European Animal Protection Directives (Directive 2010/63/EU). Tissues were dissociated in trypsin (0.5% wt/vol; Sigma-Aldrich), supplemented with DNAse I (0.2% wt/vol; Sigma-Aldrich) for 20 min at 37°C, washed three times and manually triturated in plating medium (MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.45% glucose, sodium pyruvate (1 mM), glutamine (2 mM) and penicillin/streptomycin (100 μg/mL). Cells were then plated on cell dishes coated with poly-D-Lysine (Sigma-Aldrich) and cultured in neurobasal medium (ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with B-27 (ThermoFisher Scientific), glutamine (2 mM) and, penicillin/streptomycin (100 μg/mL). After 10 days, the neurons were transduced, and functional assays were performed 1-week later.

Investigation of miRNA expression profiles in hiPSC-N

TaqMan low density array (TLDA) cards for miRNA detection and analysis

In the first set of experiments, expression patterns of 754 human validated miRNAs in differentiated ASD-vs. CTRL-derived iPSC-N samples were determined using TLDA cards in lysates from ASD-vs. control-derived terminally differentiated iPSC-N samples. To investigate the broader expression level, ASD-iPSC-N samples were extracted from idiopathic ASD (ASD1/1 and ASD3/1 iPSC lines) and syndromic ASD (Kleefstra syndrome, in two clones; for further information see Nagy et al.26), while the sample extracted from the CTRL1/1 iPSC line served as control. Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using Trizol (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol (minimum 1–2×106 cell/ml Trizol). RNA concentration and purity were determined using NanoDrop ND-1000 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Quality of the isolated RNA was further controlled by agarose gel electrophoresis.

For miRNA expression levels of the ASD-vs. control-derived iPSC-N samples, cDNA samples were prepared from 10 ng of total RNA using the TaqMan Advanced miRNA cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat. No. A28007), according to the manufacturer’s instruction, including the recommended pre-amplification step. miRNA expression levels were then measured by means of real time-quantitative polymerase chain reaction using human TLDA cards containing 754 validated human miRNAs according to the manufacturer’s protocol (TaqMan Advanced miRNA Human A and B Cards, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No.: A3180). Amplifications were performed in duplicates, and cycle threshold (CT) values were determined by the QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR system (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Further data analysis was done by the Cloud platform (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), applying the ’Ct/threshold’ method using global normalization method. To determine significantly expressed miRNAs we applied a cut-off value with a minimum of 2-fold expression change in all ASD samples compared to the control cell line. We found that ten miRNAs showed significantly higher expression in ASD-iPSC-N compared controls: hsa-miR-127-3p, hsa-miR-27b-3p, hsa-let-7e-5p, hsa-miR-494-3p, hsa-miR-135b-5p, hsa-miR-495-3p, hsa-miR-655-3p, hsa-miR-382-5p, hsa-miR-409-3p, hsa-miR-543-3p.

Validation of the expression of specific miRNAs in NPCs using RT-qPCR and functional annotation

Samples from six idiopathic (ASD1/1, ASD2/1, ASD3/1, ASD3/2, ASD4/1, ASD4/2) and one syndromic ASD- (in two clones), as well as six control- (CTRL1/1, CTRL1/2, CTRL2/1, CTRL2/2, CTRL3, CTRL4)-derived NPCs were isolated using Trizol (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (minimum 1–2×106 cell/ml Trizol). RNA concentration and purity were determined using NanoDrop ND-1000 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For cDNA preparations, 10 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed using the TaqMan Advanced miRNA cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No.: A28007) for miRNA studies. RT-qPCR on cDNA samples was performed using TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientificα, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No.: 4444557) or TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No.: 4369514) for miRNAs. The applied TaqMan are indicated in Table S3. The real-time PCR measurements were run on the StepOnePlus platform (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s intructions; relative quantifications were carried out by the ΔΔCt method, using the global normalization method (for miRNA analyses). Validated miRNAs (hsa-let-7e-5p, hsa-mir-135b-5p, hsa-mir-16-5p, and hsa-mir-27b-3p) were analyzed for clustering and pathway enrichment using DIANA-miRPath27; and gene network connection analysis were performed by miRTargetLink 2.0.30 We have used the DIANA-miRPath tool to perform functional annotation and pathway analysis for the four miRNAs upregulated in ASD samples. We analyzed the potential biological impact of upregulated microRNAs by identifying the pathways and biological processes they regulate through their target genes. This tool provides insights into the miRNAs’ role in various cellular functions. The pathway analysis is based on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) by identifying which biological pathways are significantly enriched with the target genes of the selected miRNAs. We used miRTargetLink for a presentation of miRNAs target gene and pathway network; it functionally annotates which miRNAs regulate a particular target gene or target pathway. Network analysis of miRNAs and pathophysiological condition have been performed using Cortellis Knowledge database (https://www.cortellis.com/drugdiscovery) and visualized as a relational graph database. miRNA expression analysis and functional evaluations were performed on separate set of samples.

Plasmid constructs, viral transduction, transfection and electroporation

The GCaMP6s plasmid encoding the calcium sensor have been purchased form Vigene Bioscience (pGP-AAV-syn-GCaMP6s-WPRE.4.641); DREADD-Gq encoded plasmid (cat #v101; ssAAV-hSyn1-hM3D(Gq)_mCherry-WPRE-hGHp(A)) was purchased from Viral Core Facility of University of Zürich.

For calcium imaging experiments, neurons were transduced with Adeno-Associated Virus (AAV) serotype 8 particles encoding GCaMP6s (AAV-syn-GCaMP6s-WPRE.4.64; Vigene Bioscience), while in chemogenetic experiments, neurons were transduced with virus encoded DREADD-Gq (hSyn1-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry) and cultures were imaged after 4–5 weeks of expression. Primary neuronal and iPSC-N cultures were transfected using TurboFect Transfection Reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific). Primary neurons were electroporated using the Neon Transfection System (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Confocal microscopy imaging (intracellular calcium imaging, iGluSn, Dendra-LifeAct imaging and synapse quantification by immunofluorescence)

Intracellular calcium imaging

Functional assays have been performed on terminally differentiated iPSC-derived, 8-weeks-old neuronal cultures. In iPSC-N cells, [Ca2+]i was detected using GCaMP6s, after viral transduction at 3 weeks post-differentiation neurons, in ECB buffer (20 mM HEPES pH7.4, 140 mM NaCl, 20 mM KCl, 5 mM Glucose, 1 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM CaCl2). Intracellular [Ca2+]i changes were measured at 37°C in heat and humidified atmosphere (95% air/5% CO2) controlled environmental chamber (Okolab) using the confocal spinning disk (Andromeda) iMIC system (FEI). Time lapse images were taken at 100 msec for 4 min and cells were excited using 488 nm laser line and emission measured at 530 nm. The fluorescence emission images were collected, regions of interest (ROIs; n = 10–20) were selected over cell soma and the change of fluorescence intensity over time was analyzed by the iMIC Live Acquisition Software (FEI). The change in relative fluorescence intensity (ΔF = F-Fo) over background was calculated (ΔF/Fo), and data presented as a magnitude of response as a percentage of the maximal response. For the measurements with the Ca2+-sensitive dye (in 293T cells and primary cortical neurons), cells were incubated with Calcium 6-QF (Molecular Devices) in HBSS (137 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 0.9 mM Mg2SO4, 1.4 mM CaCl2, 3 mM NaHCO3, 0.6 mM Na2HPO4, 0.4 mM KH2PO4, 20 mM HEPES, 5.6 mM glucose; pH = 7.4) for 2 h at 37 °C and fluorescence changes were measured using confocal spinning disk iMIC microscope (FEI). Signals were recorded and analyzed using a Live Acquisition Software (FEI).

iGluSn imaging

Primary rat cortical neurons were electroporated with iGluSn plasmid using Neon Transfection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and co-cultured with iPSC-N expressing DREADD-Gq, and experiments performed on terminally differentiated iPSC-derived, 8-weeks-old neuronal cultures. Fluorescence changes of the iGluSn sensor were measured at 37°C in heat and humidified atmosphere (95% air/5% CO2) controlled environmental chamber (Okolab) using the confocal spinning disk (Andromeda) iMIC system (FEI). After performing baseline imaging, we applied clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) to induce glutamate release and continuously imaged the neuronal culture to capture dynamic changes in iGluSn fluorescence. Time lapse images were taken at 100 msec for 2 min and cells were excited using 488 nm laser line and emission measured at 530 nm. The fluorescence emission images were collected, and image data analyzed by the iMIC Live Acquisition Software (FEI).

Dendra2-LifeAct imaging

iPSC-N cell lines (5 weeks old) were transfected with Dendra2-LifeAct (Addgene; Plasmid #54694); experiments were performed after 3 days of expression. Photoconversion and imaging was performed on a confocal laser scanning Olympus FV3000 microscope equipped with 405, 488 and 561 nm lasers. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere (5% CO2) for the duration of experiments. Subcellular region of the dendritic spine base was exposed to photoconversion with a sort 3s pulse of a 405 nm laser administered at 25% laser power. Once the cells were photoconverted, region of interest for each spine were collected during experiments. Samples were excited with both 488 nm and 561 nm excitation and time lapse images were collected. The fluorescence intensity was then extracted for each region of interest for each time point in the series. For each dataset, the emission intensity was normalized to the first time point. The mean of multiple regions was then plotted with error bars determined from the standard deviation.

Synapse quantification

The neuronal cultures derived from CTRL-iPSC and ASD-iPSC lines maintained in NMplus medium were fixed with 4% PFA and immunolabeled with Synapsin1 (abcam, ab8) and MAP2 (abcam, ab92434) antibodies. Five to ten confocal z stack images per well were taken from two wells in two independent experiments. The acquired images were loaded into Imaris Software for Surface and Spot analysis to determine the number of Synapsin1-positive puncta close to MAP2-positive neuronal surfaces (less than 1 μm apart). Synapse quantification was performed with multiple biological replicates per cell line, and the results were averaged for each cell line per experiment and calculating the fold difference relative to CTRL1/1.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Values are given as means ± SEM and obtained from the number of separate experiments indicated. Comparisons between the different groups were made using Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA test. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Instat software, and a difference of p < 0.05 was considered significant. For statistical analysis each iPSC line (4 control lines and 6 ASD lines) was treated as a separate biological replicate. Independent experiments were conducted on different days, where cells were independently cultured, differentiated, and assayed. Each experiment was repeated 3–4 times to account for potential variability. Within each experiment, assays were performed in duplicate to provide technical replicates and ensure reproducibility of results.
