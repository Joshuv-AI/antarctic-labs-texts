Solid-state fermented Artemisia argyi residue improves immune response and intestinal integrity of broilers challenged with Clostridium perfringens
Authors: Feng Xue, Zhiyuan Chen, Jun Wang, Jiahao Zhao, Fangqin Liu, Can Zhang, Wenhua Liu, Xiaojing Hao, Shouzhen Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNecrotic enteritis (NE) caused by Clostridium perfringens (C. perfringens) in broilers is rising with antibiotic restrictions, severely undermining their health and productivity, and necessitating safe, effective alternatives for control. Artemisia argyi is rich in bioactive compounds, and fermented herbal medicines confer multiple benefits for animal health. However, the potential of solid-state fermented Artemisia argyi residues (AARF) for controlling NE remains underexplored. This study aimed to verify the hypothesis that AARF improves immune responses and intestinal integrity in broilers challenged with C. perfringens. Broilers were assigned into five groups: an uninfected control (Con) group, an infected control (CP) without AARF group, and three infected groups treated with AARF at 15 g/L, 30 g/L, and 45 g/L (AARF-15, AARF-30, AARF-45). From day 14 to day 21 of age, the infected groups received a daily oral gavage of 2 mL (1 × 108 CFU/mL) fresh C. perfringens suspension to induce NE, the Con group received 2 mL sterile cooked meat medium. Broilers supplemented with 30 g/L AARF exhibited enhanced levels of antibodies against C. perfringens alpha toxin (α-toxin), elevated expression levels of hepatic IL-2 and IgA compared with the CP group (all P < 0.05). In the AARF-30 group, body weight (BW), average daily gain (ADG), jejunal villus height (VH), and the activity of jejunal sucrase were all higher than those in the CP group at 21 days of age. The expression level of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR-4) was upregulated in the AARF-30 and AARF-45 groups relative to the CP group, and superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) were increased in the AARF-30 group, whereas tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) was downregulated in the AARF-15 and AARF-30 groups (all P < 0.05). In conclusion, supplementation with an optimal concentration of 30 g/L AARF has been shown to better improve the immune response and intestinal integrity in broilers challenged with C. perfringens. These findings indicate that AARF serves as a promising alternative to antibiotics in poultry production.Keywords: Artemisia argyi, Solid-state fermentation, Necrotic enteritis, Clostridium perfringens

Introduction

Clostridium perfringens (C. perfringens), a Gram-positive anaerobic bacterium, is commonly found within the intestinal microbiota of poultry, which causes necrotic enteritis (NE) in broilers characterized by severe depression, reduced appetite, dark diarrhea, ruffled feathers, and death, or chronic signs, with a lower growth rate and worsened feed conversion (Waller et al., 2024). The incorporation of antibiotics into poultry feed has been extensively utilized as a primary preventive measure against NE. However, the prolonged and excessive use of antibiotics has precipitated the emergence of bacterial resistance, thereby undermining their efficacy in disease prevention and treatment. The European Union and China have banned the use of antibiotics as growth promoters (EC Regulation No. 1831/2003, Ajuwon, 2016; State Council Decree No. 676 of the People's Republic of China). With the implementation of restrictions on antibiotics as feed additives, the incidence and prevalence of NE have shown a consistent upward trend. Consequently, there is an imperative need to explore new, efficient, safe, and environmentally sustainable alternatives to antibiotics.

Artemisia argyi is a kind of traditional Chinese herbal medicine, which contains diverse bioactive compounds including phenylpropanoids, flavonoids, terpenoids, steroids, and other components (Wang, et al., 2024a). Studies have shown its potential to enhance growth performance, modulate intestinal microbiota communities, and improve intestinal health in broiler chickens (Zhang, et al., 2020). Artemisia argyi residue is the remaining material after Artemisia argyi is processed into Artemisia argyi floss through such physical methods as drying, crushing and sieving.

It has been revealed that solid-state fermentation of Artemisia argyi leaves results in a substantial increase in flavonoid and polyphenol content, along with elevated levels of oxygenated monoterpenes, oxygenated sesquiterpenes, and aromatic compounds. Furthermore, this process significantly enhances the inhibitory activity against tyrosinase and xanthine oxidase (Zhou, et al., 2025). Aspergillus niger (A. niger), is preferred for solid-state fermentation due to thriving in low-moisture environments and promoting active substances release from Artemisia argyi (Gungor, et al., 2024; Gungor and Erener, 2020; Wang, et al., 2024b), and Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis) fermentation products can not only enhance growth performance in broilers, but also alleviate C. perfringens-induced intestinal necrotic lesions (Cheng, et al., 2018). Currently, there are studies on the fermentation of Artemisia argyi using A. niger independently (Wang, et al., 2024b), but the concurrent use of B. subtilis and A. niger for the Artemisia argyi fermentation remains unexplored. This study investigated the effect of solid-state fermentation Artemisia argyi residue (AARF) with A. niger and B. subtilis on immune response and intestinal integrity of broilers challenged with C. perfringens, and explored its potential as an antibiotic alternative to control C. perfringens-induced necrotic enteritis in poultry production.

Materials and Methods

Artemisia argyi and strain

Artemisia argyi residue was purchased from Shandong Pingyi Taifeng Medicinal Materials Import and Export Co., Ltd (Shandong, China); B. subtilis (CICC 24713) and A. niger (CICC 2377) strains were provided by shandong Baolai-Leelai Bioengineering Co., Ltd (Shandong, China).

Solid-state fermentation

The Artemisia argyi residue was crushed, sieved, and sterilized at 121°C for 15 minutes. Artemisia argyi residue and wheat bran (Shandong Huamou Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shandong, China) were first mixed at a ratio of 4:1, followed by the incorporation of 1 % urea, 10 % KH₂PO₄, and 5 % MgSO₄, and sterile water was then added to form the basal medium. Subsequently, A. niger spore suspension and B. subtilis suspension (both at a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) were inoculated into the basal medium, which was then subjected to sealed fermentation at 30°C. The inoculum ratios (B. subtilis to A. niger: 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 3:1 and 2:1), inoculation size (1 %, 3 %, 5 %, 7 % and 9 %), fermentation duration (3, 4, 5, 6, 7 days), and substrate moisture (40 %, 50 %, 60 %, 70 %, and 80 %) were sequentially optimized (Chen, 2016). To determine the optimal fermentation conditions the activities of cellulase, acid protease, and amylase in each fermented sample were measured in accordance with China's national standards: Determination of Cellulase Activity in Feed Additives—Spectrophotometric Method (NY/T 912-2020), Determination of Acid and Neutral Protease Activity in Feed Additives—Spectrophotometric Method (GB/T 28715-2012), and Hebei's local standard Determination of α-Amylase Activity in Feed Enzyme Preparations—Spectrophotometric Method (DB13/T 1095-2009). The fermentation bottle was shaken every 6 hours to ensure even fermentation, after which the solid products were dried at 50°C and stored at 4°C. To ensure mycotoxin levels in AARF comply with the GB 13078-2017 Hygienic Standards for Feeds (National Standard of the People's Republic of China), 10.0 g ± 0.1 g of residue (pre- and post-fermentation residues were processed separately) was mixed with 50 mL of sample extraction solution, shaken for 5 minutes, and left to stand for another 5 minutes. The sample was centrifuged at 6,000 r/min for 30 seconds, after which 100 μL of the supernatant was mixed with 600 μL of dilution solution, vortexed for 30 seconds, and then 100 μL of the resulting mixture was placed in a test strip well. After 15 minutes, the strip was analyzed using an up-conversion luminescence immunoassay analyzer (Suzhou Uho Biotech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) to measure aflatoxin B1, zearalenone, fumonisin, T2 toxin, and vomitoxin levels.

Antibacterial activity

Ethanol extracts from Artemisia argyi residue, pre- and post-fermentation, were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. Escherichia coli (E. coli), Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and C. perfringens were cultured on solid media at 37°C for 12 hours, then transferred to liquid media for another 12 hours. Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to 10⁸ CFU/mL and spread-plated on Mueller-Hinton (MH) agar for general culture or tryptose sulfite cycloserine (TSC) agar for selective isolation of C. perfringens. Sterile Oxford cups were placed on the agar, filled with 200 μL of test solution. E. coli, Salmonella, and S. aureus plates were incubated at 37°C for 16 hours, while C. perfringens plates were incubated anaerobically. Antibacterial activity was evaluated by measuring the diameter of inhibition zones (DIZ).

Ethical statement

All animal management and treatment procedures for this study were strictly followed the National Experimental Animal Welfare Guidelines (Guidelines on the Treatment of Laboratory Animals) published by the Ministry of Science and Technology, PRC in 2006, and were approved by the Animal Welfare and Research Ethics Committee of Qingdao Agricultural University, Shandong, China (DKY20240702).

Experimental design

A total of 225 1-day-old healthy male Arbor Acres (AA) white-feather broiler chicks were randomly divided into five treatment groups, each with three replicates of 15 chicks, reared in stainless steel cages. The groups were as follows: an uninfected control group (Con), an infected control (CP) without AARF group, and three infected groups supplemented with AARF in the drinking water at concentrations of 15 g/L, 30 g/L, and 45 g/L (AARF-15, AARF-30, AARF-45). For the three AARF-treated groups, the broilers received drinking water containing AARF at the respective concentrations from day 1 to day 21 of the trial. From days 14 to 21, AARF and CP groups were orally gavaged 2 mL of fresh C. perfringens suspension (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) daily, whereas the Con group received 2 mL of sterile cooked meat medium as a control. This procedure was based on Collier, et al. (2008) with modifications. The C. perfringens solution was anaerobically incubated at 42°C for 16 hours in cooked meat medium, with the method modified based on Liu, et al. (2010). The base diet followed NRC (1994) and Chinese broiler feeding standards (NY/T 33-2004) (Table 1). All groups had ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the experiment. The room temperature was set to 33°C for days 0 to 3, 30°C for days 4 to 7, 27°C for days 8 to 14, and 24°C for days 15 to 21. The entire experiment lasted for 21 days.

Growth performance

Upon initiation of the experiment, the conditions of the chickens were recorded in detail, encompassing the daily feed consumption and the residual feed. On days14 and 21, the chickens were weighed in a fasted state. The BW was recorded, the ADFI, ADG and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated.

Jejunal lesion scoring

On the 21st day of age, all chickens were euthanized via cervical exsanguination. The intestinal tissues were dissected and pathological alterations were examined. The jejunum of each chicken was excised for labeling, and lesion scores were documented in accordance with the methodology outlined in a previous study (Dahiya, et al., 2005).

Jejunum morphology

The jejunum was fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer for more than 24 hours. Following the protocol outlined in Cao, et al. (2012), the samples were dehydrated and cleared, immersed in wax, sliced and dyed, dehydrated and sealed. Subsequently, the specimens were examined using a microscope (Motic China Group Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China) for direct visualization. The Image View software was employed to analyze the morphology of the jejunal villi, specifically measuring the villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD), and calculating the villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH/CD).

Enumeration of C. perfringens colonies

On the 21st day, 0.1 g of jejunal contents was accurately weighed and combined with 900 μL of sterile saline. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized using a homogenizer (Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China), and a 10-fold serial dilution was performed with sterile saline. Aliquots of 100 μL from each dilution were spread onto TSC agar supplemented with d-cycloserine and 50 % egg yolk. The TSC plates were incubated anaerobically at 42°C for 16 hours. The numbers of C. perfringens in the intestinal contents were determined, with results expressed as log10 CFU/g.

Antibody levels against C. perfringens alpha-toxin and perfringolysin O

At 21 days of age, blood was collected by cardiac puncture, centrifuged at 3,000 r/min for 10 minutes, and serum was obtained. Serum antibody levels against alpha-toxin (α-toxin) and perfringolysin O (PFO) were measured using ELISA. Plates were coated with 1 μL/well of antigen, washed three times with PBST containing 0.05 % Tween-20 at 4°C and sealed with 10 % horse serum for 2 hours. Serum samples, diluted 1:800, were added (100 μL/well) and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. Subsequently, 100 μL/well of rabbit anti-chicken HRP-labeled antibody (1:12,000 dilution) was added and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. After washing three times with PBST, 100 μL/well of TMB single-component substrate solution was added and incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes. The reaction was terminated by adding 2 mol/L H₂SO₄, and the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) to determine the antibody levels.

Transcript levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and antioxidant factors

0.1 g of jejunum tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen and RNA was extracted using the Trizol method. cDNA was synthesized using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit (ACRO Biosystems Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and the mRNA expression levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), toll-like receptor 4 (TLR-4), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and the reference gene (β-actin) were quantified by qPCR with SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS Kit (ACRO Biosystems Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Relative cytokine expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔct method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). The expression of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), as well as inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) were measured using the same methodology. Immune indices in liver

About 0.1 g liver tissue sample was ground with liquid nitrogen in a mortar, mixed with 1 mL of physiological saline, and homogenized with a vortex mixer. After centrifuging at 10,000 r/min for 5 minutes, the supernatant was collected to measure IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IgA, IgM, and IgG using a chicken cytokine ELISA kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), following the manufacturer's instructions, with an automated microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for analysis.

Digestive enzyme activity

About 0.1 g of jejunal tissue was weighed and ground with liquid nitrogen in a mortar. After adding 1 mL of physiological saline, the mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 10,000 r/min for 5 minutes. The supernatant was used to measure chymotrypsin, lactase, and sucrase levels using a chicken digestive enzyme ELISA kit (Shanghai Enzyme-Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer's instructions, with an automated microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) values were analyzed by one-way ANOVA using SPSS software (SPSS 25.0 for Windows, Chicago, IL). Comparisons were made using Duncan's multiple range tests. Differences between means were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Optimization of fermentation conditions

B. subtilis and A. niger were mixed and inoculated into the solid-state fermentation medium of Artemisia argyi residue. Based on the maximum enzyme activity, the optimal conditions were established as a 1:2 (B. subtilis: A. niger) inoculation ratio, 7 % inoculum size, 60 % moisture content, and 5-day fermentation at 30°C (Additional file: Figure S1). The mycotoxin levels in unfermented and fermented Artemisia argyi residue comply with the GB 13078-2017 (Hygienic Standards for Feeds). Notably, the concentrations of aflatoxin B1 and zearalenone were reduced after fermentation (Table 3).

Antibacterial activity

The ethanol extract of AARF showed larger DIZ against S. aureus (10 mm compared to 8 mm) and C. perfringens (15 mm compared to 12 mm) than the unfermented residue. However, the ethanol extracts of unfermented Artemisia argyi residue and AARF showed no antibacterial activity against E. coli and Salmonella. Meanwhile, 80 % ethanol showed no antimicrobial activity against these four bacteria (Fig. 1).

Growth performance

As shown in Table 4, AARF groups showed slightly higher BW than the CP group at 14 days of age, with a P - value of 0.152. At 21 days of age (one-week post-challenge), the AARF-15 and AARF-30 groups exhibited higher BW (653.5 ± 27.79 and 655.5 ± 35.87 g/bird, respectively) compared to the CP group (P < 0.05). No significant differences in ADG were observed compared with CP group from 1 to 14 days. However, during 15 to 21 days, the AARF-15 and AARF-30 groups achieved higher ADG (45.61 ± 1.06 and 47.37 ± 2.27 g/d/bird, respectively; P < 0.05) than the CP group. Notably, the AARF-30 group had a lower FCR (1.61 ± 0.06 g/g) than the CP group at 15 to 21 days (P < 0.05). ADFI did not differ significantly across groups during the trial.

Jejunal lesion scores

At 21 days of age, the jejunum of challenge groups showed varying damage levels, in contrast to the undamaged Con group. The CP group showed severe lesions like intestinal wall thinning and large blood spots, while other groups had milder hemorrhagic spots. The AARF groups had lower jejunum lesion scores than the CP group, with the lowest scoring of AARF-15 (1.20 ± 0.40), though differences were not significant (P > 0.05) (Table 5 and Fig. 2).

Jejunal morphology

At 21 days of age, the AARF-30 group showed increased VH (607.33 ± 19.12 μm) compared with the CP group, and all AARF groups exhibited higher VH/CD ratio than the CP group (P > 0.05). The Con group had intact jejunum tissue with closely arranged villi and without the shedding of mucosal epithelial cells. In the AARF groups, villi were compact with minimal shedding, while the CP group showed shedding of epithelial cells, loosely arranged intestinal glands, and numerous damaged or missing villi (Table 6 and Fig. 3).

Discussion

NE continues to threaten global poultry production by compromising animal health and productivity, thereby undermining both food security and economic sustainability (Lee and Lillehoj, 2021). Developing antibiotic-independent strategies to effectively control NE has become a critical priority in poultry production (Ko, et al., 2025). Artemisia argyi, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb, contains various bioactive compounds such as polysaccharides and flavonoids. Flavonoids derived from Artemisia argyi exhibit antioxidant activity (Wang, et al., 2023), and its polysaccharides show antibacterial properties (Zhou, et al., 2025). Previous research has indicated that supplementation of fermented Artemisia argyi can enhance the production performance and meat quality of broiler chickens (Wang, et al., 2024b). This study showed that supplementation with 30 g/L AARF beneficially modulated growth performance, immune response, and intestinal integrity in broilers infected with C. perfringens, indicating its potential as an antibiotic alternative.

It is becoming more and more common to replace antibiotics with probiotics or Chinese herbal medicines amid the general trend of banning antibiotics as feed additives (Prescott, et al., 2016). Artemisia argyi has been proven to contain numerous flavonoids besides polysaccharides and volatile oils (Amat, et al., 2010). Moreover, microbial fermentation can enhance its biological activities (Ng, et al., 2022; Tao, et al., 2021a), with co-culture fermentation demonstrating superior efficacy in improving bioactive content, functional quality, and sensory attributes (Mubeen, et al., 2025). In this study, Artemisia argyi residue was used as a substrate and fermented with B. subtilis and A. niger strains. At 30°C, both probiotics reached peak levels, likely due to A. niger's spore thermal tolerance during fermentation and B. subtilis's optimal growth temperature, allowing both strains to thrive and metabolize efficiently at this temperature. Existing studies have demonstrated that A. niger possesses the capability to degrade aflatoxin B1 (Kim, et al., 2017). Our study also demonstrated that the AARF reduced the levels of aflatoxin B₁ and zearalenone compared to those of the unfermented Artemisia argyi residue.

C. perfringens infection is prone to cause NE, which ultimately results in a marked decline in growth performance. Studies have demonstrated that fermented Artemisia argyi enhances growth performance in mice (Tao, et al., 2021b) and improves daily weight gain in chickens when fermented with A. niger (Wang, et al., 2024b). In this research, adding AARF to drinking water increased BW and ADG while decreasing FCR in NE model, which aligns with previous research findings. Nutrient absorption is inextricably associated with the action of digestive enzymes. Chymotrypsin primarily assists in protein breakdown, while lactase and sucrase catalyze lactose and sucrose hydrolysis. Studies have demonstrated that dietary supplementation with B. subtilis can enhance digestive enzyme activity (Kumar, et al., 2018). Our study yielded similar findings, showing that water supplementation with AARF increased intestinal lactase and sucrase activities in broilers.

C. perfringens infection can severely damage the intestines and may lead to death. In this trial, one week after C. perfringens challenge, CP and AARF groups showed significant intestinal lesions, such as hemorrhagic spots, intestinal wall thinning, etc. Notably, the AARF groups had lower lesion scores than CP group, as evidenced by the antibacterial activity of AARF against C. perfringens in vitro. Studies have demonstrated that the chloroform extract of Artemisia argyi leaves can disrupt the cell wall integrity and increase membrane permeability in S. aureus (Zhang, et al., 2022), suggesting that its inhibitory effect against C. perfringens may operate through a similar mechanism. Furthermore, AARF groups had fewer C. perfringens in their jejunal contents compared to the CP group, likely due to lactic and acetic acids and other compounds from probiotics in the fermentation preparation lowering intestinal pH (Ghasemi-Sadabadi, et al., 2019), creating an unfavorable environment for the bacteria, leading to a decrease in the number of C. perfringens, thus alleviating the intestinal lesion. B. subtilis and A. niger can produce enzymes such as protease, amylase, lipase and carboxypeptidase, which help mitigate pathogenic bacterial infections in hosts by regulating intestinal microecological balance, secreting bacteriocins, antibacterial proteins, and producing metabolites (Wang, et al., 2021). Increased VH and VH/CD reflected enhanced digestive and absorptive capacity, as well as improved growth performance (Chamorro, et al., 2019). In this research, bacterial infection damaged the intestinal villi of broilers, resulting in decreased jejunal VH and VH/CD ratio in the CP group compared to the Con group, along with evident villus fragmentation and loss. In contrast, the AARF-30 group exhibited higher jejunal VH and reduced CD relative to the CP group. These results are consistent with previous research that dietary supplementation with B. subtilis preparations promoted intestinal epithelial cell proliferation, increased VH, and improved mucosal morphology in NE-affected broilers, thereby enhancing nutrient absorption capacity (Teo and Tan, 2005). ZO-1 is a key tight junction protein that regulates intestinal barrier integrity and mucosal immunity (Fanning, et al., 1998). Dietary supplementation with Artemisia argyi increased VH/CD and ZO-1 in rabbits (Liu, et al., 2019).This study found that the AARF-30 group slightly increased ZO-1 gene expression, which possibly enhances intercellular junctions between intestinal tissues and mucosal epithelial cells and reduces intestinal barrier damage, although the underlying mechanisms require further investigation.

C. perfringens α-toxin is the main virulence factor of type A, causing cytotoxicity, hemolysis, etc., contributing to NE in poultry (Mishra and Smyth, 2017). PFO, a theta toxin, works with other toxins to induce disease (Goossens, et al., 2017). This study found that broilers given AARF in their drinking water had increased serum α-toxin antibodies and slightly elevated PFO antibodies. This may be attributed to natural polysaccharides in Artemisia argyi residue and fungi. Studies indicate these polysaccharides can activate immune responses by binding to mononuclear phagocytes and antigen-presenting cells, and directly modulating lymphocytes (T, B, NK cells) (Ren, et al., 2021), thereby enhancing antibody production. The existence of the gut-liver axis enables the translocation of microbial metabolites to the liver following intestinal barrier dysfunction, subsequently activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, which leads to the downregulating the expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Aron-Wisnewsky, et al., 2020; Chen, et al., 2023; Chung, et al., 2000; Yang, et al., 2020). The current research observed that the AARF groups showed higher hepatic IL-2 and IgA expression compared to the CP group. Because IgA is associated with mucosal immunity, the reduced jejunal lesion scores induced by AARF may be related to its ability to upregulate IgA expression. At 21 days of age, broilers infected with C. perfringens exhibited significant upregulation of jejunal IL-1β and TNF-α expression compared to the Con group, indicating infection induced intestinal inflammation, while AARF groups showed varying degrees of downregulation in IL-1β and TNF-α expression levels. This suggests that AARF can restore intestinal barrier function, and mitigate the inflammatory response caused by C. perfringens. The observed effects may be attributed to flavonoids in Artemisia argyi, which have been shown to inhibit nuclear translocation of p-NF-κB, downregulate the NF-κB signaling pathway, and subsequently reduce mRNA expression of TNF-α (Yang, et al., 2021; Zeinali, et al., 2017). Furthermore, in this study, the AARF-30 group upregulated the expression levels of antioxidant factors GSH-Px, enhancing the antioxidant capacity of broilers organism. Bioactive compounds in Artemisia argyi leaves, including polysaccharides and flavonoids, can directly enhance endogenous antioxidant enzyme activity (Ruan, et al., 2019). Artemisia argyi can enhance the antioxidant function of the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway (Yang, et al., 2024). This effect is likely mediated by polysaccharides and flavonoids present in Artemisia argyi, which activate the Nrf2/ARE pathway, thereby upregulating endogenous antioxidant enzyme activity and promoting the expression of antioxidant factors.

In conclusion, supplementing 30 g/L AARF to the drinking water of broilers infected with C. perfringens proved to be more effective in alleviating intestinal damage induced by C. perfringens, improving their growth performance, immune responses and antioxidant indices, demonstrating its potential as a promising antibiotic replacement for combating C. perfringens infections in poultry production.
