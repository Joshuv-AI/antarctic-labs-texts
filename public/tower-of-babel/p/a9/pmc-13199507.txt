Exosomal miR-4443 confers cisplatin resistance in breast cancer by inhibiting ferroptosis
Authors: Wanfu Wang, Jie Bai, Fuguo Tians
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveExosomal microRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as important regulators of chemotherapy response. This study aimed to investigate whether exosomal miR-4443 is associated with cisplatin resistance in breast cancer and to explore its potential relationship with ferroptosis.MethodsExosomes were isolated from cisplatin-sensitive and cisplatin-resistant breast cancer tissues, as well as from cisplatin-sensitive (MCF-7-S) and cisplatin-resistant (MCF-7-R) breast cancer cell lines. Exosomes were characterized by transmission electron microscopy, nanoparticle tracking analysis, and detection of canonical exosomal markers. miR-4443 expression was quantified by qRT-PCR. Cell viability, colony formation, iron accumulation, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were assessed following cisplatin treatment and modulation of miR-4443 expression. Ferroptosis-related responses were evaluated using pharmacological inducers and inhibitors. The effects of miR-4443 on cisplatin response were further examined in a subcutaneous xenograft model.ResultsExosomal miR-4443 levels were significantly higher in cisplatin-resistant breast cancer tissues and cell lines compared with cisplatin-sensitive counterparts. Exosomes derived from cisplatin-resistant samples transferred miR-4443 to sensitive cells and attenuated cisplatin-induced growth inhibition. Modulation of miR-4443 expression altered cellular sensitivity to cisplatin and ferroptosis-related stress, accompanied by changes in iron accumulation, ROS levels, and expression of the ferroptosis suppressor FSP1. In vivo, miR-4443 overexpression reduced the antitumor efficacy of cisplatin and was associated with increased FSP1 expression in tumor tissues.ConclusionThese findings suggest that exosomal miR-4443 is associated with cisplatin resistance in breast cancer and may contribute to this phenotype, at least in part, through modulation of ferroptosis-related pathways. Targeting miR-4443 or modulating ferroptosis-related pathways may represent a promising strategy to enhance cisplatin sensitivity; however, further mechanistic dissection and larger-scale clinical validation will be required before translational application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04912-6.Keywords: micro-RNA, Exosome, Chemotherapy, Ferroptosis, Breast carcinoma

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths among women globally. Although significant advances in BC therapy, particularly platinum-based chemotherapy, have improved clinical outcomes, metastatic BC remains challenging to treat, with limited survival for many patients [1]. Cisplatin, a widely used platinum-based agent, is effective in treating various cancers, including BC, by inducing DNA damage and cell death [2]. However, its clinical efficacy is often compromised by the development of resistance, making it imperative to explore mechanisms underlying this resistance.

Exosomes, small extracellular vesicles secreted by cells, play an essential role in intercellular communication by transferring bioactive molecules such as RNA, proteins, and lipids [3, 4]. In the tumor microenvironment, exosomes derived from chemotherapy-resistant tumor cells have been implicated in promoting drug resistance in recipient cells, thus contributing to tumor progression and metastasis. Recent studies [5, 6] have shown that exosomal miRNAs are key mediators of chemotherapy resistance, transferring resistance traits to sensitive cells and altering their response to treatment.

miR-4443, a recently identified miRNA, has been shown to regulate various aspects of tumor biology, including metastasis and drug resistance [7]. In breast cancer, elevated miR-4443 expression has been associated with poor prognosis and resistance to therapies [8, 9]. Despite these observations, the role of miR-4443 in mediating chemotherapy resistance, particularly through exosomal transfer, remains underexplored.

Based on preliminary screening of exosomal miRNAs from cisplatin-sensitive (Cis-S) and cisplatin-resistant (Cis-R) BC tissues, we identified miR-4443 as a candidate miRNA strongly enriched in exosomes from Cis-R samples. We therefore hypothesized that exosomal miR-4443 contributes to cisplatin resistance in BC by regulating ferroptosis, potentially through FSP1. The objective of this study was to investigate the functional role of exosomal miR-4443 in cisplatin resistance and to explore its relationship with ferroptosis-related signaling in breast cancer cells and xenografts.

Materials and methods

Patients samples

Fresh primary breast cancer tissues and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected from six patients treated at Shanxi Cancer Hospital. All patients had histopathologically confirmed breast cancer and received cisplatin-based chemotherapy. Based on progression-free survival (PFS) at 6 months following treatment, three cases were classified as cisplatin-sensitive (Cis-S) and three cases as cisplatin-resistant (Cis-R). Clinicopathological information was recorded at the time of sample collection.

All procedures involving human participants were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Cancer Hospital (Approval No. SCH20230699) and were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to sample collection.

Cell culture, transfection and treatments

The human breast epithelial cell line MCF-10 A and breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231, BT-549, MDA-MB-468, and MCF-7 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. To establish cisplatin-resistant MCF-7 cells (MCF-7-R), parental MCF-7 cells (MCF-7-S) were continuously exposed to cisplatin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) using a stepwise selection strategy. Cells were initially exposed to cisplatin at 10% of the IC50 concentration (IC50 = 2.324 nM) and, after recovery, the concentration was gradually increased over six months until a stable resistant phenotype was established. Cisplatin resistance was confirmed by comparing cell viability curves and colony formation under cisplatin treatment between MCF-7-R and MCF-7-S cells.

For functional assays, MCF-7-S and MCF-7-R cells were treated with cisplatin at concentrations indicated in the figure legends. Ferroptosis inducers (Erastin and RSL-3) and inhibitors (Ferrostatin-1 [Fer-1], deferoxamine [DFO]) and the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK, necroptosis inhibitor Necrostatin-1, and autophagy modulator chloroquine were obtained from commercial suppliers and used at the concentrations listed in manufacturer’s instructions that accompany.

miRNA transfection

miR-4443 mimics, miR-4443 inhibitors, and corresponding negative controls (NC-mimic and NC-inhibitor), as well as empty vectors, were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and transfected with 5 µg of plasmids or the equivalent amount of miRNA mimic/inhibitor using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h, cells were harvested for qRT-PCR, Western blot, or functional assays.

To assess ferroptosis-induced cell death, MCF-7-S cells were exposed to various treatments, including cisplatin, Erastin, Ferrostatin-1 (FER-1), deferoxamine, Z-VAD-FMK, Necrostatin-1, and chloroquine, either alone or in combination. Detailed reagent dosages are provided in the Supplementary Materials. After 24 h of incubation with the reagents, the cells were subjected to a cell proliferation assay.

Exosome isolation and characterization

Exosomes were isolated from six paired BC tumor and adjacent normal tissues and from cell culture supernatants using a previously described differential ultracentrifugation protocol with minor modifications, with reference to standard exosome isolation methods. Briefly, tissues were minced into small pieces and digested with collagenase 75 U/mL for 0.5 h at 37 °C. The suspension was sequentially centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, 2,000 g for 10 min, and 10,000 g for 4 h at 4 °C to remove cells and debris. The supernatant was then ultracentrifuged at 100,000 g for 4 h at 4 °C. The exosome-containing pellet was resuspended in PBS, layered on a sucrose cushion, ultracentrifuged again, washed with PBS, and finally resuspended in 200 µL PBS. For cell-line-derived exosomes, MCF-7-S and MCF-7-R cells were cultured for 48 h in medium containing 10% exosome-depleted FBS (Thermo Fisher, USA). Conditioned media were collected and processed as above.

Exosome morphology was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100 F, JEOL, Japan). Exosome size distribution and concentration were determined using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; NanoSight NS300, Malvern Instruments, UK). Exosomal markers (CD63, CD81, CD9, PDCD6IP) were evaluated by Western blot. For uptake assays, exosomes were labeled with PKH67 (Beyotime, China) for PKH67 labeling 1 h at room temperature, washed, and incubated with MCF-7-S cells for 6 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a confocal microscope (FV1000, Olympus, Japan) with a 40× objective (excitation 488 nm for PKH67 and 405 nm for DAPI).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the cells and exosomes using Trizol reagent (Thermo, USA). Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the TaqMan Reverse Transcription Kit or TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green (Toyobo, Japan). The results were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method and normalized to GAPDH or U6. The primers used in this study are listed in the Supplementary Materials.

Western blot

Cells subjected to various treatments were harvested and lysed using RIPA buffer. The total proteins were quantified and separated by SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad, USA). After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 2 h. The membranes were then incubated overnight with primary antibodies against PDCD6IP (ab105357; 1:1000, Abcam), CD81 (ab109201; 1:1000, Abcam), CD63 (ab68418; 1:1000, Abcam), CD9 (ab92726; 1:1000, Abcam), FSP1 (ab251234; 1:1000, Abcam), and GAPDH (ab9485; 1:5000, Abcam, USA) at 4 °C. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Abcam) were applied and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. GAPDH was used as the loading control.

Cell proliferation

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well. After treatment for 0–96 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Tongrendo, Japan) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. For the colony formation assay, 1 × 10³ cells were plated in 6-well plates and treated for 24 h. The cells were then maintained in fresh medium for 7 days. Colonies were observed under a light microscope after fixation and staining.

Fe2+ iron assays

Intracellular Fe²+ levels were measured using an iron assay kit (ab83366, Iron Assay Kit, Colorimetric, Abcam, USA). Briefly, cells were washed with PBS, gently homogenized, and incubated on ice with an iron-reducing agent for 30 min. The cells were then incubated with the iron probe for 1 h. The production was assessed using a microplate reader (BioTek, Beijing, China) at 593 nm.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and treated with different conditions. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with 5 µmol of CellRox Green reagent (C10444, Thermo Fisher Scientific/Invitrogen, USA) for 30 min. For intracellular superoxide detection, cells were then treated with 5 µmol of MitoSOX-Red reagent (M36008, Invitrogen, USA) for 20 min. The nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed using constructs containing the wild-type (WT) FSP1 3′UTR or a mutant (Mut) 3′UTR with substitutions in the predicted miR-4443 seed-matching region. Cells were co-transfected with reporter plasmids and miR-4443 mimics or negative control using Lipofectamine 2000. Luciferase activity was measured 24–48 h post-transfection using a Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (E1910, Madison, Wisconsin, USA), and Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Rescue experiments

For rescue experiments, cells were co-transfected with miR-4443 mimics/inhibitors together with an FSP1 knockdown siRNA or an FSP1 overexpression plasmid (84883; alisased AIFM2; Addgene), as indicated. After 24–48 h, cells were treated with cisplatin with or without ferroptosis modulators, followed by cell viability assays and measurement of ferroptosis-related readouts.

Animal assay

Approval for the animal experiments was obtained from the Ethics and Scientific Research Committee of Shanxi Cancer Hospital. BALB/c nude mice (male, 4 weeks old) were purchased from the Animal Center of Shanghai Jiao Tong University. Subcutaneous injections of 1 × 10⁶ MCF-7 cells transfected with either miR-4443 mimics or NC-mimic were administered to induce tumor formation. Tumor volume was measured and recorded. When tumor volume reached 100 mm³, mice were treated with cisplatin or PBS via intraperitoneal injection. Tumor volumes were measured every 5 days after cisplatin treatment. According to the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shanxi Cancer Hospital, the maximal permissible tumor size in mice was 1,500 mm³ or a diameter of 1.5 cm. Throughout the study, tumor size was monitored every 5 days, and the maximal tumor burden permitted by the ethics committee was not exceeded in any experimental animal. All animals were euthanized humanely before reaching the maximal allowable limits. At the end of the experiment, all mice were humanely euthanized using CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, in accordance with the institutional animal care and use guidelines released by the IACUC of Shanxi Cancer hospital.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Independent sample Student’s t-test where the data were normal distribution, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. When multiple comparisons were displayed within the same panel, asterisks (*) were used to indicate comparisons with the control group (Con), whereas hash symbols (#) were used to indicate comparisons with the cisplatin-treated control group (Cis) or the cisplatin-sensitive (Cis-S) group, as specified in the figure legends. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Throughout the manuscript, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 vs. NC-mimic; #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01,###P < 0.001 vs. Cisplatin-treated group.

Results

Enrichment of miR-4443 in exosomes from cisplatin-resistant breast cancer tissues

To investigate differences in exosomal miRNA expression associated with cisplatin response, exosomes were isolated from breast cancer tissues classified as cisplatin-sensitive (Cis-S) or cisplatin-resistant (Cis-R) based on progression-free survival. Transmission electron microscopy revealed vesicles with a typical cup-shaped morphology and a diameter of approximately 100 nm (Fig. 1A). Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of canonical exosomal markers, including PDCD6IP, CD9, CD63, and CD81, indicating successful exosome isolation (Fig. 1B). Quantitative RT-PCR analysis demonstrated that miR-4443 expression was significantly higher in exosomes derived from Cis-R tumor tissues compared with those from Cis-S tissues, while lower levels were observed in adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1C). In parallel, miR-4443 expression was examined in a panel of breast cancer cell lines and the normal breast epithelial cell line MCF-10 A. Cisplatin-resistant MCF-7-R cells exhibited markedly higher miR-4443 expression than their cisplatin-sensitive counterparts (Fig. 1D). Consistently, exosomes derived from MCF-7-R cells contained higher levels of miR-4443 than those derived from MCF-7-S cells (Fig. 1E). Based on these observations, MCF-7 cells were selected for subsequent functional experiments.

Exosomal miR-4443 modulates cisplatin sensitivity in breast cancer cells

To assess whether tumor-derived exosomes could transfer miR-4443 to recipient cells, PKH67-labeled exosomes isolated from Cis-S or Cis-R tumor tissues were incubated with MCF-7-S cells. Confocal microscopy confirmed efficient uptake of exosomes by recipient cells (Fig. 2A). Following incubation with Cis-R–derived exosomes, miR-4443 levels in MCF-7-S cells were significantly increased compared with cells treated with Cis-S–derived exosomes or vehicle control (Fig. 2B). Functionally, exposure to Cis-R derived exosomes significantly attenuated cisplatin-induced growth inhibition in MCF-7-S cells, as assessed by CCK-8 assays (Fig. 2C). Gain and loss-of-function experiments further demonstrated that transfection of miR-4443 mimics increased miR-4443 expression in MCF-7-S cells, whereas miR-4443 inhibitors reduced its expression in MCF-7-R cells (Fig. 2D). Overexpression of miR-4443 was associated with reduced sensitivity to cisplatin, while inhibition of miR-4443 enhanced cisplatin-induced growth suppression (Fig. 2E). These effects were further supported by colony formation assays (Fig. 2F).

Control cells were treated with an equal volume of PBS. C CCK-8 assay showing cell viability after cisplatin treatment for 0–96 h. Cisplatin treatment induces ferroptosis-related cellular responses

Cisplatin treatment significantly reduced the viability of MCF-7-S cells in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 3A). To explore the underlying mode of cell death, cells were treated with cisplatin in the presence of inhibitors targeting different regulated cell death pathways. Ferroptosis inhibitors, including Ferrostatin-1 and deferoxamine, partially restored cell viability following cisplatin treatment, whereas the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK exerted a comparatively weaker protective effect (Fig. 3A). Consistent with these findings, treatment with the ferroptosis inducers Erastin and RSL-3 markedly reduced cell viability, and this effect was reversed by Ferrostatin-1 (Fig. 3A). At the biochemical level, cisplatin and Erastin treatment increased intracellular ferrous iron levels (Fig. 3B) and elevated ROS and lipid ROS accumulation (Fig. 3C and D). These changes were attenuated by Ferrostatin-1. In addition, cisplatin treatment was associated with reduced expression of the ferroptosis suppressor FSP1, which was partially restored upon inhibition of ferroptosis (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these findings indicate that cisplatin induces multiple cellular features consistent with ferroptosis in MCF-7-S cells.

miR-4443 alters ferroptosis-related responses to cisplatin

To determine whether miR-4443 influences ferroptosis-related responses, MCF-7-S cells transfected with miR-4443 mimics or negative control were treated with cisplatin or ferroptosis inducers. Overexpression of miR-4443 was associated with increased cell viability under cisplatin or Erastin treatment compared with control cells (Fig. 4A). This protective effect was less pronounced when ferroptosis was pharmacologically inhibited, suggesting that ferroptosis contributes, at least in part, to miR-4443–mediated effects.

B and C Lipid ROS, MitoSOX, and iron accumulation levels in MCF-7-S cells after various treatments. D Intracellular Fe2+ levels were measured using an iron assay kit in MCF-7-S cells transfected with NC mimic or miR-4443 mimic and subsequently treated with cisplatin (5 µg/mL). Data are expressed as mean ± SD from three independent experiments. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 vs. NC-mimic; #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01 vs. Cisplatin-treated group

Furthermore, miR-4443 overexpression attenuated cisplatin-induced lipid ROS accumulation and reduced intracellular superoxide levels (Fig. 4B and C), as well as decreased Fe2+ iron accumulation (Fig. 4D). Western blot analysis revealed that miR-4443 overexpression was accompanied by increased FSP1 expression following cisplatin treatment (Fig. 4E).

Bioinformatic analyses identified a putative miR-4443 binding site within the 3’-untranslated region (3’-UTR) of FSP1. To evaluate whether miR-4443 can directly regulate FSP1 through this site, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays using constructs containing the wild-type (WT) FSP1 3’-UTR or a mutant (Mut) 3’-UTR with substitutions in the predicted seed-matching sequence. miR-4443 overexpression significantly reduced luciferase activity of the WT FSP1 3’-UTR reporter, whereas this inhibitory effect was markedly attenuated in the Mut reporter (Fig. 4F). These findings provide direct evidence that miR-4443 can regulate FSP1 via its 3’-UTR. Together, these results suggest that modulation of miR-4443 expression alters ferroptosis-related cellular responses.

FSP1 partially mediates miR-4443–associated cisplatin resistance and ferroptosis-related responses

To test whether FSP1 contributes functionally to miR-4443–associated phenotypes, we performed rescue experiments by modulating FSP1 expression in the context of miR-4443 gain- or loss-of-function. In MCF-7-S cells, miR-4443 overexpression increased cell viability under cisplatin treatment and attenuated ferroptosis-related readouts, including lipid ROS and intracellular ferrous iron accumulation. Notably, knockdown of FSP1 partially abrogated the protective effects of miR-4443 overexpression, resulting in reduced cell viability and restoration of ferroptosis-related signals compared with the miR-4443 mimic group (Fig. 5A and B).

Conversely, in MCF-7-R cells, inhibition of miR-4443 increased cisplatin sensitivity and enhanced ferroptosis-related responses. Re-expression of FSP1 partially rescued these effects, as reflected by increased cell viability and attenuation of Fe2+ iron accumulation (Fig. 5C and D). Western blot analysis further confirmed that miR-4443 negatively and directly regulates FSP1 expression in MCF-7 cells (Fig. 5E and F).Together, these data support that FSP1 contributes, at least in part, to miR-4443–associated cisplatin resistance and ferroptosis-related cellular responses.

miR-4443 reduces cisplatin efficacy in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo relevance of miR-4443, MCF-7-S cells transfected with miR-4443 mimics or negative control were implanted subcutaneously into nude mice. Cisplatin treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth in the control group, whereas tumors derived from miR-4443–overexpressing cells exhibited reduced responsiveness to cisplatin, as reflected by increased tumor volume and tumor weight (Fig. 6A–C). Analysis of tumor tissues, using immunohistochemistry (IHC) or Western blot, revealed that elevated FSP1 expression in tumors derived from miR-4443–overexpressing cells compared with controls (Fig. 6D-E). These in vivo findings are consistent with the involvement of ferroptosis-related pathways in miR-4443–associated cisplatin resistance, although additional molecular validation is required.

Discussion

Chemotherapy resistance remains a major challenge in the management of breast cancer and significantly limits the clinical efficacy of platinum-based agents such as cisplatin [10]. In this study, we investigated the role of exosomal miR-4443 in modulating cisplatin response and explored its relationship with ferroptosis-related cellular processes. Our findings indicate that exosomal miR-4443 is enriched in cisplatin-resistant breast cancer and is associated with reduced sensitivity to cisplatin in both in vitro and in vivo experimental models.

A central observation of this study is that exosomes derived from cisplatin-resistant breast cancer tissues and cell lines are capable of transferring miR-4443 to cisplatin-sensitive cells, resulting in attenuation of cisplatin-induced growth inhibition. This finding is consistent with accumulating evidence that tumor-derived exosomes mediate intercellular communication and contribute to the horizontal transfer of drug resistance traits through their molecular cargo, including microRNAs [11]. Our results extend these observations by identifying miR-4443 as a candidate exosomal miRNA associated with cisplatin resistance in breast cancer.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation, has emerged as an important determinant of chemotherapy response [11, 12]. In the present study, cisplatin treatment induced multiple cellular features consistent with ferroptosis, including increased iron accumulation, elevated reactive oxygen species and lipid ROS levels, and altered expression of ferroptosis-related regulators. Modulation of miR-4443 expression altered these ferroptosis-associated responses [13, 14], suggesting that miR-4443 may contribute to cisplatin resistance, at least in part [13–15], by influencing ferroptosis-related pathways. It should be noted, however, that although pharmacological inhibitors and biochemical markers were used to characterize ferroptosis-related responses, additional molecular markers would further strengthen the characterization of ferroptosis in this context.

Among ferroptosis-related factors, FSP1 emerged as a molecule associated with miR-4443–mediated effects [16, 17]. Bioinformatic analyses identified FSP1 as a potential downstream target of miR-4443, and dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that miR-4443 directly binds to the 3′UTR of FSP1. These findings support a direct regulatory interaction between miR-4443 and FSP1. However, further investigation is required to determine whether additional intermediates or parallel regulatory pathways also contribute to this regulatory axis. Therefore, the regulatory relationship between miR-4443 and FSP1 should be interpreted as associative rather than definitive, and future studies incorporating luciferase reporter assays will be necessary to establish direct targeting.

The in vivo xenograft experiments further supported the relevance of miR-4443 in modulating cisplatin response, as overexpression of miR-4443 reduced the antitumor efficacy of cisplatin and was accompanied by increased FSP1 expression in tumor tissues. These observations are consistent with the involvement of ferroptosis-related pathways in miR-4443–associated cisplatin resistance. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that additional in vivo validation of ferroptosis, such as immunohistochemical assessment of ferroptosis-related markers, would provide more comprehensive mechanistic insight.

From a translational perspective, our findings suggest that exosomal miR-4443 may represent a potential biomarker associated with cisplatin resistance [18] and highlight ferroptosis-related pathways [19] as possible therapeutic targets. However, the clinical component of this study was based on a relatively limited patient cohort, and larger, prospective studies will be required to validate the predictive value of exosomal miR-4443 in clinical settings. In addition, although the subcutaneous xenograft model used in this study provides useful in vivo evidence, it may not fully recapitulate the complexity of the human breast cancer microenvironment.

In summary, this study provides evidence that exosomal miR-4443 is associated with cisplatin resistance in breast cancer and may contribute to this phenotype through modulation of ferroptosis-related mechanisms. While the current findings offer a framework for understanding the role of miR-4443 in chemotherapy resistance, further mechanistic and clinical validation will be essential to fully elucidate its therapeutic and biomarker potential.
