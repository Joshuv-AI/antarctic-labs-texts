“I thought I was normal” children’s understanding of overweight and obesity: a qualitative study in chile
Authors: Cecilia Prieto Bravo, Nicola Boydell, Linda Bauld, Jeni Harden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood overweight and obesity are prevalent in Chile and globally. Health care responses typically emphasise biomedical and risk-focused approaches, with limited attention to children’s own perspectives.ObjectiveThis study explored how children clinically classified as overweight or obese understand health, growth and weight within health contexts.MethodsParticipants were recruited from primary care clinics in two municipalities in Santiago, Chile. Semi-structured online interviews were conducted with 18 children aged 10–12 years during COVID-19 restrictions, incorporating visual tools such as body silhouettes and body mapping to facilitate discussion. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsThree major themes were identified: Translating Fatness: everyday words and the softening of body labels; Body shapes, shame, and the making of ‘unhealthy’ bodies; and Making Sense of Measurement: Receiving, Interpreting and Negotiating Medical Labels.ConclusionsThe study highlights that children actively interpret and negotiate weight-related messages, drawing on emotional, social, and cultural frames rather than biomedical ones. Clinical encounters can heighten body awareness and stigma. Clinical practice should prioritise non-stigmatising communication, value children’s perspectives, and adopt approaches that support wellbeing during growth and identity development.Keywords: Children, overweight, obesity, qualitative research, primary healthcare

Introduction

The prevalence of childhood overweight and obesity has become a pressing global public health concern. Globally, rates of overweight and obesity among 5–19-year-olds have risen from 8% in 1990 to 20% in 2022 (World Health Organisation, 2025). In Chile, national data indicate that 51% of children and adolescents aged 10–19 are categorised as living with overweight, obesity, or severe obesity (Subsecretaría de Salud Pública, 2023). These figures also reveal marked inequalities by age, gender, and socioeconomic status.

Childhood overweight and obesity have largely been conceptualised and addressed through a predominantly biomedical lens. Through measuring and monitoring of weight and Body Mass Index (BMI), “higher weight” is framed as a present and future risk problem requiring clinical management (Censin et al., 2017; Lindberg et al., 2020). Within this approach, children’s own perspectives on health, growth, and body weight remain insufficiently explored, despite increasing recognition that children understand and respond to weight-related messages in ways that differ from adults’ assumptions.

Existing research on childhood obesity remains dominated by epidemiological and quantitative approaches (Fielden et al., 2011; Lachal et al., 2013). Even within qualitative studies, attention has focused primarily on parents’ understandings (Ma et al., 2023; Malek et al., 2023; Roberts et al., 2021; Sjunnestrand et al., 2025).

Although studies on overweight and obesity from the perspectives of children and young people are limited, some work has begun to examine how they interpret health and weight-related information (Concincion et al., 2024; Leaviss et al., 2025). Previous research examining this topic has shown that concerns about body size and its negative implications may emerge as early as ages 3−5 (Harriger & Trammell, 2022; Rodgers et al., 2019). Research has also shown that young children reproduce dominant discourses around bodies, obesity, and “healthy choices”, while at times resisting or reinterpreting these ideas (Pugmire & Lyons, 2018). These understandings may further shape peer relationships and social interactions by fostering weight-related biases and preferences (Harrison et al., 2016). Furthermore, the relational and emotional dimensions of childhood overweight show that children’s wellbeing is closely linked to loneliness, belonging, and social connectedness (Gunnarsson Mériaux et al., 2010).

Taken together, these findings illustrate that children’s perceptions of their own bodies do not develop in isolation; rather, they are heavily shaped by the relational emergence of body awareness during early cognitive development (León et al., 2021). Research indicates that peer interactions, family dynamics, and medical environments are key factors shaping a child’s body satisfaction (Puhl & Lessard, 2020; Rogers et al., 2019). When children internalise these influences, it directly impacts their emotional wellbeing and how they view their physical size (Warnick et al., 2022). Understanding this broader context is essential for analysing how children navigate weight classifications in their everyday lives. Indeed, research on stigma has shown that weight is highly stigmatised among children (Harriger & Trammell, 2022; Pont et al., 2017; Warnick et al., 2022) and that the physiological, social and psychological experiences of weight stigma might be seriously harmful, especially when children are transitioning to adolescence and constructing their own social identities.

However, despite these documented impacts of weight stigma, there is still limited knowledge of how children make sense of bodily changes and weight in their everyday environments, including family life, community settings and routine encounters with health services (Carroll et al., 2022; Rees et al., 2009; Roberts et al., 2021). These knowledge gaps limit understanding of how clinical classifications, measurement practices and subtle cultural cues shape children’s emerging sense of normality, health, and risk, sometimes in ways that can evoke discomfort or embarrassment.

This study addresses this gap by exploring how children diagnosed as “overweight” or “obese” understand health, growth and weight. By foregrounding children’s own language, interpretations, and emotional responses, the research offers qualitative insights relevant to child health professionals seeking to communicate more effectively, reduce unintended harms, and better support children’s wellbeing in both clinical and community settings.

A note on terminology

A number of the terms used throughout this paper are contested. Within biomedical understandings, “overweight” and “obesity” function as diagnostic categories, yet their use remains controversial. For that reason, this paper refers to “children with overweight/obesity’ or “children’s experiences with overweight or obesity”. In some instances, the terms appear in quotation marks to signal that they are socially constructed categories, open to multiple interpretations, and used here with critical awareness of their limitations.

Methods

Study design

An exploratory qualitative study was conducted in three primary care clinics within a local healthcare service network in the south-eastern area of Santiago, Chile, covering the municipalities of La Pintana and Puente Alto.

Recruitment and selection of participants

Purposive sampling was used to recruit children aged 10–12 years who had previously been classified in the clinic records as “overweight” or “obese”. According to Chilean health data, around 60% of children aged 10 to 12 are classified as “overweight” or “obese” (Subsecretaría de Salud Pública, 2023). These children, who are currently transitioning from childhood to adolescence, have been extensively exposed to messages from “Choose to Live Healthy”, a national, government-run intersectoral strategy, designed to provide a healthy lifestyle promotion framework aimed at improving nutrition and reducing overweight (Law 20670, 2013). Given that less is known about those children’s perspectives, we decided to explore this specific age group. Potential participants, children aged 10-12, diagnosed with overweight/obesity and their caregivers, were given a brief outline of the study by clinic staff during their clinical consultation. Eighteen children took part (N = 18), each completing one individual interview (see Table 1). In line with our research questions, this qualitative study prioritised depth and richness of data over large sample sizes.

Ethics

The study was approved by two ethics committees. Given that this study involved children discussing potentially sensitive topics related to their bodies and weight, particular attention was paid to ethical considerations throughout the research process (Lee, 1993; Powell et al., 2018).

Informed consent was obtained from parents or caregivers, and informed assent from child participants, prior to participation. Due to COVID-19 restrictions, consent and assent were obtained verbally rather than in written form, and all records were stored securely by the first author. Children and caregivers were given an explanation of the study’s purpose, their right to withdraw, and how their data would be stored and used. Confidentiality was explained to both children and caregivers in age-appropriate terms.

The sensitive nature of the topic, which involved discussions of body weight, body image, and clinical classification, required careful consideration of children's emotional wellbeing throughout. The first author developed the semi-structured topic guide, which was discussed and refined by all members of the research team (Supplemental material). The guide was piloted by the first author with two children of similar age to ensure that children understood the questions, further refine the wording and familiarise the first author with the online format; pilot interviews were not included in the study dataset. Interviews were conducted in a supportive and non-judgmental manner, with the first author attending carefully to signs of discomfort or distress. Children’s preferences regarding whether they were interviewed alone or accompanied by a caregiver were discussed in advance and respected, recognising that this choice itself reflects an important dimension of children’s agency and well-being. Older children (11−12 years old) preferred to be alone in a room during the interview, while two younger children chose to have an adult present.

Power dynamics inherent in adult-child research relationships, and in research on a topic that children may associate with clinical or social judgement, were carefully considered. Reflexive notes were written after each interview, including initial impressions and consideration of how the researcher’s role, positionality, and interactions during the interviews shaped the data collected. These reflections informed the subsequent stages of analysis and supported the interpretation of children’s accounts. The use of visual and interactive methods, including body silhouettes and body maps, was also intended, in part to provide children with alternative ways of engaging with sensitive topics, thereby reducing the potential for discomfort. A distress protocol was established to safeguard participants, allowing for the provision of emotional comfort if needed at both the clinics and neutral community-based youth centres.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted online due to COVID-19 restrictions. Each interview lasted approximately 50–60 minutes and was audio-recorded with consent. A semi-structured topic guide (Supplemental Material) was used to support consistency while allowing children to introduce issues of importance to them.

Visual and interactive techniques were incorporated to encourage engagement and make conversations about bodies, weight, and health more accessible. Visual representations can help make abstract or sensitive topics more meaningful and support children in narrating experiences related to the body, encouraging reflection (Gabb & Singh, 2015). As such, body silhouettes were used as prompts to explore children’s understandings of body shape and size (Figure 1). The silhouettes, adapted from the Respiratory Health in Northern Europe (RHINE III) survey (Dratva et al., 2016), and used in previous research (Agyapong et al., 2020; Lønnebotn et al., 2018; Sørensen & Stunkard, 1993; Sørensen et al., 1983), consisted of nine male and female figures increasing in size from left to right. Children were invited to select the silhouette that best represented their current or preferred body size.

Body maps were also used to explore children’s emotional and sensory experiences (Figures 2 and 3). Children placed emoticons (e.g., happy, I don’t know, shame, sad, angry (Figure 4) on an outline of a child’s body to indicate how they felt about specific body parts. These visual tools created opportunities to discuss complex or sensitive ideas that might not have emerged through verbal questioning alone. Body maps have been widely used in qualitative research to help children express embodied feelings and reflect critically on their experiences of health and sensitive topics (Arias-Urueña et al., 2024; Gabb & Singh, 2015; Hietanen et al., 2016).

Data analysis

Reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) was chosen as the analytical approach for this study as it is well-suited to exploring subjective meanings and experiences, and aligns with the study’s interpretive theoretical positioning (Braun & Clarke, 2021). Unlike other forms of thematic analysis, RTA foregrounds the active role of the researcher in knowledge production, recognising that themes are not simply identified in the data but are actively constructed through an iterative analytical process (Braun & Clarke, 2023). This approach was particularly appropriate for this study given its focus on children’s own understandings and meanings regarding their bodies and weight, where interpretive sensitivity and researcher reflexivity were essential to the analytical process. Interviews were transcribed verbatim in Spanish and translated into English. NVivo (Lumivero Version 14) was used to organise the data during coding and theme development.

The analysis was conducted by the first author and followed the six phases outlined by Braun and Clarke (2021): familiarisation with the data, generating initial codes, constructing themes, reviewing and developing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the final analysis. The process was iterative rather than linear, with ongoing movement between phases as the analysis developed. Initial familiarisation involved repeated reading of the interview transcripts. Coding was conducted systematically across the full dataset, with data segments –such as children’s descriptions of their bodies and weight (“chubby”, “gordito”, “guatita”, “normal”)– labelled and grouped according to their analytical significance in relation to the research questions. Preliminary themes were developed and refined by grouping and reviewing codes, ensuring that all participants’ perspectives were represented across the final themes.

The use of a single analyst is consistent with the principles of RTA, in which analysis is understood as an interpretive and reflexive process rather than one aimed at consensus or inter-coder reliability (Braun & Clarke, 2019; Byrne, 2021). While the first author conducted the primary coding and analysis, co-authors contributed through regular analytic discussions, critical review of theme development, and reflexive engagement with interpretations. The co-authors bring complementary expertise in applied qualitative research, critical public health, and health behaviour, and their engagement with the analysis was informed by their own positionality as researchers working in these fields (Braun & Clarke, 2019; Byrne, 2021). We acknowledge both the strengths and limitations of a single analyst approach: strengths include analytic depth, coherence, and sustained engagement with the data, while limitations include the potential for partiality. These limitations were addressed through ongoing reflexive practices and iterative discussions with co-authors to challenge and refine interpretations. The study was conducted in accordance with the criteria for qualitative research outlined in the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (Tong et al., 2007).

Findings

Thematic analysis generated three interrelated themes: i) Translating Fatness: everyday words and the softening of body labels ii) Shame, body shape and the making of “unhealthy” bodies; and iii) Making Sense of Measurement: Receiving, Interpreting and Negotiating Medical Labels. Illustrative extracts from the interview data are presented throughout, using pseudonyms for children’s names.

Translating Fatness: everyday words and the softening of body labels

Most children spoke about their bodies and their weight, but did not use the clinical classifications of “overweight” or “obese”. None described themselves as “obese”, even when they had been labelled as such in the clinic. This may reflect children’s unfamiliarity with medical terminology, but, notably, only a few of them described themselves using medical terms - “overweight” or “fat”.

The use of language may also indicate a preference for using their own, more meaningful language. Children often used Chilean expressions such as the word “gordito” (male) or “gordita” (female) to describe their bodies and weight. By adding the diminutive suffix “-ito” or “-ita” to the “gordo” word, they formed a softer word similar to “chubby” to describe themselves instead of saying “I am fat”. For instance, common phrases from the children were: “Estoy un poco gordito”/“I'm a little chubby” (male, aged 11) or “No quiero estar siempre asi gordita”/“I don't want to always be chubby like this” (female, aged 12).

Children’s use of terms like “gordito” (chubby) or “guatita” (belly fat) can be understood not only as part of a broader range of everyday language, which may reflect familiarity, or relational contexts (including how family members refer to them), but also as a deliberate strategy to mitigate embarrassment. These patterns of using gentler, colloquial words were consistently evident across other parts of the interviews with various participants. For instance, Santiago (male, aged 10) and Abi (female, aged 11) referred to their belly fat as “guatita” on several occasions, while Javi (female, aged 12) employed the word “rollito” to describe arm fat. Other children usually employed softened words when describing their bodies. Overall, children employed diverse language to characterise their bodies, utilising terms that did not necessarily align with medical terminology.

Those few children who did describe themselves as “fat” tended to soften their descriptions of “being fat” by adding other words or suffixes. For example:

Santiago (male, aged 10): I'm fat

Interviewer: And why do you think you’re fat?

Santiago: Ehm… because I have ‘guatita’ [belly fat].

Santiago located his fatness in his stomach, using the Chilean word “guatita” to soften his description, a term that translates roughly as “belly fat”. His response suggests a link between body image and a specific physical attribute that he viewed as indicative of his weight status. The use of “guatita” reflects a localised understanding of body composition, in which visible features, such as belly shape, contribute to children’s self-perceptions.

Our interpretation of “guatita” as a softening term is grounded in its common linguistic use as a diminutive, rather than as inherently positive. The incorporation of these gentler words serves as a strategic resource to alleviate potential embarrassment when children discuss their bodies.

Body shapes, shame, and the making of “unhealthy” bodies

Most children expressed discomfort with their body shape and described higher weight as something shameful or undesirable. The children expressed a preference for a thinner body or to “have a thin silhouette”, implicitly associating thinness with beauty, normality, and health.

For instance, Isa (female, aged 11), who identified her body with silhouette number 5, expressed a wish for a thinner body shape:

Interviewer: If you could choose a silhouette, would you choose the same one or another?

Isa (female, aged 11): Other

Interviewer: Which one would you choose?

Isa: The 3 or the 2 [silhouette]

Interviewer: Yeah, and why would you choose these?

Isa: Because… they are thinner.

Isa’s response suggests that thinner body shapes represented an ideal or socially valued standard of appearance. In contrast, the children associated being “overweight” with being “unhealthy” and unattractive. These narratives reflect an implicit internalisation of stigma surrounding fatness. For example, Abi said, 'I feel unhealthy because I have a bit of extra weight' (female, aged 12).

Some referred to the external influences shaping these views. Andrea (female, aged 12) explained that her desire for a thinner silhouette was motivated by medical advice, specifically being told to lose weight at the clinic:

Interviewer: And do you feel good with number 4 [silhouette], or would you change it for another?

Andrea (female, aged 12): like 3 [silhouette]

Interviewer: Like number 3 [silhouette], okay, and why would you change it?

Andrea: The doctor said I was… a little… overweight than normal, so I want to be with less mass in my body so that I'm healthier… so normal.

Andrea’s response demonstrates how medical classifications can shape children’s self-perceptions and body aspirations. Her wish to have “less mass” reveals how medical framings of normality and health influence children’s sense of self. The clinical classification of “overweight” not only reframed Andrea’s understanding of her body but may also have influenced her identity and well-being, and how she perceived herself following the medical encounter.

Children’s reflections also included how different body shapes might feel physically or socially. David (male, aged 12), who identified with silhouettes 7–8, linked his body size to sensations of heaviness and social discomfort:

Interviewer: And why do you identify with these silhouettes [7-8]?

David (male, aged 12): Because sometimes I see my body and it looks very much like the ones in the image.

Interviewer: And if you could choose… would you choose the same or another?

David: Between 4 and 5.

Interviewer: And why would you choose those?

David: Because... I think I would feel more... I mean... more, lighter, more like... because now I feel heavy, I don't feel good. But I think that with those, I'm going to feel good, I'm going to feel cool. I think so.

David’s account highlights how body image and embodied experience intertwine. His wish to “feel lighter” reflects both a physical and social ideal, one tied to comfort, confidence, and belonging. David expressed dissatisfaction with both his appearance and how it felt to him. He associated feeling “lighter” with feeling “good” and “cool”, suggesting a perceived link between body weight, self-perception, and social desirability.

When introducing body maps and emoticons, gendered differences were also evident. While both boys and girls expressed a shared aversion to belly fat, boys were more self-conscious about their chests. For instance, Carlos (male, aged 11) described a sense of sadness while discussing his chest’s increased size.

Interviewer: Mm-Hmm, and if we now look at your chest, what emoticon would you put on it?

Carlos (male, aged 11): Eh, sad [changes the tone of voice]

Interviewer: Sad, and why does the chest make you sad?

Carlos: Is that there I have like... a lot of chest... like that a lot.

Feelings of sadness, anger, or embarrassment linked to chest size among boys may reflect cultural associations between chest shape and size and the female body (femininity). This suggests that body image concerns among boys, though often less visible, are also shaped by gender norms.

Girls tended to express more anger or shame-related discomfort with fat on their arms or legs.

Interviewer: Which of these stickers [emotions] would you put on your legs?

Fernanda: eh… eh… embarrassment…

Interviewer: Could you tell me why Fer would put that sticker on it?

Fernanda: because... every time I... sit in a place... eh... they... [legs] they... [legs] get bigger, so I don’t like it (female, aged 11).

Fernanda’s description suggests that she felt self-conscious and uncomfortable about how her legs appeared when seated. The use of the word “bigger” implies that Fernanda may have perceived her legs as larger than she would prefer them to be, or expected, which contributed to her sense of discomfort and embarrassment. Failing to meet these non-clinical, gendered body standards transforms a normative physical occurrence into a source of immediate social shame.

Making sense of measurement: receiving, interpreting and negotiating medical labels

Children’s awareness of body changes often emerged through everyday experiences with clothing, which provided tangible cues of shifting size and shape. For example, Beto (male, aged 10) described noticing changes in his body before being diagnosed as “overweight”:

Beto: But it was me who discovered it; first [overweight], it wasn't my brother who came and told me, ‘Hey, you're fat, you're not like before' no. I realised it.

Interviewer: And how did you notice yourself differently?.

Beto: Mmm… I felt weird… because my look wasn't the same and… my favourite shirts had to go for new ones because they were starting to get too tight.

Beto’s account suggests a process of self-discovery and agency in understanding bodily changes when his clothes became too tight. The need to replace his “favourite shirts” marks a small but meaningful disruption to his daily life—a sign that his body was changing in ways he had not previously experienced.

However, for most children, the clinical encounter was pivotal in shaping their understanding of weight. It marked the moment when they became aware of their bodies as problematic. Routine measurements of weight and height, and being classified outside the “normal” BMI range, often led children to perceive their bodies as abnormal. Andrea (female, 12 years) described this experience as follows:

Andrea: They [health professionals] measured me, and weighed me… and in a notebook, they have standards: overweight, normal… less than overweight… and underweight…they saw my height and my weight, my mass. And then I saw that I was a little overweight.

Interviewer: And how did you feel when they told you that you were overweight?

Andrea: Like… I hadn't realised it because I thought I was normal.

Being categorised as “overweight” by a clinician transformed Andrea’s understanding of her body from normal to abnormal. This experience exemplifies how clinical labels can generate shame and reshape children’s self-concepts, making them hyperaware of their bodies and their deviation from perceived norms.

Similarly, Mateo (male, aged 11) reported feeling uncomfortable when clinicians noticed his weight:

Interviewer: And how was your experience in the clinic when they told you these things? How did you feel?

Mateo: Mmm… nothing… I got uncomfortable… but they treated me well (…)

Interviewer: And what was it that made you uncomfortable?

Mateo: [silence] That… [silence] that I… that they realise that I am overweight.

Mateo’s discomfort reflects the sensitivity of children to others’ judgements about their bodies, especially in settings where medical authority figures evaluate them. Flor also described her discomfort and embarrassment when others (clinicians) saw and evaluated her weight and the implications this might have for her future follow-up consultation:

Interviewer: How was that experience of going to the clinic for you?

Flor (female, aged 11): The truth is that I felt a little uncomfortable.

Interviewer: Why did you feel uncomfortable?

Flor: That… I don't like it very much, like… mmm… I'm a little embarrassed… let them see how much I weigh.

Interviewer: And if you had to go again now, would you go?

Flor: The truth... no, that’s the truth.

Interviewer: Why wouldn't you go again?

Flor: I don't know is that... I don't like being weighed.

Flor’s reluctance to be weighed again at the clinic highlights that, for some children in our sample, being weighed was not a neutral experience. While they did not explicitly say they felt judged, there was clearly a sense of discomfort at the close scrutiny to which they (their weight) were subjected.

Although many children described discomfort and embarrassment with being measured, few questioned the broader practice of monitoring weight, height, or BMI, or how these measurements were used to assess “normal standards.’ Nevertheless, there were also ways in which some children challenged the labels by temporalising their “overweight”. For most children, “overweight” was viewed not as a fixed condition but as a temporary state that could change naturally through growth. Marcela (female, aged 12) illustrated this by applying her understanding of the relationship of height and weight to describe how her status may change as she grows up:

Interviewer: Do you remember what they [health professionals] told you?

Marcela (female, aged 12): That… mmmmm… I had a few extra kilos.

Interviewer: Yeah.

Marcela: And then when I grow up because sometimes a few more centimetres I could complement it with losing weight, so it would look good there.

Similarly, Abi, who was aware of her body weight before going to the clinic, agreed to a follow-up consultation because her goal was to check her weight. Like Marcela, she was certain that increased height would lead to a change in her weight status such that she would be “fine”.

Interviewer: And would you like to go [to the clinic] again?

Abi (female, aged 11): Yes, yes, I would like to go again because I don't know if I weigh 50, 40 or 45 [kilograms].

Interviewer: Would you like to check the weight?

Abi: Yes… before I was very short so I say that now I must be fine because… now I am 1.62… no, 1.63, so… I must be fine now with my weight.

Marcela’s and Abi’s accounts highlight that children understand and can apply the medical information given to them. Children, may internalise and process the weight labels and measures they receive from health professionals, while personalising them and interpreting them through their own developmental logic.

Discussion

This study explored how children classified as “overweight” or “obese” understand and give meaning to weight, growth, and health in three primary care clinics in Santiago, Chile. The children revealed diverse ways of resisting, reinterpreting, or softening the labels given by clinicians, shedding light on the limits and possibilities of children’s agency in navigating medicalised definitions of the body. These findings might have relevance beyond Chile by showing how children actively negotiate and resist medicalised labels in ways that may also occur in other health systems, highlighting the need for more child-centred, culturally sensitive approaches to obesity care in primary health care.

Children’s language and meanings of “fatness”

Children rarely used biomedical terms such as “overweight” or “obesity” to describe themselves, instead preferring everyday expressions that felt more familiar and less judgmental. This aligns with qualitative research showing that children and adolescents often resist or reinterpret clinical labels in favour of everyday language (Fielden et al., 2011; Rees et al., 2009; Wills et al., 2006). Studies in the UK and Scotland have documented terms such as “chubby” or “heavy”, and have shown that being labelled “overweight” or “obese” is commonly perceived as undesirable, particularly among younger children (Fielden et al., 2011; Rees et al., 2009; Wills et al., 2006). Differences across studies may reflect age- or context-specific dynamics, as children aged 10–12 may be more sensitive to social comparison and stigma (Martin, 2015; Rees et al., 2009).

In this study, terms such as “gordito” or “gordita” appeared to carry softer connotations than clinical labels, enabling children to discuss their bodies without fully internalising stigmatising meanings. As documented in studies of Chilean discourse and identity (Joelson, 2005), there is a willingness among Chilean people to achieve consensus or adapt to the preferences of others, which causes them to use gentler and more endearing language, often by transforming words into diminutives. Perhaps, by employing these diminutive, familiar, and less clinical terms, children are attempting to convey their feelings about their bodies in a more approachable and emotionally manageable manner, to be kinder to themselves. This supports evidence that the meanings of weight-related terms are context-dependent and shaped by social and healthcare settings.

Alternative terms such as “gordito” or “gordita” may carry less negative connotations than clinical labels, allowing children to talk about their bodies without fully adopting stigmatising meanings. This resonates with research in the Netherlands that explored weight-related words used by children and adolescents in health-care contexts, which found that the meanings attached to such terms vary by context and setting (Stuij et al., 2020). Similarly, quantitative research conducted in the same country among individuals aged 7–19 suggests that health professionals should employ more positive or neutral language, such as “healthier weight” or “above a healthy weight” (Van Maarschalkerweerd et al., 2021).

Our findings echo previous research, which suggests a need for greater consideration of children’s perspectives and vocabulary in discussions surrounding weight-related issues, as well as the importance of considering cultural and social contexts when discussing these issues with them. Naming the body opens (or closes) pathways to seeking help, resisting stigma, and navigating treatment. Taken together, this suggests that health professionals should be attentive to children’s own vocabularies when discussing weight and health, recognising that language is shaped by cultural norms, affective responses, and social experiences rather than biomedical definitions alone.

Appearance, health, and the limits of biomedical framing

The children’s accounts of being “overweight” centred on appearance, bodily comfort, and social judgement. Health was often understood through visible or embodied cues, such as carrying “extra kilos” or having a prominent belly, rather than through biomedical notions of risk or disease. Similar findings have been reported elsewhere, in which children’s ideals of body size reflect broader social norms and expectations rather than clinical constructions of health (Drilen et al., 2025).

The study findings also echo critiques of obesity as a diagnostic category that may obscure rather than clarify children’s lived experiences of health (Gutin, 2022). For the children in this study, clinical classifications appeared to shape how bodies were evaluated and compared, sometimes intensifying concern about normal developmental changes. This highlights the importance of distinguishing between medical risk frameworks and children’s own understandings of their bodies and wellbeing.

Awareness, surveillance, and stigma

For many children in this study, awareness of “being overweight” emerged during moments of measurement and evaluation in clinics. Such encounters often transformed previously unremarkable bodily awareness into a sense of difference or abnormality. Drawing on concepts of the “dys-appearing bodies” (Leder, 1990) and the hyperawareness of the body (Evans et al., 2021), these findings suggest that clinical categorisation might heighten bodily self-consciousness, particularly within settings characterised by power asymmetries.

Children’s accounts also point to how institutional practices can make bodies visible and open to judgement, reinforcing feelings of embarrassment or discomfort. This is especially salient during puberty, a period marked by rapid bodily change and identity formation (Viner et al., 2019). Experiencing weight-related scrutiny at this stage may contribute to feelings of shame, and have potential implications for wellbeing, as suggested by existing research on weight stigma (Puhl & Heuer, 2010; Puhl et al., 2007).

Negotiating measurement and growth

BMI was the dominant indicator used in clinical assessments, however, children engaged with these evaluations primarily through everyday understandings of height, weight and growth, rather than biomedical categories. Their accounts suggest not a direct critique of BMI as such, but negotiations of how these measurements relate to their lived bodily experiences. Similar debates about the appropriateness of BMI as an indicator of children’s health have been raised elsewhere, with critics highlighting its limitations in capturing children’s developmental trajectories and lived experiences (Gard & Wright, 2005; Grogan, 2021; Gutin, 2021; Koivumäki et al., 2025).

Previous research suggests that children and caregivers may be familiar with BMI as a height-weight measure even when clinical terminology is not fully understood (Van Maarschalkerweerd et al., 2021). In the present study, children demonstrated an understanding of BMI in these terms; however, they frequently challenged its relevance to their own bodies. Rather than viewing BMI as a stable indicator of health, children framed their weight and height as temporary and changing, shaped by growth and puberty rather than as predictors of their future adult bodies. This points to a disconnect between biomedical classifications and children’s own understandings of growth and health.

Reliance on BMI and routine measurement may also contribute to experiences of discomfort or stigma, particularly when assessments foreground weight over broader aspects of wellbeing. Children’s accounts suggest that negative experiences of weighing and labelling can shape how clinical encounters are perceived. Consistent with previous research, weight stigma within health care settings may discourage future engagement with health services, in relation to weight but also other health concerns (Alberga et al., 2019; O’Donoghue et al., 2021; Phelan et al., 2015; Puhl et al., 2014). Evidence indicates that weight stigma remains prevalent within primary health care, underscoring the need for careful reflection on how measurement practices are enacted and communicated (Kyler et al., 2024).

Gender and embodiment

Gendered patterns were evident in children’s responses to negative stereotypes associated with higher-weight bodies. Both boys and girls described discomfort with their bodies following weight classification, suggesting that weight-related judgements shaped how they felt about themselves. However, gender differences emerged in the specific concerns children expressed about body fat distribution, reflecting socially embedded expectations of femininity and masculinity. Within a cultural context such as Chile, where binary and heteronormative ideals of masculinity persist, these experiences may contribute to body-related insecurities and reduced self-esteem among boys as well as girls.

This divergence aligns with Harjunen (2023) analysis of the gendered fat body, which posits that societal constructions of weight do not affect bodies uniformly; rather, they intersect with patriarchal norms that demand thinness and physical minimisation from girls, while confronting boys with rigid standards of muscularity and structural size within a neoliberal context. Likewise, these gendered patterns align with the feminist research of Sutton (2010), who, through interviews with Argentine women, problematised what it means to be a woman in the region and outlined the strict rules of normative femininity.

Body dissatisfaction and identity formation in relation to weight have been more extensively explored among young women and girls (Grogan, 2021; Rees et al., 2009), particularly in Western contexts, where female bodies are subject to more intense and critical scrutiny than those of men. Furthermore, ideals of the female body have narrowed considerably, alongside increasingly negative attitudes toward fatness (Harjunen, 2023; Sutton, 2010).

Existing research has explored weight stigma and masculinity primarily in adult men (Monaghan, 2023). In these accounts, “fat men” are often represented as soft, vulnerable, or feminised bodies, and there is evidence that men and boys may be stigmatised when their bodies diverge from dominant masculine ideals (Martino & Pallotta-Chiarolli, 2003; Monaghan & Malson, 2015). However, there remains relatively limited qualitative research examining how younger boys experience and make sense of being labelled as “overweight”.

Consequently, our findings highlight the critical value of adopting feminist lenses when analysing bodies, weight, and clinical classifications in younger childhood populations, while underscoring the importance of attending to boys’ gendered experiences of body shape and weight.

Study strengths and limitations

This study provides in-depth qualitative insight into how children make sense of weight and health within primary care contexts in Chile. To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study in the region to use online methods to explore understandings of health, growth and weight among children diagnosed as “overweight” or “obese”. However, the findings are drawn from a specific urban setting and age group and may not reflect the experiences of all children. Interviews were conducted online due to COVID-19 restrictions, which may have shaped how children expressed themselves. While the study foregrounds children’s perspectives, it does not include the views of health professionals, which could be explored in future research.

Implications for practice

The findings suggest that primary health care professionals should be attentive to how children understand and experience discussions about weight, growth, and health. Approaches that focus exclusively on weight or risk may overlook children’s embodied and emotional experiences. Greater sensitivity to language, attention to privacy during measurement, and communication that acknowledges developmental change may help reduce unintended distress. Supporting health professionals working with children who are classified as “overweight” to reflect on assumptions about weight and health, including through training, may contribute to more child-centred care.

Conclusion

This study highlights that children have their own perspectives, meanings, and language for understanding weight, growth, and health, which do not always align with biomedical classifications. Recognising these perspectives is important, as mismatches between clinical labels and children’s self-understandings may contribute to feelings of stigma or disengagement. Attending to children’s accounts can support more responsive and sensitive approaches to child health care.
