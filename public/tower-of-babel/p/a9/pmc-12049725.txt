Introduction

Antero-septal intramural scar in nonischemic cardiomyopathy is one of the most challenging substrates to modify with radiofrequency catheter ablation for ventricular tachycardia (VT).1,2 Bipolar ablation has been reported as a bail-out treatment option for intramural substrates caused by transmural lesion creation.3,4 Conversely, an effective radiofrequency delivery from an anatomical structure distinct from but adjacent to the earliest activation site of a ventricular arrhythmia has been reported as an anatomical approach.5 However, few reports exist about the feasibility of an anatomic approach in scar-related VT patients.

Case report

A 61-year-old man with cardiac sarcoidosis was referred for catheter ablation of a sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT). The morphology of the clinical VT exhibited a right bundle branch block pattern with a left inferior axis, suggesting that the origin was the anterior basal septum (Figure 1A). Enhanced cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed a septal late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), and an isolated intramural substrate was predicted in the superior intraseptal region (Figure 1B).6

B: An Image of enhanced cardiac magnetic resonance before the procedure. This shows a full-length septal LGE and isolated intramural LGE in the superior intraseptal region (yellow circle). RV = right ventricle; LV = left ventricle; VT = ventricular tachycardia; LGE = late gadolinium enhancement.

An over-the-wire (OTW) multielectrode catheter (EPstar Fix AIV [2.7 Fr]; Japan Lifeline, Tokyo) was inserted into the septal perforator vein (Figure 2A, B). The clinical VT was easily induced by programmed stimulation. At the earliest activation site on the left ventricular (LV) septum, the local potential preceded the onset of the QRS by 34 ms during the VT. Conversely, a long fractionated diastolic potential was recorded from electrodes 1,2 of the OTW catheter in the septal perforator vein, suggesting a reentrant mechanism (Figure 2C, Supplemental Video). We could not perform entrainment mapping because of VT termination by overdrive ventricular pacing. Multiple unipolar radiofrequency applications from the LV close to electrodes 1,2 were performed with an irrigated radiofrequency (RF) catheter (TactiFlex, Abbott, Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL) with a maximum output of 50 W, temperature limit of 45°C, and 13 to 30 mL/min flow rate. The VT terminated during the radiofrequency delivery, but the inducibility was not inhibited even after intensive ablation from the LV. Given that a long fractionated potential recorded from one bipolar electrogram covered the entire diastolic period, a small circuit was predicted.7

We supposed that the VT could be eliminated by creating a transmural lesion in a small area and decided to perform bipolar ablation targeting electrodes 1,2 of the OTW catheter. Another irrigated RF catheter (FlexAbility, Abbott) was used as the ground on the right ventricular (RV) septum (Figure 3A). The connection of the 2 ablation catheters was performed as shown in Supplemental Figure 1. We used 2 radiofrequency generators to monitor the temperature of the ground catheter during the bipolar ablation. The active catheter was connected to generator A, and the pin of the T-cable (IBI GenConnect, Abbott) was connected to the impedance port of generator A with a jumper cable. The T-cable was then connected to generator B and the ground catheter.8 With this setup, the impedance between the 2 ablation catheters could be monitored during the bipolar ablation.

Successful anatomic approach with bipolar ablation targeting the late potentials recorded by the over-the-wire microelectrode catheter. A: The catheter position for the bipolar ablation. B: The late potentials recorded on electrodes 1–2 of the OTW catheter were eliminated by multiple bipolar ablation applications.

During sinus rhythm, fractionated late potentials were recorded within the septal perforator vein. However, the ablation catheter on the RV and LV surface did not record any late components during sinus rhythm (Figure 3B). We manipulated the 2 ablation catheters to place them close to the electrodes of the OTW catheter, using fluoroscopic guidance (anatomic approach). The distance between the tip of the active (LV septum) and ground (RV septum) catheters was 24 mm. Those ablation catheters were 13 mm and 11 mm, respectively, away from the OTW catheter. To obtain stable contact with the 2 catheters, the assistant doctor held the ground catheter placed on the RV septum. Radiofrequency delivery was started at 20 W and was gradually increased to 35 W from the LV side with a 10 to 20 g contact force. The local impedance decreased by 19 ohms during a 60-second ablation application. There was no injury current recorded by the OTW catheter during the bipolar ablation (Supplemental Figure 2). A total of 30 bipolar ablation applications eliminated the late potentials on the OTW catheter, after a gradual delay of the late components (Supplemental Figure 3). After the elimination of the intramural late potentials, noninducibility was achieved. After the procedure, the S wave in lead inferior leads became larger on the 12-lead electrocardiogram during sinus rhythm, suggesting that the left anterior fascicle was injured (Supplemental Figure 4).

The patient had been followed up for 26 months, and no further VT from the septal substrate had recurred. Twelve months after the procedure, another VT from an LV lateral substrate occurred, and a repeat procedure successfully eliminated the VT.

Discussion

This report highlighted that an anatomic approach with bipolar ablation could modify an isolated intramural septal substrate in a nonischemic scar-related VT patient. In the current case, the target of this approach was based on the bipolar electrograms recorded by the OTW catheter in the septal perforator vein. High-density septal branch mapping with the OTW catheter could provide more detailed information for the specific intramural ablation target than wire mapping.9 The anatomic approach was first reported in patients with idiopathic ventricular arrhythmias by Yamada et al.5 They described that ablation with an anatomic approach was performed at the shortest distance from the earliest ventricular activation. In this case, the catheter placed in the RV was closer to the OTW catheter than the LV catheter. This may explain why an ablation from the LV alone was ineffective. In the bipolar ablation, another option was to use the LV side as the ground and deliver the RF energy from the RV. However, we chose the LV site as the active catheter because the RV catheter was positioned parallel to the cardiac surface, suggesting a larger lesion could be created as reported previously.10 A unique aspect of this case was that the electrodes of the OTW catheter in the septal perforator vein might have worked as passive electrodes during bipolar ablation. The distance between the 2 ablation catheters during bipolar ablation was 24 mm, which was not always short enough to create transmural lesions.11 The electrodes in the path of bipolar ablation might have functioned as an additional heat sink to encourage a transmural lesion creation. Although an ethanol infusion into the septal perforator vein may have been effective in this case, the limitation was the inability to accurately predict the size of the lesion.12,13 In the current case, the disappearance of the fractionated potentials on the OTW catheter was an indicator of an effective ablation. Moreover, the change in the 12-lead electrocardiogram also suggested an intensive lesion creation. What the appropriate indicators and safe wattage are for an effective anatomic approach for an isolated intramural substrate in scar-related VT remains unclear. A further detailed systematic analysis of this theme is desired.

Conclusions

When a critical site of VT is identified within a septal venous branch, an anatomic approach with bipolar ablation may be a beneficial treatment option for an isolated intramural septal substrate in patients with nonischemic cardiomyopathy.
