Assessment of the COVID-19 impact on the Brazilian Unified Health System (SUS) financing: an analysis of the financing dynamics of 2020 and 2021
Authors: Ana Carolina Costa Corrêa, Maria Luísa Oliveira Rigotti, Hugo Dilhermando Souza Lacerda, Bruno Pérez Ferreira
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAfter the establishment of the public health emergency of international concern in 2020, health systems worldwide and in Brazil observed the need to apply more extraordinary logistical efforts and possibly resources to combat the imminent pandemic.MethodsUsing the historical series of public expenditures of the National Health Fund (FNS), 2015 to 2021, the number of confirmed cases of COVID-19, and a seasonal ARIMAX model, we sought to assess how the increase in the new virus infections affected the systematic financing of the SUS in Brazil.ResultsThere were signs of seasonality and an increasing trend in the expenditure variable, which in practical terms, only indicated that the resource contributions followed an increasing trajectory already underway before the advent of the pandemic. The 1% increase in COVID-19 cases, with a one-month lag, contributes to the 0.062% increase in the variation in FNS expenditures but a decrease of 0.058% with a two-month lag.ConclusionThe tests showed no evidence to confirm a positive shift on FNS spending growth trajectory due to the increase of COVID-19 cases, only observing a significant increase one month after the occurrence of COVID cases, probably due to their worsening after this period, which was followed by a similar and comparable decrease in percentage of growth in the following month.

Background

Formally instituted by Law No. 8080 of September 19, 1990 [1], the Brazilian Unified Health System (SUS) is one of the largest and most complex public health systems in the world. Its scope of action ranges from the basic levels of primary care to highly complex procedures such as transplants and other hospital treatments; its objective is to provide universal access to health [2] based on the principles of universalization, equity, and comprehensiveness (integrality) [1, 2]. In Brazil, it is estimated that around 76% [3, 4] of the population relies exclusively on the system (SUS dependents), not being covered by health plan operators [3, 4].

The SUS financing base is the taxes collected by the three entities of the federation: Union, States, and Municipalities. Regarding the funding at the federal level – National Health Fund (FNS)—established by Decree No. 64,867 of July 24, 1969 [5], monitored and supervised by the Executive Secretariat of the Ministry of Health, manages the resources transferred to the states, municipalities, and the Federal District so that these entities carry out health actions and services in a decentralized manner, besides investing in the service network, and care and hospital coverage [5].

On December 31, 2019, the World Health Organization (WHO) was notified of cases of pneumonia in Wuhan City, Hubei Province, People’s Republic of China. From that moment on, what would later be called the COVID-19 pandemic was established. On January 30, 2020, the WHO declared that the outbreak of the new coronavirus constitutes a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) – the Organization’s highest alert level, as provided for in the International Health Regulations [6]. An unprecedented global crisis was quickly established, with the collapse of healthcare systems worldwide as it’s crucial point. Lockdown policies were implemented in several countries as a measure to prevent an eventual collapse of systems, pressure especially on the supply of ICU beds are factors that justified social distancing measures, a fact that did not spare the SUS from being affected, especially with several peaks in the new coronavirus case numbers [7]. Given that, in such a young system, 32 years old, there was a need to make logistical and budgetary efforts never before made.

Health expenditure has been analyzed across different countries in the context of the pandemic. A systematic review examined national health financing policy responses for 15 countries, including Brazil [8]. Findings suggested that one of the main strategies was the establishment of extra-budgetary funds—Brazil’s primary response. A similar study in the WHO South-East Asia Region demonstrate great response flexibility, despite pre-existing underinvestment in those countries [9]. Research conducted with Italian data showed that the national financing system covered costs incurred by the pandemic [10]. On the other hand, evidence from Europe reveal persistent weaknesses in health financing policy [11]. Finally, a study on “catastrophic health expenditure” in Belarus, Mexico, Peru, Russia, and Viet Nam, with a time series approach, identified possible shifts to private care and the necessity to reestablish the public sector [12]. Such studies demonstrate heterogeneity when it comes to health expenditure approaches. While some health systems had pre-existing investment or sufficient structural changes to accommodate the impact of the pandemic, others revealed weaknesses in health financing policies to deal with an unexpected shock.

In the context of the COVID-19 pandemic and considering its contagion peaks in the Brazilian territory, this article aims to evaluate the impacts of the virus contagion on the dynamics of SUS funding at the federal level concerning resource transfers. The paper proposes the introduction of a time series model to evaluate such impact.

Methods

ARIMA models have been applied to model and forecast health expenditure in a variety of countries using the Box-Jenkins methodology. Recent studies have used the methodology with annually aggregate data to predict total health expenditure in the US [13]. ARIMA models were also used to predict changes in total health expenditure, social health expenditure, government health expenditure and out-of-pocket health expenditure in China [14]. A similar study was conducted in Iran to calculate total health expenditure, public health expenditure and out-of-pocket payment [15]. The ARIMA model is suitable to model public health expenditure since it carries procedure changes through the time range and considers the seasonality in public regulation.

ARIMA models can also be expanded to assess the effects of exogenous variables (ARIMAX models). Applications of this methodology have been used in the context of viral diseases, such as to evaluate the association between weather on Influenza and COVID-19 transmission [16, 17]. Given the suitability of model for health expenditure data and evaluating exogenous effects, this paper is an application of the ARIMAX model to evaluate the association between COVID-19 cases and public health expenditure in Brazil. This methodology relies on the hypothesis that the number of cases is exogenous to health expenditure, that is, public health expenditure comes before COVID-19 cases and is affected by it. Furthermore, the usage of an ARIMA model assumes that changes in funding are taken into consideration to estimate the next observations, such as government policies which raise expenditures and may impact the funding trajectory for the following months.

To assess COVID-19 possible impacts on the SUS funding dynamics, a seasonal ARIMAX model was used, which regresses FNS expenditures to their lagged observations and number of confirmed COVID-19 cases in Brazil as an exogenous variable, both monthly, nationally aggregated [18]. The data on FNS expenditures COVID-19 cases are publicly available [19, 20]. The series of health expenditures financed by the SUS was obtained from daily FNS microdata from January 2015 to December 2021, summed for all municipalities in the federation, and grouped monthly, as in Fig. 1 [19]. Complementarily, the series of disease cases was extracted from the Ministry of Health portal, also daily—from February 25, 2020, to December 31, 2021, summing up each municipality and grouping them monthly; to complete the series, a zero value was added to months from January 2015 to January 2020 (see Fig. 2) [20]. The Covid-19 cases series starts on February 25, 2020, because the first case in Brazil was announced in February 26, 2020. The requested FNS microdata were made available starting from January 2015 and the most recent aggregate data were available up to December 2021 at the time of the research, which resulted in a time series from January 2015 to December 2021. As the variables have very different magnitudes, two possibilities were used to estimate the models: models with expenditures on the FNS in millions or logarithms, in both, using the logarithm of COVID-19 cases. The series of expenditures with the FNS was adjusted by the dollar exchange rate provided by Ipeadata [21]. Augmented Dickey-Fuller tests were used to determine the model’s integration order [18]. The tests evaluate if a time series has a unit root – is not stationary—for three specifications: trend and constant, no constant and no trend, and constant with no trend. The time series was considered stationary after a simple or seasonal differentiation, which indicated that the model should be estimated integration order one. Once the integration order was defined, 27 different models were compared according to coefficient, residual and information criterion tests. Significance Z tests were performed on the regression coefficients (Table 1). The hypotheses on residuals were tested using the Ljung-Box test for twelve lags for autocorrelation, Shapiro and Jarque–Bera tests for normality, and a conditional autoregressive heteroskedasticity test. Wald tests were applied to test the joint significance of the coefficients, considering a significance level of 5%. Finally, we conducted the regression that reconciled all residue tests by a seasonal treatment, the X-13 ARIMA, of the expenditure series with the FNS from the “seasonal” package made available by the US “Census Bureau,” in which ARIMA models are adjusted from exogenous variables that contribute to their stability [23]. The exogenous variable created is a time series, defined from seasons, holidays, or events in a given year, with the distribution of its immediate, previous, and subsequent effects and zero value for periods without impact on the dependent variable of the original model. AIC information criterion and root-mean-square deviation were analyzed to compare the models. The possible models were evaluated regarding the coefficient and residual tests and information criterion, which resulted in the final model ARIMA(2,1,0)(1,1,0) [12]. Considering the models that confirmed the hypotheses on coefficients and residuals, this model minimized the AIC criterion. The software R was used for the statistical tests.

Methods – dollarization

The model proposed in this article is presented in a currency different from the country in which the financing of the public health system is analyzed. Before presenting the final arguments to support the decision for monetary conversion, we must understand how the productive base that supplies the health complex and its maintenance is structured.

It is possible to mark out a health industrial complex that involves a set of industries that support the specificities of the health system in which there is notable sectoral interdependence. In this context, it is possible to delimit three groups that make up this intersectionality and design the health industrial complex. The primary sector condenses industries, mainly pharmaceuticals, reagent vaccines, and blood products, with the drug sector being the largest of this set. The second sector comprises the physical, mechanical, electronic, and material bases condensed in the medical-hospital equipment industry. At last, the third sector concentrates on activities related to health service provisions, encompassing hospital, outpatient, and diagnostic structures [24].

Health components, in general, employ high technology, which makes innovation a national strategic differentiator; in this context, a low potential to disseminate innovation in the fine chemicals, electronics, and biotechnology sectors determines the productive potential of a country regarding drugs, medical-electronic equipment, vaccines, diagnostic reagents, and blood products [24].

Since we have described the synthetic structure of the health complex, it is necessary to observe the example of the Brazilian context that justifies the choice for monetary conversion. All productive segments until 2001 were characterized by dependency on imports. Previous periods also showed high deficit intensification in the sector’s payment balance due to the exchange rate devaluation [24].

Exchange rate variations can lead to an increase in health care spending or straight in imports for that purpose. The aspect of currency devaluation at an early stage can put pressure on health spending that is not consistent with the current budget availability, and the import reduction effect would be felt later [24].

The sensitivity that hovers before the Brazilian health system maintenance due to its dependence on input importation, such as drugs and equipment, was also perceived during the COVID-19 coronavirus pandemic. The growing demand for respirators and pulmonary ventilators, medicines and drugs, and personal protective equipment reflected in balance deficits, which were not greater due to the supply shortage caused by the proper pandemic and for exporters trying not to be affected by that scarcity [25].

These arguments support Brazil’s current need to access the international market to support and maintain SUS and also lead to the choice of currency conversion, more specifically, the choice of executing the model with values in dollar currency. The dollar prevails as the international reference currency due to two main factors: its use in international reserves and the large volume of trade in its country of origin, the United States. Despite the US not being involved in all negotiations, the dollar is still the reference currency for international reserves and the liquidity capacity of US bonds [26].

Results

From a graphical analysis (Fig. 3), there were indications of seasonality and a growing tendency of the expenditure variable. The analysis of autocorrelation functions and partial autocorrelation functions also indicated seasonality—with a peak up to the third order and in the twelfth order. Graphical analysis, stationarity tests, autocorrelation functions and partial autocorrelation functions led to specifying the following model combinations: autoregressive component from one to three, simple and/or seasonal integration of order one, moving average component of order zero or one, and the seasonal autoregressive component of order zero or one.Fig. 3Comparison between logarithm values of the expenditure volumes with the FNS in dollars observed and adjusted by the model with two lags of the logarithm of COVID-19 cases from January 2015 to December 2021

Comparison between logarithm values of the expenditure volumes with the FNS in dollars observed and adjusted by the model with two lags of the logarithm of COVID-19 cases from January 2015 to December 2021

The model was stabilized using the “seasonal” package, which identified a variable that applies positive impact before and after Easter. Since the data are aggregated monthly, the package indicates a seasonal increase in April expenses. The estimated model was not directly extracted from the X13 adjustment (seasonal package): the time series referring to Easter was added to the previously estimated ARIMA models. Better results were obtained with both variables in logarithm, which eliminated the possibility of conditional autoregressive heteroskedasticity and allowed the analysis of the percentage impact of COVID-19 cases on FNS expenditures over the period.

The final model, ARIMA(2,1,0)(1,1,0) [12], minimized the result of the AIC information criterion, which was prioritized for having more parsimonious results. All coefficients are significant, individually and collectively, and there is no evidence to reject the residual hypotheses according to the tests above. The adjustment was closer to the observed data when the lags of one and two months of the cases logarithms are added as exogenous variables and the current cases variable was removed, because it was not significant when added to the other two. The model accuracy according to the criterion of the root-mean-square deviation is better than that of the model without exogenous variables of the cases. Removing the current COVID-19 cases variable indicates that, after the increase in cases across the country, there is a lag of at least one month for a significant impact on expenditure. Thus, the final model represents the association between current variation in SUS financing, previous variations in financing with one- and two-months lag, and number of COVID-19 cases in one- and two-months lag (Table 1).

Since the model has data in logarithm and first integration order, the results must be interpreted as percentual changes in the variation of funding. The xreg1 and xreg2 coefficients show the effects of COVID-19 cases in previous month and two before the observation on the variation of current SUS funding: a 1% increase in the number of COVID-19 cases in Brazil generates, after one month, an average increase of 0.0621% in financing variation from the FNS, and, after two months, a decrease of 0.0588%.

In addition to the number of cases, the final model also includes autoregressive coefficients, in which it is possible to evaluate the effects of previous health expenditure in current financing. According to the final model, lags in the increase of SUS financing in Brazil negatively impact its current growth: a 1% increase in variation one and two months before the observed decreases the present variation by about 0.34% and 0.28%, respectively. The third lag is not significant at a 5% significance level, which means that, besides seasonal effects, the variation of financing in SUS depends on the variation from the past two months. A possible interpretation would be that the growth generated in one month, free of seasonal impacts, dissipates over the next two months.

The study has also identified seasonality in April. The possible explanation for the seasonal increase of expenses in April is that the resource transfer flows are dependent on the annual budget laws that go through the House of Representatives for consideration and later publication. The execution of the publication acts occurs in mid-January, which may indicate an execution schedule that starts the transfer of resources from the maintenance and investment blocks of the FNS only in April, right after all the technical and technological procedures are completed to start the massive transfer of resources.

In summary, the estimated model indicates that COVID-19 case numbers in Brazil in 2020 and 2021 did not produce long-term financial effects on SUS’ structure. In other words, the financial inputs to the SUS will probably follow an increasing trajectory, which already existed before the onset of the pandemic, and respond to variation on COVID-19 cases for up to two months. On average, there is a significant increase in the funding variation one month after the occurrence of COVID cases, probably due to their worsening after this period, followed by a similar and comparable decrease in percentage in the following month. Therefore, the current research indicates that SUS financing responded to a chock, represented by the number of cases, and may return to the pre-pandemic growth trend in the absence of cases—Fig. 4 shows the difference between the estimated FNS expenditure with the effects of COVID-19 cases (ARIMAX) and without it (ARIMA). Discussion

Regarding the health sphere, since its creation in 1990, the SUS has become the principal pillar of public policies in Brazil, most of which are funded by the FNS. For the period analyzed in the model—2015 to 2021—expenditures with the FNS have had a consistently increasing trajectory. However, despite the growth, authors point it out as still insufficient [27–31]. Still, in the pre-pandemic context, Santos and Vieira point out several implications of austerity policies amid the economic recession in Brazil [7]. As of Constitutional Amendment number 95, approved in 2016, a spending ceiling was established regarding the Union’s primary expenses, but with no limit for financial expenses, which removed the link between health expenses and revenues in 2018.

Servo et al. discuss the financing of the SUS, both historically and at the beginning of the pandemic [32]. The advent of the Coronavirus comes at a time when, despite a total spending increase, there is a reduction in the federal funding share and coordination problems between the other entities. The federal participation reduction is worrying since the minimum application of health resources depends on the federal government, given its attributions and collection capacity. Given the release and execution delays of the new resources approved by the National Congress, the authors conclude that there are no perspectives to prioritizing the SUS and expanding its financing in the post-pandemic period.

Our findings suggest that national health funding in Brazil responded to short-term shocks represented by the number of COVID-19 cases, but there is no evidence to conclude that there was a long-term structural change. The findings corroborate existing literature on health financing policies during the COVID-19 pandemic and show both differences and similarities to other countries’ responses. Dee Foo et al. identified that, in Brazil, the primary response was the approval of Emergency Constitutional Amendments that bypassed conventional budget regulations [8]. Other countries such as India, Mexico and South Africa relied on reallocation from existing programs [8]. The results of short-term responses in Brazil are consistent with the mentioned approach, since the amendments do not change the FNS structure, rather provide additional funding.

Evidence from Latin-American, European and Asian responses also demonstrate financing systems weaknesses. An analysis of catastrophic health expenditure in Belarus, Mexico, Peru, Russia and Vietnam concluded that health systems failed to maintain health care access in 2020 [12]. Shifting to private care may require policies to motivate patients to return to the public sector, thus restructure the financing system. Countries in the WHO South-East Asia Region, despite demonstrating great flexibility with revenue raising and removing financial barriers, revealed pre-existing underinvestment and inadequate regulatory capacity [9]. Responses to COVID-19 in Europe reveal persistent weaknesses in health financing policy [11]. Thomson et al. suggested that permanent changes are needed in order to reduce cyclicality and raise revenues in the future [11]. As this study shows that the financial increase in Brazil ceases, on average, after two months, permanent changes to SUS financing would be more appropriate to handle the long-term effects of the pandemic and possible economic shocks in the future. The establishment of a specific line for COVID-19 spending in the FNS was a pioneering but temporary approach. The establishment of a FNS permanent fund, instead of extra-budgetary funds, could improve the Brazilian response.

In contrast, some regions demonstrated greater capacity to accommodate costs and promote structural changes. Evidence from two regions in Italy demonstrated that, unlike in Brazil, national fundings were able to cover costs incurred by the pandemic [10].

Conclusion

The final model in this study brought unexpected results given the pandemic context, which profoundly affected the country in terms of infected people, deaths, and countless economic impacts. Faced with such health and economic crisis, several authors recommended increasing the existing investment growth in public health spending to diminish contamination and return economy to the expected levels, at least in terms of the internal market. The model, which evaluates the spending variation on the FNS, indicates that the temporal trajectory of the FNS alone would already cause similar growth compared to the observed from 2020 to 2021, as in Fig. 4. In this way, the spending policy during the pandemic responded to the number of COVID-19 cases in the short term: on average, a month after a 1% increase in cases, the spending variation on the FNS (adjusted for seasonality and given its time dependence) increases by 0.062%, followed by a decrease of 0.058% in the next month. Over a three-month range, the variation in cases is no longer significant for funding growth, which may indicate a higher case growth only immediately after confirmed cases escalate.

Despite the lack of evidence for an increase in long-term financing variation, the effects of the pandemic in Brazil still extended into 2022. That is, there was no evidence to confirm that the COVID-19 pandemic, specifically assessed on the number of disease cases, generated a change in growth of the financing structure of SUS, via the FNS, on the long term. This study concludes that permanent changes are still necessary to improve SUS’ response capacity to shocks. A possible intervention would be the creation of a permanent fund in the FNS to cover catastrophic health expenses, not only for the pandemic response but also to cope with any economic or environmental unpredicted shocks.

The paper reveals original evidence on the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on health financing using a time series methodology. However, some limitations must be addressed. First, ARIMA models consider observed changes in the dependent variable to estimate the next observations. These models do not include other variables that may be endogenous to the system, such as changes in government policies or the system’s capacity. In addition, the model does not consider regional disparities, which are important to fully comprehend Brazilian’s health financing system. The methodology also implies linear association between variables, and the usage of nonlinear models should be considered to compare results. Thus, further research should consider the usage of different methodologies, including nonlinear models, to accommodate different context variables, consider the possibility of endogeneity and evaluate regional disparities.
