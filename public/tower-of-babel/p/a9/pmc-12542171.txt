Strain-level characterization of bacterial pathogens using metagenomic sequencing for patients with pneumonia
Authors: Hua Zhou, Xi Li, Ying Mao, Qiuli Chen, Dan Zhu, Chao Liu, Yake Yao, Yinan Yao, Yunsong Yu, Ye Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has emerged as an indispensable diagnostic tool for infectious diseases. The disparity in virulence and antimicrobial resistance among strains of the same species requires mNGS to reach strain-level resolution.Methods To compare the subtyping performance between mNGS and culture, thirty bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) or blood specimens positive by culture were sequenced using the Illumina NextSeq platform, while whole genome sequencing (WGS) was conducted on the corresponding cultured colonies. Additionally, thirty BALF specimens underwent both mNGS and Oxford nanopore technology (ONT)-based metagenomic third-generation sequencing (mTGS) to compare the subtyping efficacy of the two platforms. To characterize the strain-level composition of pneumonia pathogen Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae, 185 BALF specimens from three hospitals were analyzed by mNGS.Results WGS of the cultured colonies yielded identical subtyping results to mNGS at the level of clonal complex (CC). Although mNGS and mTGS predicted largely consistent primary CCs, mTGS demonstrated less accuracy and precision in CC identification. Co-infections at the CC level were detected in 5.40% of A. baumannii-positive and 19.55% of K. pneumoniae-positive BALF specimens. CC composition differed markedly according to bacterial load and between primary and secondary CCs in co-infection specimens. Antimicrobial resistance profiles remained constant for patients with single-infection but varied for those with co-infection. Spatial and temporal consistency of CC composition was observed within individual patients.Conclusions The heterogeneity in virulence and antimicrobial resistance among CCs, together with the prevalence of strain-level co-infections, highlights the need to extend pathogen identification to the strain level. Under current technical conditions, mNGS is a more suitable subtyping tool compared to culture and mTGS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07134-6.Keywords: Co-infection, Culture, Clonal complex, mNGS, mTGS, Pneumonia, Strain typing

Introduction

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has revolutionized clinical microbiology through its culture-independent and unbiased approach, enabling simultaneous identification of all potential pathogens without a priori assumptions [1, 2]. While mNGS currently achieves species-level identification, it is widely acknowledged that bacterial strains exhibit substantial variations in virulence and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) due to significant intra-species sequence polymorphisms and gene content diversity. A notable example is Escherichia coli: although most strains are commensal, certain strains can be highly pathogenic, such as the O104:H4 strain responsible for the 2011 outbreak in Germany [3]. Similarly, the international clones I and II of Acinetobacter baumannii have evolved pan-drug resistance and become a leading cause of hospital-acquired pulmonary infections, whereas other clones generally remain susceptible to most antimicrobials [4]. The critical importance of strain awareness underscores the urgent need to extend pathogen identification to the strain level.

A range of bioinformatics tools have been developed for metagenotyping on both short-read platforms, such as Illumina, and long-read platforms, such as oxford nanopore technology (ONT). The Illumina-based tools perform subtyping using single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), k-mer frequencies, or the phylogeny of metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) [5–9].These methods typically require high sequencing coverages; for instance, SNP calling requires > 5 × coverage, whereas MAG assembly requires > 20 × coverage. In theory, ONT generates reads long enough to span strain-specific genomic regions [10], enabling direct strain assignment through BLAST searches. Nevertheless, similar to Illumina-based tools, current ONT-based metagenotyping tools still rely on SNP, repeat allele, or k-mer strategies [11–13].

The aforementioned tools have demonstrated robust subtyping performance when applied to gut microbiome or contaminated food specimens, which typically exhibit high bacterial loads [10, 14–17]. In contrast, many clinical specimens, such as blood, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), often contain only a minute fraction (typically 0.001%–1%) of pathogen-derived DNA, resulting in insufficient bacterial reads for reliable subtyping with existing tools [1]. Most studies on strain-level typing using clinical mNGS have predominantly focused on specimens with high pathogen loads [18–25], which do not accurately reflect real-world conditions. We recently introduced metagenomic intra-species typing (MIST), a software that simultaneously exploits strain-specific SNPs and gene content information [26]. By integrating these signals, MIST reduces the sequencing depth required for accurate strain delineation. In silico analysis using synthetic datasets demonstrated that MIST can resolve co-occurring strains at an average nucleotide identity (ANI) resolution of 99.9% with coverage as low as 0.001 × per strain. Yet the clinical value for strain-level subtyping in mNGS remains unclear.

This study aims to pioneer systematic strain-level characterization in real-world clinical specimens. Considering the clinical importance of pulmonary infection [27], we chose bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) specimens from pneumonia patients as a model. We concentrated on subtyping A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae, two major causative agents of pneumonia with well-defined clonal population structures [28, 29]. Comparison of subtyping performance between mNGS, culture and metagenomic third-generation sequencing (mTGS) identified mNGS as the currently most suitable subtyping tool for clinical practice. We then applied mNGS to subtype 185 BALF specimens, revealing strain-level co-infections and marked differences in virulence and AMR profiles among strains. Comparative subtyping analysis of sequential BALF specimens and paired BALF and blood specimens further traced the spatial and temporal variation at the strain level within individual patients. These findings underscore the clinical value of integrating strain-level subtyping into routine mNGS diagnostics.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study retrospectively analyzed the mNGS data of BALF specimens collected from the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University, Zhejiang Provincial People’s hospital, and Jinhua municipal central hospital during September 2020 to April 2023. All corresponding patients were diagnosed as pneumonia [30] and underwent bronchoalveolar lavage based on the infection lesions indicated by lung CT or chest radiography. Each BALF specimen was submitted for both culture and mNGS testing. When peripheral blood specimens from the same patients were also analyzed by mNGS, the data were also analyzed.

Sample processing and sequencing

For Illumina sequencing, total DNA was extracted from 1 mL of BALF or from 1 mL of the supernatant of peripheral blood after centrifugation at 3000 g for 10 min. Host DNA was depleted using saponin-based differential lysis as previously reported [31]. A synthetic spike-in control (0.02 ng/μL) was added to each sample prior to nucleic acid extraction to quantify bacterial load. Shotgun sequencing was performed on the NextSeq 550 platform (Illumina, CA, US), generating an average of 18 million 50-bp single-end reads per sample.

For ONT sequencing, host DNA was depleted using the GensKey Host DNA Depletion kit (GensKey, Beijing, China). Total microbial DNA was then extracted using the Tiangen NG550 kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). Purified DNA was used for library preparation with the ONT Rapid PCR Barcoding Kit (ONT, Oxford, UK). Sequencing was performed on the GridION X5 platform with R9 flow cells (ONT, Oxford, UK) under standardized conditions, yielding an average of 1 million reads per sample. Adaptive sampling was not employed.

Microbial identification at species and subspecies level

For Illumina sequencing, we firstly performed pathogen identification at the species level. The sequences of human origin were filtered by mapping the reads against the human reference genome (GRCh38.p13) using Bowtie v2.4.1 [32]. The remaining reads were aligned to a microbial reference database integrating NCBI nt and RefSeq database, FDA-ARGOS, and FDA Reference Viral Database. The uniquely aligned reads were used to identify the microbial species following the method from the literature [31]. The mNGS reads encoding the AMR genes were detected using the SRST2 software v0.2.0 plus the CARD v3.0.8 database [33].

Subsequent subtyping analysis was conducted using the MIST software [26]. Briefly, the complete genomes of A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae were downloaded from NCBI GenBank database. The module ‘Cluster’ in the MIST package assigned the reference genomes into clusters at the ANI thresholds of 99.5%. The module ‘Strain’ processed the mNGS reads to infer the strain composition. The module ‘Bootstrap’ conducted 200 bootstrapping replicates to calculate the mean (± SE) abundance of clonal complexes (CCs). A CC was considered reliably present if the lower bound of its 95% confidence interval exceeded 1% abundance. Co-infection was defined when the top two CCs were detected with an abundance ratio < 10:1. Specimens containing three or more CCs were deemed medically unreliable, as real infections typically involve no more than two strains.

For ONT sequencing, Guppy v5.0.12 was used for base calling (https://nanoporetech.com/document/Guppy-protocol). Error correction was not applied. The reads were firstly preprocessed with Fastplong v0.2.2 [34]. Species-level pathogen identification followed the same protocol as that for mNGS, except that Minimap2 v2.28-r1209 instead of Bowtie was used for alignment [35]. For subtyping analysis, reads from A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae were mapped against from their respective reference genomes with Minimap2. The mapping scores were extracted from the field “ms:i:” in the resulting SAM file. For each read, the highest mapping score against the reference genomes from the same CC represented the CC score. Reads with identical scores across multiple CCs were deemed non-specific; otherwise, each read was assigned to the CC with the highest score. Strain-level criteria were adapted from mNGS pathogen identification standards [36, 37]: each CC required ≥ 3 CC-specific reads and an abundance > 10% of the primary CC; otherwise, the CC was considered absent.

Bacterial culture and antimicrobial susceptibility test (AST)

The BALF and blood specimens were cultured according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guideline. Species identification of the cultured isolates were conducted using the VITEK 2 system (Sysmex-bioM´erieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France). AMR was determined by broth microdilution method, and the minimal inhibitory concentration was interpreted according to the CLSI M100 33th Edition [38].

WGS and subtyping of the cultured isolates

One or ten colonies were picked from the culture plate. Genomic DNA of the colonies was sequenced by the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA), generating approximately 15 million 150-bp paired-end reads per isolate. Reads were de novo assembled into contigs using Unicycler v0.4.8 [39]. Seven-gene multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) analysis was performed using the BacWGSTdb online service [40], following the MLST schemes described by Bartual et al. for A. baumannii [41] and by Diancourt et al. for K. pneumoniae [42]. The SNP-based whole genome phylogeny was also constructed using BacWGSTdb.

Results

Consistent subtyping results between mNGS and culture

Previously, we validated the accuracy of mNGS-based subtyping using the MIST software [26]. Here, we assessed whether conventional culture could achieve comparable subtyping performance. Subtyping was conducted at the clonal complex (CC) level, defined as groups of isolates differing by 0–2 alleles across the seven MLST loci [43]. To establish a link between the MLST scheme and whole-genome-based subtyping, we clustered the reference genomes of A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae based on ANI thresholds of 99%, 99.5%, 99.9% and 99.99%, respectively, among which the 99.5% clustering results most closely approximated the partitioning of reference genomes by CC (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Fifteen specimens positive for A. baumannii and fifteen specimens positive for K. pneumoniae were selected for mNGS and WGS confirmation (Fig. 1A). Of them, fifteen A. baumannii-positive specimens and ten K. pneumoniae-positive specimens were predicted by mNGS to involve a single CC (hereinafter referred to as single-infection). These predictions were perfectly consistent with the MLST results of the cultured colonies by WGS. In particular, for five of these specimens, ten colonies per specimen were sequenced, all belonging to the same sequence type (ST) (Fig. 1B). Whole genome-based pairwise SNP comparisons revealed minimal variation between these colonies (Supplementary Fig. 2), which further validated the predicted single-infections at the clone level.

The remaining three K. pneumoniae-positive specimens were predicted by mNGS to involve co-infections (i.e. containing two CCs in the same specimen). The WGS-based results classified one specimen as single-infection, while the other two specimens encompassed mixed colonies that matched the mNGS-based subtyping results, albeit with different compositional percentages (Fig. 1B). Based on the K. pneumoniae data (since no co-infections were detected for A. baumannii), the culture approach achieved a sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and precision of 1.00, 0.92, 0.93, and 1.00, respectively, for identification of co-infection.

Comparison of subtyping between mNGS and mTGS

Another set of specimens positive for A. baumannii (n = 15) or K. pneumoniae (n = 15) by mNGS were analyzed using mTGS (Supplementary Table 1). Following standard protocol, the total reads for mNGS was 35 times more than that for mTGS (Supplementary Fig. 3A), but the proportion of non-human reads for mNGS was 1/20 of that for mTGS in average (Supplementary Fig. 3B). The number of reads derived from A. baumannii or K. pneumoniae were strongly correlated between the two technologies (Pearson r = 0.76; Fig. 2A).

By mapping the mTGS reads against the reference genomes of different CCs, we defined the reads with equal score to multiple CCs as non-specific reads and the others as specific reads. Over half of the mTGS reads were non-specific and therefore contributed little to CC identification (Fig. 2B). The median length of mTGS reads was 507 bp, with the non-specific reads being significantly shorter than the specific reads (Mann–Whitney test, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2C).

Unlike mNGS in which CC composition was inferred via bootstrap resampling and maximum likelihood estimation, mTGS measured CC composition by directly counting CC-specific reads. Six specimens could not be subtyped because no CC had ≥ 3 supporting reads, while another three specimens exhibited ≥ 3 CCs, a clinically implausible scenario that may result from mis-assignment of reads due to sequencing errors (Fig. 2D). Nevertheless, the primary CCs predicted by mNGS and mTGS were largely concordant. Using mNGS as the benchmark, mTGS achieved a sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and precision of 0.33, 0.76, 0.63, and 0.33, respectively, for identification of co-infections.

CC-level composition in BALF specimens

We retrospectively analyzed mNGS data of 185 BALF specimens collected from three tertiary hospitals in China during September 2020 to April 2023, including 127 A. baumannii-positive specimens and 101 K. pneumoniae-positive specimens. These specimens were from 179 pneumonia patients, with their demographic characteristics and clinical symptoms shown in Supplementary Table 2. Elevated procalcitonin or C-reactive protein levels were observed in 87.15% of patients.

A. baumannii or K. pneumoniae were the most abundant species in 121 (67.60%) specimens. Genome sequencing coverage was < 1 × in 80% of the A. baumannii-positive or K. pneumoniae-positive specimens and < 0.1 × in 60% (Supplementary Fig. 4A,B). Meanwhile, 48.82% of the A. baumannii-positive specimens and 54.46% of the K. pneumoniae-positive specimens were culture-positive for the respective organism. Sequencing coverage in culture-negative specimens, over half of which was lower than 0.01 × , was significantly lower than that in culture-positive specimens (Mann–Whitney test, p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 4C). Similar trends were observed using spike-relative abundance (Supplementary Fig. 4D).

A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae exhibited distinct CC-level compositional patterns (Supplementary Table 3). 118/125 (94.40%) of A. baumannii-positive specimens and 70/87 (80.46%) of K. pneumoniae-positive specimens were dominated by a single CC, whereas the others involved co-infections. Co-infection frequencies did not differ significantly across the three hospitals (Chi square test, p = 0.247; Supplementary Fig. 5) and was independent of culture positivity or pathogen abundance (Supplementary Fig. 6).

The dominant CC for A. baumannii was CC208 (94.92%), while K. pneumoniae exhibited much greater diversity: CC11 (24.29%), CC29 (18.57%), CC15 (14.29%), CC25 (12.86%) and CC23 (8.57%) (Fig. 3A,B). CC-level distribution varied between the three hospitals but did not reach statistical significance (Chi square test, p = 0.089; Supplementary Fig. 5B). Notably, for A. baumannii, non-CC208 was more frequently observed as the secondary CC than as the primary CC (Chi square test, p < 0.0001), while K. pneumoniae showed no such pattern (Fig. 3C). Meanwhile, CC composition differed significantly between specimens with different bacterial load. The specimens containing non-CC208 A. baumannii or CC29 K. pneumoniae were more likely to have their bacterial sequencing coverage < 1 × (Chi square test, p < 0.0001), whereas specimens with CC25 K. pneumoniae tended to show > 1 × coverage (Chi square test, p = 0.060; Fig. 3C,D).

Association between CC composition and antimicrobial resistance

All patients in this study were hospitalized and might have multiple AST records during their stay (Fig. 4A). Patients with predicted single-infection exclusively exhibited consistent AST results. In contrast, patients with co-infection displayed significantly more heterogeneous AST results (Chi square test, p < 0.0001), which reflected different AMR profiles of the co-infecting strains (Fig. 4B). Exceptions included one A. baumannii-positive patient and two K. pneumoniae-positive patients, with predicted co-infections but consistent AST records. This is likely due to repeated sampling of the same strain or coincidentally identical AMR profile between the co-infecting strains.

For the single-infection specimens, 34/35 of CC208 A. baumannii and 2/2 CC11 K. pneumoniae were resistant to ceftazidime, piperacillin/tazobactam, imipenem, meropenem and levofloxacin (Fig. 4B). Other CCs were more susceptible. For instance, imipenem resistance was observed in 1/5 of CC23 and 1/5 of CC25 K. pneumoniae (Fig. 4B). Among the 34 A. baumannii-positive specimens and six K. pneumoniae-positive specimens with imipenem resistance, reads of the blaOXA gene were only detected in eight A. baumannii-positive specimens; and reads of the blaKPC gene were only detected in two K. pneumoniae-positive specimens. All of the ten specimens had sequencing coverage > 1 × for the respective organism.

Consistent CC composition within the same patients

Twelve patients had simultaneous BALF and peripheral blood specimens analyzed by mNGS. Bacterial abundance in blood was an order of magnitude lower than in BALF. Subtyping results were generally consistent between the paired BALF and blood specimens (Fig. 5A). The mNGS prediction of three K. pneumoniae-positive blood specimens (from Patient 8, 10, 12) showed limited reliability, but one of the CC components predicted from blood matched that from the paired BALF.

Six patients had BALF specimens tested twice within a one-month interval. All showed increased bacterial abundance in the later specimen compared to the earlier specimen, indicating unresolved infections (Fig. 5B). Subtyping results were consistent across time points for all patients except Patient 8, in whom the earlier specimen was dominated by CC37 whereas the later specimen showed co-infection with CC37 and CC11.

Discussion

This study represents the first strain-level characterization of pulmonary pathogens using mNGS. While demonstrating the superiority of mNGS over culture and mTGS in subtyping, we identified dominant CCs responsible for pneumonia and predicted CC-level co-infection in 5.40% of A. baumannii-positive specimens and 19.55% of K. pneumoniae-positive specimens. Epidemiological and AMR differences were observed among different CCs and between single- and co-infections. Moreover, we discovered the spatial and temporal consistency of CC composition within the investigated individual patients through sequential BALF sampling and paired BALF-blood analyses.

Culture remains the established standard for species-level pathogen identification. However, for strain-level diagnostics, mNGS offers distinct advantages over culture, including the ability to subtype culture-negative specimens and reduced labor and cost requirements. When specimens were culture-positive and reported as single infections by mNGS, both methods yielded identical subtyping results. Regarding co-infection identification, while the CCs detected by mNGS and culture demonstrated general concordance, quantitative discrepancies in CC composition were observed. Potential explanations for these discrepancies include limited colony sampling, mNGS prediction uncertainties, and differential growth characteristics in vivo versus in vitro (e.g., non-viable bacteria).

This study presents the first comparative analysis of subtyping performance between mNGS and mTGS. Bacterial strains within the same species share highly similar genomes, with variations primarily consisting of strain-specific SNPs and genomic segments. Common error-correction strategies include self-correction, which relies on redundant overlapping regions in ONT reads and typically requires > 30 × sequencing depth [44], and hybrid correction, which leverages the high accuracy of Illumina short reads to rectify ONT errors [45]. However, mTGS data often suffer from low pathogen coverage; and performing both mNGS and mTGS on a single specimen is usually impractical due to cost constraints. Consequently, neither correction strategy is applicable, precluding SNP-based subtyping via mTGS. To enable accurate read assignment to individual strains, ONT must therefore leverage its long-read advantage to maximize coverage of strain-specific segments. Nevertheless, suboptimal DNA extraction protocols in current mTGS workflows often yield reads shorter than anticipated. In our recent work [46], we demonstrated that replacing the terminal repair enzyme with one possessing both "terminal repair" and "incision repair" activities, alongside increasing the volume of magnetic beads, extended read lengths from 200–300 bp to 1000 bp. Algorithmic optimization presents another avenue to enhance mTGS performance. The MIST software addresses Illumina’s short-read limitation by integrating statistical frameworks: maximum-likelihood estimation for CC inference and bootstrap resampling for confidence assessment. While these frameworks hold significant potential to improve mTGS subtyping accuracy, the higher cost of mTGS remains a barrier to its adoption as a routine strain-level subtyping tool.

Our retrospective analysis of mNGS data from pneumonia patients confirmed the predominance of CC208 A. baumannii and CC11, CC15, CC23, CC25, and CC29 K. pneumoniae in pneumoniae patients, which aligned with the previous isolate-based epidemiological studies in China [47, 48]. The observed biased distribution of CC may reflect different virulence potential between CCs. For instance, CC29 K. pneumoniae appeared more frequently as the secondary CC in co-infection specimens or in specimens where K. pneumoniae exhibited a low sequencing coverage; whereas CC25 and C11 K. pneumoniae showed the opposite trend. Assuming that increased virulence correlates with enhanced in vivo replication rates, these findings suggest that CC29 may be less virulent than CC25 and CC11, which was supported by previous literatures [49, 50].

In addition to virulence assessment, subtyping may also facilitate AMR predictions. Notably, direct detection of AMR genes from mNGS reads remains challenging due to insufficient pathogen coverage. Despite host DNA depletion, nearly 60% of specimens in this study had pathogen coverage below 0.1 × , whereas detection of AMR gene requires a minimum coverage of 1 × . Previous epidemiological studies have shown that AMR profiles are often CC-specific. For example, most of CC208 A. baumannii and CC11 K. pneumoniae isolates in China are carbapenem resistant [47, 48]. Consequently, when the subtyping information is available, predicting the likelihood of resistance to specific antimicrobials becomes feasible. Given the spatial and temporal variability in epidemiological and AMR characteristics, we recommend that large medical centers routinely collect clinical bacterial isolates, conduct subtyping, and assess virulence and AMR profiles for each strain. An isolate-derived knowledge database would enable clinicians to leverage mNGS-based subtyping results for personalized treatment strategies, such as selecting targeted antimicrobial therapies.

The most remarkable finding of this study was the high incidence (19.55%) of co-infections involving multiple K. pneumoniae CCs in BALF specimens. Strain-level co-infections have been documented across diverse pathogens and specimen types using culture-based methods, with an average detection rate of 21.7% [51]. It is important to note that subtyping in this study was conducted at the CC level. Consequently, the co-infection rate at finer genetic resolutions (e.g., ST- or clone-level) might be higher. However, such closely related strains are more likely to show similar clinical manifestations (virulence/AMR profiles), rendering their differentiation less clinically relevant. Moreover, distinguishing these similar strains would necessitate higher sequencing coverage, which would render more specimens untypable due to insufficient pathogen-derived reads. Different CCs may exhibit varying propensities for involvement in co-infection. Two hypotheses may explain this phenomenon: (1) biological synergy between strains that potentially enhances infection establishment or progression, or (2) coincidental coexistence where one strain might represent colonization or contamination (typically characterized by low-abundance). The abundances of non-CC208 A. baumannii and CC29 K. pneumoniae are indeed lower than that of other CCs. While this observation supports the second hypothesis, the first hypothesis cannot be definitively ruled out.

The clinical significance of co-infection is underscored by the pronounced heterogeneity in AMR profiles. Antimicrobial-resistant strains are more likely to involve co-infection due to their increased difficulty in eradication in vivo. Their prolonged persistence (as either active infection or colonization) therefore elevates the probability of co-infection with other strains. In cases where co-infection comprises both antimicrobial-susceptible and resistant strains, treatment targeting solely the susceptible strain would otherwise fail to address the resistant strain, leading to therapeutic failure. Therefore, once co-infections are identified, antimicrobials should be prescribed to ensure effective treatment.

Strain-level pathogen characterization further enhances the assessment of infectious disease progression. In the present study, sequential BALF specimens from patients consistently exhibited nearly identical CC compositions, indicating persistent infections caused by the same CC rather than reinfection with distinct CCs. For clinical implication, this finding indicates that, if antimicrobial therapy was administered, it may have been ineffective and thus requires optimization, such as switching to alternative antimicrobials, increasing dosages, or implementing adjunctive systemic antibiotic therapy. Additionally, comparison of CC compositions between paired BALF and blood specimens facilitates determination of whether lung infection is the source of bacteremia. Previous studies have attempted to establish translocation links between bacterial isolates from different body sites, but they relied on culture-positive specimens or those with extremely high pathogen loads [52–54]. However, many mNGS specimens are culture-negative or contain insufficient pathogen DNA. In this context, our approach provides a novel framework for identifying the primary infection site and tracing bacterial migration in these challenging scenarios.

As a proof-of-concept study, this work demonstrates the distinct clinical value of strain-level subtyping. However, several challenges must be addressed before its clinical implementation. First, the necessity of host DNA depletion during library preparation requires evaluation. Conventional depletion strategies (e.g., saponin-based differential lysis) represents a double-edged sword: while potentially amplifying microbial signals to benefit subtyping, they may further deplete limited pathogenic DNA. Recent methodological advances offer promising solutions to this dilemma. For instance, mechanical lysis of human cells with zirconium-silicate microspheres selectively enriches non-human DNA with markedly improved sensitivity and specificity [55]. Second, establishing appropriate sequencing coverage thresholds for mNGS-based subtyping is critical. Analysis of simulated datasets indicates that a minimum coverage of 0.001 × coverage is required for strain detection. However, bacterial pathogen coverage does not directly correlate with total sequencing throughput. Occasionally, specimens sequenced at high throughput (e.g., > 100 million reads) may still yield low signals of pathogens due to inherent biological or clinical specimen properties. Hence, while greater sequencing depth is theoretically advantageous, an empirically derived depth that accommodates most samples often represents a more cost-effective alternative. Read length should also warrants consideration: while 50-bp reads suffice for species-level identification in most mNGS workflows, longer reads enhance subtyping precision at the cost of increased run time and sequencing expenses. Collectively, substantial optimization of mNGS subtyping protocols is required before strain-level typing can achieve routine clinical application.

Conclusions

While mNGS has significantly advanced clinical diagnosis, the species-level pathogen characterization is yet to fully manifest its value due to the great waste of its single-base resolution. This study, for the first time, compares the subtyping performance between different technologies and profiles the strain-level composition of A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae in BALF specimens, revealing a high prevalence of strain-level co-infection for K. pneumoniae. Given that strain-level compositional characteristics may vary between pathogens, diseases, and specimen types, this work lays groundwork for future mNGS subtyping studies targeting other bacterial pathogens, diseases and specimens. By leveraging a local database that accumulate strain-specific virulence and AMR information, subtyping can guide precise medicine instead of empirical treatment, particularly for culture-negative specimens or those involving strain-level co-infections. Collectively, we advocate for the prompt integration of mNGS subtyping into routine clinical practice to enhance diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic precision.
