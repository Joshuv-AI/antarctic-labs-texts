An evidence review and gap analysis for obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Michael Butzner, Elise Aronitz, Heather Cameron, Krista Tantakoun, Sanatan Shreay, Christopher Drudge
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM) have a substantial humanistic, clinical, and economic burden due to the array of symptoms and complications associated with the disease. The objective of this review was to identify key evidence gaps related to oHCM, specifically in Europe, North America, and Japan.MethodsA targeted literature review was conducted using PubMed to identify English-language studies published between 2012 and 2022 assessing patients with HCM/oHCM in France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the United Kingdom (UK), the United States (US), Canada, and Japan. Outcomes of interest were epidemiology, natural history, pathophysiology, management, and clinical, economic, and humanistic burden. Identified studies were assessed qualitatively to characterize evidence gaps.ResultsAmong 2,262 abstracts and 531 full-text articles screened, 178 articles were included from PubMed searches. An additional 16 unique studies were identified via a supplemental Google Scholar search initially conducted in January 2023 and updated in July 2024. Disease natural history, pathophysiology, and management were well documented globally. Significant evidence gaps were noted for the epidemiology, treatment, and burden of oHCM. Although multiple US studies were identified on the clinical, economic, and humanistic burden of oHCM, and one clinical burden study was found for Japan, there was a lack of evidence for France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the UK, and Canada.ConclusionsMajor evidentiary gaps exist for the epidemiology, treatment, and burden of oHCM. Future research should address these gaps, with a specific focus on generating real-world evidence for Canada and European countries that will support the evaluation of emerging therapies in these regions.Keywords: Evidence gap, Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Obstructive, Literature review

Background

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a chronic and progressive myocardial disorder defined by left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy that is characterized by the presence of increased LV wall thickness that is not solely explained by abnormal loading conditions [1]. HCM has obstructive (oHCM) and nonobstructive (nHCM) subtypes, with oHCM being characterized by dynamic LV outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction (LVOTO). This results from apposition of anterior leaflet of the mitral valve to the hypertrophied and hyperdynamic left ventricular septum during systole [2]. In oHCM, obstruction is indicated by the presence of a peak LVOT gradient of ≥ 30 mm Hg at rest or after provocation such as Valsalva maneuver, standing test, or postexercise [2]. LVOTO is thought to be the primary driver for symptoms associated with oHCM, which include shortness of breath, chest pain, light-headedness, and syncope [2, 3].

Due to the array of symptoms and complications associated with oHCM, the disease has a substantial humanistic, clinical, and economic burden. Compared to patients with nHCM, patients with oHCM tend to have increased healthcare resource use and experience a greater number of symptoms, which tend to be of higher severity [3–7]. However, there are limited reviews summarizing the broad clinical and economic burden of HCM globally. Given the substantial burden associated with oHCM, there is a need to identify available evidence and understand key gaps related to HCM to inform future research. The objective of the current review is to identify and describe key evidence gaps related to the epidemiology, treatment, and burden of HCM/oHCM in Europe, North America, and Japan, as well as globally.

Methods

A targeted literature review (TLR) was conducted to identify published evidence related to oHCM and HCM more broadly. Six searches targeting topics of interest on HCM were conducted in PubMed (Table 1). Search strategies included a broad disease-specific term (HCM) intended to capture both HCM studies in general and those focused on subtypes such as oHCM as well as terms related to epidemiology, natural history, pathophysiology, management, and clinical, economic, and humanistic burden. No limits were placed on study design, geography, or language at the search stage. Details including the specific dates for each search are provided in Table 1.

Records were screened according to pre-specified eligibility criteria (Table 2). These criteria were agreed upon by all authors prior to conducting the TLR. Studies of interest were original research articles, review articles, and congress abstracts on adult patients with HCM. Any studies focused on oHCM were noted during screening. Studies were included if they had a global scope or specifically covered one or more countries of interest (i.e., France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the United Kingdom [UK], the United States [US], Canada, and Japan). Countries of interest were selected for alignment with the major HCM guidelines [1–3, 8] and to enable the assessment of evidence for different healthcare settings and payer types. Included studies reported outcomes related to efficacy, safety, and/or quality of life, and treatments including cardiac myosin inhibitors (CMIs), beta-blockers (BBs), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), disopyramide, and/or other interventions such as septal reduction therapy (SRT) as interventions/comparators. Titles, abstracts, and full texts of the potentially relevant articles of records identified from PubMed searches were screened by a single researcher (EA or KT) to identify those that met the eligibility criteria. Included studies were assessed qualitatively to identify and characterize evidence gaps.

Results

PubMed searches identified 2,262 unique records with 1,731 records excluded after title and abstract screening. The remaining 531 records underwent full text review, of which 353 were excluded. An additional 16 unique studies were identified via a supplemental Google Scholar search initially conducted in January 2023 and updated in July 2024. Altogether, 195 articles were included in the TLR and considered for this review. Details of the TLR search are summarized in Fig. 1. Significant evidentiary gaps related to disease epidemiology, treatment, and burden were identified (Fig. 2).

Disease epidemiology

Limited information on the epidemiology of HCM/oHCM

The global prevalence of HCM was well documented in clinical reviews and guidelines [2, 3, 9–11]. Global HCM prevalence estimates were consistently reported to be 1:200 to 1:500 [2, 3, 9–11]. Prevalence estimates for HCM were identified for the US, Germany, and the UK; however, no estimates were found for France, Italy, Spain, Canada, and Japan. Prevalence estimates for HCM/oHCM from identified articles are summarized in Table 3. Prevalence estimates for HCM ranged from 3.1 to approximately 10 per 10,000 [12–17]. Additionally, HCM prevalence was found to be increasing over time [13, 15, 16]. Two identified studies reported incidence of HCM in the US. The first study estimated incidence across the US to be 3.0 in 10,000 in 2013 and 3.8 in 10,000 in 2019 [15]. The second study estimated the age- and sex-adjusted HCM incidence rate in a county in Minnesota to be 6.6 per 100,000 person-years in 2016 [14].

In contrast to HCM, studies reporting global epidemiological data for oHCM were not identified, with limited data found for specific countries of interest. Single studies reporting oHCM prevalence were identified for the US [15], Germany/UK [16], and Japan [4] (Table 3). One identified study reported oHCM incidence in the US, which was estimated to be 2.1 in 10,000 in 2013 and 1.5 in 10,000 in 2019 [15]. No studies reporting oHCM prevalence or incidence data were identified for France, Italy, Spain, or Canada.

Estimates of HCM/oHCM prevalence and incidence may be inaccurate

Reported HCM/oHCM prevalence and incidence estimates may not accurately represent the true prevalence as the disease is frequently misdiagnosed or underrecognized in clinical practice [2, 9, 18]. It is estimated that only 10% of HCM cases are clinically identified, with only 6% of identified cases being symptomatic [2, 18]. Inadequate disease awareness and common misconceptions (e.g., HCM is a disease of the cardiac muscle only) may limit the ability to accurately diagnose HCM/oHCM [19]. These challenges may ultimately lead to less accurate epidemiological (and burden) estimates. In general, the obstructive aspect of oHCM is defined similarly across guidelines (i.e., having a peak LVOT gradient ≥ 30 mm Hg). However, inconsistencies in the broader definition of HCM were identified with respect to the disorders that HCM may encompass [20]. The American guidelines define HCM as LV hypertrophy in the absence of another cardiac, systemic, or metabolic disease [2]. The European guidelines define HCM as the presence of increased LV wall thickness or mass that is not solely explained by abnormal loading conditions [1]. The Japanese guidelines note that HCM can be diagnosed after secondary cardiomyopathies are excluded by considering family history and genetic mutations [3]. These inconsistencies may result in differences in the patient populations who are diagnosed with HCM across different regions and therefore impact epidemiology (and burden) estimates.

Finally, studies that report prevalence estimates for HCM and oHCM have limitations, such as small sample size or potentially including misdiagnosed patients, that may impact the accuracy of the estimates. Well-known prevalence estimates of 1:200 and 1:500 are outdated and may lack generalizability as these estimates are from studies published in the 1990s with small sample sizes [21, 22]. Whereas newer studies leveraged medical codes for diagnosing HCM/oHCM, a reliance on medical diagnosis codes can impact the overall accuracy of estimates as cardiac hypertrophy caused by another disorder can be misdiagnosed as HCM/oHCM [4, 12, 15, 16]. A Swedish cohort study found that approximately one third of patients diagnosed with HCM were misclassified (mainly hypertension and aortic stenosis), demonstrating that prevalence/incidence estimates from claims databases and registries using medical diagnosis codes should be interpreted with caution as false classification is not accounted for [23].

Disease Treatment

Limited economic evidence for CMIs for the treatment of oHCM

Recommendations from European, American, and Japanese guidelines for pharmacological therapy of symptomatic oHCM include pharmacotherapies such as BBs, CCBs, and disopyramide [1–3]. These drugs typically do not offer complete symptom relief and there is little to no evidence they prevent disease progression or alter the natural course of the disease [11, 24–26]. The development of disease-modifying therapies for HCM and oHCM has been impeded by the limited understanding of the etiology and pathophysiology of these diseases [2]. Several novel disease-modifying therapies are being evaluated, including the CMIs mavacamten and aficamten. Identified clinical trials of CMIs are summarized in Table 4. The clinical development program for mavacamten includes the PIONEER-HCM, EXPLORER-HCM, and VALOR-HCM trials, all of which were conducted in symptomatic patients with oHCM. The Phase II PIONEER-HCM trial demonstrated that mavacamten reduced postexercise LVOT gradient (primary endpoint; from 103 mm Hg at baseline to 19 mm Hg in Cohort A [10–20 mg/d mavacamten without background medications] and from 86 mm Hg at baseline to 64 mm Hg in Cohort B [2–5 mg/d mavacamten with BB allowed]) and also improved exercise capacity and symptoms [27]. The placebo-controlled Phase III EXPLORER-HCM trial demonstrated that mavacamten had a better clinical response rate (primary endpoint; 37% of patients on mavacamten versus 17% on placebo, p = 0·0005) and specifically improved exercise capacity, LVOT obstruction, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, and health status [28]. Finally, in the placebo-controlled Phase III VALOR-HCM trial, mavacamten reduced the need for SRT (8.9% of patients in the original mavacamten group versus 19.2% of patients in the placebo crossover group underwent SRT, were SRT eligible, or were not SRT evaluable) with sustained improvements in LVOT gradients and symptoms [29].

The clinical development program for aficamten includes the REDWOOD-HCM, FOREST-HCM, SEQUOIA-HCM, and MAPLE-HCM trials. The placebo-controlled Phase II REDWOOD-HCM trial demonstrated that aficamten was well tolerated (75% of patients on aficamten versus 88% on placebo experienced ≥ 1 treatment-emergent adverse event [AE]), reduced LVOT gradient, and improved symptoms in patients with oHCM [30]. The placebo-controlled Phase III SEQUOIA-HCM trial demonstrated that aficamten improved peak oxygen uptake at 24 weeks (primary endpoint; difference: 1.7 milliliters per kilogram per minute, p < 0.001) and also improved patient-reported quality of life, NYHA functional class, and LVOT obstruction in symptomatic oHCM patients [31]. An ongoing open label extension study (FOREST-HCM) aims to assess the long-term safety and tolerability of aficamten, with interim results having demonstrated that aficamten was well tolerated and associated with sustained benefits [32]. Finally, MAPLE-HCM is an ongoing Phase III trial assessing aficamten as a monotherapy compared to metoprolol for patients with oHCM [33].

A single economic evaluation of mavacamten was identified. This study, by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review, estimated that while mavacamten led to more quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) compared to BBs and CBBs, the incremental cost per QALY was significantly higher than the incremental cost of BBs and CBBs [34]. When compared to septal myectomy (SM) and alcohol septal ablation (ASA), mavacamten produced fewer QALYs and was more costly [34]. No economic evaluations were identified for aficamten at the time of review.

The 2023 European guidelines recommend CMIs (mavacamten) as a second-line therapy when BBs, CCBs, and/or disopyramide are poorly tolerated or ineffective [1]. Notably, the MAPLE-HCM trial is an ongoing trial assessing aficamten compared to metoprolol as first-line therapy for patients with oHCM.

Limited evidence directly comparing invasive surgical oHCM therapies

Only a minority of patients with oHCM experience complete relief from obstruction on pharmacotherapies such as BBs and CCBs, with the majority of treated patients continuing to experience persistent LVOT gradients and symptoms on exertion [26]. For patients that continue to experience symptoms despite attempts at pharmaceutical management with non-specific treatments, have a gradient ≥ 50 mm Hg, and/or have moderate-to-severe symptoms, invasive SRT with either surgical SM or catheter-based ASA is recommended [1, 2]. Both SM and ASA are effective at relieving symptoms/obstruction in most cases however 5-10% of patients continue to experience persistent functional limitations and symptoms following the intervention [35, 36], and approximately one quarter of patients with oHCM treated with SRT require reintervention [5]. Retrospective comparisons have shown similar safety and efficacy between SM and ASA, but ASA was associated with lower hospitalization costs and shorter length of stay compared to SM [35, 37]. ASA is generally preferred when anatomy is favorable or for patients with high surgical risk. Although the reduction in the LVOT gradient is similar between SM and ASA, ASA is associated with higher likelihood of heart block [35].

Notably, this TLR did not identify any head-to-head randomized controlled trials or prospective observational studies comparing ASA and SM. As retrospective comparisons can be limited by factors including study design, data availability, and uncontrolled confounding variables, head-to-head clinical trials and prospective observational studies evaluating the comparative effectiveness of SM and ASA for oHCM are needed [35, 38].

Unclear impact of current interventions on quality of life

Pharmacotherapies such as BBs and CCBs are commonly used for early treatment and relief of LVOTO in patients with oHCM [1, 2, 26], but have important side effects (e.g., fatigue, dizziness, and sexual dysfunction) that can impact quality of life [34]. Although the side effects associated with these therapies are well documented, the TLR did not identify information on patient reported quality of life measures (e.g., EuroQoL 5-dimension 5-level index questionnaire [EQ-5D-5 L], Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire - Clinical Summary Score [KCCQ-CSS]). Limited information was found on how mavacamten and aficamten impact quality of life. Identified mavacamten studies reported AEs associated with this therapy to include stress cardiomyopathy and atrial fibrillation, with one study suggesting that mavacamten improves health-related quality of life in patients with oHCM as assessed by the EQ-5D-5 L, EuroQoL visual analog scale, and KCCQ-CSS [28, 39, 40]. The SEQUOIA-HCM trial demonstrated that aficamten improved quality of life as assessed by the KCCQ-CSS [31]. Information was also limited regarding the impact of SRT on patient quality of life, however the high need for reintervention [5], and risk of conduction block requiring a permanent pacemaker [35, 41], are likely to have an impact.

Disease burden

Limited information on the burden of HCM and oHCM

The TLR identified several studies describing the clinical burden of HCM from a global perspective, and a single study for Italy [42], however, there was a lack of data specific to the other countries of interest. As there is no cure for HCM, treatment is focused on reducing mortality and managing symptoms. While current treatment strategies are effective in a large proportion of patients, allowing them to have an average life expectancy with mitigating debilitating symptoms or need for medical devices or invasive surgery, 30–40% of HCM patients experience a more severe disease course, with patients experiencing AEs including symptoms due to LVOTO or diastolic dysfunction, heart failure symptoms, atrial fibrillation with increased risk of thromboembolic stroke, or sudden cardiac death [2, 43]. Nevertheless, with current treatment strategies, HCM-related mortality has been reported to be around 0.5–1.5% per year [20]. While mortality is low, hospitalizations are common in patients with HCM for cardiovascular-related reasons. An Italian single-center retrospective cohort study of 253 patients with HCM found that between 2010 and 2015 there were 187 hospitalizations in 92 patients (36%, rate of 5.7% per year). Most hospitalizations were cardiovascular-related (84.5%), and were often unplanned and due to acute disease-related complications [42]. Limited information was identified on the economic and humanistic burden of HCM. A web-based survey that sought to gain a better understanding of patient experience for patients with HCM in the US found that symptoms of tiredness, shortness of breath, and dizziness were commonly reported. The most frequently cited impacts of symptoms on patients’ lives included limitations related to physical activities, emotional impacts, and impacts on work, suggesting that HCM is associated with a decrease in psychological well-being and health-related quality of life [6].

A number of studies were identified covering the clinical, economic, and humanistic burden of oHCM in the US, as well as one clinical burden study for Japan [4]. However, there was a lack of oHCM burden studies for France, Germany, Italy, Spain, UK, and Canada. Based on the identified data for the US and Japan, oHCM is associated with a substantial clinical and humanistic burden due to an array of symptoms and complications, such as atrial fibrillation, ventricular or supraventricular arrhythmias, sudden cardiac arrest, stroke, and heart failure, and compared to patients with nHCM, patients with oHCM tend to experience a higher number and more severe symptoms [3–6]. Additionally, oHCM is associated with a substantial economic burden, with pharmacy utilization and physician visits increasing after an oHCM diagnosis [44]. The economic burden increases further among patients whose symptoms can not be effectively managed by typical interventions such as BBs and CCBs and require further intervention, with healthcare costs increasing further for oHCM patients undergoing SM or ASA [45, 46]. According to a 2009–2019 US medical claims analysis, compared to matched controls, oHCM was associated with substantial increases in all-cause hospitalizations, hospital days, outpatient visits, and total healthcare costs leading to an incremental cost of $20,000/year. When accounting for symptomatic oHCM patients, the healthcare resource utilization and costs were substantially higher compared to the matched controls, and the incremental cost increased to $35,000/year [7].

Discussion

Leveraging a large array of published literature globally, our review reports on several key findings in HCM. The global prevalence of HCM was well documented in clinical reviews and guidelines [2, 3, 9–11], and prevalence estimates for HCM were identified for the US [15], Germany [16], and the UK [16]. However, no estimates were found for France, Italy, Spain, Canada, and Japan. No studies reporting global epidemiological data for oHCM were identified, and limited data were found for specific countries of interest. Single studies reporting oHCM prevalence were identified for the US [12, 15], Germany/UK [16], and Japan [4] but no studies reporting oHCM prevalence or incidence data were identified for France, Italy, Spain, or Canada. Studies reporting prevalence estimates for HCM and oHCM have limitations that may impact their accuracy. Understanding the country-specific epidemiology of HCM/oHCM can be valuable to healthcare decision makers, as it can guide the appropriate allocation of healthcare resources and inform the potential economic impact of managing the condition.

Clinical evidence was found for the CMIs mavacamten and aficamten, reflecting the extensive clinical development programs for these therapies. However, limited economic evidence was found for CMIs and no clinical evidence was identified that directly compared invasive surgical oHCM therapies. The TLR identified limited information on how mavacamten and aficamten impact quality of life as measured through patient reported outcomes. However, equivalent patient reported quality of life evidence was not identified for other therapies. Given that BBs, CCBs, and SRT are the most common interventions for oHCM, more information is needed about associated AEs and how these interventions impact patient quality of life.

Although the TLR identified several studies describing the clinical burden of HCM from a global perspective, and a single study for Italy [42], no evidence specific to the other countries of interest was found. Several studies were identified describing the clinical, economic, and humanistic burden of oHCM in the US, as well as one clinical burden study for Japan, but there was a lack of oHCM burden studies for France, Germany, Italy, Spain, UK, and Canada.

Overall, significant gaps in prevalence and incidence data for HCM and oHCM were identified, with no data reported for most of the countries of interest. Evidence gaps related to accuracy of prevalence and incidence estimates were also identified. Future research should focus on assessing HCM and oHCM prevalence and incidence in countries in which no data or only limited data were available. More broadly, estimates should be assessed in larger patient populations in which HCM/oHCM diagnosis is anatomically confirmed. Furthermore, future real-world evidence generation is needed to understand the clinical and economic burden of oHCM in Europe and Canada.

Limitations

This review is subject to several limitations that are typical of literature reviews. To allow for an in-depth review of the disease, this TLR was designed to specifically focus on oHCM where possible. The search strategy was not vetted with external experts. However, to ensure a thorough review of the disease area, we identified and evaluated multiple study types and data sources using a comprehensive search strategy that encompassed key disease topics. Although the review was carefully designed and conducted, it was not systematic and was restricted to publications since 2012. Whereas this approach may have resulted in an incomplete capture of the topics of interest, it prioritized newer publications to ensure the most up-to-date material was screened and current evidence gaps were identified.

Conclusions

Major evidence gaps exist for data on the effectiveness of key oHCM treatments and country-level oHCM epidemiology and burden data. To address these gaps, future evidence generation should focus on gaining a better understanding of country-specific oHCM epidemiology and burden, as well as the economic impact CMIs will have on the disease space. Specifically, real-world evidence is needed for Canada and European countries to quantify the burden and unmet need and support the evaluation of emerging evidence in these regions.
