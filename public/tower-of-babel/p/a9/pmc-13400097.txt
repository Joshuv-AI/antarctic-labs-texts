Radial Access Strategies: Focus on Coronary Function Testing for Angina With Nonobstructive Coronary Arteries
Authors: Nathaniel R. Smilowitz, Samit M. Shah, Douglas E. Drachman, Coen Boerhout, Tim P. van de Hoef, Javier Escaned, Colin Berry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Invasive coronary function testing (CFT) allows comprehensive assessment of the mechanisms of angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries, which can include coronary microvascular dysfunction, coronary vasospasm, endothelial dysfunction, or myocardial bridging. There is significant heterogeneity in approaches to CFT, including in the selection of arterial access site for the procedure. Transradial arterial access is associated with less bleeding and fewer vascular complications, but can be challenging in patients with chest pain and nonobstructive coronary arteries, as they are disproportionately of female sex, with higher risks of radial spasm and crossover. Radial vasodilators for radial spasm prophylaxis may lower the diagnostic yield of CFT. In this review, we highlight strategies to ensure diagnostic fidelity while enabling radial-first CFT, including conscious sedation, left radial arterial access, atraumatic wire advancement, pressure-mediated vasodilation using a saline “mocktail,” selective use of short-acting arterial vasodilators when needed, mother-and-daughter catheter advancement, balloon-assisted tracking techniques, and the use of long hydrophilic sheaths. A pragmatic approach to radial vasodilators is reasonable when CFT is performed ad hoc, whereas withholding prophylactic radial vasodilators is preferred in patients electively referred for definitive CFT. Although the access site should be selected based on anatomic considerations and patient preference, a cocktail-free radial-first strategy is feasible for most patients undergoing CFT.

Background

Angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA), also known as ischemia with nonobstructive coronary arteries (INOCA), is a common syndrome that affects up to 4 million patients in the United States.1 Among patients referred for invasive coronary angiography for suspected ischemic heart disease, nearly 50% do not have obstructive epicardial disease.2 Invasive coronary function testing (CFT) allows comprehensive assessment of the pathophysiological mechanisms of ANOCA, which can include coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD), epicardial or microvascular coronary vasospasm, endothelial dysfunction, or myocardial bridging; each of these ANOCA “endotypes” has important implications for treatment.3,4 Two randomized trials demonstrated that invasive angiography with CFT to guide medical therapy for ANOCA was superior to usual care to improve quality of life in patients with ANOCA.5,6 Based on these data, societal guidelines recommend invasive CFT to improve symptoms in patients with ANOCA. The 2021 American Heart Association (AHA)/American College of Cardiology (ACC) Guideline for the Evaluation and Diagnosis of Chest Pain provides a class IIa recommendation for CFT in the setting of chest pain and suspected ANOCA,7 and the 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guideline for the management of chronic coronary syndromes provides a class IB recommendation for CFT in patients with ANOCA, persistent symptoms despite medical treatment, and a poor quality of life.8 Acetylcholine coronary reactivity testing is a critical component of invasive CFT, with epicardial coronary spasm diagnosed in 37%-51% of patients and microvascular spasm reported in 22% to 26% of patients undergoing CFT in large series.6,9, 10, 11 The prevalence of endothelial dysfunction in response to acetylcholine has been reported as high as 78%.12 Multiple white papers provide guidance on technical approaches to perform invasive CFT.3,4,13 Significant heterogeneity remains in the approach to CFT, including in the selection of the arterial access site for the catheterization procedure.

Radial access considerations for CFT

Transradial arterial access is the preferred approach for coronary angiography at many centers to mitigate bleeding and vascular complications, facilitate rapid mobilization after cardiac catheterization, and enhance patient satisfaction. In a meta-analysis of 31 trials including 30,096 patients, radial access was associated with lower risks of mortality, bleeding, and vascular complications.14 Accordingly, ESC guidelines recommend radial artery access as the preferred approach to coronary angiography in patients with suspected chronic coronary syndrome (class IA recommendation) referred for invasive management.8 In contrast, the 2021 AHA/ACC guidelines for the Evaluation and Diagnosis of Chest Pain do not provide any guidance or recommendation on arterial access.7 Both ESC and AHA/ACC guidelines are concordant in providing a class IA recommendation for the preferred use of radial artery access in patients undergoing urgent or emergency invasive management for acute coronary syndromes to reduce bleeding, vascular complications, and death.15,16

A common strategy to facilitate painless and successful execution of radial access-based catheterization involves the administration of arterial vasodilators intended for radial artery spasm prophylaxis, such as a “cocktail” of verapamil and nitroglycerin. For patients undergoing CFT with acetylcholine coronary spasm testing, arterial vasodilators may blunt the vasomotor response to acetylcholine provocation and may theoretically lead to falsely normal testing.

In the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute-sponsored Women’s Ischemia Syndrome Evaluation study, patients were instructed to hold oral vasoactive medications for a minimum of 24 to 48 hours prior to CFT, with a particular focus on withholding calcium channel blockers (CCBs) and long-acting nitrates based on their potential impact on reducing the diagnostic yield of intracoronary acetylcholine testing.3,17 Similar recommendations have been espoused in other expert consensus statements.3,13 Femoral access obviates the need for arterial vasodilators and is the traditional access site preferred by many cardiologists for cardiac catheterization with invasive CFT. As many catheterization laboratories have moved to workflows that predominantly use radial access with arterial vasodilators, femoral approaches to CFT may decrease operator and staff enthusiasm and may pose a barrier to perform this diagnostic procedure.

Radial spasm is a significant concern in patients undergoing CFT. Coronary function testing and coronary physiology are best validated using 6 French (Fr) guiding catheters, but ∼80% of patients with ANOCA undergoing CFT are female, and female patients tend to have smaller body habitus and smaller radial artery diameters than male patients. Radial artery spasm, which may lead to conversion from radial to femoral access, is also reported to be more common in female patients.18 Consequently, female sex is an established predictor of radial to femoral crossover.19,20 In a large CFT registry from the Netherlands, 71% of patients underwent transradial CFT, with a 17% crossover to femoral access.10 In an interim analysis of the prospective DISCOVER INOCA registry, 52% underwent radial access, with 7% crossover to femoral access, which is consistent with the rate of crossover in non-INOCA patients.21,22

Recommendations to withhold vasoactive medications such as intraarterial CCBs may impact decisions regarding the selection of a radial vs femoral access site. However, the differential effects of various vasodilators have not been fully established, and the impact of these agents on the diagnostic yield requires further study. Nitroglycerin (NTG) has a short half-life of ∼3 minutes and may be administered after radial sheath placement to mitigate radial spasm; intraarterial NTG does not reduce the risk of radial artery occlusion.23,24 In contrast, verapamil has a half-life of approximately 2 to 5 hours after a single dose.25 Based on the theoretical effects of vasodilators used for radial artery spasm prophylaxis on intracoronary acetylcholine, decisions regarding access site are typically left to operator preference.3 If radial access is used, a whitepaper published by the Microvascular Network recommends avoiding the use of a CCB, which could mask spasm with acetylcholine administration.3 Therefore, strategies to mitigate radial spasm without affecting the fidelity of CFT are required. Sedation

Conscious sedation with anesthesia and analgesia is an effective means to ensure patient comfort and mitigate or reverse radial artery spasm. In a large, randomized trial of 2013 patients undergoing transradial PCI, the administration of intravenous fentanyl and midazolam was associated with a lower risk of radial artery spasm vs placebo (2.6% vs 8.3%, P < .001) and a 34% lower risk of access site crossover.26 However, although sedation reduces radial spasm, it may also blunt symptoms of ischemia and impair a patient’s ability to communicate during CFT. Based on current best practices, the clinical, symptomatic response to acetylcholine provocation testing is an important component of the diagnostic assessment, and chest pain is required for a diagnosis of epicardial and microvascular spasm endotypes according to Coronary Vasomotor Disorders International Study Group (COVADIS) criteria.27 More potent anesthesia and analgesia administration may therefore reduce radial artery spasm and facilitate transradial invasive CFT, but may also reduce the diagnostic sensitivity of the testing and thereby reduce the measured prevalence of coronary spasm.27 Therefore, the level of sedation should be titrated to patient comfort without oversedating patients to the extent that it affects the diagnostic yield of testing.

Radial access techniques and initial detection of radial artery spasm

Introduction of a vascular access sheath into the radial artery may provoke spasm. Ultrasound-guided transradial access permits precise needle positioning and minimizes the number of attempts needed to cannulate the vessel;28 this may reduce the risk of radial spasm. When radial artery spasm occurs, it can be detected by measurement of the arterial blood pressure via the side-arm of the sheath. Arterial waveforms will appear “damped” in cases when radial spasm has occurred. Alternatively, radial spasm may also be first appreciated when resistance is encountered upon advancement of the diagnostic catheter beyond the radial sheath. Confirmation of radial spasm can be established with angiography of the radial artery with the manual injection of 5 to 10 mL of contrast media during fluoroscopy or cine imaging. Contrast angiography at this point has several benefits. First, contrast angiography provides a roadmap of radial artery vascular anatomy, including the bifurcation of the brachial artery, which can facilitate guidewire tracking. Second, contrast media also have local vasodilator properties, and injection of contrast media may facilitate relief of vasospasm. Third, radial artery spasm may spontaneously resolve with time. Simply waiting and observing the patient for a few minutes after a diagnosis of radial artery spasm before proceeding with catheter advancement can also be an effective treatment strategy.

Use of the standard radial cocktail

Despite theoretical concerns about the impact of arterial vasodilators on the results of acetylcholine spasm testing, some of the landmark studies reporting clinical benefits of CFT permitted standard radial artery spasm prophylaxis at the time of testing. In the CorMicA and ILIAS-ANOCA randomized trials, radial access was strongly encouraged and radial cocktails were administered at the discretion of the operators.5,6 The AID-ANGIO study also adopted a pragmatic approach, integrating CFT into the standard catheterization laboratory workflow, with liberal use of medications for radial artery spasm prophylaxis.11 Despite this use of vasoactive radial “cocktails,” the incidence of epicardial coronary spasm diagnosed across these studies was 33% to 51%, roughly in line with other CFT protocols that mandated withholding vasoactive medications and radial artery spasm prophylaxis prior to acetylcholine provocation testing (Table 1).5,6,10,11,21,29,30 In comparison, in a retrospective study of 110 patients undergoing CFT without radial spasm prophylaxis or intracoronary nitrates before acetylcholine infusion, 79% had endothelial dysfunction defined as a <50% increase in coronary blood flow by intracoronary Doppler and 62% had coronary spasm, including 36% with epicardial spasm and 26% with microvascular spasm.29Table 1Landmark trials of invasive CFT for INOCA, arterial access site, use of radial artery spasm prophylaxis, and the results of acetylcholine testing for epicardial spasmStudyYear publishedINOCA tested, nStudy designFemale sexAccess siteRadial accessRadial-femoral crossoverRadial cocktail usedEpicardial spasm prevalenceCorMicA52018151Prospective74%Radial preferredNot reported (likely >90% radiala)Not reportedNot reported37.1% overallILIAS-ANOCA62025153Prospective55%Radial preferred95%0Pragmatic (liberal) use51% overallAID-Angio112025187Prospective44%Radial preferred97%1%Pragmatic (liberal) use33.2% overallDISCOVER INOCA212025166Prospective80%Operator preference52%7%12.7%49% with radial access vs 69% femoral (P = .014)NL-CFT Registry1020251207Prospective81%Operator preference71%17%Not reported42% overallCFT, coronary function testing; Other evidence may support the concept that vasodilator use has less of an impact on CFT results than was historically believed. In a retrospective analysis of 372 consecutive patients with ANOCA undergoing CFT via radial access, radial artery spasm prophylaxis (ie, nitroglycerin and verapamil) was administered in 156 (42%), whereas no spasm prophylaxis was administered in 216 (58%).30 No difference in the prevalence of a pathological test result was observed among patients with and without radial artery spasm prophylaxis (46% with vs 48% without, P = .75). Among those with an abnormal response to acetylcholine provocation, there was a nonsignificant trend toward less epicardial spasm (28% with vs 41% without, P = .12), and a trend toward more microvascular spasm (72% with and 59% without, P = .36) that did not reach statistical significance. Overall, the authors concluded that radial artery spasm prophylaxis did not impact the frequency or type of coronary spasm identified in this study.30

In contrast, other registries report conflicting findings, and there is a growing body of literature that suggests that nitroglycerin may significantly impact the coronary response to acetylcholine. In a study of 95 patients with coronary spasm, of whom 55 had microvascular spasm and 40 had epicardial spasm, intracoronary administration of nitroglycerin (200 μg) followed by acetylcholine rechallenge at 3 minutes mitigated epicardial spasm in all patients with focal spasm and 80% of those with diffuse spasm.31 However, in the patients with epicardial spasm prior to nitroglycerin, acetylcholine rechallenge 3 minutes after nitroglycerin revealed residual microvascular spasm in a large proportion of patients.31 In a separate study of 40 patients with epicardial spasm in response to acetylcholine followed by radial intraarterial nitroglycerin (200 μg) administration and acetylcholine rechallenge, epicardial spasm could be induced in only 12/40 and 10/28 patients at 5 and 10 minutes after intraradial NTG, respectively.32 Overall, by 10 minutes after intraarterial NTG, epicardial coronary spasm was inducible in only 55% of these individuals. Thus, nitroglycerin substantially reduced the diagnostic yield of acetylcholine provocation testing for spasm in patients with ANOCA.32 These results, however, conflict with the clinical trial data from ILIAS-ANOCA and the AID-ANGIO study, which studied patients with acetylcholine after intracoronary nitrate administration and reported coronary spasm prevalence comparable with studies prohibiting upfront administration of nitrates. The impact of intraarterial CCBs on the diagnostic yield of coronary reactivity testing for spasm during CFT has not been formally evaluated in randomized trials, and their effects relative to nitroglycerin remain unknown. In an observational analysis of 1885 patients undergoing CFT in a large multicenter registry from the Netherlands, 76.2% of CFTs were performed via a primary transradial approach with routine administration of intraarterial verapamil at most centers, and 20.2% were performed via primary femoral arterial access.33 Patients undergoing radial CFT were numerically more likely to have a diagnosis of CMD than those undergoing femoral CFT (40.1% vs 34.5%, P = .06), with no difference in overall spasm prevalence between the groups (68.5% vs 64.2%; P = .1).33 After adjustment for baseline clinical covariates, no significant difference in diagnostic yield was reported by primary access site (P = .2). In an exploratory analysis comparing the diagnostic yield of CFT from hospitals that routinely administered intraarterial verapamil monotherapy vs a single hospital that administered a combination of intraarterial nitroglycerin and verapamil, a greater prevalence of vasospasm was observed in the verapamil-only centers (70.7% vs 50.7%; P < .001). Unfortunately, radial vasodilators were not reported on a per-patient level in this analysis.33 Randomized trials are needed to understand the impact of specific agents on the yield of coronary reactivity testing and measured coronary flow reserve (CFR) and microvascular resistance. Preliminary data suggest that nitrates may significantly affect baseline coronary flows and therefore may impact measured CFR.34

In summary, these data support a more pragmatic approach to CFT in routine clinical practice, although the context of testing may determine the approach and when necessary. In patients in whom CFT is not preplanned, existing data support the use of a verapamil-only and nitroglycerin-only radial cocktails when needed, particularly if it facilitates ad hoc testing in patients with unknown coronary anatomy and suspected obstructive coronary artery disease. In contrast, the potential risks of misdiagnosis of epicardial spasm diminish the enthusiasm for the use of combinations of intraarterial CCBs and nitrates as radial artery vasodilators when patients are electively referred for definitive CFT. In the absence of more definitive data from randomized controlled trials, it is prudent to avoid intraarterial administration of CCB and nitroglycerin when CFT is preplanned.

Atraumatic wire advancement through the radial artery

Atraumatic approaches to wire placement in the radial and brachial arteries can avoid iatrogenic radial spasm and facilitate catheter advancement. A simple, effective approach utilizes a 0.035-inch hydrophilic coated wire with a 1.5-mm J-tip (GlideWire Baby-J, Terumo) or a steerable 0.035-inch hydrophilic coated wire with an angled tip (Silverway Wire, Asahi) advanced under fluoroscopic guidance. Once the wire is positioned in the ascending aorta, catheters can be advanced through the radial artery. Alternatively, a standard 0.014-inch coronary guidewire can be loaded through a diagnostic catheter inserted into the radial sheath and then manually torqued and advanced from the radial to the brachial arteries, guided according to a radial artery roadmap. This approach minimizes the risk of vascular injury from blind manipulation of the guidewire, which may provoke iatrogenic vasospasm. Once the coronary guidewire is positioned in the subclavian artery, it should provide adequate support to permit catheter advancement guided by fluoroscopy. At any point, a 0.035-inch wire can replace the 0.014-inch wire to provide additional support.

Saline-based radial “mocktail”

Healthy vascular endothelium releases endogenous nitric oxide (NO) in response to increased shear stress from high blood flow. This, in turn, leads to smooth muscle relaxation and arterial vasodilation. This physiologic principle can be exploited to enable a vasodilator “cocktail”-free approach to radial access and radial spasm prophylaxis. Repeated administration of forceful injection of 10 cc of saline into the radial artery through a short sheath can lead to local NO release, which in turn induces flow-mediated vasodilation in the radial artery (Supplemental Video 1). Although the saline administration may initially induce mild discomfort for the patient, serial injections lead to improved radial patency, enhanced flow, and diminished discomfort. A small, randomized trial enrolling 20 patients with radial artery spasm during cardiac catheterization (mean age 61 years, 53% women) tested pressure-mediated vasodilation through the radial sheath (automated pump injection of 10 mL of saline injected at a rate of 10 mL per second at a pressure of 400 psi) vs nitroglycerin and verapamil as interventions to relieve radial artery spasm. The primary study end point was radial artery acute luminal diameter gain by quantitative radial angiography. High-pressure saline injections increased radial artery diameters assessed by quantitative radial angiography to a greater extent than pharmacotherapy.35 Moreover, unlike the pharmacologic vasodilators, pressure-mediated vasodilation with a saline-based radial “mocktail” caused no decrease in systemic blood pressure and no lasting systemic vasodilator effects, a consideration that may be particularly helpful in patients with borderline or low blood pressures who are referred for invasive CFT. Pressure-mediated vasodilation did cause more arm pain at the time of administration than pharmacologic vasodilators, and a single case of radial artery occlusion was diagnosed at 90-day postprocedure follow-up in a participant assigned to pressure-mediated vasodilation. Although larger studies are needed to confirm the safety of this approach, a saline-based “mocktail” is a promising alternative to pharmacology to relieve radial artery spasm in patients undergoing CFT.

Brachial artery occlusion flow-mediated dilation

Temporary occlusion of the brachial artery with a sphygmomanometer cuff is another approach to provoke endogenous NO release, facilitate radial artery access, and reduce radial spasm. In this technique, a cuff is placed on the upper arm over the brachial artery and inflated above systolic blood pressure for 5 to 10 minutes. Upon deflation, the resulting reactive hyperemia promotes transient NO release and flow-mediated dilation of the radial artery, which may help relieve radial and brachial artery spasm.36 When applied before the procedure, this maneuver can improve the likelihood of successful radial access. In a randomized trial of 1156 patients undergoing coronary angiography, prolonged occlusion flow-mediated dilation improved radial access cannulation success.37 However, the impact on catheter-induced radial spasm was not reported. Importantly, the systemic effects of temporary brachial artery occlusion on coronary vasomotor function, and any impacts on the results of CFT, are unknown and warrant further investigation.

Mother-and-daughter catheter advancement and balloon-assisted tracking techniques

Advancing a 6F guide catheter over a long 0.035-inch wire in the setting of radial spasm can be uncomfortable for the patient and may lead to radial or brachial artery dissection or perforation due to trauma to the vessel (razor effect). Instead, catheter-assisted tracking of the guide catheter can facilitate guide catheter delivery, particularly in patients with radial spasm.38,39 This simple technique involves placing a 110 or 125 cm 4F, 5F, or 6F diagnostic catheter (pigtail, multipurpose, etc) within the lumen of a 100-cm 6F guide catheter and then advancing the 2 catheters together over the 0.035-inch wire within the radial artery, with the longer diagnostic catheter protruding from the guide catheter (Figure 1). This technique provides a more tapered tip to the guide catheter and may facilitate delivery of the guide catheter in small-caliber or tortuous radial arteries. Balloon-assisted tracking, a complementary technique, involves a low-pressure inflation of a compliant coronary balloon (2.0 mm in diameter) protruding from the tip of the guide catheter to facilitate safe catheter tracking and advancement.40,41 Although effective, balloon-assisted tracking traditionally requires replacement of the 0.035-inch wire with a 0.014-inch coronary guide wire, adding cost and complexity to the procedure.

Long hydrophilic sheaths

Thin-walled, hydrophilic, 75- to 85-cm long sheaths can be helpful to facilitate guide catheter delivery with difficult radial or brachial artery anatomy and may mitigate arm pain in the setting of radial artery spasm when catheter movement is anticipated, reducing the need for radial to femoral conversion.42, 43, 44 The 75-cm 6F Destination Slender Introducer Sheath (Terumo) has been successfully used in this manner.42 Although the outer diameter is larger than a 6F guiding catheter alone, the hydrophilic coating and tapered sheath dilator facilitate both placement and removal at the end of the procedure. This approach mitigates, but does not entirely eliminate, the risk of radial sheath entrapment in the setting of radial spasm.45 Furthermore, long sheaths remain stationary throughout the procedure, permitting unimpeded guide catheter movement without pain, irritation, or trauma to the radial and brachial arterial walls. Similarly, sheathless guide catheters feature hydrophilic coatings that can be used to overcome radial artery tortuosity or spasm.

Smaller guide catheter diameters

Coronary function testing is traditionally performed using 6F guide catheters. By expert consensus, fractional flow reserve measurement may be performed through 5F or 6F guide catheters. The use of 5F guide catheters can facilitate catheter delivery and minimize the risks of radial to femoral crossover.46 Although acetylcholine coronary reactivity testing for coronary spasm can be safely and effectively performed through any caliber guide catheter, few studies have assessed the effects of internal catheter diameter on the validity of coronary physiology measurements for assessment of CMD. All the early in vivo validation studies of CFR and the index of microcirculatory resistance measured by bolus thermodilution were performed using 6F guide catheters.47 A recent in vitro study using a coronary flow simulator reported that bolus thermodilution measures of fractional flow reserve, CFR, and the index of microcirculatory resistance were generally comparable when using 5F vs 6F guide catheters, with a low rate of misclassification by catheter diameter.48 Unfortunately, in vivo data validating CFT measurements with 5F guide catheters are lacking. Consequently, the use of 6F catheters remains the preferred approach in CFT, when feasible.

Sheathless guide catheters (eg, SheathLess Eaucath Catheter, Asai Intecc Medical; RAILWAY System, Cordis) can be considered to reduce the outer catheter diameter at the radial access site while maintaining inner catheter dimensions validated for CFT. These hydrophilic guide catheters eliminate the need for a separate introducer sheath and may reduce the risks of radial artery trauma, spasm, and the need for crossover to femoral arterial access.

Left radial access

Left radial access permits access to the coronary arteries via a path that is similar to transfemoral cardiac catheterization. This may facilitate fewer catheter manipulations and more straightforward engagement of the coronary arteries, particularly in patients of short stature who may have right subclavian tortuosity and a short ascending aorta. Less catheter manipulation may translate into a lower incidence of radial spasm. Other advantages of the left radial approach include lower risks of stroke and preservation of the dominant hand in right-handed individuals.49 Left radial access is associated with comparable procedural success rates compared with right radial access and may less commonly require crossover to femoral access because of challenging vascular anatomy.49

Future developments

Further research should be considered to expand the evidence regarding the impact of various radial artery vasodilators on the results of CFT, the efficacy and safety of pressure-mediated vasodilation with a radial “mocktail,” and other anatomic approaches to radial access, such as the distal radial artery “snuffbox” access site. Less commonly used approaches to avoid radial spasm, such as subcutaneous administration of nitroglycerin, have not been studied in the context of CFT.

Conclusions

Invasive coronary function testing is increasingly recognized as a comprehensive diagnostic strategy for patients with ANOCA, and radial artery access continues to gain momentum as the preferred technique for cardiac catheterization among both providers and patients. The goal to avoid using a vasodilator “cocktail” to mitigate radial artery spasm prior to invasive CFT can pose significant challenges, but alternative strategies exist to reduce radial artery spasm and improve patient procedural comfort. The strategies described above may help facilitate safe, comfortable radial access while preserving the diagnostic fidelity of invasive coronary function testing with acetylcholine provocation testing. Even after administration of radial artery vasodilators, ad hoc CFT still provides meaningful clinical information that can improve angina and quality of life. Although the access site should be selected based on anatomic considerations and patient preference, a cocktail-free radial-first strategy is feasible for most patients undergoing CFT.
