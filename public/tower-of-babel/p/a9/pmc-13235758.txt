Inner Plexiform Layer Microstructure Is Altered in Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Maria Emfietzoglou, Victor S. M. C. Correa, Gustavo Sakuno, Solomon Frances, Maria Anna Bantounou, Konstantinos G. Baroutis, Dimitrios Papadopoulos, Alexander Charonis, Demetrios G. Vavvas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIn multiple sclerosis (MS), optical coherence tomography (OCT) is used to quantify retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and ganglion cell complex (GCC) thinning; however, thickness metrics may not capture microstructural alterations within the synapse-rich inner plexiform layer (IPL). The purpose of this study is to determine whether IPL microstructure differs in MS, whether abnormalities occur independently of optic neuritis and inner retinal thinning, and whether IPL metrics improve discrimination beyond thickness metrics.MethodsCross-sectional case-control study (49 MS [92 eyes], 59 controls [109 eyes]). IPL reflectivity was quantified from OCT. Mixed-effects models included RNFL/GCC adjustment; receiver operating characteristic curve assessed discrimination.ResultsThe percentage of IPL segments with an identifiable five-sublayer pattern was lower in MS (−20 percentage points; 95% confidence interval [CI], −25 to −13). MS eyes demonstrated higher outer hyporeflective sublayer intensity (0.15; 95% CI, 0.09 to 0.21) and lower middle hyperreflective–outer hyporeflective contrast (−0.13; 95% CI, −0.19 to −0.08). Findings persisted in non–optic neuritis eyes and after RNFL/GCC adjustment. The percentage of identifiable IPL segments discriminated MS from controls (area under the curve, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.73–0.90) exceeding RNFL/GCC.ConclusionsIPL microstructure was altered in MS independent of optic neuritis and inner retinal thinning. Quantitative assessment of IPL microstructure may provide complementary structural information in MS.

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic immune-mediated demyelinating disease of the central nervous system that affects approximately 2.8 million people worldwide1 and leads to cumulative neuroaxonal injury and disability. Although magnetic resonance imaging and clinical assessments remain central to diagnosis and monitoring, scalable noninvasive biomarkers that reflect neuroaxonal and synaptic integrity are needed to support diagnostic confidence and improve prognostic stratification.

The visual pathway is frequently involved in MS. Optic neuritis is the presenting manifestation in approximately one-fourth of patients and occurs in about half over the disease course.2 Reflecting this burden, the 2024 revisions of the McDonald criteria recognize the optic nerve as a fifth anatomic site for dissemination in space3 and endorse optical coherence tomography (OCT) as a supportive paraclinical tool.4 When alternative causes are excluded and image quality is adequate, intereye asymmetry in peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness (≥6 µm) or macular ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) thickness (≥4 µm) can provide supportive evidence of optic nerve involvement.4

Beyond detecting optic nerve injury, OCT offers rapid, reproducible, micrometer-resolution assessment of retinal neuronal structures and has been proposed as an in vivo window on central nervous system integrity. In MS, OCT biomarkers have largely focused on thickness measures, particularly RNFL and GCIPL or ganglion cell complex (GCC) thinning, which correlate with disability progression and global neurodegeneration, including in eyes without prior optic neuritis.5–8 Thickness, however, represents a relatively coarse structural metric and may not capture microstructural alterations within retinal synaptic circuitry.

The inner plexiform layer (IPL) contains synapses between bipolar, amacrine, and ganglion cells and is organized into five sublayers corresponding to ON and OFF pathways.9 High-resolution and adaptive-optics OCT have confirmed this laminar architecture in vivo as alternating hyper- and hyporeflective bands.10 Despite growing evidence that synaptic dysfunction is central to MS pathogenesis, including within the retinal IPL,7,8 IPL assessment in MS has typically been limited to composite thickness measurements.5,6

Whether the IPL laminar reflectivity pattern is altered in MS using widely available commercial OCT, and whether such alterations provide information beyond conventional thickness metrics, remains unknown. In this initial cross-sectional case–control study, we quantified IPL sublayer reflectivity using enhanced high-definition scans from commercial spectral-domain OCT and a validated semiautomated analysis pipeline.11 We evaluated whether IPL microstructural alterations occur independently of optic neuritis and inner retinal thinning and whether IPL metrics improve discrimination of MS from controls beyond RNFL and GCC thickness.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a cross-sectional case-control study at the Athens Vision Eye Institute from May to July 2025, enrolling adults with MS and age- and sex-matched healthy controls. The study was approved by the Athens Vision Eye Institute Institutional Review Board, Athens Vision Eye Institute, Athens, Greece (approval date: March 12, 2025). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants.

MS diagnosis was confirmed by an MS neurologist (D.P.) according to contemporaneous McDonald criteria. Neurologic characterization included disease duration (years since diagnosis), disease course/subtype, history of MS-associated optic neuritis, current disease-modifying therapy use, and standardized functional assessments when available.

Controls had no history of demyelinating disease and no known ophthalmic or systemic conditions expected to affect retinal structure, including glaucoma, retinal vascular disease, macular disease, prior retinal surgery or trauma, diabetes, or uncontrolled hypertension.

A priori, the study was designed to detect a moderate standardized difference in IPL microstructure metrics between groups (effect size ≈0.6) with 80% power at a 2-sided α of 0.05; the final sample supported prespecified MS–control comparisons (details in Supplementary Methods).

Ophthalmic Assessment and Eligibility

All participants underwent best-corrected visual acuity testing (Snellen; converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution for analysis), intraocular pressure measurement (Goldmann applanation), slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and dilated fundus examination. Exclusion criteria included ocular diseases that could affect OCT-derived inner retinal measures (e.g., glaucoma, retinal disease, prior retinal surgery, or ocular trauma) and systemic diseases with known retinal effects (e.g., diabetes). Additional exclusions were high astigmatism (≥3.0 diopters), intraocular pressure greater than 21 mm Hg, or clinically significant media opacity (nuclear sclerosis grade ≥2). Axial length was measured in a subset of eyes using optical biometry (IOLMaster 500; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany) and evaluated in sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Methods).

OCT Acquisition and Quality Control

Spectral-domain OCT imaging was performed on the same day as clinical assessment using the Avanti platform (Optovue, Fremont, CA, USA) by a single experienced operator (A.C.). The primary scan for IPL microstructure analysis was an enhanced high-definition horizontal line scan (12 mm) centered on the fovea and extending nasally through the papillomacular region. Conventional OCT measures (including peripapillary RNFL and GCC thickness) were obtained using the device's standard analytic outputs (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Scans were excluded before IPL analysis if image quality was inadequate (e.g., low signal strength, motion artifact, segmentation failure, or localized signal loss involving the region of interest). Examples of excluded scans are shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

Quantification of IPL Microstructure

IPL microstructure was quantified using a previously validated semiautomated pipeline applied to the enhanced high-definition line scans (Supplementary Methods; Fig. 1).11 In brief, scans were exported and processed using standardized preprocessing applied identically across groups. A prespecified region of interest (1.0–2.5 mm nasal to the foveal center) was selected because it consistently displays the five-sublayer IPL architecture on commercial OCT. A trained grader (M.E.) masked to the participant group performed region placement using a standardized protocol.

IPL microstructure analysis workflow. Representative workflow for IPL microstructure analysis from enhanced high-definition (HD) line OCT. Enhanced HD line scans were exported and preprocessed in ImageJ/FIJI with aspect-ratio correction, image resizing, local contrast enhancement, and contrast normalization. A standardized region of interest was manually selected from 1000 to 2500 µm nasal to the foveal center. Perpendicular intensity profiles were extracted across the IPL at 15-µm intervals and analyzed in Python in 300-µm segments to account for minor IPL undulations. Hyperreflective peaks and hyporeflective valleys were identified to derive sublayer intensity, adjacent-sublayer contrast, relative sublayer distance, percentage of segments with an identifiable five-sublayer structure, and mean squared error as a summary measure of laminar definition. CLAHE, contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization; MSE, mean squared error; ROI, region of interest.

Depth-resolved reflectivity profiles across the IPL were extracted and normalized to permit comparison across eyes. Quantitative features included sublayer mean intensities (hyperreflective and hyporeflective bands), contrast between adjacent sublayers, relative sublayer spacing on a normalized IPL depth scale, and summary measures of laminar definition. The prespecified primary IPL outcome was the percentage of IPL segments within the region of interest demonstrating an identifiable five-sublayer pattern. Additional feature definitions and algorithmic details are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1; R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are summarized as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) and categorical variables as number (percentage). Eye-level comparisons were performed using mixed-effects models with a participant-specific random intercept to account for correlation between eyes. Primary models adjusted for age, sex, spherical equivalent refraction, and OCT signal strength. Effect estimates are reported as adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

To address multiple comparisons, false discovery rate control (Benjamini–Hochberg) was applied within prespecified families of IPL microstructure metrics; conventional OCT measures were adjusted as a separate family. Prespecified sensitivity analyses evaluated (1) robustness to modeling the primary IPL outcome as a bounded count, (2) differences in eyes without prior optic neuritis, and (3) independence of IPL microstructure from inner retinal thinning by additionally adjusting models for RNFL and GCC thickness (Supplementary Methods). A further sensitivity analysis adjusted for axial length in the subset with available measurements.

For patient-level analyses, OCT and IPL metrics were averaged across eyes to generate a single value per participant. Discrimination of MS versus control status was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) estimates with 95% CIs (DeLong method). Prespecified comparisons assessed whether key IPL metrics improved discrimination beyond RNFL and GCC thickness (Supplementary Methods). For exploratory discrimination analyses, a composite IPL microstructure score was derived using principal component analysis (first principal component; details in Supplementary Methods). Associations with disease duration and functional measures were evaluated using adjusted linear regression models and were considered exploratory. All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05, and analyses used complete cases.

Results

Participants

Between May and July 2025, 54 participants with MS and 59 healthy controls were enrolled (Fig. 2). After exclusions for nystagmus and OCT quality control, the final analytic sample included 49 participants with MS (92 eyes) and 59 controls (109 eyes). All participants self-reported race as white. Median age was 47 years (IQR, 37–52) in controls and 45 years (IQR, 36–50) in MS; 39 of 59 controls (66%) and 34 of 49 participants with MS (69%) were female (Table 1). In the MS cohort, median disease duration was 9 years (IQR, 4.5–14.0), 14 of 49 participants (29%) had prior optic neuritis, and median expanded disability status scale (EDSS) score was 1.0 (IQR, 0–2.0) (Supplementary Table S1). Disease course data were available for 43 participants: 29 had relapsing disease (67%), and 14 had progressive disease (33%). MS subtype was available for 42 participants: 26 had relapsing-remitting MS (62%), 11 had secondary progressive MS (26%), and 5 had primary progressive MS (12%).

Conventional OCT Measures

Conventional OCT measures demonstrated inner retinal thinning in MS (Supplementary Table S2; unadjusted comparisons in Supplementary Table S3). In adjusted mixed-effects models, nasal parafoveal inner thickness was lower in MS (−6.11 µm; 95% CI, −9.90 to −2.31; q = 0.018), as were RNFL thickness (−5.08 µm; 95% CI, −8.43 to −1.73; q = 0.018) and GCC thickness (−4.28 µm; 95% CI, −7.02 to −1.54; q = 0.018). IPL thickness did not differ between groups (0.76 µm; 95% CI, −0.47 to 1.99; q = 0.46), and outer retinal measures were similar. Conventional OCT metrics stratified by optic neuritis history are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

IPL Microstructure Abnormalities in MS

IPL microstructure metrics differed between MS and controls (Table 2; Fig. 3; unadjusted comparisons in Supplementary Table S5). In adjusted models, MS eyes showed higher intensity in hyporeflective IPL sublayers (inner hyporeflective: 0.10; 95% CI, 0.03–0.17; q = 0.008; outer hyporeflective: 0.15; 95% CI, 0.09–0.21; q < 0.001). Measures reflecting laminar definition were also altered, with lower contrast between adjacent sublayers, most prominently at the middle hyperreflective–outer hyporeflective boundary (−0.13; 95% CI, −0.19 to −0.08; q < 0.001). The percentage of IPL segments with identifiable sublayers was reduced in MS (−20 percentage points; 95% CI, −25 to −13; q < 0.001), and reflectivity-fit mean squared error was lower (−0.01; 95% CI, −0.01 to −0.00; q = 0.003), consistent with reduced laminar definition.

Sensitivity Analyses

Findings were similar when analyses were restricted to MS eyes without optic neuritis (Supplementary Table S6). In models additionally adjusted for RNFL and GCC thickness, key IPL microstructure abnormalities remained (Supplementary Table S7), including higher outer hyporeflective intensity (0.12; 95% CI, 0.06 to 0.18; q < 0.001) and lower middle hyperreflective–outer hyporeflective contrast (−0.11; 95% CI, −0.16 to −0.06; q < 0.001). In additional sensitivity analyses adjusted for axial length (available for 105 eyes), results were consistent (Supplementary Table S8).

Discrimination of MS Versus Controls

Principal component loadings for the composite IPL score and correlations among IPL microstructure metrics are provided in Supplementary Tables S9 and S10. In patient-level ROC analyses (Fig. 4; full AUC estimates in Supplementary Table S11), IPL microstructure metrics showed higher discrimination between MS and controls than conventional thickness measures. The percentage of IPL segments with identifiable sublayers had the highest AUC, 0.81 (95% CI, 0.73–0.90). Outer hyporeflective intensity (AUC, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.70–0.87) and middle hyperreflective–outer hyporeflective contrast (AUC, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.69–0.87) also showed high discrimination. By comparison, RNFL thickness (AUC, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.57–0.78) and GCC thickness (AUC, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.55–0.76) showed weaker discrimination. In pairwise comparisons, the AUC for the percentage of identifiable IPL sublayers exceeded both RNFL (P = 0.026) and GCC (P = 0.006) (Supplementary Table S12). In incremental models, adding the percentage of identifiable IPL sublayers to RNFL and GCC increased AUC from 0.67 to 0.82 (P = 0.002) (Supplementary Table S13).

Disease Duration and Exploratory Functional Analyses

Longer MS disease duration was associated with higher outer hyporeflective intensity (β = 0.06; 95% CI, 0.01 to 0.10; q = 0.032) and thinner GCC (β = −3.04; 95% CI, −5.41 to −0.67; q = 0.032) (Supplementary Table S14). Exploratory comparisons between relapsing and progressive MS showed no statistically significant differences in IPL or OCT metrics after adjustment (Supplementary Table S15). Functional analyses were exploratory and limited by sample size and generally mild disability; although not statistically significant after multiple-comparison correction, point estimates for EDSS were directionally consistent with greater IPL abnormality (e.g., β = −0.52 for percentage of identifiable sublayers and β = 0.52 for outer hyporeflective intensity; Supplementary Table S16).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, quantitative analysis of the IPL laminar reflectivity pattern using commercial OCT revealed microstructural alterations in multiple sclerosis (MS). The two hyporeflective sublayers appeared brighter, contrast between adjacent sublayers was reduced, and the typical five-sublayer pattern was less consistently identifiable. These changes were present in eyes without a history of MS-related optic neuritis and remained significant after adjustment for RNFL and GCC thickness. Several IPL microstructure metrics also discriminated MS from healthy controls more strongly than conventional OCT thickness measures, and key metrics were associated with disease duration.

The IPL consists of the synapses between bipolar, ganglion, and amacrine cells. Its organization into five sublayers corresponds to distinct retinal circuits and has been validated by histology and high-resolution OCT. In our prior work in a normal aging cohort, the 5-sublayer reflectivity pattern was discernible on commercial OCT and became less distinct with age, consistent with reduced laminar definition and possible synaptic disorganization. The similar pattern observed here in MS, characterized by reduced laminar contrast and fewer segments with a clearly defined five-sublayer structure, suggests that MS may be associated with the disruption of IPL microarchitecture that is not captured by thickness alone. Synaptic dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a key mechanism in MS pathobiology7,8 but remains difficult to quantify in vivo using standard neuroimaging. Retinal OCT, by directly visualizing neuronal layers with micrometer-scale resolution, may provide a practical, noninvasive window into synapse-rich central nervous system tissue.

Microglial activation may contribute to the observed reflectivity changes. Microglia are present within the IPL, where they help maintain synaptic integrity, conduct immune surveillance, and clear cellular debris.12 In MS, microglia become chronically activated, releasing inflammatory mediators and complement proteins.13 In OCT imaging, microglia have been hypothesized to manifest as hyperreflective foci in retinal diseases, including diabetic retinopathy,14–16 and such foci have been described in the retina of MS patients.17,18 These foci remain difficult to interpret because they may represent microglia, lipid‐laden macrophages, small vessels, or imaging noise. Although our analysis did not focus on detecting or quantifying such foci, the increased brightness within the hyporeflective IPL sublayers observed in our MS cohort could be consistent with local microglial activation or accumulation. However, this interpretation remains highly speculative.

Because OCT reflectivity represents backscattered light rather than a direct measure of cellular composition, optical and analytic factors can influence intensity-based metrics. For example, thinning of overlying inner retinal layers could reduce attenuation and increase apparent IPL brightness. In our analyses, key IPL differences persisted after adjustment for RNFL and GCC thickness, supporting that the observed pattern was not fully explained by inner retinal thinning alone. Subtle misregistration, segmentation uncertainty at IPL boundaries, and preprocessing steps can also influence contrast estimates. We attempted to minimize these effects by excluding scans with motion or boundary artifacts, applying standardized preprocessing across groups, using a prespecified region of interest where IPL stratification is most consistently visible, and conducting masked analyses. Nonetheless, reflectivity-based biomarkers require careful standardization and external validation before clinical adoption.

These findings introduce IPL microstructure analysis as a complementary extension to existing OCT biomarkers in MS. Conventional OCT measures, particularly RNFL and GCIPL or GCC thickness, largely index neuroaxonal loss and are well established in MS. Our results suggest that IPL microstructure metrics capture additional information related to laminar organization and that these alterations are detectable even in eyes without MS-ON and after accounting for thickness measures. If validated, this approach could strengthen the role of OCT as a supportive paraclinical tool, including in the context of evolving diagnostic frameworks and longitudinal disease monitoring. However, discrimination in a case-control setting is not equivalent to diagnostic performance in clinical practice, and the present AUC estimates likely overstate real-world diagnostic utility.

This study has limitations. It was conducted at a single center and included only participants who self-identified as white, which limits generalizability. The MS cohort largely reflected mild disability and high disease-modifying therapy use, potentially underrepresenting more advanced or untreated disease. Subgroup analyses, including MS subtype and MS-ON comparisons, were underpowered, and MS-ON analyses may have been influenced by the exclusion of a subset of MS-ON eyes with poor IPL boundary visibility. Functional associations were exploratory and limited by sample size and multiple-comparison correction; the direction of several point estimates was consistent with worse function and greater IPL microstructure abnormality, but these results require confirmation in larger cohorts. The cross-sectional design precludes inference about temporal change or responsiveness to inflammatory activity, and we did not incorporate magnetic resonance imaging measures (e.g., lesion burden, cortical atrophy, or activity) that could help link retinal microstructure to broader central nervous system pathology. Finally, IPL analysis relied on enhanced high-definition line scans from a single OCT platform and a semiautomated pipeline, which has demonstrated reproducibility11 but has not yet been fully automated.

Future studies should evaluate IPL microstructure in larger, more diverse, multicenter cohorts and incorporate longitudinal follow-up to determine whether these metrics change over time and whether they predict disability progression or treatment response. Studies designed to address diagnostic questions should include relevant MS mimics and assess incremental value over established OCT and magnetic resonance imaging measures. Integration with brain imaging and fluid biomarkers could clarify biological correlates of reflectivity changes and help determine whether IPL microstructure is a marker of inflammatory activity, neurodegeneration, or both. Automation and device standardization will be essential to enable broad clinical translation.

Conclusions

Quantitative IPL microstructure analysis using commercial OCT identified MS-associated changes in laminar reflectivity that were detectable in non-ON eyes, remained significant after accounting for RNFL and GCC thickness, and showed stronger case-control discrimination than conventional thickness measures. These findings support IPL microstructure as a promising candidate OCT biomarker that may capture synapse-rich retinal changes relevant to MS, pending validation in longitudinal and diagnostic cohorts.
