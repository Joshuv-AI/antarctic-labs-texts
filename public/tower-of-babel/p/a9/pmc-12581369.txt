The effects of L-tartaric acid on diabetic cataracts through modulation of oxidative stress and inflammation in diabetic rats
Authors: Shengyu Wang, Li Meng, Hengmin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic cataract remains a prominent complication of diabetes mellitus, predominantly driven by oxidative stress and low-grade inflammation in the lens. Despite surgical remedies, efficacious pharmacological interventions to delay or prevent cataract progression are limited. L-tartaric acid, a naturally occurring dicarboxylic organic acid, exhibits notable antioxidative and anti-inflammatory properties, suggesting its potential therapeutic value in diabetic lens pathology.MethodsThis study explored the effects of L-tartaric acid on cataractogenesis in a streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic rat model. Oral administration of L-tartaric acid (50 mg/kg/day) or vehicle commenced one-week post-diabetes induction and continued for 12 weeks. Cataract formation was evaluated via biomicroscopic scoring. Biochemical analyses of oxidative stress markers (malondialdehyde and antioxidant enzymes) and inflammation-related cytokines were conducted, alongside quantitative PCR to assess the expression of inflammation-related cytokines and transcription factors Nrf2 and NF-κB in lens tissues.ResultsChronic hyperglycemia significantly elevated oxidative damage and pro-inflammatory mediators in the lens, culminating in advanced cataract formation in untreated diabetic rats. In contrast, L‑tartaric acid treatment markedly reduced the incidence and severity of cataracts, lowering the proportion of lenses with advanced opacities (score ≥ 3) from 80 to 30%, a 62.5% relative reduction, concomitantly attenuating lipid peroxidation and improving antioxidant enzyme activities. Notably, L-tartaric acid suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and restored Nrf2 and NF-κB to near-normal levels, without altering blood glucose concentrations.ConclusionL‑tartaric acid significantly reduces oxidative damage and inflammation in the lens, and shows potential in protecting against diabetic cataract by reinforcing the endogenous antioxidant defense network and moderating inflammatory processes. These data suggest that L‑tartaric acid may serve as a promising adjunct therapy to curb the onset and progression of diabetic cataract, possibly through mechanisms independent of glycemic control. Further studies, including topical formulations and well-designed human clinical trials, are needed to validate these preliminary observations.Keywords: Diabetic cataract, L-tartaric acid, Oxidative stress, Inflammation

Introduction

Diabetic cataract, a progressive opacification of the lens, is a principal cause of vision impairment in individuals with diabetes mellitus worldwide, contributing to nearly one-third of global blindness cases according to recent epidemiological data [1]. According to the IDF Diabetes Atlas 2025, an estimated 589 million adults (20–79 years) were living with diabetes in 2024, and this number is projected to rise to 853 million by 2050 [2], underscoring the urgent need for targeted interventions that can slow cataract onset and progression in this population. While persistent hyperglycemia is a well-recognized driver of diabetic ocular complications, cataractogenesis in diabetes is multifactorial, involving not only osmotic stress and the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), but also robust oxidative and inflammatory signaling [3, 4]. Hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress arises predominantly through excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which overwhelm the lens’s intrinsic antioxidant defense systems [5]. This ROS burden triggers lens protein aggregation, lipid peroxidation, and DNA damage, resulting in lens fiber cell dysfunction and opacification [6]. In parallel, diabetes produces a chronic low-grade inflammatory state characterized by elevated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and activation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) in lens tissues [7]. These inflammatory signals further exacerbate oxidative damage and can hasten the apoptosis of lens epithelial cells, leading to pronounced structural changes and impaired transparency [3]. Consequently, inhibiting both oxidative and inflammatory cascades has emerged as a promising strategy to prevent or slow diabetic cataractogenesis [8]. Nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a key transcription factor orchestrating the expression of cytoprotective enzymes that quell ROS-induced cell injury. In healthy tissue, Nrf2 activation drives the production of glutathione peroxidase (GPx), catalase (CAT), and other antioxidant factors essential for normal lens function [9]. Conversely, triggers ROS accumulation and cytokine release, leading to a sustained activation of NF-κB, which amplifies inflammation and suppresses Nrf2-driven antioxidative responses [10]. The intricate interplay between Nrf2 and NF-κB determines the severity of lens damage in diabetes, underscoring the need for therapeutic agents capable of modulating both pathways. L-tartaric acid, a dicarboxylic organic acid abundant in grapes and commonly employed as a food additive, has been noted for its antioxidant capacity and favorable safety profile [11, 12]. In selecting L‑tartaric acid for the present work, we considered several features that distinguish it from more extensively studied polyphenolic antioxidants such as resveratrol or curcumin. First, L‑tartaric acid is highly water‑soluble and chemically stable across a broad pH range, whereas resveratrol and curcumin have poor aqueous solubility and are prone to rapid photodegradation and metabolic inactivation, limiting their ocular bioavailability. Second, its low molecular weight (150 Da) is expected to facilitate diffusion through the lens capsule, in contrast to the larger, lipophilic structures of resveratrol (228 Da) and curcumin (368 Da). Third, L‑tartaric acid is inexpensive, widely available, and generally regarded as safe (GRAS) for human consumption, making it an attractive candidate for topical or systemic translation. Finally, preliminary in‑vitro studies indicate that tartaric acid chelates pro‑oxidant transition metals and boosts endogenous antioxidant enzyme activities, suggesting a dual mechanism of action that aligns with the pathogenic profile of diabetic cataract [13–15]. Although L-tartaric acid has demonstrated protective effects in inflammatory and oxidative stress contexts outside the lens [11], its utility in diabetic ocular conditions, particularly cataract, remains unexplored. Considering its low cost, straightforward production, and longstanding use in consumable products, L-tartaric acid presents a plausible candidate for mitigating the deleterious combination of oxidative stress and inflammation seen in diabetic lenses. Here, we evaluated the potential of L-tartaric acid to avert diabetic cataract formation in a streptozotocin (STZ)-induced rat model, leveraging established protocols to assess lens pathology and molecular endpoints. We hypothesized that L-tartaric acid would preserve lens transparency by enhancing Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defenses and restraining NF-κB-driven inflammatory signals, independent of glycemic control. Our findings illuminate how this simple organic acid may interfere with critical pathogenic pathways in the diabetic lens, providing a foundation for future translational studies on L-tartaric acid-based strategies to curtail diabetes-related vision loss.

Materials and methods

Structural characteristics of L-tartaric acid

L-tartaric acid (IUPAC name: (2R,3R)−2,3-dihydroxybutanedioic acid) is a naturally occurring dicarboxylic acid with the molecular formula COOH(CHOH)2COOH​. It is widely recognized for its array of hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, which confer strong chelating and antioxidant properties. Figure 1 illustrates the 2D chemical structure of L-tartaric acid, highlighting its two chiral centers (at carbons 2 and 3) and the hydroxyl substituents that underlie its reactivity and biological functionality.

The dicarboxylic and hydroxyl groups confer notable reactivity and metal-chelating capability, supporting L-tartaric acid’s antioxidant capacity and potential therapeutic effects in oxidative stress–related conditions

Laboratory animals and study protocol

A priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1) was performed using the incidence of advanced cataract reported by Kubo et al. [16]. With a two-tailed α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and an expected effect size (Cohen’s h = 1.0), the analysis indicated that 8 rats per group were required. To accommodate potential attrition or STZ non-responders, we enrolled 10 rats per group in each treatment arm. Eight-week-old male Wistar rats (200–250 g) were obtained from the animal facility at Xi'an Ai'er Ancient City Eye Hospital, Xi'an, Shaanxi province, China. Upon arrival, rats underwent a one-week acclimatization under standard laboratory conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, temperature 22 ± 2 °C, humidity 55 ± 5%). During this period, they had unrestricted access to standard pelleted chow and water. All experimental protocols were approved by the Medical and Research Ethics Committee of Xi'an Ai'er Ancient City Eye Hospital and Xi'an Municipal Health Commission—Youth Cultivation Project (No.2024qn18) and complied with ARRIVE guidelines. Following acclimatization, rats were randomly assigned into four groups to evaluate the therapeutic effect of L-tartaric acid, an organic acid recognized for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties:

Diabetes induction and confirmation

Rats in the diabetic groups received a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 60 mg/kg in 0.1 M citrate buffer, pH 4.5) following an overnight fast. Control rats received only citrate buffer. Seventy-two hours post-injection, fasting blood glucose levels were measured (Accu-Chek, Roche Diagnostics). Rats with fasting glucose ≥ 250 mg/dL were classified as diabetic and included in the study.

Administration of L-tartaric acid

One week after STZ or buffer injection, rats assigned to the L-tartaric acid groups (C + T and D + T) were administered L-tartaric acid at 50 mg kg−1 day−1 by gavage for 12 weeks. A preliminary four-week dose-finding study (25, 50, 100 mg kg−1 day−1) demonstrated that 50 mg kg−1 achieved maximal suppression of cataract progression without adverse effects, and thus this minimally effective, well-tolerated dose was selected for the definitive experiment. L-tartaric acid was prepared in 0.5% carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) to ensure uniform dispersion; vehicle groups (C + V and D + V) received an equal volume of 0.5% CMC without L-tartaric acid.

Ocular examination for cataract development

Rats underwent bi-weekly slit-lamp examinations under 1% tropicamide to monitor ocular status and welfare. Quantitative cataract grading (Stage 0–V) was prespecified and performed at the study end (week 12) only, on both eyes, by two independent observers blinded to group allocation using slit-lamp biomicroscopy (SL-15, Kowa). Data interpretation

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and homogeneity of variance (Levene) were assessed. When assumptions were met, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc was used; in parallel, given the modest biological n = 10 per group, we performed a nonparametric sensitivity analysis (Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s multiple comparisons) for all endpoints, which yielded conclusions concordant with the parametric tests. For categorical data, Fisher’s exact test was used. A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Blood glucose measurements verify glycemic independence of L-tartaric acid

Fasting blood glucose was monitored throughout the experiment to confirm that the protective actions of L-tartaric acid were not attributable to changes in glycemic status (Fig. 2). As expected, diabetic rats (D + V and D + T) exhibited significantly higher fasting blood glucose levels than the control groups (C + V and C + T) (P < 0.0001). There was no statistical difference in blood glucose levels between the D + V and D + T groups (P = 0.45), indicating that L-tartaric acid treatment did not normalize hyperglycemia. These results imply that its anti-cataract effects chiefly stem from antioxidative and anti-inflammatory mechanisms rather than direct regulation of blood glucose.

Fasting blood-glucose levels 12 weeks after STZ or buffer injection. C + V = Control + Vehicle; C + T = Control + L-tartaric acid; D + V = Diabetic + Vehicle; D + T = Diabetic + L-tartaric acid. Values are mean ± SD (n = 10 rats per group); ****P < 0.0001

L-tartaric acid decreases cataract incidence and severity in diabetic rats

After 12 weeks of treatment, cataract formation was confined to the diabetic groups, with clear distinctions observed among all study cohorts (Table 2 and Fig. 3). Both control groups (C + V and C + T) exhibited no lens opacities (score 0), confirming the absence of spontaneous cataracts. Conversely, rats in the diabetic vehicle group (D + V) displayed pronounced lens opacification, leaving only 4 of 20 lenses with a score below III, with many progressing to advanced stages, including fully mature cataracts (score V). Intriguingly, diabetic rats receiving L-tartaric acid (D + T) demonstrated a notable decline in both the frequency and severity of cataracts: 14 of 20 lenses remained below a score of III and fewer lenses advanced to higher opacity stages.

Lipid peroxidation (MDA)

Diabetic vehicle-treated rats (D + V) had significantly elevated MDA relative to the control vehicle group (C + V) (P < 0.0001), indicating heightened lipid peroxidation in hyperglycemia. Supplementing L-tartaric acid (D + T) substantially lowered MDA levels compared to D + V (P = 0.0001), nearly normalizing MDA in diabetic lenses (C + V vs. D + T, P = 0.77). Interestingly, a small yet significant decline in MDA was also noted in control rats receiving L-tartaric acid (C + T) compared to control vehicle (P = 0.006).

GPx activity

The D + V group showed a marked reduction in GPx activity (U mg−1 protein) versus C + V (P < 0.0001). L-tartaric acid restored GPx function in diabetic lenses (D + T vs. D + V, P < 0.0001), though the rescue was incomplete, as GPx in the D + T group remained below the level in C + V (P < 0.008). No significant difference in GPx activity was observed between C + V and C + T (P = 0.98).

CAT activity

Similar to GPx, CAT activity (U mg−1 protein) was markedly suppressed in D + V compared to C + V (P < 0.0001). L-tartaric acid treatment increased CAT activity in diabetic rats (D + T vs. D + V, P = 0.0001), though it remained below that of the control group (P = 0.01). Notably, CAT activity in the control rats did not change significantly after L-tartaric acid supplementation (C + T vs. C + V, P = 0.83).

GSH content

Diabetic lenses (D + V) were depleted in GSH compared to controls (C + V, P < 0.01). L-tartaric acid substantially replenished GSH (D + T vs. D + V, P = 0.0002), bringing levels close to those in non-diabetic eyes (C + V vs. D + T, P = 0.54). A significant difference was found between C + V and C + T (P = 0.03).

Overall, these results suggest that L-tartaric acid not only reduces the oxidative burden imposed by hyperglycemia but also supports intrinsic antioxidant enzyme systems essential for preserving lens transparency.

L-tartaric acid restrains inflammatory mediators in diabetic lenses

Diabetic vehicle-treated rats (D + V) showed a robust rise in IL-1β versus C + V. By contrast, L-tartaric acid administration (D + T) significantly dampened IL-1β expression compared to D + V, though it did not fully match the basal levels in controls. Similarly, IL-6 expression was highly induced in D + V, while L-tartaric acid reduced IL-6 in D + T. TNF-α displayed a comparable pattern, with L-tartaric acid decreasing its diabetic overexpression. Importantly, no pronounced differences were observed between the two control groups (C + V vs. C + T), indicating that L-tartaric acid does not negatively alter inflammatory pathways under non-diabetic conditions. Thus, the findings confirm L-tartaric acid’s ability to attenuate inflammatory stimuli in the lens, a plausible factor in mitigating cataract development under diabetic stress.

L-tartaric acid orchestrates Nrf2 upregulation and NF-κB downregulation

To deepen our understanding of how L-tartaric acid moderates oxidative and inflammatory networks, we evaluated expression levels of Nrf2 (a master regulator of antioxidative genes) and NF-κB (a central mediator of inflammation) (Fig. 5).

Vehicle-treated diabetic rats (D + V) exhibited a marginal reduction in Nrf2 (compared with C + V, P = 0.0001) accompanied by a marked rise in NF-κB expression. By contrast, L-tartaric acid restored—and even elevated—Nrf2 levels in diabetic lenses (D + T vs. D + V, P < 0.0001). Simultaneously, it suppressed the diabetic upsurge of NF-κB to near-control levels (D + V vs. D + T, P < 0.0001; C + V vs. D + T, P = 0.16). Notably, Nrf2 and NF-κB remained unchanged in non-diabetic rats, suggesting that L-tartaric acid selectively impacts these transcription factors in pathologic but not normal states. Together, these data position L-tartaric acid as a key modulator of the Nrf2–NF-κB axis in the diabetic lens. By boosting Nrf2-driven antioxidant pathways and diminishing pro-inflammatory NF-κB signaling, L-tartaric acid provides a protective molecular framework against diabetes-induced lens opacification. At the protein level, TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 (pg·mg−1 lens) mirrored the mRNA pattern (Fig. 6A-C).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that L-tartaric acid confers significant protection against diabetic cataract formation in a STZ-induced rat model, offering insights into a novel therapeutic avenue for managing diabetes-related ocular complications. By mitigating both oxidative and inflammatory insults to the lens, L-tartaric acid reduced cataract incidence and severity, despite persistent hyperglycemia. Notably, these beneficial effects were associated with the normalization of key antioxidant parameters and the downregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators, suggesting that L-tartaric acid may counter lens pathology by restoring the delicate redox and inflammatory balance disrupted by chronic hyperglycemia.

Although L-tartaric acid did not modify fasting glycaemia in our cataract model, recent in-vivo work shows that the compound improves insulin sensitivity and lipid homeostasis in STZ-diabetic and tyloxapol-induced hyperlipidaemic rats [17]. This suggests that systemic metabolic benefits could accompany the ocular protection observed here, and dedicated profiling of insulin and lipid parameters should therefore be incorporated into future studies. A central observation in this study is the substantial reduction in MDA levels and concomitant enhancement of enzymatic defenses, namely GPx and CAT, following L-tartaric acid supplementation (Fig. 4A–D). These enzymes are critical for detoxifying ROS whose overproduction is commonly implicated in diabetic cataractogenesis [18, 19]. The accumulation of ROS within the lens drives lipid peroxidation, protein aggregation, and lens epithelial cell damage, all of which expedite cataract formation [20]. Our findings align with earlier work demonstrating that exogenous antioxidants can curb these effects by directly scavenging ROS or bolstering endogenous detoxification pathways [21, 22]. L-tartaric acid, in particular, possesses multiple hydroxyl groups potentially capable of binding free radicals and chelating transition metals that catalyze ROS formation [23, 24]. Through these mechanisms, it appears to intervene at crucial steps in the oxidative cascade, diminishing cellular damage even under hyperglycemic conditions. In this study, diabetic vehicle-treated rats showed a marked rise in IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α mRNA (Fig. 5A–C) together with up-regulation of NF-κB and down-regulation of Nrf2 (Fig. 5D, E). L-tartaric acid administration sharply reversed these transcript changes (same panels), underscoring its anti-inflammatory and pro-antioxidant effects. Consistent with the mRNA results, ELISA normalised to lens weight (pg mg⁻1) confirmed parallel changes in TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 protein levels (Fig. 6A–C). L-tartaric acid administration significantly curbed the expression of these mediators, underscoring its anti-inflammatory prowess. Interestingly, it concurrently enhanced the expression of Nrf2, a master regulator of antioxidant and cytoprotective genes [25]. This dual modulation of NF-κB and Nrf2 has been documented for other natural or food-derived compounds [26–28], but to our knowledge, this is the first report describing a similar mechanism for L-tartaric acid in the diabetic lens. The interplay between Nrf2 and NF-κB is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of cellular fate, where enhanced Nrf2 activity can dampen inflammatory signaling, and, conversely, unchecked NF-κB activation can suppress Nrf2, exacerbating oxidative damage [29]. By recalibrating this intricate balance, L-tartaric acid may preserve lens transparency despite persistently high blood glucose levels. Several prior studies have demonstrated that certain nutraceuticals and small-molecule antioxidants, notably flavonoids, polyphenols, and α-lipoic acid, exert protective effects in diabetic models, mostly by reducing ROS and blocking inflammatory pathways [30–32]. Our work extends this paradigm to L-tartaric acid, a compound less explored in the context of diabetic complications. Tartaric acid’s recognized metal-chelating and antioxidant properties have primarily been studied in general biochemical assays and certain food science contexts [23]. Yet, the current results suggest that its use may be more wide-ranging, especially in ocular diseases that hinge on oxidative and inflammatory mechanisms. These data also diverge from some previous investigations that focused on agents such as quercetin, curcumin, or resveratrol [33–36]. Unlike polyphenols that modulate additional signaling cascades (e.g., the Sirt1 pathway), L-tartaric acid may work more directly by neutralizing free radicals or supporting Nrf2 activity via its hydroxyl-rich structure. Although the magnitude of its effect is on par with other antioxidants reported in diabetic cataract models, additional studies are needed to delineate precisely how L-tartaric acid compares in potency, bioavailability, and long-term efficacy.

In STZ-induced cataract models, resveratrol administered at 20 mg kg−1 day−1 reduced lens opacity by ≈55% [37], whereas curcumin at 75 mg kg−1 day−1 achieved a similar ≈60% reduction [38]. In the present study, L-tartaric acid produced a 62.5% reduction at only 50 mg kg−1, a lower dose than that required for curcumin and on par with resveratrol, while benefiting from markedly higher aqueous solubility (133 g 100 mL−1 at 20 °C) and GRAS regulatory status. These characteristics suggest that L-tartaric acid may combine comparable efficacy with more favourable pharmaceutical properties, although direct pharmacokinetic comparisons remain to be performed.

From a pathophysiological perspective, the data presented here reinforce the view that local oxidative and inflammatory signaling in the lens is a critical driver of cataract formation. Hyperglycemia accelerates damage in lens epithelial cells, which undergo apoptosis or necrosis under excessive stress, culminating in protein misfolding and opacification [5]. By ameliorating both free radical generation and inflammatory cascades, L-tartaric acid addresses the principal etiological mechanisms behind diabetic cataractogenesis. This represents an advantageous strategy over interventions that solely target blood glucose, since many patients experience fluctuations in glycemic control [39]. Clinically, if L-tartaric acid or derivatives thereof prove effective and safe, they could provide a complementary therapeutic option, potentially in oral or topical formulations, to delay or prevent cataract onset in at-risk populations.

Strengths and limitations

A notable strength of this work is the comprehensive assessment of biochemical and molecular markers of oxidative stress and inflammation, permitting clear correlations between L-tartaric acid treatment and preservation of lens function. Furthermore, the use of a well-established STZ model of diabetes offers reliable translational value, as it replicates core hyperglycemic features observed in human disease [40]. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while the STZ model captures aspects of type 1 diabetes, its relevance to insulin-resistant type 2 diabetes remains to be confirmed [41]. Second, the definitive study used a single dose over 12 weeks; a full dose–response and longer-term exposure are needed to define the therapeutic window and durability. Third, although we measured multiple redox and inflammatory readouts, protein-level analyses for all transcriptional targets (e.g., Nrf2/NF-κB) were limited, and the study was powered primarily for cataract outcomes rather than every secondary biomarker. Fourth, we did not measure systemic metabolic endpoints (e.g., indices of insulin resistance or serum lipids), nor did we evaluate extra-ocular organs; broader metabolic or off-target effects therefore remain unknown. Fifth, several informative oxidative-injury metrics, lens protein oxidation (protein carbonyls, AOPP), superoxide dismutase activity, and global redox indices (TAC/TOS), were not included and should be incorporated in future work. Sixth, malondialdehyde (MDA) was assayed in the post-centrifugation supernatant rather than the total homogenate; whole-tissue lipid-peroxidation measurements would provide a more complete assessment of oxidative damage. Seventh, cataract scores were collected only at the 12-week end point, precluding time-course analysis of onset and progression. Eighth, only male rats were studied; potential sex-specific effects were not assessed. Finally, while cataract grading was conducted by two blinded observers, biomicroscopic scoring is clinician-dependent; incorporation of objective lens densitometry (e.g., Scheimpflug tomography or comparable imaging) would strengthen future studies [42, 43].

Future directions

Future work should: (1) confirm efficacy in insulin-resistant/type-2 diabetes models; (2) profile ocular pharmacokinetics and optimise delivery, L-tartaric acid’s high water solubility suits eye-drop formulations, while intravitreal or nano-carrier systems may further enhance lens and vitreous exposure; (3) map Nrf2–NF-κB crosstalk via proteomic, metabolomic and imaging approaches; (4) test synergy with other antioxidants [43]; (5) run ≥ 6-month studies with systemic histopathology to verify durability and safety; and (6) proceed to dose-finding, safety and controlled trials to support clinical translation.

Conclusion

In a streptozotocin-induced rat model, L-tartaric acid was associated with lower cataract severity and favorable modulation of redox and inflammatory pathways without altering glycemia. These findings support further preclinical evaluation, including dose–response testing, longer-term studies, comprehensive oxidative-injury profiling (total-homogenate lipid peroxidation), ocular pharmacokinetics/biodistribution, and longitudinal cataract grading, to define mechanism, durability, and optimal delivery. Given the above limitations and the use of a single animal model, our results should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of efficacy; confirmation in insulin-resistant models and across sexes is warranted before considering translational studies.
