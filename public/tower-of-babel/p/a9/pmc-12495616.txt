A comparison of efficacy and safety of oral versus intravenous applications of tranexamic acid in total hip and knee arthroplasty: an updated systematic review meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Pengrui Feng, Li Feng, Zhencheng Xiong, Chaoyi Zhang, Guanglin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranexamic acid (TXA) has been extensively used in total hip and total knee arthroplasty (THA and TKA) to reduce blood loss. The commonly investigated routes of TXA administration include oral, intravenous, intra-articular, and topical methods. However, the ideal route of administration remains controversial. The objective of this study was to evaluate and compare the efficacy and safety of oral versus intravenous tranexamic acid in primary total knee and hip arthroplasty.MethodsThe PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library and Embase databases were systematically searched for relevant randomized controlled trials (RCTs) which updated on October 2024. Decline of hemoglobin (DHB), total blood loss (TBL), length of hospital stays (HS), intra-operative blood loss (IBL), operative time, transfusion rate, deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and intramuscular venous thrombosis (IVT) were extracted and analyzed. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for each outcome based on the degree of heterogeneity. Furthermore, we assessed publication bias through funnel plots, Egger tests and the trim-and-fill method. The study adhered to PRISMA and AMSTAR guidelines. All of the analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4 and Stata 15.0 software.ResultsA total of 2,262 patients from 16 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were ultimately included in the meta-analysis. No statistically significant differences were observed in terms of decline of hemoglobin (P = 0.95), total blood loss(P = 0.59), length of hospital stays (P = 0.28), intra-operative blood loss (P = 0.64), operative time (P = 0.67), transfusion rate (P = 0.96), deep vein thrombosis (P = 0.14), intramuscular venous thrombosis (P = 0.14) between the oral and intravenous groups. But five studies reported that oral TXA has lower cost than intravenous.ConclusionThis meta-analysis provided updated evidence that suggests oral and intravenous TXA have similar efficacy and safety. However, oral tranexamic acid can reduce financial burden of joint replacement on patients. So more high-quality RCTs are needed to confirm that oral may be the ideal route of administration for TXA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09193-8.Keywords: Oral, Intravenous, Tranexamic acid, Primary total hip/knee arthroplasty

Introduction

The prevalence of degenerative knee or hip diseases and osteoarthritis, has been reported to show a markedly increasing trend. These conditions result in chronic joint pain and represent major contributors to impaired mobility and functional disability [1–3].

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and total hip arthroplasty (THA) are widely suggested as effective surgical interventions for alleviating pain and enhancing joint function in patients with osteoarthritis [4]. However, perioperative blood loss remains a major clinical concern. The incidence of transfusion-requiring events has been reported to be approximately 1.4%. Low perioperative hemoglobin levels may reduce quality of life, delay postoperative recovery, increase healthcare costs, and heighten the risk of morbidity [5]. To minimize perioperative blood loss and improving surgical quality, various strategies such as tourniquet use, intravenous iron, and pharmacologic interventions have been adopted by orthopedic surgeons [6–10]. Tranexamic acid (TXA), an antifibrinolytic agent, has emerged as a cornerstone due to its proven efficacy and ease of administration in reducing perioperative blood loss and transfusion requirements.

Although there are four routes of administration of TXA: orally, intravenously (IV), intra-articularly and topically, the ideal route of administration remains controversial [11]. Numerous studies have compared the efficacy of these three administration routes. However, compared to the other two routes, there is a paucity of literature examining oral administration of TXA. From a pharmacological standpoint, oral administration of aminocaproic acid exhibits relatively low bioavailability, delayed time to reach peak plasma concentration, and a prolonged elimination half-life. In contrast, intravenous administration results in a more rapid achievement of peak plasma levels and a shorter elimination half-life [12]. Nevertheless, high-quality evidence comparing the clinical benefits and drawbacks of the two administration methods is still insufficient. Three previous meta-analyses (Zhang LK et al., 2017; Ye W et al., 2020; Sun C et al., 2020) compared the efficacy of oral and IV tranexamic acid eventually presented there is no significant difference between two modes. However, these analyses had limitations, including a limited number of included studies, incomplete outcome assessment, and insufficient strength of evidence [13–15]. As new RCTs continue to emerge, an updated meta-analysis is warranted to synthesize current evidence and provide more definitive guidance for clinical practice.

To ensure the robustness of evidence on the comparison of oral and intravenous applications, our meta-analysis systematically reviews and synthesizes evidence from more high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs), aiming to provide clear guidance for clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

This study adhered to the proposals of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) and the Assessment of Methodological Quality in Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) [16, 17]. Using the GRADE approach to assess strength of evidence.

Search strategy

We comprehensively and systematically searched the Literature which updated on October 2024 in four databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and Cochrane Library using MeSH terms and free key words, such as “Tranexamic Acid”, “TXA”, “AMCA”, “AMCHA”, “t-AMCHA”, “trans-4-(Aminomethyl)cyclohexanecarboxylic Acid”, “Cyklokapron”, “Ugurol”, “Transamin”, “KABI 2161”, “Amchafibrin”, “Anvitoff”, “Spotof”, “Exacyl”, “intravenous”, “oral”, “total knee/hip arthroplasty”, “THA”, “TKA” in conjunction with Boolean operators “AND” or “OR” (Supplementary Table 1). We set the language for searching literature to English. In addition, we identified full papers from references to ensure no relevant literature was missed. The literature search was completed independently by two team members. The search results are presented in Fig. 1.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

Two reviewers independently evaluated the methodological quality of each study based on the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews, which includes six aspects: “Randomization”, “Allocation concealment”, “Blinding”, “Incomplete outcome data”, “Selective reporting” and other sources of bias [18]. Particular emphasis was placed on allocation concealment and blinding domains due to their critical role in minimizing Bias in surgical intervention trials. The GRADE approach was used to evaluate the certainty of the evidence for each outcome. The results of the GRADE assessment are presented in Supplemental Fig. 2.

Data extraction

Data extraction was done by two people independently. If a disagreement occurred, the result was reached after discussion with the third review member. We extracted data including the first author, Year of publication, country, type of surgery, number of people in each group, mean age, gender, body mass index, dosage of TXA, transfusion criteria, anesthesia, and various outcome measures. The primary outcome measures consisted of TBL, DHB. Secondary outcome measures included IBL, operating time, DVT, IVT, HS and transfusion rate. As for continuous variables, we extracted the mean and standard deviation with a 95% confidence interval (95%CI) of the intervention and control groups for continuous variables and the number of events for dichotomous variable.

Statistical analysis

Review Manager 5.4 and Stata 15.0 were used to perform statistical analyses. Std. Mean Difference (SMD) and Odds Ratio (OR) were used as effect sizes for continuous and dichotomous variables respectively. Forest plots were generated to visualize the results of the statistical analysis. If P-values were less than 0.05, we considered the results to be statistically significant. The value of I [2] symbolized the magnitude of heterogeneity. I2 > 50% implies severe heterogeneity, so the random-effects model was used. In contrast, we used fixed-effects model when I2 < 50%. Further sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially removing data from the final model. Publication bias was evaluated by visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression test for outcomes which include more than 5 studies. If the funnel plot is asymmetric or the Egger’s test (P < 0.05), it indicates the potential presence of publication bias. In such cases, the trim-and-fill method was applied to account for potentially missing studies. If the adjusted pooled effect size remains consistent with the original analysis, it suggests that our conclusions are robust and unlikely to be substantially affected by publication bias.

We Further eliminated 59 studies by reading the full articles, which did not meet the inclusion criteria (Supplementary Table 2). Therefore, a final total of 16 RCTs were included [19–34]. Both Fillingham 2019 and Sershon 2020 demonstrated two different doses of intravenous tranexamic acid versus oral. So we respectively described them as “Fillingham 2019a”, “Fillingham 2019b”, “Sershon 2020a”, “Sershon 2020b”. The characteristics of included studies are shown in Table 1.

Risks of bias assessment

The assessment of risks of Bias in 16 RCTs were exhibited in Figs. 2 and 3. We rated them as having unclear other risk of bias to avoid ignoring other potential risks of bias in the included studies. Some randomized controlled studies did not fully conduct blinding procedures because of the financial and organizational constraints of a multicenter randomized clinical trial. The risk of bias assessments are summarized in Figs. 2 and 3. Several included studies were judged as having high or unclear risk, particularly in the domains of blinding of participants and personnel as well as allocation concealment. These challenges are common in surgical trials, where blinding is often difficult to implement, potentially leading to performance and detection bias. We have acknowledged these methodological limitations and considered them when interpreting the pooled results.

Results of the meta-analysis

Total blood loss (TBL)

A total of 12 data sets from 10 studies comprising 1,370 patients were pooled for the analysis of total blood loss (TBL). The results showed no statistically significant difference between oral and intravenous (IV) administration of tranexamic acid (TXA) (standardized mean difference [SMD]: − 0.03; 95% confidence interval [CI]: − 0.14 to 0.08; P = 0.59; Fig. 4A). A subgroup analysis stratified by procedure type (total hip arthroplasty [THA] vs. total knee arthroplasty [TKA]) similarly revealed no significant differences in TBL between the two administration routes in either subgroup (Supplementary Fig. 1 A, 1B). Clinically, the effect size (SMD = −0.03) is negligible, suggesting that the difference in total blood loss between oral and intravenous TXA administration is not only statistically non-significant but also lacks meaningful impact in practical settings. This supports the clinical equivalence of the two routes in terms of total blood loss control. Sensitivity analyses exhibited the combined results are not affected by individual studies (Fig. 7A). Publication bias was assessed using both funnel plot inspection and Egger’s regression test, with the latter yielding a P-value of 0.026, indicating potential bias. Visual asymmetry in the funnel plot further supported this finding. To account for potential missing studies, the trim-and-fill method was applied. After imputing three studies, the adjusted pooled effect size remained consistent with the original analysis (P = 0.11), suggesting that the findings regarding TBL are robust and unlikely to be materially affected by publication bias (Fig. 4B and C).

Decline of hemoglobin (DHB)

In terms of DHB, we found no significant difference (SMD: 0.00; 95%CI: −0.10 to 0.10; P = 0.95; Fig. 5A) between oral and iv TXA through analyzing 14 groups of data from 12 studies, which include 1422 participants. Similarly, in both the THA and TKA subgroups, there were no statistically significant differences in DHB between the oral and intravenous TXA groups (Supplementary Fig. 1 C, 1D). From actual clinical practice, the observed effect size (SMD = 0.00) reflects a complete equivalence between the two administration routes with respect to postoperative hemoglobin drop, suggesting that both oral and intravenous TXA are equally effective in minimizing perioperative blood loss. Sensitivity analysis shows the result is robust (Fig. 7B). Funnel plot and Egger test (P = 0.30 > 0.05) detect that there is no publication bias (Fig. 8A).

Length of hospital stays (HS)

There is no statistical difference in the length of hospitalization of patients between the oral and iv TXA groups (SMD: −0.06; 95%CI: −0.18 to 0.05; P = 0.28; Fig. 5B). Consistent findings were observed in both the THA and TKA subgroups, where the reduction in HS did not differ significantly between the oral and intravenous TXA administration routes (Supplementary Fig. 1 F, 1E). Clinically, HS generally influenced by multiple factors (such as complications, functional recovery, anesthesia, surgical procedures), the small effect size (SMD = −0.06) indicates that the difference in hospital stay between oral and IV TXA is minimal and likely not meaningful in real-world practice, further supporting the comparable perioperative efficacy of both administration routes. Sensitivity analyze shows that the combined data are not affected by one of studies (Fig. 7C). Funnel plot is symmetrical and egger test (P = 0.44 > 0.05) demonstrate the absence of publication bias (Fig. 8B).

Intra-operative blood loss (IBL)

Three studies included 428 patients were tested the effect of oral TXA versus IV TXA on IBL. The result (SMD: −0.05; 95%CI: −0.24 to 0.14; P = 0.64; Fig. 5C) means there is no significant difference between the oral TXA versus IV TXA groups. In most TKA/THA surgeries, this degree of difference does not have a practical impact on intraoperative procedures or postoperative recovery. The funnel plot is exhibited in Fig. 8C. Given that fewer than 10 studies were included for several outcomes, the funnel plot may have limited validity in detecting publication bias.

Intramuscular venous thrombosis (IVT)

686 patients from five studies were included in the analysis. We found no statistical difference between two groups (OR: 0.66; 95%CI: 0.39 to 1.14; P = 0.14; Fig. 5D). Although the result did not reach statistical significance, the OR of 0.66 suggests a potential trend toward reduced risk in the oral TXA group. However, given the wide confidence interval and lack of significance, this difference should be interpreted with caution and more RCTs are needed to validate this. As shown in Fig. 8D, funnel plot symmetry implies the absence of publication bias, but the results lack robustness due to the few studies included.

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)

Four studies, including 626 patients, tested the effect of oral TXA versus IV TXA on the incidence of DVT and found no significant difference (OR: 0.37; 95%CI: 0.1 to 1.4; P = 0.14; Fig. 7E). Although OR indicates a possible reduction in the incidence of deep vein thrombosis, the wide confidence interval and lack of statistical significance indicate uncertainty around the true effect. Funnel plot was shown in Fig. 8E.

Transfusion rate

Pooled data for the transfusion rate from 12 studies, including 1581 patients, showed no statistical difference in blood transfusion rate between the oral and intravenous TXA groups (OR: 0.99; 95%CI: 0.66 to 1.48; P = 0.96; Fig. 6A). The OR of 0.99 indicates nearly identical transfusion rates between oral and IV TXA groups, reinforcing the notion that both administration routes are equally effective in reducing the need for perioperative transfusion. The results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in Fig. 7D. In addition, we did funnel plot and Egger test (P = 0.64 > 0.05) to detect publication bias and got negative results (Fig. 8F).

Operative time

As shown in Fig. 6B, there is no statistical difference between two groups regarding operative time (SMD: 0.02; 95%CI: −0.07 to 0.11; P = 0.67; Fig. 6B). The extremely small effect size (SMD = 0.02) implies that oral and intravenous TXA have virtually no impact on operative duration. Furthermore, the duration of surgery is influenced by multiple factors, and the method of administering TXA does not significantly alter the surgical process. Supporting the notion that the choice of administration route does not influence surgical workflow or efficiency. The p-value in egger test was 0.49, combined with funnel plot can lead to the conclusion that there is no publication bias in this study (Fig. 8G). The result of sensitivity analysis is displayed in Figure which symbolizes the consolidated data is reliable (Fig. 7E).

Discussion

The number of total hip/total knee arthroplasty remains consistently high due to the increasing prevalence of degenerative joint diseases in the aging population. THA or TKA can reduce chronic pain and improve patients’ quality of life [1–4]. Although both THA and TKA are well-established orthopedic procedures, perioperative blood loss remains a notable clinical concern. It has been demonstrated in the Literature that an average of 700 to 2000 ml of bleeding per THA or TKA procedure which may increase the probability of postoperative complications [35, 36].

Tranexamic acid (TXA) is an antifibrinolytic drug that has been proven to minimize blood loss in arthroplasty. TXA can be administered through four routes: oral, intravenous (IV), intra-articular and topical [11]. In terms of the intravenous and topical use of tranexamic acid, there has been a sufficient amount of literature examining and comparing their efficacy and safety [37, 38]. However, the efficacy and safety of oral TXA compared to IV TXA in THA and TKA remain a subject of ongoing research and debate, particularly given the limited number of high-quality studies available. To our knowledge, there have been three meta-analyses comparing oral and IV administration of TXA prior to our study [13–15]. However, these studies are constrained by methodological limitations, including a limited number of included RCTs and insufficient consideration of relevant clinical outcomes. Thus, the purpose of our study is to perform an update meta-analysis, including more RCTs, taking into account new outcomes, reducing the risk of bias and misinterpretation of results in order to strengthen the evidence.

This meta-analysis demonstrates that oral tranexamic acid (TXA) provides comparable efficacy and safety to intravenous (IV) TXA with respect to hemoglobin drop (DHB) and total blood loss (TBL). These findings align with previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses that have reported similar reductions in perioperative blood loss between the two administration routes. Notably, our analysis incorporates several recently published, high-quality RCTs, which strengthen the overall certainty of the evidence. Given that blood loss remains a significant concern during THA and TKA, often leading to allogeneic blood transfusions which may increase patients’ risk of postoperative complications, our findings suggest that oral TXA can serve as an effective alternative to IV administration for minimizing perioperative blood loss.

Subsequently, we found no statistical difference in intra-operative blood loss (IBL) and operative time, representing the two modes of tranexamic acid administration had no effect on the surgical procedure.

Although allogeneic blood transfusion remains an effective strategy to manage perioperative blood loss, it is associated with numerous adverse effects, including hemolytic reactions, infections, immune suppression, and increased morbidity [5, 39]. In our meta-analysis, no statistically significant difference in transfusion rates was observed between oral and intravenous (IV) TXA administration. This finding is clinically relevant, as minimizing transfusion requirements is essential for improving patient outcomes and reducing healthcare expenditures. Therefore, the comparable transfusion rates between the two routes further support the role of oral TXA as a viable and effective option for blood conservation in total joint arthroplasty.

Safety remains a critical concern when evaluating TXA administration routes, particularly regarding the risk of thromboembolic events such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and intramuscular venous thrombosis (IVT). Our meta-analysis showed no statistically significant differences in the incidence of DVT and IVT between oral and IV TXA. This result suggests that oral TXA does not increase thromboembolic risk compared to IV administration. In view of the fact that TXA has been associated with concerns over hypercoagulability, particularly in high-risk patients [40], these findings are reassuring and support the broader adoption of oral TXA in clinical practice.

Length of hospital stay (HS) is another critical factor influencing healthcare resource utilization and patient recovery. Our findings, consistent with prior meta-analyses, demonstrated no significant difference in HS between oral and IV TXA. Shorter HS is often associated with improved recovery and reduced risk of hospital-acquired complications, making it an important outcome in modern healthcare delivery. This result provides further evidence of the clinical utility of oral tranexamic acid.

The cost-effectiveness of oral TXA is one of its most compelling advantages. Five RCTs have demonstrated that oral TXA is significantly less expensive than IV formulations (Supplementary Table 3), with cost reductions ranging from 70 to 90% [22, 23, 25, 27, 28]. This substantial cost difference has important implications for healthcare systems, particularly in resource-limited settings, as it enables cost savings without compromising patient outcomes. Moreover, all included RCTs indicated that oral TXA administration simplifies perioperative workflows by eliminating the need for intravenous access, thereby reducing both procedural costs and logistical complexity. Given these advantages, oral TXA represents a particularly promising option in low-resource settings. Its lower cost, ease of transportation, and independence from infusion equipment make it a potentially more accessible alternative in settings with limited healthcare infrastructure. Collectively, these findings may have important implications for optimizing perioperative blood management strategies worldwide, particularly within resource-constrained healthcare systems.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations in our analysis must be acknowledged. First, significant heterogeneity was observed in TXA dosing regimens and administration timing among the included studies. No consensus currently exists regarding the optimal dosing and timing for either route of administration, and further research is needed to standardize these protocols. Secondly, variations in surgical techniques, anesthesia methods, tourniquet usage, and follow-up durations across studies may have introduced heterogeneity and influenced the results. Moreover, due to limitations in data availability, we were unable to perform more extensive subgroup analyses. Third, although oral TXA is generally easier to administer and more acceptable to patients, adherence variability (e.g., missed doses or delayed ingestion due to nausea or sedation) was not evaluated in the included studies, and should be considered a potential limitation that warrants further investigation through high-quality studies. fig 6

Conclusion

This meta-analysis of high-quality RCTs provides robust evidence that oral TXA is as effective and safe as IV TXA for reducing perioperative blood loss, hemoglobin (Hb) drop, transfusion rates, length of hospital stays, intra-operative blood loss, operative time, DVT and IVT in patients undergoing THA and TKA. Additionally, oral TXA offers significant cost advantages and simplifies perioperative management, making it an attractive alternative to IV TXA in clinical practice.
