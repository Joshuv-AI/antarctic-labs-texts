Effects of institutional rearing and foster care intervention on error monitoring and externalizing behaviors in adolescence
Authors: Ranjan Debnath, Sonya V. Troller-Renfree, Charles H. Zeanah, Charles A. Nelson, Nathan A. Fox
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Children raised in institutions display deficits in error monitoring and increased psychopathology. Deficits in error monitoring might be a pathway for the emergence of psychopathology in previously institutionalized adolescents. Here we investigate the impact of early psychosocial deprivation and a foster care intervention on error monitoring and its association with internalizing and externalizing behavioral problems in adolescence A modified Flanker task assessed error monitoring in 16-year-old adolescents from the BEIP. The ERN and mid-frontal theta power were computed as indices of neural responses of error monitoring. Adolescents who experienced early institutional rearing and were subsequently placed into foster care showed comparable behavioral (RT, accuracy) and neural (ERN, theta power) measures of error monitoring to their never institutionalized peers; whereas adolescents who received care as usual showed both perturbed behavioral performance and neural responses. Longer duration of institutional care was associated with a reduction in mid-frontal theta power. The results further demonstrated a link between error monitoring as measured by ERN and mid-frontal theta and externalizing-ADHD behavioral problems in adolescents who continued receiving care as usual. The results highlight the long‐term positive impact of early foster care placement and perturbations due to prolonged institutional care in neural responses of error monitoring.

Introduction

Children reared in institutions are at increased risk of long‐term negative developmental outcomes in cognition and psychopathology. Significant impairments across various developmental domains have been found among children experiencing early psychosocial deprivation. Institutional care has been linked to perturbations in executive functions (Loman et al., 2013, McDermott et al., 2013, Wade et al., 2023). Executive function encompasses a set of cognitive processes essential for cognitive control of behavior and achieving behavioral goals. Primary components of executive function include inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility (Diamond, 2013). Of particular relevance to the current paper, institutional rearing has been associated with deficits in specific executive function skills such as inhibitory control (McDermott et al., 2012), response or error monitoring (Loman et al., 2013, McDermott et al., 2013, Troller-Renfree et al., 2016). In this study, we investigate the development of error monitoring in children removed from institutions and placed into foster care in infancy as part of the Bucharest Early Intervention Project (BEIP). We also examine the relations between the neural correlates of error monitoring and behavioral problems. The BEIP is a randomized control trial of foster care as an alternative to institutional care (Zeanah et al., 2003). The BEIP follows a group of institutionalized infants who either received care as usual (CAUG) or were randomized to high‐quality foster care (FCG) and a group of demographically matched never institutionalized (NIG) children. The BEIP aimed to investigate the physical, psychological, and neural development related to institutional care and the effects of foster care intervention early in life (Zeanah et al., 2003).

Error monitoring is the ability to detect an error once it occurs (Moser et al., 2013). Error monitoring works in tandem with inhibitory control by signaling and detecting errors in order to optimize behavior goals (McDermott et al., 2013, Moser et al., 2013). Deficits in error monitoring have been found in previously institutionalized children (Loman et al., 2013, Troller-Renfree et al., 2016). Children raised in institutions showed persistent deficits in inhibitory control and error monitoring into early adolescence (Buzzell et al., 2020, Colvert et al., 2008a, Colvert et al., 2008b, Loman et al., 2013, McDermott et al., 2013, Troller-Renfree et al., 2016). However, studies examining the possibility of improving these executive skills following a change in caregiving environments are scarce.

Error monitoring underlies a well-established ERP component known as error-related negativity (ERN), which is a negative deflection that is maximal at frontocentral scalp sites and peaks within the first 100 ms following an error response (Gehring et al., 1990). The ERN is at least in part originated in the anterior cingulate cortex and reflects the error detection associated with response selection (Dehaene et al., 1994, Veen and Carter, 2002). Previous works examining ERN in children who experienced early institutional care produced mixed findings. Loman et al. (2013) found no difference in ERN amplitudes between children adopted internationally from institutions, non-adopted children, and post-foster care children. In the BEIP at 8-year assessment, McDermott et al. (2013) also did not find differences in the ERN amplitude between CAUG and FCG. However, the impact of foster care placement on ERN amplitude emerged when the children were examined at 12 years of age. The 12-year assessment showed that children randomized to care as usual and experienced prolonged institutional care exhibited reduced ERN amplitudes compared to those who received high-quality foster care intervention (Troller-Renfree et al., 2016).

Another measure of error monitoring, which has received less attention in previously institutionalized children, is mid-frontal theta (4–8 Hz) power. Mid-frontal theta is believed to reflect the activity of the brain mechanism supporting error monitoring (Cavanagh et al., 2009). Occurrences of the error have been shown to increase theta power at the mid-frontal cortex (Buzzell et al., 2019, Cavanagh et al., 2009). Only a handful of previous studies examined mid-frontal theta power among children with early psychosocial deprivation (Buzzell et al., 2020, Wade et al., 2023). In the BEIP sample at age 16-year assessment, Buzzell et al. (2020) found that children who experienced institutional care exhibited reduced mid-frontal theta power compared to those who received a foster care intervention on a go/no-go task. Their findings suggest that the development of the mid-frontal theta is impacted by removal from institutional care and placement into foster care.

In addition to deficits in executive functions, institutional care has been associated with elevated risks of psychiatric disorders. Children raised in institutions show poor attention and dysregulated emotional and behavioral control (Beckett et al., 2006). Studies examining the etiology of these disorders have identified links between brain functions underlying error monitoring and psychopathology among children with a history of institutional care (Frenkel et al., 2020, Troller-Renfree et al., 2018). At the 8-year BEIP assessment, McDermott et al. (2013) found that, among children randomized to foster care, those who exhibited larger ERN had lower levels of socio-emotional behavioral problems. This association between ERN amplitude and psychopathology remained at 12 years of age, as reductions in ERN amplitude were associated with increased ADHD and externalizing problems (Troller-Renfree et al., 2016). Further investigation of time-frequency analyses at the 16-year assessment, Buzzell et al. (2020) found that institutional rearing predicted reduced mid-frontal theta, which was linked to a heightened risk for psychopathology. In contrast, foster care placement was associated with increased theta, which was, in turn, associated with reductions in psychopathology. While these findings suggest that removal from institutions and placement into foster care reduces psychosocial behavioral problems, the continued risk for psychopathology persists even after placement into foster care (Zeanah et al., 2009) or adoption (Colvert et al., 2008a, Colvert et al., 2008b), with some problems persisting into adolescence.

In the current study, we examined data from follow-up measures of error monitoring in the BEIP at 16 years of age. The main goal of this study was to examine the continuous effect of foster care intervention on the development of error monitoring in adolescents removed from institutions and placed into foster care in infancy. We investigated behavioral measures and EEG data collected during a flanker task when children were 16 years of age. Consistent with previous BEIP studies examining the effect of institutionalized care on error monitoring at 8 years (McDermott et al., 2013) and 12 years (Troller-Renfree et al., 2016), we predicted that the CAUG would show perturbed behavioral performance and error monitoring as evidenced by lower ERN amplitude and mid-frontal theta power whereas performance and ERN of the FCG would be comparable to the NIG. We also examined whether prolonged institutional care and placement timing (i.e., age at original placement) into foster care affected the error monitoring. Additionally, consistent with findings at 12 years (Troller-Renfree et al., 2016), we predicted that institutional care would moderate the relation between error monitoring and externalizing-ADHD problems.

Material and methods

Participants

The sample comprised 136 children abandoned in infancy and placed into institutional care in Bucharest, Romania, enrolled in the BEIP. The trial design and participant selection of the BEIP are summarized in the consort diagram (Fig. 1). At the inception of the BEIP, 68 infants remained in institutions receiving care as usual (CAUG) and 68 infants were randomized to foster care (FCG). A group of 72 typically developing children who never spent time in institutions (NIG) were also recruited at baseline assessment. At 16 years, 47 CAUG (22 female), 49 FCG (23 female), and 48 NIG (27 female) completed the Flanker task. The mean age of assessment was 16.51 years (SD =.589) for CAUG, 16.41 years (SD =.749) for FCG, and 16.67 years (SD =.894) for NIG. Although many of the children were no longer in their original randomized placements at age 16, the data to be presented in this paper uses an intent-to-treat approach such that data are analyzed using a child’s initial placement. The University Institutional Review Boards of the principal investigators (Fox, Nelson & Zeanah) and the University of Bucharest approved the 16-year study protocol. Flanker task

In this study, participants performed an adapted version of the Flanker task. The stimuli consisted of five angle brackets presented horizontally on a computer screen via E-Prime software. In congruent trials, all of the arrows were facing the same direction (<<<<< or >>>>>), and in incongruent trials, the middle arrow facing the opposite direction of the four flanking arrows (<<><< or >><>>). Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and correctly as possible via button press to indicate the direction of the middle arrow (right or left). Participants completed 20 practice trials, followed by 160 test trials. The test trials comprised equal numbers of congruent and incongruent trials (80 trials each). Trials began with the presentation of a warning cue (*****) for 500 ms, followed by a blank screen and then the presentation of the target flanker stimulus, and then another blank screen for 500 ms. Consistent with previous Flanker tasks used with the present institutional sample (McDermott et al., 2013, Troller-Renfree et al., 2016), the target stimulus presentation time was dynamically adjusted based on each participant’s performance. If a participant had a cumulative accuracy greater than 60%, the subsequent trial presentation time was decreased by 50 ms. Conversely, if their cumulative accuracy was less than 60%, the subsequent trial presentation time was increased by 50 ms. Furthermore, stimulus presentation times were jittered by one of three values: −60 ms, −10 ms, and +40 ms. The intertrial interval was randomly assigned to be one of four durations: 773 ms, 790 ms, 807 ms, or 824 ms. The response time and response accuracy per trial were recorded.

EEG data recording and preprocessing

EEG data were recorded with a 64–channel HydroCel Geodesic Sensor Net and a NetAmps 300 amplifier (Electrical Geodesics, Inc., Eugene, OR). EEG data were sampled at 500 Hz and impedances were kept below 50 kΩ. The Cz electrode was used as a reference. EEG data were preprocessed with the EEGLAB toolbox (Delorme and Makeig, 2004) and MADE preprocessing pipeline (Debnath et al., 2020). EEG data were down-sampled to 250 Hz and bandpass filtered (0.3–40 Hz). Artifact-laden channels were identified and removed. Independent component analysis (ICA) was performed on continuous EEG data to remove ocular artifacts and generic noise. Artifactual independent components (ICs) were identified and removed from the data using the Adjusted-ADJUST scripts (Leach et al., 2020) and by visual inspection of individual ICs. EEG data were then segmented into 1.5–s epochs starting 500 ms before and ending 1000 ms after response onset. Epochs containing artifacts were removed from the analysis using a voltage threshold (± 150μV) rejection. After artifact rejection, missing channels were interpolated using spherical interpolation and epoched data were re‐referenced to an average of all channels.

ERP processing

After pre-processing of EEG data, participants were included in ERP and time-frequency analyses if they had at least 10 artifact-free error trials. This process excluded 14 subjects (CAUG = 4, FCG = 4, NIG = 6) from analyses. To compute ERP, correct and error trials were separately averaged for each participant and were baseline corrected to the average activity from −200 ms to −100 ms before the response. As in the previous 8- and 12-year assessments, we computed ERP on both congruent and incongruent trials (McDermott et al., 2013, Troller-Renfree et al., 2016). To examine brain activity specific to errors (ERN), a difference wave was created by subtracting the brain activity on correct trials from the brain activity on error trials (i.e., error – correct, ⵠERN). The differences in brain activity were evaluated as the average activity at the frontocentral electrode (FCz), where the difference was maximum.

Time-frequency processing

Time-frequency analysis was performed by the MATLAB script provided by Cohen (2014). Time-frequency power was calculated for the epoch data. Time-frequency power provides a two-dimensional (time × frequency) estimate of average changes in spectral power (in dB) relative to the baseline. To compute time-frequency power, each epoch was convolved with complex Morlet wavelets, which estimated power in the frequency range of 1–30 Hz (in 60 linearly spaced steps). The wavelet cycles were set at 2 cycles at the lowest frequency (1 Hz), increasing to 7 cycles at the highest frequency (40 Hz). Spectral power was calculated for all channels and separately for error and correct trials. Power was calculated for each epoch relative to the −200 to −100 ms baseline period. As in ERP, time-frequency power was calculated on both congruent and incongruent trials. A power difference was computed by subtracting the power on correct trials from the power on error trials (i.e., error–correct). The primary activity of interest was theta (4–8 Hz) power at FCz electrodes.

MacArthur Health and Behavior Questionnaire

The Health and Behavior Questionnaire (HBQ) comprises multiple scales that measure children’s mental health and well-being. For the present paper, two subscales of the HBQ were used: internalizing behaviors and externalizing-ADHD behaviors. The internalizing scale is comprised of items related to depression, separation anxiety, and overanxious behaviors. The externalizing-ADHD scale consists of oppositional defiance, conduct problems, overt aggression, relational aggression, inattention, and impulsivity. As in the previous 8- and 12-year assessments, we analyzed the accuracy and reaction time on both congruent and incongruent trials (McDermott et al., 2013, Troller-Renfree et al., 2016). To assess group differences in the ERN and theta power, we adopted the intent‐to‐treat analysis approach and directly compared the brain responses between FCG and CAUG separately for ERN and theta power. We then examined whether the brain responses of the two intervention groups (FCG, CAUG) were comparable with the never-institutionalized (NIG) participants. We performed separate one‐way ANOVA for ERN and theta power with three groups (CAUG, FCG, NIG) as the between‐subjects variable.

Additionally, to examine the influence of the participant group and the brain responses (ERN, theta power) on mental health outcomes (Internalizing, Externalizing-ADHD behaviors composites), a series of moderation analyses were performed. To address potential multicollinearity, interaction terms were standardized and mean-centered. Significant Group (CAUG, FCG, NIG) by brain responses (ERN, Theta power) moderation effects were probed by follow-up simple slope analyses.

Results

Accuracy

Accuracy was assessed by comparing the percentage of correct responses on congruent and incongruent trials. Intent-to-treat analysis revealed a main effect of trial type (F(1, 83) = 291.561, p < 0.001, η2 = .484) with higher accuracy on congruent (M = 82.26, SD = 11.36) compared to incongruent trials (M = 57.39, SD = 14.29). The main effect of Group was marginally significant (F(1, 83) = 3.657, p = 0.059, η2 = .016). Follow‐up comparisons revealed that collapsed across trial type, the FCG (M = 72.16, SD = 10.40) had more correct responses than the CAUG (M = 67.64, SD = 11.30). The Group x Trial type interaction was not significant (F(1, 83) = .023, p = .879).

Analyses comparing the CAUG and FCG with the community sample (NIG) were almost identical to the intent-to-treat analyses. A main effect was found for Trial type (F(1, 127) = 501.093, p < 0.001, η2 = .456) with more accurate responding on congruent (M = 84.78%, SD = 11.09) compared to incongruent trials (M = 60.58%, SD = 15.12). Additionally, a main effect of Group emerged (F(2, 127) = 10.024, p < 0.001, η2 = .058) with the NIG (M = 78.07, SD = 11.26) outperforming the CAUG (t(87) = 4.35, p < .001) and FCG (t(84) = 2.48, p = .014). The Group x Trial type interaction (F(2, 127) = .383, p = .683) failed to reach significance.

Congruency reaction time

Congruency effects were analyzed by comparing reaction times on correct congruent to correct incongruent trials. Intent-to-treat results revealed a main effect of Trial type (F(1, 88) = 70.096, p < 0.001, η2 = .071) with quicker responses on congruent trials (M = 376.88, SD = 57.75) as compared to incongruent trials (M = 413.58, SD = 75.27). Additionally, the main effect of Group (F(1, 88) = 10.82, p = 0.001, η2 = .092) revealed differences in RTs across the groups. Follow‐up analysis revealed that collapsed across trial type the CAUG (M = 415.31, SD = 60.57) had slower RTs than the FCG (M = 373.28, SD = 60.51). The Group x Trial type interaction (F(1, 88) = .34, p = .559) did not reach significance.

The comparison with the NIG revealed a main effect for Trial type (F(1, 132) = 168.068, p < 0.001, η2 = .106) and a main effect for Group (F(2, 132) = 5.38, p = 0.006, η2 = .060). There was a significant interaction between Trial type and Group (F(2, 132) = 6.39, p = 0.002, η2 = .008). Follow-up t-tests for Trial type and Group interaction revealed that the CAUG (M = 398.20, SD = 55.64) had slower RTs than the NIG (M = 362.51, SD = 51.13, t(90) = 3.19, p = .002) groups on congruent trials but not on incongruent trials (p = .648). The FCG and NIG groups did not differ in their overall reaction times on congruent trials (p = .415), but FCG (M = 392.97, SD = 75.23) had faster RTs than the NIG group (M = 425.46, SD = 75.00, t(86) = 2.02, p = .046) on incongruent trials.

Accuracy reaction time

Differences in reaction time were also examined for correct and incorrect responses. Intent-to-treat results revealed a main effect of Trial type (F(1, 87) = 233.81, p < .001, η2 = .207) with quicker responses on incorrect trials (M = 325.80, SD = 64.55) compared with correct trials (M = 389.54, SD = 60.67) and a main effect of Group (F(1, 87) = 6.38, p = 0.013, η2 = .049) with the FCG (M = 342.02, SD = 59.41) responded faster than the CAUG (M = 372.96, SD = 55.92). The Group and Trial type interaction was not significant (p = .175).

The comparison with community controls revealed a main effect of Trial type (F(1, 130) = 453.37, p < .001, η2 = .254) and a main effect of Group (F(2, 130) = 4.225, p = 0.017, η2 = .041). These main effects were qualified by a Group and Trial type interaction (F(2, 130) = 4.267, p = 0.016, η2 = .005). Post hoc comparisons revealed that the CAUG (M = 338.27, SD = 63.23) was significantly slower to respond than the NIG (M = 304.43, SD = 48.60) on incorrect trials (t(87) = 2.82, p = .006) and marginally significantly slower on correct trials (MCAUG = 407.65, MNIG = 385.88, t(87) = 1.80, p = .075). The FCG did not significantly differ in response speed from the NIG on either correct (p = .235) or incorrect trials (p = .479).

Timing effects

We next explored the effect of the timing of foster care placement on brain activity within the FCG. We performed Pearson's correlations between the age at foster care placement and brain activity as measured by ERN and theta power. The analyses revealed no significant correlations between age at foster care placement and ERN (r(43) = 0.099, p = 0.529) or theta power (r(43) = −0.157, p = 0.314). To further probe whether there is a timing threshold linked to foster care placement, we adopted an approach described in previous BEIP studies (Vanderwert et al., 2010, Nelson et al., 2007). We split the FCG group into those placed into foster care before 24 months (N = 21) and those after 24 months (N = 22) of age and performed independent samples t-test to assess the group differences. There was no significant difference between the FCG children placed into foster care before and after 24 months of age (FCG <24 M, FCG >24 M) for either ERN (t(41) = −1.573, p = 0.123) or theta power (t(41) = .846, p = 0.402).

Mental health

Internalizing behaviors

The ΔERN x Group and Theta-power x Group moderations examining internalizing behaviors failed to reach significance (ps > .05).

Externalizing-ADHD behaviors

Moderation analyses revealed that Group status (CAUG, FCG, NIG) significantly moderated the relations between the magnitude of the ΔERN (β = −0.144, t(66) = −2.201, p = .031) and Externalizing-ADHD Behaviors (see Fig. 5). Follow-up analyses revealed that a smaller (less negative) ΔERN magnitude was significantly related to higher Externalizing-ADHD Behaviors in the CAUG (β = .143, t(21) = 2.837, p = .009). Moderation analyses of Group × ERN (A) and Group x Theta (B) power for externalizing-ADHD behaviors as measured by the HBQ.

Moderation analyses revealed that Group status (CAUG, FCG, NIG) significantly moderated the relations between the magnitude of the Theta Power (β = −0.134, t(67) = 2.073, p = .042) and Externalizing-ADHD Behaviors (see Fig. 5). Follow-up analyses revealed that lower theta power was significantly related to higher Externalizing-ADHD Behaviors in the CAUG (β = −0.152, t(22) = −2.643, p = .015), but that theta and Externalizing-ADHD Behaviors were unrelated in the FCG (β = −0.059, t(23) = −1.482, p = .152) and NIG (β = −0.018, t(22) = −0.573, p = .572). Together, these moderation analyses suggest that reductions in error-related activity (ΔERN and Theta activity) may serve as a predictor of Externalizing-ADHD behaviors for children who experience early and prolonged psychosocial deprivation.

Discussion

In the current study, we examined the effects of early institutional care as well as a foster care intervention on the development of error monitoring and the emergence of psychopathology in adolescence. Our data suggest that 16-year-old adolescents who as young children were randomized to foster care (FCG) exhibited enhanced response accuracy and processing speed on a modified Flanker task when compared to those who experienced care as usual (CAUG). Furthermore, the care as usual group (CAUG) showed deficits in brain activity associated with error monitoring as exhibited by reduced ERN amplitude and mediofrontal theta power. The foster care group’s (FCG) evoked ERN and theta power were comparable with their never institutionalized peers, whereas the CAUG showed a reduction in ERN amplitude and theta power compared to both the FCG and NIG. Moreover, we found that prolonged institutional care was negatively associated with error monitoring, such that longer institutional care was associated with diminished mid-frontal theta power. The neural markers of error monitoring moderated relations between early psychosocial deprivation and externalizing behavioral problems. Altogether, the present findings suggest that deficits in error monitoring are associated with externalizing behavior problems, and continued high‐quality foster care might mitigate the externalizing problems in previously institutionalized children.

The current findings demonstrate that children who experienced prolonged psychosocial deprivation exhibit impaired ERN in adolescence. This is consistent with the previous work showing that exposure to prolonged early adversity and neglect lead to lasting impairment in neural processing of errors (Banica et al., 2022, Frenkel et al., 2020, Letkiewicz et al., 2023, Loman et al., 2013, McDermott et al., 2013, Troller-Renfree et al., 2016). Loman et al. (2013) examined the development of ERN in orphaned or abandoned children and found that children with an extensive history of institutionalization exhibited a reduced ERN even after adoption. In a recent study with community samples of young adults, Letkiewicz et al. (2023) reported that childhood sexual trauma and emotional neglect were associated with a blunted or reduced ERN amplitude. In the current study, the atypical pattern of ERN activity of the CAUG might reflect continued deficits in the development of the brain circuits involved in error monitoring. Moreover, the current findings on the effect of early foster care intervention on error monitoring converge with the findings reported in the 12-year assessment (Troller-Renfree et al., 2016). At age 12, the FCG exhibited ERN amplitude and response speed comparable with the NIG. The FCG and NIG displayed comparable brain and behavioral responses related to error monitoring in the current 16-year assessment. The current findings suggest that early intervention can improve and normalize the perturbed error monitoring of individuals who experienced severe early psychosocial deprivation.

While there are several ERN studies with institutionalized children, there is limited research investigating oscillatory activity in previously institutionalized children. One previous study from the same sample found that prolonged institutional rearing was associated with a reduction of mid-frontal theta power in a go/no-go task in adolescents indicating diminished cognitive control (Buzzell et al., 2020). However, no previous work investigated the relations between mid-frontal theta power and the development of error monitoring in previously institutionalized children. Theta power in the mid-frontal region increases substantially in response to the occurrences of error signaling the need to monitor the response (Cavanagh et al., 2009). In this study, adolescents who spent their early lives in institutional care but were subsequently placed into foster care (FCG) exhibited enhanced error-related mid-frontal theta power compared to those who experienced prolonged institutional rearing (CAUG). While the increased error-related mid-frontal theta power in FCG was comparable with their never institutionalized peers (NIG), the theta power in CAUG was significantly reduced than the FCG and NIG. Mid-frontal theta modulation reflects a central mechanism that responds to novelty, conflict, punishment, and error, each of which indicates a need for enhanced control processes to change behavior adaptively (Caravaglios et al., 2010). Neglect and other childhood adversities seem to disrupt the maturation of the neural circuits involved in mid-frontal theta oscillations and removal from such situations impacts the development of mid-frontal theta (Buzzell et al., 2020, Meyers et al., 2019). In this study, the diminished theta power for the error responses in the CAUG might be the result of deficits in neuronal systems involved in error monitoring. On the other hand, the comparable theta power between the FCG and NIG indicates the continued benefits of early foster care intervention in the development of error monitoring skills. We computed ERN and theta power on both congruent and incongruent trials. Collapsing across congruent and incongruent trials increased the trial counts and was consistent with the previous 8- and 12-year assessments (McDermott et al., 2013, Troller-Renfree et al., 2016). However, it might be a potential limitation of our analyses since errors are more common on incongruent trials and not restricting the analyses only to error trials might be marred by stimulus congruency.

Previous studies have consistently reported the adverse effects of prolonged institutional care on behavioral, socio‐emotional, physical and cognitive development (Almas et al., 2016, Beckett et al., 2006; Tottenham et al., 2010). Tottenham et al. (2010) examined the impact of prolonged institutional rearing on the neurobiological development associated with socio-emotional behaviors and found that late adoption was associated with larger amygdala volumes, poorer emotion regulation, and increased anxiety. In the current study, we examined the effect of the amount of time spent in the institution on neural activity associated with error monitoring. Our result showed a clear association between the amount of time spent in the institution and theta power such that longer institutional rearing was associated with the reduction of theta power. However, the association between time spent in the institution and ERN was only marginal. While our results do not indicate a causal connection between prolonged institutional care and the development of error monitoring, they do illustrate the benefits of earlier removal of children from institutional care.

The relations between ERN as a neural index of error monitoring and socio-emotional behavioral problems are well established. This study extends that literature and shows the moderating effect of the mid-frontal theta on externalizing-ADHD behavior problems. In this study, ERN moderated externalizing-ADHD behaviors for adolescents who experienced prolonged institutional care (CAUG). Like ERN, the mid-frontal theta also moderated externalizing-ADHD in CAUG. Specifically, ERN and mid-frontal theta were not influential for behavioral problems in the FCG and NIG but they predicted externalizing-ADHD behaviors in CAUG. Consistent with previous findings from this sample, ERN did not moderate the internalizing behaviors (McDermott et al., 2013, Troller-Renfree et al., 2016). Like ERN, the mid-frontal theta also did not moderate internalizing behavior problems. The lack of relations between internalizing behaviors and ERN or mid-frontal theta might be due to the low incidence of clinical anxiety disorders in the present sample (Humphreys et al., 2015).

Conclusions

In the current study, we demonstrate the long‐term impacts of institutional care and early foster care intervention on the development of error monitoring. Early psychosocial deprivation disrupts the development of error monitoring with effects lasting through adolescents. On the other hand, early foster care intervention may remediate the detrimental impacts of psychosocial deprivation on the development of error or response monitoring skills. Moreover, the results suggest that longer institutional care is associated with more deleterious effects. Additionally, deficits in error monitoring illustrate a possible pathway to the development of externalizing and ADHD behaviors in adolescents who remained in the institution and experienced persistent psychosocial deprivation in childhood. Together present findings suggest that early foster care intervention can yield long‐term improvements in neural activity involved in the development of error monitoring and mitigate the symptoms of psychopathology for children living in institutions.
