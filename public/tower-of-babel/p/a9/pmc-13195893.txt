Canadian healthcare provider perspectives and practices in the provision of sexual health support for people with chronic kidney disease: a mixed methods study
Authors: Shannan Love, Sabrina Jassemi, Maoliosa Donald, Dwight Sparkes, Sandra Dumanski, Clare McKeaveney, Heather Beanlands, Janine Farragher, Lori Harwood, Joanne Kappel, Gillian Crysdale, Kaitlin Ahrenholz, Laurinda Ferreira, Maria Delgado, Nancy Verdin, Betty Pearson, Brenda R. ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople with non-dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease (CKD) have identified sexual health as an important aspect of kidney care and a research priority, yet limited information is available on how sexual health is provided within multidisciplinary clinics. We aimed to explore the perceptions, needs, and practices of healthcare providers in Canadian multidisciplinary CKD clinics related to sexual health support for individuals with non-dialysis-dependent CKD.MethodsWe used a convergent mixed methods design involving a self-administered online survey and semi-structured interviews to collect information from healthcare providers working in CKD clinics. The survey included questions related to providers’ understanding of sexual health, current practices, and perceived gaps in how sexual health is addressed in routine CKD care, and was analyzed using descriptive statistics. We undertook follow-up interviews with a subset of survey respondents to elaborate on perspectives about sexual health support, which we analyzed using inductive, content analysis.ResultsThirty-three nurses, 23 nephrologists, 9 allied health professionals, and 8 administrators completed the survey between September and December 2023. Five survey respondents participated in an interview. Quantitative and qualitative results were integrated and summarized in four themes describing current perceptions and practices in the provision of sexual health support for patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD: (1) Mismatch between perceived value and ability to respond; (2) Roles and processes for integrating sexual health into CKD care; (3) Insufficient knowledge and competence in sexual health provider practices; (4) Influence of patient and provider characteristics in sexual health discussions. Key barriers to integrating sexual health into routine care were lack of knowledge, experience, and training; insufficient time and competing priorities; lack of resources; need for clarity in scope of practice; and concern for patient discomfort. Providers suggested access to credible, appropriate resources and clear expectations and guidelines around sexual health care provision for providers working in CKD clinics and other specialty areas (e.g., primary care) to support sexual health care for those with non-dialysis-dependent CKD.ConclusionsWe identified variability in care practices among healthcare providers from multidisciplinary CKD clinics. Identified barriers will inform strategies to enhance sexual health supports for individuals with non-dialysis-dependent CKD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04961-7.

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects more than 13% of individuals worldwide and has been associated with high symptom burden and adverse health outcomes [1, 2]. One important health outcome that is not well understood is sexual health. Sexual health is defined as physical, emotional, mental, and social well-being related to sexuality [3] and can be negatively impacted by symptoms associated with chronic conditions such as CKD. Impaired sexual health in CKD can be related to both physiologic (e.g., CKD-associated reproductive hormone dysregulation, gonadal dysfunction) and/or sociocultural factors (e.g., body image, intimate partner challenges, medicalization of intimate spaces) and may manifest as sexual dysfunction and/or reduced sexual activity and satisfaction [4–7]. Not only does sexual dysfunction contribute to high rates of depression and anxiety among people with CKD, but the relationship is bidirectional such that depression and anxiety can also increase their risk of developing sexual dysfunction [8]. People with CKD, healthcare providers, and policy makers have identified sexual health as a priority area for health research, however this has not yet translated to clinical care [9, 10].

Limited information exists about sexual health issues specific to individuals with non-dialysis-dependent CKD and how best to manage these issues in CKD care. Most studies have explored sexual health needs of people with kidney replacement therapy (i.e., dialysis, transplant) [4] and studies specific to people with non-dialysis-dependent CKD tend to focus more on reproductive needs [6, 11, 12]. Research in those not receiving dialysis has focused on challenges in managing sexual health, such as patient and provider comfort, limited time and access to resources. Little is known about how healthcare providers in Canada perceive and currently integrate sexual health discussions and management into current CKD clinic practices [9, 13–15].

Given the high burden of non-dialysis-dependent CKD worldwide and increasing prevalence of sexual health concerns as kidney function declines [8, 16], we sought to address this research gap by building on existing work from our research team on optimizing strategies for self-management in CKD. As part of the Can-SOLVE CKD Network [17], a pan-Canadian group of researchers, patients, and clinicians working to transform care for individuals affected by CKD, this research has focused on enhancing an evidence-informed self-management tool, ‘My Kidneys My Health’ (MKMH) [18] to be more representative of the needs of diverse patient groups, including those with sexual health concerns. Enhanced understanding of how sexual health is currently addressed will inform integrated CKD care strategies for people with CKD and healthcare providers as well as adaptations to the MKMH resource. The objective of this study was to explore multidisciplinary healthcare provider perceptions and practices related to the provision of sexual health support for individuals with non-dialysis-dependent CKD.

Methods

Study design

We used a sequential explanatory mixed methods design to understand how sexual health support is perceived and delivered in multidisciplinary CKD clinics across Canada [19]. Our use of quantitative survey methods followed by qualitative interviews allowed for methodological triangulation, whereby complementary information is gained that would otherwise be incompletely captured by each method alone. The survey and semi-structured interview guide were developed specifically for this study and have not been previously published. English-language versions are provided as Additional Files 1 and 2. We collected quantitative data from healthcare providers working in CKD clinics through an online survey, followed by semi-structured interviews with a subset of survey respondents to provide further insights. We triangulated and integrated survey and interview data in the interpretation phase to elaborate on complementary findings. We followed the Mixed Methods Article Reporting Standards (MMARS) and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) to report results [20, 21]. Ethics approval was granted by the University of Calgary, Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB23-0272).

Sample and recruitment

Eligible participants included healthcare providers working in a Canadian CKD clinic. We used purposive and snowball sampling to recruit participants through the CKD Clinic Network, a voluntary, pan-Canadian organization of multidisciplinary CKD team members (e.g., nurses, nephrologists, allied health professionals [e.g., social worker, dietician, occupational therapist, pharmacist], administrators) who care for individuals with advanced, non-dialysis-dependent CKD [22]. A network coordinator emailed study information to members from approximately 70 CKD clinics across Canada. Members were asked to share the survey link with other team members who might be interested in participating. Healthcare providers whose scope of practice did not include non-dialysis-dependent CKD were ineligible. Eligibility was determined based on self-reported screening questions at the beginning of the survey. All respondents were asked to indicate their interest in completing a follow-up interview once they reached the end of the survey. Consent to participate was outlined on the survey instruction page and implied by advancing through survey questions. Oral consent was obtained from interested survey respondents before proceeding with an interview.

Data collection

Quantitative - online survey

We conducted an online survey between September and December 2023 using the Qualtrics survey platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT) [23]. Questions were developed through a literature review [9, 13, 14] and in consultation with healthcare providers from multidisciplinary CKD clinics, experts in the field of sexual and reproductive health in CKD (i.e., nephrologist, chartered psychologist), and people with lived experience of CKD. The survey included questions related to: (1) provider and clinic-level characteristics; (2) provider understanding of sexual health and current treatments; (3) current care structures and practices; (4) provider comfort and preparedness; (5) perceived barriers and enablers [see Additional file 1]. Questions were in a variety of formats including fixed choice, Likert scale, ranking (of barriers and enablers), and open-ended questions. We used the Capability Opportunity Motivation Behaviour (COM-B) behaviour change theory in survey development by focusing on individual factors influencing sexual health conversations between individuals with CKD and their healthcare providers [24]. The survey was developed in English and translated into French using Can-SOLVE CKD translation services. All translations were edited by a second translator to verify accuracy. The survey was assessed for content and face validity through pilot testing in both English and French by researchers with experience in the field (JF, LH, SD).

Qualitative - semi-structured interviews

An interview guide was developed and reviewed by patient partners on the research team with experience of kidney disease (BP, MD) and researchers with qualitative and clinical expertise (SD, MJE) [see Additional file 2]. Interview questions expanded on aggregated survey results and did not reference individual participant responses. A member of the research team with experience in qualitative interviewing (SL) completed interviews virtually with interested healthcare providers. Interviews, ranging in length from 29 to 50 min, were recorded using a virtual platform (i.e., Zoom™), then transcribed verbatim. A team member (GC) verified transcripts with the audio recording, which were then uploaded to NVivo 14 to assist with data management [25].

Data analysis

Quantitative

Healthcare provider and CKD clinic characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics and presented in tabular form. Survey data were exported from the Qualtrics™ server and analyzed using descriptive statistics using SPSS 29 [26]. Average and frequency scores were calculated for Likert scale and fixed choice survey questions, respectively. Response patterns based on participant demographics were also calculated to explore trends. Individual rankings for the top barriers and enablers were determined through reverse scoring, with items ranked highest receiving a score of 13, those ranked lowest receiving a score of 1, and those not ranked scored as 0. The aggregate priority score was calculated for each barrier and enabler, which were then ranked.

Qualitative

We used conventional content analysis involving systematic, line-by-line coding and category generation to provide a descriptive account of the participants’ views [27]. Analysis was inductive in that codes were derived directly from the data. The analysis was completed by three clinicians with experience in qualitative research (MJE, MD, SL) and a trainee (GC). Team members regularly discussed how their clinical and research backgrounds potentially influenced interpretation of the qualitative findings. An initial transcript was coded by all team members to generate a list of preliminary codes. Two team members (SL, GC) applied codes to the second transcript, resulting in revisions to the codes. The remaining transcripts were then coded in duplicate using the revised codes, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Open-ended survey responses were coded using a similar method. Team members grouped related codes into subcategories and categories, then into overarching themes. Finally, themes were mapped deductively to behaviour change theory using the COM-B model [24] to supplement survey findings on barriers and enablers to integrating sexual health into CKD care.

Data integration

Qualitative and quantitative data were collected concurrently, analyzed separately, and integrated using the COM-B. Interim analysis of survey responses helped to inform development of interview questions, with interviews being used to elaborate on survey findings. In the analysis phase, team members (MJE, MD, SJ, SL, GC) compared and contrasted barriers and enablers across survey and interview responses in an additional integrative step.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 116 survey respondents initiated the survey, with 73 respondents from 10 unique provinces completing or partially completing survey questions (Table 1). Partial responders completed some survey questions beyond the demographic questions and nonresponders were healthcare providers whose primary work setting was solely dialysis or transplant (n = 30) or who did not answer survey questions beyond the demographics (n = 13). Of respondents included in the analysis, almost half were nurses (n = 33, 45.2%), one third were nephrologists (n = 23, 31.5%) and 12% were allied health professionals (n = 9, 12.3%). Most reported working as part of a multidisciplinary CKD team (n = 62, 84.9%), and many also provided care to patients in other kidney disease settings (e.g., dialysis, general nephrology) (Table 1).

Theme 1: Mismatch between perceived value and ability to respond

Survey and interview participants considered sexual health as a component of holistic CKD care, yet rarely discussed the topic unless initiated by the patient. Survey respondents indicated sexual health was ‘essential to comprehensive CKD care’ and should be a ‘priority within routine kidney care’ as reported in mean agreement scores of 3.89 and 3.50 on a 5-point Likert scale for these statements, respectively. Administrators rated these statements higher (4.8, 4.4) than nephrologists (3.4, 2.8) and allied health professionals (4.0, 3.7), indicating higher perceived value and priority. One interview participant shared:I think anything that is going to impact an individual’s quality of life is a massive priority and this [sexual health] is one that we probably don’t truly understand the extent of how important it is. (HCP5, nephrologist)

I think anything that is going to impact an individual’s quality of life is a massive priority and this [sexual health] is one that we probably don’t truly understand the extent of how important it is. (HCP5, nephrologist)

Despite the perceived value of sexual health discussions, most survey respondents across clinical roles reported either never (14%) or rarely (17%) having sexual health conversations with their patients during clinic visits. They seldom initiated sexual health conversations due to a reported lack of time (60.3%) and competing priorities (45.2%) and relied instead on the patient to raise concerns. Despite this, respondents estimated that most patients do not report concerns related to their sexual health, which was echoed in interview responses:In the beginning [of asking about sexual health during clinic intake], we had a few questions [raised by patients]. We rarely get them now. (HCP1, social worker)

Interview participants shared that competing concerns (e.g., urgent symptoms) took priority during clinic visits or that caseload demands (e.g., volume of patients) resulted in less time available to discuss sexual health. Some providers questioned whether other medical concerns may take priority for patients, as “they’ve got so many other things that they are dealing with” (HCP1, social worker).

Theme 2: Roles and processes for integrating sexual health into CKD care

When sexual health was discussed, respondents reported that the nephrologist (52.4%), nurse (38.1%), or social worker (28.6%) was most likely to be involved in the conversation. Almost one quarter of respondents reported that no kidney care team member was currently involved in these discussions (19%) or they were unsure about who was involved (17.5%) (Fig. 1). This was reinforced in interviews, with one healthcare provider sharing, “We used to have a social worker working with us who was very keen to talk to patients about their sexual health” (HCP2, nurse).

Some healthcare providers questioned whether CKD clinics were the most appropriate setting for sexual health discussions, with one third of respondents indicating that they advised patients to discuss sexual health with a provider outside of the CKD clinic (35%), indicating it was not part of their role (16%) or professional scope of practice (61%). Almost half of respondents suggested designating responsibility for addressing sexual health to a specific team member (43.1%) or referring to primary care physicians, other specialists, or healthcare providers with more experience in managing sexual health issues (Fig. 1). Some interview participants suggested that primary care physicians may be best suited for these discussions given that they “do a lot of counselling, learn about [sexual health], and are comfortable prescribing certain medications” (HCP5, nephrologist).

Most interview participants shared that they “don’t have a systematic way to ask the question [about sexual health]” (HCP4, nephrologist) in their clinic and suggested that it would be helpful to have a consistent process for identifying concerns, such as a checklist or set of questions. Three quarters of survey respondents (74.1%) identified embedded processes as a potential enabler to systematically identifying and addressing sexual health concerns in routine CKD care.

Theme 3: Insufficient knowledge and competence in sexual health provider practices

Participants acknowledged the potential influence of CKD on a person’s sexual health but reported an overall lack of knowledge about sexual health symptoms, treatments, and access to credible resources. On a 10-point Likert scale, survey respondents reported low overall knowledge about potential impact of CKD (mean = 4.6) and its treatments (mean = 4.69) on sexual health, management options (mean = 3.59), resources (mean = 2.77), and clinic protocols/guidelines (mean = 2.46) to guide care. Three quarters of respondents defined sexual health as physical symptoms related to sexual function (79.5%), with less than half defining sexual health as related to emotional (49.3%) and social factors (42.5%). Their definition of sexual health tended to align with the topics they discussed during clinic visits, with many reporting more willingness to discuss physical aspects of sexual health compared with emotional or social aspects, such as body image or gender identity.

Most respondents had not received any formal training related to sexual health and instead independently developed skills in this area. Allied health professionals reported the highest rates of dedicated sexual health training (44%) yet had the lowest overall average perceived level of knowledge score (2.69/10) compared to nurses (3.40/10) and nephrologists (4.3/10). One healthcare provider shared that “[nephrologists] have not been trained, and it’s not something that we have a lot of evidence base for” (HCP4, nephrologist). Interestingly, social workers had the highest perceived knowledge scores of all participating disciplines (4.73/10) when their scores were analyzed distinctly from other allied health professions.

Survey respondents reported not using any sexual health resources in their clinic (76%) or lacking credible resources to share with patients, especially for culturally, linguistically, and gender diverse patients (52.5%). Some participants identified specific sexual health resources (e.g., handouts, websites, videos) they developed or adapted to use with patients in their clinical practice.

Theme 4: Influence of patient and provider characteristics on sexual health discussions

Survey respondents indicated how patient and provider characteristics may impact their perceived comfort in having conversations about sexual health. Despite not often discussing sexual health, almost half of respondents reported feeling comfortable talking about the topic (44%), although some expressed concern about offending their patients (25%) or making them uncomfortable by introducing sexual health during a clinic visit (37%). In interviews, age and gender differences between patients and providers were seen as a barrier to sexual health discussions, which contrasts with survey results suggesting age (17%), sexual orientation (12%), and gender (10%) differences were infrequent barriers. As a female nurse shared during an interview, “I think with women I’m more comfortable than with men” (HCP2, nurse), while a social worker reported that “some older women were very unhappy with this question [about sexual health]” (HCP1, social worker).

Limited access to translated and culturally sensitive materials impacted providers’ perceived ability to provide sexual heath support to patients in settings with “a huge mixture of cultures” (HCP4). Approximately two thirds of respondents did not think that interacting with patients of different genders (59.3%) or sexual orientations (62.7%) to their own negatively impacted their comfort discussing sexual health. Discussion

This study’s findings suggest that healthcare providers working in multidisciplinary CKD clinics perceive sexual health to be important to a person’s overall health, yet they have limited opportunities to discuss and address sexual health issues with their patients in practice. Low perceived knowledge, skills, confidence, and access to credible resources made addressing sexual health concerns challenging in CKD care. A systematic approach for identifying and managing sexual health issues and defining scope of sexual health practice both within and outside of CKD clinic settings were considered essential for its integration into routine care. Providers stressed the importance of considering patient characteristics such as culture, language, gender, and age when approaching sexual health concerns.

Limited knowledge related to sexual health and the need for formal education and training in this area were top barriers to providing sexual health care in the CKD clinic setting. These findings reinforce those identified by McKeaveney and Dumanski (2024), who reported that effective management of sexual dysfunction in individuals with kidney failure requires a multipronged approach that hinges on education of kidney health professionals [28]. However, as reported in our study and others, kidney care providers often lack formal training and experience related to sexual health care provision [9, 10, 13, 14, 29]. In one study, social workers working in CKD care who reported feeling more knowledgeable about sexual health, were more likely to inquire about the topic in their clinical practice and refer patients for sexual counselling [13]. Similarly, in our study, healthcare providers indicated they were more likely to discuss topics that were aligned with their definition of sexual health (i.e., physical symptoms) and about which they felt more knowledgeable. Having a consistent definition of sexual health remains challenging given discrepancies between established classification systems [30, 31], yet efforts to standardize the definition may help to address the large variations in perceived facets of sexual health reported in this study.

Lack of education and training related to sexual health is not unique to kidney care, as reported in a recent survey of oncology care providers in which nearly half of respondents identified lack of training as a main limitation to approaching patients’ sexual health concerns [32]. Participants in our study identified a lack of appropriate and credible sexual health resources specific to patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD as an additional constraint to engaging in sexual health discussions and reported often having to adapt materials from other kidney care settings or disciplines. A need for shared decision-making interventions and care pathways to assist healthcare providers in providing appropriate sexual health care for their patients has been identified in the non-dialysis-dependent CKD context [11, 12]. Further evidence on how the pathophysiology of CKD impacts sexual health is also important for developing novel screening and management resources for application in routine CKD care [6, 33–35].

Study participants recognized the importance of discussing sexual health with people who have CKD but questioned whether this was part of their role or scope of practice. In one scoping review, role confusion, lack of training, provider discomfort, and workflow considerations posed challenges for nurses addressing sexual health concerns with patients receiving hemodialysis [29]. Our study elaborates on these findings by examining healthcare provider perspectives broadly across roles and in the context of multidisciplinary, non-dialysis CKD care. Provider views varied, with some reporting that sexual health should be the responsibility of the nephrologist, the multidisciplinary team, or a care provider outside of kidney care (e.g., primary care). Other studies exploring clinical roles related to sexual health in kidney disease have reported overall positive attitudes among CKD team members (i.e., nurses, nephrologists, social workers) about discussing sexual health with patients, suggesting that this should be a shared responsibility across the multidisciplinary team and not that of a single care provider [9, 14, 29]. Manninen et al. [36] found that primary care physicians seldom discuss sexual issues with patients who have chronic diseases, and Chang et al. [33] reported that patients usually preferred discussing reproduction and fertility concerns with their nephrologist rather than their primary care physician, illustrating gaps and inconsistencies in preferred care responsibility.

Healthcare providers in our study identified lack of formal processes or guidelines as a key challenge to integrating sexual health discussions into CKD clinics. Participants reported feeling that sexual health was not prioritized during clinical encounters due to competing care tasks and workload demands. Having a systematic method of identifying and addressing sexual health concerns with clear guidelines as to who is responsible for these discussions was suggested as an important step to support its integration into routine CKD care. Data suggest that proactive engagement of patients about their sexual health concerns using established screening tools at the point of care can increase the number of patients who receive care and can promote shared decision-making [37]. Strategies for enhancing sexual health care in the CKD context may be adapted from other chronic disease care models, such as through the provision of multidisciplinary sexual health clinics, embedded screening prompts within current care models, or integrated care pathways for referrals to appropriate healthcare providers [12, 38–40].

Our study was strengthened by the inclusion of healthcare provider perspectives from CKD programs across Canada to address an important research priority for people living with CKD [41]. However, we acknowledge some limitations. Although we attempted to recruit from across Canada, we were unable to reach CKD clinics in every region and likely missed some perspectives from certain geographical locations and roles. Additionally, we excluded survey respondents if they reported working exclusively in dialysis or transplant settings, which may have further limited our sample. However, we did include respondents who provided care in multiple settings, including multidisciplinary CKD clinics, which may have influenced their experiences. As the qualitative sample was limited to survey respondents indicating interest in an interview, the small sample and limited heterogeneity likely limited the depth of our qualitative findings and our ability to achieve data saturation. As such, our qualitative findings should be considered as exploratory and identifying areas for future dedicated work. Finally, while this research was designed to capture provider practices and perspectives specific to CKD care delivery in Canada, we anticipate findings will have implications in other kidney care and chronic disease settings.

Conclusion

Results from this study suggest variability in how sexual health is currently integrated into CKD care in Canada. While healthcare providers see value in addressing sexual health related issues with patients, they identified important challenges to systematically identifying concerns, acquiring knowledge and confidence, and integrating clinic-level processes and resources to support this care. Having a clearer picture as to how individuals with non-dialysis-dependent CKD are currently supported in their sexual health will help inform strategies that facilitate its integration into CKD care. Future research is needed to explore patient perspectives and needs related to sexual health, and to develop systematic approaches and resources to support healthcare providers in providing sexual health care to individuals with CKD.
