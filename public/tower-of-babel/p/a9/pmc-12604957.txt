Fer-1 alleviates traumatic brain injury-induced cognitive impairment by inhibiting hippocampal ferroptosis
Authors: Jin-Xia Kuai, Jin-Jing Hao, Zi-jun Meng, Fu-xing Dong, Yi-Yang Chen, Xiao-fang Yang, Zheng Chu, Hai-Jun Bao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The hippocampus plays a pivotal role in the development of cognitive disorders induced by traumatic brain injury (TBI). Recent studies have elucidated the involvement of ferroptosis in the pathophysiological cascade of TBI. However, the specific role of ferroptosis and the therapeutic potential of ferrostatin-1 in mitigating hippocampal damage post-TBI remain unclear. In this study, we established a TBI model and conducted a series of behavioral and histomorphological experiments in mice to assess outcomes. Our assessments included evaluating ferroptosis levels using Perl's staining, assessing neuronal cell death through PI staining, examining mitochondrial shrinkage with transmission electron microscopy, and analyzing the expression levels of key proteins such as glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), transferrin receptor, and SLC40A1 via Western blotting. Our results revealed that TBI led to iron disrupted iron metabolism, mitochondrial atrophy, neuronal cell death, and downregulation of GPX4 expression in the hippocampus. Importantly, administration of Ferrostatin-1 into the lateral ventricle significantly mitigated iron accumulation and neuronal cell death in the hippocampus, thereby attenuating injury and long-term cognitive impairment following TBI. This study highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting hippocampal ferroptosis as a novel treatment approach for TBI.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) remains a significant public health concern and a leading cause of mortality worldwide, affecting tens of millions of individuals each year (Brazinova et al., 2021, Maas et al., 2022, Majdan et al., 2017). The survival of TBI patients following the initial insult largely hinges on secondary injury mechanisms. Previous research, including our own investigations, has illuminated the involvement of autophagy, apoptosis, and necrosis in neuronal cell death and subsequent functional impairment post-TBI (Jia et al., 2009).

Evidence suggests that ferroptosis may be a significant cause of secondary damage in the hippocampal tissue following TBI due to several factors: 1) The hippocampal cells are rich in unsaturated fatty acids, which are important precursors for lipid peroxidation, making them more susceptible to ferroptosis (Bazinet and Layé, 2014, Kim et al., 2014). 2) The depletion of glutathione and the accumulation of lipid peroxides are associated with cognitive impairments caused by TBI (Kenny et al., 2019). 3) The brain consumes more oxygen than other organs and contains metals with high redox activity, such as iron and copper, making it more prone to iron accumulation (Tian et al., 2021). 4) Studies have shown that levels of 15-HpETE-PE and 15LO2 increase while GPX4 levels decrease in the hippocampus in a pediatric rat model of TBI, indicating early ferroptosis (Wenzel et al., 2017).

Ferroptosis has been observed in acute injury models of various organs such as the liver, kidney, and lung, distinguished by its reliance on iron and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Pan et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2022). Notably, Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a specific ferroptosis inhibitor, exhibits protective effects across various disease models including cardiac injury, diabetic nephropathy, Parkinson's disease, and TBI (Li et al., 2020, Yang et al., 2023, Xie et al., 2019). Ferroptosis can be specifically antagonized by compounds like Fer-1 (Sheng et al., 2017).

The hippocampus, a component of the CNS, is responsible for functions such as spatial orientation, learning, memory storage, and conversion. Research has shown that the CA1 region primarily encodes time- and space-related memories, while the CA3 region primarily encodes spatial memories (Bazinet and Layé, 2014, Kim et al., 2014). Therefore, we focus on the hippocampus as the subject of this study and, from behavioral and molecular biology perspectives, administer the ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Fer-1 to TBI mice intraventricularly. The aim is to evaluate whether Fer-1 can alleviate hippocampal neuronal damage and thereby improve cognitive dysfunction in TBI mice.

Methods

Animals

Male adult C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks, weighting 23–28 g, n = 176) were utilized for all experiments in this study, which approved by the laboratory animal centre of Xuzhou Medical University. The mice were acclimatized for at least a week before surgery in a given temperature (22–25°C) and a controlled humidity (50 % relative humidity), and a 12 h dark and light cycle. According to animal ethical requirements, all mice were allowed to access to food and water freely, except for a 12-hour period preceding the surgery. Mice were randomly divided into eight groups: Sham, TBI 6 h, 12 h, 1d, 3d, 7d, 14d, 21d. For the second experiment, mice were randomly assigned to four groups: Sham group, TBI group, TBI+DMSO group, TBI+Fer-1 group.

This study strictly adheres to the relevant regulations of the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments at Xuzhou Medical University. The design of animal experiments involved in this study has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments at Xuzhou Medical University (Ethics License NO:202308T013). The experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and were carried out in accordance with the National Research Council's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Traumatic brain injury model and drug administration

TBI models was established using the modified Feeney’s weight drop injury (WDI) model (Flierl et al., 2009, Tian et al., 2020). The mice were anesthetized initially with 3 % isofluranemaintain, followed by maintenance of anesthesia with 1.5 % isoflurane. Subsequently, they were secured in a stereotaxic apparatus, and their hair was removed using a razor. The scalp was incised longitudinally to reveal the skull, and a 5 mm diameter circular craniotomy was performed at the left side of the midline of the skull, 1.5 mm from the coronal suture and 2.5 mm posterior. The bone flap was gently removed, taking care to avoid damaging the dura mater. The weight dropped in this study was 200 g from a height of 10 cm, inducing a deformation depth of 1.0 mm. Post-surgery, the bone flap was replaced and the incision sutured. Anesthesia was then discontinued, and the mice were placed on pure oxygen for 5–10 min to facilitate rapid recovery. Mice in the Sham group underwent the same procedure as those in the TBI group, but without the weight drop injury.

Before administering the drug, Fer-1 (HY-100579, MCE, USA), was prepared by diluting it in 0.01 % DMSO in saline to achieve a final concentration of 1 mmol/L. Following the WDI, the mice were fixed to a brain stereotaxator, either 3 μL of Fer-1 or a vehicle solution was injected into the right lateral ventricle at a rate of 0.5 μl/min (coordinates: AP −0.5 mm, lateral 1.0 mm, and 2.0 mm beneath the pia mater) 30 min post-injury. After the injection there was a 2-minute pause to prevent secondary injury caused by rapid withdrawal of the needle, which could lead to brain edema. These procedures were informed by protocols established in prior research. All surgical interventions were conducted on a constant temperature heating pad to maintain the mice's body temperature at 37°C.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Mice were perfused with 4 % paraformaldehyde dissolved in Phosphate Buffer Saline (PBS). Subsequently, brain samples from the left hippocampus CA1 region were post-fixed in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered paraformaldehyde (4 %) and glutaraldehyde (2.5 %). The brain samples were then stained with 2 % uranyl acetate (UA), dehydrated in graded acetone solutions, and embedded in epoxide resin. Next, sections (70 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Through this method, three mice were used in each group and the ultrastructural details of the cells were visualized using transmission electron microscopy (Teneo VS, FEI, Czech Republic), each group captured 5 pictures.

PI staining

Mice received intraperitoneal injections of propidium iodide (PI, C0080, Solarbio, China) at a concentration of 0.4 mg/ml (100 μl) one hour prior to euthanasia. Subsequently, the mice were euthanized, and their brain tissues were harvested and immediately cooled to 4°C. These tissues were then rapidly frozen for 30 s at −80°C. Continuous coronal sections of the hippocampus were prepared using a cryostat and stored at −80°C in a light-protected environment. The sections were immersed in anhydrous ethanol for 5 min and counterstained with DAPI for 30 min in preparation for microscopic examination. Three mice were used in each group and three slices from each animal were observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (MG607CCool, ZEISS, Germany). PI-positive cells in both the CA1 region was quantified at 200x magnification using microscopic fields. Cell degeneration counts were performed using NIH Image J software (Bethesda, MD, USA), six slices were selected from each group for statistics.

Perl’s staining

Iron deposition in hippocampal tissues was assessed using Perl's Prussian blue staining (G1428, Solarbio, China), with slight modifications from previously described methods (Wu et al., 2011). Initially, three mice were used in each group and three slices from each animal slices were equilibrated to room temperature (25–30°C). They were then incubated in Perl's solution (5 % potassium ferrocyanide mixed with 5 % hydrochloric acid) for 20 min, followed by three washes in PBS. Counterstaining was performed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine and hematoxylin. Iron-positive cells within the CA1 region of the hippocampus was identified and quantified under a microscope (MG607CCool, ZEISS, Germany) at 200x magnification, six slices were selected from each group for statistics.

Western blotting

The protein samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE on either a 10 % or a 15 % polyacrylamide gel, and the sample amount for each swim lane was 30 μg. Subsequently, the proteins were electrophoretically transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (ISEQ00010, Millipore, USA). The membrane was blocked with 5 % skim milk at room temperature for 2 h and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Following three washes in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 10 min each, the polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for two hours. Detection of the signals was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (P10200, NCM, China). The intensity of the protein bands was quantified using NIH Image J software (Bethesda, MD, USA), with the results normalized to GAPDH levels. The primary antibodies employed in this study included polyclonal rabbit anti-GPX4 (1:1000, Beyotime, China), monoclonal rabbit anti-TFRC (1:1000, Bioss, China), polyclonal rabbit anti-SLC40A1 (1:1000, Proteintech, USA), and mouse anti-GAPDH (1:1000, Proteintech, USA).

Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA) and Glutathione (GSH) levels

In order to determine lipid peroxide levels in different groups,we collected hippocampus from the injury group, and the corresponding area from the sham group, and used commercially available kitsto measure the MDA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, S0131S) and GSH (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, S0053) levels.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in hippocampal tissues were determined using a ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, S0033S) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Behavioral test

For animals undergoing behavioral tests, Fer-1 or vehicle was administered once daily for 7 consecutive days, with the first dose given 30 min after the induction of TBI. The open field test (OFT), novel object recognition test (NOR) and Morris water maze test (MWM) were performed 21 days post-injury as previously (Patel et al., 2019, Gyoneva and Ransohoff, 2015).

For the OFT test, briefly, the open field (40 cm length × 40 cm width) was placed in the middle of the room, free of other noises and unexpected interruptions. And before each trial, the field was cleaned with 75 % alcohol and left to air dry completely. Then the mice were placed in the centre of the field, and recorded the free movement of the mice in 5 min by the camera overhead. The movement distance in centre region and the time spent in centre region were automatically analyzed by an behavioral tracking system (Any-maze, Stoelting, USA).

The NOR test was was performed in an open-field arena (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm) under dim lighting. The experimental procedure spanned three days and consisted of three distinct phases. On day one, mice were individually placed in the empty arena and allowed to freely explore for 10 min to acclimate to the testing environment. Twenty-four hours after habituation, two identical objects were placed symmetrically in the center of the arena. Each mouse was gently placed facing the center of the opposite wall and allowed to explore the two identical objects for 10 min. The exploration was recorded by a ceiling-mounted camera. After a 24-hour inter-trial interval, one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object of a similar size but distinct in shape and texture. The mouse was then returned to the arena and allowed to freely explore for 5 min, and the session was recorded. The primary measure of recognition memory was the Discrimination Index (DI), which was calculated using the following formula: DI = (Time spent exploring the novel object - Time spent exploring the familiar object) / Total exploration time of both objects.

For the MWM test, each mouse was released from one of four directions (east, west, north, south), and allocated 60 s to search for the hidden platform. Each trail ended with the mice has finding the platform. If the mouse failed to find the platform in 60 s, the mouse was guided to the platform and allowed to remain on it for 15 s. After each trail, the mice were placed in a dry cage. In the training phase, each mouse was tested in four trails per day for four consecutive days. On the fifth day, each mouse was tested from one direction to access cognitive function. The movement of each mouse was recorded by camera above the pool. And the results, including latency time, swimming distance and the number of times it crossed the original platform, were calculated by an behavioral tracking system (Any-maze, Stoelting, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data conforming to a normal distribution were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test, and are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis of the data was performed using Prism 9 software. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

TBI induces dynamic hippocampal ferroptosis and neuronal death

To investigate the temporal progression of ferroptosis following TBI, we first assessed key hallmarks of ferroptosis at multiple time points. Perl’s staining revealed a significant and time-dependent accumulation of iron in the hippocampus, commencing as early as 12 h post-TBI, remaining elevated at day 1, and peaking at day 3 (Fig. 1A, C). Concurrently, PI staining showed a progressive loss of neuronal viability in the hippocampal region, initiating at 6 h and extending throughout the 21-day observation period (Fig. 1B, D). At the molecular level, Western blot analysis demonstrated that TBI triggered a dramatic dysregulation of ferroptosis-related proteins. The expression of the iron import protein TFRC (Fig. 2C) was significantly upregulated, while the iron export protein FPN1 (Fig. 2D) and the key antioxidant enzyme GPX4 were markedly downregulated in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 2B). Cognitive function was assessed using the MWM and the NOR test. In the MWM, TBI mice showed significant impairments in spatial learning and memory, as evidenced by prolonged escape latencies during training days (Fig. 5H) and a marked reduction in the number of crossing the platform (Fig. 5I) and the time spent in the target quadrant (Fig. 5J) during the probe trial. Fer-1 treatment significantly improved both learning and memory retention. In the NOR test, TBI mice failed to discriminate between the novel and familiar objects, indicating recognition memory deficits. In contrast, Fer-1 treated mice spent significantly more time exploring the novel object, demonstrating preserved recognition memory (Fig. 5F). The PI staining demonstrated that the cognitive improvement was associated with enhanced neuronal survival in the hippocampus (Fig. 3E, F).

Discussion

This study establishes ferroptosis as a key pathogenic mechanism in TBI, directly linking it to secondary hippocampal damage and cognitive decline. We found that TBI triggers a concerted activation of ferroptosis in the hippocampus, characterized by lipid peroxidation, GSH depletion, and mitochondrial shrinkage. Importantly, administration of Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, mitigated these molecular and ultrastructural disruptions, improving long-term spatial memory and reducing anxiety-like behavior. These findings suggest that ferroptosis inhibition offers translational potential as a therapeutic strategy for TBI.

Ferroptosis, recognized as a novel form of regulated cell death (Dixon et al., 2012), has garnered attention for its involvement in numerous neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases (Li et al., 2017, Yan et al., 2021, Mahoney-Sánchez et al., 2021), as well as acute brain injuries including intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhages, and TBI (Bao et al., 2021). In this experiment, we aimed to use intraventricular injection of Fer-1 to specifically inhibit iron death, further verify the effect of iron death on hippocampal neurons in mice after TBI, and explore its impact on cognitive dysfunction in mice after TBI.

The iron accumulation is the most important signs of ferroptosis. Evidence manifested that TBI patients show a higher level of iron accumulation than normal people, what could be the cause of cognitive disorder (Lu et al., 2015). Previous studies have showed that TfR, which transports iron from outside the cell into the cell, plays an important role in inducing ferroptosis in cells. And knocking down of TfR followed by cell death was reduced (Ma et al., 2016, Hiromatsu et al., 2023). On the contrary, FPN transports iron from inside the cell to outside and inhibits the occurrence of ferroptosis in breast cancer, FPN overexpression resulted in cell death whereas knockdown of FPN increased cell death after siramesine and lapatinib treatment. And in an other study, in an oral orthotopic mouse xenograft model, FPN induction yields a significant decrease in tumor growth (Belvin and Lewis, 2022). Our results showed that changes in intracellular iron levels in hippocampal neuronal cells. Iron accumulation started in 12 h after TBI and continued to accumulate at least until 3 day after TBI. To explore the mechanism of iron accumulation, we chose two proteins involved in iron transport to study, included TfR and FPN. In our study, the western blotting results demonstrated the expression level of TfR was significantly increased at 6 h post-TBI, while FPN expression markedly decreased and did not recover until 3 days after TBI. This might explain the persistent iron accumulation observed within the first 3 days post-TBI.

Other defining features of ferroprosis are lipid ROS accumulation and mitochondrial atrophy (Gao et al., 2019). Numerous studies have underscored the pivotal role of GPX4, which mitigates the production of lipid peroxides, as a critical regulator of ferroptosis (Yang et al., 2014, Imai et al., 2017, Maiorino et al., 2018). Prior investigations have suggested that GPX4 expression decreases following TBI, consequently leading to elevated expression of 15LO2 during the early stages in rat models of TBI (Cao et al., 2016). Our study revealed that the expression level of GPX4 initially diminishing 6 h post-injury, persisting in its decline until 7 days post-injury. Using TEM, we observed mitochondrial atrophy in the hippocampus tissue of the injured side at 3 days post-TBI, providing strong evidence for the occurrence of ferroptosis after TBI. We also observed the presence of cytolysosomes in hippocampal cells under TEM, indicating the potential existence of various types of cell death in hippocampus after TBI. Previous research has also documented the occurrence of other forms of cell death, including pyroptosis, autophagic cell death, necrosis, and apoptosis, in brain injuries (Liu et al., 2016, Jin et al., 2015).

The results showed that compared to the TBI group, the degree of neuronal damage and level of iron accumulation in the Fer-1 intervention group were significantly reduced. We also observed that Fer-1 treatment significantly upregulated GPX4 expression levels compared to the TBI group. TEM hint that after Fer-1 treatment, the number of mitochondria with shrinkage morphology and mitochondrial crest reduction was significantly reduced compared to the TBI group. These findings confirm that Fer-1 can effectively inhibit iron death and alleviate hippocampal neuronal damage after TBI. Since rodents may not be sensitive to certain changes in emotional and cognitive behaviors, we conducted two behavioral experiments to assess the cognitive abilities of mice. The mice exhibited improved performance in the OFT and MWM test, with statistically significant differences observed. These results suggest that Fer-1 can enhance spatial learning, memory ability, and cognitive competence after TBI.

In summary, our study confirmed the occurrence of ferroptosis in hippocampal neuronal cells following TBI, importantly, the intervention of Fer-1 significantly inhibited this process and led to improved cognitive functions. These findings shed light on precise targets for developing treatments for cognitive impairments post-TBI.
