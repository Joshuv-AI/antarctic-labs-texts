Proton shuttling at electrochemical interfaces under alkaline hydrogen evolution
Authors: Naoki Kuroda, Airi Katase, Tomohiro Hayashi, Katsuyoshi Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrochemical water splitting is a promising method for sustainable hydrogen production, with alkaline electrolysis being a mature and cost-effective option for large-scale use. However, the hydrogen evolution reaction proceeds slowly in alkaline media, and its mechanism remains less understood than in acidic conditions. In particular, the role of alkali metal cations in water dissociation is unclear, despite their influence on hydrogen-bond structures of water. To investigate this, surface-sensitive terahertz spectroscopy is required for observing hydrogen-bond dynamics. Here, we show that interfacial water dynamics during hydrogen evolution reaction can be detected using surface-enhanced Raman scattering combined with vibrational analysis in a density-of-states format. Evidence from a KOH/Au interface shows that proton shuttling is facilitated by the constrained translational and rotational motion of water molecules around K⁺ ions. These findings provide molecular-level insights that could guide the design of more efficient alkaline electrolysis systems.Subject terms: SERS, Chemical hydrogen storage, Electrochemistry

Introduction

Green hydrogen is essential for advancing sustainable energy systems. Among various hydrogen production methods, water electrolysis powered by renewable electricity holds substantial potential for green hydrogen production. In particular, alkaline water electrolysis (AWE) is a relatively mature and cost-effective method for large-scale green hydrogen production1. However, the rate of hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) is considerably slow in alkaline solutions2,3; the development of efficient electrocatalysts is crucial for advancing this technology as a feasible alternative to low-cost grey hydrogen production from fossil fuels. Nevertheless, mechanistic aspects of water reduction in the alkaline HER (2H2O + 2e− → H2 + 2OH−) are yet to be settled while the proton reduction in the acidic HER (2H+ + 2e− → H2) is recognized as an ideal model reaction in the field of electrocatalysis. For example, the activity of the alkaline HER is largely influenced by the concentration of alkaline metal cations, suggesting that water-cation interactions would affect the HER activity via modification of hydrogen-bond (HB) structures of water in the vicinity of cations4,5. The effect of ions with distinct charge densities on their surrounding water HB networks is indeed significant in various processes like the ion mobility, the ion selectivity of biological membrane channels, and the formation of electrical double layer (EDL) at an electrochemical interface6–8. In the alkaline HER, a critical and long-standing question is how hydration water of cations behaves as both solvents and reactants in the EDL during the water splitting process.

Surface-selective vibrational spectroscopies are known to be a powerful method to gain molecular-level insights into electrochemical reactions, i.e., the relationship between the microscopic view of reaction processes and the macroscopic reaction kinetics. For example, vibrational sum-frequency generation (VSFG)9,10, surface-enhanced infrared absorption (SEIRA)11, and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS)12 have been widely used to obtain information about local structures of an EDL where electrochemical reactions take place. Indeed, these spectroscopies have provided many insights into reaction intermediates via monitoring of intramolecular vibrations lying in the mid-infrared (MIR) region. For the intramolecular vibrations of liquid water, there exist O-H stretching band (νO-H) at ~3400 cm−1 ( ~ 100 THz) and H-O-H bending band (δH-O-H) at ~1600 cm−1 (48 THz), which are informative for ensemble-averaged local structures of interfacial HB networks13. On the other hand, these vibration modes provide only limited information about dynamic properties of reactant or solvent molecules in the EDL, which are characterized by intermolecular vibrations lying in the far-infrared (FIR) or terahertz (THz) region below 300 cm−1 (9 THz)14,15. For liquid water, there exist the rocking-wagging libration at around 700 cm−1 (21 THz), the twisting libration at around 400 cm−1 (12 THz), the HB stretching (νHB) at 180 cm−1 (5.4 THz), the HB bending (δHB) at 50 cm−1 (1.5 THz), and the Debye-type local relaxation (τloc) at 10 cm−1 (0.3 THz)16. In spectroscopic studies for bulk liquids, such low-frequency vibrations have been measured using FIR absorption, neutron scattering, and low-frequency Raman scattering17–19. However, it is still a challenge for experimental spectroscopies to probe THz responses at buried interfaces like electrochemical interfaces. Among various surface-selective vibrational spectroscopies, SERS is potentially capable of detecting THz vibrations even at such interfaces because of its detection principle based on inelastic light scattering. One serious problem of SERS is that THz vibrational features in SERS spectrum are invariably concealed by a large background continuum generated from plasmonic metal surfaces20–22. Recently, several reports have strongly suggested that SERS background originates from plasmon-enhanced electronic Raman scattering in the conduction band of metal substrate23–28. This allows us to convert a measured power spectrum of SERS into a density-of-states (DOS) format of Raman susceptibility, in which THz-vibrational features are well-uncovered by reducing the Bose-Einstein thermal factor and the radiative Purcell factor (For the details, see the Methods)23,24,29–31.

Here we show THz-responses, i.e., picosecond dynamic behaviour, of interfacial hydration water undergoing the HER become detectable using surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) combined with vibrational analysis in a DOS format23,24. Direct spectral evidence obtained at KOH solution/Au interface reveals that proton shuttling associated with water reduction in alkaline HER is mediated by constrained translational and rotational motions of water molecules around K+. This molecular-level insight opens some perspectives on the optimum design of AWE.

Results

A cyclic voltammogram for a SERS-active Au electrode was measured against the reference of reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) in 0.1 M KOH aqueous solution, as shown in Fig. 1; for anodic responses, a broad feature in the range of ca. +0.4–1.2 V and a small peak with a shoulder at around +1.27 V are assigned to OH− adsorption and formation of surface oxides, respectively32. The steep current increase above +1.8 V corresponds to oxygen evolution reaction (OER). For cathodic responses, a sharp peak at +1.02 V is due to reduction of surface oxides. The desorption of OH− is also seen as a broad feature below the reduction peak of surface oxides. The steep increase of the cathodic current below −0.3 V corresponds to hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). Considering the onset potential for the HER, SERS spectra were measured below +0.1 V vs. RHE. It is worth noting that the Au surface is negatively charged in this potential region because the potential of zero charge (pzc) at the Au/KOH solution interface is located at around +0.96 V vs. RHE29. This means that SERS spectroscopy in this work was conducted in the potential range where K+ rather than OH− tend to enrich in the EDL. (We have previously reported electrochemical SERS spectra in the THz region in the capacitive potential range near the pzc29).

Figure 2a–c shows normal Raman spectra in the DOS format (χ″vRS) for a bulk electrolyte solution containing 0.1 M KOH, obtained in the low (< 1100 cm−1), mid (1400–1800 cm−1), and high (> 2900 cm−1) frequency regions. The spectral appearance was very close to that for pure water in the entire frequency ranges19,30, meaning that Raman signals from bulk water rather than from hydration water are dominant in this low-concentration electrolyte solution. Figure 2d–f shows a series of vibrational SERS spectra in the DOS format (χ″vSERS) taken at an interface between the electrolyte solution and Au electrode under various applied potentials. (For the low-frequency region, high-resolution χ″vSERS spectra with both Stokes and anti-Stokes branches are provided in Suppl. Fig. 1.) To extract the potential-induced changes, difference spectra relative to the spectrum taken at +0.1 V (Δχ″vSERS) are also presented in Fig. 2g–i. All Raman bands of liquid water, including the THz bands, were clearly seen in the χ″vSERS spectra. On the other hand, spectral features such as peak positions, widths, and shapes were different between χ″vSERS and χ″vRS, indicating structural variations of the HB networks in the EDL. (For the intensity ratio of νOH to the HB bands, the difference between SERS and normal Raman is probably not related to the structural differences in the HB network but to the lower plasmonic enhancement in νOH mode due to the vibrational energy dissipation through HB networks of water30.) Because K+ tends to enrich in the EDL in this potential range, the characteristic features in χ″vSERS are attributable to HB networks in the hydration shell of K+; this assignment is indeed confirmed by density functional theory (DFT) calculations and the multivariate curve resolution (MCR) analysis30. As shown in Suppl. Fig. 2, the spectral feature of νO-H in the χ″vSERS for KOH well agrees with that of the MCR-solved pure spectrum for the hydration water of K+; the same applies to the χ″vSERS for LiOH. For the other intermolecular vibration modes, we could not apply the MCR analysis because the simple mixture model may not be valid for explaining the spectral components of such collective vibrations. Nevertheless, it is reasonable to assume that the contribution of the hydration water of K+ to the χ″vSERS is dominant not only for the νO-H but also for the other vibrational features in the K+-enriched EDL.

For the potential dependence of SERS spectra, the observed spectral variations in the high-frequency νO-H and the mid-frequency δH-O-H were similar with previously reported electrochemical SERS behaviour33. On the contrary, distinctive spectral variations were found in the low-frequency libration and νHB; the former exhibited an anomalous sharp peak superimposed on the broad spectral feature when the applied potential was close to the HER onset, and the latter evolved significantly undergoing the HER. It is worth noting that these water-related spectral features were observed only when carbonaceous surface contamination was removed from the Au surface; all these bands emerged after electrochemical surface cleaning, as shown in the Supplementary Fig. 3. To analyse the potential dependence of the χ″vSERS spectra, peak positions and integrated intensities for νHB, δH-O-H, and νO-H were plotted as a function of the applied potentials. (Deconvolution of the libration band was not performed due to the presence of the anomalous sharp peak while the normal Raman libration band of pure water can be fitted with three components34,35, which will be discussed later.) As shown in Fig. 3, the tendency of the spectral variations during the cathodic sweep was different across the HER onset potential, suggesting that the HER significantly perturbs the structure and dynamics of hydration water of K+ in the EDL. First, we summarize the potential-induced changes observed in the MIR region where many spectroscopic studies have already been performed. The νO-H band showed the positive Stark shift with the tuning rate of ca. 115 cm−1 V−1 in the potential region before the HER onset, owing to the change in the electrostatic field at the interface. However, in the potential region beyond the HER onset, the peak was oppositely shifted with broadening of the band width; this can be interpreted as distortion of the hydration shell of K+ caused by the water dissociation. The νO-H intensity showed a steep increase near the HER onset and then rapidly decreased at more negative potentials. This implies that the surface density of water molecules increased during the cathodic sweep owing to the enrichment of the hydration water of K+ in the EDL, and then these water molecules were consumed by the water dissociation. For the δH-O-H band, the negative Stark effect with the tuning rate of ca. −23 cm−1 V−1 was observed before the HER onset. Then it became nearly constant beyond the HER onset because the charge transfer between Au and H2O molecules prevents the increase of the electrostatic field at the interface. The trend of the intensity variation for δHOH was similar with that for νO-H, suggesting the consumption of water molecules by the water dissociation during the HER.

Next, we focus to elucidate the potential-induced changes in the THz region. As mentioned earlier, the spectrum in this region is decomposed into distinct but overlapping δHB, νHB, and libration bands. Given that the intermolecular vibrational DOS (vDOS) of water is resolved into translational and rotational degrees of freedom, the libration band is composed of purely rotational motions of water. On the other hand, δHB and νHB can be expressed as translational motions and combined motions of translation and rotation, respectively8. The gradual redshift of the νHB band during the cathodic sweep indicates the decrease in the HB bond strength of the K+ hydration shell, which is probably due to the potential-induced “H-down” orientation of water molecules in the vicinity of Au surface29. For the νHB intensity, the significant increase was observed when the potential was more negative than the HER onset, which corresponds to the increase in the degree of freedom, i.e., the available volume, for translational and rotational motions of water molecules hydrating K+ in the EDL during the water dissociation. Indeed, this characteristic spectral change during the HER was similarly observed in various control experiments in electrolyte solutions of 0.01 M KOH (pH dependence), 0.1 M LiOH (cation dependence), 0.1 M KOD (isotope effect), and 0.1 M KOH aqueous/methanol solution (organic additive), as shown in Suppl. Figs. 4–9. These results can rule out the possibility that the Raman activity of water itself was changed by the interfacial electric field or double-layer restructuring under the potential scan. Besides, no reaction intermediates such as Au-H nor OH− cation were detected during the experiment. Therefore, this change in the dynamic behaviour of water molecules should be related to compensation for consumed protons at cathode surface during the splitting process.

It is here noted that the band shape of the νO-H in the bulk water can be decomposed into five sub-bands: DAA (single donor-double acceptor), DDAA (double donor-double acceptor), DA (single donor-single acceptor), DDA (double donor-single acceptor), and free OH, as shown in the Suppl. Fig. 1036. In the SERS spectra, the νO-H bands were dominated by the DA sub-band. In other words, the ratio of tetrahedral “ice-like” structure was relatively low in the hydration shell of K+. This is probably because the HB network in the first hydration shell is terminated by the presence of the central K+. To obtain in-depth understanding of the relation between the local HB structure and the dynamic behaviour of water molecules, two-dimensional correlation analysis was performed between the high-frequency νO-H and low-frequency THz vibrations37. Fig. 4 shows synchronous and asynchronous off-diagonal spectra of χ″vSERS between νO-H and THz regions. For the libration band, the sharp feature at 530 cm−1 exhibited a positive correlation with the νO-H band in both the synchronous and asynchronous spectra. On the other hand, a broad component at around 700 cm−1 exhibited a negative synchronous correlation with the DA component of the νO-H band; that is, two spectral components are changing in opposite directions at the same time. In normal Raman spectrum of water, the libration band can be fitted with three components with low, middle, and high frequencies. Although their assignments are still under discussion, the low- and high-frequency libration components are associated to optical phonon-like collective modes and the middle-frequency libration component to single-molecule motions34,35. By considering this, the sharp feature is assignable to local motions of water in the hydration shell of K+, and the broad high-frequency component to the optical phonon-like mode. The νHB at 120 cm−1 also exhibited a strong negative synchronous correlation with the DA component of the νO-H band; as seen in the HER region of Fig. 3a, 3c, the νHB band evolved with decreasing the νO-H intensity. The positive sign of νHB band in the asynchronous correlation with the νO-H peak is due to the Stark shift of the νO-H band at the more positive potential region than the HER onset. In addition to these THz vibrations, it appears to be a correlation between the very low-frequency component at around 10 cm−1 and the high-frequency νO-H. This component is attributable to the Debye-type fast relaxation around local defects of HB network (τloc)16,29. Because of the detection limit below 8 cm−1 in our Raman microscope, the redshift of the τloc leads to the decrease of the apparent intensity. Thus, this correlation can be interpreted as slowing down of the relaxation time in HB networks of hydration shells during the water dissociation, which is in consistent with the redshift of the νHB band and the blue shift of the νO-H band during the HER.

Two-dimensional correlation spectra for the potential-induced changes.

a, Synchronous off-diagonal spectrum of χ″vSERS between THz and νO-H regions. b Asynchronous off-diagonal spectrum of χ″vSERS between THz and νO-H regions.

The potential-induced variation of the THz responses as well as the O-H vibrations for the hydration shell of K+ yields molecular-level insights into the alkaline HER. Figure 5 illustrates dynamic behaviour of water molecules in the hydration shell during the HER. During the cathodic scan, a hydration shell of K+ approaches to the Au surface, and then the average orientation of water molecules is modified to a “H-down” configuration in the vicinity of Au33. Next, the Volmer step in alkaline HER, i.e., water dissociation coupled with H adsorption (H*), leads to formation of OH− in the hydration shell (H2O → H* + OH−). This results in the increase of the available volume for translational motions of HB-bonded neighbouring water molecules in the hydration shell. Because the mobility of these hydration water molecules is restricted by their oxygen atom pointing toward K+, the νHB vibration, consisting of translational and rotational motions of water molecules, is eventually enhanced by the HER. (Because the δHB vibration consists of purely translation motions of water molecules8, enhancement of such a vibration is not expected in the first hydration shell even though protons are consumed on the electrode.) Given that this enhanced motion of water molecules can promote proton hopping among HB-boned water molecules, it is reasonably assumed that continuous alkaline HER, which requires compensation for the lack of protons at the reaction sites, are mediated by this dynamic motion of water in the hydration shell of K+. The increase in the high-frequency libration component, as shown in Figs. 2g and 4a, also supports this assumption; This short-lived optical phonon-like mode propagates along the HB networks. Thus, this collective mode can transport protons to reaction sites when coupled with νHB in the hydration shells. This is indeed consistent with the previously reported kinetic behaviour of alkaline HER4. In other words, the dynamic behaviour of interfacial water molecules, facilitated by proton shuttling within the HB network, was visualized using low-frequency SERS.

Proton shuttling to the reaction site is promoted by translational and rotational motions of HB-bonded water molecules in the hydration shell of K+.

Discussion

In this work, low-frequency SERS spectroscopy uncovered detailed mechanistic insights into the alkaline HER. From the experimental evidence obtained in the wide frequency range covering both THz and MIR regions, we conclude that vibrational motions of HB networks of water hydrating cations accommodate proton transfer in the EDL during the HER in alkaline media. We showed that the intermolecular HB stretching (νHB) at around 140 cm−1 was largely enhanced under the HER, which corresponds to restricted translational motions of water molecules with their oxygen atom pointing toward K+. That is, the role of the hydration shell of cation is to mediate proton shuttling during HER. Moreover, this mode is coupled with the optical phonon-like mode observed in the high-frequency component of the libration so that protons are transported from the bulk solution. Because the nature of νHB is influenced by the degree of cation-water interactions, it is naturally explained that the HER activity in the AWE is cation-dependent. Such dynamic behaviour of interfacial water has never been examined in previous experimental studies because of the lack of information about THz responses of interfacial water in the EDL. Considering that this work was performed only on an inert Au model surface, it is essential to validate the proposed mechanism on non-noble metal catalysts. Our methodology is indeed applicable to such a transition metal catalyst such as Ni, Fe, and Co-P when combined with the so-called intensity borrowing method38,39 or shell-isolated nanoparticle-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SHINERS)40. These transition metal surfaces possess much stronger chemical adsorption capabilities and are rich in hydroxyl groups. Thus, the HB networks in hydration shells of cation will be strongly reshaped under the combined influence of the electric field and surface adsorption, resulting in a further increase in the available volume for proton shuttling. The key issue for higher HER efficiency would be how νHB is strongly coupled with the optical phonon-like libration modes. We believe that a direct measure of interfacial THz responses will shed some light on fundamental and applied studies of electrochemical or biological aqueous systems.

Methods

Water purity

All the experiments in this work were conducted using ultrapure water (resistivity 18.2 MΩ⋅cm and total organic carbon < 5 ppb), which was provided by Milli-Q EQ7000 ultrapure water purification system (Merck).

Sample preparation

SERS-active Au working electrode was prepared on a smooth Au substrate (99.999%, Tanaka precious metals) by drop casting of Au colloidal solution containing Au nanoparticles with a diameter of 100 nm or 50 nm (Tanaka precious metals) (Suppl. Fig. 11); we confirmed that the experimental result was not affected by the size of Au nanoparticles. The obtained electrode was cleaned using hydrogen evolution reaction in aqueous solution with 0.1 M KOH ( > 85% guaranteed grade, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals), immediately followed by SERS measurements. After SERS measurements, the electrochemical surface area of the Au electrode was estimated using the charge required to reduce the surface oxide monolayer formed during cyclic voltammetry in 0.1 M H2SO4 solution.

Electrochemical SERS spectroscopy

SERS spectra for the Au surface were measured in a three-electrode cell with an optical window; a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) and a Pt coil were used as the reference and the counter, respectively (see Suppl. Fig. 11). The calibration of RHE was conducted by measuring HER on Pt working electrode. Luggin capillary was used for the reference electrode to minimize the contribution of the solution resistance; all electrochemical data is presented without iR correction. A potentiostat (HZ-7000, Hokuto Denko) was used to control potentials and record electrochemical data. All measurements were conducted in Argon-purged electrolyte solutions: 0.1 M KOH (99.99% trace metals basis, Aldrich and > 85% guaranteed grade, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals) with and without methanol (99.8%, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals), 0.01 M KOH, and 0.1 M LiOH aqueous solutions and 0.1 M KOD heavy-water solution; LiOH∙H2O (99.995% trace metals basis) and 40 wt. % KOD in D2O were purchased from Aldrich, and D2O (99.90% D) was purchased from Eurisotop. All electrolytes were used as received, and freshly prepared electrolyte solutions were used in the experiments. pH values of the solutions were measured using a pH electrode (LE409, Mettler Toledo). For detection of low-frequency SERS signals, monochromatic He-Ne laser radiation (HNL100L, Thorlabs) with a wavelength of 632.8 nm, obtained using an ultra-narrowband laser line filter (volume Bragg grating; VBG, OptiGrate Corp), was used for excitation; the back-scattered signals, collected with a purpose-built inverted Raman microscope fitted with an objective (40x, 0.6 N.A., Nikon), were measured using a single-stage spectrometer with a CCD detector (IsoPlane & PIXIS 100BR eX-I, Princeton Instruments) after Rayleigh scattering was removed by VBG filters. The bubble formation under the HER is indeed problematic for the spectroscopic observation. However, we can avoid it under microscope observation because macroscopic bubble formation, influenced by the morphology of the electrode surface, occurs at same positions of the sample. Normal Raman spectra for saturated alkali halide aqueous solutions were also taken using the same Raman system. All the measured normal Raman and SERS spectra were divided by the Bose-Einstein thermal factor at 300 K. For normal Raman spectra, this reduced form corresponds to the imaginary part of vibrational Raman susceptibility (χ″vRS) in the vibrational density-of-states (vDOS) format. For SERS spectra, the obtained spectra were further divided by the Purcell factor so that reduced SERS spectrum (χ″SERS) was obtained23,24; the Purcell factor can be obtained by comparison of electronic SERS spectrum for dried SERS-active Au in a vacuum cell with normal electronic Raman spectrum for SERS-inactive smooth Au surface. (The Purcell factor in vacuum may be different from that in aqueous solution because of the difference in refractive index. However, we confirmed that this is negligible by comparing with electronic SERS spectrum for Au in ionic liquids31.) Because χ″SERS encompasses the information on both vDOS for water (χ″vSERS) and a convolution of joint density of electron/hole states (jDOS) for Au (χ″eSERS), χ″vSERS for water was finally obtained by subtraction of χ″eSERS for Au. Each measurement was at least performed twice to confirm the reproducibility.

Computational methods

To evaluate the contribution from hydration shells of K+ and OH− to SERS spectra, two different computational methods were employed. One is vibrational calculations of O-H stretching frequencies for water clusters. In this method, three different water clusters, consisting of 16 H2O molecules surrounding one central H2O, K+, or OH−, i.e., H2O@16H2O, K+@16H2O, and OH−@16H2O, were utilized in the DFT calculations; the M06-2X level of theory was employed with the def2-SVPD basis set in the Gaussian09 program. The optimized structures are provided in Supplementary Data files. The obtained frequencies were scaled by 0.9656.

The other method is the spectral deconvolution of saturated halide solutions using the MCR method. Given that the fraction of hydrated water reaches 100% in many saturated halide solutions, the O-H stretching band for saturated solutions of KCl, KBr, LiCl, and LiBr can express as a mixture of pure spectra for hydration shells of K+, Li+, Cl−, and Br−. The MCR analysis was performed in the frequency range of 2800 – 3800 cm−1 using original code developed on a Python platform. Prior to the analysis, Raman spectra of saturated aqueous solutions of KCl, KBr, LiCl, and LiBr were smoothed using the Savitzky–Golay method with a second-order polynomial and a 101-point window, and then baseline contributions were removed using an iteratively reweighted asymmetric least-squares algorithm based on Whittaker smoothing. In principle, the MCR analysis can decompose signal contributions coming from each ion without information about pure spectrum for each of the components. In this analysis using the limited data set, however, SERS spectrum of KOH was used as the starting estimate for the hydration water of K+. The starting estimate for the hydration water of Cl− was obtained by subtracting the hydration water spectrum of K+ from the KCl solution spectrum, assuming that the spectral areas of the hydration water components are equivalent. Subsequently, the hydration water spectra for Li+ and Br− were derived from the solution spectra of LiCl and KBr, respectively, through a similar process. Finally, a collective MCR analysis was conducted on the four Raman spectra using these initial estimates. During this optimization, the concentrations of ions absent from the specific solution were constrained to zero. The experimental spectra for the saturated solutions were successfully reconstructed with minimal error using the MCR-derived spectra and their concentrations, allowing for the precise determination of the pure spectrum for hydration shell of each individual ion.
