Chromosome-level and haplotype-resolved genome assembly of Dracaena cambodiana (Asparagaceae)
Authors: Bao-Zheng Chen, Da-Wei Li, Wei-Jia Wang, Ya-Xuan Xin, Wei-Bin Wang, Xu-Zhen Li, Ting-Ting Hao, Yang Dong, Wen-Bin Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDracaena cambodiana Pierre ex Gagn. (Asparagaceae) is the source plant of Dragon’s blood and has high ornamental values in gardening. Currently, this species is classified as the second-class state-protected species in the National Key Protected Wild Plants (NKPWP) of China. However, limited genomic data has hindered a more comprehensive scientific understanding of the processes involved in the production of Dragon’s blood and the related conservation genomics research. In this study, we assembled a haplotype-resolved genome of D. cambodiana. The haploid genomes, haplotype A and haplotype B, are 1,015.22 Mb and 1,003.13 Mb in size, respectively. The completeness of haplotype A and haplotype B genomes was 98.60% and 98.20%, respectively, using the “embryophyta_10” dataset. Haplotype A and haplotype B genomes contained 27,361 and 27,066 protein-coding genes, respectively, with nearly all being functionally annotated. These findings provide new insights into the genomic characteristics of D. cambodiana and will offer additional genomic resources for studying the biosynthesis mechanism of Dragon’s blood and the horticultural application of Dragon trees.Subject terms: Conservation genomics, Genome duplication

Background & Summary

Dracaena Vand. ex L., with over 190 species, is the second largest genus of Asparagaceae1 (https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn:lsid:ipni.org:names:30010732-2). Dracaena spp. have a common name as Dragon tree or Dragon’s blood tree2,3, and they are mainly distributed in tropical and subtropical regions of Asia and Africa4. Most Dragon trees have high values in gardening and horticulture, because they are evergreen, long-lived and slow-growing, and can be capable of thriving in extremely dry and arid environments. Given their significant decorative appeal and cost-effective management, Dragon trees have become increasingly popular for both indoor and outdoor ornamental plants. Importantly, some Dragon trees can secrete deep red resins (Fig. 1D), commonly known as Dragon’s blood or Sanguis Draconis, which is widely used as a wash to heal wounds or as a powder to block bleeding2,5. Dragon’s blood is mainly composed of flavonoids, steroids, phenolic compounds, and other bioactive components6–8 that contribute to its anti-inflammatory6, antioxidant3, antibacterial9, and analgesic effects10.

Unfortunately, the massive harvesting of Dragon trees for their horticultural and medicinal values, along with the dramatic habitat degradation resulting from pervasive anthropogenic activities, has severely threatened their populations, pushing them toward the brink of extinction6. For example, seven species, including D. cinnabari Balf.f., D. draco (L.) L., D. caboverdeana (Marrero Rodr. & R.S.Almeida) Rivas Mart., Lousã, J.C.Costa & Maria C.Duarte, D. ajgal (Benabid & Cuzin) Rivas Mart., Molero Mesa, Marfíl & G.Benítez, D. tamaranae Marrero Rodr., R.S.Almeira & M.Gonzáles-Martin, D. ombet Kotschy & Peyr., and D. serrulata Bake, are now classified as endangered according to the Red List of Threatened Species established by International Union for Conversation of Nature (http://www.iucnredlist.org)6. Meanwhile, two important source plants of Sanguis Draconis, D. cambodiana11 (Fig. 1A–D) and D. cochinchinensis2, have also been listed as the second-class state-protected species in the National Key Protected Wild Plants (NKPWP) of China6. In response to this critical situation, the establishment of protective laws and immediate conservation measures are essential. Recent advancements in genomics, particularly in high-quality genome assembly, offer new perspectives for species conservation and understanding the biosynthesis of bioactive compounds. For example, deciphering the genome of Rhododendron griersonianum Balf.f. & Forrest has provided critical insights into its genetic diversity, offering valuable information for conservation genomics studies12. Similarly, the high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly of Camptotheca acuminata Decne. has revealed the functional divergence of the loganic acid O-methyltransferase gene, enhancing our understanding of camptothecin biosynthesis13. Currently, only two reference genomes of Dracaena species, have been reported i.e., D. cochinchinensis at the chromosome level, which consists of 1,898 scaffolds with a contig N50 of 1.1 Mb14, and D. cambodiana at the scaffold level, comprising 2,379,659 scaffolds with a contig N50 of 1.87 kb15. However, the current genome assembly of D. cambodiana is fragmented and limited15, rendering it insufficient for further research. Therefore, we reassembled a high-quality reference genome of D. cambodiana using both long-reads and short-reads data, which will be important for future studies in resource sustainable utilization and conservation biology of D. cambodiana.

In this study, samples of D. cambodiana were collected and processed to achieve a chromosome-level and haplotype-resolved genome assembly by combining PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) sequencing data and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) data. The final assembled genome contains haplotype A (1,015.22 Mb) and haplotype B (1,003.13 Mb), with the contig N50 length of 6.23 Mb and 6.10 Mb, respectively (Table 2). Based on the karyotype (2n = 40)16, 99.97% of the assembled contig sequences were successfully anchored to 40 pseudo-chromosomes (Fig. 1F). The completeness of the genome assemblies for the two haplotypes was evaluated using the “embryophyta_odb10” dataset by Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO, v5.4.7)17. The result revealed that 98.5% of the haplotype A genome assembly and 98.1% of the haplotype B genome assembly were identified as completeness, respectively, indicating the high-quality genome assembly for both two haplotype genomes. The haplotype A and haplotype B genomes were annotated with 713.59 Mb (70.28%) and 701.17 Mb (69.90%) repetitive sequences, respectively. Furthermore, 27,361 protein-coding genes were identified in haplotype A genome, and 27,066 protein-coding genes were found in haplotype B genome. This study represents the first report of a chromosome-level and haplotype-resolved assembly of D. cambodiana genome. We believe that this comprehensive genomic resource will significantly contribute to the understanding of the mechanisms underlying Dragon’s blood formation, its evolutionary history, and conservation genomics, as well as its diverse applications in ornamental horticulture.

Methods

Sample collection and sequencing

To conduct the genome survey, assembly, and protein-coding gene prediction of D. cambodiana, an adult Dragon tree was selected for sampling in the Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden, Chinese Academy of Sciences, which was identified by Dr Wen-Bin Yu (Fig. 1A–D). Young fresh leaves were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, followed by storage at –80 °C for use as sources of genomic DNA extraction. All library preparations and sequencing procedures were conducted by Novogene Bioinformatics Co., Ltd., China (Beijing, China). The specific protocols employed are detailed as follows.

For PacBio long-reads sequencing, genomic DNA was extracted utilizing a modified Cetyltrimethylammonium Bromide (CTAB) method18. Following the assessment of the quality and quantity of the extracted DNA using the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), approximately 10 µg of genomic DNA were sheared into fragments ranging from 10–20 kb using a g-TUBE (Covaris, USA). The fragmented DNA was then purified using the AMPure PB magnetic beads. Subsequently, the HiFi library was prepared using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0, and sequencing was performed on the PacBio Sequel II platform (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, USA). Totally, ~71.03 Gb (~69 × of D. cambodiana genomes) HiFi reads with N50 sizes of 20,179 bp were obtained using the CCS (Circular Consensus Sequencing) software with the standard parameters (https://ccs.how/) (Table 1).

For Illumina sequencing, the genomic DNA was isolated and subjected to quality control following the same method as used for long-read sequencing. A short-read library, with DNA-fragment insert sizes ranging between 200–400 bp, was constructed. This library was then sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, USA) using the PE 150 sequencing model. In total, ~52.96 Gb (~51.92× of the D. cambodiana genome) short reads were sequenced to assist in genome surveying and to correct errors in the genome assembly (Table 1).

For the preparation and sequencing of the Hi-C library, fresh young leaves of D. cambodiana were first fixed using 4% formaldehyde, in accordance with the protocol outlined by Belton, et al.19. The fixed tissues were then homogenized and centrifuged to isolate the nuclei. Following this, the cross-linked chromatin was digested using DpnII and labeled with Biotin, and the subsequent ligation was performed using T4 DNA ligase. The DNA was then purified and examined using agarose gel electrophoresis. Finally, the Hi-C library was constructed according to the above-mentioned Illumina sequencing protocol and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, which yielded 154.66 Gb clean data, accounting for ~151.63× of the genome (Table 1).

For transcriptome sequencing, tissues including leaves, stems and roots were separately sampled. Total RNA was isolated using the standard Trizol protocol (Invitrogen, USA). The library construction was constructed using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA), followed by sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). A total of 25.36 Gb of RNA sequencing reads were generated, which were utilized for the prediction of whole-genome protein-coding genes (Table 1).

Genome assembly

The raw short reads were filtered using FASTP v.0.12.620 with default parameters. After removing the adapters in reads and filtering out low-quality data, the clean reads were then used for the genome size and heterozygosity estimation. First, Jellyfish v.2.2.1021 was employed to statistic the 19 k-mer frequencies (count -F 2 -m 19 -C -s 10 G). And then, GenomeScope v.2.022 with default parameters estimated the haploid genome size at 1,020.07 Mb with a heterozygosity rate of 1.42% (Fig. 1E).

The PacBio HiFi reads were used to generate haplotype-resolved contigs by using Hifiasm v0.18.0-r465 with default parameters23. Then the redundant haplotigs and the rDNA fragments in two haplotypes (haplotype A and haplotype B) were removed by using the Purge_dups pipeline v1.2.524 with default parameters, respectively. As a result, haplotype A assembly contained 1,015.22 Mb in 291 contigs with a contig N50 of 6.23 Mb and haplotype B assembly contained 1,003.00 Mb in 277 contigs with a contig N50 of 6.10 Mb (Table 2). To achieve the chromosome-level assembly, the cleaned Hi-C reads were mapped to the corresponding contigs using Juicer v1.9.925, respectively. After filtering out redundant Hi-C reads, the uniquely mapped reads were taken as input for 3D-DNA pipeline v18011426 with default parameters. Subsequently, the missing assembly and the order of contigs were manually corrected by using JuicerBox v1.11.0827 and reviewed by 3D-DNA pipeline v18011426 with the following parameters “-s finalize --sort-output --build-gapped-map”. In total, 40 pseudochromosomes were obtained, accounting for 99.99% and 99.94% of the assembled contigs for haplotype A and haplotype B, respectively (Fig. 1F; Table 2).

To assess the completeness of the genome assembly, the software BUSCO v5.4.717 (Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs) was employed to blast single-copy genes using the ‘embryophyta_odb10’ dataset with 1,614 conservation sing-copy orthologs as the reference. Analysis revealed that, the completeness of haplotype A and haplotype B genome assemblies was 98.60% [S:91.80%, D:6.80%, F:0.60%, M:0.80%, n:1614] and 98.20% [S:91.60%, D:6.60%, F:0.80%, M:1.00%, n:1614] respectively (Table 2). Furthermore, the consensus quality values (QV) of the two haplotype genomes were assessed using Merqury v2020-01-292728 (k = 20) with Illumina PE150 reads. For haplotype A and haplotype B genome assemblies, the mapping rates of the short reads were 99.28% and 99.28%, and their corresponding QV values were 43.53 and 43.49, respectively (Table 2).

Genome annotation

For repeat elements annotation, a combined approach integrating both ab initio and homology-based evidence was employed. In summary, a de novo repeat sequences library was built using LTR_FINDER v.1.052329, RepeatScout v.1.052430 and RepeatModeler v.2.0.12531. Then RepeatMasker v.4.1.232 was applied to annotate both novel and previously known repeat sequences according to the de novo repeat library and the Repbase v.19.062733 database. In total, 713.59 Mb (70.28%) and 701.17 Mb (69.90%) repetitive sequences were annotated in the haplotype A and haplotype B genomes, respectively (Fig. 1G; Table 3).

Genome annotation of repetitive sequences and protein-coding genes.

For protein-coding genes prediction and functional annotation. Before predicting genes, the genome assemblies were first soft masked using Bedtools v2.31.034 with the ‘maskfasta’ option based on the repetitive annotation file. Subsequently, three gene identification methods, including de novo-based, RNA-seq-based, and homology-based approaches, were employed to predict gene structures. For de novo-based prediction, the Braker v3.0.335 auto pipeline with default parameters and AUGUSTUS v3.4.036 was used to train the parametric model and complete the de novo-based gene prediction. To train the parametric model, the RNA-Seq reads were first mapped to the D. cambodiana genome using Hisat v.2.2.137 with default parameters, and then set as the input file. For RNA-Seq-based prediction, all RNA-Seq reads were filtered using Trimmomatic-0.3638 (TruSeq 3-PE.fa:2:30:10 LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15) and de novo assembled using Trinity v.2.1.139. The assembled transcripts were then mapped to the genome assembly to accomplish RNA-Seq-based gene prediction using Program to Assemble Spliced Alignments (PASA) v.2.4.140. For homology-based prediction, the protein-coding sequences of six species, Arabidopsis thaliana (GCF_000001735.4)41, Oryza sativa (GCF_001433935.1)42, Solanum lycopersicum (GCF_000188115.5)43, Zea mays (GCF_902167145.1)44, Ricinus communis (GCF_019578655.1)45 and Cucumis melo (GCF_025177605.1)46, were individually blasted with D. cambodiana genome using BLAST v.2.2.2647 with the following parameters “-F F -e 1e-05”. Genewise software v.2.2.048 with the following parameters “-trev -sum -genesf -gff” was subsequently utilized to accomplish homology-based gene prediction. Finally, the above three gene sets were integrated into a consensus set using EVidenceModeler v.1.1.149 according to different weight values (RNA-Seq-based: 10, homology-based: 5, de novo-based: 3). The information on alternatively spliced sites and untranslated regions (UTR) were added using PASA v.2.4.140. In total, 27,361 and 27,075 protein-coding genes were predicted in haplotype A and haplotype B genomes, respectively (Fig. 1G; Table 3).

Functional annotation of protein-coding genes were completed based on seven public databases, including SwissProt (https://www.uniprot.org/), GenBank nr (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/), EggNOG (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/), GO (http://geneontology.org/), and Pfam (https://ftp.ebi.ac.uk/pub/databases/Rfam/), This annotation was conducted using DIAMOND v0.9.14.115 with the parameter ‘-e 1e-5’50. Totally, 27,361 (100%) and 27,066 (99.97%) genes were successfully annotated in at least one database for haplotype A and haplotype B, respectively (Fig. 2A,B; Table 3). Furthermore, the BUSCO completeness values for the predicted proteins were 96.8% for haplotype A and 96.0% for haplotype B (Fig. 2D, Table 3).

Annotation of noncoding RNAs

The identification of transfer RNAs (tRNAs) was performed using tRNAscan-SE v. 2.0.751. Other non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), such as microRNAs (miRNAs), ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) and small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), were identified using Infernal v.1.152 by searching against the Rfam v.14.153 database with default parameters. Lastly, the number of rRNAs, snRNAs, miRNAs, and tRNAs predicted from the haplotype A/B genome were 5,417/4,947, 315/317, 196/181, and 1,623/1,643, respectively (Table 4).

Technical Validation

Evaluation of the genome assembly

To evaluate the genome assembly quality, several parameters such as contiguity, completeness, and correctness were utilized. For contiguous, high-fidelity reads generated from the CCS model, which exhibit higher correctness compared to continuous long reads (CLR), were utilized to complete the genome assembly. Furthermore, Hi-C interaction analysis also showed well-organized interactions among the 40 pseudochromosomes, which was consistent with the reported chromosomes numbers of D. cambodiana15 (Fig. 1F). For completeness, the BUSCO17 assessment revealed that within the Embryophyta BUSCO genes, 98.6% (1591) and 98.2% (1586) were identified as complete and single-copy or duplicated genes in the contig-level haplotype A and haplotype B assemblies, respectively (Table 2). Additionally, only 0.6% (10) and 0.8% (13) of the sequences were fragmented, and only 0.8% (13) and 1.0% (15) were absent in the contig-level haplotype A and haplotype B assemblies, respectively. Moreover, the completeness and the contig N50 of the two sets of haplotypes of D. cambodiana were higher than chromosome-level genome of D. cochinchinensis14 and fragmented assembly15 of D. cambodiana (Table 2). Furthermore, the BUSCO17 analysis was performed again after the Hi-C assembly, yielding similar results with the contig-level genome assemblies. These results were also comparable with those of some related species, such as Ananas. comosus (GCF_001540865.1)73, Phoenix dactylifera (GCF_009389715.1)74 and other species42,44,75–77 (Fig. 2C). For correctness, the evaluation of the consistency between the assembly and paired-end DNA short reads indicated that the high mapping rates were 99.28% and 99.28% in haplotype A and haplotype B genomes, respectively (Table 2). Overall, these results collectively demonstrate the genome assembly of D. cambodiana in this study are high quality, and completeness.

Evaluation of the gene repertoire

To ensure the completeness and accuracy of the annotated gene sets, the predicted gene models were used to blast with multiple protein databases, including SwissProt, NR, TrEMBL, KEGG, EggNOG, GO, and Pfam. Those results indicated that 100.00% (27,361/27,361) and 99.97% (27,066/27,075) of the predicted gene models exhibited significant similarity to proteins in at least one of these databases in haplotype A and haplotype B genome, respectively (Fig. 2A,B). Moreover, the BUSCO assessment revealed that the completeness of those gene sets were comparable with those of some related species, such as A. comosus (GCF_001540865.1)73, P. dactylifera (GCF_009389715.1)74 and other species42,44,75–77 (Fig. 2D).
