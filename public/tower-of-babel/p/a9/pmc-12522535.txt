Sixty years of experience with hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis: Insights from the Swedish transthyretin amyloidosis registry
Authors: Lotta Stenberg, Björn Pilebro, Intissar Anan, Jorge Mejia Baranda, Kristin Samuelsson, Per Eldhagen, Rolf Backlund, Jonas Wixner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHereditary transthyretin (ATTRv) amyloidosis was first described in Sweden in the late 1960s. Selected patient data have been collected since then and have now been transferred to a national quality registry.MethodsThis is the first report from SveATTR—a longitudinal Swedish web‐based registry open for TTR variant carriers and patients with ATTR amyloidosis. The registry covers basic background information, as well as relevant clinical follow‐up measures and data on disease‐modifying therapies. Data from all ATTRv amyloidosis patients registered through December 2022 were included.ResultsIn total, 1055 patients were included, of whom 65% were males and 95% carried the V30M variant. Median age of onset was 64 years, and 79% had a late disease onset (≥50 years). Eighty‐seven percent of the patients had peripheral polyneuropathy at onset, whereas 10% had cardiac symptoms, 8% had visual disturbances, and 6% had gastrointestinal symptoms. A total of 159 patients had undergone liver transplantation, and 233 had received a disease‐modifying drug. Improved survival was seen for transplanted patients and for patients on drug therapy.ConclusionThis report highlights the importance of SveATTR for further characterization of the Swedish ATTRv amyloidosis population as well as for evaluating the efficacy of disease‐modifying therapies.Keywords: drug therapies, hereditary amyloidosis, liver transplantation, registry, survival, transthyretin

Introduction

Transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis is, together with AL amyloidosis, the most common systemic amyloidosis. The disease is caused by amyloid formation from misfolded transthyretin (TTR) and manifests in two forms—wild‐type (ATTRwt) and hereditary/variant (ATTRv) amyloidosis. ATTRwt amyloidosis is an age‐related disorder that mainly causes cardiomyopathy in elderly males and is being increasingly recognized around the world. ATTRv amyloidosis, on the other hand, is a rare multi‐systemic and autosomal dominant genetic disease associated with TTR gene variants, of which V30M (p.V50M) and V122I (p.V142I) are the most common variants [1]. The disease is spread worldwide, but clustering areas of ATTRV30M amyloidosis are found in, for example, Portugal, Brazil, Japan, and Sweden. Common complications of ATTRv amyloidosis include peripheral polyneuropathy, autonomic neuropathy, gastrointestinal (GI) disturbances, cardiac arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, visual disturbances, and renal failure. Both the genotype and the amyloid fibril type have been associated with the phenotype and the prognosis of the disease [2]. The median survival without disease‐modifying treatment ranges from 3 to 6 years after diagnosis in some cohorts [3, 4, 5], and between 7 and 12 years after disease onset in other series [4, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Reduced survival has been shown for patients presenting with cardiomyopathy [3, 4, 5]. During the last three decades, several disease‐modifying therapies have emerged. Liver transplantation (LTx) was introduced in 1990 and has been an effective treatment option for selected patients since [10]. Later, disease‐modifying drugs were introduced, and both TTR stabilizers [11, 12, 13, 14] and TTR gene silencers [15, 16, 17] have been shown to halt the progression of the disease. An improved survival has been demonstrated for tafamidis‐treated compared to untreated patients [8, 14, 18, 19], as well as for patisiran‐treated patients [20].

ATTRv amyloidosis, or familial amyloidosis with polyneuropathy (FAP) at that time, was first described in Sweden in 1968 [21]. Later, the Swedish population with ATTRv amyloidosis was nicely described in Rune Andersson's thesis from 1976 [6]. Since then, selected data from ATTRv amyloidosis patients have been collected in a local database at Norrland's University Hospital in Umeå, Sweden. In 2020, the data were transferred to a national web‐based quality registry, and the Swedish transthyretin amyloidosis registry (SveATTR) was established. During the last few years, several other hospitals have joined the registry and continuously enter new data from patients with ATTRwt and ATTRv amyloidosis, as well as data from healthy TTR variant carriers. To date, more than 1300 individuals have been registered, most of them with a diagnosis of ATTRV30M amyloidosis, but a full data analysis has not yet been conducted.

The aim of the present study was to provide a comprehensive overview of the Swedish ATTRv amyloidosis population and to assess the natural history survival. We also wanted to explore prognostic factors, including the treatment effect of LTx and disease‐modifying drugs, for patients included in the SveATTR registry through December 2022.

Materials and methods

The registry

SveATTR is a longitudinal web‐based registry open for Swedish TTR variant carriers and patients with ATTRv and ATTRwt amyloidosis established in 2020. The registry is based on the national INCA platform and is accessible via a secure two‐step login procedure. SveATTR is linked to the Swedish population registry via the subjects’ personal identity number. The registry contains two data cohorts—one historical cohort with data imported from a local database at Norrland's University Hospital in Umeå, Sweden, started in the 1960s, and one cohort with data from subjects entered directly into the web‐based registry from 2020 and onward. Most variables are shared by the two data cohorts, but some relevant treatment and clinical follow‐up variables have been added when SveATTR was established. So far, most subjects in the registry are from the historical data cohort. SveATTR is administered by Registercentrum Norr, but the Region of Västerbotten is responsible for the personal registry data (CPUA), and a national multi‐professional steering group is leading the registry work. Jonas Wixner is chairman of the steering group.

Registry variables

The registry variables are grouped in four main categories—background, follow‐up visit, treatment, and survival data. A list of all the variables is shown in Table S1. The subjects’ personal data, sex, and place of residence are automatically entered into the registry via the Swedish population registry, as are the name and workplace of the reporting health care personnel. All other data are manually entered via the online registry form, mainly through checkboxes. However, the survival data (disease duration from onset and from diagnosis) are automatically calculated based on the current date (or date of death) in relation to the registered dates of disease onset and diagnosis, respectively.

Symptom variables

Symptoms of peripheral polyneuropathy were defined as sensory loss/paresthesia and/or neuropathic pain in the upper and/or lower extremities. GI symptoms were defined as early satiety, nausea/vomiting, constipation, alternating constipation/diarrhea, diarrhea, fecal incontinence, and abdominal pain. Cardiac symptoms were defined as tachycardia, bradycardia, fainting, fatigue, and dyspnea. The other symptom variables were not more closely defined. For each patient, multiple symptoms within a symptom category or between categories could be selected.

Patient data

Data from all patients diagnosed with ATTRv amyloidosis registered in SveATTR until December 31, 2022 were included in the analysis. Baseline data and data from the last follow‐up visit were collected. The variables included in the analyses are listed in Table S1 and were considered sufficient for the aims of our study. Late disease onset was defined as symptom onset from 50 years of age. Cardiac septum hypertrophy was defined as a septal thickness of >12 mm. A positive 99mTc‐DPD scintigraphy was defined as grade 1–3 uptake according to the Perugini score [22].

DNA analysis for TTR variants was implemented in Swedish clinical practice in the late 1980s, whereas diagnostic abdominal fat pad biopsy with amyloid fibril typing (type A or B) was implemented in 2006 and 99mTc‐DPD scintigraphy in 2012. LTx was introduced as a therapy in 1990, and after that, modified body mass index (mBMI) [23], polyneuropathy disability (PND) scale [23], and echocardiography started to be used for routine follow‐up of patients. The first disease‐modifying drug (tafamidis) was approved in Sweden in 2011, but open‐label treatment with tafamidis and diflunisal has been available through trials and license prescription (of diflunisal) since 2007. The current and more detailed follow‐up program for ATTRv patients, including the Kumamoto score [24], Karnofsky performance status, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, and various laboratory tests, was established in 2020 with the initiation of the SveATTR, and these variables are now systematically recorded during follow‐up visits. Additionally, the introduction of SveATTR has facilitated the data recording of disease‐modifying therapies.

Given the differences in diagnostics and treatment over time, three different time periods based on the year of patients’ diagnosis were created—the pre‐transplant era (before 1990), the liver transplant era (1990–2010), and the pharmaceutical era (from 2011).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as medians (interquartile range, IQR, or full range) and valid percentages if not stated otherwise. Non‐parametric tests were used because a normal distribution of data could not be guaranteed. Differences between groups were analyzed with the Chi2 and the Mann–Whitney U tests. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method and the log‐rank test, as well as the Cox regression model to adjust for confounding covariates. For treatment‐related survival, Cox regression analyses with time‐dependent covariates were used to adjust for the time between diagnosis and treatment initiation. Survival was calculated from disease onset if not stated otherwise. p values <0.05 (95% confidence interval, CI) were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.

Ethical considerations

Informed consent is not required for participation in Swedish quality registries. However, patients are informed that data are collected for the registry, for example, via posters in our outpatient departments, including information on how to opt out from the registry. Data collection and analysis of the registry data were approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (reference numbers 2022‐05184‐01 and 2022‐06719‐02).

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

Of the total 1382 registry subjects, 1055 ATTRv amyloidosis patients were included in the analyses, whereas 29 ATTRwt patients and 298 healthy TTR variant carriers were excluded. Baseline characteristics of the included patients are presented in Table 1. The first patient was diagnosed in 1960 and the last in 2022, and the median year of diagnosis was 2003 (IQR 1990–2014).

Most patients were of Swedish origin and carried the V30M variant, but 45 patients with 14 different non‐V30M variants were also included. Of those with Swedish origin, 52% originated from the region of Västerbotten and 40% from the region of Norrbotten in the northernmost part of Sweden. Almost 80% of the patients had a late disease onset (≥50 years of age), and 65% of the patients were males. Median age at onset was 39 (21–49) years in the early‐onset group and 68 (50–92) years in the late‐onset group. Among those with late‐onset disease, 68% were male and 32% were female (p < 0.01). Of the patients with non‐V30M variants, 78% had a late disease onset (p < 0.01). Patients diagnosed before 1990 all carried the V30M variant and had a lower median age at onset (58 years) than those diagnosed from 1990. Conversely, patients who did not present with peripheral polyneuropathy at disease onset showed a higher frequency of non‐V30M variants (22%) and had a higher median age at onset (71 years).

Amyloid fibril type

The amyloid fibril type had been determined in 132 patients, of whom 77 (58%) patients displayed type A fibrils (both full‐length and fragmented TTR). Of those with type A fibrils, 96% had a late‐onset disease, in contrast to only 48% of the patients with type B fibrils (only full‐length TTR, p < 0.01). Among patients with non‐V30M variants, 9 out of 10 displayed type A fibrils, whereas 1 patient with the H88R (p.H108R) variant exhibited type B fibrils. No significant difference in amyloid fibril distribution was observed between the sexes.

Symptoms at disease onset

Most patients (87%) presented with symptoms of peripheral polyneuropathy at onset, whereas around 10% of the patients presented with symptoms of either cardiac, ocular, or autonomic dysfunction (including GI symptoms). Only few patients presented with CNS symptoms or unintentional weight loss. Patients who did not present with peripheral polyneuropathy more frequently had cardiac symptoms at disease onset than those who presented with polyneuropathy (37% vs. 6%, p < 0.01).

Male patients were more likely to have cardiac symptoms at onset than women (12% vs. 7%, p < 0.01), but otherwise no significant differences were found between the sexes. Patients with late disease onset more often presented with cardiac symptoms than early‐onset patients (12% vs. 3%, p < 0.01) and less frequently with polyneuropathy symptoms than those with early disease onset (86% vs. 94%, p < 0.01). Similar results were found for patients with type A fibrils compared to those with type B fibrils. Patients diagnosed before 1990 less frequently presented with cardiac symptoms (1% vs. 5% vs. 23%, p < 0.01) than those diagnosed in the later time periods (1990–2010 and 2011–2022, respectively). Peripheral polyneuropathy at onset was significantly more common in patients diagnosed before 1990 than those diagnosed 2011–2022 (89% vs. 81%, p < 0.01) but not compared to those diagnosed 1990–2010 (89% vs. 87%, p = 0.35).

Comorbidities

Any of the listed comorbidities had been reported for 176 (17%) of the patients. Hypertension was the most frequently reported comorbidity (6%), followed by carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS, 5%), chronic kidney disease (4%), ischemic heart disease (3%), diabetes mellitus (2%), spinal stenosis (1%), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, 1%). With CTS and spinal stenosis excluded, any other chronic comorbidity was reported for 121 (11%) of the patients.

Follow‐up visits

A follow‐up visit had been recorded for 386 (27%) of the patients. Although the overall data integrity varied, the echocardiography variable had the least missing data (4%). For subsequent analyses, only echocardiography and 99mTc‐DPD scintigraphy data from the follow‐up visits were included.

Cardiac involvement

Data on cardiac septum thickness had been recorded for 272 patients, of whom 100 (37%) had septal hypertrophy. Moreover, 153 patients had undergone 99mTc‐DPD scintigraphy, of which 115 (75%) indicated amyloid cardiomyopathy (Perugini grades 1–3). Altogether, 141 patients had signs of cardiac involvement from either a positive 99mTc‐DPD scintigraphy or septum hypertrophy on echocardiography. Septal hypertrophy was more common among patients with a positive 99mTc‐DPD scintigraphy than among those with negative scintigraphy (88% vs. 12%, p < 0.01). There was no significant difference in septum hypertrophy between the sexes, but a positive 99mTc‐DPD scintigraphy was more common in males than in females (79% vs. 21%, p < 0.01). No significant difference in cardiac involvement (from echocardiography and/or scintigraphy) was seen between patients who presented with peripheral polyneuropathy at onset and those who did not. Cardiac involvement was not documented in any patient diagnosed before 1990, whereas 43% of patients diagnosed 1990–2010 had signs of cardiac involvement and 55% of those diagnosed 2011–2022.

A total of 122 patients (12%) had received a pacemaker implantation, either as prophylaxis (15%) or due to symptoms/arrhythmia (85%). Pacemaker implantation was more common in males than in females (74% vs. 26%, p = 0.03), but no significant difference was found between patients who presented with polyneuropathy and those who did not (11% vs. 16%, p = 0.08). Pacemaker implantation was less common in patients diagnosed before 1990 compared to the later time periods (6% vs. 12% vs. 15%, p < 0.01).

Organ transplantation

A total of 159 patients (15%) had undergone an LTx through December 2022, whereas 6 (0.6%) had received a heart transplant and 2 (0.2%) had received a kidney transplant due to end‐organ failure. Four (67%) of those who had received a heart transplant had also undergone an LTx, as had both kidney transplanted patients. No differences in transplant frequencies were found between the sexes. Median year of LTx was 2004 (range 1990–2020). Most (77%) of the transplantations had been performed in patients diagnosed 1990–2010, whereas 10% and 13% of the transplants had been performed in patients diagnosed 1960–1989 and 2011–2022, respectively. Among liver transplanted patients, early‐disease onset was more common than in non‐transplant patients (63% vs. 13%, p < 0.01), but no difference was seen in genotype distribution. The median time from disease onset and from diagnosis to liver transplant was 4.5 and 1.9 years, respectively.

Disease‐modifying drugs

Altogether, 233 (22%) of the patients had received a disease‐modifying drug, mostly tafamidis, diflunisal, patisiran, and/or inotersen (n = 216) per their latest follow‐up visit. Of those, 10 patients had also received a liver transplant, and all but one started drug therapy after transplantation. The number of patients treated with the various drugs, as well as data on treatment initiation and duration, are presented in Table 2. Each patient could have been treated with multiple drugs over time, but none were used simultaneously. Of those who had received a disease‐modifying drug, 70% were male and 30% were female (p = 0.04). No patient diagnosed before 1990 had received drug therapy. Of those who received a disease‐modifying drug, 15% had been diagnosed 1990–2010 and 85% had been diagnosed 2011–2022. Late‐onset disease was more common in patients who received drug therapy than in untreated patients (88% vs. 77%, p < 0.01), but no difference was seen in genotype distribution. Median time from disease onset and from diagnosis to first treatment initiation was 3.1 and 0.7 years, respectively.

Survival

By December 31, 2022, 341 (32%) of the patients were still alive, and 714 (68%) patients had died. Of the liver transplanted patients, 73 (46%) were still alive at the time of the study endpoint. The median overall survival for the entire cohort of patients was 11.6 (IQR 8.0–18.0) years from disease onset. Poorer survival was seen for male versus female patients (11.5 vs. 12.5 years, p = 0.02), patients with late versus early disease onset (10.4 vs. 21.5 years, p < 0.01), and for patients without peripheral polyneuropathy at onset versus those presenting with polyneuropathy (Fig. 1). Cox regression analyses showed that sex, age at disease onset, non‐V30M variant, peripheral polyneuropathy at onset, and diagnostic period were significant predictors of mortality, and all predictors but sex remained significant in the multivariable analysis (Table 3). Similar results were found when analyzing survival from diagnosis instead of from disease onset.

Lower hazard ratios (HR) for mortality and longer estimated median survival times from disease onset were seen for patients diagnosed in the later time periods compared to patients diagnosed before 1990 (Fig. 2). Further, after adjusting for age at onset and time from diagnosis to treatment initiation, liver transplanted patients displayed a lower HR for mortality than untreated patients (0.39, 95% CI 0.17–0.91), as did patients who received disease‐modifying drugs compared to untreated patients (HR 0.22, 95% CI 0.09–0.51). Similar results were found when analyzing the 10 patients who received combined therapy (liver transplant and drug therapy) as a separate group.

Lower hazard ratios (HR) for death, as well as longer estimated median survival times, were seen for patients diagnosed 1990–2010 (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.55–0.77) and 2011–2022 (HR 0.39, 95% CI 0.31–0.50), compared to those diagnosed before 1990.

Although the median survival for untreated patients was 10.3 (IQR 7.1–14.5) years after disease onset, 50 of the untreated patients had survived for 20 years or longer after disease onset, and, of those, 50% were male, 60% had late‐onset disease, and all but one (ATTRY69H) carried the V30M variant. All but three of these patients presented with polyneuropathy symptoms. Echocardiography data were available for five patients, and none of them had cardiac hypertrophy. No data on amyloid fibril type were available. This subgroup of patients had been diagnosed between 1960 and 2014.

Causes of death

A cause of death had been reported in 325 (46%) of the 714 deceased patients. The most frequently reported cause of death was unspecified complications related to amyloidosis (39%), followed by heart failure (15%), infection (11%), surgical complications (6%), sudden death (6%), kidney failure (6%), and malignancies (5%).

Discussion

In this first report from the nationwide Swedish ATTR amyloidosis registry, we were able to include 1055 ATTRv amyloidosis patients diagnosed during the last 60 years. In addition to providing an overview of the registry, it is the largest review of the Swedish ATTRv population and the first comprehensive report since the 1970s [6]. To our knowledge, it is also one of the first national ATTR amyloidosis registries ever to be presented.

Our main finding was a significantly improved survival for patients who had received disease‐modifying therapy, which was illustrated by lower HRs for mortality for both liver transplanted patients and patients who received disease‐modifying drugs compared to untreated patients. Indirectly, this was also supported by lower HRs for death in patients diagnosed during the treatment era than in those diagnosed before 1990. However, earlier diagnosis and optimized general care might also have contributed to better outcomes for patients diagnosed from 1990 and onward.

Additionally, we confirmed that the median untreated survival remained around 10 years from disease onset [6], underlining the importance of an early and adequate disease‐modifying treatment. Notably, some untreated patients had survived for over 20 years after onset, suggesting a high variability in the natural history of the disease. We did not identify any major genotypic or phenotypic differences within this subgroup of patients, but a low prevalence of amyloid cardiomyopathy was presumably a contributing factor to their prolonged survival, although our data were insufficient to confirm this hypothesis.

Male patients displayed poorer survival in a univariable analysis; however, in a multivariable analysis only (higher) age at onset, absence of peripheral polyneuropathy at onset, and a non‐V30M variant, apart from the diagnostic period, remained significant predictors of mortality. These three variables are all associated with older age and cardiac complications [2], which probably explains the higher mortality risk. For patients who did not present with peripheral polyneuropathy, a delayed diagnosis might also contribute to their worse outcome. Thus, patients without polyneuropathy at onset and those with non‐V30M variants will require special attention and optimized care to further improve their outcomes.

Expectedly, 95% of the patients carried the V30M variant [25, 26, 27], but 14 non‐V30M variants, out of the 16 known in Sweden [28], were also included in the study. Given the few patients with non‐V30M variants, subgroup analyses on these patients should be interpreted with caution. Over 90% of the patients originated from the clustering areas in northern Sweden, which is higher than the expected frequency of around 75% [28], indicating that patients from the southern half of Sweden are underrepresented in SveATTR. A minority (1%) of the patients were homozygous for a TTR variant, which is high compared to other clustering areas but corresponds well with earlier Swedish data [29].

Most patients in the study cohort and most of those receiving disease‐modifying drugs were male (65% and 70%, respectively), which is in line with previous publications [6, 27, 30]. Because ATTRv amyloidosis is an autosomal dominant disorder, both sexes carry the same risk of inheriting the mutation. Why males are more frequently affected by the disease is not fully elucidated, but a maternal anticipation (that is strongest for mother–son pairs) may play a role [29], as may the fact that males are at higher risk of developing amyloid cardiomyopathy [31, 32, 33], which in turn might be related to hormonal and genetic factors [34]. However, underdiagnosis of ATTRv amyloidosis in women cannot be ruled out.

The Swedish ATTRV30M population is usually described as having late‐onset disease with a mixed phenotype with both polyneuropathy and cardiomyopathy [25, 27, 35], as opposed to, for example, the Portuguese population with a mainly early‐onset neuropathic phenotype [36, 37]. Our data support these findings with a median age at onset of 64 years and 79% of patients with late‐onset disease, as well as a large proportion of patients with signs of amyloid cardiomyopathy. Fifty‐eight percent of the patients displayed type A fibrils, which is associated with a later onset and a risk of amyloid cardiomyopathy [32, 38]. However, 21% of the patients had early‐onset disease, and the youngest patient was diagnosed already at the age of 22, confirming that the early‐onset phenotype is also present in Sweden. Previous studies have shown that there are regional differences in the age of onset within Sweden [39] and Japan [40], but these are not as evident in Portugal, although late‐onset cases are quite common even in Portugal [37, 41]. The exact reasons for these differences within the ATTRV30M population remain to be elucidated.

In our study, most (87%) of the patients presented with symptoms of peripheral polyneuropathy, whereas around 10% had either cardiac symptoms, ocular symptoms, or symptoms of autonomic neuropathy (including GI symptoms) at onset. This is in line with previous findings [6, 26, 32], but it is possible that symptoms other than those from peripheral polyneuropathy were underreported in the older dataset because less attention was given to these variables in the earlier days. Moreover, a higher incidence of ocular manifestations of around 35% at disease onset has been previously reported [42].

A comorbidity was reported for 17% of the patients, which is probably significantly lower than the true frequency because most of the comorbidities were included as variables after the introduction of the SveATTR registry in 2020. For example, in a recent study from the region of Norrbotten, 46% of the patients had hypertension and 22% had chronic kidney disease [27]. In our material, the presence of a comorbidity did not significantly impact survival, but this was likely affected by the low reported prevalence of comorbidities.

Three hundred and forty‐one patients were still alive at the time of our study. Unpublished data from the National Board of Health and Welfare suggest that 325 patients were living with ATTRv amyloidosis in Sweden in 2018. Given that 20–30 new patients are diagnosed each year, the disease prevalence should be around 400 patients in 2022 [28], which means that SveATTR covered around 85% of all Swedish ATTRv patients at this time point.

The high coverage rate of the registry is, of course, a strength of our study, as is the high number of patients included. Much of the baseline (background) and survival data was complete, but there was quite a lot of missing data for the follow‐up visits, which is a limitation. Due to this, many of the follow‐up variables were not included in this analysis. Of the variables included, limited data were available for inheritance, proven amyloid deposits, amyloid fibril type, comorbidities, echocardiography, 99mTc‐DPD scintigraphy, and cause of death. Moreover, data on drug therapy were probably not complete because this variable was introduced with the SveATTR registry in 2020. Therefore, the most robust findings of our study are the baseline genotypic and phenotypic characteristics of the patients, as well as the natural history survival and the survival after LTx.

To further refine the registry, efforts will be made to invite more clinics to report data and to make the dataset more complete, especially when it comes to comorbidities and follow‐up data (including drug therapy). Future studies will hopefully be able to include most follow‐up variables and present more detailed evaluations of the disease‐modifying therapies.

In conclusion, SveATTR serves as a valuable resource for detailed characterization of the Swedish ATTR amyloidosis population and for monitoring the efficacy of therapeutic interventions. Our study confirms that the Swedish patient cohort is characterized by late‐onset ATTRV30M amyloidosis with a mixed phenotype. Significantly improved survival was seen for liver transplanted patients and for those receiving pharmacological therapy, as well as for patients diagnosed from 1990 onward, reflecting the advancements in treatment strategies over the years. Still, patients without peripheral polyneuropathy at disease onset and patients with non‐V30M variants will require tailored management to further improve their clinical outcomes.
