Gender representation in oral and maxillofacial radiology societies: a longitudinal multi-society analysis of speakers and leadership
Authors: Murat Icen, Serkan Yilmaz, Katibe Tugce Temur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGender representation in academic dentistry is increasingly recognized as a key indicator of equity, visibility, and leadership. Scientific conferences serve as highly visible platforms for academic recognition; however, women’s representation among invited speakers and leadership positions in radiology-focused dental societies remains insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to evaluate temporal and organizational patterns of gender representation among invited speakers and presidents across major international and national oral and maxillofacial radiology (OMFR) societies, and to assess whether women-led presidencies were associated with higher proportions of women invited speakers.MethodsThis retrospective observational study analyzed publicly accessible conference programs and leadership records from eight professional societies. Invited speaker gender was recorded for conferences held between 2015 and 2025, while president gender was documented from 1949 to 2025. Descriptive statistics summarized annual and organizational distributions. Temporal and organizational trends were evaluated using generalized linear mixed models with a binomial distribution and logit link, including organization as a random intercept. Additional models examined concurrent and one-year lagged effects of women presidencies on speaker composition.ResultsOf 490 invited speakers, 35.1% were women, with a significant increase over time and no difference between international and national societies. Among 169 presidents, 11.2% were women, and the likelihood of women presidents increased significantly across years. Gender representation varied markedly between societies. Neither concurrent nor lagged women presidencies were associated with higher proportions of women invited speakers.ConclusionsAlthough gender representation in OMFR societies has improved over time, it remains below parity. The lack of association between women leadership and speaker composition suggests that leadership diversity alone may not be sufficient to fully explain observed patterns in speaker representation. OMFR societies should adopt transparent, merit-based speaker selection protocols. Leadership diversity alone may be insufficient; structural reforms and continued equity monitoring may help reduce the gender gap in academic visibility.Keywords: Gender equity, Women in radiology, Oral and maxillofacial radiology, Scientific conferences, Academic leadership

Introductıon

Leadership within academic dentistry profoundly influences research direction, mentorship culture, and the transmission of professional values [1, 2]. Particularly, scientific conferences serve as highly visible platforms for showcasing expertise, establishing collaboration networks, and shaping future disciplinary agendas. Consequently, the representation of women among conference speakers and organizational leaders serves as a measurable indicator of gender equity within a specialty [3, 4].

Despite the increasing feminization of the dental and medical workforce, women remain disproportionately underrepresented in senior academic and leadership positions [2, 5–8]. In radiology, women comprise roughly one-third of the workforce but occupy fewer than 20% of department chair or society president roles; [9–12] in North America, fewer than 10% hold executive leadership positions [13]. This ‘leaky pipeline’—where women’s talent is drained at successive career stages—is attributed to structural barriers, including implicit bias in promotion, lack of mentorship, and societal expectations [9, 14–17]. Beyond representation, gender diversity serves as a critical tool for excellence, enhancing organizational performance, innovation, and clinical decision-making [9, 14].

Pediatric radiology serves as an instructive parallel; despite achieving near workforce parity, leadership remains male-dominated, with over 80% of society presidents being men [13, 18]. Furthermore, while women’s participation in scientific sessions reached 50%, they comprised only 38% of lecturers—a disparity linked to non-transparent, memory-based selection rather than objective review [11]. Similarly, recent dental studies in prosthodontics and endodontics show women representing only 16% of invited speakers, with no consistent upward trend [2, 19]. Together, these data underscore a structural inertia across radiologic and dental domains, where visibility inequities persist through informal networks and unconscious mechanisms rather than overt exclusion [11, 19].

Oral and maxillofacial radiology (OMFR) occupies a particularly important position at this intersection. Radiology societies are pivotal in disseminating diagnostic innovations and shaping imaging standards; therefore, equitable participation in their scientific and leadership platforms is essential for balanced professional development. Yet, unlike prosthodontics or endodontics, OMFR has not been systematically evaluated for gender representation among invited speakers or association presidents. This evidence gap limits the profession’s ability to assess progress, identify institutional disparities, or design effective equity initiatives.

Given this background, assessing gender dynamics in OMFR conferences and professional societies provides a valuable lens for understanding whether increased participation in the workforce translates into visibility in leadership. This study therefore aimed to investigate temporal and organizational trends in gender representation among keynote or invited speakers and association presidents across major international and national OMFR societies from 2015 to 2025. The following hypotheses were tested:

H1: The proportion of women invited as speakers has increased over time;

H1: The proportion of women invited as speakers has increased over time;

H2: The proportion of women speakers differs among radiologic societies;

H2: The proportion of women speakers differs among radiologic societies;

H3: The likelihood of women being elected as presidents has increased over time;

H3: The likelihood of women being elected as presidents has increased over time;

H4: The proportion of women presidents differs among societies, and

H4: The proportion of women presidents differs among societies, and

H5: Years with women presidents are associated with higher proportions of women invited as speakers.

H5: Years with women presidents are associated with higher proportions of women invited as speakers.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This retrospective, observational study examined gender representation among invited speakers and elected presidents across major international and national OMFR societies. Speaker data were collected for the 2015–2025 period, whereas historical records of society presidents were retrieved from 1949 to 2025 to capture the full leadership chronology. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility [20].

All information was obtained from publicly accessible sources, including official conference programs, association websites, and verified digital archives. The study did not utilize any confidential or personally identifiable information; consequently, it was exempt from institutional ethics review, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Analytical procedures comprised two main levels: descriptive and inferential.

Descriptive analyses

Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize gender distributions among invited speakers and presidents by year and organization. Temporal trends were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square and linear-by-linear association tests.

Inferential analyses (Generalized Linear Mixed Models, GLMMs)

To account for repeated yearly observations within societies and between-organization heterogeneity, GLMMs with a binomial distribution and logit link were fitted. Organization was modeled as a random intercept, and fixed effects were defined as detailed below. Outcome coding was defined according to the reference category used in each model, and odds ratios were interpreted accordingly.

Results

Of these, 172 (35.1%) were women and 318 (64.9%) were men, demonstrating a persistent but gradually improving gender imbalance in academic visibility (Table 1).

Discussion

The present multi-organizational analysis revealed a persistent yet progressively narrowing gender gap in academic visibility and leadership within OMFR. Across the decade analyzed (2015–2025), the proportion of women invited as keynote or plenary speakers increased significantly, while the likelihood of women presidents also rose. Nevertheless, women comprised only one-third of invited speakers and roughly one-tenth of presidents, indicating that, despite measurable improvement, gender parity remains distant. These findings mirror broader patterns in radiology and dentistry, where structural inequities in leadership visibility endure despite the feminization of the professional workforce [9, 11, 13, 21, 22].

Temporal improvement in women representation

The steady rise in women speakers aligns with global data showing gradual increases in women participation in radiology conferences [11, 18]. However, this trend should be interpreted with caution, as improved digital archiving and increased conference data availability after 2018 may have partially contributed to the observed temporal increase. Kubik-Huch et al. [9] emphasized that although women now represent nearly half of medical students in many countries, they remain substantially underrepresented among academic radiologists and leadership positions—a “leaky pipeline” that continues to lose women talent at each successive career stage [14]. The current OMFR findings suggest that this attrition may be narrowing, although it has not yet been eliminated. The positive year effect observed for invited speakers supports the notion that early-career participation among women is improving, likely reflecting a generational shift in academic demographics and broader inclusion initiatives within professional organizations. Pfeifer et al. [11] reported a similar trend in pediatric radiology, where women attendance and authorship in scientific sessions approached parity, even though leadership and invited-lecture roles remained men dominated. Consistency across these subspecialties indicates the emergence of systemic progress, albeit limited by long-standing cultural and institutional inertia. Furthermore, the lack of significant differences between international and national societies implies that gender disparity in OMFR is systemic rather than region-specific, paralleling Sharma et al. [13] conclusion that underrepresentation of women in radiology leadership is a globally shared challenge, not confined to a single geographical or economic context.

Leadership disparity and organizational heterogeneity

Although women presidents increased significantly over time, overall representation remained limited (11.2%). The higher intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC ≈ 12.8%) compared to the speaker model (ICC ≈ 4.5%) indicates that leadership diversity is more dependent on organizational culture than temporal trends alone. Although statistically significant, the magnitude of ICC values suggests modest clustering effects. Sharma et al. [13] identified similar variability across North American and Canadian radiology institutions, where departments with explicit equity, diversity, and inclusion policies exhibited markedly higher women’s leadership rates than those without such frameworks [12]. Kubik-Huch et al. [9] argued that gender diversity should not be viewed as a metric but as a tool for excellence, enhancing team performance, innovation, and clinical decision-making. In that sense, the persistently men-dominant leadership structures observed in OMFR may perpetuate inequity and hinder organizational excellence. Institutional inertia and differences in governance structures may partly explain the slow pace of improvement. The descriptive differences among societies further reinforce this interpretation: European and Asian academies showed comparatively higher women presidents proportions, while American organizations remained lower. This pattern mirrors the international data compiled by Kubik-Huch et al. [9], where cultural and policy heterogeneity strongly influenced leadership diversity outcomes.

Understanding the null finding for Hypothesis 5

Despite significant gains in both women speakers and presidents, the present analysis found no evidence that years led by women presidents corresponded to higher proportions of women invited speakers, either contemporaneously or with a one-year lag. This null finding is informative and corroborates the observation by Pfeifer et al. [11] that the selection process for invited speakers at radiology meetings frequently depends on informal recall networks instead of standardized, gender-neutral methods. Consequently, even when leadership becomes more gender balanced, these systemic processes may remain unaffected in the short term. Moreover, speaker selection for major conferences typically occurs 12–18 months prior to the event—often before new leadership takes office—limiting the direct temporal linkage between presidential gender and speaker composition. As Sharma et al. [13] noted, achieving equity requires structural reform rather than reliance on individual champions. Leadership gender may symbolize institutional openness but does not, on its own, dismantle entrenched biases within program committees or academic hierarchies. Therefore, the absence of a measurable presidency effect in OMFR reinforces the need for transparent, multi-level policies rather than reliance on top-down influence alone.

Cross-disciplinary perspectives and structural determinants

Comparing OMFR with other dental and medical disciplines reveals that radiology has advanced further toward gender equity than traditional dental specialties, yet full parity remains unachieved. In prosthodontics, women represented only about 16% of invited speakers from 2010 to 2024, with little evidence of upward progression [2], while endodontics exhibited similarly stagnant trends [19]. By contrast, radiology-oriented societies—including OMFR—show a clearer positive trajectory, with women speakers now exceeding one-third and women presidents gradually emerging. However, interpretation of the 35.1% female speaker proportion requires consideration of the underlying workforce composition. Although comprehensive global data on the gender distribution of oral and maxillofacial radiologists are unavailable, the largest available workforce survey of oral and maxillofacial radiologists in the United States and Canada reported that 46% of respondents were women [23]. This figure is notably higher than the proportion of women invited speakers observed in the present study (35.1%), suggesting that conference representation may not yet fully reflect the available professional workforce, at least within North American OMFR communities. A subsequent survey of board-certified oral and maxillofacial radiologists further confirmed the substantial participation of women within the specialty [24]. To our knowledge, no global survey characterizing the gender composition of the OMFR workforce is currently available; the North American data therefore represent the most directly relevant benchmark in the existing literature. Because comparable workforce data are lacking for many regions represented in the current analysis, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. Taken together, these observations suggest that radiology, as a technologically dynamic and research-driven field, may offer a more meritocratic platform and attract younger, gender-diverse cohorts [11, 25]. However, the persistence of men predominance in leadership and keynote roles demonstrates that systemic inequities remain entrenched despite relative progress. Thus, OMFR occupies an intermediate but evolving position: more inclusive than prosthodontic and endodontic organizations, yet still far from gender parity. Bridging this remaining gap will require institutional accountability and continuous monitoring rather than passive demographic change.

Implications for policy and organizational reform

The persistence of gender imbalance despite temporal improvement illustrates the importance of deliberate, institutional interventions. Both Sharma et al. [13] and Pfeifer et al. [11] emphasized that mentorship and sponsorship pipelines are critical to increasing women’s participation in leadership and speaker roles. Structured mentorship programs—such as the “speed mentoring” initiatives in pediatric radiology—could be replicated within OMFR societies to connect early-career women with established leaders and committee members. Further, organizations should implement transparent and auditable speaker-selection protocols, ensuring that invited lecturers are chosen via merit-based and gender-neutral review processes. Training on unconscious bias for conference planners and selection committees, as recommended by Kubik-Huch et al. [9], could mitigate inadvertent exclusion patterns. Finally, mandatory annual reporting of gender representation within programs and leadership boards, as advocated by Sharma et al. [13], would enhance accountability and allow benchmarking across organizations. Such reforms align with the World Health Organization’s 2021 framework calling for systemic, not individual, correction of leadership inequities [26]. If OMFR follows these principles, the specialty could become a model of inclusive excellence in the fields of dentistry and radiology.

Beyond the metrics of representation, the visibility of women in technology-intensive branches like OMFR is not merely a matter of equity; it is essential for integrating diverse diagnostic perspectives and varied mentorship models into the profession. Ensuring that women are prominently represented on scientific platforms is critical for the transmission of these diverse professional values to the next generation of specialists. Ultimately, fostering an inclusive environment directly influences the quality and sustainability of the future ‘talent pool’ in OMFR, ensuring the specialty remains innovative and reflective of the global dental workforce.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. This study relied exclusively on publicly available conference archives, which varied in completeness and detail. Gender identification was confined to a binary classification, illustrating the constraints of historical records and preventing the examination of nonbinary or gender-diverse identities. Additionally, the relatively low number of women presidents may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle associations between leadership gender and speaker composition. Furthermore, the analysis was unable to consider possible variations in invitation acceptance, declined offers, or abstract submission trends, all of which may influence the observed representation. Nonetheless, the longitudinal GLMM approach, incorporating random effects for organization, allowed robust inference by modeling both time-dependent and structural heterogeneity—an advantage over purely cross-sectional studies. Future research should expand the scope to include gender and ethnicity intersections, committee composition, and editorial board membership, thereby mapping the broader ecosystem of visibility and authority within OMFR.

Conclusion

Gender equity in OMFR is improving but remains incomplete. Significant year effects in speaker and presidency models confirm a gradual shift toward inclusivity, yet women remain underrepresented in senior leadership. The lack of a measurable presidency effect on speaker composition (H5) suggests that individual leadership change alone may not be sufficient to overturn entrenched systemic norms. Sustainable progress is likely to require structural transparency, formal mentorship, and continuous equity monitoring. This study supports the view that gender diversity is not merely a fairness metric but may also serve as a catalyst for excellence; embedding this principle into society governance could help ensure that future leadership more closely reflects the true diversity of the profession.
