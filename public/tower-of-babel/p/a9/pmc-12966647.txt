Introduction

The agri-food sector generates substantial volumes of by-products each year during food production, processing, and consumption (Ingale et al., 2025), causing serious environmental, social, and economic problems (Mellinas et al., 2022). It is estimated that about one-third of food intended for human consumption, equivalent to approximately 1.3 billion tons annually, is wasted (Bekavac et al., 2025). Thirty-nine percent of this waste is generated by the food manufacturing industry, consisting mainly of peels, seeds, stems, and pulp from fruits and vegetables (Drishya & Wani, 2024), accounting for between 8% and 10% of greenhouse gas emissions (Mansouri & Kaswengi, 2025).

In light of this problem, it is important to note that, in recent years, agri-food waste is no longer perceived as refuse but is now recognized as a valuable source of compounds with potential for reuse. Researchers such as Priti et al. (2025), Chaves et al. (2024), Yata et al. (2025), and Ampese et al. (2025) have highlighted agri-food waste as an important source of bioactive compounds, including phenolic acids, polyphenols, carotenoids, terpenoids, flavonoids, betalains, and anthocyanins (Sharma et al., 2025), which offer significant health benefits (Dulyanska et al., 2022). These compounds are valued for their biological activities, including their antioxidant (Plaza & Marina, 2025), anticancer (Abdel et al., 2025), antimicrobial (Cavallero et al., 2025), anti-inflammatory, anti-UV (Xue et al., 2025), antidiabetic (Thangsiri et al., 2024), antitumor, and hypoglycemic effects (Pedro et al., 2024).

The valorization of agri-food waste through the extraction of bioactive compounds is a strategy aligned with the principles of the circular economy and represents an opportunity to produce biofunctional foods sustainably (Rabbi & Amin, 2024). However, conventional extraction methods (solvent extraction, maceration, Soxhlet extraction, percolation, infusion) have several disadvantages (Khan et al., 2024), including the use of organic solvents, high energy consumption, prolonged extraction time, increased toxicity, and environmental pollution (Ballistreri et al., 2025). To address these limitations, ecological and emerging methods have emerged, such as ultrasound-assisted extraction (Oliveira et al., 2022), ohmic heating, pulsed electric fields, microwaves, natural eutectic solvents, and extraction with subcritical and supercritical fluids (Kainat et al., 2022).

The incorporation of bioactive compounds into food systems is a promising strategy for developing nutraceutical and functional products with higher biological quality (López et al., 2025). The inherent susceptibility of these compounds to factors such as temperature, humidity, oxygen, pH variations, and light exposure during processing and storage (Kobo et al., 2022), coupled with the adverse conditions of the digestive tract, poses a critical challenge that negatively affects their biological value and limits their applicability (Martinović et al., 2025). These limitations have prompted researchers to seek techniques that improve their stability and enhance their functionality.

In this context, microencapsulation is a novel technology designed to protect bioactive compounds through the use of a safe biopolymer that creates a protective membrane or coating (Tocto et al., 2024). This technology not only improves the stability to external conditions and solubility of bioactive compounds (Ben Mansour et al., 2025), but also optimizes their bioaccessibility and bioavailability by allowing controlled release in a specific environment (Martinović et al., 2025). In addition, microencapsulation prolongs the shelf life of bioactive compounds and masks unwanted flavors, improving consumer acceptance (Abdel et al., 2025).

The most commonly used methods for encapsulating bioactive compounds include spray drying (Stabrauskiene et al., 2024), freeze drying (N. C. Santos et al., 2025), and complex coacervation (Bhagya & Dash, 2022). The most prominent encapsulating agents are gum arabic (Hay et al., 2025), maltodextrin (Li et al., 2022), sodium alginate, and chitosan (Savic et al., 2022), as well as mixtures of these (Bastos et al., 2024). Extraction using ecological methods and subsequent microencapsulation has enabled better application of bioactive compounds from agri-food waste in products such as yogurts (López et al., 2025), cookies (Shoukat et al., 2025), snacks (Pinho et al., 2024), functional beverages (Kochubei et al., 2025), and mayonnaise (Shaygannia et al., 2021).

Likewise, the use of agri-food waste through emerging technologies for the ecological extraction and microencapsulation of bioactive compounds is aligned with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) (Ahmad et al., 2024). In particular, these strategies contribute to SDG 12 (responsible production and consumption) by valorizing by-products and reducing food waste (Mulligan et al., 2024), and to SDG 9 (industry, innovation, and infrastructure) by promoting the use of sustainable and innovative technologies (Kyei et al., 2025). Likewise, the development of stable functional ingredients with higher nutritional value is linked to SDG 2 (zero hunger) (Boudalia et al., 2025).

Considering the growing interest in valorizing agri-food waste through more environmentally friendly technologies and better preserving compounds of interest, this study seeks to address the following research question: What is the available evidence on ecological extraction and microencapsulation technologies for bioactive compounds derived from agri-food waste, and their impact on the development of functional foods?

In this regard, the objective of this review is to analyze and synthesize scientific advances related to the valorization of agri-food waste as a source for the extraction of bioactive compounds. Specifically, methods for extracting bioactive compounds will be documented, with an emphasis on emerging technologies. In addition, microencapsulation techniques will be reviewed, and their applications in the formulation of functional foods and in food preservation will be explored. Based on this analysis, the review not only summarizes the current state of knowledge, but also identifies several emerging lines of research as key contributions, including the development of hybrid extraction technologies, the use of more environmentally friendly solvents (Osamede et al., 2024), the integration of extraction processes with microencapsulation, and the exploration of microencapsulation technologies that have yet to be studied in depth, opening up new opportunities for future research.

Materials and methods

Databases and search strategy

To identify relevant studies examining ecological methods for extracting and encapsulating bioactive compounds extracted from agri-food waste, the review adopted the PRISMA protocol (Page et al., 2021). To this end, the ScienceDirect and Scopus databases were consulted, which offer extensive coverage of literature related to food science and technology. Scopus, in particular, offers greater scope and data representativeness, surpassing other databases such as WOS in terms of coverage (V. K. Singh et al., 2021). The main terms (including synonyms and other related words) were considered for the formulation of the search equations, together with Boolean operators:

Scopus: (Food waste OR fruit waste OR vegetable waste) AND (bioactive compounds OR phenolic compounds OR flavonoids OR carotenoids OR anthocyanins OR betalains) AND ((extraction OR recovery OR ultrasound OR microwave OR ohmic heating OR pulsed electric field OR supercritical fluid OR subcritical water OR NADES OR pressurized liquid) OR (microencapsulation OR encapsulation OR food applications OR “functional foods”)).

ScienceDirect: (food waste OR fruit waste) AND (bioactive compounds OR phenolics) AND (extraction OR recovery OR ultrasound OR microwave) AND (encapsulation).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To be included, studies had to meet the following criteria: (i) be experimental studies published between 2020 and August 2025; (ii) use only agri-food waste (peels, pomace, seeds, stems, non-commercial pulp) as raw material for the extraction of bioactive compounds; (iii) address the application of ecological/emerging extraction methods (UAE, MAE, PEF, SFE, SWE, NADES) to obtain bioactive compounds; and (iv) include the microencapsulation of the extracts obtained or their application (encapsulated or free) in food matrices, evaluating technological properties (encapsulation efficiency, solubility, stability), functional properties (bioaccessibility, antioxidant capacity, preservative effect) or sensory properties (impact on taste, color, or odor).

Conversely, studies were excluded that: (i) use raw materials other than waste to obtain bioactive compounds; and (ii) narrative reviews, meta-analyses, book chapters, conferences, or studies published before 2020 in order to prioritize emerging technologies and current approaches in extraction and microencapsulation.

Evaluation, selection and analysis of studies

The execution of the search strategy in the selected databases yielded a total of 3909 records. All records were exported to Rayyan AI, where duplicates were automatically removed, resulting in a corpus of 3813 studies for initial evaluation. Sequential evaluations by title, abstract, and full text were performed by two reviewers, who evaluated each document independently according to the inclusion/exclusion criteria. Each record was classified as “include,” “exclude,” or “maybe”; the latter category, along with any discrepancies, was resolved by a third reviewer. This process resulted in the inclusion of 68 primary studies, as detailed in Fig. 1.

PRISMA diagram for study selection.

In addition, a bibliometric analysis was performed using Bibliometrix and VOSviewer software, based on the co-occurrence of keywords and the analysis of journals and countries with the highest scientific output, both in the general corpus extracted from the databases and in the selected studies (Mondal, 2025). This analysis made it possible to map the intellectual structure and research trends.

Microwave-assisted extraction (MAE)

MAE is a fast, efficient, and environmentally sustainable method that uses electromagnetic radiation (0.3–300 GHz) to extract bioactive compounds from agri-food waste, with higher yield and purity than conventional methods (Alvarez et al., 2025). Its mechanism consists of three main stages: (i) selective absorption of microwave energy by intracellular water generates localized heating, vaporization, and increased pressure that breaks cell membranes and walls; (ii) cell disruption facilitates mass transfer from the solvent to the matrix and from bioactive compounds to the solvent; and (iii) the released compounds dissolve in the surrounding solvent (Fig. 2) (Alvarez et al., 2025).

Main components of microwave-assisted pectin extraction.

The main advantage of MAE over conventional extraction lies in the drastic reduction in processing time. For example, while CE of prickly pear peel required 50 min, MAE achieved superior recovery in just 10 min (J. A. Gómez et al., 2022). In black bean husks, 197.23 mg GAE/g of TPC was achieved using MAE in 6 min, with an AA of 91.37% (ABTS), surpassing the results of EC with a value of 129.32 mg GAE/g in 30 min (Mali & Kumar, 2023). In addition, variables such as temperature, time, pH, power, solid/liquid ratio, and solvent type have a significant effect on the recovery of bioactive compounds (Ramírez et al., 2024). In pineapple peel waste, using ethanol as a solvent (at 300 W) increased the TPC to 255,236 mg GAE/g, a higher value than that achieved with distilled water (Harith et al., 2023). This is because most phenolic compounds are moderately polar (lipophilic), and ethanol has a higher affinity and solubilization capacity for these less polar solutes compared to water.

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE)

UAE is a technique that uses high-frequency sound waves (20 to 100 kHz) in a liquid medium, generating the phenomenon of acoustic cavitation, i.e., the formation and violent collapse of microbubbles (Alvarez et al., 2025). This collapse produces a localized increase in pressure and temperature, causing disruption of the cell walls of the plant material. This rupture facilitates greater penetration of the solvent into the matrix and the efficient release of intracellular compounds (such as bioactive compounds) into the extraction medium (Fig. 3) (Haque et al., 2025).

Representation of ultrasound-assisted extraction.

Karadbhajne et al. (2025) showed that EAU reduces processing time from 48 h to 5 min in green pea husks, maintaining an AA of 87.25% by DPPH. In potato peel, UAE reached 9.30 mg GAE/g of TPC in 30 min, compared to the 6 days required by conventional extraction; in addition, it was found that recovery yield increases with decreasing potato peel particle size and increasing extraction temperature (Xu et al., 2022). The synergy between the UAE and natural deep eutectic solvents (NADES) optimizes the recovery of bioactive compounds. (M. Santos et al., 2023) reported increases of 1.6 to 2.8 times in the recovery of phenols in blueberry leaves using NADES compared to organic solvents.

In addition to extraction efficiency, Priti et al. (2025) demonstrated that pomegranate peel extracts obtained by UAE have greater in vitro bioaccessibility (85.96 mg GAE/g in the digested state) than those obtained by conventional methods. These results position the UAE as a key technology for the circular economy in the agri-food industry.

Supercritical fluid extraction (SFE)

SFE is a novel and environmentally friendly technique that uses a fluid (usually CO2) above its critical temperature (31.1 °C) and critical pressure (73.8 bar) (Alvarez et al., 2025). In this state, the fluid acts as a solvent with high diffusivity (like a gas) and high solvation power (like a liquid), allowing for efficient and selective extraction (Zhang & Wu, 2025). The process involves passing supercritical CO2 through the plant matrix, dissolving the bioactive compounds. Its dissolving power can be adjusted with pressure, temperature, or a cosolvent (such as ethanol) for greater selectivity (Zhang & Wu, 2025). Finally, by reducing the pressure in a separator, the CO2 turns into a gas, and the concentrated extracts are recovered (Fig. 4).

Representation of supercritical fluid extraction.

Goyeneche et al. (2020) optimized SFE in beet leaves using CO2 with ethanol as a co-solvent, achieving a TPC of 3.37 mg GAE/g and an AA of 1.45 mg TE/g at 35 °C and 400 bar. Complementarily, (Pinto et al., 2024) applied SFE to chestnut shells, obtaining extracts with 45.78 mg GAE/g of TPC before digestion and 38.57 mg/g after the in vitro gastrointestinal process, achieving a bioaccessibility of 30%. Despite the decrease in phenolic content, the extracts retained significant AA, hypoglycemic, and neuroprotective properties, attributed to the biotransformation of phenolic acids into more active metabolites such as hydroxybenzoic and phenylpropanoic acids. These results confirm that SFE not only maximizes polyphenol recovery but also preserves their biological functionality after digestion.

Subcritical water extraction (SWE)

SWE is an environmentally friendly, acid-free technique that uses water as a solvent at high temperatures (between 100 °C and 374 °C) and pressures between 2 and 22 MPa to keep it in a liquid state (without boiling) (Haque et al., 2025). Under these conditions, the dielectric constant of water decreases, increasing its capacity to solubilize compounds of varying polarity, also acting as a hydrolytic médium (Pinto et al., 2021). Heat and pressure soften cell walls, facilitating mass transfer and the efficient and selective release of bioactive compounds from the plant matrix into the solvent (Fig. 5) (Haque et al., 2025).

Representation of extraction with subcritical water.

Krivošija et al. (2025) applied SWE to onion and garlic agricultural waste, identifying protocatechuic and neochlorogenic acids, as well as flavonoids such as quercetin and catechin, with cardioprotective and anticancer effects demonstrated in silico. For their part, Cardona et al. (2025) reported that the use of subcritical fluids (220 °C, 50% ethanol/water, 5 g/min) increased TPC by 200% and AA by 500% compared to conventional extraction by agitation. These studies show that SWE allows TPC-rich extracts and antioxidants to be obtained without the need for organic solvents, reducing environmental toxicity and improving process efficiency compared to conventional methods.

Ohmic heating assisted extraction (OHAE)

OHAE is a heating technique that uses the electrical impedance inherent in food to produce heat, ensuring rapid, uniform, and efficient heating that minimizes the thermal degradation of bioactive compounds (Sánchez et al., 2023). OHAE is based on the application of an electric current that generates heat within the sample, causing the cell wall to rupture and improving the solubility of bioactive compounds (Haque et al., 2025).

The superiority of OHAE over conventional extraction has shown a 40% increase in TPC yield and between 20% in AA (Sánchez et al., 2023). In addition, the extracts showed low toxicity in non-tumor cells and preventive effects of ROS, highlighting their potential applicability in functional foods and nutraceuticals. Likewise, a study conducted on rambutan peel has shown that OHAE produced an extract with greater power to neutralize free radicals and oxidants compared to those reported by other methods (Torgbo et al., 2022).

Hybrid methods for the extraction of bioactive compounds

Hybrid methods combine two or more environmentally friendly techniques to optimize the extraction of bioactive compounds, reducing time and costs. Among the most notable combinations is ultrasound-microwave, whose synergistic action improves cell disruption, mass transfer, and the release of bioactive compounds from plant tissues (Haque et al., 2025). These two methods can be used either simultaneously or sequentially (ultrasound followed by microwave or vice versa), as shown in Fig. 6.

(a) Simultaneous extraction (ultrasound and microwaves together); (b) sequential extraction (ultrasound followed by microwaves).

Jha and Sit (2023) indicated that the combination of ultrasound followed by microwave (UMAE) was the most efficient treatment, achieving the highest values of TPC (346.05 mg GAE/g), TFC (116.93 mg QE/g), and antioxidant activity (91.57% inhibition) in Terminalia chebula samples. In comparison, these values were approximately 13–15% higher than those obtained with individual methods (MAE and UAE) and between 3 and 6% higher than those obtained with combined treatments with enzymes. The triple combination (UAE + MAE + enzymes) did not outperform UMAE, indicating that the additional inclusion of enzyme treatment did not generate a significant improvement in extraction efficiency.

For their part, Osamede et al. (2024) showed that extraction yield in sequential hybrid processes depends significantly on the order in which the techniques are applied. For example, UAE followed by MAE (UMAE) increased yield by 52%, while MAE followed by UAE resulted in a 7.38% decrease compared to individual extraction using MAE. In addition, they indicated that when ternary hybrid combinations (UAE + MAE + homogenization) were performed, the TPC of date seeds was reduced by 9%.

Among the variables that must be taken into account to optimize UMAE hybrid extraction, a study conducted with pomegranate peel showed that an intermediate solid-liquid ratio (S/L, 1:50) and an ethanol concentration (60%) maximize TPC recovery (N. Singh et al., 2025). Given that lower values limit mass transfer and higher values reduce efficiency due to lower microwave absorption, compound degradation, and changes in solvent polarity. Likewise, low microwave power (150 W) combined with 200 W of ultrasonic power promotes efficient cell disruption, while higher powers (300 W) promote thermal degradation of bioactive compounds (N. Singh et al., 2025). The study also indicated that extraction time had a positive but less decisive effect, suggesting that prolonged exposure (30 min) does not guarantee higher yields.

Comparison of emerging techniques in the extraction of bioactive compounds

Both EAU and MAE have proven to be efficient technologies by drastically reducing extraction time and energy consumption, while increasing yield. Miera et al. (2023) reported that UAE outperformed MAE in TPC and TFC extraction from orange peels. Under optimized conditions, EAU (1.5 min, 10 W) had the lowest energy consumption (18 kJ), followed by MAE (306 kJ) and conventional extraction (927 kJ), highlighting the energy efficiency of EAU.

UAE also excelled in the extraction of specific phenolic compounds, such as ferulic acid (1.49 mg/g) and hesperidin (1.34 mg/g), which were between 25% and 35% higher compared to other techniques (CE and MAE) (S. Kaur et al., 2024). This increase is attributed to the cavitation mechanism generated by ultrasound, which facilitates the rupture of cell walls and the release of compounds (Oliveira et al., 2022). In the extraction of TPC and TFC from peach waste, higher values were obtained with UAE; however, MAE proved to be faster and more effective in the extraction of vitamin C, extracting 10.8 mg/g of dry matter in just 50 s, half the time required by UAE (Plazzotta et al., 2020). This indicates that the choice of technique should be based on the nature of the target bioactive compound.

For its part, pressurized liquid extraction (PLE) is presented as a high-performance alternative, although potentially slower. In Tahitian lime pomace, PLE (performed at a high temperature, 110 °C) consistently yielded the highest yields and maximum antioxidant capacity compared to UAE, although with a longer extraction time (40 min PLE vs. 6 min UAE) (Oliveira et al., 2022). This finding suggests that PLE may be preferable when the primary objective is maximum overall extract yield, while EAU offers a better balance between yield, time, and energy expenditure.

On the other hand, UMAE has shown that the synergistic combination of technologies can increase TPC extraction by up to 52% and obtain extracts with up to 49% greater antioxidant capacity, compared to individual methods (Osamede et al., 2024). In this sense, the combination of technologies suggests that their application not only allows for greater recovery of bioactive compounds, but also extracts with better functional quality.

Overall, the studies highlight that MAE and UAE, alone or in combination, allow extracts with high phenolic, flavonoid, and antioxidant content to be obtained, while optimizing the time, energy, and efficiency of the process. These green technologies represent an efficient strategy for the recovery of agri-food waste, providing functional compounds with applications in food, nutraceuticals, and bioactive products, while reducing environmental impact and promoting agri-food sustainability.

Microencapsulation as a technology for protecting bioactive compounds

Microencapsulation is a technology that protects bioactive compounds using a polymer matrix that forms a protective layer around the active core (Tocto et al., 2024). This coating improves its stability against environmental factors such as light, oxygen, and temperature (Ben Mansour et al., 2025), and optimizes its bioaccessibility through controlled release in a specific environment (Martinović et al., 2025). In addition, it extends the shelf life of compounds and helps mask undesirable flavors, promoting consumer acceptance of the product (Fig. 7) (Abdel et al., 2025).

(a) Advantages of microencapsulation of bioactive compounds, (b) classification of microcapsules.

In this context, an efficient microencapsulation system must simultaneously meet five key objectives for its application in food (Marques et al., 2022): (i) produce microcapsules suitable for human consumption, (ii) allow the incorporation of compounds into food matrices with high physicochemical stability and minimal sensory impact, (iii) protect the compounds from degradation induced by temperature, light, pH, or interaction with other ingredients, (iv) ensure maximum absorption through controlled release in response to specific physiological stimuli (such as intestinal pH), and (v) be technologically scalable for industrial production (Marques et al., 2022).

According to the research analyzed on the microencapsulation of bioactive compounds from agri-food waste (Table 2), the most commonly used methods are spray drying (Stabrauskiene et al., 2024) and freeze drying (N. C. Santos et al., 2025).

Spray drying microencapsulation

Spray drying (SD) is one of the simplest, fastest, most efficient, and economical methods, with wide industrial applicability, that allows liquid solutions to be converted into powder, facilitating the microencapsulation of bioactive compounds (Yata et al., 2025).

As illustrated in Fig. 8, the SD microencapsulation process can be briefly described as follows: the liquid feed (bioactive extract + coating material) is homogenized to form a dispersion or emulsion. Subsequently, this solution is pumped by a peristaltic pump and atomized into small droplets through a nozzle inside the drying chamber (Pattnaik et al., 2021). There, the droplets come into contact with a stream of hot air, causing the solvent to evaporate rapidly and microcapsules to form, coated with a thin layer of the encapsulant (Tocto et al., 2024). Finally, the microcapsules are collected in the collector containers.

Representation of the microencapsulation process using spray drying.

Microencapsulation by freeze-drying

Freeze-drying preserves heat-labile nutrients by sublimating the solvent under vacuum conditions at low temperatures (Stabrauskiene et al., 2024). The LD process comprises three stages (Fig. 9): (i) freezing, where the feed solution (bioactive extract and wall material) is rapidly cooled to solidify the solvent and maintain structural integrity (Stabrauskiene et al., 2024); (ii) primary drying, where the frozen solvent is removed by sublimation under vacuum at temperatures below −50 °C, removing most of the solvent; and (iii) secondary drying, where residual water is removed by desorption at temperatures of 20–40 °C, breaking the hydrogen bonds between the bound water and the matrix (Pattnaik et al., 2021). As a result, a dry, stable product with high retention efficiency is obtained, minimizing thermal degradation and preserving bioactive functionality.

From an industrial application perspective, the selection of the microencapsulation method should be evaluated not only based on the information provided in Table 3, but also considering its operational feasibility, energy consumption, scalability, and environmental impact. In this context, SD has clear advantages over FD, as it is a continuous process with greater production capacity, shorter operating times, and lower energy consumption. For example, SD can operate with feed flows of up to 30 mL/min (Stabrauskiene et al., 2024), while FD is a batch process with slow operation (24–96 h) (N. C. Santos et al., 2025). This implies high energy consumption, especially due to the freezing conditions (up to −80 °C), sublimation, and vacuum creation during freeze-drying (Ioannou et al., 2024).

In this context, EE is established as the primary indicator of the effectiveness of the wall material in trapping the active compound during the drying process. For example, soy protein isolate (SPI) showed EE values greater than 97% for TPC and 96% for anthocyanins (Chen et al., 2025), attributable to its amphiphilic nature and the formation of dense networks around the core. Similarly, gum arabic (GA) achieved EE of 92.26% for betacyanins, surpassing maltodextrin (88.36%) (Bhagya & Dash, 2022).

Polymer blends exhibit additional advantages over single-component systems. The combination of sodium alginate and gelatin (SA–GEL) achieved EE of 95.9–98% of TPC, far exceeding alginate used individually (<70%) (Martinović et al., 2025). This synergistic behavior is attributed to electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged biopolymers, which generate more compact, less permeable, and structurally more stable matrices during drying.

The physicochemical properties of microencapsulated powder also depend significantly on the wall material. Low hygroscopicity, crucial for product stability and flowability, was observed in microcapsules formulated with guar gum (1.36%), associated with an EE of 94.56% (A. Kaur & Ghoshal, 2025). Likewise, SPI, in addition to its high EE, conferred low residual moisture and solubilities of 60–64%, favoring industrial handling and dispersion of the final product (Chen et al., 2025). These characteristics are particularly relevant in industrial applications, where the physical stability of the powder determines its technological and commercial viability.

In terms of stability, wall materials significantly reduce the degradation of bioactive compounds during storage and processing conditions. Microcapsules with SPI retained more than 90% of bioactive compounds after 90 days at 25 °C (Chen et al., 2025), while combinations of pectin and microcrystalline cellulose significantly improved the thermal stability of betalains at 80–100 °C (Tran et al., 2022). Similarly, a combination of chia gum and MD retained between 87.5 and 96.3% of TPC for up to 8 weeks at 40 °C (Abdel et al., 2025). GA- and MD-based systems also demonstrated prolonged protection against oxygen and light, retaining high TPC contents (>121 mg GAE/g) (Ali et al., 2024). The improvement in stability is attributed to the increase in the glass transition temperature of the encapsulating matrix, which immobilizes the bioactive compound, reducing its reactivity.

Furthermore, it is important to note that wall–core interactions and the type of encapsulating agent significantly influence gastrointestinal release and bioaccessibility of bioactive compounds. In this regard, Aldana et al. (2024) reported significant variations in the bioaccessibility of carotenoids depending on the encapsulation system, suggesting that molecular interactions between the matrix and the active compound condition its release in the gastrointestinal tract. Consistently, it has been observed that protein matrices and certain gums better preserve TPC and carotenoids during in vitro digestion compared to systems based exclusively on carbohydrates such as gum arabic and inulin (Stajčić et al., 2021). For example, soy protein isolate allowed for 90.73% TPC release (Chen et al., 2025), while alginate- and gelatin-based systems released between 64.19% and 75.93%, followed by alginate and GA (Martinović et al., 2025). In systems with chia gum, it allowed the release of up to 70% of TPC, followed by MD + chia gum (63%) and MD (53%) (Abdel et al., 2025).

Therefore, proteins not only encapsulate but also actively regulate the protection and release of the bioactive compound, explaining their superior performance compared to exclusively polysaccharide systems. However, depending on the source, these can affect the sensory acceptance of microencapsulates.

Based on the evidence analyzed, it can be inferred that carbohydrates (MD, GA) are suitable when solubility, thermal stability, and cost are prioritized. On the other hand, proteins and their combinations with certain gums maximize storage stability, bioaccessibility, and controlled release. Finally, polymer-protein blends emerge as the most efficient strategy for achieving a comprehensive balance between encapsulation efficiency, physicochemical stability, and gastrointestinal functionality, overcoming the limitations of single-component matrices.

Regulatory considerations for wall materials for microencapsulation

From a regulatory standpoint, the industrial viability of microencapsulated systems depends on a comprehensive assessment of the safety, chemical inertness, and biodegradability of the encapsulating materials (Shavronskaya et al., 2023). These criteria form part of the risk assessment processes carried out by regulatory authorities to ensure that wall materials do not pose risks to human health or the environment.

For example, in the United States, these systems are generally evaluated under the GRAS framework administered by the FDA, which requires demonstrating the safety of the encapsulating material and the active compound under the intended conditions of use (Szpicer et al., 2025). Within this framework, safety is assessed through toxicological studies, history of safe consumption, and dietary exposure assessment (Szpicer et al., 2025). Likewise, the wall material is expected to be chemically and physically inert, meaning that it should not react with either the encapsulated bioactive compound or the food matrix containing it (Shavronskaya et al., 2023). This property ensures that the functionality of the microcapsule (protection and controlled release) is predictable and that no unwanted or toxic by-products are formed during processing, storage, or digestion (Shavronskaya et al., 2023). In this regard, most of the wall materials reported in the studies analyzed (Table 2), including maltodextrin, gum arabic, proteins, and pectin, have a history of safe use and exhibit stable behavior in food systems, which facilitates their regulatory acceptance.

However, more complex systems, including nanostructures such as nanoliposomes, nanocrystals, or polymeric nanoparticles (Biswas et al., 2022), require specific evaluations that consider particle size, surface properties, digestibility, biodistribution, and structural stability (Szpicer et al., 2025). In these cases, the regulatory assessment also considers the potential for bioaccumulation and the behavior of the material after release.

For its part, the European Union states that any encapsulating material not included in the list of authorized additives must undergo a thorough evaluation by the EFSA. This evaluation includes a complete physicochemical characterization, in vitro digestion studies, and toxicological data (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion) (More et al., 2021). In addition, EFSA considers the biodegradability of the encapsulating material, assessing its ability to degrade into harmless compounds within the body or in the environment after disposal (More et al., 2021).

Unlike the GRAS system, the EFSA regulatory framework is centrally managed and requires pre-market authorization for any novel encapsulating material not included in the list of food additives approved in the European Union (Szpicer et al., 2025). This highlights the need to develop wall materials that combine technological functionality, safety, and sustainability.

Incorporation of bioactive compounds into food matrices

The integration of bioactive compounds derived from agro-industrial waste is a strategy for the development of functional foods, simultaneously addressing food waste and the production of foods with biological activities. Studies demonstrate the effectiveness of these extracts, derived from by-products such as grape pomace (López et al., 2025), pomegranate peel (Shoukat et al., 2025) and passion fruit waste (M. P. de Souza et al., 2022), in various food matrices, including meat products (hamburgers and pâté), dairy products (yogurt), and even cookies and chocolate bars (Table 5).

The main function of bioactive compounds lies in their powerful antioxidant activity, which is crucial for inhibiting lipid and protein oxidation, significantly extending the shelf life of products. For example, microencapsulated grape pomace extracts reduced malondialdehyde in pâtés to levels (<2.50 mg/kg) well below the control (3.62 mg/kg) after 42 days (Carpes et al., 2020). Similarly, extracts from passion fruit peel (M. P. de Souza et al., 2022) and encapsulated purple sweet potato achieved oxidative protection comparable to or superior to that of synthetic antioxidants such as BHT or sodium erythorbate (Moraga et al., 2025).

In dairy systems (yogurt), the incorporation of microencapsulated extract not only exponentially increased TPC, TAC, and AA (López et al., 2025), but also significantly improved the bioaccessibility of bioactive compounds after in vitro digestion. In chocolate, microencapsulation reduced TPC loss during accelerated storage from 50% (control) to only 22% (Grassia et al., 2021). In mayonnaise, microencapsulation in basil and Persian gum was essential for effective antioxidant and antimicrobial activity (Shaygannia et al., 2021). These studies show that microencapsulation is important for ensuring the stability, release, and bioaccessibility of bioactive compounds.

For their part, the functional results of bioactive compounds in food matrices depend largely on the efficiency of the previous stages of extraction and encapsulation. For example, pistachio shell extracts obtained under optimal conditions using MAE significantly increased the antioxidant activity of cookies (Kazemi et al., 2023). Similarly, extracts rich in phenolic compounds obtained by UAE from pomegranate peel showed positive functional effects in hamburgers (Ordaz et al., 2023). However, a high initial concentration of bioactive compounds does not guarantee their final performance if appropriate stabilization strategies are not applied. In this regard, microencapsulation plays a key role in preserving biological activity and improving compatibility with the matrix (Grassia et al., 2021). The choice of wall material, such as specific gums in emulsions or protein systems in dairy matrices, allows the high encapsulation efficiencies reported in laboratory studies to be validated at the level of real food (Shaygannia et al., 2021).

Despite promising results, the industrial application of bioactive compounds faces certain limitations. One of the main ones is the narrow window between the functionally effective dose and sensory acceptability. Although adding 1% pomegranate peel extract was effective in hamburgers (Ordaz et al., 2023), similar or higher concentrations in other matrices may produce undesirable bitter or astringent flavors. Although several studies report neutral or even improved sensory acceptability (Table 5), these results depend heavily on the type of extract, the food matrix, and the consumer profile, underscoring the need for specific sensory evaluations for each application. Therefore, the integration of microencapsulation is a key technological strategy for masking unwanted flavors and odors of bioactive compounds, favoring a more viable food application.

With regard to regulatory compliance, although many bioactive compounds are naturally occurring, concentrated extracts obtained from by-products using emerging environmentally friendly technologies may be considered “novel foods” by the European Union (More et al., 2021). This implies that free or microencapsulated bioactive extracts could be subject to safety assessments, toxicological and exposure studies, and prior authorization for marketing. Likewise, the incorporation of extracts and microencapsulated systems poses additional challenges related to the definition of safe and effective doses that must be optimized.

Word cloud and keyword co-occurrence

The bibliometric analysis was performed based on the terms identified in the titles, abstracts, and keywords of documents retrieved from the Scopus and ScienceDirect databases, published between 2020 and August 2025. To visualize general trends using a word cloud (Fig. 10a), the 50 most frequently occurring keywords were selected, a widely used criterion for representing dominant concepts without introducing excessive redundancy. For the analysis of keyword co-occurrence (Fig. 10b), a minimum threshold of 10 occurrences per term was established in order to exclude generic or marginal words and reduce noise in the analysis. This criterion allowed us to focus on terms with sufficient thematic relevance to identify consistent conceptual relationships, from which clusters representative of the main lines of research in the field were defined.

(a) Keyword cloud (2020–2025) generated with Bibliometrix (data extracted from Scopus); and (b) Keyword co-occurrence network (2020–2025) generated with VOSviewer (data extracted from ScienceDirect).

Keyword analysis of the 714 studies retrieved from Scopus (Fig. 10a) shows that “antioxidants” (n = 208) was the most frequent term, followed by “antioxidant activity” (n = 149), “chemistry” (n = 140), “extraction” (n = 137), ‘encapsulation’ (n = 121), “bioactive compounds” (n = 114), and “polyphenols” (n = 103). This pattern suggests that research has been oriented toward a cycle of technological valorization, ranging from the extraction and chemical characterization of bioactive compounds (particularly polyphenols), through the evaluation of their antioxidant activity, to their stabilization using microencapsulation techniques.

The co-occurrence network was constructed from 3195 studies from ScienceDirect, considering 201 keywords that exceeded the minimum frequency threshold (≥ 10 occurrences). This can be summarized into three main clusters (Fig. 10b). The green-red cluster, focused on encapsulation, spray drying, and microencapsulation, reflects the consolidation of strategies for the protection and controlled release of compounds when applied in food matrices such as meat products. The gray-blue clusters, associated with the terms ultrasound and microwave-assisted, show that these are the most studied green technologies for the extraction of bioactive compounds, contrasting with the evidence compiled in Table 1. The orange cluster, which links phytochemicals and bioaccessibility, shows an emerging focus on the biological and functional validation of bioactive compounds.

Together, Fig. 10a and b show that research follows a sequence of valorization, progressing from the efficient extraction of bioactive compounds using technologies such as ultrasound and microwaves, to their stabilization through microencapsulation, and finally to their functional validation. However, the relative disconnect of the cluster focused on bioaccessibility could indicate a persistent gap between current research and its practical application. This highlights that future studies should go beyond the stages of extraction and microencapsulation and incorporate comprehensive biological validation through in vitro digestibility tests of the encapsulating matrix and the evaluation of the in vitro bioaccessibility of the released compounds in order to assess their release and potential availability, thus contributing to greater scientific maturity.

Furthermore, terms such as NADES appear relatively isolated in the thematic network (Fig. 10b). Given that these solvents offer ecological advantages and can improve extraction yields, greater integration into future lines of research represents an opportunity to move toward more sustainable and efficient processes.

Thematic co-occurrence analysis reveals a clear conceptual evolution (Fig. 13b): in the initial phase (2020−2022), research focused on optimizing extraction conditions. Subsequently (2023–2025), attention shifted to the protection of bioactive compounds through microencapsulation and biological validation through bioavailability and gastrointestinal digestion studies, but these are still emerging fields.

Despite the sustained growth in studies and the evolution of the approach from extraction to encapsulation, the literature has certain limitations. Contribution to the Sustainable Development Goals

The recovery of bioactive compounds from agri-food waste using green extraction and microencapsulation technologies represents a key opportunity to move toward sustainable and circular food systems aligned with the objectives of the 2030 Agenda (Pandey et al., 2025). For example, SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) is addressed by valorizing agri-food waste as sources of bioactive compounds, transforming them into nutritious ingredients that can be incorporated into functional foods, contributing to food security. SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production) is promoted through the adoption of eco-friendly extraction and microencapsulation techniques, which minimize waste, maximize resource utilization, and comply with zero-waste initiative standards (Pandey et al., 2025). SDG 13 (Climate Action) is reinforced by reducing the environmental footprint and greenhouse gas emissions, taking advantage of efficient technologies such as EAU and MAE, which consume less energy and time than conventional methods (Miera et al., 2023). SDG 9 (Industry, Innovaton, and Infrastructure) is promoted through the implementation of green extraction and microencapsulation technologies, which enable the efficient and sustainable production of bioactive compounds, modernizing the food industry and generating high-value functional products.

Furthermore, the adoption of these technologies depends on clear regulatory frameworks and international collaboration between academia, industry, and governments, which are necessary to ensure safety, circularity, and life cycle assessment, thereby also reinforcing SDG 17 (Partnerships for the Goals). However, challenges remain, such as standardizing protocols, optimizing dosages, and evaluating health effects in vitro and in vivo, as well as implementing life cycle assessment systems for each technology. These aspects are essential to ensure reproducibility and safety in the industrial application of these compounds.

Future directions in extraction and microencapsulation of bioactive compounds from food waste

Extraction of bioactive compounds

The future prospects for the ecological extraction of bioactive compounds are geared toward the systematic and robust optimization of process variables such as temperature, time, particle size, and liquid/solid ratio, as well as parameters specific to each technology, in order to maximize the efficiency, selectivity, and reproducibility of the processes. At the same time, the use of more environmentally friendly solvents such as water or the development of natural deep eutectic solvents (de Lima et al., 2024).

Likewise, further exploration of combinations of green technologies (simultaneous or sequential) is required to maximize extraction performance and minimize energy consumption and environmental impact (Osamede et al., 2024). In this context, the systematic application of life cycle analysis becomes essential to assess the actual sustainability of these processes.

Finally, a comprehensive assessment of biomass is proposed; this means that the waste produced after extracting bioactive compounds could be used to obtain useful biopolymers, such as pectins or fibers, which could be used as materials in microencapsulation systems. This approach would reduce losses, improve the stability of bioactive compounds, and advance circular economy schemes in the food industry.

Microencapsulation of bioactive compounds

Firstly, the future of microencapsulation is geared toward the development of functional and sustainable wall materials, with a particular emphasis on plant-based biopolymers and derivatives of agro-industrial by-products. In this context, greater use of plant mucilages (such as chia and flaxseed) (Abdel et al., 2025), tuber starches (Samaniego et al., 2025), as well as encapsulating agents obtained from residues such as pectins, fibers, and mucilages (Tran et al., 2022). Likewise, the integration of these biopolymers with proteins of plant and animal origin (e.g., pea, soy, or whey protein) represents a promising strategy for the design of more efficient encapsulated systems capable of improving the stability, encapsulation efficiency, controlled release, and bioaccessibility of bioactive compounds (Chen et al., 2025).

As for microencapsulation methods, future prospects point to a diversification of the technologies used, beyond spray drying and conventional freeze drying. In particular, further development and application of emerging methods such as electrospray, emulsion-based freeze-drying (C. Gómez et al., 2024), spray freeze-drying (Ioannou et al., 2024), and supercritical-assisted atomization is expected (Ampese et al., 2025). In addition to the integration of pretreatments such as ultrasound assistance, due to its potential to improve encapsulation efficiency and the stability of sensitive compounds, as well as to allow greater control of the microstructure.

Another promising area to explore is the integration of microencapsulation with 3D food printing for the development of personalized functional foods. This approach will improve the stability, bioaccessibility, and controlled release of bioactive compounds through multi-material designs and programmable microstructures (Ahmadzadeh & Ubeyitogullari, 2024). However, this integration will also drive the design of new advanced encapsulation systems, such as liposomes, hydrogels, and hybrid structures, capable of combining protection, stability, and controlled release of bioactive compounds in complex food matrices (Ahmadzadeh et al., 2025; Meigui et al., 2025).

Prospects for incorporating bioactive compounds into food matrices

In future studies, it will be essential to optimize the doses of both free and microencapsulated bioactive compounds, as well as to conduct sensory evaluations to ensure consumer acceptance. Likewise, the incorporation of bioactive compounds into food matrices must consider not only their technological stability, but also their potential impact on shelf life, nutritional value, and effects on consumer health. In this regard, it will be necessary to conduct further studies on the stability of bioactive compounds during processing and storage, through assessments of degradation kinetics that allow their shelf life to be estimated and ensure that they maintain their functionality until the moment of consumption.

In addition, scientific validation must move from in vitro studies to in vivo models, which will confirm the potential health benefits of the compounds once incorporated into the food matrix. These studies should focus on evaluating relevant physiological effects, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, or antimicrobial activity, as well as their possible contribution to the prevention of chronic diseases (Tsoupras et al., 2024).

From a nutritional point of view, the incorporation of bioactive compounds should be evaluated considering their interaction with the components of the food matrix, their effect on the bioavailability of nutrients, and their contribution to the development of functional foods. In this context, advances in personalized nutrition, including approaches based on nutrigenomics, will enable the design of foods enriched with bioactive compounds targeted at specific population groups, optimizing their physiological benefits and their role in the prevention of chronic diseases (Kamdi et al., 2026; Lam et al., 2025).

Finally, it will be necessary to explore and diversify applications in specific products, such as ice cream, instant beverages, snacks, pasta, and sauces, as well as their integration into personalized diets, in order to broaden the impact of these compounds in the food sector.

Conclusion

Based on the 68 studies analyzed, the review highlights the importance of green extraction techniques for the sustainable recovery of bioactive compounds (phenols, flavonoids, anthocyanins, betalains, carotenoids) from agro-industrial waste. Methods such as ultrasound, microwaves, subcritical water, and supercritical fluids improve performance, preserve the quality of compounds, and reduce energy consumption and dependence on toxic solvents compared to conventional methods. This approach not only allows high-value compounds to be recovered, but also reduces environmental impact, thus aligning with the objectives of the 2030 Agenda. Furthermore, microencapsulation using polysaccharides and proteins increases the stability, release, and bioavailability of bioactive compounds. This has facilitated their favorable incorporation into food matrices (yogurts, hamburgers, cookies), where they not only improve functional value but also help extend product shelf life by reducing oxidation processes. The bibliometric analysis also revealed a sustained increase in scientific evidence, from one article in 2020 to 19 publications in 2025. Likewise, a transition in the research focus has been observed, from the extraction of bioactive compounds (2020–2022) to the protection of bioactive compounds through microencapsulation and biological validation (2023–2025), although these are still emerging fields.

Despite these advances, limitations remain that will need to be addressed in future studies, such as the standardization of methods through robust optimization of extraction conditions, the combination of extraction techniques, the development of more selective environmentally friendly solvents such as NADES, and the exploration of new microencapsulation technologies and materials. It is also essential to optimize the doses of bioactive compounds in food matrices, perform sensory evaluations, and conduct in vitro and in vivo studies to assess their health benefits in order to consolidate the development of functional foods.
