Levothyroxine supplementation and pregnancy outcomes in women with thyroid disorders: an umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Jing Wang, Jiashu Li, Jing Zhang, Aihua Liu, Wanyu Yang, Xiaodan Zhai, Weiping Teng, Yongze Li, Zhongyan Shan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSTUDY QUESTIONDoes levothyroxine (LT4) treatment reduce adverse pregnancy outcomes in pregnant women with thyroid dysfunction?SUMMARY ANSWERLT4 treatment significantly reduces the risks of pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and gestational hypertension, with no significant impacts on rates of live birth, placental abruption, or gestational diabetes.WHAT IS KNOWN ALREADYMultiple meta-analyses have examined the impact of LT4 on pregnancy outcomes, but quantitative confidence assessments are still lacking. Thus, an umbrella review is needed to systematically synthesize and assess the quality of this evidence.STUDY DESIGN, SIZE, DURATIONThis umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses used PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. Searches included studies published in English or Chinese up to 20 March 2025.PARTICIPANTS/MATERIALS, SETTING, METHODSIncluded studies were systematic reviews or meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials assessing the effects of LT4 on pregnancy outcomes in women with subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) or thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) positivity. A set of 24 associations across six pregnancy outcomes were analyzed: pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, live birth, placental abruption, gestational hypertension, and gestational diabetes. Methodological quality was assessed using AMSTAR 1, and evidence quality was graded using GRADE. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to confirm robustness.MAIN RESULTS AND THE ROLE OF CHANCEEleven meta-analyses were included. High-quality evidence showed that LT4 treatment reduced the risks of pregnancy loss (RR = 0.43; class III evidence), preterm delivery (RR = 0.56; class III evidence), and gestational hypertension (RR = 0.63; class IV evidence). Moderate-to-low-quality evidence indicated no significant impact on rates of live birth, placental abruption, or gestational diabetes. Of the 24 associations, 22 were rated as high confidence and two as medium confidence based on AMSTAR 1. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings, but treatment effects varied according to the study population, treatment timing, and method. For pregnancy loss and preterm delivery outcomes, the risk was significantly reduced only when LT4 treatment was initiated in early pregnancy (pregnancy loss: RR = 0.60, P = 0.03; RR = 0.59, P = 0.003; preterm labor: RR = 0.58, P < 0.0001; RR = 0.46, P < 0.00001). Additionally, women with TSH levels greater than 4.0 mU/l derived greater benefits from LT4 treatment compared to those with TSH levels between 2.5 and 4.0 mU/l.LIMITATIONS, REASONS FOR CAUTIONLimitations include small sample sizes, potential biases (selection and reporting), and language restrictions to English and Chinese studies, which may affect generalizability.WIDER IMPLICATIONS OF THE FINDINGSBy integrating high-quality evidence, this study confirms that LT4 can reduce the risk of pregnancy loss and preterm delivery in pregnant women with thyroid dysfunction, supports its clinical application in specific populations, such as SCH patients with positive TPOAb or TSH > 4.0 mU/l, and warns against the risk of overtreatment. The results of this study provide an evidence base for clinical precision intervention. Future studies should address the heterogeneity through standardized large studies, clarify the dose threshold, and evaluate the long-term health effects of LT4 on mothers and infants, to further improve the guidelines and optimize risk stratification management.STUDY FUNDING/COMPETING INTEREST(S)This work was supported by the National Key R&D Program of China (Grant No. 2023YFC2508300, 2023YFC2508303, and 2023YFC2508305), the National Science and Technology Major Project (Grant No. 2024ZD0533403), and the National Natural Science Foundation of China (Grant No. 82100831, 81570709, and 82470826). This umbrella review of previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses examined whether taking a medication called levothyroxine (LT4) during pregnancy improves pregnancy outcomes in pregnant women with abnormal thyroid function. Abnormal thyroid function, especially subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) and thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) positivity, may increase the risk of pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and other complications in pregnant women. Therefore, timely and effective treatment is essential to safeguard the health of mother and child.

The main finding of the study was that LT4 treatment significantly reduced the risk of pregnancy loss and preterm delivery, but had less effect on rates of live birth, placental abruption, and gestational diabetes. The effect of LT4 treatment was especially pronounced in pregnant women with high thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels (>4.0 mU/l). Studies have also shown that LT4 therapy is more effective when started in early pregnancy compared to when started before conception.

In addition, the researchers caution that care needs to be taken to avoid the risk of overtreatment when using LT4 therapy. Overtreatment may lead to high thyroid hormone levels, which may not only affect the pregnant woman’s own health but may also adversely affect fetal development. Therefore, it is crucial to regularly monitor thyroid hormone levels of pregnant women and adjust the dosage according to individual circumstances.

Introduction

Thyroid hormones play a vital role during pregnancy and are essential for the healthy growth of the fetus and placenta. In particular, in the early stages of pregnancy, the development of the fetus and placenta depends primarily on the thyroid hormone levels in the mother’s body (Korevaar et al., 2017). Increasing research evidence shows that maternal subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) and thyroid autoimmunity during pregnancy, especially thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) positivity, may have adverse effects on pregnancy outcomes and on the healthy growth of children (Korevaar et al., 2016). SCH is more common in pregnant women and is characterized by elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels, whereas free thyroxine (FT4) levels remain normal (Stryhn et al., 2023). SCH is estimated to occur in 3.5–18% of all pregnancies (Stagnaro-Green et al., 2011; Bath and Rayman, 2012), affecting ∼2–6% of women of childbearing age and pregnant women (Casey et al., 2005). Thyroid autoimmunity (TAI) is usually characterized by the presence of TPOAbs and/or thyroglobulin antibodies (TgAbs) (Tańska et al., 2023). Studies have shown that the prevalence of TPOAb positivity in early pregnancy is 5–15% (Moreno-Reyes et al., 2013). The risk of miscarriage in TAI patients is two to three times greater than that in the general population (Chen and Hu, 2011; Thangaratinam et al., 2011), and the proportion of women with TAI who have experienced multiple miscarriages is significantly greater, reaching 25% (Iravani et al., 2008; Toulis et al., 2010). In addition, SCH and TAI are associated with an increased risk of multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as preterm delivery, gestational diabetes, pregnancy loss, placental abruption, and neonatal death (Karakosta et al., 2012; Pop et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2014; Negro and Stagnaro-Green, 2014; Sheehan et al., 2015; Maraka et al., 2016; Ying et al., 2016; Thompson et al., 2018), so timely identification and treatment of thyroid dysfunction is critical to safeguard maternal and infant health. In response to these problems, levothyroxine (LT4), a synthetic thyroid hormone for T4, is used to supplement or replace deficient thyroid hormones in the body (Alexander et al., 2004; Upadhyay et al., 2023). LT4 is converted in the body into the more active form, triiodothyronine (T3), which helps regulate metabolic activity and maintain thyroid hormone levels in pregnant women (Antonelli et al., 2021), thereby helping to reduce these risks and safeguard maternal and child health.

Several studies have confirmed that treatment with LT4 can significantly improve pregnancy outcomes for pregnant women with SCH. In particular, there is a broad consensus with respect to the effect of LT4 on reducing the risk of pregnancy loss and preterm delivery (Rao et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2022; Provinciatto et al., 2024). Although studies have shown some positive effects of LT4 in preventing hypertension during pregnancy, there is insufficient evidence on its role in reducing the risk of complications such as gestational diabetes mellitus and placental abruption (Aromataris et al., 2015; Bein et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2021). In addition, some studies have indicated that pregnant women may have an increased need for LT4 during pregnancy, and inappropriate dose adjustments may have adverse effects on maternal and fetal health (Abalovich et al., 2007; Lazarus, 2011; Shinohara et al., 2018; Kahaly and Gottwald-Hostalek, 2022). In summary, more high-quality studies on the specific effects of LT4 on pregnancy outcomes are still required to provide more definitive guidance and recommendations.

In studies evaluating the effects of LT4 treatment on pregnancy outcomes in women with thyroid disorders during pregnancy, the results of previous randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have been pooled to some extent through systematic reviews and meta-analyses, but quantitative confidence assessments are lacking. The aim of this review was to provide a comprehensive assessment of the available evidence on the association between LT4 treatment and various pregnancy outcomes through an umbrella review (UR) of systematic reviews and meta-analyses. The sample size, association strength, and risk of bias were investigated, and the strength of evidence was graded and ranked (Aromataris et al., 2015). This study is expected to provide clinicians with more precise treatment guidelines to improve pregnancy outcomes in pregnant women with thyroid dysfunction.

Methods

We conducted an UR and collected and evaluated multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses investigating the relationships between LT4 and various pregnancy outcomes. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al., 2009; Shamseer et al., 2015; Gates et al., 2022) were followed to ensure the transparent and comprehensive reporting of the results of our UR. In addition, we registered the protocol for this UR in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, CRD: 42024586105).

Literature search

We conducted searches in the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane systematic review databases from inception until 20 March 2025. We used a combination of keywords related to pregnant women, SCH, TPOAb, thyroid autoimmunity, thyroid disorders, levothyroxine, and meta-analysis. For more information about the search policies, see Supplementary Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

Two trained evaluators independently screened the titles and abstracts retrieved from the databases and screened the full texts to identify those that met the inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies in the literature screening were resolved by a third evaluator.

Studies were included according to the population, exposure, comparison, outcome, study design (PICOS) strategy: 1. Population: pregnant women with thyroid disorders; 2. Exposure: LT4 treatment before pregnancy and/or started in early pregnancy (at least within 20 weeks of pregnancy); 3. Comparison: the control group included women with thyroid disorders who were not treated with LT4 or who were treated with placebo; 4. Outcome: the main outcomes were pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and live births; secondary outcomes included placental abruption, gestational hypertension, and gestational diabetes; and 5. Study design: systematic review or meta-analysis of RCTs.

Studies were excluded if they met the following criteria: 1. observational, laboratory, or animal study; 2. systematic evaluation without quantitative analyses; 3. failure to report comprehensive data that can be reanalyzed, such as the effect size (hazard ratio, relative hazard ratio (RR), odds ratio (OR), 95% CI); 4. systematic review or meta-analysis of RCTs containing fewer than three studies; 5. systematic review or meta-analysis published in languages other than Chinese and English; and 6. study sample size that was too small.

When faced with two or more systematic evaluations containing overlapping meta-analyses, we preferred the systematic evaluation that included the most original studies or, if these systematic evaluations included the same number of original studies, the one with the largest sample size was included to maximize statistical validity and reduce the risk of bias that might arise from the repeated inclusion of the same original studies (Senn, 2009; Pieper et al., 2014; Neuenschwander et al., 2019).

Data extraction

Two trained evaluators independently collected information on each eligible study. All differences were resolved via consultation with a senior evaluator (Y.Z.L.). The information extracted from each eligible systematic review and meta-analysis included the first author, year of publication, number of included studies, population, mean age, number of participants, intervention, duration of intervention, comparison, and type of pregnancy outcome. From each association included, we extracted data on the pregnancy outcome, intervention, study design, number of included studies, meta-analysis measures, specific risk estimates and corresponding 95% CIs, and heterogeneity. We also extracted and tabulated the LT4 dosing regimen, timing of intervention, TSH target values, and post-treatment thyroid function for each of the original RCT studies.

Data analysis

When processing data from qualified meta-analyses, we extracted data for each association from the original study and applied a random effects model to recalculate the adjusted aggregate effect size and its corresponding 95% CI (Lau et al., 1997). In each meta-analysis, we assessed interstudy heterogeneity via the I2 statistic, which ranged from 0% to 100%. When the I2 value exceeded 50%, moderate to significant heterogeneity was considered; if the I2 value exceeded 75%, high heterogeneity was considered (Higgins et al., 2003). In addition, we divided all the included participants into two groups: the SCH group and the TPOAb-positive group (Supplementary Figs S1, S2, and S3). For the primary outcome, subgroup analyses were performed based on the timing of intervention (pre-conception or early pregnancy). Furthermore, women with SCH were further divided into subgroups according to their TSH levels: 2.5–4.0 and >4.0 mU/l. To evaluate the robustness of the results, we also conducted sensitivity analyses, excluding studies with a high risk of bias (Dechartres et al., 2014). In this study, statistical analysis was performed via Stata version 16.0 and REVMAN version 5.4.

Assessment of the quality of evidence and methods

In this study, we adopted the Grading of Recommendation, Evaluation, Development and Evaluation grading (GRADE) system to evaluate the quality of evidence and divided it into four grades: high, medium, low, and very low (Guyatt et al., 2011; Mercuri and Gafni, 2018). Initially, we rated the quality of evidence from RCTs as high. However, the quality of evidence rating could be reduced by factors such as risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, a lack of accuracy, or publication bias. If the proportion of studies scoring less than six points on the Newcastle–Ottawa scale exceeded 50%, a risk of bias was indicated. When the sample size was insufficient or the number of events was less than 300, we considered the results inaccurate. The diversity of the study population led to indirectness. When the I2 statistic was more than 50%, we considered the results inconsistent. An I2 value of more than 75% indicated high heterogeneity. In addition, if the funnel plot was asymmetrical and the P-value of the Egger test was less than or equal to 0.10, we considered that the study may have publication bias (Guyatt et al., 2008).

We used A MeaSurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) to assess the quality of the systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Shea et al., 2009). AMSTAR consists of 11 items, with each receiving one point for a ‘yes’ answer and zero points for any other answer. On the basis of the scores, we classified studies as low quality (0–3), medium quality (4–7), or high quality (8–11). In addition, we categorized the outcome evidence according to established criteria: class I (corroborative), class II (strongly suggestive), class III (suggestive), class IV (nonsubstantive), and NS (not significant) (Ioannidis, 2009; Huang et al., 2023; Lane et al., 2024). Results

Study selection

A total of 253 records were identified through four electronic databases. After the titles and abstracts were filtered, 29 records remained. After full-text screening, 18 records were excluded (Supplementary Table S2), and 11 (Rao et al., 2019; Bein et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2021; Lau et al., 2021; Di Girolamo et al., 2022; Jiao et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023; Provinciatto et al., 2024; Sankoda et al., 2024) articles were included (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of the study selection process.

Basic information included in the meta-analysis

A total of 24 associations across the 11 meta-analyses were included between 2019 and 2025 (Table 2), covering a wide range of pregnancy outcomes, including pregnancy loss (n = 9), preterm delivery (n = 10), live birth (n = 2), placental abruption (n = 1), gestational hypertension (n = 1), and gestational diabetes (n = 1) (Table 3). Statistically significant associations were observed among the pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and gestational hypertension outcomes. These associations were related primarily to two thyroid disorder states: SCH and TPOAb positivity.

Characteristics of LT4 interventions

Among the 21 original RCT studies included in the systematic reviews and meta-analyses, the dosage strategies, timing of intervention, TSH targets, and outcome performance of LT4 interventions were as follows (Supplementary Table S3).

In terms of dosing strategy, nine studies used a fixed initial LT4 dose (25–100 μg/day), with 50 μg/day being the most common starting dose (Zhang et al., 2016; Casey et al., 2017; Li and Dai, 2018; Bai, 2019; Dhillon-Smith et al., 2019; Shu et al., 2019; Wang and Chen, 2020; Wang et al., 2021; Leng et al., 2022). The other 12 studies used a dynamic dosing strategy; of these, nine studies adjusted the dose according to pre-treatment TSH levels or specific gestational week targets (Negro et al., 2006, 2016; Rahman et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2016; Nazarpour et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2018; Alcázar Lázaro et al., 2019). For example, Negro et al. (2006) adjusted the dose to 0.5–1.0 μg/kg/day based on baseline TSH levels and Alcázar Lázaro et al. (2019) adjusted ±12.5 μg based on TSH fluctuations. The remaining three studies based the dose on body weight (Negro et al., 2005; Nazarpour et al., 2018; van Dijk et al., 2022).

In terms of timing of intervention, pre-conception initiation was mainly used in women who were TPOAb positive and have infertility/IVF or recurrent miscarriage (RPL). In contrast, initiating an intervention in early pregnancy (≤12 weeks) is standard practice in the management of SCH. Treatment usually continues throughout pregnancy (19 studies), but the study by Wang et al. stopped treatment at 28 weeks of gestation (Wang et al., ​​2020​​).

In terms of TSH targets and outcomes, TSH targets were usually more stringent in the first trimester (<2.5 mU/l in nine studies), whereas TSH thresholds were relaxed in late pregnancy (<3.0 mU/l in seven studies). Post-treatment TSH attainment rates varied by dosing strategy: dynamic dosing regimens had lower mean TSH levels, whereas fixed-dose studies occasionally showed residual TSH elevations (Nazarpour et al., 2018 at 3.8 mU/l in the first trimester). Notably, in the study by Casey et al. (2017), 93% of participants reached the target range (0.1–2.5 mU/l) by mid-pregnancy.

Methodological quality of the included meta-analyses

The AMSTAR scores for the included articles ranged from 7 to 10 (out of a total of 11), with a median of 9, including 10 (91%) high-quality articles and one (9%) medium-quality article (Fig. 2).

Summary findings and heterogeneity of the included meta-analyses

Across the 24 associations, 7 associations (29%) had statistically significant effects (P < 0.05). Among these associations, 3 (43%) fitted the statistical significance principle of P < 10−3. Four (17%) and two (8%) associations had significant heterogeneity estimates (50% < I2 ≤ 75%) and considerable heterogeneity estimates (I2 > 75%), respectively (Table 3).

Quality of evidence included in the meta-analyses

An assessment of the certainty of the evidence via the GRADE framework revealed that 8 (33%) and 10 (42%) associations were supported by high- and moderate-quality evidence, and 4 (21%) and 2 (8%) associations were supported by low- and very-low-quality evidence, respectively (Table 3), with the most common reason for downgrades being imprecision. Of these associations of higher quality evidence, seven were statistically significant, including the following: LT4 reduced the risk of pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and gestational hypertension compared with no intervention or placebo.

Pregnancy loss

Pregnancy loss, also known as miscarriage or spontaneous abortion, refers to the unintentional termination of a pregnancy before the fetus has reached a viable stage, specifically before the 24th week of gestation (Yang et al., 2024). However, in the eligible studies included in this work, there were discrepancies in the specific definitions of miscarriage, which include losses at 13, 20, 22 weeks, and other gestational ages. To maintain consistency within this study, we will collectively refer to these miscarriages under various definitions as ‘pregnancy loss’. Of the nine associations supported by moderate to high-quality evidence, four of the high-quality evidence (Rao et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2022; Provinciatto et al., 2024; Sankoda et al., 2024) and one of moderate quality evidence (Yu et al., 2023) were statistically significant (Fig. 3). Compared with placebo or no treatment, LT4 supplementation significantly reduced the risk of pregnancy loss in pregnant women with SCH or TPOAb-positive women without significant thyroid dysfunction (RR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.32–0.57, P < 0.001, I2 = 0, high, class III; RR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.42–0.79, P = 0.0005, I2 = 22%, high, class III; RR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.52–0.91, P < 0.01, I2 = 0, high, class IV; RR: 0.64, 95% CI: 0.45–0.01, P = 0.01, I2 = 14, high, class IV; RR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.41–0.94, P = 0.02, I2=0, moderate, class IV). Four moderating, non-significant lines of evidence showed that supplementation with LT4 did not significantly reduce the risk of pregnancy loss in pregnant women with thyroid disease compared with placebo or no treatment (RR: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.47–1.01, P = 0.06, I2=0, moderate, class V; RR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.76–1.14, P = 0.49, I2=0, moderate, class V; RR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.69–1.05, P = 0.14, I2=1, moderate, class V; RR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.18–1.08, P = 0.07, I2=0, moderate, class V) (Fig. 3) (Bein et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2021; Lau et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023). Subgroup analyses revealed significant differences in the impact of treatment timing on outcomes among women with SCH and TPOAb positivity. Initiation of LT4 treatment during early pregnancy significantly reduced the risk of pregnancy loss (SCH: RR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.37–0.95, P = 0.03; TPOAb positive: RR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.42–0.84, P = 0.003). In contrast, pre-conception intervention did not demonstrate a significant improvement (SCH: RR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.39–1.15, P = 0.15; TPOAb positive: RR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.69–1.07, P = 0.17) (Figs 4–6).

Preterm delivery

Preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 gestational weeks, is a leading cause of neonatal and infant morbidity and mortality (Mitrogiannis et al., 2023). Ten studies evaluated the effect of LT4 on preterm delivery (three high-quality, three medium-quality, and four low-quality studies). However, only one difference was statistically significant (Fig. 3). This study revealed that supplementation with LT4 significantly reduced the risk of preterm delivery for pregnant women with SCH compared with placebo or no treatment (RR: 0.56, 95% CI: 0.43–0.73, P < 0.001, I2=0, high, class III) (Jiao et al., 2022). Compared with placebo or no treatment, supplementation with LT4 did not significantly reduce the risk of preterm delivery in pregnant women with thyroid disease in nine studies (Fig. 3) (Rao et al., 2019; Bein et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2021; Lau et al., 2021; Di Girolamo et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023). Subgroup analyses revealed significant differences in the impact of treatment timing on outcomes among women with SCH and TPOAb positivity. Initiation of LT4 treatment during early pregnancy significantly reduced the risk of preterm delivery (SCH: RR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.46–0.74, P < 0.0001; TPOAb positive: RR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.33–0.65, P < 0.00001). In contrast, pre-conception intervention did not demonstrate a significant improvement (SCH: RR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.72–1.75, P = 0.60; TPOAb positive: RR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.74–1.84, P = 0.50)) (Figs 4–6).

Live birth

When the effect of LT4 on improving live birth rates was assessed, relevant data were provided by two studies, with quality ratings of moderate and very low, respectively. No statistically significant differences were detected in live birth rates (RR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.99–1.03, P = 0.29, I2=0, moderate, class V; RR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.97–1.13, P = 0.21, I2=89, very low, class V) (Provinciatto et al., 2024; Sankoda et al., 2024) among women treated with LT4 and controls (Fig. 3). Subgroup analyses showed that LT4 treatment before and during pregnancy did not show a significant effect in improving the live birth rate in either women with SCH and TPOAb positive (SCH: RR = 1.41, 95% CI: 0.84–2.36, P = 0.20; RR = 1.01, 95%CI: 0.99–1.02, P = 0.26; TPOAb: RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.88–1.13, P = 0.99; RR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.97–1.29, P = 0.14) (Figs 4–6).

Preliminary results regarding pregnancy complications

The available evidence on the effect of LT4 on other pregnancy complications is mainly based on a single meta-analysis each. For placental abruption, a study by Bein et al. (2021) showed that LT4 intervention did not significantly reduce the risk of its occurrence (RR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.18–1.97, I2=81, P = 0.40, very low, class V). In another study, Jiao et al. (2022) showed that supplementation with LT4 significantly reduced the incidence of gestational hypertension (RR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.47–0.84, P = 0.002, I2=42%, high, class IV), but the effect on gestational diabetes mellitus was not statistically significance (RR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.51–1.25, P = 0.32, I2=34%, moderate, class V) (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis by TSH levels (2.5–4.0 and >4.0 mU/l)

Subgroup analyses revealed that in both subgroups with TSH levels of 2.5–4.0 and >4.0 mU/l, LT4 treatment during early pregnancy significantly reduced the risk of preterm delivery (RR = 0.66, P = 0.03; RR = 0.53, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5). Although the test for subgroup differences did not show a significant difference between the two groups regarding pregnancy loss outcomes (I2=65.9%, P = 0.09), the potential positive effect of LT4 treatment on pregnancy loss appeared to be more pronounced in SCH studies with TSH levels >4.0 mU/l than in those with TSH levels of 2.5–4.0 mU/l. In sensitivity analyses excluding studies with a high risk of bias, a significant difference in live birth rate was observed between the subgroups with TSH levels of 2.5–4.0 and >4.0 mU/l (I2=80.5%, P = 0.02). In the subgroup with TSH levels >4.0 mU/l, LT4 treatment significantly increased the live birth rate compared with the control group (RR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.07–4.21; P = 0.03).

Sensitivity analyses

We performed a sensitivity analysis via the GRADE system for studies with evidence rated above medium quality (Supplementary Tables S4, S5, S6, and S7). After studies with a higher risk of bias were excluded, a total of 18 associations were eligible for analysis. These sensitivity analyses evaluated multiple pregnancy outcomes, including pregnancy loss (n = 9), preterm delivery (n = 6), gestational hypertension (n = 1), gestational diabetes (n = 1), live birth (n = 1). Specifically, in terms of pregnancy loss rates, the LT4 intervention had a significant protective effect, which remained stable even after excluding studies with a high risk of bias, with a change in the RR value from 0.58 to 0.49 (P = 0.0005, high, class III), and another set of data showed a change in the RR value from 0.43 to 0.36 (P = 0.06, moderate, class V), although no significant difference was found in this study. In terms of preterm birth rates, after studies with a high risk of bias were excluded, two pieces of moderate evidence revealed that the LT4 intervention did not reduce the risk of preterm delivery (RR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.48–1.22, P = 0.26, moderate, class V; RR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.19–1.09, P = 0.08, moderate, class V). The effect size (RR) and level of significance (P-value) of the association of LT4 with other pregnancy outcomes changed only slightly, and the level of heterogeneity indicated by the I2 statistic was relatively low, suggesting good consistency in the findings. However, the insufficient number of remaining studies limited further meta-analysis.

Subgroup specific analyses

After further excluding original RCTs with high risk of bias, subgroup analysis showed that LT4 treatment significantly reduced the risk of pregnancy loss in TPOAb-positive pregnant women compared to the control group (RR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.66–0.97, P = 0.02) but did not reduce the risk of pregnancy loss in women with SCH (RR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.20–2.32, P = 0.54). In addition, no significant associations were found between LT4 treatment and rates of preterm delivery (RR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.54–1.13, P = 0.19; RR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.37–1.15, P = 0.14) and live births (RR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.95–1.15, P = 0.34; RR = 1.10, 95% CI: 0.83–1.46, P = 0.53) in both SCH and TPOAb-positive pregnant women (Supplementary Figs S4, S5, S6, and S7).

Discussion

Main findings and explanations

The findings of this study suggest that LT4 treatment has significant benefits for pregnant women with SCH or TPOAb positivity, reducing the risk of pregnancy loss and preterm birth. A meta-analysis revealed that LT4 supplementation reduced the risk of pregnancy loss by 43% (RR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.32–0.57, P < 0.001, class III). Similarly, high-quality evidence revealed that LT4 supplementation significantly reduced the risk of preterm delivery (RR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.43–0.73, P < 0.001, class III), with low heterogeneity. Specifically, among women with SCH and TPOAb positive, LT4 significantly reduced the risk of pregnancy loss and preterm delivery, but only when LT4 treatment was initiated during early pregnancy (pregnancy loss: RR = 0.60, P = 0.03; RR = 0.59, P = 0.003; preterm delivery: RR = 0.58, P < 0.0001; RR = 0.46, P < 0.00001). In contrast, pre-conception intervention showed no significant benefit. Our supplementary data suggest that pre-conception LT4 initiation may be prioritized for TPOAb-positive women or those with recurrent pregnancy loss, as early pregnancy intervention aligns with critical windows of embryogenesis (Shahbazi, 2020). In terms of live birth rates, no significant improvement was observed overall (RR = 1.04, P = 0.16). However, in sensitivity analyses excluding studies with a high risk of bias, a significant increase in live birth rates was seen in the subgroup with TSH levels >4.0 mU/l, where LT4 treatment significantly improved live birth rates compared with the control group (RR = 2.13, P = 0.03). In contrast, no change in live birth rates was observed in the subgroup with TSH levels of 2.5–4.0 mU/l (RR = 0.93, P = 0.50), suggesting that the therapeutic effect may be TSH-dependent, which is consistent with the threshold effect conclusion by Sankoda et al. (2024).

Notably, LT4 showed a significant effect on reducing the risk of gestational hypertension (RR = 0.63, P = 0.002), but had no significant effect on the risk of gestational diabetes (RR = 0.80, P = 0.32) or placental abruption (RR = 0.60, P = 0.40). However these results are derived largely from isolated studies, and the evidence was of variable quality (GRADE: very low to high), making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions. Therefore, further validation by multicenter RCTs with standardized endpoints is urgently needed to clarify the role of LT4 in these complications.

Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of LT4 in pregnancy loss outcomes, but the significant heterogeneity observed in preterm delivery and live birth outcomes suggests that differences in study populations, intervention timing, or research designs may influence efficacy. Notably, the differential effects based on thyroid autoimmunity status and therapeutic windows may relate to the mechanisms of LT4 in modulating immune dysregulation and optimizing thyroid function during critical gestational stages, underscoring the necessity of individualized treatment strategies for high-risk subgroups. Future research should systematically investigate sources of heterogeneity (sample characteristics, intervention timing), refine risk stratification, and explore precise intervention timing to enhance the reliability and clinical translational value of findings.

Overtreatment concerns

When treating pregnant women with thyroid disease, overtreatment is a particular concern. Initial laboratory tests have shown that many patients with SCH can return to normal thyroid function after repeat tests (Fan et al., 2019; Yamamoto et al., 2020). Therefore, in assessing the effect of LT4 treatment, we must not only focus on its clinically significant protective effect but also be vigilant about the potential risks of overtreatment.

Overtreatment increases the risk of iatrogenic thyrotoxicosis, which can lead to serious maternal and fetal complications (Thayakaran et al., 2019). Specifically, cardiovascular dysfunction and osteoporosis are direct risks. High doses of LT4 can exacerbate maternal thyroid hormone abnormalities and increase the risk of preterm birth, gestational hypertension, and fetal neurodevelopmental disorders. In addition, maternal endocrine disorders can affect fetal hormone balance through the placenta, leading to metabolic abnormalities during development. Notably, overtreatment can also lead to overmedicalization of borderline cases, which not only increases the consumption of medical resources but may also cause unnecessary psychological burden and iatrogenic harm to patients.

To reduce the risk of overtreatment, the six original RCTs included in our study adopted a dynamic dose-adjustment strategy (such as increasing the dose by 12.5–25 μg each time based on TSH monitoring) to optimize LT4 treatment (Negro et al., 2016). However, the lack of a standardized protocol highlights the need for personalized algorithms in future guidelines. To avoid overtreatment, pregnant women receiving LT4 should have their serum TSH levels checked every 4–6 weeks during the first trimester until the second trimester. Thereafter, monitoring should be performed at least once more at around 30 weeks of gestation (Alexander et al., 2017). The dose and timing of treatment should be dynamically adjusted according to the individual patient’s condition (thyroid hormone levels and immune status) to balance efficacy and risk.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first UR to systematically assess the associations between LT4 treatment and various pregnancy outcomes, integrating the results of an existing meta-analysis of RCTs to provide a comprehensive assessment of the impact of treatment with LT4 in women with thyroid disorders during pregnancy. This approach improves the statistical validity and enhances the generalizability and applicability of the findings. The registration of the study protocol and the application of the AMSTAR and GRADE tools and classification (evidence classification criteria) further ensured the transparency of the study design, the methodological quality, and the reliability of the evidence, providing a solid scientific basis for clinical decision-making. To further increase the reliability of the results, we excluded studies with a high risk of bias, performed sensitivity analyses on studies with moderate- to high-quality evidence, and found that the quality of most of the evidence remained unchanged, suggesting that the results are somewhat robust. Moreover, this review advances prior literature by conducting stratified analyses based on thyroid status (SCH versus TPOAb positivity) and intervention timing (pre-conception versus early pregnancy), revealing critical nuances.

Despite these strengths, several limitations are worth considering. Small sample sizes in the study may reduce statistical power for rare outcomes. The inclusion of studies published only in English and Chinese raises concerns about regional and publication bias, potentially excluding relevant evidence from other populations. Furthermore, despite our efforts to prioritize larger-scale or more comprehensive analyses, the overlap of RCTs across different meta-analyses may have resulted in disproportionate weighting of certain trials. Although this review highlighted the risk of overtreatment, there was insufficient evidence to determine the optimal LT4 dosage, TSH monitoring intervals, or long-term maternal and neonatal health outcomes. This underscores the need for standardized protocols and extended follow-up periods in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this UR showed that LT4 treatment significantly reduces the risks of pregnancy loss, preterm delivery, and gestational hypertension in pregnant women with thyroid dysfunction, with no significant impact on live birth rates, placental abruption, or gestational diabetes. However, these effects vary depending on the study population, timing of treatment, and methodology. Subgroup analysis revealed that initiating LT4 treatment during early pregnancy provides greater benefits than starting pre-conception. Additionally, women with TSH levels greater than 4.0 mU/l derive greater benefits from LT4 treatment compared to those with TSH levels between 2.5 and 4.0 mU/l. Given these findings, it is important to exercise caution to avoid the risk of overtreatment. Regular monitoring of TSH levels is crucial, and dosages should be adjusted based on individual circumstances.
