Alpha‐Globin Gene Triplication in Central Thailand: Prevalence, Haematological Findings and Clinical Impact With Co‐Inherited Beta‐Globin Mutations
Authors: Boonyanuch Dujjawan, Yingyong Chinthammitr, Theera Ruchutrakool, Chattree Hantaweepant
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesWe aimed to ascertain the prevalence of αααanti‐3.7 and αααanti‐4.2 triplications in central Thailand and to characterise the phenotypes of individuals harbouring these variants.Materials and MethodsWe performed a retrospective review of adult (≥ 18 years) samples submitted for polymerase chain reaction‐based α‐globin mutation analysis at Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok (January 2012–December 2021). Haematological indices were compared between carriers and non‐carriers of α‐globin triplications, and clinical severity was assessed in those with co‐inherited β‐globin mutations.ResultsAmong 1559 subjects, α‐globin triplication prevalence was 1.28%, with αααanti‐4.2 (0.83%) more frequent than αααanti‐3.7 (0.45%). Double heterozygosity for an α triplication and a β‐globin mutation occurred in 0.9%. Individuals carrying αααanti‐3.7 or αααanti‐4.2 had lower mean haemoglobin levels (10.00 and 11.23 ± 1.85 g/dL, respectively) than those without triplication (12.35 ± 2.59 g/dL). Among heterozygous β‐thalassaemia individuals, co‐inheritance of αααanti‐3.7 or αααanti‐4.2 was associated with significantly reduced mean haemoglobin (8.38 ± 1.58 and 8.28 ± 1.15 g/dL, respectively). This contrasts with the non‐triplication group (11.14 ± 1.86 g/dL). Of 14 subjects with double heterozygosity, 8 had thalassaemia intermedia and 6 had thalassaemia trait.ConclusionsAlthough uncommon in central Thailand, α‐globin triplications can exacerbate anaemia in individuals with co‐inherited heterozygous β‐globin mutations, supporting routine screening in symptomatic cases. Trial Registration: The authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.Keywords: anti‐3.7 alpha‐gene triplications, anti‐4.2 alpha‐gene triplications, beta‐thalassaemia trait, double heterozygote, homozygous Hb E, Thailand

Introduction

Thalassaemia is one of the most common inherited autosomal recessive haemolytic anaemias worldwide. It arises from defects in haemoglobin (Hb) synthesis. In normal red blood cells, 2 α‐like and 2 β‐like globin chains combine with haems to form Hb, the primary molecule responsible for oxygen transport. Thalassaemia is categorised into two main types according to the specific globin mutation: α‐thalassaemia and β‐thalassaemia. Mutations in these α‐ or β‐globin genes reduce or abolish synthesis of the respective globin chains. The resulting imbalance leads to the accumulation and precipitation of excess unpaired globin on the red blood cell membrane, causing ineffective erythropoiesis, haemolytic anaemia, iron overload and related complications. Thalassaemia is highly prevalent in Thailand. Approximately, 20%–30% of the population carries the α‐thalassaemia trait, 3%–9% the β‐thalassaemia trait, 10%–53% the Hb E trait and 1%–8% the Hb Constant Spring trait [1].

Mutations in the α‐globin genes are classified as deletional or non‐deletional, with deletional mutations being more prevalent. A deletional mutation affecting both α‐globin genes on the same chromosome abolishes α‐globin chain production. This condition is known as α0‐thalassaemia or α‐thalassaemia 1 heterozygote (– –/αα), as illustrated by the Southeast Asia (– –SEA/αα), Mediterranean (– –MED/αα) and Filipino (– –FIL/αα) deletions. In contrast, a single‐gene deletion reduces α‐globin synthesis. This condition is termed α⁺‐thalassaemia or α‐thalassaemia 2 heterozygote (–α/αα), which includes the 3.7 kilobase (kb) deletion (α3.7) and the 4.2 kb deletion (α4.2). In Thailand, the 3.7 kb and 4.2 kb deletion alleles occur at approximately 25.1% and 0.8%, respectively [2].

On a single chromosome, α‐globin genes are duplicated and embedded within two highly homologous units known as the X, Y and Z boxes. The development of α⁺‐thalassaemia arises from unequal crossing over between misaligned homologous segments in meiosis. A crossover between homologous Z boxes produces the 3.7 kb deletion (α3.7) allele and its reciprocal anti‐3.7 α‐globin gene triplication (αααanti3.7), referred to as ‘rightward crossover.’ Conversely, a crossover between homologous X boxes yields the 4.2 kb deletion (α4.2) allele and its reciprocal anti‐4.2 α‐globin gene triplication (αααanti4.2), termed ‘leftward crossover’ [3].

Recent studies, although limited, have reported the prevalence of α‐globin gene triplications. Among these, αααanti3.7 is documented more frequently than αααanti4.2. Specifically, αααanti3.7 has been observed in approximately 0.04% of Sardinians (Italy), 5% of Greek Cypriots, 1.2% of Southern Chinese, 2.7% of North Indians, [4, 5] 1.39% of Iranians [6] and 10% of Mexicans [7]. By contrast, the prevalence of αααanti4.2 is about 0.523% in the Chinese population [8]. Two large Iranian studies, comprising 4010 [9] and 6404 individuals [6], reported no cases of αααanti4.2, although αααanti3.7 was identified in 1.7% and 1.39% of those cohorts, respectively.

α‐Globin gene triplications are considered rare because these mutations do not confer malarial resistance, offer no selective advantage and are thus likely to have been lost over time [10]. Nonetheless, α‐globin gene triplication can worsen the clinical severity of β‐thalassaemia. When co‐inherited with heterozygous β‐thalassaemia, it may exacerbate the imbalance between α‐ and β‐globin chains by increasing the excess of α‐globin, impairing erythroid maturation and cell survival [3, 8, 11] and potentially transforming the typically asymptomatic phenotype of β‐thalassaemia heterozygotes into clinically apparent thalassaemia. Although α‐globin gene triplication can intensify disease severity in individuals with a heterozygous β‐globin mutation, it is difficult to detect. It cannot be identified through a complete blood count or Hb typing, and therefore requires genetic testing for confirmation.

In Thailand, research on α‐globin gene triplications is relatively limited and was undertaken several years ago with small sample sizes. Early investigations carried out between 1987 and 1997, when the Thai population was approximately 52 to 60 million [12], reported a prevalence ranging from about 0.25% to 2.83% across populations in the Northern, Northeastern, Southern and Central regions [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Some studies reported only the αααanti3.7 triplication [14, 15, 16], whereas others did not specify which types were identified [13, 17]. Therefore, the prevalence of the αααanti4.2 triplication in Thailand has not been previously documented. Consequently, the current study aimed to investigate the prevalence of both αααanti3.7 and αααanti4.2 in individuals from Central Thailand, using a larger sample size in the context of a growing national population, now estimated at approximately 71 million. This substantial population growth, along with increased migration may have influenced the current distribution of α‐globin gene triplications [12]. Furthermore, it sought to examine the clinical manifestations of patients who carry co‐inherited β‐globin gene mutations and α‐globin gene triplications.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

The study protocol was approved by the Siriraj Hospital Institutional Review Board (reference: Si 279/2566) and conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. This retrospective investigation took place between January 2012 and December 2021 at the Division of Haematology, Department of Medicine, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand.

The inclusion criteria encompassed adults (≥ 18 years) whose blood samples were submitted for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing to detect α‐globin gene mutations for various indications. These included screening couples at risk of having a child with thalassaemia major, evaluating healthy individuals with low mean corpuscular volume (MCV) who were suspected of carrying a thalassaemia trait, or investigating anaemic patients presumed to have thalassaemia disease. PCR testing routinely targeted seven deletional α‐globin mutations (–α3.7, –α4.2, – –SEA, – –FIL, – –MED, ‐(α)20.5 and – –THAI), as well as anti‐3.7 and anti‐4.2 α‐globin gene triplications. Non‐deletional α‐globin mutations, such as Hb Constant Spring and Hb Pakse, were not included in this panel. Both healthy individuals and patients with complete results for α‐globin mutations, complete blood count and Hb typing were eligible. In subjects carrying double heterozygosity for α‐globin gene triplications and β‐globin mutations, additional information was obtained, including clinical characteristics and the specific β‐globin gene mutation, if available.

DNA Analysis for α‐Globin Gene Triplication

DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using the Gentra Puregene Blood Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The identification of anti‐3.7 and anti‐4.2 α‐gene triplications was carried out by multiplex PCR [18], modifying the initial denaturation step from 96°C to 95°C. PCR was performed on a Veriti thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems) with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 15 min. This was followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 45 s, annealing at 60°C for 90 s, and extension at 72°C for 135 s. The process concluded with a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. Primer sequences have been described previously [3].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic and laboratory data. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations for normally distributed data or as medians with interquartile ranges (Q 1, Q 3) for non‐normally distributed data. The normality of variable distributions was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Comparisons of categorical variables between patients with and without α‐globin gene triplications were made using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. For continuous variables, student's t‐test was applied to normally distributed data, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non‐normally distributed data. A 2‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using PASW Statistics, version 18 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Prevalence of α‐Globin Gene Triplications

This 10‐year retrospective study included 1559 subjects from central Thailand whose blood samples were tested for deletional α‐globin mutations and α‐globin gene triplications. The overall prevalence of heterozygous α‐globin gene triplications was 1.28% (20/1559). Among these, 0.83% (13/1559) had αααanti4.2 (69% female), and 0.45% (7/1559) had αααanti3.7 (71% female). No cases of homozygous or compound heterozygous α‐globin triplications were identified. All positive individuals resided in the central region of Thailand, apart from one who lived in the Southern region. When considering the results of Hb typing and deletional α‐globin mutations, heterozygous α‐globin gene triplications were found to co‐occur with other α‐gene mutations in 0.19% (3/1559) and with β‐gene mutations in 0.9% (14/1559) of the overall cohort (Table 1).

Among individuals with heterozygous β‐thalassaemia (Hb typing A2A, A2 > 3.5%), those with αα/αααanti‐3.7 had significantly lower mean Hb levels (8.38 ± 1.58 g/dL) than individuals without triplications (11.14 ± 1.86 g/dL; p = 0.005). Likewise, αα/αααanti‐4.2 subjects had significantly lower mean Hb levels (8.28 ± 1.15 g/dL) compared to αα/αα (p = 0.004). By contrast, the mean MCV did not differ significantly compared αα/αααanti‐3.7or αα/αααanti‐4.2 and αα/αα (67.32 ± 6.06 fL, p = 0.608; 71.25 ± 7.14 fL, p = 0.084; and 65.72 ± 6.02 fL, respectively). Hb F levels were higher (though not significantly) in αα/αααanti‐3.7 or αα/αααanti‐4.2 individuals (6.10 ± 4.61%, p = 0.222; 5.13 ± 5.44%, p = 0.269) than in αα/αα (1.44 ± 1.31%). Meanwhile, Hb A2 levels were comparable among the three groups (4.90 ± 0.62%, p = 0.116; 4.95 ± 0.53%, p = 0.149; and 5.45 ± 0.67%), remaining within typical ranges for β‐thalassaemia heterozygotes. Furthermore, the red blood cell indices and Hb analysis parameters were comparable between individuals co‐inherited with αα/αααanti‐3.7 and those with αα/αααanti‐4.2.

Among individuals with co‐inherited heterozygous Hb E, αααanti‐4.2 carriers tended to show lower mean Hb levels than those with αα/αα (11.07 ± 1.17 g/dL vs. 12.45 ± 2.04 g/dL; p = 0.242), whereas the single αα/αααanti‐3.7 subject had a higher Hb level (13.40 g/dL). Their MCV values were similar among αα/αααanti‐4.2, αα/αααanti‐3.7 and αα/αα (76.50 ± 10.90, 74.00 and 74.89 ± 5.88 fL, respectively). Hb F and Hb E levels in triplication carriers also remained within typical ranges for Hb E heterozygotes (1.15 ± 0.92%, 0.00% and 0.96 ± 0.91% for Hb F; and 25.80 ± 1.04%, 24.90% and 24.42 ± 1.78% for Hb E, respectively). Among individuals with homozygous Hb E, the single αα/αααanti‐4.2 patient and those without triplications (αα/αα) had comparable Hb levels (10.2 vs. 11.56 ± 1.61 g/dL), MCV (62 vs. 59.38 ± 4.04 fL) and Hb F (2.6% vs. 3.71 ± 3.20%).

Clinical and Laboratory Manifestation of Individuals With Double Heterozygosity of β Mutation and α‐Globin Gene Triplication

The clinical phenotype, haematological parameters and genotypes of 14 participants are summarised in Tables 3 and 4. Based on Hb typing, five individuals had heterozygous Hb E, one had homozygous Hb E and eight carried heterozygous β mutations. Molecular analysis of β mutations was performed in nine participants, revealing codon 26 (G > A) (βE), codon 41/42 (–TTCT) (β0), IVS‐II‐654 (C > T) (β+severe) and codon 19 (A > G) (β+). However, one individual had no detectable β mutation, despite confirmation by direct sequencing.

Discussion

This study revealed that 1.28% of the 1559 evaluated subjects carried α‐globin gene triplications. Among these, αααanti‐4.2 occurred more frequently than αααanti‐3.7, at 0.83% and 0.45%, respectively. To date, our investigation involves the largest sample of Thai individuals, primarily from the country's central region. The observed prevalence exceeds a prior report indicating 0.25% (1/406) of α‐globin gene triplications (unspecified genotype) in the same region [13]. This discrepancy may stem from variations in study populations and laboratory techniques. The earlier research, conducted in 1987, employed DNA mapping on umbilical cord blood samples, whereas our study used multiplex PCR on samples from both healthy and anaemic individuals. Testing symptomatic individuals could result in a higher apparent prevalence of α‐globin gene triplications.

Most studies investigating αααanti‐3.7 have examined both healthy individuals and patients with haemoglobinopathies, reporting a prevalence from 0.12% to 10% [6, 7]. In contrast, αααanti‐4.2 has often gone undetected or occurred at a lower prevalence. In Thailand, the αααanti‐3.7 triplication has been noted in 1.4% (3/215) and 2.83% (3/106) of healthy adults from the Northern region [15, 16], and in 1.56% (1/64) of healthy adults from the Northeastern region [14], regarding its prevalence among individuals with thalassaemia, αααanti‐3.7 has been identified in 0.68% (1/148) of individuals—both healthy and thalassaemic—carrying β0‐thalassaemia with 3.4 kb deletion [19], in 0.32% (1/312) of patients with thalassaemia intermedia from the Northeastern region [20], and in 0.43% (4/925) of patients with β‐thalassaemia/Hb E disease from the central region [21]. In all of these studies, αααanti‐4.2 was not detected. In the Southern region, α‐globin gene triplications of unspecified genotype were found in 1.0% (3/300) of healthy adults [17]. Therefore, the present study is the first to report the prevalence of αααanti‐4.2 in Thailand. Only a few reports have shown a higher prevalence of αααanti‐4.2 compared to αααanti‐3.7, consistent with our findings. For example, a study of 7644 blood samples from Guizhou Province in Southwestern China identified αααanti‐4.2 at 0.52% and αααanti‐3.7 at 0.25% [8], while an analysis of 73,967 individuals in Ganzhou Province, also in Southwestern China, reported 1.03% for αααanti‐4.2, slightly exceeding 0.92% for αααanti‐3.7 [22].

The more frequent observation of αααanti‐3.7 compared to αααanti‐4.2 is often attributed to the higher prevalence of the –α3.7 deletion relative to –α4.2 in endemic regions [2, 7, 23]. Both –α3.7 and αααanti‐3.7 arise from unequal crossing over between misaligned homologous segments during meiosis. However, the underlying reason why certain populations display a predominance of αααanti‐4.2, despite a greater frequency of the –α3.7 allele, remains unclear [23, 24, 25].

Our study found that 0.9% of subjects carried α‐globin gene triplications co‐inherited with β‐globin mutations and 0.19% had co‐inheritance with other α‐globin mutations (i.e. αααanti‐3.7/– –SEA, αααanti‐4.2/– –SEA and αααanti‐4.2/–α3.7). Xie et al. reported that 0.37% of 73,967 Chinese individuals harboured α‐globin triplications alongside various other α‐globin mutations, including αααanti‐4.2/–α3.7, αααanti‐4.2/– –SEA, αααanti‐4.2/αwsα, αααanti‐4.2/HKαα/– –SEA, αααanti‐4.2/αcsα, αααanti‐3.7/– –SEA, αααanti‐3.7/–α3.7, αααanti‐3.7/–α4.2, αααanti‐3.7/αααanti‐4.2 and αααanti‐3.7/Hb Phnom Penh [22]. In Iran, Hamid et al. found that 1.04% of 6460 healthy individuals and carriers of haemoglobinopathy had α‐globin triplications alongside β‐thalassaemia trait, [6] and Abedini et al. reported that 1.7% of 4010 individuals, both healthy and anaemic, inherited α‐globin triplications together with β‐globin mutations [9].

Most studies have indicated that the haematological parameters and Hb typing results of individuals with heterozygous α‐globin gene triplications do not significantly differ from those of people with a normal genotype (αα/αα) [9, 10, 22]. However, in our cohort, individuals with αααanti‐3.7/αα or αααanti‐4.2/αα tended to exhibit lower Hb and MCV compared to those with αα/αα, with mean Hb levels of 10.00, 11.23 and 12.35 g/dL, and mean MCV values of 75.9, 74.7 and 80.94 fL, respectively. Similarly, an Iranian study reported that 18 individuals carrying αααanti3.7/αα displayed an average MCV of 81.47 ± 5.76 fL, suggesting that some carriers may indeed have reduced MCV values [6].

A key limitation of our analysis is its retrospective design, which precludes ruling out other potential causes of microcytosis, such as iron deficiency anaemia, anaemia of inflammation or heterozygous of non‐deletional α‐globin mutations. Moreover, our population included individuals with low MCV and/or anaemia who were suspected of having thalassaemia traits or thalassaemia diseases. This selection bias may explain the comparatively lower Hb and MCV values observed in our study relative to earlier reports.

Previous investigations of heterozygous α‐globin gene triplication co‐inherited with heterozygous β‐thalassaemia have demonstrated a broad range of clinical phenotypes, including thalassaemia trait, thalassaemia intermedia and thalassaemia major [6, 9, 26, 27, 28]. For example, Hamid et al. studied 67 Iranian individuals with this co‐inheritance and reported that all were clinically asymptomatic, with no requirement for blood transfusions. Their Hb levels ranged from 10.2 to 15.6 g/dL, although four subjects had lower Hb values of 7.9–8.6 g/dL [6]. Conversely, Abedini et al. analysed 69 Iranian subjects and found that 31.9% had thalassaemia trait, 42% had thalassaemia intermedia and 26.1% had thalassaemia major, with Hb levels of 6.8–16.1 g/dL, Hb A2 of 1.6%–8.5% and Hb F of 0.3%–40.8% [9]. Similarly, Ropero et al. described 64 Caucasian individuals and noted that 14% had a thalassaemia trait phenotype and 86% had thalassaemia intermedia, with Hb levels of 7.2–13.8 g/dL, MCV of 55.8–81.3 fL, Hb A2 of 1.8%–6.6% and Hb F of 0.3%–21.7% [27].

In our study, seven out of eight participants co‐inherited with heterozygous β‐thalassaemia had thalassaemia intermedia, with Hb levels of 6.4–9.8 g/dL, and the remaining individual displayed a thalassaemia trait phenotype. All 8 exhibited Hb A2 values of 4.2%–5.6% and Hb F levels of 0%–12.2%, which are consistent with previous reports. Among five subjects co‐inherited with heterozygous Hb E, all showed a thalassaemia trait phenotype, whereas the single participant with homozygous Hb E and α‐globin triplication presented with thalassaemia intermedia. Their Hb typing parameters resembled those of individuals lacking triplications. These observations, along with prior studies, underscore that the same genotype can manifest with diverse clinical phenotypes.

As this was a retrospective study, certain limitations must be acknowledged. We could not identify other potential causes of anaemia in all participants or fully collect clinical information for individuals with double heterozygosity for β‐globin mutations and α‐globin gene triplication. In addition, comprehensive molecular data, including results for non‐deletional α‐globin mutations, were unavailable. We also could not distinguish α‐globin gene triplication from quadruplication, as the requisite Southern blot analysis was not performed.

In conclusion, screening for α‐globin gene triplication is advisable if the clinical presentation suggests thalassaemia disease but DNA analysis or Hb typing aligns with β‐thalassaemia trait (Hb A2 > 3.5% and Hb F ranging from normal up to 40%), Hb E trait, or homozygous Hb E. Furthermore, antenatal screening through DNA analysis should be considered if an individual has a family history of α‐globin gene triplication and their partner carries a β‐globin mutation. Prenatal diagnosis is also recommended if the first child is affected by thalassaemia major attributable to double heterozygosity for an α‐globin triplication and a β‐globin mutation.
