Severe Sepsis in an Adolescent With a De Novo Variant in the EP300 Gene Associated With Rubinstein-Taybi Syndrome Type 2: A Case Report
Authors: Adriana Pinheiro, Mariana Oliveira, Beatriz Gonçalves, Cláudia F Reis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rubinstein-Taybi syndrome type 2 (RSTS2) results from pathogenic variants in the EP300 gene, manifesting with intellectual disability, facial dysmorphism, and multisystem anomalies. Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) require family segregation studies for reclassification.A 17-year-old male adolescent with intellectual developmental disorder and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) presented at the age of 12 with pneumonia complicated by severe infection, bilateral pleural effusion, and rupture of the left renal excretory system. A multigene panel identified a VUS in the EP300 gene (c.4331_4332delinsGA; p.Asp1444Gly). Family segregation analysis demonstrated a de novo origin, allowing reclassification as likely pathogenic and establishing the diagnosis of RSTS2.This case highlights the value of family segregation analysis for VUS reclassification in autosomal dominant disorders. The associated urinary abnormalities and severe infectious manifestations reinforce the importance of multidisciplinary assessment and long-term follow-up in patients with RSTS2.

Introduction

Rubinstein-Taybi syndrome (RSTS) is a polymalformative clinical entity of autosomal dominant inheritance, with an estimated incidence between 1:100,000 and 1:125,000 live births [1]. From a molecular perspective, RSTS is classified as a chromatinopathy, resulting from heterozygous pathogenic variants in the CREBBP (16p13.3) or EP300 (22q13.2) gene [1,2]. These genes encode the CBP and p300 proteins, respectively, which function as ubiquitous transcriptional coactivators and possess intrinsic histone acetyltransferase (HAT) activity [1,3]. The pathophysiological mechanism is based on haploinsufficiency, in which reduced histone acetylation impairs chromatin remodeling and regulation of gene expression, essential for embryonic development and neurogenesis [1,4].

Clinically, RSTS presents with marked pleiotropy. The facial phenotype is characterized by arched eyebrows, long eyelashes, downward-slanting palpebral fissures, a broad nasal bridge, and a prominent columella (extending below the alae nasi), as well as micrognathia and a characteristic grimacing smile [2,5]. Oral manifestations, such as a high-arched palate and talon cusps - present in approximately 73% of patients - are important diagnostic markers [5]. Additionally, there are broad and often angulated thumbs and halluces, poor growth, microcephaly, and intellectual disability of variable severity [2,5].

At a multisystem level, RSTS is characterized by a high prevalence of structural renal anomalies (52%), such as hydronephrosis or horseshoe kidney, and congenital heart defects in about one-third of cases [5]. Ophthalmologic abnormalities are also prominent, affecting up to 80% of individuals, particularly strabismus and refractive errors, as well as persistent chronic constipation [5]. The risk of tumor development (benign and malignant) is approximately 30%, with malignant tumors occurring in about 5%-6% of cases, most commonly involving neural or hematological neoplasms [5,6].

Variants in the EP300 gene (RSTS type 2) are typically associated with a milder phenotype, including less evident facial dysmorphism, a lower frequency of digit angulation, and, in some cases, relatively preserved cognitive abilities, with intelligence quotient values falling within the normal range [7,8]. Due to phenotypic overlap with other epigenetic syndromes, clinical diagnosis can be challenging, requiring careful correlation with molecular studies for definitive confirmation [9].

Patients with RSTS, including those with EP300 variants (type 2), have a predisposition to recurrent and severe infections due to immunological defects documented in approximately 46% of cases, particularly antibody deficiency and B-cell dysfunction [10-12].

Case presentation

We report the case of a 17-year-old male adolescent with intellectual developmental disorder and autism spectrum disorder (ASD). On physical examination, the patient presented notable dysmorphic features, including brachycephaly, frontal angioma, small ears, and synophrys. Additional findings included a single palmar crease, fifth finger clinodactyly, and joint hyperlaxity. The patient did not present any abnormalities in the thumbs or halluces.

At the age of 12 years, the patient developed severe pneumonia complicated by acute respiratory failure, bilateral pleural effusion, and sepsis. Blood cultures were positive for Staphylococcus capitis. He required hospitalization and treatment with broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics.

During the same hospitalization, persistent abdominal pain and worsening inflammatory markers prompted further investigation. Contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT with delayed excretory-phase imaging revealed a left perirenal urinoma contained within Gerota’s fascia, highly suggestive of rupture of the left collecting system, with apparent communication with the inferior calyceal group (Figure 1).

Due to subsequent clinical deterioration, urgent endoscopic urinary diversion was performed. Retrograde pyelography confirmed contrast extravasation from the left inferior calyx. A Double-J ureteral stent was successfully placed, with its proximal end positioned within the inferior calyceal group to facilitate urinary drainage and promote healing (Figure 2).

Follow-up renal ultrasonography demonstrated no structural urinary tract abnormalities or predisposing anatomical factors. No clear underlying etiology was identified. The patient's clinical course was favorable, with progressive resolution of the infection, preservation of renal function, and no subsequent episodes of severe or recurrent infections.

Initial etiological investigation of intellectual developmental disorder, including array CGH and testing for FMR1 triplet repeat expansion, was negative. Due to persistent clinical suspicion, a multigene panel for intellectual disability was performed at age 11, identifying two variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in TFAP2B and EP300 (NM_001429.3:c.4331_4332delinsGA; p.Asp1444Gly).

Family segregation analysis was performed for the EP300 variant, which was confirmed as de novo, being absent in both parents. No segregation studies were performed for the TFAP2B variant. However, the patient’s clinical phenotype was not consistent with TFAP2B-related disorders, and this variant was therefore considered unlikely to be clinically relevant.

Based on the confirmed de novo occurrence of the EP300 variant and its correlation with the clinical phenotype, this variant was reclassified from VUS to likely pathogenic, establishing the diagnosis of Rubinstein-Taybi syndrome type 2 (RSTS2).

The patient was evaluated by Pediatric Cardiology after diagnosis, with no abnormalities detected on echocardiography, and was discharged from follow-up. Flow cytometry immunophenotyping of T-cell subsets, B cells, and NK cells, as well as serum IgG, IgA, IgM, and IgE levels, were within age-adjusted normal reference ranges, and no clinically relevant immune deficiency was identified.

Discussion

The interpretation of the identified variants illustrates the importance of integrating molecular findings with clinical correlation and family studies in multigene panel testing [13-15]. Although two VUS were initially identified, only the EP300 variant underwent segregation analysis, confirming its de novo occurrence and supporting its pathogenic role in accordance with ACMG/AMP (American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology) principles. The TFAP2B variant was not further investigated through family studies; however, the lack of phenotypic concordance with TFAP2B-associated disorders made its clinical relevance unlikely. This case highlights the importance of careful clinical interpretation of incidental findings in multigene testing.

Diagnostic confirmation of RSTS2 (EP300-related) is essential for implementing rigorous and personalized multidisciplinary surveillance protocols [2,5,16]. Although most cases occur sporadically, the recurrence risk for siblings - while low (estimated <1% due to the possibility of germline mosaicism) - should be addressed in genetic counseling [5]. Furthermore, establishing a diagnosis allows anticipation and management of common systemic complications, including cardiac and renal anomalies, specific anesthetic risks related to airway anatomy, and predisposition to neoplasms [5,6,13].

Regarding neurodevelopment, long-term follow-up is essential to manage behavioral comorbidities such as ASD and anxiety, which significantly impact quality of life and autonomy during the transition to adulthood [5].

Although urinary tract anomalies - including hydronephrosis, ectopic kidney, and ureteral duplications - have been reported in patients with RSTS (approximately 52% in CREBBP variants and 6% in EP300 variants [5,7,8]), rupture of the renal collecting system associated with pyelonephritis has not previously been described in these patients [17]. In the present case, follow-up imaging demonstrated no persistent hydronephrosis, structural urinary tract abnormalities, or other predisposing anatomical factors. Therefore, the mechanism underlying the collecting-system rupture remains uncertain. We speculate that the severe infectious and inflammatory process may have resulted in transient urinary outflow obstruction caused by ureteral edema, inflammatory debris, or ureteral spasm, leading to increased intrapelvic pressure and subsequent rupture. Local inflammation may also have contributed to tissue fragility. However, given the absence of previously reported cases and the lack of persistent anatomical abnormalities, a direct causal relationship between EP300-related RSTS2 and collecting-system rupture cannot be established.

This case highlights the importance of integrating clinical correlation and family segregation analysis in resolving VUS identified through multigene panel testing.

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of integrating molecular genetic analysis with clinical and family data for accurate variant classification in rare autosomal dominant disorders. Family segregation analysis was crucial for the reclassification of the identified variant and the establishment of the diagnosis. Early recognition of RSTS2 enables appropriate multidisciplinary management, surveillance, and genetic counseling. The associated urinary and infectious complications reinforce the importance of long-term multidisciplinary follow-up.
