Mpox with painless anogenital and palmar lesions
Authors: Kota Yoshida, Takamasa Ishiuchi, Satoshi Kutsuna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Mpox presented without fever or systemic symptoms in an MSM on HIV PrEP.•Painless anogenital lesions resembled other STIs, complicating diagnosis.•PCR confirmed clade II mpox virus in pustular material, blood, and urine.•Even mild mpox may be complicated by secondary bacterial soft-tissue infection.•Careful clinical follow-up is essential even when mpox symptoms appear mild.

A 24-year-old man who has sex with men presented to our clinic with skin lesions. He had engaged in condomless receptive anal intercourse with a male partner six days before symptom onset. He had no notable recent travel history. Six days after the sexual exposure, vesicular lesions first appeared on his arms and subsequently spread to his face and trunk. He reported no fever or systemic symptoms prior to his first clinic visit. At the initial visit, 14 days after exposure, physical examination revealed vesicular and pustular lesions on the palms (Fig. 1), dorsum of the hands, face, and back. Asymptomatic vesicular lesions were also observed on the genital (Fig. 2) and perianal areas; however, the exact timing of their appearance was unclear. The patient was HIV negative and was using HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). Polymerase chain reaction testing detected mpox virus in purulent material from the pustular lesion, crusts from the pustule, as well as in blood and urine samples. Genomic analysis revealed that the mpox virus belonged to clade II. Screening tests for other sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, hepatitis B surface antigen, syphilis, and genital gonorrhea and chlamydia, were negative. Because the patient had no pain or other subjective symptoms and was in stable general condition, he was initially managed conservatively with isolation and symptomatic care. Twenty-five days after exposure, he returned to the clinic because of pain in the left inguinal region and was diagnosed with a secondary bacterial soft tissue infection. Oral cephalexin therapy was initiated, resulting in prompt clinical improvement. This case highlights that mpox can occur without fever or systemic symptoms and may present with painless anogenital lesions that are easily misdiagnosed as other sexually transmitted infections [1], [2]. Even mild cases of mpox may be complicated by secondary bacterial infection, emphasizing the need for careful clinical follow-up [1], [2].Fig. 1Well-circumscribed pustule on the palm.
