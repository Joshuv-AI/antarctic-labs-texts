Real-world single-center analysis of efficacy and safety in newly diagnosed marginal zone lymphoma
Authors: Xin Zhou, Jingshu Ruan, Yuqi Shi, Ningning Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to analyze newly diagnosed marginal zone lymphoma (MZL) patients treated in the Department of Hematology at our center. A retrospective analysis was conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety profiles of various treatment regimens for MZL. Data were retrospectively collected from 177 newly diagnosed MZL patients hospitalized in the Department of Hematology at Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University between October 2006 and October 2024. Compared to chemotherapy alone, treatment combining CD20 monoclonal antibody with chemotherapy significantly improved the complete response (CR) rate in patients with MZL. Additionally, the incidence of progression of disease within 24 months (POD24) was significantly lower in patients receiving CD20 monoclonal antibody ± chemotherapy than in those treated with chemotherapy alone (11.7% vs. 35.7%, P = 0.018). Comparative analysis of rituximab combined with various chemotherapy regimens revealed no statistically significant differences in either short-term or long-term efficacy (P > 0.05). When comparing short-term outcomes at initial diagnosis, numerically higher CR rates (86.7% vs. 56.3%) and overall response rates (ORR) (100% vs. 90.6%) were observed with the obinutuzumab (G) regimen ± chemotherapy compared to the rituximab (R) regimen ± chemotherapy; however, these differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.144 and P = 0.541, respectively). Compared with chemotherapy alone, CD20 monoclonal antibody-containing regimens significantly improved CR rates and effectively prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in MZL patients. No statistically significant differences were found among various chemotherapy regimens combined with rituximab, irrespective of whether short-term or long-term outcomes were assessed. Similarly, no significant differences were observed between obinutuzumab (G) ± chemotherapy and rituximab (R) ± chemotherapy in terms of PFS, CR rate, or ORR. However, grade 3–4 adverse events occurred more frequently in patients treated with the G ± chemotherapy regime.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-026-02116-4.

Introduction

Marginal zone lymphoma (MZL), the second most common type of indolent lymphoma, originates in the marginal zone of lymphoid follicles and accounts for approximately 7% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas [1, 2]. According to the 2022 WHO classification, MZL consists of three subtypes: nodal MZL (NMZL), splenic MZL (SMZL), and extranodal mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma [3]. In China, MZL represents 12.4% of B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas, comprising NMZL (1.5%), SMZL (0.6%), and MALT lymphoma (10.3%). The median age at diagnosis is approximately 55 years. MZL typically exhibits an indolent clinical course, with median survival often exceeding 10 years. However, prognosis differs notably among subtypes, with MALT lymphoma demonstrating superior outcomes compared to SMZL and NMZL [4]. Extranodal involvement in MALT lymphoma commonly occurs in the stomach, salivary glands, lungs, and ocular adnexa, with the stomach being the most frequent site [5, 6]. The anatomical distribution and epidemiological features suggest a close association with chronic antigenic stimulation within specific microenvironments [7]. Among these, gastric lesions show the strongest link with Helicobacter pylori infection [8].

At the molecular pathology level, MZL pathogenesis likely involves both antigen-driven mechanisms and genetic alterations. On one hand, chronic infection or autoimmune processes trigger B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling and inflammation within the microenvironment, facilitating clonal expansion. On the other hand, characteristic translocations and mutations affecting the NF-κB pathway frequently occur across MZL subtypes [8]. For instance, aberrations such as t(11;18)(q21;q21)/BIRC3–MALT1, t(14;18)(q32;q21)/IGH–MALT1, and API2-MALT1 fusions in MALT lymphoma lead to constitutive activation of NF-κB. Conversely, mutations in NOTCH2 and KLF2 commonly appear in nodal and splenic subtypes, influencing marginal zone differentiation and survival signaling [9]. These molecular distinctions explain the biological heterogeneity of subtypes and offer a foundation for risk stratification and targeted therapy.

Treatment decisions for MZL should be individualized, taking disease-specific features into consideration. Current therapeutic options include anti-infective therapy, immunochemotherapy, chemotherapy, watchful waiting, surgical resection, and radiotherapy. In contemporary clinical practice, anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody (CD20 mAb)-based regimens, either as monotherapy or combined with chemotherapy, are widely accepted as first-line treatments. Common regimens include BR (bendamustine and rituximab), R-CHOP/CDOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, prednisone, epirubicin/liposomal doxorubicin), and rituximab combined with chlorambucil [10, 11]. Previous studies reported ORR of 81% with rituximab ± chemotherapy, with OS and 4-year PFS rates of 78.1% and 64.1%, respectively [11]. Despite improved prognoses, resistance persists in some patients, manifesting as early progression or primary refractoriness [12]. The introduction of obinutuzumab (G), a novel type II anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, shows promise. G, the first glycoengineered humanized anti-CD20 antibody, demonstrates enhanced antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), improved antibody stability, and faster target-binding kinetics [13–15]. Given its established efficacy in follicular lymphoma (FL) and the shared characteristics between MZL and FL, both being incurable, indolent, and heterogeneous non-Hodgkin lymphomas, G emerges as a potentially novel therapeutic option for MZL patients. Additionally, small-molecule agents such as Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors and new combination strategies are progressively expanding the therapeutic landscape for relapsed or refractory cases [16]. Overall, treatment decisions must comprehensively evaluate disease stage and site, etiological infections, patient comorbidities, and treatment tolerance. In cases of relapse, priority should be given to small-molecule targeted therapies, cell-based treatments, or rational combination regimens to optimize clinical outcomes. Therefore, a retrospective study was conducted involving 177 consecutive patients newly diagnosed with MZL who were hospitalized at our institution from October 2006 to October 2024, aiming to evaluate the real-world clinical outcomes of therapeutic interventions in MZL.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study included consecutive patients newly diagnosed with MZL who received treatment at the Department of Hematology, Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University between October 2006 and October 2024. Diagnoses were confirmed according to the 2022 WHO classification for lymphoid neoplasms [3]. Treatment initiation was not solely based on diagnosis. Systemic therapy was administered only when patients exhibited one or more of the following: lymphoma-related symptoms (including B symptoms), symptomatic bulky or high-tumor-burden disease, organ dysfunction caused by lymphoma, cytopenias secondary to bone marrow involvement, rapid disease progression, lymphoma-related ECOG performance status ≥ 2, or suspected histologic transformation. Asymptomatic patients with low tumor burden and no organ impairment were managed with a watch-and-wait approach and were excluded from efficacy analyses.

This study utilized the inpatient electronic medical record system from our hematology department. During the study period, patients with newly diagnosed MZL managed exclusively as outpatients were uncommon at our institution. Patients requiring systemic therapy were routinely admitted for initial treatment, monitoring, and management of potential toxicities. Consequently, outpatient-only cases were not systematically recorded in this database and were excluded from the analysis.

Study data

Comprehensive patient information was collected, including demographics, comorbidities, B symptoms, ECOG performance status, and IPI scores. Bone marrow aspiration/biopsy results, electrocardiograms, imaging studies, and pathological diagnoses were obtained. In addition, hematological parameters, such as complete blood count, biochemistry panel, lactate dehydrogenase, serum β2-microglobulin, and viral serologies, were recorded. Treatment regimens and efficacy outcomes were documented in detail.

Efficacy assessment

Efficacy was evaluated according to the Lugano classification. Short-term efficacy endpoints included ORR, partial response (PR), and CR. Long-term outcomes included OS and PFS. Before therapy initiation, imaging evaluations (PET-CT, MRI, CT, and ultrasound) were conducted. Interim assessments occurred after 3–4 treatment cycles. Final efficacy was evaluated by PET-CT 6–8 weeks after completion of chemotherapy. For patients undergoing maintenance therapy, “end of chemotherapy” referred to the conclusion of induction chemotherapy before maintenance. Final efficacy was consistently assessed 6–8 weeks after the final induction therapy cycle based on Lugano criteria.

Follow-up

Follow-up was conducted using medical record reviews and telephone communication. The follow-up period began at the date of diagnosis and ended in January 2025.

Statistical methods

Data analysis and processing were performed using SPSS Statistics 27.0. Continuous variables underwent normality testing. Continuous data that did not conform to normal distribution were presented as median and interquartile range. Normally distributed continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons involving categorical variables were performed using either Fisher’s exact test or the Chi-square test, depending on the expected counts within cells. Survival differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test, and survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan-Meier (K-M) method. Graphical illustrations were generated with GraphPad Prism 9. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 177 patients with newly diagnosed MZL were enrolled for baseline characteristic analysis. After applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 108 cases were included in the efficacy analysis (Fig. 1).

Efficacy analysis

Efficacy in 108 MZL patients

Following induction therapy, the chemotherapy-alone group achieved an ORR of 71.4% and a CR rate of 21.4%, compared to an ORR of 91.5% and a CR rate of 67.0% in the CD20 ± chemotherapy group. The addition of CD20 monoclonal antibody significantly improved the CR rate (67.0% vs. 21.4%; P = 0.004) and indicated a trend toward improved ORR (91.5% vs. 71.4%; P = 0.076). Long-term outcomes showed that the chemotherapy-alone group had a median OS of 20 months, with an unreached median PFS and a 5-year PFS rate of 62.5%. In the CD20 ± chemotherapy group, median OS and PFS were not reached during the observation period, with 5-year PFS and OS rates of 89.5% and 89.2%, respectively. Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated significantly improved OS (P < 0.001) and moderately improved PFS (P = 0.049) with CD20 monoclonal antibody-based regimens (Fig. 2A-B). Group-level response rates (ORR, CR, PR by regimen) are summarized in Supplementary Table I.

Efficacy analysis in 79 MZL cases

A total of 79 MZL patients receiving R ± chemotherapy were divided into five groups according to their treatment regimens: R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone; n = 16, 20%), R-CDOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, liposomal doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone; n = 36, 46%), R-monotherapy (n = 12, 15%), BR (bendamustine, rituximab; n = 9, 11%), and R2 (rituximab, lenalidomide; n = 6, 8%). The median follow-up duration was 45 months. Short-term efficacy analysis showed response in 14 of 16 R-CHOP patients (ORR 87.5%, CR 68.8%), 31 of 36 R-CDOP patients (ORR 86.1%, CR 61.1%), all BR patients (ORR 100%, CR 55.6%), 5 of 6 R2 patients (ORR 83.3%, CR 66.7%), and all R-monotherapy patients (ORR 100%, CR 66.7%). Pairwise comparisons using Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni-adjusted P-values showed no statistically significant differences in ORR (P = 0.564) or CR rates (P = 0.804) among the regimens. Exact two-sided P-values for pairwise comparisons are listed in Supplementary Table II. For long-term outcomes, the R² group primarily treated elderly or frail patients. However, due to limited enrollment, OS and PFS were compared only among the R-CHOP, R-CDOP, and BR groups. Survival analyses showed median OS was unreached in all groups, with 4-year OS rates of 90.3% for R-CDOP and 93.8% for R-CHOP, while median OS for BR extended beyond 42 months. The log-rank test revealed no significant differences in OS (P = 0.713). Median PFS remained unreached in all cohorts, with 4-year PFS rates of 85.7% (BR), 90.3% (R-CDOP), and 78.6% (R-CHOP); log-rank test confirmed no meaningful difference in PFS (P = 0.470). Thus, these three therapeutic strategies exhibited comparable efficacy. All patients in the R2 group (n = 6) remained alive without progression at the end of follow-up; however, limited follow-up and small sample size mean these observations should be interpreted cautiously and considered descriptive rather than statistically definitive (Fig. 4A-B).

Short-term efficacy analysis revealed CR rates of 56.3% and ORR of 90.6% in the R ± chemotherapy group, compared with CR rates of 86.7% and ORR of 100% in the G ± chemotherapy group; however, differences between groups were not statistically significant for CR (P = 0.144) or ORR (P = 0.541). Overall, G ± chemotherapy showed no significant superiority over R ± chemotherapy regarding short-term or long-term efficacy outcomes.

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify independent prognostic factors for OS and PFS among 94 patients treated with CD20 monoclonal antibody ± chemotherapy. Variables included in the analysis were sex, age, disease stage, tumor burden, B symptoms, bone marrow involvement, ECOG performance status, serum LDH, and β2-microglobulin levels. As indicated in Supplementary Table III, none of these variables emerged as independent predictors of OS or PFS (all P > 0.05). These findings suggest that no single baseline clinical parameter significantly affected long-term survival outcomes in this patient cohort.

Safety analysis

In the CD20 monoclonal antibody ± chemotherapy group, 87.2% of patients experienced at least one adverse event, primarily grade 1–2, which typically resolved with observation or symptomatic treatment. The most common severe toxicities were hematologic, with grade 3–4 events including neutropenia, leukocytopenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia. Severe non-hematologic adverse events mainly consisted of pulmonary infection and febrile neutropenia. The incidence of grade 3–4 adverse events was higher in the obinutuzumab ± chemotherapy group compared to the rituximab ± chemotherapy group (46.7% vs. 35.4%; Table 3). A detailed analysis of individual adverse events (Fig. 8) indicated that this difference resulted primarily from higher rates of hematologic toxicities in the G ± chemotherapy cohort. Infusion-related reactions were also more frequent in the G ± chemotherapy group, though mostly grade 1–2. Grade 3 neutropenia responded effectively to granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) treatment, with rapid recovery in most cases. For grade 4 neutropenia complicated by fever or pulmonary infection, chemotherapy was delayed or suspended, and patients received G-CSF along with antibiotic therapy. Among 14 patients with grade 3–4 anemia, 10 required leukocyte-depleted red blood cell transfusions. Thrombocytopenia was managed using thrombopoietic agents and type-matched platelet transfusions when indicated.

Discussion

MZL typically follows an indolent clinical course associated with prolonged survival. Previous studies reported 5-year PFS and OS rates of approximately 76.0% and 86.6%, respectively [3]. Our analysis, including 108 treatment-eligible patients with a median follow-up of 40 months, demonstrated an ORR of 88.9% and CR rate of 61.1% after induction therapy, accompanied by 5-year PFS and OS rates of 84.7% and 81.6%, confirming durable survival benefits for most patients. Comparative analyses showed significantly improved CR (67.0% vs. 21.4%, P = 0.004), 5-year PFS (89.5% vs. 62.5%, P = 0.049), and OS (89.2% vs. 42.9%, P < 0.001) associated with CD20 monoclonal antibody-containing regimens compared to chemotherapy alone, highlighting the crucial role of CD20-directed therapy. These findings align with recent real-world data reporting ORRs of approximately 93% and CR rates around 75% using first-line rituximab-based treatments in newly diagnosed MZL patients [17]. The notably shorter median OS (20 months) in the chemotherapy-alone group likely reflects selection bias due to small sample size and adverse baseline features. Most chemotherapy-alone patients were treated before widespread rituximab use, often presenting with advanced disease or poor performance status, contributing to inferior outcomes.

Early disease progression (POD24) is recognized as a critical prognostic indicator, historically associated with reduced median survival of 3–5 years [18–20]. A recent real-world study from China noted that 84.5% of POD24 patients had advanced disease, frequently with B symptoms and elevated serum β2-microglobulin levels [21]. Similarly, an observational study involving 321 MZL patients reported a POD24 rate of 18.4%, with significantly lower 3-year OS in POD24 patients (53% vs. 95%) [22]. Our study identified POD24 in 14.8% (16/108), with a median OS of 24.5 months, while 85.2% (92/108) without POD24 achieved a 3-year OS of 93.6%, consistent with international studies. These data further support POD24 as an essential prognostic marker. Although disease-specific prognostic models are currently unavailable for MZL, elevated platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR ≥ 95) and hypoalbuminemia have emerged as independent prognostic factors in advanced MZL [23]. Unlike static markers, POD24 incorporates dynamic treatment response data, allowing more precise survival risk stratification. Therefore, POD24 is recommended as a key endpoint for prospective clinical trials to refine therapeutic strategies.

Analysis of 79 MZL patients receiving R ± chemotherapy (median follow-up, 45 months) revealed an ORR of 89.9%, CR rate of 63.3%, and 4-year OS/PFS rates of 91.5%/88.6%. Compared with the GALLIUM trial [12], our R ± chemotherapy regimens exhibited numerically higher OS and PFS. Additionally, R-monotherapy demonstrated favorable results in stage I-II MZL patients ineligible for radiotherapy (CR 66.7%, 5-year OS/PFS 100%), retaining clinical relevance despite limited sample size. In advanced MZL (stage III–IV), all R-based regimens demonstrated efficacy: R-CHOP achieved a CR of 68.8% with 4-year OS/PFS of 93.8%/78.6%, and R-CDOP showed a CR of 61.1% with 4-year OS/PFS of 90.3%/90.3%, surpassing international reports [24, 25]. BR produced a CR of 55.6% with 4-year OS/PFS of 85.7%/50%, consistent with retrospective studies [26]. Statistical analysis indicated no significant OS (P = 0.598) or PFS (P = 0.341) differences among R-CHOP, R-CDOP, and BR, aligning with multicenter findings [27].

In previous studies comparing R-CHOP and BR in MZL, median PFS was 47.2 months (R-CHOP) and 57.2 months (BR), without significant differences (P = 0.3249). Similarly, 10-year OS rates (66% for R-CHOP vs. 71% for BR; P = 0.249) lacked statistical significance. BR showed significantly improved short-term efficacy (CR rates of 40% vs. 30% for BR and R-CHOP; P = 0.021) [28]. The BRIGHT study, comparing R-CHOP and BR, demonstrated significantly higher 5-year PFS in the BR group (65.5% vs. 55.8%; P = 0.0025), although OS remained similar [29]. A multicenter study by Wang et al. [30] using propensity score matching found comparable 2-year PFS (85.4% vs. 85.8%) and 2-year OS (94.2% vs. 94.7%) between R-CDOP and R-CHOP, respectively. ORR and CR rates were also similar between groups.

This study included 47 MZL patients treated at our center since January 1, 2022, comparing R ± chemotherapy versus G ± chemotherapy. No significant differences in baseline characteristics emerged between the two groups (P > 0.05). Short-term efficacy revealed a numerically higher CR rate in the G ± chemotherapy cohort (86.7% vs. 56.3%, P = 0.144). With a median follow-up of 16 months, the 2-year PFS was 89.0% for R ± chemotherapy and 100% for G ± chemotherapy (P = 0.335), consistent with prior studies indicating no significant differences in CR or PFS rates [31]. The pivotal phase III GALLIUM trial, comparing G-chemo versus R-chemo in indolent non-Hodgkin lymphoma, reported no significant OS differences for MZL patients in updated analyses after median follow-ups of 34.5 and 57.3 months [12, 32]. Furthermore, the hazard ratio for PFS between G-chemo and R-chemo in the MZL subgroup revealed no significant differences, although G-chemo significantly prolonged PFS in FL patients [2, 33]. These findings align with ours, suggesting that disease heterogeneity in MZL may influence treatment responses.

Safety profiles of G-chemo differed notably from R-chemo despite similar efficacy. Patients treated with G-chemo experienced significantly higher rates of serious adverse events (SAEs), infusion-related reactions (IRRs), and grade 3–5 adverse events, primarily due to enhanced antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). Additionally, G-chemo led to higher frequencies of grade 3–4 neutropenia and infection risk. Previous studies reported fewer patients in the G cohort completing the full 2-year maintenance therapy compared to the R cohort, potentially reflecting cumulative toxicity [34]. Consistent with these observations, our study found that the G ± chemotherapy group experienced higher grade 3–4 adverse event rates (46.7% vs. 35.4%) and infusion reactions (26.7% vs. 11.4%). Therefore, R-chemo remains the preferred first-line regimen for MZL, particularly in older patients or those with comorbidities requiring treatments with better tolerability.

Analysis combined patients treated with CD20 monoclonal antibody monotherapy and those receiving CD20 antibody combined with chemotherapy, despite distinct clinical strategies. The small number of patients undergoing CD20 monotherapy precluded adequately powered survival analyses. Within the R ± chemotherapy subgroup, short-term response rates appeared comparable across R-monotherapy, R-CHOP, R-CDOP, BR, and R2, with no significant differences in ORR or CR. However, combining data from these regimens may obscure differences in response depth and durability. Furthermore, comparison of obinutuzumab-based versus rituximab-based regimens requires cautious interpretation due to the limited sample size, likely insufficient to detect modest yet clinically relevant differences. OS data for the G ± chemotherapy and R ± chemotherapy subgroups remain immature due to the short median follow-up of 16 months; therefore, the observed 100% OS primarily reflects short-term outcomes. Larger cohorts with longer follow-up periods are needed to clarify potential differences in long-term survival.

This study was a single-center, real-world analysis designed to summarize practical clinical experiences with various immunochemotherapy regimens, rather than a strictly time-sequential comparative trial. The prolonged study period mirrors actual changes in clinical practice at our center, evolving from early rituximab-based therapies to recent obinutuzumab-based treatments. Nevertheless, some temporal bias remains inevitable. Given the extended study duration, chemotherapy-only cases mostly occurred during earlier years, whereas CD20 monoclonal antibody-based regimens became increasingly common later. During this period, advances in supportive care, imaging technologies, infection prophylaxis, and general lymphoma management likely contributed independently to improved patient outcomes. As a retrospective analysis, unmeasured confounding factors such as comorbidities, immune status, and treatment adherence could not be fully accounted for. Moreover, patients newly diagnosed with MZL and managed entirely in outpatient settings were not systematically included, potentially enriching our cohort for patients with more severe disease, higher tumor burdens, or greater symptom severity requiring hospitalization. Thus, a selection bias toward more clinically severe cases cannot be excluded, limiting generalizability to the broader population of newly diagnosed MZL patients. Future research incorporating molecular profiling and novel biomarkers could further refine patient stratification and guide personalized treatment strategies in MZL.
