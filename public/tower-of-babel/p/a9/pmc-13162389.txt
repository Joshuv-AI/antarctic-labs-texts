A multi-parameter ultrasound prediction model for nutrition-associated skin failure in hospitalized elderly patients: a prospective cohort study protocol
Authors: Yan Shen, Xue Mi, Yi Wu, Yun Wei, Huili Shen, Jiapei Tan, Jian Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn malnourished geriatric patients, deficient intake of energy and protein impairs tissue metabolism and microcirculation, significantly contributing to the development of skin failure. This study aims to develop and validate a prediction model for protein-energy malnutrition-associated skin failure among hospitalized elderly patients, facilitating early identification and targeted clinical interventions.MethodsThis prospective, single-center study involves 391 hospitalized patients aged ≥ 65 years. The research is conducted in two stages: identifying independent risk factors and developing a risk prediction model. Bedside ultrasound (US) is utilized as a core assessment tool to quantify nutritional and tissue integrity markers. Specifically, the model incorporates: (a) dermal blood perfusion via power Doppler to evaluate microcirculatory status; (b) subcutaneous tissue thickness to assess structural depletion; and (c) tissue elastography to measure changes in skin stiffness and biomechanical properties. These sonographic data are integrated with clinical and biochemical variables to derive a risk score. Model performance is evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity.Expected results/conclusionBy quantifying early subclinical changes in tissue perfusion and elasticity, the model is intended to identify high-risk patients before overt skin breakdown occurs, providing a critical window for preventive nutritional and nursing care. This prospective study establishes a novel, nutrition-based prediction model for skin failure in geriatric patients. By integrating multi-parametric ultrasound indicators, the model enables a shift from reactive to proactive care. Early identification of high-risk individuals facilitates targeted interventions, ultimately reducing skin-related complications and optimizing the quality of care for the hospitalized elderly population.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2500111057, registered October 24, 2025.Keywords: Skin failure, Protein-Energy Malnutrition, Risk Assessment, Nutritional Status, Inpatients, Aged

Introduction

While demographic shifts have led to a significant increase in the elderly population with multiple comorbidities [1, 2], managing skin integrity in this group remains a major clinical challenge. Although pressure injuries are common among bedbound older adults, conventional pressure-relieving interventions and repositioning often fail to prevent unexplained skin breakdown in certain patients. This clinical observation suggests that not all skin injuries are caused by external pressure alone, leading to the emerging concept of skin failure (SF). SF is defined as the progressive loss of the skin’s physiological functions—including thermoregulation, fluid balance, and barrier integrity—due to acute or chronic illness. This condition can be categorized into three clinical types: acute, chronic, and end-stage (terminal). Acute skin failure typically occurs rapidly in tandem with critical illness or multi-organ dysfunction; chronic skin failure develops gradually over time due to long-term systemic decline; and end-stage skin failure manifests as a physiological component of the dying process [3, 4]. In critically ill elderly patients, systemic hypoperfusion often results in the redistribution of blood flow to vital organs, leaving the skin vulnerable to cutaneous ischemia and subsequent necrosis [5]. These pathological changes frequently manifest as irregular, sudden-onset ulcers that mimic pressure injuries but reflect a deeper failure of tissue repair and microcirculation. However, a critical distinction remains: while pressure injuries are primarily driven by external mechanical forces, SF represents an “internal-out” failure reflecting a deeper compromise of tissue repair and microcirculation [5]. Given its association with poor clinical outcomes and a mortality rate as high as 42%, early identification of SF is critical for optimizing geriatric care [6].

Among the multifactorial drivers of SF, protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) is a primary contributor to compromised skin barrier function and impaired repair mechanisms [7, 8]. Malnutrition, often exacerbated by disease-related inflammation and hypermetabolism, creates a state of negative nitrogen balance that diminishes the body’s responsiveness to clinical interventions [9, 10]. In this physiological environment, systemic deficits—including hypoalbuminemia and vascular leakage—directly impair tissue integrity across all skin layers [11]. Consequently, PEM significantly lowers the skin’s threshold to physiological and pathological stressors, leading to increased vulnerability to breakdown and failure.

Although malnutrition prevalence among hospitalized elderly patients reaches up to 78.6%, geriatric malnutrition remains a persistent challenge that significantly predisposes patients to impaired skin perfusion and breakdown [12]. Despite this clear clinical link, research investigating the specific relationship between nutritional status and SF is limited. Existing literature has focused primarily on identifying risk factors and assessment tools for acute SF within intensive care settings [13, 14]. Consequently, there is a significant knowledge gap regarding nutrition-associated SF in the broader hospitalized geriatric population, emphasizing the need for objective, targeted screening models.

Significant gaps remain in this field. Traditional risk assessment tools often lack the sensitivity to distinguish SF from other skin injuries, while current research often relies on isolated nutritional indicators rather than multidimensional assessments, leaving the specific predictive value of nutritional status for SF risk poorly defined in the hospitalized elderly. This lack of evidence-based tools hampers the development of personalized prevention strategies. To address these limitations, the present study aims to identify independent risk factors and establish a validated prediction model for nutrition-associated SF. Compared with existing tools like the Braden Scale or NRS-2002, our model addresses a critical unmet need: the early, subclinical identification of skin failure. While traditional scores focus on external pressure or generalized nutritional status, they cannot detect the real-time microvascular and structural integrity changes in the skin. By integrating objective clinical data and real-time ultrasound parameters, this research provides a theoretical foundation for targeted nutritional interventions, ultimately aiming to optimize clinical outcomes and enhance the quality of life for this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design

This study will be designed as a prospective cohort study comprising two distinct stages. In the first stage, independent risk factors associated with nutrition-related SF in hospitalized elderly patients will be identified. The occurrence of nutrition-related SF in elderly patients admitted to the geriatric department will be defined as the dependent variable, while 17 selected observational indicators served as independent variables. Univariate analyses will be initially conducted on the modeling cohort dataset, and variables that demonstrated statistical significance will be subsequently entered into a binary logistic regression model to determine independent predictors of nutrition-related SF in this population. The second stage focused on the development and validation of a predictive model. Based on the training dataset derived from the first phase, a random forest algorithm will be employed to construct a risk prediction model for nutrition-related SF in hospitalized elderly patients. The model will be internally validated to assess its predictive accuracy and clinical applicability.

The relative importance of each risk factor will be ranked, and partial dependence plots will be generated to visualize the effects of variations in these factors on the risk of SF. Model performance will be evaluated using the validation cohort, with a confusion matrix applied to calculate key performance metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The AUC value ranges from 0 to 1, where values greater than 0.5 indicate some degree of predictive discrimination, and values closer to 1 reflect stronger model performance. Specifically, an AUC between 0.5 and 0.7 represents low predictive accuracy, between 0.7 and 0.9 indicates moderate to good performance, and above 0.9 reflects excellent predictive capability. Conversely, an AUC below 0.5 suggests that the model’s predictive outcomes are inconsistent with actual observations.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study will adopte a convenience sampling method to prospectively collect clinical data from patients admitted to the Department of Geriatrics at a tertiary hospital in Shanghai, China, between October 2025 and March 2028, in a single-center design. The inclusion criteria are as follows: patients aged 65 years or older; those whose family members provided written informed consent; and those with a hospital stay of at least one week in the geriatrics department. The exclusion criteria included: patients presenting with pressure injury, skin infection, or skin failure at the time of hospital admission; those with rapidly deteriorating conditions or in the terminal stage of disease; and patients with incomplete medical records or missing essential clinical data.

Sample size

As there is currently no standardized method for sample size estimation in random forest modeling, the approach proposed by Kurisu et al. [15] was adopted to ensure model accuracy and stability. According to this method, the sample size should be at least 20 times the number of independent variables. Study group allocation

In accordance with the estimated minimum sample size and clinical admission trends, elderly patients admitted to the Department of Geriatrics between October 2025 and March 2028 (a 30-month period) will be enrolled. Participants will be allocated into a model development group and an internal validation group based on the chronological order of admission. Specifically, patients admitted between October 2025 and May 2027 (20 months) will be assigned to the model development group, whereas those admitted between June 2027 and March 2028 (10 months) will constitute the internal validation group. The approximate ratio of sample sizes between the two groups is 7:3.

Definition of covariates

Based on a comprehensive review of the literature, clinical guidelines, and expert consensus on nutrition-associated skin failure—retrieved from databases including PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Medline, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure—together with clinical data analysis and expert panel discussions, potential risk factors influencing the development of nutrition-associated skin failure in elderly patients were identified and included in this study. A total of 17 variables, listed in Table 1, were ultimately selected, encompassing general patient information (age, sex, body mass index [BMI], Barthel Index score, presence of underlying diseases or organ failure, and use of vasoactive agents); nutrition-related parameters (nutritional risk screening score [16], serum albumin level and total lymphocyte count [17], route of nutritional administration, and type of nutritional support [18]); and skin-related parameters (Braden score [19], skin temperature [20], and presence of edema [21]). In addition, ultrasound assessment data included dermal blood perfusion, subcutaneous tissue thickness, and tissue echogenicity [22].

Research tools

General information questionnaire

The collected data includes the following variables: age, sex, BMI, admission diagnosis, mechanical ventilation status, serum albumin level, total lymphocyte count, route of nutritional administration, type of nutritional support, Braden score, skin temperature and moisture, presence of edema, as well as ultrasound-based parameters such as dermal blood perfusion and subcutaneous tissue thickness.

The Braden Scale [19] is a widely recognized clinical risk assessment tool for predicting the development of pressure injuries and plays an important role in the prevention and management of skin failure among hospitalized elderly patients. It comprises six domains—sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, and friction/shear—each scored from 1 to 4, yielding a total score ranging from 6 to 23. Lower total scores indicate a greater risk of skin injury, with risk categories defined as follows: mild risk (15–18), moderate risk (13–14), high risk (10–12), and very high risk (≤ 9).

Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002)

The NRS-2002 [16] is a validated tool designed to assess a patient’s nutritional risk. It evaluates recent nutritional status by considering the severity of the primary disease and its influence on nutritional condition, changes in body weight over the preceding 1–3 months, alterations in dietary intake during the previous week, and BMI. In addition, age is included as an independent risk factor, with 1 point added for patients aged 70 years or older. A total score of ≥ 3 indicates the presence of nutritional risk and the need for nutritional intervention, whereas a score < 3 suggests no significant nutritional risk, with weekly reassessment recommended. In this study, a total score of ≥ 3 is defined as the presence of nutritional risk, which serves as a primary independent variable for the skin failure prediction model.

Skin Failure Clinical Indicator Scale (SFCIS)

SFCIS [23] is a clinical assessment tool developed by Hill and Petersen for the identification and evaluation of skin failure. The SFCIS incorporates key clinical indicators, including serum albumin, primary diagnosis, presence of sepsis or multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), use of vasopressors or inotropes, and mechanical ventilation. Scores are interpreted as follows: a score of 0–6 indicates low risk; 7–14 suggests moderate risk, warranting heightened vigilance for skin failure; and 15–21 signifies high risk, consistent with a diagnosis of skin failure.

Ultrasound methodology

Ultrasound examinations will be performed using a Hitachi Noblus (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with a 5–18 MHz linear transducer. All sonographic assessments will be performed by two specialized nurses who have completed a two-week intensive standardized training program within the hospital’s Department of Ultrasound. To minimize operator-dependent bias, a standardized measurement manual will be strictly followed, and a senior ultrasound specialist will periodically audit the image quality and data accuracy to ensure inter-rater consistency throughout the study. Eight anatomical sites will be systematically evaluated for each patient: the upper back, sacrococcygeal region, bilateral forearm (volar and dorsal aspects), pretibial area, and heels. To ensure data objectivity, dermal blood perfusion will be categorized using a semi-quantitative grading scale (absent, diminished, or normal), while tissue echogenicity will be used to differentiate pathological states: homogeneous for normal tissue, hypoechoic for subclinical edema, and mixed/anechoic patterns for advanced necrosis or liquefaction. Subcutaneous thickness will be recorded as a continuous quantitative variable. Outcomes

The dependent (outcome) is the occurrence of nutrition-associated skin failure, defined as the progressive loss of skin integrity and function secondary to severe illness. In this study, SF is operationally adjudicated based on a combination of clinical manifestations and a Skin Failure Clinical Indicators Scale (SFCIS) score ≥ 7. To ensure diagnostic transparency and minimize bias, the outcome will be independently assessed by two senior wound care specialists who are blinded to both the ultrasound data and nutritional risk (NRS-2002) scores. In cases of disagreement, a third expert will be consulted to reach a consensus, ensuring the highest level of diagnostic rigor..

Statistical analysis

A database will be established using Epidata 3.1 software. Double data entry will be performed by two independent operators, followed by consistency checks to verify and correct any discrepancies. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 24.0. Univariate analysis, multicollinearity analysis, and logistic regression will be employed to identify risk factors. Variables demonstrating statistical significance in the univariate analysis will be initially assessed for multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor (VIF); variables with a VIF of ≥ 5 will be deemed to exhibit significant multicollinearity and will be excluded prior to the logistic regression analysis.

Using R software (version 3.5.3), patients will be randomly divided into a training set and a validation set at a ratio of 7:3. Based on the training set, a predictive model will be developed. The importance of risk factors will be ranked by calculating the mean decrease in accuracy of the random forest model following variable permutation. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve will be computed to assess discriminatory performance. Utilizing the validation set, a confusion matrix will be constructed to compare actual versus predicted classifications. Model performance will be evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and the area under the ROC curve. A p-value of < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study will established an early prediction model for nutrition-associated SF in hospitalized elderly patients. While numerous nutritional and metabolic indicators have been proposed in the literature, the strength of evidence varies significantly. For instance, while certain metabolic supplements have established robust evidence for enhancing muscle function, many indicators for predicting SF still require further validation to establish strong clinical correlations. Systemic infections, notably Clostridioides difficile infection, further exacerbate the risk of skin failure by increasing the length of hospitalization and potentially compromising skin integrity in vulnerable elderly populations. Clostridioides difficile infection has been associated with limited rehabilitation outcomes and reduced patient compliance due to isolation-related distress, highlighting the importance of integrating infectious risk into comprehensive early warning systems [24]. The indicators proposed in this study—specifically dermal blood perfusion and tissue elastography—address this gap by providing clinically novel and objective information regarding microcirculatory status and biomechanical properties. Unlike conventional biochemical markers (e.g., serum albumin) that reflect systemic trends, these sonographic parameters offer localized, real-time insights into tissue vulnerability. Therefore, this trial is expected to advance current knowledge by shifting the diagnostic focus from systemic metabolic deficit to early subclinical tissue compromise, facilitating a more proactive and precise approach to geriatric skin care.

Established tools, such as the SFCIS developed by Hill et al., have utilized clinical variables like serum albumin to categorize the likelihood of skin failure [23]. While the SFCIS enhances the differentiation between skin failure and pressure injuries—thereby reducing the clinical misconception that all breakdown results from inadequate care—its reliance on general clinical data may limit its precision in specific cohorts. Notably, the SFCIS was validated using emergency department data, which may not fully capture the complex, chronic physiological shifts seen in the hospitalized geriatric population. This study will build upon such foundational work by integrating multi-parametric ultrasound assessments with traditional indicators. Unlike the SFCIS, which relies on systemic parameters, the inclusion of dermal blood perfusion and tissue elastography provides clinically novel information regarding localized tissue vulnerability. This trial will be expected to advance the field by refining the predictive accuracy for elderly patients, transitioning from generalized clinical scoring to an objective, tissue-specific evaluation that accounts for the unique metabolic and microcirculatory profiles of hospitalized older adults.

The use of ultrasonography is supported by its documented precision, accuracy, and reliability. Evidence indicates that high-resolution ultrasound is a highly reproducible method (ICC: 0.78–0.97) for assessing tissue morphology and detecting subclinical alterations often missed by physical examination. This reinforces its utility as an objective bedside tool for monitoring skin integrity [25]. High-frequency ultrasound has emerged as a cornerstone in dermatological assessment, offering a non-invasive "gold standard" for quantifying the thickness of the epidermis, dermis, and subcutaneous layers [26]. Furthermore, by analyzing tissue echogenicity, ultrasound can effectively differentiate pathological changes: a normal dermis appears homogeneous and moderately echogenic; tissue edema is hypoechoic, fibrotic areas are hyperechoic, while necrotic or liquefied regions manifest as anechoic or exhibit mixed echogenicity. This characteristic provides an imaging basis for distinguishing various stages of skin failure, such as reversible edema versus irreversible necrosis [27]. Complementing these structural insights, advanced functional techniques such as superb microvascular imaging (SMI) allow for the precise, non-invasive quantification of millimeter-level microvessels, providing essential data on cutaneous hypoperfusion that traditional clinical observation cannot detect [28, 29]. Although ultrasound is currently utilized in pressure injury assessment, there remains a notable lack of studies systematically integrating the aforementioned quantitative ultrasound parameters for the early warning and diagnostic grading of skin failure. This study will advance current knowledge, transitioning the diagnostic paradigm from subjective visual inspection to objective, evidence-based grading of tissue compromise in its subclinical stages.

In summary, traditional nutritional assessments—including screening scales, BMI, and systemic biomarkers such as albumin—primarily reflect generalized metabolic status rather than the specific perfusion and oxygenation of peripheral tissues. Consequently, these indicators often fail to provide a localized perspective on skin integrity. Ultrasound technology addresses this critical limitation by serving as an objective, quantitative tool: dermal thickness provides a direct proxy for protein reserves, while subcutaneous fat thickness reflects systemic caloric reserves. This study will represent the inaugural attempt to incorporate these sonographic indicators into a predictive model, thereby achieving objective quantification in the modeling process.

Limitations of this study include its single-center design, with data derived solely from one municipal tertiary hospital, which may not be representative of the broader elderly patient population across the city. Second, due to equipment constraints, tissue assessment was restricted to localized skin blood flow and structural markers, without the capacity to measure systemic perfusion pressure. Furthermore, as a pilot investigation, the clinical correlations established between certain sonographic parameters and SF remain exploratory. While these indicators provide promising "new information" regarding tissue integrity, their diagnostic robustness requires further validation in larger, multi-center cohorts to achieve the level of consistent evidence seen in more established metabolic markers.

The development of an early warning model for skin failure has the potential to significantly enhance the quality and safety of care for hospitalized elderly patients, effectively reducing the incidence of severe complications such as skin injuries and infections. By facilitating a transition from subjective nursing assessments to objective quantification, this model provides a standardized framework for identifying patients at high risk of skin failure versus those with avoidable pressure injuries. Such differentiation is critical not only for clinical decision-making but also for clarifying nursing responsibilities and mitigating potential medical-legal disputes. Once standardized, the research outcomes can be disseminated through training programs to medical institutions at all levels across the country, supporting the national healthy aging strategy and demonstrating significant social value.
