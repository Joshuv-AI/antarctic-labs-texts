Joint accumulation and sustained remission in early rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Hyoun-Ah Kim
Published: 2026

Early rheumatoid arthritis (eRA) has long been regarded as a critical therapeutic window during which timely intervention may fundamentally alter the disease trajectory [1]. Within this early phase, increasing attention has been directed toward even shorter symptom durations—often referred to as very eRA (veRA)—in which immunologic processes may still be relatively plastic [2,3]. Yet even under treat-to-target strategies, sustained remission is heterogeneously achieved in practice, and the clinical meaning of “remission” differs depending on whether it is transient or durable. In this context, distinguishing short-term from long-term sustained remission is not merely a methodological nuance; it is central to how clinicians should frame prognosis and treatment intensity from the outset.

In recent years, the joint accumulation model has gained attention as an alternative approach to understanding RA progression [4]. Rather than relying solely on cross-sectional disease activity states, this framework emphasizes the longitudinal expansion of joint involvement and suggests that preventing the stepwise accrual of newly affected joints may be essential for durable control. In the present study, this concept is operationalized through the joint accumulation rate (JAR), defined as the time-normalized net change in tender joint counts between consecutive visits and categorized using the patient-level mean over follow-up [5].

In this issue of the Journal of Rheumatic Diseases, Maldonado-Cañón et al. [5] provides timely real-world evidence by explicitly distinguishing eRA (≤12 months) from veRA (≤3 months) and demonstrating that predictors of sustained remission differ materially by disease stage. Using a Clinical Disease Activity Index–based definition—sustained remission maintained for ≥6 months (short-term) and ≥24 months (long-term)—they show that in eRA, short-term remission was more likely among patients with antinuclear antibody positivity and less likely in those with higher baseline functional impairment. In contrast, long-term remission was associated with male sex and lower baseline Simplified Disease Activity Index. In contrast, in veRA, short-term sustained remission was negatively associated with anti-citrullinated protein antibodies, swollen joint count, and patient global assessment, while long-term remission was associated with higher rheumatoid factor levels, fewer tender joints, and—importantly—slower JARs. These patterns remained consistent in analyses restricted to patients who had not previously received disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, supporting the robustness of the stage-specific signals. Several implications follow. First, the study reinforces that “early control” and “durable control” are not interchangeable outcomes: factors linked to achieving remission within months can differ from those linked to sustaining remission over years. Second, the association between long-term remission and a slower JAR in veRA offers concrete real-world support for a dynamic disease framework, suggesting that the durability of remission may depend not only on suppressing disease activity but also on limiting the longitudinal accrual of symptomatic joints over time. Clinically, however, translating JAR into routine workflows will require careful consideration. Because JAR is derived from serial tender joint assessments across visits, its feasibility and standardization in busy practice settings will depend on how consistently joint counts are performed and recorded within routine care pathways.

The retrospective nature of the study and the absence of standardized treatment algorithms reflect real-world clinical practice but inevitably introduce residual confounding. In addition, JAR was derived from serial clinical joint assessments, which may be influenced by interobserver variability and visit intervals. The lack of imaging data further limits the ability to distinguish true inflammatory joint accumulation from subclinical synovitis or noninflammatory pain. Finally, although the study focused on clinical remission, patient-reported outcomes beyond global assessment were not systematically incorporated, which may have provided additional insight into the durability and patient relevance of sustained remission.

Despite these limitations, the study by Maldonado-Cañón et al. [5] provides important conceptual and practical insights into remission biology in eRA and veRA. By demonstrating that predictors of remission differ according to disease stage and by highlighting the role of joint accumulation dynamics in long-term outcomes, the authors move the discussion beyond static disease activity targets toward a longitudinal, time-sensitive framework of disease control [1,2,6,7]. Although JAR is not yet a bedside metric, its grounding in routinely assessed joint counts renders it a clinically accessible concept that may help refine treat-to-target strategies in the earliest phases of RA. Ultimately, integrating longitudinal joint dynamics with clinical, imaging, and biomarker data may be key to achieving truly durable remission and to individualizing treatment intensity during the critical early window of disease.
