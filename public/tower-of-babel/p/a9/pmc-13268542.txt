Trap shape affects efficacy of sticky traps for New World screwworm, Cochliomyia hominivorax (Diptera: Calliphoridae)
Authors: Paul V Hickner, Pamela L Phillips, Eduardo Ibarra Cerdas, Lucas Pacheco, Carlos Sanchez Ortiz, Perot Saelao, John B Welch, Phillip Kaufman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNew World screwworm, Cochliomyia hominivorax (Coquerel), is an obligate parasite of warm-blooded animals and a serious threat to livestock, wild animals, and humans. The sterile insect technique (SIT) was used to eradicate screwworm from North and Central America during the mid-1900s to the early-2000s. Traps are an important tool for screwworm control programs, where they are used for surveillance, evaluation of production strains, and to estimate the ratio of wild to sterile flies following aerial and ground releases. Here, we tested two trap designs for capturing New World screwworm. The vertical sticky trap, the trap currently used, is a triangle-shaped trap with 1,710 cm2 surface area coated with insect adhesive. The cylinder sticky trap is a cylinder-shaped trap with 1,700 cm2 surface area coated with the same insect adhesive. Both traps were baited with swormlure-4 as an attractant. We used a release-recapture study with a randomized block design to test the efficacy of the two trap designs. The cylinder sticky trap captured significantly more C. hominivorax than the vertical sticky trap, likely due to an increase in the ability of the flies to land on the surface of the cylinder-shaped trap.Keywords: swormlure, release-recapture, sterile insect technique, eradication

Introduction

The New World screwworm (NWS), Cochliomyia hominivorax, is an obligate parasite of warm-blooded animals, including humans, that has caused substantial economic damage to the livestock industries in the Americas. The sterile insect technique (SIT), as part of an area-wide integrated pest management program, was used to eradicate NWS from North and Central America during the mid-1900 and early 2000s (Wyss 2000, Mastrangelo and Welch 2012). A cooperative agreement between the Panamanian Ministry of Agriculture and the U.S. Department of Agriculture was established to prevent NWS reinfestation of Central America by forming a barrier in the isthmus of Panama (Darién Province) using SIT (Mastrangelo and Welch 2012). In 2022, cases of NWS myiasis in the Darién province began to increase, which progressed to complete failure of the barrier in 2023. Over the last 2 yr, NWS has reinfested Central America and southern Mexico (Valdez-Espinoza et al. 2025). Failure to stop their spread in Mexico could result in the reemergence of NWS in the U.S.

Traps are a vital tool in NWS control programs where they are used for surveillance, assessment of production strains, and to estimate the ratio of sterile to wild flies following aerial and ground releases. The first traps used by the NWS eradication program were based on the Bishopp trap, which is a cylinder-shaped trap that uses decomposing meat, usually beef liver, as an attractant (Bishopp 1916). Advancements in trap design were aided by the development of the first chemical attractants for NWS in the 1970s, which allowed greater flexibility because they required less space and preparation, and they were not as affected by variation in temperature and humidity as the decomposing meat baits. Four formulations of swormlure (abbreviation for screwworm lure) have been described, each containing 10 chemicals (Jones et al. 1976, Coppedge et al. 1977, Mackley and Brown 1984, Hickner et al. 2023). A wind-oriented trap (Broce et al. 1977) was utilized until it was replaced by the vertical sticky trap (VST), which is a triangle-shaped trap covered with an adhesive to capture flies as they land near the attractant. Here, we used a release-recapture study with sterile C. hominivorax and a randomized block design to compare the efficacy of the VST and a cylinder-shaped sticky trap (CST) baited with the chemical attractant, swormlure-4 (SL-4) (Mackley and Brown 1984).

Materials and Methods

Cochliomyia hominivorax used to evaluate the VST and CST were the production strain, PanCR-24, which was established in 2024 from 11 lines obtained from myiasis cases in Panama and Costa Rica during 2022 and 2023. Flies were reared at the Panama-United States Commission for the Eradication and Prevention of Screwworm (COPEG) biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) facility in Panama. Pupae were irradiated at 00:00 and kept in a cold room at 10 °C for approximately 8 h. Each of the three releases comprised ten liters of pupae from two ground release chambers. Pupae were dyed either red, blue, or green (DayGlo Color Corp., Cleveland, Ohio, United States) so the flies could be traced to their respective release. Upon emergence, the dye is retained in the ptilinum in the adult fly. One hundred pupae from each release chamber were retained to estimate size, emergence rate, and sex ratio.

The VST is the trap currently used by COPEG. It is a 57 × 30 cm triangle made of black fiberglass sheet covered with adhesive (Novi Glue) on both sides providing 1,710 cm2 surface area to capture the flies. The VSTs were suspended ∼1.5 m above the ground using nylon fishing line (15 kg test) attached to a branch and connected to a swivel at the top of the triangle. A second line was attached to a swivel at the bottom of the triangle suspending a 350 g weight to reduce movement caused by wind. The cylinder sticky traps (CST) were made with PVC pipe 16.8 cm outside diameter and 34 cm in length and painted black providing 1,700 cm2 surface area to capture the flies. The CSTs were suspended using nylon fishing line (15 kg test) at a height of ∼1.5 m. No weight was used to stabilize the CST. On both the VST and CST, vinyl tubing with an inside diameter of 1.5 cm and a length of 7 cm was attached to two opposing sides of each trap using plastic wire ties. Cotton roll, 1.5 cm in diameter, was inserted into the vinyl tubing and 2.5 ml of SL-4 was applied to each of the two cotton rolls. SL-4 was re-applied every 3 d. Release-recapture experiments were conducted at a privately owned ranch in Los Llanos de Chepo, Panama Province, Republic of Panama. Sterile flies were released at the same location for three consecutive weeks (3, 11, 17 September 2024). A randomized complete block design with four blocks was used to evaluate the two traps (VST and CST). Each block was a similar distance from the release site. The blocks were numbered by distance, with block 1 being nearest (∼1,000 m) and block 4 being farthest (∼2,400 m) from the site of release (see Fig. 2). The first release was on 3 September 2024 (day 0). Captured flies were counted daily through 29 September 2024 (day 26).

A map of the release and collection sites. A randomized block design was used, with each of the four blocks chosen based on habitat and distance from the release site. Each block included two sites, one with the vertical sticky trap (VST) and one with the cylinder sticky trap (CST).

Trap captures were evaluated with a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) using the glmmTMB package in R (v4.4.1). The code can be found in Supplemental R_code. The model was fit with a negative binomial (nbinom2) distribution to account for significant overdispersion in the count data (dispersion = 2.54), which violated Poisson assumptions (observed variance was ∼68X the mean).

“Log (E [Yijk] = β0 + β1 * Treatment + uj + vk”, where E[Yijk] is the expected count of collected samples, β0 is the intercept (expected log count for the reference treatment), β1 is the fixed effect coefficient for trap type, uj is the random intercept for release, and vk ∼ N (0, σ2block) is the random intercept for block. The response variable Yijk is a negative binomial distribution to account for overdispersion with a dispersion parameter estimated at 2.54. Model convergence was confirmed (convergence = 0), indicating stabilized parameter estimation.

Results

An estimated 215,742 sterile flies were released during the study, of which 1,298 were recaptured (0.60%). An additional 89 flies were captured that had no perceptible dye and were omitted from the analyses because they could not be traced to their release date. Dissection of the ovaries suggested 75 of these were sterile females that did not retain the dye, and six were wild females. The remaining eight flies were males of unknown origin. The majority of the recaptured flies were collected between 2 and 5 d post-release, and no flies were recaptured more than 9 d after release (Fig. 3). The recapture rates varied across the releases, with 666 of 73,248 (0.91%), 220 of 68,040 (0.32%), and 412 of 74,454 (0.55%) being recaptured from release 1–3, respectively. Of the 1,298 flies recaptured, 417 were collected with the VST and 881 were collected with the CST, which was a significant difference based on the GLMM (z = −3.98, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). We also tested for treatment x block interaction and found that only block 2 demonstrated a significant interaction (z = 2.45, P = 0.014) with the other blocks non-significant (P > 0.05), but the CST outperformed the VST across all blocks. The proportion of females to males was similar for both traps, with females making up 74.6% (311 females: 106 males) of the catches in the VST and 75.1% (662 females: 219 males) of the catches in the CST (Table S1).

Discussion

The CST captured more than twice the number of NWS than the VST during the course of the study. However, the reasons underlying the difference in trap captures between the VST and the CST are not well understood. One explanation is that the cylinder shape produces more favorable dispersion of the odor plume, which has a large influence on the tracking of an insect to an odor source (Murlis et al. 1992, Cardé and Willis 2008). Another possibility is that the cylinder was a better visual target than the flat triangle of the VST, allowing the flies to land with greater success. A fly following the odor plume would encounter the edge rather than the sides due to tendency of the VST to become oriented with the wind. Moreover, as the VST spins, the distance to the surface changes according to the degree of rotation, likely making it difficult for the flies to land on the trap surface. In contrast, the visual target of the CST changes little in the wind, and flies following the odor plume would encounter the same shape regardless of trap orientation as viewed by the approaching fly. The importance of the visual target was previously demonstrated in a study that evaluated NWS behavior to odor-baited traps with various colored targets. Although color had no effect on the number of flies approaching the target, the landing rates were highly influenced by color, with 80% of all landings being on black in comparisons with blue, yellow, and white targets (Torr and Hall 1992).

Trapping will continue to be an important component of NWS area-wide integrated pest management programs as re-eradication of North and Central America is achieved. A dosage of at least 9 sterile males to 1 wild male was suggested to drive NWS populations to extinction using SIT (Knipling 1955), and trapping is currently the only way to estimate this ratio in areas where SIT is being deployed. Trapping for early detection of wild NWS can be effective, as demonstrated by a study in 12 Texas counties where standard collapsible traps baited with SL-2 were deployed prior to the seasonal NWS incursions. Adult NWS were captured before any animal infestations were detected in six of the 12 counties (Coppedge et al. 1978). Our findings suggest that replacing the VST with the CST could strengthen early detection and response protocols by placing them in high-risk areas prior to releasing sterile flies for the SIT.
