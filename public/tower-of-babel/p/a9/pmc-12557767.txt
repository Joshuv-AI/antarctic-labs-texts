Attitudes towards and willingness to work with older adults among nurses in a university hospital, Sri Lanka: a cross-sectional study
Authors: RT Balachandra, RAP Hansamali, SM Dhananjaya, WPIP Premarathna, H S Maliga S K Wijesiri, Sarath Rathnayake
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the rapid increase in the number of older adults living with chronic diseases and disabilities, there is a growing need to prepare nurses with positive attitudes and a willingness to work with older adults. This study aims to examine attitudes towards and willingness to work with older adults among nurses.MethodologyA cross-sectional survey was conducted among a purposive sample of 267 nurses at the University Hospital of Kotelawala Defence University, Sri Lanka. Participants completed a self-administered questionnaire consisting of socio-demographic variables, Kogan’s Attitudes Toward Old People Scale and questions related to willingness to work with older adults. Descriptive statistics and linear and logistic regression analyses were used in data analysis.ResultsThe majority were female nurses (71.9%, n=192) and held slightly negative (58%, n=155) or slightly positive (41.9%, n=122) attitudes towards working with older adults. Only 35.2% (n=94) were willing to work with older adults in this study. A male nurse (beta coefficient (β)=4.644, CI 2.392 to 6.896, p<0.001), aged 30–40 years (β=4.734, CI 1.877 to 7.592, p<0.01) and living in their own house (β=5.071, CI 2.819 to 7.323, p<0.001) had significant positive associations with nurses’ attitudes towards older people, whereas living in hospital quarters (β=−5.358, CI −8.889 to −1.817, p<0.01), having more than 15 years of work experience (β=−16.754, CI −28.926 to −4.582, p<0.01) and having a family income between 50 000 and 100 000 Sri Lankan rupees (β=−4.060, CI −6.213 to −1.907, p<0.001) had significant negative associations with attitudes in adjusted models. Positive significant associations were found only in male nurses (OR=0.473, CI=0.266 to 0.840, p<0.05), living in an extended family (OR=2.290, CI 1.096 to 4.783, p<0.05) and having a family income of less than 50 000 Sri Lankan rupees (OR=0.168, CI=0.038, 0.737, p<0.05), whereas no negatively associated factors were found with willingness to work with older adults in logistic regression models.ConclusionsMost nurses express slightly negative to positive attitudes and have little interest in working with older adults. Various socio-demographic factors have an impact on attitudes towards and willingness to work with older adults. Since nurses play a significant role in providing care for older adults, the development of positive attitudes is essential, and interventions and strategies are crucial to increasing their willingness to work with older adults.

Introduction

Ageing is a normal and irreversible process that occurs within the body, accompanied by numerous physiological, psychological and social changes. The rapid increase in the older population has become a global challenge.1 Between 2021 and 2050, the number of older adults, aged 65 years and over, is expected to more than quadruple, rising from 761 million to 1.6 billion.1 Sri Lanka is considered one of South Asia’s fastest ageing countries.2 According to the most recent census statistics, in 2012, Sri Lanka experienced an increase in the number of people aged 60 years and older, accounting for 12.4% of the country’s overall population.3 Therefore, it is predicted that the older population will again double and represent 24.8% of the total population by 2041 if the current trend in the increase of the older population continues.3 As the life expectancy of a population increases, an ageing society faces several challenges due to an increased risk of various chronic diseases, such as neurodegenerative, cardiovascular, metabolic, musculoskeletal and immune system diseases, which may lead to disability and death among older adults.4 Therefore, workforce planning is necessary to ensure that healthcare systems have the capacity to meet the needs of the older population, including recruitment and retention of healthcare professionals,5 such as gerontological nurses. Positive attitudes among nurses are important for providing quality health services to older adults, whereas negative attitudes can lead to various problems related to healthcare, financial matters and unnecessary hospitalisations of older adults.6 Literature reported that nursing students showed a poor interest in gerontological nursing as a career choice.7 In Sri Lanka, undergraduate nursing students had shown a moderately positive attitude towards older adults; however, their willingness to work with older adults was poor.8 Due to the rapid increase in the older population, gerontological nurses face several challenges in providing care for older adults.9 There is no well-established specialty in geriatric or gerontological nursing in Sri Lanka, an essential part of nursing practice, as older adults need particular care during physiological and health deterioration. Caring for older adults among nurses is a relatively understudied area in Sri Lanka, and limited evidence is available related to attitudes towards and willingness to work with older adults among nursing professionals in Sri Lanka.8 Therefore, this study was conducted to assess the attitudes towards and willingness to work with older adults among nurses, as nurses are the frontline healthcare workers for older adults in a wide variety of settings.

Aims

This study aims to examine the attitudes towards and willingness to work with older adults among nurses at the University Hospital of Kotelawala Defence University (UHKDU), Werahera, Sri Lanka.

Methodology

Study design, setting and sample

This descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted among nurses at the UHKDU, Werahera, Sri Lanka. The sample consisted of 304 nursing officers from the UHKDU. The whole-sample method was applied in this study; therefore, all nurses who worked at the UHKDU during the data collection period were included. Nurses who were on maternity vacation leave were excluded.

Instruments

The data collection instrument was a self-administered questionnaire consisting of three sections: (1) a socio-demographic questionnaire, (2) Kogan’s Attitudes Toward Old People Scale (KAOP)10 and (3) the willingness to work with older adults among nurses. The socio-demographic variables included gender, age, educational level, type of residence, family structure, work experience and family income.

The KAOP was used to examine the attitudes of nurses towards older adults.10 The KAOP consists of 34 questions (17 positive items and 17 negative items), including three domains: personal appearance, resemblance and the nature of interpersonal relations across age generations.10 It is a 7-point Likert scale, and the scoring of the participants was based on their agreement or disagreement with the questions. The cumulative KAOP score ranged from 34 to 238, with higher total scores indicating more positive attitudes.10 The scores related to respondents’ attitudes were assessed by dividing the potential range of scores into six equal categories based on KAOP scores, namely very negative (score 34–67), negative (68–101), slightly negative (102–135), slightly positive (136–169), positive (170–203) and very positive (204–238) based on a previous study conducted in Sri Lanka.8

The willingness to work with older adults was assessed using a single question developed by the investigators: “If a specialty in geriatric or gerontological nursing in Sri Lanka is established, I would like to work in the above field as my career choice.” The responses of the participants were based on their agreement and disagreement with the question, and responses included ‘Strongly agree’, ‘Agree’, ‘Not decided’, ‘Disagree’ and ‘Strongly disagree’.

The English version of the questionnaire was used in this study. After achieving consensus among the research team, minor modifications were made to the items of the KAOP to ensure readability and cultural appropriateness. We considered an older person to be over 60 years of age. This data collection instrument was pretested among 10 nurses with characteristics similar to those of the study population. This helped to gain feedback on the readability, understandability and relevancy of the questions as well as respondents’ overall impression of the instrument. There were no changes identified.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted by the research team members in December 2021. Permission to collect data was obtained from the Executive Director of the UHKDU, Werahera, Sri Lanka. The participants were initially contacted at the UHKDU and informed about the study’s purposes. The written information sheet was delivered, and the written informed consent was obtained from each participant before data collection. Data were collected from nurses after their duty hours. The questionnaire was hand-delivered to participants and completed without any interruptions to their duties.

Data analysis

The data were analysed by SPSS V.26. Descriptive statistics were used to analyse socio-demographic characteristics, attitudes and willingness to work with older adults. The Shapiro-Wilk test for normality was conducted, and the p value was <0.05. When considering the attitude score, normality was assessed by visual inspection of histograms, Q-Q plots, box plots, skewness and kurtosis, which indicated that the attitude score is normally distributed. Linear regression analysis was conducted to determine the factors associated with the nurses’ attitudes towards older adults, and logistic regression analysis was used to identify the factors associated with willingness to work with older adults. The multiple logistic regression model included the statistically significant variables (p<0.05) from the bivariate logistic regression analysis. The reference groups for variables in the regression analysis were selected systematically, with the group containing the largest number of participants chosen as the reference category, ensuring stability and meaningful comparisons.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The total sample consisted of 267 nurses, indicating a response rate of 87.82%. Table 1 shows descriptive statistics for the socio-demographic data of the respondents. The respondents’ ages ranged from 20 to 70 years, and the majority of nurses were in the age category of 20–30 years (72.7%, n=194). The majority of respondents (85%, n=227) held bachelor’s degrees (BSc in Nursing) and were females (71.9%, n=192). From the sample, 45.7% (n=122) of nurses resided in rental houses and 45.3% (n=121) lived in their own homes. The work experience of most respondents ranged from 0 to 5 years (83.5%, n=223). From the sample, 83.9% (n=224) of respondents had a nuclear family structure.

Attitudes towards older adults among nurses

Most of the respondents reported slightly negative (58.1%, n=155) to slightly positive (41.9%, n=122) attitudes towards older adults. There were no nurses who scored very negative (34–67), negative (68–101), positive (170–203) and very positive (204–238) scores. The mean overall score of the attitudes among nurses towards older adults was 133.24±8.73. The scores ranged from 110 (minimum) to 158 (maximum).

Willingness to work with older adults among nurses

The majority of the respondents (46.4%, n=124) reported ‘not agreeing’ to work with older adults. Subsequently, 27.3% (n=73) agreed, 5.6% (n=15) strongly agreed and only 3% (n=8) strongly disagreed with working with older adults. However, among the total, only 35.2% (n=94) were willing to work with older adults in this study.

Simple and multiple linear regression models were used to identify the relationship between socio-demographic factors and nurses’ attitudes towards older adults. The attitude score was considered the dependent variable. Discussion

This cross-sectional survey examined the attitudes and willingness to work with older adults, as well as related factors, among nurses working in a university hospital in Sri Lanka. The study found a slightly negative to slightly positive attitude towards older adults among nurses, indicating that their attitudes towards older adults were neutral. In contrast to this finding, various studies have reported different results on nurses’ attitudes towards older adults. A study conducted in Egypt by Mohammed et al11 showed a negative attitude towards older adults among nursing students, whereas studies conducted in China12 and Sri Lanka8 found a medium level of attitude and a slightly positive attitude towards older adults, respectively. However, many studies conducted among nursing students and nurses have shown positive attitudes towards caring for older adults.1317 Furthermore, although almost all the nurses agreed that they felt positive about caring for older adults, they reported that providing such care was complex, difficult (73%) and time-consuming (96%) and about 80% suggested that having special training in caring for older adults would help nurses provide more effective care.14

According to the analysis, the current study showed significant positive associations between socio-demographic variables—such as male gender, age 30–40 years and living in one’s own house—and nurses’ attitude, whereas residences in hospital quarters, work experience of more than 15 years and family income were negatively associated with their attitude. Accordingly, the results show that younger nurses (aged 30–40 years) have better attitudes towards older adult care, which reflects their professional maturity and ability to cope with workload. Similar results were found in another study conducted in Ethiopia.18 However, nurses with more than 15 years of experience showed negative attitudes, which could be due to increased workload, role strain or changing personal priorities near retirement. A study conducted by Fita et al in Ethiopia showed that a significant association exists between attitudes towards older adult care and socio-demographic variables, such as being female, having a BSc degree or higher in nursing, living with older adults and providing care for older adults.6 Living in one’s own house was strongly associated with positive attitudes in the present study, possibly due to reduced stress, as they were able to share their role strain with family members; in contrast, residence in hospital quarters was negatively associated with attitudes. With regard to family income of nurses, there was a negative association between an income range of 50 000–100 000 Sri Lankan rupees and attitudes, which may be because nurses with moderate family income may face unique stressors, probably related to cost-of-living pressures.

The ﬁndings of our study depict a slightly negative to positive attitude towards older adults among nurses in the UHKDU and indicate the need to instil more positive attitudes towards older adults among nurses. Therefore, examining any possible reasons behind negative attitudes towards older adults among nurses in this population is essential. In addition to the individual socio-demographic factors among nurses that influence their attitudes towards older adults, it is essential to consider organisational and societal contexts. Previous studies have highlighted that institutional values, staffing-related factors, environmental factors, organisational factors and support systems, such as the availability of age-friendly infrastructure, would enhance the age-friendly healthcare system.1921 In addition, various societal and cultural norms may also affect attitudes towards older adult care in different societies and countries.19 22 Although consideration of societal and organisational factors would enhance the positive attitudes of healthcare professionals, including nurses, towards older adults, this has not been taken into consideration in our study.

Some studies showed a lack of interest in working with older adults, similar to the present study.8 23 However, a study conducted by Tianjin University, China,12 found that nursing students had a medium to high willingness to work with older adults. Zhang et al found that willingness to care for others was strongly associated with knowledge and attitudes towards older adults.12 Our study reveals a lack of willingness among nurses in UHKDU to work with older adults; thus, the underlying causative factors should be examined further. Dai et al7 conducted a systematic review to investigate nursing students’ willingness to work with older adults. Among the 24 studies included, only two12 24 showed that nursing students had a positive attitude towards caring for older adults. On the other hand, one study showed that nursing students’ willingness to work with older adults was negative,23 whereas King et al25 found that this negative attitude was related to working in nursing homes rather than working with older adults. Therefore, most studies presented a contradictory or negative attitude regarding the willingness of nursing students to engage as gerontological nurses, whereas prior clinical experience towards caring for older adults had a greater impact.7

The present study found a significant positive association between male gender, living in an extended family structure and a lower level of family income with nurses’ willingness to work with older adults. Some studies showed no significant association between gender and willingness,26 27 whereas a few studies28 29 showed a significant association between gender and willingness. Some studies showed that males were more willing to work with older adults,23 as in our study, whereas others found that females were more willing.28 30 According to the Sri Lankan context, male nurses may be more capable of physically handling older patients, such as lifting and moving, and may have greater emotional stability, and thus, they may be more willing towards older people’s care than females. When considering extended family structure, the nurses living with extended families are more familiar and comfortable caring for older adults, as they are frequently in contact with older family members. Therefore, the nurses’ respect, patience and interpersonal skills relevant to older adult care may be at a good level, which will enhance their willingness to work with older adults when they are living in extended families. Although the age of the nurses has no significant association with willingness in the present study other than attitudes, two studies showed that younger nursing students were more willing to work with older adults than older students,31 32 whereas Neville showed that younger participants were less willing to work with older people.30 However, similar to our study, few studies found no significant association between age and willingness among nursing students.26 28 29 Similar to the present study, some studies found an association between family structure and/or living with older family members and willingness to work with older adults32 and also revealed that experience in caring for older adults was associated with greater willingness.29 33 A study conducted in India showed that the culture of the joint family increases the bond among family members.15 In addition, the present study showed a positive relationship with family income below 50 000 Sri Lankan rupees, which may reflect that nurses with lower incomes place greater value on stable employment and patient relationships. Hebditch et al found that financial and status considerations will also negatively affect working with older adults.34 Consequently, current literature, including the present study, recommends taking these factors into account to enhance the nurses’ willingness to work with older adults.

Similar to our study, the recent two studies reported that there was no significant difference between educational background and willingness.27 28 However, Cheng et al and Carlson and Idvall revealed a significant association between willingness and clinical practice environment26 33 and clinical experience of student nurses.28 Similar to our findings, Liu et al also reported that nurses’ knowledge of gerontological care, along with clinical exposure to caring for older people, can improve attitudes towards older people, thereby increasing their willingness to work with this population.22 Xiao et al emphasise the need for improving attitudes towards working with older adults among nursing students.32 Findings from another study35 showed a significant association between total family income and the willingness to care for older adults. Similar to our study, these studies revealed no significant association between the level of education and the willingness to work with older adults among nurses.

Based on findings related to nurses’ attitudes and willingness to work with older adults, many studies have highlighted gerontological nursing as a specialty11 and continuous professional development6 as factors contributing to enhancing the knowledge and attitudes of nurses towards older adult care. Moreover, continuing education on quality care would improve nursing practice in the care of older people.14 Further, Shen and Xiao31 suggested designing a nursing curriculum to improve the attitudes of nurses, and Oyetunde et al14 recommended focusing on specialised training, gerontological wards, adequate staffing to enhance the quality of work, productivity and job satisfaction and reduce the stress towards providing quality care. Therefore, inculcating positive attitudes towards older people and improving the willingness to work with older adults in hospital-based care is an important approach to motivate nurses and nursing students during their clinical training.

Strengths and limitations

This study has a few strengths and limitations. This cross-sectional study was conducted in a hospital that is attached to a university. Therefore, the authors find that it may limit the generalisability of findings when compared with the whole country. However, all nurses working in the hospital were selected using the whole-sample method, which minimised bias and enhanced the representativeness of the sample within the same hospital setting. The major outcome variable of this study is assessing attitudes among nurses, and it was measured using a validated and reliable instrument, which strengthens the credibility of the findings. This study used regression analysis to identify factors associated with nurses’ attitudes and willingness to work with older adults, providing a better understanding of these factors. Furthermore, the causal relationships between variables cannot be obtained since the current study used a cross-sectional design. The present study only focused on nurse-related individual factors and did not consider the organisational, cultural and societal influences that may also affect attitudes and willingness to work with older adults. Therefore, the study suggests incorporating these limitations and conducting future research to understand attitudes and willingness to work with older adults among nurses in Sri Lanka.

Conclusions

This study provides insight into the attitudes towards and willingness to work with older adults among nurses in a Sri Lankan university hospital. It concludes that there is a slightly negative to positive attitude and a lack of willingness to work with older adults. The factors associated with better attitudes towards older adults were male gender, younger age and living in one’s own house. Conversely, older nurses with longer work experience, who reside in hospital quarters and have moderate family incomes, were linked to less positive attitudes. Moreover, male gender, living in an extended family and having a lower level of family income were significantly associated with the willingness. Recognising an increased need to develop nurses’ attitudes towards older adults is recommended. Continuous professional development and training programmes are necessary for nurses to improve knowledge and awareness towards caring for older adults. The reasons for poor attitudes and a lack of willingness to work with older adults need to be further explored, and strategies must be sought to address these issues. This study recommends conducting further studies with larger samples and in different settings to determine the factors impacting attitudes towards older adult care. Especially, future research should focus on cultural, societal and organisational aspects to provide a broader understanding of nurses’ enrolment in gerontological care.
