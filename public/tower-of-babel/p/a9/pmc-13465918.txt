Addressing urban child malnutrition through a maternal nutrition lens
Authors: Shubham J. Shivale, Akash Nagar, Supriya S. Desai
Published: 2026

Dear Sir,

We found the article by Kaler et al.[1] evaluating the nutritional conditions of children under five in the urban slums of Patiala to be very engaging. The authors have effectively emphasized the correlation between exclusive breastfeeding, maternal education, and socio-economic status with under-nutrition. Nonetheless, understanding child nutrition in these vulnerable environments should be contextualized within a comprehensive lifecycle framework that encompasses maternal nutritional health and ongoing national nutrition programs. Nutritional factors related to women, such as thinness (body mass index [BMI] <18.5 kg/m2), short stature (<145 cm), anemia, as well as overweight and obesity, significantly impact pregnancy results and growth patterns spanning generations. According to 5th National Family Health survey (NFHS)-5, 57% of women aged 15–49 is anemic, and although thinness remains an issue, the rise in overweight and obesity showcases the dual burden of malnutrition.[2]

Latest NFHS-5 reveals slight reductions in stunting and underweight rates among children, but a troubling increase in anemia among women and children when compared with NFHS-4. Notably, the uptake of iron–folic acid (IFA) tablets for at least 100 days during pregnancy rose from roughly 30% in NFHS-4 to 44% in NFHS-5, although this level of coverage is still inadequate.[2] Sufficient intake of folic acid during the periconceptional period is vital for preventing neural tube defects, highlighting the necessity of enhancing IFA supplementation and dietary guidance.[3]

The “first 1,000 days” from conception to the age of two represents a crucial time to intervene for preventing permanent growth delays and cognitive issues.[4] In this context, the Poshan Abhiyan (National Nutrition Mission) employs a coordinated strategy to manage stunting, undernourished, anemic, and low birth weight by enhancing Integrated child development scheme (ICDS) services, utilizing real-time monitoring through the Poshan Tracker, and implementing behavior change communication focused on maternal and child nutrition.[5] The Child Development Project Officer (CDPO) should oversee Anganwadi services, Providing extra nutrition, monitoring growth, advising on newborn and young child feeding methods, and facilitating collaboration with health-care providers for iron and folic acid distribution and prenatal care.

Moreover, Mission Vatsalya enhances these initiatives by advocating for comprehensive child welfare and reinforcing community-focused support networks. Incorporating maternal nutrition metrics into child nutrition approaches, particularly in urban slums, would thus offer a deeper insight into the factors contributing to malnutrition and improve the efficacy of public health programs.
