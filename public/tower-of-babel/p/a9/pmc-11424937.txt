A case of retinoblastoma masquerading as endophthalmitis: Unusual presentation and clinicopathological correlation
Authors: Ghadah MS AlQahtani, Hind M Alkatan, Saleh AlMesfer, Sahar Elkhamary, Azza MY Maktabi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRetinoblastoma is a serious childhood intraocular neoplasm that can be diagnosed clinically with the aid of B-scan ultrasound, and radiological examination. On the other hand, the differential diagnosis includes benign and other masquerading conditions such as uveitis and endophthalmitis thus adding challenge to the proper diagnosis.Presentation of caseA six-year-old girl presented with leukocoria and decreased vision of the right eye. Patient was diagnosed and treated as a case of endophthalmitis elsewherewith no improvement. She was re-evaluated in our hospital and found to have cataract with ruptured capsule, and no view to the fundus. B scan was conducted and showed posterior cavity mass with calcification, and retinal detachment. Retinoblastoma was suspected and proved by examination under anesthesia. Enucleation was carried out and the diagnosis was further confirmed by histopathological examination.DiscussionDiagnosis of retinoblastoma can be challenging since leukocoria is the most common presenting complaint, which can be also seen in other benign conditions such as Coat's disease. It may also masquerade as endophthalmitis, uveitis with or without glaucoma, and retinal detachment. High suspicious of retinoblastoma in children and appropriate examination and work up with the aid of Biomicroscopy and radiological examination will help reaching the appropriate diagnosis saving patients unnecessary interventions with related morbidity.ConclusionRetinoblastoma has a wide spectrum of clinical presentations and must be ruled out before performing any intraocular procedure.Keywords: Retinoblastoma, Endophthalmitis, Uveitis, Intravitreal injection, Case report, Masquerade

Introduction

Retinoblastoma is known to be the most common primary intraocular malignant tumor in pediatric age group. It is estimated to affect one of each 15,000 to 35,000 newborns globally each year [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. In Saudi Arabia, one child out of each 15,000 live births is diagnosed with retinoblastoma annually [5,6]. In another report, the annual incidence has been calculated to be 1 in 15,000–20,000 live births in Saudi Arabia with more than 95 % cases occurring in children less than 8 years old [ 5,6]. The median age of diagnosing unilateral retinoblastoma is 2 years, and 1 year for bilateral retinoblastoma [7]. More than 90 % of retinoblastoma cases are diagnosed by the age of 5 years [3]. Timely diagnosis and treatment are essential to prevent further local extension, if any, systemic metastasis, and mortality. Commonest presenting complaint of retinoblastoma is leukocoria, followed by eye deviation [8]. Retinoblastoma presenting with eye inflammation is less common, and might delay the diagnosis, expose the patient to unnecessary procedures, and end with extended tumor and worse prognosis.

Here in this article, we are reporting a case of Retinoblastoma presenting at an atypical age and masquerading as endophthalmitis. This case has been prepared and presented in line with SCARE guidelines [9].

Presentation of case

A 6-year-old girl presented elsewhere with gradual decreased vision in the right eye. Her neonatal history was uneventful, and her development was normal for age. There was no history of ocular surgery, trauma, or contact with animals. Upon clinical examination, her uncorrected visual acuity was recorded to be no light perception in the right eye and measured 20/20 in the left eye. Slit lamp examination of the right eye showed retrolental floppy mass with retinal detachment and pre-retinal infiltration while the left eye had normal anterior and posterior segment examination. A clinical diagnosis of endophthalmitis in the right eye was made and vitreous tap with intravitreal injection of Cefazolin (2.25 mg/0.1 ml) and Vancomycin (1 mg/0.1 ml) was performed.

Upon follow up two weeks later, there was no change in her vision. Intraocular pressure of the right eye was documented in that visit and measured 18 mm Hg. Slit lamp examination of the right eye as per her referral report showed clear cornea, deepanterior chamber, ectropion uvea, neovascularization of the iris, dilated fixed pupil, and a complicated cataract.

She presented to our emergency room one week later with no improvement of vision and progressive leukocoria. Her examination findings in our hospital confirmed light perception vision in the right eye and normal vision in the left eye. Intraocular pressure measured 30 mm of mercury in the affected right eye and 15 mm of mercury in the left. Extraocular muscles motility was full in both eyes and external examination was normal. Slit lamp assessment of the right eye showed injected conjunctival vessels inferiorly, a clear cornea, deep anterior chamber with +4 cells, dilated non-reactive pupil, ectropion uvea, and a retrolental whitish hemorrhagic membrane. There was no fundus view in that eye. The examination of her left eye was normal including the fundus examination. Ultrasound Biomicroscopy was performed for the right eye and revealed a posterior cavity mass, a ruptured lens, dense vitreous opacities with area of calcification, an open funnel shaped retinal detachment, a sub-retinal cyst nasally, and mild subretinal opacities (Fig. 1).

A: Ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) of the right eye demonstrating posterior cavity mass and a ruptured lens. B: B-scan of the right eye demonstrating dense vitreous opacities with area of calcification and acoustic shadowing.

The patient was admitted for further evaluation. She underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of brain, and which demonstrated: a heterogeneously enhancing soft tissue mass in the posterior chamber of the right globe with multiple flecks of calcifications, funnel-shaped total retinal detachment with typical low signal intensity compared to the vitreous body on T2-weighted images. The images also showed Fluid attenuation inversion recovery (FLAIR) with increased vitreous signal, heterogenous contrast enhancement, vitreous seeding, and marked blooming hemorrhage behind the deformed thinned lens most likely representing complicated cataract. The presence of intraocular mass with calcifications, retinal detachment, and the signal intensity were not explained fully by the clinical diagnosis of endophthalmitis but rather consistent with retinoblastoma. This was further suspected given the patient's clinical history of leukocoria. A suspicious entry needle site was seen nasally without evidence of extra-ocular extension. . There was no evidence of optic nerve or intracranial invasion (Fig. 2).

A suspicious entry needle site (white dashed arrow).

The patient underwent examination under anesthesia (EUA), which confirmed an intraocular pressure of 21 mm Hg in the right eye and 14 mm Hg in the left eye. The most striking findings of the EUA was the presence of leukocoria, scleral mild elevation inferiorly, and a large whitish mass with hemorrhage behind the lens suggestive of retinoblastoma with anterior chamber seeding of the tumor (Fig. 3).

A: Conjunctival injection inferiorly at the propable site of vitreous tap and intravitreal injection. B: An external photo exhibiting a white mass with hemorrhage seen behind the lens through a dilated pupil.

The patient's condition and the diagnosis of retinoblastoma were discussed thoroughly with her guardians, with supportive clinical and imaging findings. Treatment options were offered along with their risks and benefits, as well as the prognosis. Her parents agreed to proceed with enucleation, which was carried out successfully by the pediatric ocular oncologist.

Histopathological evaluation of the enucleated right globe revealed iris and anterior chamber angle invasion by undifferentiated retinoblastoma and ruptured cataractous lens with leaking cortical substances (Fig. 4). The posterior cavity showed vitreous tumor seeding, epiretinal fibrovascular membrane, and a superficial optic nerve invasion. There was no choroidal invasion, and no definite extraocular extension. The TNM classification of this case according to the AJCC | 2017 8th addition was pT2b (tumor invasion of stroma of the iris and or trabecular meshwork and or Schlemm's Canal).

C: Higher power of the undifferentiated retinoblastoma (Original magnification x400 Hematoxylin and eosin). D: The ruptured cataractous lens with leaking lens material and retrolental vitreous seeding by tumor cells (Original magnification x200 Periodic acid Schiff).

Pediatric oncology team were involved, and adjuvant systemic chemotherapy was recommended. The patient's guardians were involved in the final decision for chemotherapy and accepted the expectations regarding the side effects of therapy versus the benefits in terms of the prognosis. The patient received 6 cycles of systemic chemotherapy. A psychologist was engaged, and the parents were added into a supportive group. There was no recurrence or metastasis over a follow up period of 6 years.

Discussion

Retinoblastoma is a common intraocular malignancy of childhood that has 4 types depending on its growth pattern: endophytic (towards the vitreous cavity), exophytic (towards the sub-retinal space), combined endophytic-exophytic, and diffuse infiltrating type. Diffuse infiltrating retinoblastoma is the least common among the 4 types, and it is the most likely to have anterior segment seeding [[9], [10], [11]].

Retinoblastoma is diagnosed clinically with the aid of B-scan ultrasound, computed tomography scan (CT) and MRI to assess its extension [12,13]. Intraocular interventions must be avoided in these cases because of the non-cohesive nature of this tumor and the risk of seeding into the extra-ocular tissues [[12], [13], [14]].

Diagnosis of retinoblastoma might become challenging in the presence of anterior segment or vitreous seeding leading to the misdiagnosis of endophthalmitis [9,10,15,16]. Shields et all described the clinical features of diffuse infiltrating retinoblastoma, which shares presenting symptoms and clinical signs with endophthalmitis; conjunctival injection in 18 %, pseudo hypopyon in 32 %, and vitreous seeds (which can mimic vitritis) in 91 % of their included eyes [9]. Stafford et al. showed that 41 (6.6 %) out of 618 retinoblastoma cases were misdiagnosed and mistreated as ocular inflammation; with endophthalmitis being the initial diagnosis in 14 of them [17]. In a retrospective study done by Kaliki et al., 14 patients were exposed to intraocular surgeries prior to being diagnosed with retinoblastoma [12]. The most frequent misdiagnosis in those patients was endophthalmitis in 4 cases, with the most common surgery being pars plana vitrectomy with or without lensectomy in 6 patients [12]. Meel et al. also found endophthalmitis was the most prevalent misdiagnosis in retinoblastoma patients aged more than 6 years [18]. In addition, endophthalmitis and sympathetic ophthalmia were the initially suspected diagnoses of an 8-years-old girl who underwent pars plans vitrectomy, then retinoblastoma was considered after observing a mass intra-operatively [19].

The misdiagnosis of endophthalmitis in retinoblastoma cases, however, should not be common in the presence of the typical leukocoria as the commonest complaint followed by eye deviation in children below the age of 5 years and associated with decreased vision in older children [13,18]. On the other hand, endophthalmitis should classically presents with swelling, pain, and redness even though atypical presentations might exist [12,20,21]. Diffuse infiltrating retinoblastoma and retinoblastoma with necrosis resulting in secondary inflammation can still mimic endophthalmitis -as in our patient- with possible lack of a retinal mass initially, and such cases need a sophisticated ocular oncologist to pick up the accurate diagnosis especially in the presence of associated orbital cellulitis picture [12,22].

It is worth mentioning that in the absence of history of trauma or surgery at first presentation in our patient, endogenous endophthalmitis should have been considered to be least likely in a systemically healthy child. Azar et al. investigated 26 endophthalmitis cases among children in a single center, and 3 cases only had endogenous endophthalmitis; secondary to septicemia in 2 children, and meningitis in 1 patient [20]. The misdiagnosis of endophthalmitis and the subsequent vitreous tap and injection in our patient resulted in the need for systemic chemotherapy after enucleation with its potential adverse events such as neutropenia, infection, thrombocytopenia, and need of blood transfusion [23].

Moreover, retinoblastoma patients can present with angle closure glaucoma [24,25]. This misdiagnosis can also be associated with the misdiagnosis of uveitis reaching up to 9 % of diffuse retinoblastoma [ 9,22,[26], [27], [28]]. This is more evident in older children between the age of 5 to 12 years, when retinoblastoma is less suspected [28].

Apart from endophthalmitis and uveitis as common masquerading conditions, retinoblastoma can be misdiagnosed as retinal detachment, retrolental fibroplasia, cataract, traumatic hyphema, phthisis bulbi, Coats disease, ocular toxoplasmosis, and Von Hippel's disease [12,17]. Even though the outcome in our case was favorable, misdiagnosis of retinoblastoma exposes the patients to unnecessary procedures and additional treatment modalities with higher risks and burden [12,26].

Kaliki et al. reported 14 patients who underwent intra-ocular surgeries prior to their accurate diagnosis of retinoblastoma, 8 (57 %) died over 27 months mean period of follow-ups; due to advanced disease despite receiving multimodal treatment with the mean duration between the surgery and the treatment being 7 months [12].

Engaging parents and patients' families during care of any pediatric patient is extremely important including history taking. We believe that overlooking the observed leukocoria by the parents by ophthalmologists in the other healthcare facility has aided in the misleading diagnosis of endophthalmitis without suspecting a tumor. The age of the patient at presentation might have been an additional reason for lower suspicious index for retinoblastoma. However, this case carries an important message to pay attention to clinical and ancillary tests fine details as we believe that the some of the initial B scan findings were unfortunately overlooked. This should be aided by expanded knowledge about diffuse retinoblastoma, which may lack a definite mass on imaging in order to be able to accurately diagnose these masquerading cases. It is worth mentioning that our case had a higher risk for extraocular extension in relation to the point of entry of the vitreous tap and intravitreal injections and was subjected to systemic chemotherapy that might have been otherwise avoided if accurately diagnosed.

The limitation of this case report is the lack of fine clinical details at the initial presentation since she was first evaluated elsewhere. However, the availability of comprehensive radiological data, expert histopathological evaluation, and long follow up period all add strength to our case. In addition, the novelty of this case is related to the late presentation of RB, the atypical presentation, and the long term follow up of 6 years with good outcome despite the misdiagnosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, retinoblastoma can clinically simulate various ocular conditions. Retinoblastoma must be considered within the differential diagnoses of any clinical picture that does not fully match the clinical course, especially in children older than 5 years of age. Once retinoblastoma is suspected, any intraocular intervention should be deferred till the diagnosis is confirmed to avoid further risk of morbidity and mortality. Upon suspicion of retinoblastoma, suggestive signs in imaging must be looked for, and opinion of an ocular oncologist must be sought. Clinical suspicion of retinoblastoma, timely accurate diagnosis, and prompt treatment are essential to save patients' lives.
