Prevalence of acquired resistance to HIV integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) in clinical samples from treatment-experienced patients in Chile, 2012–2023
Authors: P Ferrer, V Ramos, M Durán, D Maureira, C Beltrán, A Afani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntegrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) are widely used in HIV treatment, yet few large-scale studies have examined acquired resistance (AR) at the population level. This study assessed the prevalence, patterns, and clinical implications of AR to INSTIs in Chile over a 12-year period.MethodsWe analyzed 5266 genotypic sequences from ART-experienced individuals with confirmed virological failure and INSTI-based regimens between 2012 and 2023. Resistance-associated mutations were classified using the Stanford HIVdb algorithm. Resistance trends were compared between pre-pandemic (2012–2019) and post-pandemic (2020–2023) periods.ResultsHigh AR rates were observed for first-generation INSTIs: raltegravir (28 %) and elvitegravir (27.7 %). Lower resistance levels were found for dolutegravir (8.3 %), bictegravir (8.3 %), and cabotegravir (18.7 %). Major resistance mutations included N155H (25.2 %), Q148 H/K/R (17.2 %), and Y143R (14.3 %). Notably, Q148 H/K/R mutations were always found in combination with other major mutations. Subtype-specific differences were observed, with higher first-generation INSTI resistance in subtype F (55.6 %) than in subtype B (23.9 %). Post-pandemic resistance increased significantly for first-generation INSTIs (25 % vs. 31 %, p < 0.05). The mutation N155H, while not impacting DTG or BIC significantly, conferred intermediate resistance to CAB. CAB resistance was detected despite its absence in treatment protocols, likely due to cross-resistance pathways.ConclusionsAcquired INSTIs resistance in Chile is high, especially for first-generation drugs. These findings highlight the need for nationwide molecular surveillance and pre-treatment genotyping, particularly before initiating long-acting regimens such as CAB-LA. Strategic monitoring will be critical to maintaining the efficacy of current and next-generation INSTIs.

Introduction

The success of antiretroviral therapy (ART) lies in achieving sustained virological suppression over time. This is accompanied by immune system recovery, reduced morbidity and mortality, and an overall improvement in quality of life.1 The most effective way to assess treatment success has been through regular monitoring of plasma viral load and CD4+ counts, typically performed every six months in people living with HIV (PLWH) undergoing ART.2 The primary treatment goal is to achieve undetectable viral load as early as possible.

One of the main challenges threatening ART success is virological failure (VF), which must be carefully evaluated to determine whether it is due to poor adherence or the emergence of antiretroviral resistance.3, 4, 5

Currently, many drugs are available for the treatment of HIV, grouped into four major classes: reverse transcriptase inhibitors (RTIs), protease inhibitors (PIs), and integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs). INSTIs act by blocking the integration of proviral DNA into the host genome by binding to the viral integrase catalytic site, thereby preventing viral replication. INSTIs are subdivided into first-generation agents, raltegravir (RAL) and elvitegravir (EVG), and second-generation agents, dolutegravir (DTG), bictegravir (BIC), and cabotegravir (CAB).6, 7, 8, 9

In Chile, the use of INSTIs began in 2008 with the introduction of RAL, initially employed as salvage therapy in heavily treatment-experienced PLWH with VF and resistance to both RTIs and PIs.9,10 EVG became available in 2013, co-formulated with cobicistat (COBI), emtricitabine (FTC), and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) in a fixed-dose combination single-tablet regimen. DTG was approved in 2015, both as a standalone formulation and co-formulated with abacavir (ABC) and lamivudine (3 TC) as triple therapy, or with 3 TC alone as dual therapy.8 In 2020, BIC was approved in combination with FTC and tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) and on December 2023, CAB was approved in Chile like medication.9

Integrase genotyping has been available in Chile since 2012, initially used to assess patients on RAL with VF. Major resistance mutations such as N155H, Q148 H/K/R, Y143 H/C/R, and G140S were identified.10 These mutations confer high-level resistance to RAL and EVG due to their low genetic barrier. When two or more major mutations accumulate, cross-resistance to DTG and BIC has also been observed.12

To better characterize these preliminary findings on INSTIs resistance, all samples from PLWH with VF received in our laboratory between 2012 and 2023 were analyzed. The objective was to describe mutational patterns and evaluate the prevalence of acquired resistance (AR) to INSTIs.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This work was conducted to evaluate the AR to INSTIs in 5266 clinical samples corresponding to 4321 Chilean patients with confirmed HIV infection, all of whom were receiving ART containing a first- or second-generation INSTI. The difference between the number of sequences and patients reflects that some individuals underwent genotypic testing more than once during the 12-year study period. Samples were received under a coded, consecutive, and blinded system, with no direct linkage to prior genotypes from the same patient. Therefore, repeated samples were not systematically excluded. Each genotyping request was triggered by a distinct episode of virological failure, typically occurring months apart. Given the study's objective to maximize the detection of clinically relevant and evolving resistance patterns, each sequence was treated as an independent unit of analysis.

Samples were analyzed in the Clinical Hospital of the University of Chile in Santiago. Importantly, integrase genotyping was requested exclusively for patients presenting VF, defined as two consecutive HIV RNA values above 1000 copies/mL. All patients were ART-experienced and had received a regimen containing an INSTIs, mainly RAL or DTG. Notably, at the time of integrase resistance testing, approximately 26 % of patients had received RAL, 14.1 % EVG, 21 % DTG, 2.3 % BIC, and 0 % CAB.

To ensure methodological transparency and international comparability, the study was structured according to the CEDRIC-HIV guideline, recommended for studies reporting the prevalence or incidence of antiretroviral resistance.13

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our institution whose Hospital Research Project registration number was OAIC 560/12, and the Approval certificate was number 54 (Nov/2012). All patients with genotypification signed an informed consent at the time of sample collection.

RNA was extracted using the EasyMag system. A nested RT-PCR was performed to amplify the full HIV-1 integrase gene.10,14 Amplicons were sequenced by Sanger. Sequences were assembled using RECall software15 and were analyzed using the Stanford University HIV Drug Resistance Database (HIVdb), v9.6.16 Resistance profiles were categorized as susceptible, potential low-level resistance, low-level resistance, intermediate resistance, or high-level resistance. For this analysis, the proportion of resistant samples to each INSTIs was calculated based on the total number of sequences analyzed.

Furthermore, resistance trends across INSTI generations were compared between the pre-pandemic period (2012–2019) and the post-pandemic period (2020–2023).

Frequencies of major and accessory integrase mutations were calculated only within the subset of samples that exhibited any degree of resistance to at least one first-generation INSTIs.

Group differences were tested using Mann–Whitney U and chi-square. Significance was set at p < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 10.

Results

Between 2012 and 2023, our laboratory observed a progressive increase in integrase genotyping requests, providing indirect evidence of the growing importance of INSTIs in the treatment regimens of PLWH in Chile (Fig. 1). However, the lower number of samples observed in 2023 reflects a structural change in the national HIV surveillance system. Until that year, our laboratory served as the sole national reference center for integrase genotyping, receiving samples from all regions of the country, including remote locations such as Easter Island. In 2023, the Chilean Ministry of Health established its own center for integrase genotyping, to which all public HIV care centers were required to refer their samples. As a result, our laboratory became limited to processing genotyping requests from private HIV centers. This policy shift explains the reduction in samples processed at our center and does not reflect a decrease in clinical demand or resistance testing activity nationwide.

Annual number of HIV-1 integrase genotypic resistance tests performed in Chile between 2012 and 2023.

A progressive increase in the number of tests was observed, reflecting the growing incorporation of integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) into antiretroviral treatment regimens over the study period.

Among the 5266 samples analyzed, 1472 (28 %) were resistant to RAL, 1457 (27.7 %) to EVG, 435 (8.3 %) to DTG, 435 (8.3 %) to BIC, and 983 (18.7 %) to CAB. Fig. 2 displays the median prevalence of acquired resistance to each INSTIs. The proportion of samples showing low, intermediate, and high-level resistance varied across the five INSTIs, as detailed below: RAL and EVG showed the highest rates of resistance across all levels, followed by CAB, while DTG and BIC exhibited considerably lower resistance. Specifically, for RAL, 16.1 % of samples showed high-level resistance; for DTG, only 2.6 %. The complete distribution of resistance levels—high, intermediate, and low—for all five INSTIs is presented in Fig. 3.

Resistance to first-generation INSTIs (RAL and EVG) was consistently higher than to second-generation agents (DTG and BIC). When examining resistance trends over the study period, resistance to first-generation INSTIs remained consistently higher than to second-generation agents (Fig. 4).

Evolution of acquired resistance to integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) in Chile, 2012–2023.

The figure illustrates the year-by-year evolution of genotypic resistance to raltegravir (RAL), elvitegravir (EVG), dolutegravir (DTG), bictegravir (BIC), and cabotegravir (CAB) among clinical samples analyzed over a 12-year period. Resistance to first-generation INSTIs (RAL and EVG) remained consistently higher, while resistance to second-generation INSTIs (DTG and BIC) was notably lower. CAB resistance was detected despite its absence from national treatment protocols, likely due to cross-resistance mechanisms.

We further investigated whether the COVID-19 pandemic had influenced AR levels before and after its onset. Resistance rates from 2012 to 2019 (pre-COVID-19; n = 2700) were lower than those from 2020 to 2023 (post-COVID-19; n = 2147). While median prevalence values for each INSTIs were higher in the post-pandemic period, the difference was statistically significant only for the increase in resistance to first-generation INSTIs.

On the other hand, individuals with AR to at least one INSTI had significantly higher viral loads and lower CD4+ counts compared to those without resistance. The median viral load was 12,500 copies/mL in the resistant group versus 4384 copies/mL in the susceptible group (p < 0.0001). Similarly, the median CD4+ count was lower in resistant individuals (197 cells/μL) than in susceptible individuals (242 cells/μL) (p < 0.0001).

Fig. 5 shows the frequencies of major resistance-associated mutations in the integrase gene were as follows: G118R (0.34 %), E138 A/K/T (9.2 %), G140 A/C/R/S (16.1 %), Y143 A/C/G/H/R/S (14.3 %), S147G (4.2 %), Q148 H/K/Q/R (17.2 %), N155H (25.2 %), and R263K (1.1 %). For accessory mutations, the frequencies were: L74 F/M (9.1 %), T97A (21.8 %), G163 E/K/R/S/T (39.2 %), E157K/Q (6.5 %), S232R (3.9 %), and D232N (10.8 %).

Frequency of individual major integrase resistance mutations among samples with acquired INSTI resistance.

This figure shows the frequency of individual major HIV-1 integrase resistance mutations identified in 1472 clinical samples with acquired resistance to at least one integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTIs). The most common mutations were N155H (25.2 %), Q148 H/K/R (17.2 %), G140 A/C/R/S (16.1 %), and Y143 A/C/G/H/R/S (14.3 %). Other detected mutations included E138 A/K/T, S147G, R263K, and G118R.

Most samples with high or intermediate resistance to DTG harbored Q148H, Q148K, or Q148R mutations, which, alone or in combination with other major and accessory mutations, affected all INSTIs. Conversely, the R263K mutation, which also impacts DTG and other INSTIs, was found in samples with intermediate resistance to DTG and BIC, though at much lower frequency. R263K was more commonly detected as a single mutation, whereas Q148H was frequently found in combination with other major mutations (p < 0.05).

Fig. 6 shows the proportional (%) distribution of the most frequent combinations of major INSTI-resistance mutations. Q148H/K/R mutations were always observed in combination with other major mutations. The most frequent combination was Q148 H/K/R with G140 A/C/S/T (34.3 %). Other common combinations included E138A/D/K/T + G140 A/C/S + Q148 H/K/R (9.8 %), E138K + S147G + Q148 H/K/R (8.4 %), and T66A/I/K/S + E138K + Q148 H/R (5.5 %). Overall, the Q148 H/K/R mutational pathway was the most prevalent, detected in 58 % of cases and associated with resistance to all INSTIs. Additionally, the N155H pathway was associated with E92G/Q in 5.7 % of cases and with Y143 A/C/G/R in 4.1 %. These combinations did not confer full resistance to all INSTIs, as DTG and BIC showed only potential low-level resistance, which was considered clinically susceptible.

Percentages are calculated based on the subset of samples with combinations, not the total cohort of 5266 sequences. The most frequent combinations were: G140 + Q148 (34.3 %), E138 + G140 + Q148 (9.8 %), E138 + S147 + Q148 (8.4 %), T66 + E138 + Q148 (5.5 %), E92 + N155 (5.7 %) and Y143 + N155 (4.1 %). These combinations include clinically relevant substitutions such as Q148 H/K/R, G140 A/S, E138K, S147G, T66A, E92Q, and N155H, which are associated with different levels of cross-resistance to integrase inhibitors. Q148-based combinations were the most prevalent and are known to confer broad resistance across the INSTIs class, while N155-associated combinations represent alternative resistance pathways.

Subtype B was predominant (3882/5266; 73.7 %), followed by subtype F (649/5266; 12.3 %); each remaining non-B subtype was rare (<2 %). In an exploratory comparison restricted to B and F, we observed subtype-specific patterns at several INSTI resistance sites (e.g., higher G163 in F; higher S230/Q148/G140/E138/E157 in B; adjusted p < 0.05 after multiple-testing correction) and different resistance frequencies by drug generation: first-generation INSTIs resistance (RAL/EVG) was higher in F than B (55.6 % vs 23.9 %), whereas second-generation agents (DTG/BIC/CAB) showed similar overall resistance (19.0 % vs 20.7 %).

Discussion

This study determined the prevalence of AR to INSTIs in clinical samples from ART-experienced patients between 2012 and 2023. While preliminary findings on acquired and transmitted resistance to INSTIs had previously been reported in our country,10,11 to our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive national study including over 5000 clinical samples.

By the end of 2023, UNAIDS estimated that approximately 40 million people were living with HIV worldwide, of whom more than 30 million were receiving ART.17 Expanded access to treatment and the use of antiretrovirals for prevention have increased overall drug exposure, potentially facilitating the emergence of resistance. Although INSTIs had already been recommended in several treatment guidelines, it was not until 2019 that the World Health Organization (WHO) formally endorsed DTG as a first-line agent, significantly accelerating global INSTIs implementation. In Chile, out of approximately 91,000 people living with HIV, 65,000 were receiving ART, most with INSTIs-based regimens. By 2023, over 70 % of individuals on ART in Chile and much of Latin America were receiving INSTIs.18

RAL has been available in Chile since 2008, and current national guidelines prioritize personalized regimens that include both first- and second-generation INSTIs as preferred options.19 Initially, however, first-generation INSTIs were used in restricted settings, such as in patients with VF, many of whom had existing resistance to NRTIs and NNRTIs. The presence of these mutations likely contributed to subsequent resistance to first-generation INSTIs.10

Our findings align with this context, showing high levels of exposure to RAL, EVG, and DTG, limited use of BIC, constrained by national guideline, and no documented use of CAB, which remains excluded from clinical protocols. In our cohort, a greater proportion of patients had been exposed to first-generation INSTIs at the time of resistance testing compared to second-generation agents. As previously mentioned, at the time genotyping was performed, the known distribution of INSTI use in our country was approximately: 26 % for RAL, 14.1 % for EVG, 21 % for DTG, 2.3 % for BIC, and 0 % for CAB. Consequently, we observed a high prevalence of resistance to first-generation INSTIs, particularly RAL (28 %) and EVG (27.7 %). In contrast, resistance to second-generation drugs was markedly lower: 8.3 % for both DTG and BIC. These high resistance rates to first-generation INSTIs—which may eventually compromise the efficacy of second-generation agents—can be attributed to many years of widespread use in Chile, which has exerted significant selective pressure on HIV-1. International comparisons are challenging due to differences in how AR data are reported. Some countries report resistance to INSTIs as a class, while others, such as the USA and Mexico, like our study, report drug-specific data. Our findings for RAL, EVG, and DTG resemble those reported in the USA more than those from Mexico. Notably, the 8.3 % DTG resistance rate observed in our study closely approaches the WHO's global resistance alert threshold for 2024.20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26

Temporal analysis revealed wide variability in resistance rates, ranging from 33 % in 2012 to between 5 % and 15 % during 2016–2018. This fluctuation likely reflects the small number of samples analyzed in the early years. Specifically, in 2012, only three clinical samples were analyzed. One of them harbored both the Q148H and G140A mutations, a classical pathway that confers intermediate to high-level cross-resistance to all integrase inhibitors. As a result, the Stanford HIVdb algorithm classified all five INSTIs as resistant in this sample. This led to identical resistance percentages (33 %) for each drug in that year. Since 2017, resistance to RAL and EVG steadily increased, peaking at 31 % in 2020, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic. In contrast, resistance to DTG and BIC increased more modestly, from 5 % to 9 %.

When comparing pre- and post-pandemic periods, resistance increased for all INSTIs, with a statistically significant rise only for first-generation drugs (25 % vs. 31 %, p < 0.05). The increase in DTG/BIC resistance (5 % vs. 9 %) was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). These changes likely reflect treatment delays, reduced clinical monitoring, and limited access to resistance testing during lockdowns.27

The most frequent major mutations in our study were N155H, Q148 substitutions, and Y143 mutations, all classically associated with RAL resistance and EVG cross-resistance, except Y143, which has a different impact on second-generation INSTIs. We also observed high frequencies of accessory mutations contributing to low-level resistance to RAL and EVG without affecting DTG or BIC. DTG and BIC exhibit a high genetic barrier, with very low failure rates and no resistance selection in pivotal trials among ART-naïve patients.

However, their use as second-line therapy following NNRTI failure has been associated with emerging INSTIs resistance.28, 29, 30 Recent reports from Africa and Mexico have highlighted increasing resistance to DTG, and WHO reports rates ranging from 4 % to 20 % in countries with available data.30, 31 A collaborative study in Europe, Canada, and South Africa reported a 6 % DTG resistance rate among viremic ART-treated patients receiving INSTIs.32

Resistance to CAB was detected in 18.7 % of samples, despite its absence from treatment in Chile. This is primarily attributed to the N155H mutation, which confers cross-resistance due to prior exposure to RAL/EVG. Although CAB is classified as a second-generation INSTI, it may have a lower genetic barrier than DTG or BIC.32,33 These findings are relevant for the future implementation of CAB in dual therapy or PrEP strategies.9 Ideally, integrase, RT, and protease genotyping preferably via next-generation sequencing (NGS) should be performed before initiating CAB.12,34 The clinical interpretation of “low-level” and “intermediate” resistance to second-generation INSTIs varies depending on the drug.

Dolutegravir may retain antiviral activity in the presence of certain mutations such as Q148H or R263K, and treatment guidelines recommend intensifying DTG to 50 mg twice daily when resistance is suspected. Bictegravir, in contrast, is only available as a fixed-dose combination (BIC/TAF/FTC) and does not allow dose adjustment, potentially increasing the clinical impact of even low-level resistance. Cabotegravir, which is administered as a long-acting injectable co-formulated with rilpivirine, poses the greatest clinical concern: any level of resistance—low, intermediate, or high—is considered a contraindication to initiating CAB-LA/RPV-LA, due to its long half-life, pharmacological irreversibility, and the risk of VF. Therefore, these Stanford-defined categories should not be dismissed and must be interpreted in light of the pharmacokinetic profile and clinical formulation of each second-generation INSTIs.12,16

Four main resistance pathways were identified: Y143R, N155H, R263K, and Q148 H/K/R.12 N155H was the most prevalent, followed by Q148H, Y143R and R263K. Intermediate to high-level resistance to DTG and BIC can result from combinations involving Q148 H/K/R and other major mutations or from isolated mutations such as G118R or R263K. Interestingly, G118R was detected at very low frequency in our cohort, likely due to the predominance of subtype B, the conservative use of DTG in Chile, and the fact that G118R is uncommon outside specific viral or genetic contexts, more prevalent in non-B subtypes.35,36

Therefore, its low frequency in our study reflects the molecular epidemiology of HIV in Chile, not technical limitations. The low frequency of R263K, found as a standalone mutation, may reflect selective pressure from DTG, in contrast to Q148 H/K/R, which typically emerges after cumulative cross-resistance from prior exposure to first-generation INSTIs.35, 36, 37, 38 Combinations of Q148 H/K/R with G140A, E138K, S147G, or with N155H plus E92Q/G, Y143R, or T66A represent viral adaptations that not only confer resistance but also restore viral fitness.

Codon 155 plays a critical role in the resistance profile of CAB. While the N155H mutation alone has minimal impact on DTG or BIC, it confers intermediate-level resistance to CAB, according to Stanford HIVdb. Additional substitutions at this codon, such as N155S and N155T, also generate low-level resistance specific to CAB. The resistance potential increases when N155H co-occurs with other mutations such as E92Q, Q148 H/K/R, or G140 A/S, which are commonly associated with cross-resistance across INSTIs. This distinct vulnerability reinforces the need for integrase genotyping prior to initiating CAB-based regimens, particularly long-acting formulations where prolonged drug exposure may exacerbate virological failure and select for further resistance.

A key limitation of this study is the incomplete availability of treatment histories for all genotyped individuals, which may have limited more in-depth analyses of resistance selection pressures. However, this limitation does not compromise the accuracy of resistance prevalence estimates or mutational frequency analyses. Genotypic testing was consistently requested in the context of confirmed VF on INSTIs-based regimens, ensuring high specificity in the clinical indication for sequencing. These findings strongly support the urgent need to implement systematic molecular surveillance of INSTIs resistance in Chile—particularly in light of the growing use of long-acting injectable therapies and the introduction of next-generation INSTIs such as GS-1720 and VH-18439

In conclusion, AR to INSTIs in Chile is high, particularly to first-generation drugs such as RAL and EVG. This compromises the efficacy of second-generation agents like DTG, BIC, and CAB. Major mutations, particularly Q148 H/K/R and N155H, in combination with accessory mutations, confer cross-resistance to multiple INSTIs. In this context, systematic molecular surveillance and pre-treatment genotyping, especially prior to the use of long-acting therapies, are essential to preserve the effectiveness of current treatment regimens and prevent the spread of drug-resistant HIV variants in Chile.

Strengthening national resistance monitoring and ensuring equitable access to integrase genotyping will be critical to achieving durable viral suppression and meeting the UNAIDS 95–95–95 goals in Chile.
