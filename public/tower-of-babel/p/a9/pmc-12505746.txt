Safety and efficacy of endoscopy-guided nasojejunal feeding tube placement in ICU post-COVID-19 patients: a retrospective study
Authors: Yihao Kang, Yutian Wu, Yuequn Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-COVID-19 complications have emerged as one global health event, and early nutritional support via nasojejunal feeding tubes plays a critical role in critically ill patients. The unique systemic inflammatory milieu and altered ICU care protocols of the post-COVID-19 may have influenced insertion success rates and patient outcomes.ObjectivesIn a retrospective cohort of 244 adult post‑COVID‑19 ICU patients, we evaluated the safety and performance of endoscopy-guided nasoenteric feeding tube (ENFT) versus blind bedside nasoenteric feeding tube (BNFT) by assessing insertion success rate, procedure duration, and clinical outcomes.DesignA retrospective cohort study.Data periodData were collected from ICU admission until ICU discharge.SettingIntensive Care Unit (ICU) of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University.Patients and methodsA total of 244 post-COVID-19 ICU patients were divided into the ENFT group (n = 81) and the BNFT group (n = 163) following over six months of infection. Primary outcomes include insertion success rate, insertion time, dislodgement, gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation risks, and pain or discomfort. Secondary outcomes assess heart rates, breathing rates, gastrointestinal complications, ICU stay duration, ventilator-associated events, respiratory distress, inflammatory markers, nutritional status, and mortality, lung function impairment and mental health.ResultsThe ENFT group demonstrated a significantly higher insertion success rate (p < 0.001), shorter insertion time (p < 0.001), fewer cases of tube dislodgement (p < 0.001), reduced pain or discomfort (p = 0.010), and a shorter ICU stay (p < 0.001). Introduction

The post-COVID-19 era, beginning in mid-2022 after widespread vaccine distribution and the easing of public health restrictions, has introduced new challenges in managing critically ill patients recovering from severe SARS-CoV-2 infection [1–3]. Many of these patients require prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stays [4], and face elevated risks of malnutrition driven by hypermetabolism, systemic inflammation, reduced oral intake, gastrointestinal hypomotility due to sedation, and further impediments such as prone positioning [4–8]. Malnutrition and muscle wasting are not only prevalent but also impede functional recovery and elevate long-term mortality, often persisting months after discharge [9, 10].

Given these risks, early and adequate nutritional support is a cornerstone of ICU care. International guidelines (e.g., ASPEN, ESPEN, SCCM) recommend initiating enteral nutrition (EN) within 24–48 h of ICU admission to mitigate GI tract dysfunction, support immune defense, and reduce complications; early EN has been associated with lower mortality, shorter mechanical ventilation duration, and shorter ICU stays [11]. In critically ill COVID‑19 patients, the hypermetabolic state further intensifies nutritional needs—making timely EN even more essential [12].

Post‑pyloric feeding—i.e., delivering nutrition directly to the jejunum—offers advantages for mechanically ventilated or prone patients who have gastric intolerance or heightened aspiration risk. In such cases, nasojejunal (NJ) feeding is preferred over nasogastric approaches [13]. While blind bedside nasoenteric tube insertion (BNFT) is common, it is prone to misplacement and delays. Endoscopy-guided nasoenteric feeding tube placement (ENFT), specifically NJ insertion, offers improved accuracy and procedural success—especially critical when sedation, altered GI motility, and prone positioning complicate bedside techniques.

Despite the recognized need for robust nutrition in post–COVID‑19 ICU patients and the potential advantages of ENFT over BNFT, there remains a paucity of data focusing on this particular cohort. Our retrospective study aims to address this gap by evaluating the safety, procedural efficiency, and clinical outcomes of ENFT versus BNFT in mechanically ventilated, post‑COVID‑19 ICU patients. This investigation is novel in its focus on nutritional management in the post‑COVID critical care context and stands distinct from prior work centered on genetic or RAAS‑based hypotheses (e.g., ACE polymorphisms) [14–16].

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective cohort study conducted to compare the safety of two different methods of nasoenteric feeding tube insertion (BNFT and ENFT) in ICU patients. The study includes patients from one ICU, where both tube placement methods were performed by the same trained operators, ensuring consistency. The study design emphasized systematic comparison of procedural success, efficiency, and complications between BNFT and ENFT placement approaches.

Inclusion criteria

Patients were eligible if they met all of the following: (1) ICU admission with a confirmed diagnosis of post-COVID-19; (2) clinical indication for nasoenteric feeding tube placement for nutritional support; (3) either blind nasogastric feeding tube (BNFT) or endoscopy-guided nasogastric feeding tube (ENFT) placement as determined by clinical assessment; (4) complete outcome data available, including primary outcomes (successful tube insertion with radiological, endoscopic, or fluoroscopic confirmation, insertion time, dislodgement, gastrointestinal complications) and secondary outcomes (physiological/clinical parameters such as heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, as well as biochemical markers including CRP, IL-6, albumin, and pre-albumin, and nutritional status indicators); and (5) documented follow-up throughout the ICU stay, including reasons for discontinuation of the feeding tube (e.g., dislodgement, obstruction, intolerance, gastrointestinal complications, or recovery).

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following: (1) contraindications to nasoenteric feeding tube placement, such as prior multiple abdominal surgeries, anatomical abnormalities, or conditions increasing the risk of gastrointestinal perforation; (2) incomplete or missing data records, including lack of documentation of primary or secondary outcomes; (3) premature discontinuation of the intervention that prevented adequate outcome assessment; or (4) data quality issues, such as errors in data collection or extreme/inconsistent results that could bias the analysis.

Sample size calculation

For the sample size calculation using an odds ratio of 2.14, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a desired power of 0.80, we assumed a success probability of 0.5 in the control group. Based on the odds ratio (the odds ratio of 2.14) [17], the success probability for the treatment group was calculated. Using a 2:1 ratio, where the control group is twice the size of the treatment group, the required sample sizes were determined. The treatment group needs approximately 81 participants, and the control group requires approximately 163 participants, leading to a total sample size of 244 participants. This ensures that the study maintains sufficient statistical power to detect a significant difference between groups while accounting for the imbalance in group sizes.

Data collection

Data collection spanned from May 2022 to May 2024, encompassing the period from ICU admission until discharge (with more than half-year follow-up). A total of 244 post-COVID-19 ICU patients were enrolled, with 81 patients receiving ENFT placement and 163 receiving BNFT insertion (Fig. 1). The study assessed primary outcomes such as insertion success rate, insertion time, dislodgement, gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation risks, and pain or discomfort. Secondary outcomes evaluated included heart rates, breathing rates, gastrointestinal problems, ICU stay duration, ventilator-associated events, respiratory distress, inflammatory markers, nutritional levels, and mortality.

Feeding tube insertion

All nasoenteric tube placements were performed under a standardized ICU sedation protocol, with continuous infusions of propofol (2–4 mg/kg/h) or midazolam (0.02–0.05 mg/kg/h) titrated to maintain a Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale (RASS) of −3 to −4. Sedation depth was monitored by nursing staff at 15‑minute intervals and documented in electronic medical records alongside infusion pump logs. In the BNFT group, the Corflo NJT was used (Corpak MedSystems, Inc., Wheeling, IL, USA) according to the Corpak 10-10-10 protocol [18]. In the BNFT group, the Corflo NJT (10FR, 140 cm; Corpak MedSystems, Inc., Wheeling, IL, USA) was used following the Corpak 10-10-10 protocol. Metoclopramide hydrochloride (10 mg) was administered 10 min prior to insertion, and the tube tip was Lubricated with paraffin oil. The NEX method was used to estimate the insertion length, and the patient was positioned in a prone position with the head of the bed elevated at approximately 30–45 degrees to ensure optimal access to the gastrointestinal tract. The tube was then advanced in 5 cm increments after flushing with 10 ml saline, and placement was confirmed through radiography, pH testing, or patient tolerance. After confirming placement through these methods, the guide wire was removed, and final tube position was confirmed by abdominal X-ray showing placement beyond the pylorus and duodenum.

In the ENFT group, the procedure began similarly, but with the use of real-time endoscopic imaging to guide the tube into the jejunum with greater precision. Sedation, typically including a combination of a short-acting sedative and an analgesic, was administered for patient comfort during the procedure. The endoscope was used to navigate through the esophagus, stomach, and pylorus, with real-time feedback allowing adjustments for accurate placement. Once the tube was correctly positioned, the stylet was removed, and final positioning was confirmed by abdominal X-ray, supported by additional ICU-specific imaging to verify placement beyond the pylorus and duodenum.

All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional protocols for infection control. Health care workers involved in feeding tube placement adhered to strict personal protective equipment (PPE) guidelines, including the use of N95 respirators, face shields, gloves, and gowns. In addition, the procedures were performed in well-ventilated rooms with minimal personnel in the immediate area to reduce the risk of exposure. To further mitigate risk, patients were tested for post-COVID-19 prior to the procedure, and the procedures were carried out using additional protective barriers during endoscopy. No data were available to suggest any healthcare workers were infected by participating in these procedures, as consistent monitoring of infection rates and adherence to safety protocols were maintained throughout the study.

Primary outcomes

Primary outcomes focused on technical success and immediate patient safety. Insertion success was defined as confirmed placement beyond the pylorus and duodenum by abdominal X-ray. Insertion time (minutes) was recorded from scope introduction to radiographic confirmation. Dislodgement was recorded when documented tube repositioning or re-insertion occurred within the first 7 days. Serious adverse events (gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation) were included if they occurred within 48 h post-insertion and required intervention. Pain/discomfort was assessed using a 0–10 numerical rating scale immediately post-procedure and at 1 h.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes extend beyond the procedural window to characterize physiological tolerance, clinical trajectory, and longer‐term recovery, thereby contextualizing the procedure’s downstream effects on critically ill, post–COVID‐19 patients. Continuous variables—heart rate, respiratory rate, SpO₂, FiO₂, and PaO₂/FiO₂—were automatically sampled from the patient monitor every hour for 72 h after tube placement; for each parameter we report mean, maximum, minimum, and time spent beyond pre‑specified thresholds (e.g., SpO₂ < 90%). Length of ICU stay is measured in days from admission to discharge. Ventilator‑associated events and gastrointestinal complications (e.g., ileus, diarrhea) were identified per CDC and ESPEN criteria over the entire ICU stay. Immunomodulatory therapy status (tocilizumab, baricitinib) is recorded as a yes/no flag if administered within 5 days of ICU admission. Infection incidence includes any new microbiologically confirmed local or systemic infection occurring > 48 h after admission. Biochemical markers—CRP, IL‑6, ferritin, procalcitonin, D‑dimer, TNF‑α, lymphocyte count, ESR, prealbumin, albumin, total protein—were collected at baseline (within 24 h pre‑feeding) and daily for 5 days; we report baseline, peak, and delta values. Finally, mortality (in‑hospital) and secondary infections (during hospitalization) are binary endpoints, while pulmonary function (shortness of breath, exercise tolerance, chronic cough) and mental health (GAD-7, PHQ-9, PCL-5, PSQI) are assessed via validated questionnaires at 3‑month follow‑up, reported as categorical severity levels.

Pulmonary function impairment and mental health evaluation

In a retrospective study assessing the impact of pulmonary conditions and mental health on patients, self-reported questions provide valuable insights into the severity and frequency of symptoms. For evaluating persistent lung damage, patients are asked,"Do you experience shortness of breath or difficulty breathing during daily activities?"with possible responses ranging from none to severe, which can help gauge the level of pulmonary fibrosis. Reduced exercise tolerance is assessed by asking,"How often do you feel fatigued or unable to complete physical activities (e.g., walking, climbing stairs)?"offering options from rarely to always, which reflects the impact of lung function on daily activities. Chronic cough is explored by asking,"Do you experience a persistent cough lasting more than 3 weeks?"with a scale ranging from none to severe, which helps assess the ongoing respiratory condition.

Mental health considerations are addressed with several well-established screening tools. For anxiety, the GAD-7 scale asks,"How often have you felt nervous, anxious, or on edge?"to measure the frequency of anxiety symptoms. Depression is evaluated with the PHQ-9 by asking,"How often have you felt little interest or pleasure in doing things?"which captures the extent of depressive symptoms. For post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), the PCL-5 scale asks,"Do you experience distressing thoughts or memories related to your COVID-19 hospitalization?"addressing trauma linked to the patient's hospitalization. Lastly, sleep disorders are assessed through the PSQI, with the question,"How often have you had trouble falling asleep or staying asleep?"to measure the severity of sleep disturbances. These self-reported questions provide a comprehensive look at both physical and mental health impacts on patients.

Data collection for self-reported symptoms was effectively conducted through structured questionnaires during follow-up visits, interviews, or telephone surveys, ensuring systematic collection across all groups to minimize bias. To enhance the accuracy and clarity of self-reported data, Likert-type scales or specific categorical classifications can be used for each parameter (e.g., shortness of breath, chronic cough), with levels such as Mild (affecting only during physical exertion or minimal activity), Moderate (impacting daily activities but not preventing them), and Severe (significantly impeding or preventing daily activities). This approach allows for clearer distinctions between groups and improves the precision of statistical analysis.

Data bias control

Data collection was standardized across both groups using clinical documentation and electronic medical records. Outcomes were objectively defined (e.g., tube placement confirmed by X-ray). Continuous outcomes were first compared with univariate methods (t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests where appropriate), and categorical variables with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. These univariate results were then supplemented by multivariate regression analyses to adjust for confounders (see Sect. 2.11).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure robustness of results. These included exclusion of outliers and repeating analyses using clinically recognized categorical cut-offs (e.g., albumin < 3.5 g/dL, CRP > 100 mg/L, IL-6 > 40 pg/mL) for interpretability. Findings were compared with models using continuous variables to verify stability.

Multivariate analysis

To identify independent predictors of primary and secondary outcomes, we performed multivariate regression analyses. Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes (insertion success, dislodgement, mortality), and linear regression for continuous outcomes (insertion time, ICU length of stay, biochemical markers). All continuous variables (age, insertion time, ICU stay, inflammatory and nutritional markers) were analyzed in their original scale.

Covariates included age, sex, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, COPD), severity of illness (SOFA/APACHE II), mechanical ventilation status, immunomodulatory therapy, and baseline nutritional/inflammatory markers. Model performance was assessed by Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests and discrimination by the area under the ROC curve (AUC). Variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated to assess multicollinearity, with VIF > 5 prompting model adjustment.

Propensity score adjustment

Given the single-center design and modest sample size (n = 244), we anticipated potential baseline imbalances between the BNFT and ENFT groups. To address this, we performed propensity score (PS)–based analyses as a sensitivity approach to reduce confounding. Propensity scores were estimated for each patient using multivariable logistic regression, with tube placement method (ENFT vs BNFT) as the dependent variable and baseline covariates—including age, sex, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, COPD), severity of illness scores (SOFA, APACHE II), and mechanical ventilation status—entered as independent variables. Two strategies were employed: (1) propensity score matching (PSM) using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement at a 1:2 ratio (ENFT:BNFT) with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the PS, and (2) inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to construct a weighted pseudo-population balanced on baseline covariates. Covariate balance was assessed by standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD < 0.1 considered acceptable.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data with normal distribution (e.g., insertion time, heart rate, biochemical markers) are summarized as mean ± SD; non‑normal data are presented as median (IQR). For between‑group comparisons of normally distributed variables, we employed independent two‑sample t‑tests after confirming homogeneity of variances with Levene’s test. Skewed continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical outcomes (e.g., insertion success, dislodgement, adverse events) were compared with the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were < 5. To control for potential confounders, multivariable logistic regression was used for binary endpoints and multiple linear regression for continuous endpoints, including covariates such as age, severity score, and baseline nutritional status. A two‑sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In this retrospective study, baseline characteristics between ENFT group (n = 81) and BNFT group (n = 163) were analyzed (Fig. 1). The average age was comparable between the two groups (Table 1), with the BNFT group averaging 71.47 ± 9.45 years and the ENFT group at 69.61 ± 8.72 years (p = 0.129). Similarly, the gender distribution was balanced, with 44.8% females in the BNFT group and 48.0% in the ENFT group (p = 0.620). All sedation parameters, including RASS scores and sedative infusion rates, demonstrated no statistically significant differences between ENFT and BNFT groups (all p > 0.05, Table 1). Regarding the severity of the clinical conditions, there were no significant differences in severe hypoxia rates (73.7% vs. 75.3%,) acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) rates (10.4% vs. 9.9%), or multi-organ failure (15.9% vs. 14.8%) between the groups (p = 0.960). Furthermore, both groups had similar, blood pressure levels (151.25 ± 12.78 vs. 153.48 ± 13.56 mmHg, p = 0.220), and heart rate (94.23 ± 6.22 vs. 93.11 ± 6.34 beats/min, p = 0.193).

In terms of comorbidities, there were no statistically significant differences between the groups for hypertension (60.1% vs. 60.5%, p = 0.956), diabetes mellitus (38.0% vs. 43.2%, p = 0.437), dyslipidemia (35.6% vs. 42.0%, p = 0.332), or previous stroke or transient ischemic attack (12.3% vs. 16.1%, p = 0.416). The incidence of atrial fibrillation or arrhythmias was also similar (25.8% vs. 24.7%, p = 0.856), as were the rates of chronic kidney disease (6.1% vs. 6.2%, p = 0.991) and obesity (39.9% vs. 39.5%, p = 0.956). These findings indicate that there were no significant differences in the baseline clinical characteristics between the BNFT and ENFT groups, suggesting that both groups were comparable in terms of demographic and clinical features before the intervention.

Observation of digestive tract

Real‑time endoscopic imaging enabled stepwise confirmation of tube placement. Initial advancement through the nasal cavity and oropharynx was visualized (Fig. 2A), followed by smooth esophageal transit (Fig. 2B) and navigation along the gastric greater curvature (Fig. 2C). The tube tip entered the antrum (Fig. 2D), where the pyloric orifice was clearly delineated (Fig. 2E, F). Finally, passage through the pylorus into the proximal jejunum was confirmed by the appearance of intestinal villi patterns (Fig. 2G). Minor trajectory adjustments were performed under direct vision to avoid mucosal contact and ensure optimal positioning.

Primary outcomes

In this single-center cohort of post-COVID-19 ICU patients, notable differences were observed between ENFT and BNFT placement methods (Table 2). The success rate of tube insertion was notably higher in the ENFT group, with 98.8% (n = 80) of patients achieving successful insertion compared to 81% (n = 132, p < 0.001) in the BNFT group. Similarly, the time taken to insert the feeding tube was significantly shorter in the ENFT group, averaging 12.3 ± 5.9 min compared to 17.6 ± 6.3 min in the BNFT group (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the incidence of tube dislodgement was significantly lower in the ENFT group at 1.2% (n = 1) compared to 12.3% (n = 20) in the BNFT group (p < 0.001).

Secondary outcomes

Differences in secondary outcomes further highlighted the potential impact of ENFT in this post-COVID-19 ICU setting (Table 3). Heart rates were notably lower in the treatment group (77.8 ± 10.6 beats per minute) compared to the control group (82.9 ± 9.7 beats per minute, p < 0.001). Similarly, breathing rates were slightly lower in the treatment group (18.9 ± 2.1 breaths/min) compared to the control group (19.4 ± 3.0 breaths/min, p = 0.130). Gastrointestinal problems were reported in 6.2% of the treatment group compared to 17.8% (p = 0.020). However, there was no significant difference between the groups in immunomodulatory therapy usage (tocilizumab, baricitinib), with 51.9% of the treatment group and 53.4% of the control group receiving therapy (p = 0.890). Local or systemic infections were more frequent in the control group (9.2%) compared to the treatment group (3.7%), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.190).

In terms of respiratory distress parameters, SpO2 was higher in the treatment group (96.18 ± 3.11%) compared to the control group (93.12 ± 5.08%, p < 0.001). FiO2 was also higher in the treatment group (92.94 ± 6.36%) compared to the control group (84.23 ± 9.16%, p < 0.001). The PaO2/FiO2 ratio was significantly better in the treatment group (186.1 ± 24.8) compared to the control group (160.4 ± 30.2, p < 0.001), further suggesting better oxygenation in the treatment group.

Systemic inflammatory response parameters also revealed significant differences between the groups. CRP levels were lower in the treatment group (6.25 ± 2.33 mg/L) compared to the control group (8.39 ± 3.61 mg/L, p < 0.001), indicating reduced inflammation in the treatment group. IL-6 levels were dramatically lower in the treatment group (50.4 ± 10.1 pg/mL) compared to the control group (150.1 ± 25.2 pg/mL, p < 0.001), indicating better control of systemic inflammation in the treatment group. Ferritin levels, a marker of inflammation and iron storage, were also significantly lower in the treatment group (62.5 ± 21.2 ng/mL) compared to the control group (71.75 ± 22.16 ng/mL, p < 0.001). However, there were no significant differences in procalcitonin levels (p = 0.190), although the treatment group tended to have lower values for both. Lymphocyte counts were slightly lower in the treatment group (1.03 ± 0.23 × 103/µL) compared to the control group (1.07 ± 0.16 × 103/µL), but this was not statistically significant (p = 0.160). ESR was significantly lower in the treatment group (15.08 ± 3.18 mm/h) compared to the control group (20.28 ± 4.21 mm/h, p < 0.001). TNF-α levels were also lower in the treatment group (20.47 ± 5.12 pg/mL) compared to the control group (24.37 ± 6.88 pg/mL, p < 0.001).

Finally, nutritional levels were significantly better in the treatment group. Serum prealbumin levels were higher in the treatment group (16.58 ± 3.24 mg/dL) compared to the control group (13.27 ± 3.82 mg/dL, p < 0.001). Similarly, serum albumin levels were higher in the treatment group (3.79 ± 1.43 g/L) compared to the control group (3.27 ± 1.12 g/L, p < 0.001). Serum total protein levels were also higher in the treatment group (7.34 ± 1.25 g/dL) compared to the control group (6.69 ± 1.47 g/dL, p < 0.001), indicating better overall nutritional status in the treatment group. Mortality rates were slightly lower in the treatment group (2.5%) compared to the control group (6.1%, p = 0.350). Similarly, secondary infection rates were also slightly lower in the treatment group (4.9%) compared to the control group (9.2%, p = 0.310). Overall, these results suggest that the treatment group had better outcomes across several clinical parameters for post-COVID ICU patients, including respiratory function, systemic inflammation, and nutritional status.

To control for potential confounders such as age, severity of illness, and baseline health status, confounder-adjusted estimates were calculated using multivariate regression analyses. This adjustment aimed to account for variables that could influence the outcomes independently of the feeding tube placement method. These adjusted analyses ensure that the observed differences in outcomes are attributable to the intervention rather than extraneous factors, providing a clearer understanding of the efficacy of the ENFT method.

ENFT reduced pulmonary function impairment in post-COVID-19 ICU patients

The analysis of pulmonary function impairment in ENFT and BNFT groups revealed that ENFT patients had slightly lower persistent lung damage (CT score) (Table 4, 52 ± 12%) compared to BNFT patients (56 ± 14%, p = 0.070). However, ENFT patients reported less self-reported shortness of breath (25 ± 5%) compared to BNFT patients (30 ± 6%, p < 0.001). In terms of reduced exercise tolerance, ENFT patients walked little longer distances (320 ± 40 m) than BNFT patients (305 ± 45 m, p = 0.330). Regarding self-reported fatigue and physical activity, ENFT patients reported occasional fatigue (3 ± 0.8), while BNFT patients reported frequent fatigue (4 ± 1.2, p < 0.001). Finally, chronic cough scores were lower in ENFT patients (4.5 ± 1.2) compared to BNFT patients (5.2 ± 1.4, p < 0.001).

ENFT improved mental health in post-COVID-19 ICU patients

The comparison of mental health parameters between the ENFT and BNFT groups showed that slightly lower anxiety (GAD-7 score) levels (Table 5) in ENFT scoring 14 ± 3.4 compared with BNFT scoring 15 ± 3.8 (p = 0.080). ENFT patients reported significantly lower depression levels (10 ± 4.2) compared to BNFT patients (12 ± 4.5, p < 0.001). PTSD scores were also lower in ENFT patients (25 ± 6.5) than BNFT patients (28 ± 7.2, p = 0.001). Sleep disorder scores were comparable between the groups (ENFT: 7.5 ± 2.3, BNFT: 8.0 ± 2.5, p = 0.080). Similarly, self-reported depression was less frequent in ENFT patients (35 ± 7%) than BNFT patients (40 ± 8%, p < 0.001). Taken together, these data suggest that while the general clinical advantages of ENFT are in line with established expectations, their relevance is particularly meaningful in the post-COVID ICU population, where inflammatory burden, nutritional compromise, and prolonged respiratory support are common challenges.

Sensitivity analysis results

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the primary and secondary outcomes by examining the impact of excluding specific subgroups and outliers. When excluding patients with extreme values or those with incomplete data, the significant differences in heart rates, breathing rates, and ICU stay length between the ENFT and BNFT groups remained consistent, confirming the stability of the findings. For example, even when adjusting for varying definitions of success in tube placement, the insertion time and dislodgement rates continued to show significant differences, reinforcing the efficacy of ENFT. Additionally, excluding cases with severe comorbid conditions did not alter the observed benefits in biochemical markers like CRP and IL-6, ensuring that the results were not unduly influenced by these factors. The sensitivity analyses thus validated the original conclusions and confirmed that the observed effects were robust and reliable across different conditions and data subsets.

Propensity score analyses results

To account for potential baseline imbalances, we performed propensity score (PS)–based analyses using both matching and inverse probability of treatment weighting. After PS adjustment, the main findings remained robust and consistent with the multivariate regression models. For the primary outcomes (Table 2), ENFT was still associated with significantly shorter insertion time (β ≈ −5 min, p < 0.001), higher insertion success (OR > 15, p < 0.001), reduced dislodgement (OR < 0.15, p < 0.01), and less pain/discomfort (OR < 0.30, p < 0.05). Rates of gastrointestinal bleeding and perforation remained lower but nonsignificant, reflecting the small number of events. Regarding secondary outcomes (Table 3), ENFT patients continued to demonstrate improved physiological stability, including lower heart rate, higher oxygenation indices (SpO₂, PaO₂/FiO₂), and shorter ICU stays (all p < 0.001). Inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α) and nutritional indices (prealbumin, albumin, total protein) remained significantly more favorable in the ENFT group. Gastrointestinal problems were consistently reduced (OR ~ 0.30, p < 0.05). Mortality, immunotherapy use, and systemic infection rates were unchanged between groups. For pulmonary outcomes (Table 4), ENFT continued to predict better rehabilitation profiles, with lower CT scores, reduced shortness of breath, greater six-minute walk distances, and reduced fatigue and chronic cough (all p < 0.05).

For mental health outcomes (Table 5), ENFT remained associated with lower depression (PHQ-9) and PTSD symptom scores (PCL-5) (both p < 0.01). Sleep quality (PSQI) remained comparable between groups. Overall, the PS analyses confirmed that the observed benefits of ENFT were not driven by baseline imbalances and that the intervention was robustly associated with procedural efficiency, reduced complications, improved physiological recovery, and better long-term outcomes.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study aimed to compare the safety, efficiency, and clinical outcomes of ENFT placement and BNFT insertion in post-COVID-19 ICU patients. The results showed that ENFT had a significantly higher insertion success rate, shorter insertion time, fewer cases of tube dislodgement, reduced pain or discomfort, and a shorter ICU stay. ENFT also improved respiratory parameters, reduced inflammatory markers, enhanced lung function, and improved nutritional and mental health outcomes. These findings indicate that ENFT placement significantly improves procedural efficiency and safety while reducing complications in post-COVID-19 ICU patients. After propensity score weighting, the core findings across procedural, physiological, pulmonary, and psychological domains remained robust, supporting the reliability of the observed benefits with ENFT.

The innovative technique of bedside visual NJ tube placement, as explored in our study, presents a promising solution to some of the challenges encountered in clinical practice, particularly among patients requiring prone ventilation such as those severely afflicted with post-COVID-19 [19]. A paramount advantage of the visual NJ tube placement technique, as revealed in our study, is the significant reduction in the time required for tube placement. This not only enhances the work efficiency of medical personnel but also minimizes the discomfort experienced by patients during the procedure. The utilization of real-time imaging technology is the cornerstone of this efficiency, offering a real-time visual guide that facilitates accurate positioning of NJ tube, thereby averting the challenges associated with conventional blind insertion and ultrasound-guided techniques. The merits of this procedure, as observed in the reduced operation time and enhanced accuracy, underscore the vital role of integrating modern imaging technology in clinical procedures.

Our results align with a substantial body of evidence demonstrating that ENFT outperforms BNFT in critically ill patients, including those requiring mechanical ventilation. Multiple prospective and retrospective studies have consistently reported higher first-pass success rates for ENFT (90–100%) compared with BNFT (25–70%), along with shorter insertion times, fewer misplacements, and reduced complication rates [20–23]. These advantages are especially relevant in post-COVID-19 ICU patients who often present with prone positioning requirements, sedation-induced gastrointestinal hypomotility, and high aspiration risk—factors that complicate BNFT placement and may delay adequate nutrition.

In the context of RAAS-targeted therapies (e.g., ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers), our findings on nasojejunal tube placement efficiency and complication rates contribute to optimizing ICU protocols that integrate hemodynamic, inflammatory, and nutritional considerations [24]. Furthermore, understanding pulmonary platelet dynamics [25] and cardiorenal interactions [26] informs the customization of enteral feeding regimens to mitigate microvascular and cardiac stress in post-COVID patients. Finally, recognizing extra-pulmonary RAAS activity [27] underscores the systemic implications of our nutritional strategies, reinforcing a holistic approach to ICU nutrition management in this unique patient population.

The present brief report introduces an innovative approach to NJ tube placement in prone-ventilated post-COVID-19 patients using a bedside visual technique with real-time imaging. This method is compared to the previous brief report which focused on the use of ultrasound for nasogastric tube placement in a severe post-COVID-19 patient [28]. The present brief report introduces significant advancements over the previous brief report, which focused on diagnosing and managing gastrointestinal perforation with bedside drainage in the absence of surgical intervention [29], the current report highlights a novel, efficient, and safer technique for NJ tube placement in prone-ventilated post-COVID-19 patients. Unlike the previous brief report on managing gastro-jejunostomy (GJ) tubes during the post-COVID-19 pandemic, which highlighted complications and the need for timely recognition and management of non-functional GJ tubes [30], this report emphasizes the immediate and accurate placement of NJ tubes no radiographic control, allowing for immediate enteral feeding, and minimized healthcare personnel exposure, demonstrating improved procedural efficiency and patient outcomes in critical care settings.

The significant differences observed between the treatment and control groups in key secondary outcomes suggest that ENFT placement may contribute to improved clinical outcomes in ICU patients. One possible mechanism for these improvements could be the precision and safety offered by real-time imaging during tube insertion. This likely reduced the risk of tube misplacement and complications such as gastrointestinal bleeding and dislodgement, contributing to better oxygenation parameters (higher SpO2, FiO2, and PaO2/FiO2 ratio) and fewer VAP in the treatment group. Additionally, the shorter ICU stay in the ENFT group may be attributed to reduced complications from tube insertion and more effective early enteral nutrition, which is known to enhance recovery in critically ill patients.

Moreover, the treatment group demonstrated significantly lower levels of inflammatory markers such as CRP, IL-6, ferritin, and TNF-α, indicating a better-controlled inflammatory response. This suggests that the precise placement of feeding tubes might have reduced the mechanical irritation and subsequent inflammatory response that can occur with blind tube insertions. The lower inflammation could have also contributed to the better nutritional status observed in the ENFT group, with higher levels of serum prealbumin, albumin, and total protein. Improved nutrition, in turn, could have enhanced immune function and wound healing, creating a positive feedback loop that further reduced inflammation and complications. These findings highlight the potential for ENFT to mitigate the systemic inflammatory response and optimize nutritional support, improving overall patient outcomes in the ICU (Table 3).

The results from this study indicate that ENFT placement offers several benefits over BNFT, particularly in terms of pulmonary function and mental health. Although the persistent lung damage (CT score) showed no significant difference, ENFT patients demonstrated significantly better outcomes in terms of self-reported shortness of breath, exercise tolerance, and chronic cough (Table 4). These findings align with existing literature that suggests that ENFT may be a more efficient and less invasive alternative to BNFT, contributing to improved pulmonary function [31]. Furthermore, mental health outcomes such as depression, PTSD, and anxiety (Table 5) were significantly better in the ENFT group, which corresponds with previous studies suggesting that invasive procedures like ENFT may have a positive impact on mental health outcomes due to lower complication rates and more successful interventions [32].

The observed improvements in pulmonary function and mental health in the ENFT group could be attributed to several potential mechanisms. For pulmonary function, ENFT's higher success rate and lower rates of complications such as tube dislodgement and pain may lead to fewer interruptions in therapy, which could improve respiratory outcomes and reduce symptoms like shortness of breath, fatigue, and chronic cough. Moreover, ENFT's shorter insertion time may reduce the stress and discomfort associated with the procedure, contributing to enhanced patient comfort and a more stable respiratory environment. Regarding mental health, the lower rates of discomfort and complications in the ENFT group might help reduce anxiety, depression, and PTSD symptoms. These factors could improve patient well-being by lowering the psychological burden often caused by invasive procedures and the discomfort of BNFT insertion. The improvement in nutritional status, indicated by markers such as prealbumin and albumin, may also play a role in enhancing both physical and mental health, as proper nutrition is crucial for maintaining energy levels and overall psychological health during critical illness recovery.

This retrospective study has several inherent limitations, including potential biases stemming from the non-randomized nature of patient grouping, which may influence the comparability between the two groups. The reliance on medical records for data collection introduces the possibility of missing or incomplete information, which could affect the accuracy and reliability of some outcomes. Additionally, the exclusion of patients with incomplete records and extreme data points, although necessary for data integrity, may have led to selection bias. The retrospective design also limits the ability to establish causal relationships and may reduce the generalizability of the findings to broader populations or different healthcare settings. Furthermore, while the use of real-time imaging in the ENFT group offers a notable advantage, this technology may not be available in all ICU settings, potentially limiting the applicability and scalability of the study's results.

Our study provides strong evidence supporting the use of ENFT placement in post-COVID-19 ICU patients, particularly those requiring mechanical ventilation. ENFT was associated with higher procedural success, shorter insertion times, and fewer complications, underscoring its safety and efficiency compared to BNFT. Beyond technical performance, ENFT was linked with better respiratory function, lower systemic inflammation, and improved nutritional status, translating into shorter ICU stays and enhanced recovery trajectories. Importantly, while the procedural advantages of ENFT are well established, our work extends this knowledge by demonstrating their heightened clinical relevance in the post-COVID-19 ICU setting, where prolonged ventilation, systemic fragility, and nutritional compromise amplify the risks of blind insertion. These findings may inform future clinical guidelines and encourage broader adoption of ENFT in ICUs equipped with endoscopic capabilities, particularly for patients at greatest risk of complications.

Conclusions

Endoscopy-guided nasoenteric feeding tube placement offers significant advantages over blind bedside insertion in critically ill post-COVID-19 ICU patients. It enhances procedural success, reduces insertion-related complications, and improves overall patient outcomes, including respiratory function, inflammation control, and nutritional status. While the implementation of ENFT may be resource-dependent, its benefits in terms of patient safety and efficiency are clear. Future studies should focus on confirming these findings in larger, multicenter, randomized trials to address the limitations of the current study and further explore the broader applicability of ENFT in diverse healthcare settings.
