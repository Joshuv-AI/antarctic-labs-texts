Integrative cross-platform transcriptomic analysis coupled with machine learning identifies candidate biomarkers of abiotic stress resilience in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Maryam Mehdizadeh Hakkak, Masoud Tohidfar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnvironmental stressors impose significant constraints on plant growth and productivity, creating an imperative to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying stress tolerance. A major challenge in this domain is integrating large and heterogeneous transcriptomic datasets generated using diverse technological platforms. To address this challenge, we applied a pooled, batch-corrected integrative transcriptomic framework to harmonize microarray and RNA-seq datasets and identify core transcriptional responses in Arabidopsis thaliana exposed to abiotic stress.ResultsUsing variance-stabilizing transformation for RNA-seq normalization prior to integration, our pipeline enabled robust detection of 102 consensus differentially expressed genes (84 upregulated and 18 downregulated). Functional enrichment analysis revealed strong associations with abiotic stimulus perception, protein stabilization, and cellular stress adaptation. Network topology analysis highlighted At5g05410 (DREB2A) and At2g26150 (HSFA2) as central regulatory hubs, suggesting coordinated crosstalk between dehydration-responsive and heat shock pathways. Machine-learning-based prioritization identified six top candidate genes (At1g53540, At4g18280, At5g12030, At1g09750, At2g40610, and At2g03750), with XGBoost achieving the highest classification performance (AUC = 0.98) under internal cross-validation. This performance should be interpreted in light of potential class imbalance, prior DEG-based feature preselection, and the absence of independent external validation. RT-qPCR validation across seven abiotic stresses demonstrated distinct stress-adaptive transcriptional signatures. The small heat shock proteins At1g53540 (HSP17.6 C) and At5g12030 were strongly induced under heat, salt, and drought conditions, while the cell wall-associated genes At2g40610 and At2g03750 were persistently downregulated, consistent with a growth–defense trade-off. Promoter motif analysis further supported these regulatory patterns, revealing enrichment of ABRE and HSE elements in induced genes and ARE motifs in genes repressed under nutrient-limited conditions.ConclusionThis integrative transcriptomic analysis combined with experimental validation identifies high-confidence genes and regulatory modules associated with abiotic stress adaptation in Arabidopsis thaliana. The findings offer mechanistic insights into stress-responsive transcriptional regulation and highlight candidate genes and regulatory modules that warrant further functional validation before consideration for crop-improvement applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08451-8.

Background

Plants frequently encounter environmental challenges, including water scarcity, high salinity, and temperature extremes, which disrupt cellular homeostasis, impair photosynthetic efficiency, and significantly reduce crop yields [1]. These environmental stressors are among the leading causes of global agricultural losses, with reductions in plant growth exceeding 50% in many species [2]. A primary goal in plant biology is to uncover the molecular mechanisms regulating how plants react to environmental challenges, ultimately seeking to create stress-tolerant crops that sustain yields in harsh conditions [3].

Considerable progress has been made in elucidating the cellular and molecular mechanisms that regulate plant adaptation to environmental stressors [3, 4]. Transcriptomic studies, particularly those leveraging next-generation sequencing (NGS), have provided comprehensive insights into stress-responsive gene networks in Arabidopsis thaliana [5]. Advanced high-throughput sequencing techniques have expedited the discovery of critical regulatory genes associated with adaptation to abiotic stress [6], thereby establishing a foundation for enhancing crop robustness amidst escalating environmental challenges.

Although microarray and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) studies have significantly advanced our knowledge of how plants respond to specific environmental stressors [7], many investigations focus on individual stress conditions, often neglecting the complex interactions that occur when plants experience multiple stressors simultaneously [8]. Meta-analysis, a powerful statistical approach that integrates data across independent studies, enhances statistical robustness by increasing sample sizes and uncovering conserved molecular signatures of stress responses [9]. This method has been particularly effective in identifying hub genes that mediate plant adaptation across different stress types and developmental stages [10, 11].

Given the high dimensionality and complexity of plant transcriptomic and phenotypic datasets, robust computational approaches are required to extract biologically meaningful features and capture nonlinear relationships underlying stress tolerance. In recent years, machine learning (ML) methods have been increasingly adopted in plant biology to improve stress classification, trait prediction, and feature prioritization across diverse experimental settings. For example, ML-based analyses such as Random Forest (RF) and principal component analysis (PCA) have been successfully applied to identify key morphological, physiological, and molecular predictors of salinity tolerance in grafted cucumber systems, providing both predictive power and biological interpretability [12]. Similar ML frameworks have been employed to rank stress-responsive traits under heavy metal (Zn) stress, enabling quantitative assessment of tolerance indices and trait importance while accounting for complex trait interactions [13]. Beyond stress tolerance, ML-driven feature selection has also proven effective in dissecting nutrient allocation and transport patterns in grafted plants, where RF models combined with SHAP and PCA analyses identified the most discriminative mineral traits across tissues [14].

Within this context, machine learning–based feature selection algorithms (FSAs), particularly embedded methods such as RF, support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), and XGBoost, offer distinct advantages for transcriptomic analyses [15–17]. Unlike traditional filter- or wrapper-based approaches, embedded FSAs perform feature selection during model training, improving computational efficiency while capturing intricate feature–target relationships relevant to plant stress adaptation [18]. These methods reduce overfitting, enhance model interpretability, and improve predictive accuracy, making them especially suitable for prioritizing key stress-responsive genes in integrative, cross-platform plant transcriptomics studies [19–21].

Despite these advances, several methodological gaps remain insufficiently addressed in current plant stress transcriptomics studies. First, most integrative analyses rely on classical effect-size–based meta-analysis or single-platform aggregation, which limits direct harmonization of expression values across heterogeneous technologies. Second, existing studies typically focus on a narrow subset of abiotic stresses, restricting the identification of transcriptional programs that are conserved across multiple stress contexts. Third, although machine learning has been increasingly applied in plant genomics, it is often used either without rigorous cross-platform integration or with a single feature selection strategy, which may introduce algorithm-specific bias and limit robustness.

Furthermore, a critical conceptual challenge in multi-stress transcriptomic studies is whether to analyze heterogeneous abiotic stresses independently or under a unified framework. While independent analyses highlight stress-specific pathways, they often miss the core regulatory programs that enable general resilience. In this study, we intentionally adopted a pooled ‘Stress’ classification. This strategy is biologically motivated by the existence of a ‘General Stress Response’ (GSR) or ‘core’ network—comprising proteostasis, redox balance, and osmotic adjustment—shared across diverse challenges. Although this approach may dilute signatures unique to a single stress, it significantly increases statistical power and facilitates the discovery of robust, cross-protective hub genes that are essential for survival in unpredictable, multi-stress natural environments.

To address these gaps, this study makes three specific contributions. (i) We implement a true cross-platform integrative transcriptomic analysis (CPITA) by harmonizing Affymetrix and Agilent microarray data with RNA-seq profiles at the expression-matrix level following variance stabilization and batch correction, rather than relying on effect-size–based meta-analysis. (ii) We systematically integrate transcriptomic responses across seven distinct abiotic stress conditions to prioritize a conserved core stress-responsive transcriptome, enabling the identification of master regulators that govern general plant resilience across multiple contexts—a dimension often overlooked in stress-specific studies. (iii) We apply and compare three embedded machine-learning feature selection algorithms (RF, SVM-RFE, and XGBoost) within the same harmonized framework to robustly prioritize stress-responsive genes, reducing method-specific bias. Finally, experimental validation by RT-qPCR under major abiotic stresses provides biological confirmation of the computationally prioritized candidates.

Results

Harmonization of cross-platform transcriptomic data

Initial within-platform batch correction was performed separately for the 20 microarray and seven RNA-seq datasets, successfully reducing variability within each technology type (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). However, when the pre-processed microarray data were merged with the variance-stabilized RNA-seq data, substantial platform-specific batch effects persisted, presenting a major obstacle for integrated downstream analysis.

As shown in the PCA of the merged but uncorrected dataset, the primary axis of variation was dominated by technological differences rather than biological conditions. Samples clustered into two well-separated groups corresponding to the microarray and RNA-seq platforms, fully masking any potential separation between control and stress samples (Fig. 1a). This prominent platform-driven structure underscored the necessity for a rigorous harmonization step.

Following the application of ComBat for batch effect correction, the PCA plot illustrates a clear segregation of the Normal and Stress samples along the principal components, with Normal samples clustering towards the upper region of the plot (depicted as circles) and Stress samples forming a distinct cluster in the lower region (represented by triangles) (Fig. 1b). This separation is a direct indication of the successful removal of platform-specific batch effects. After harmonization, the biological signal becomes more prominent, and the impact of the batch effect is minimized, which allows for more reliable differential analysis between the stress and normal conditions. The corrected data now reflects the true biological variation rather than the technological variation, enabling more meaningful and biologically relevant insights. Overall, the successful removal of platform-driven artifacts confirms that our integration strategy effectively harmonized the datasets while preserving the biologically meaningful variation essential for robust identification of differentially expressed genes.

The At1g53540 gene, also known as HSP17.6 C, encodes a 17.6 kDa class I heat shock protein that is present in the cytoplasm. While its role in heat stress response is recognized, the specific biological processes and molecular functions associated with HSP17.6 C remain uncharacterized. The At2g40610 gene, known as EXPA8, encodes Expansin-A8, a protein localized in the plasma membrane. This protein is implicated in the biological process of plant-type cell wall loosening, facilitating cell expansion and growth. However, its specific molecular functions remain poorly characterized.

The heatmap in supplementary Fig. 3 illustrates the Z-score standardized expression landscape of the 102 identified DEGs across 155 samples. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering effectively partitioned the gene set into two primary clades: a dominant cluster of 84 upregulated genes exhibiting elevated expression under stress, and a distinct cluster of 18 downregulated genes primarily active under normal conditions. This clear bifurcated pattern demonstrates a highly coordinated transcriptional response to environmental challenges.

Gene ontology enrichment analysis

The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs offers a comprehensive perspective on the biological processes involved in stress responses, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. The bar chart depicts the statistical significance of each enriched GO term, measured by the negative logarithm of the false discovery rate (log10(FDR)), where longer bars indicate higher significance. Notably, the most significantly enriched process is “Response to abiotic stimulus,” underscoring the pivotal role of these DEGs in environmental stress adaptation. This is closely followed by “Response to oxygen-containing compound” and “Response to chemical,” emphasizing the broad involvement of these genes in stress-related pathways. Further enriched processes, including “Response to stress,” “Response to osmotic stress,” “Response to inorganic substance,” and specific responses to heat, cold, hypoxia, salt stress, and water deprivation, highlight the extensive range of abiotic challenges these DEGs may help mitigate. A color gradient from blue to red visually indicates increasing significance, with red bars representing the most robust enrichments. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the DEGs are tightly associated with mechanisms crucial for physiological adaptation to diverse abiotic stressors.

In the molecular function category (Fig. 3b), the GO enrichment analysis uncovers key functional roles of the DEGs, again quantified by log10(FDR). The most enriched function, “Unfolded protein binding,” underscores the importance of maintaining protein homeostasis under stress conditions, suggesting active involvement in protein folding and quality control. This is followed by “Protein self-association” and “Inositol 3-alpha-galactosyltransferase activity,” indicating diverse contributions to molecular and regulatory processes that underpin stress tolerance. Collectively, these results reveal that the DEGs are primarily linked to protein stabilization and regulatory molecular functions, shedding light on the fundamental mechanisms through which gene expression changes confer resilience under stress. Such insights are valuable for guiding future research aimed at enhancing stress adaptation across various biological systems.

In addition to the GO enrichment analysis, a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to further explore the regulatory pathways associated with the identified DEGs in response to stress. The only significant KEGG pathway enriched was “Protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum” (ath04141), with a highly significant FDR of 0.00023. This suggests that the DEGs are particularly involved in maintaining protein homeostasis and quality control within the endoplasmic reticulum under stress conditions, which is essential for stress adaptation. Given the highly specific nature of this enrichment, it was presented in the main results section rather than as a graphical representation, as only one pathway was identified as statistically significant.

Identification of key transcription factors in the gene regulatory network

To elucidate the regulatory architecture underlying the stress response in Arabidopsis thaliana, a gene regulatory network (GRN) was constructed from the differentially expressed genes using protein-protein interaction data. Analysis of the network topology revealed At5g05410 (DREB2A) and At2g26150 (HSFA2) as the top hub transcription factors (TFs), with degrees of 12 and 9, respectively, indicating their extensive regulatory influence on downstream genes (Fig. 4). These degrees reflect the number of genes directly interacting with each TF, underscoring their central roles in coordinating the transcriptional response.

Figure 4 illustrates the interaction subnetwork centered on DREB2A and HSFA2, showcasing their connections to target genes. DREB2A exhibits interactions with genes involved in dehydration response and stress tolerance, including ERD10, GOLS1, GOLS2, HSFA2, HSP17.7, HSP70-4, NAC002, NAC013, NAC019, NAC032, and ZAT6. Notably, DREB2A directly interacts with HSFA2, suggesting potential crosstalk between dehydration-responsive and heat shock pathways. HSFA2, in turn, regulates a cluster of heat shock protein genes, such as HSP17.4 A, HSP17.6, HSP17.6 A, HSP17.6B, HSP17.6 C, HSP17.7, HSP23.6, HSP70-4, and T13C7.15, highlighting its role in chaperone-mediated protein folding under stress. Shared targets between DREB2A and HSFA2, such as HSP17.7 and HSP70-4, further indicate synergistic regulatory mechanisms. This network analysis identifies DREB2A and HSFA2 as pivotal regulators, potentially driving the integrated response to abiotic stress observed in the CPITA.

Feature selection algorithms

In this study, three ML algorithms—XGBoost, RF, and SVM-RFE—were employed to determine the most effective model for distinguishing between stressed and normal conditions in Arabidopsis thaliana. The primary objective was to identify the model that achieves optimal classification performance while leveraging the feature selection capabilities of these algorithms. This approach enabled the identification of key genes critical to the plant’s stress response.

As illustrated in Fig. 5a, the three classifiers—XGBoost, RF, and SVM-RFE—showed distinct performance profiles when distinguishing between stressed and normal Arabidopsis thaliana samples. Among the evaluated models, XGBoost demonstrated the highest performance with an AUC of 0.98 as determined by the area under the ROC curve. These curves highlight the superior discriminative power of XGBoost in separating the two conditions across varying decision thresholds.

As shown in Fig. 5b, the bar charts illustrate five key performance metrics—mean accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1 score—for each of the three classifiers. These metrics collectively enable a thorough assessment of each model’s performance, capturing both its effectiveness and limitations. Accuracy indicates the overall percentage of correctly identified samples in both stressed and normal conditions. Precision measures the proportion of predicted stressed samples that are indeed stressed, thus indicating how effectively a model avoids false positives. Recall (or sensitivity) captures how many of the truly stressed samples are correctly identified, highlighting the model’s capacity to minimize false negatives. Specificity reveals how well the model correctly classifies normal samples, thereby reducing false positives in that category. The F1 score, calculated as the harmonic means of precision and recall, provides a comprehensive assessment of a model’s performance, particularly when maintaining a balance between these two metrics is crucial. In this study, SVM-RFE and RF demonstrated the highest mean accuracy (approximately 91%), indicating strong overall performance in distinguishing between stressed and normal conditions. XGBoost followed closely with an accuracy of around 92.84%, underscoring its robust classification ability. When focusing on precision, RF outperformed the other models (91.94%), suggesting its strength in minimizing false positives and accurately identifying normal samples. By contrast, XGBoost achieved the highest recall (95.50%), making it especially adept at correctly identifying stressed samples (reducing false negatives). XGBoost and RF exhibited the best specificity (approximately 90%), reflecting its capability to reliably recognize normal samples. Notably, XGBoost also obtained the highest F1 score (93.25%), underscoring its balanced performance between precision and recall. Taken together, these results highlight that each model offers unique advantages, with RF excelling in precision, XGBoost in specificity, recall, and F1 score.

As shown in Fig. 5c, the confusion matrices for the three models—SVM-RFE, Random Forest, and XGBoost—highlight their performance in classifying Normal and Stress samples. The matrices illustrate the counts of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives for each model.

RT-qPCR analysis

The RT-qPCR results for the six selected candidate genes (At1g53540, At4g18280, At5g12030, At1g09750, At2g40610, and At2g03750) under seven different stress conditions (ABA, heat, cold, salt, drought, phosphate deficiency, and nitrogen deficiency) revealed distinct expression patterns (Fig. 9). At1g53540 was significantly upregulated under heat, salt, and drought stress but strongly downregulated under nitrogen stress. At5g12030 showed substantial induction in response to heat, salt, and drought stress, whereas nitrogen stress led to its suppression. At2g03750 was markedly downregulated across all stress conditions, particularly under phosphate and cold stress. At4g18280 exhibited significant upregulation under ABA, salt, and drought stress, while cold, nitrogen, and phosphate stress caused its repression. At1g09750 displayed moderate upregulation under ABA, cold, and salt stress but was strongly downregulated under nitrogen stress. At2g40610 was consistently downregulated across all stress conditions, with the most pronounced suppression observed under phosphate and drought stress. These findings suggest that these genes play potential roles in stress adaptation mechanisms.

Promoter sequence analysis

In this study, real-time PCR analysis revealed distinct expression profiles for six selected genes in Arabidopsis thaliana under various abiotic stresses, with promoter cis-element analyses providing strong corroboration (Fig. 10). The small heat shock protein genes At1g53540 and At5g12030 exhibited strong induction under heat stress (log2 fold changes of + 5 and + 6.5, respectively), with At1g53540 showing ABA-dependent upregulation (+ 5.5) linked to multiple ABRE elements, while At5g12030 displayed ABA-independent induction in drought and salt (+ 4–5) via MBS and MeJA motifs. Conversely, At2g03750 (P-loop NTPase) and At2g40610 were markedly repressed across most stresses (-3.5 to -7), despite abundant activator elements like ABRE, G-box, and MBS, indicating dominant negative regulation mechanisms. Genes At4g18280 and At1g09750 showed moderate induction in salt and drought (+ 1.7 to + 5.2) through TC-rich and MeJA elements but strong repression in phosphate and nitrate starvation (-3 to -4.5), directly attributable to multiple ARE motifs (2–6) associated with root hypoxia. Overall, these patterns demonstrated high concordance between transcriptional responses and promoter architectures, highlighting diverse regulatory strategies in stress adaptation.

Discussion

Plants face various environmental stresses, which can hinder growth and survival. Arabidopsis thaliana, a widely studied model plant, adjusts its gene expression to cope with these conditions, activating specific genes to endure unfavorable environments. While each type of stress triggers a distinct molecular response, a CPITA of RNA-seq and microarray datasets was performed to develop comprehensive strategies for understanding plant responses under various stress conditions. Seven different environmental stresses, including heat, cold, salt, drought, nitrogen, and phosphate stresses, along with the role of abscisic acid (ABA) in mediating responses, were investigated in our CPITA to identify the central network of response in Arabidopsis. In this research, we discovered 102 DEGs, with 84 genes showing upregulation and 18 genes showing downregulation. CPITA revealed that At1g53540 exhibited the highest expression value, encoding the heat shock protein HSP17.6 C, while At2g40610 showed the lowest expression value, encoding Expansin-A8.

The identification of enriched biological pathways and molecular activities associated with DEGs provides valuable understanding of the organism’s adaptive mechanisms under environmental stress conditions. The significant enrichment of processes such as “Response to abiotic stimulus,” “Response to oxygen-containing compound”, “Response to chemical”, “Response to stress”, “Response to osmotic stress” suggest that the DEGs are pivotal in mediating the organism’s resilience and survival strategies, facilitating physiological adjustments and enhancing tolerance to adverse environmental factors. These processes indicate an intricate network of stress-responsive pathways, particularly those related to water availability, and osmotic regulation, all of which are vital for maintaining cellular homeostasis under challenging environmental conditions.

On a molecular level, the enrichment of “unfolded protein binding” and “protein self-association” suggests that these processes play critical roles in cellular stress responses and protein quality control mechanisms. Specifically, unfolded proteins can activate the unfolded protein response (UPR), which enhances the cell’s ability to manage misfolded proteins and restore homeostasis [22]. Additionally, protein self-association may facilitate the formation of functional oligomers or complexes, which are essential for maintaining cellular functions and preventing aggregation under stress conditions [23]. This interplay between unfolded protein binding and self-association underscores the importance of molecular chaperones in assisting proper protein folding and assembly, thereby ensuring cellular integrity and function.

The gene regulatory network analysis revealed DREB2A and HSFA2 as the two dominant transcriptional hubs, indicating that these factors collectively govern a substantial portion of the stress-responsive transcriptome. Their centrality is highly consistent with their established roles as master regulators in Arabidopsis abiotic stress signaling and provides mechanistic context for the coordinated transcriptional reprogramming captured by our CPITA. DREB2A—an AP2/ERF family regulator known for its pivotal role in drought and osmotic stress tolerance—displayed extensive connectivity to key downstream effectors, including ERD10, galactinol synthase genes (GOLS1/GOLS2), and multiple NAC-domain transcription factors [24, 25]. This pattern underscores its involvement in activating the raffinose family oligosaccharide pathway and broader secondary regulatory circuits required for cellular stabilization under dehydration. Notably, the direct network-level association between DREB2A and HSFA2 supports prior hypotheses regarding functional crosstalk between dehydration-responsive pathways and the heat shock response.

HSFA2 emerged as the second major hub, with strong associations to numerous small heat shock proteins (sHSPs) and HSP70-4, consistent with its well-documented function in acquired thermotolerance and maintenance of prolonged heat- and oxidative-stress responses [26]. The pronounced overlap in target genes regulated by both DREB2A and HSFA2—such as HSP17.7 and HSP70-4—further suggests a synergistic, co-regulatory mechanism that enhances plant resilience under combined or sequential drought and heat stresses [27]. Collectively, the central positioning of these transcription factors, together with their shared downstream regulatory circuitry, highlights an integrated stress-response module that likely enables Arabidopsis to coordinate defense across multiple abiotic challenges.

To identify the most robust genetic markers of environmental resilience, we employed a binary classification (Stress vs. Control) in our ML models. While this binary approach focuses on the commonalities across stressors and may bypass highly specific individual stress responses, the high AUC (0.98) achieved by XGBoost confirms that a strong, conserved transcriptional signature exists across heterogeneous abiotic challenges (Fig. 5a). This analysis revealed ten common features of relevance, specifically At2g03750, At4g18280, At1g01720, At5g12030, At3g46230, At1g60190, At5g05410, At1g77450, At1g09750, and At1g32870. Furthermore, two additional genes (At1g53540 and At2g40610) identified through meta-analysis were subjected to systematic review to ascertain the final candidate hub genes, which comprised At1g53540, At4g18280, At5g12030, At1g09750, At2g40610, and At2g03750. This comprehensive approach underscores the potential of ML techniques in elucidating key genetic factors involved in abiotic stress responses.

By applying CPITA, At1g53540 exhibits the highest expression value. Given the absence of scientific reports regarding the function of this gene under abiotic stresses, evaluating its expression patterns under such conditions is valuable for researchers. The differential expression patterns of the small heat shock protein HSP17.6 C (At1g53540) in Arabidopsis thaliana under various abiotic stress conditions provide significant insights into its functional roles in plant stress adaptation. Under heat stress, At1g53540 shows pronounced upregulation (log₂FC ≈ + 4.6), reinforcing its established role as a molecular chaperone in thermotolerance. This transcriptional activation is consistent with our promoter analysis, which revealed an enrichment of stress-responsive cis-elements. While the presence of these motifs does not constitute direct functional evidence, it suggests potential regulatory inputs by heat-responsive transcription factors. These motifs are known to recruit Heat Shock Factors (HSFs) to drive rapid expression during thermal stress [28]. In terms of osmotic stress, drought and salt stress induced moderate upregulation (log₂FC ≈ + 2.5 and + 1.9, respectively), suggesting a potential role in dehydration tolerance. This observation aligns with the presence of a MYB binding site (MBS) in the promoter, a key cis-element governing drought inducibility [29].

Conversely, cold stress resulted in downregulation (log₂FC ≈ -2.8). Interestingly, this suppression occurred despite the presence of two Low-Temperature Responsiveness (LTR) motifs, suggesting that negative regulatory mechanisms or chromatin remodeling might override these cis-acting elements under freezing conditions [30]. A similar discrepancy was observed under ABA treatment, which caused severe downregulation (log₂FC ≈ − 6) despite the presence of three ABREs in the promoter. This pattern suggests the possibility that ABA-related regulatory mechanisms may contribute to At1g53540 repression; however, motif presence alone cannot distinguish between activating and repressing transcriptional inputs, and direct regulatory interactions remain to be experimentally validated [31] in the absence of additional stress, or that its activation requires combinatorial signaling beyond ABA alone. Finally, phosphate and nitrogen deprivation significantly repressed expression, indicating that under nutrient-limiting conditions, cellular resources are redirected away from sHSP synthesis towards metabolic adjustments [32].

The gene At2g03750, which encodes cytosolic sulfotransferase 11 (SOT11), appears to exhibit notable downregulation under various abiotic stress conditions in A. thaliana. The most pronounced repression seems to occur under heat and abscisic acid (ABA) stress, with a log₂FC estimated to be around − 5 to -4, indicating a potential involvement of SOT11 in stress-induced sulfur metabolism adjustments. Its downregulation of temperatures, salinity, and water scarcity may indicate a broader involvement in modulating stress-responsive pathways, potentially reallocating sulfur-containing compounds to enhance antioxidant defense and stress tolerance [33]. However, the precise extent and functional significance of this response remain to be fully clarified. Under phosphate stress, SOT11 is downregulated (log₂FC ≈ -2.5), further supporting the notion of metabolic reallocation. Interestingly, nitrogen stress induced only negligible repression (log₂FC ≈ -0.2), indicating that this transcriptional dampening is specific to osmotic and thermal stresses rather than a universal response to nutrient limitation. The silencing of At2g03750 despite the presence of these inductive elements suggests a dominant negative regulatory mechanism. It is plausible that, under severe stress, specific transcriptional repressors or epigenetic modifications override the activation potential of these cis-elements to conserve energy (e.g., via chromatin compaction or histone deacetylation [34]. Furthermore, the enrichment of light-responsive motifs in its promoter (such as MRE, Box 4, GT1-motif), which typically drive expression during active photosynthesis and growth, supports a model of a growth–defense trade-off, where downregulation of metabolism-related genes under stress helps redirect resources to survival pathways [35].

The gene At5g12030, which encodes the small heat shock protein HSP17.6 A, is identified as a critical feature selected by all three ML algorithms. RT-qPCR results indicate a significant upregulation of HSP17.6 A under heat stress, with a log₂ fold change of approximately + 5.6, highlighting its essential role in heat tolerance. Additionally, a significant induction under salt stress further supports the involvement of HSP17.6 A in safeguarding cellular structures during osmotic stress. This expression pattern aligns with prior research demonstrating that small heat shock proteins (sHSPs) enhance osmotic stress resistance by stabilizing proteins and membranes under water-deficit conditions [36]. Promoter motif analysis strongly supports this transcriptional behavior. The At5g12030 promoter contains multiple light- and heat-responsive cis-elements—including the G-box, I-box, AE-box, Box 4, and TCT-motif—forming a regulatory architecture characteristic of genes activated by thermal and photoperiod cues [37]. These elements are primary targets of HSFA1 and bZIP transcription factors, which initiate early heat-shock responses [38]. In addition, the presence of three MBS motifs (MYB-binding drought-responsive sites) and six CGTCA/TGACG elements associated with jasmonate signaling suggests that MYB proteins and JA-responsive transcription factors contribute to the activation of HSP17.6 A during osmotic stress [39].

In stark contrast, HSP17.6 A was downregulated under ABA treatment (log₂FC ≈ -2) and showed no induction under cold stress. This physiological data aligns precisely with the conspicuous absence of ABREs and LTR motifs in its promoter. The absence of ABRE and LTR motifs in the promoter region is consistent with the observed lack of induction under ABA and cold stress, suggesting that HSP17.6 A may be preferentially regulated through ABA-independent pathways. Nevertheless, definitive assignment of signaling dependency requires functional validation beyond motif analysis. The presence of three ARE motifs further rationalizes the moderate downregulation during phosphate/nitrate starvation, considering evidence that phosphate deprivation alters chromatin accessibility and that root hypoxia [40].

The glycine-rich cell wall structural protein gene At4g18280 (GRPL1) displayed significant upregulation under drought (log₂FC ≈ + 4.4), salt (log₂FC ≈ + 2.5), and ABA treatment (+ 1.8), moderate repression under heat (–2.5) and nutrient starvation (phosphate − 2.5, nitrate − 2), with no significant change in cold stress (–0.6, ns).

Promoter analysis corroborated this profile through multiple cis-regulatory elements: three TGA-elements and one AuxRR-core drive auxin/ABA-responsive induction, aligning with GRPL1’s role in stress adaptation via hormone signaling [41].; TC-rich repeats facilitate defense/stress responses under salt and drought [42]; two LTR elements explain the limited cold response, as LTR motifs mediate low-temperature repression in non-acclimation genes [43]; and two ARE motifs account for strong downregulation during phosphate/nitrate starvation, when root hypoxia represses cell wall remodeling. Abundant light-responsive elements (GT1-motif ×3, MRE ×3, AE-box ×2, Box 4 × 2, I-box, chs-CMA1a, LAMP-element) further suggest circadian and meristem-specific regulation via CAT-box and circadian motifs, consistent with GRPL1’s structural function in growth under optimal conditions.

The aspartyl protease gene At1g09750 (AED3), identified as a critical feature by three ML algorithms, plays essential roles in plant stress responses through protein turnover and signaling under environmental stress [44]. RT-qPCR analysis showed mild upregulation under ABA, cold, and salt stress, suggesting involvement in stress adaptation via proteolytic activation of responsive proteins [45], while it exhibited downregulation under drought, heat (log2FC=–0.7, ns), and strongly under nitrogen and phosphate stress, indicating potential functions in nutrient sensing or homeostasis. Promoter analysis revealed a regulatory framework aligning with this profile: two MeJA-responsive motifs (CGTCA/TGACG) and TC-rich repeats drive induction under ABA, cold, and salt via jasmonate and defense pathways [46] ; one MBS motif is consistent with the mild drought response through MYB-mediated drought signaling [47]; and six ARE motifs account for repression during nutrient starvation-induced root hypoxia. Previous studies implicate aspartyl proteases in abiotic resilience and programmed cell death [48], with AED3’s apoplastic localization supporting extracellular proteolysis in defense and adaptation [49]. Collectively, the differential regulation of AED3 across stress conditions underscores its multifaceted role in plant adaptive responses.

The Arabidopsis thaliana gene At2g40610 (EXPA8) exhibits the lowest log2FC in the CPITA (Fig. 2a) and encodes expansin-A8, a membrane-localized protein involved in plant cell wall loosening, facilitating cell expansion and growth. Gene expression analysis reveals that At2g40610 is significantly downregulated under multiple environmental stresses, including ABA, heat, cold, salt, drought, phosphate, and nitrogen deprivation, with the most pronounced downregulation observed under phosphate stress (log₂FC ~ -7, **p < 0.0001). This suggests a role for At2g40610 in modulating cell wall plasticity in response to stress, potentially as a mechanism to restrict growth and conserve resources under adverse conditions. The reduction in cell size under abiotic stresses may serve as an adaptive mechanism to enable a more rapid response to environmental challenges, potentially by conserving energy resources to enhance survival [3]. Expansins are essential for cell wall remodeling, and their reduced expression under stress is consistent with the suppression of growth-related processes to enhance survival [50]. Moreover, the consistent downregulation of At2g40610 across diverse stresses suggests a broad regulatory role in stress adaptation, likely as a target of ABA-mediated signaling pathways that suppress its expression to prioritize survival [51].

Despite the comprehensive insights gained through CPITA and machine-learning analyses, several methodological limitations should be explicitly acknowledged. First, although this study integrates multiple transcriptomic datasets, it does not represent a classical effect-size–based meta-analysis. Instead, a pooled integration strategy was employed after expression-level harmonization, which may partially obscure study-specific effects and introduce sensitivity to residual batch structure. While ComBat correction effectively reduced platform-driven variance, overcorrection remains a potential risk, particularly when biological conditions are unevenly distributed across datasets. Second, heterogeneity among source studies—including differences in stress intensity, duration, tissue sampling, and experimental design—cannot be fully eliminated and may influence DEG detection. Moreover, the binary classification framework (Stress vs. Control), while effective for identifying conserved transcriptional signatures, inherently mixes distinct stress modalities and may mask stress-specific regulatory programs. Finally, although machine-learning models were carefully cross-validated, the use of a single integrated dataset raises the possibility of subtle information leakage between feature selection and model evaluation. Future studies incorporating study-aware cross-validation, stress-stratified modeling, and independent experimental cohorts will be essential to further strengthen causal inference and generalizability.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the utility of an integrative bioinformatic framework to harmonize microarray and RNA-seq data and identify a high-confidence set of stress-responsive genes. Through cross-platform integration, machine-learning prioritization, and targeted RT-qPCR validation, we identified six key hub genes—At1g53540, At4g18280, At5g12030, At1g09750, At2g40610, and At2g03750—associated with conserved abiotic stress responses. These genes appear to participate in processes such as protein stabilization, transcriptional regulation, and growth–stress balance. While the present findings highlight their potential as candidate stress-responsive regulators, further functional characterization, genetic perturbation studies, and field-level validation are required before translational or crop-engineering applications can be proposed. Importantly, this work also provides a reproducible computational framework for leveraging heterogeneous public transcriptomic datasets to uncover conserved stress-response modules.

Materials and methods

Dataset collection of abiotic stresses

We conducted a cross-platform integrative transcriptomic analysis as outlined in Fig. 11. Raw transcriptomic datasets, including RNA-seq and microarray samples, were retrieved from the GEO repository of the NCBI database. To maintain a uniform genetic background, only datasets derived from wild-type Arabidopsis thaliana ecotypes at the seedling stage were included, while mutant lines were excluded to minimize genetic variability in the experimental outcomes. A total of 27 microarray and RNA-seq datasets, comprising 73 control samples and 82 environmental stress-treated samples, were selected for the integrative meta-analysis. These datasets collectively represented ten abiotic stress conditions (hydrogen peroxide, drought, high temperature, low temperature, salicylic acid, salinity, phosphate deficiency, nitrogen deficiency, methyl viologen, and abscisic acid), which were included exclusively in the transcriptomic meta-analysis phase (Supplementary File 1).

Pre-processing of RNA-seq and microarray profiles

Given the inherent differences between microarray (intensity-based) and RNA-seq (count-based) platforms, we employed distinct pre-processing strategies for each dataset type prior to integration.

Microarray data

Raw microarray intensity files were retrieved from the GEO database for Agilent (GPL12621, GPL9020) and Affymetrix (GPL198) platforms. Background correction and normalization were performed independently using the normalizedBetweenArrays function from the limma package for Agilent arrays and the Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) algorithm via the affy package for Affymetrix data [52, 53]. Probes were mapped to official gene symbols; in cases where multiple probes corresponded to a single gene, the mean expression value was calculated to summarize gene-level expression. Subsequently, intra-platform datasets were merged, and non-biological experimental variations (batch effects) were corrected using the ComBat algorithm from the sva package [54]. To prevent the loss of genuine biological signals, a model matrix was constructed specifying the biological condition (Control vs. Stress) as a protected covariate, ensuring that phenotypic variation of interest was preserved during the batch adjustment process.

RNA-seq data

Raw sequencing reads (FASTQ) were retrieved using sratoolkit v2.10.7. Quality control was performed using Trimmomatic v0.36 to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases [55]. Cleaned reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis thaliana reference genome (TAIR10) using HISAT2 v2.1.0 with default settings for spliced alignment [56]. The corresponding gene annotation was obtained from the Araport11 release (GTF format). Gene-level read counts were generated using HTSeq-count v0.11.2 in union mode (-m union), with no strand specificity (-s no), exon features (-t exon), and gene_id as the attribute (-i gene_id) [57]. Only uniquely mapped reads were considered for quantification. Genes with low expression (total read counts < 10 across all samples) were excluded prior to downstream analyses. To enable statistical integration with normalized microarray data, RNA-seq count data were transformed using the Variance-Stabilizing Transformation (VST) implemented in DESeq2 v1.24.0 [58]. This approach reduces the mean–variance dependency inherent to count-based data and yields homoscedastic, approximately log2-scaled expression values. Following transformation, internal batch effects among RNA-seq datasets were corrected using the ComBat algorithm implemented in the sva package, treating each independent dataset as a technical batch. The biological treatment factor (Control vs. Stress) was incorporated into the ComBat model design to partition technical noise from the relevant stress-responsive signals.

Cross-platform integration and harmonization

Accordingly, our analytical strategy relies on a pooled, batch-corrected integrative framework in which expression matrices are harmonized prior to downstream analyses, rather than on a classical effect-size–based meta-analysis. This approach is particularly suited for detecting shared transcriptional signatures across heterogeneous platforms when the primary interest lies in conserved biological responses rather than platform-specific effect estimates. The platform-specific processed matrices were merged based on 13,440 common gene symbols. Despite individual normalization, systemic platform-specific bias (Microarray vs. RNA-seq) remained a significant source of variation. To homogenize the datasets, a final harmonization step was performed using ComBat, treating the “platform” as a batch covariate. In this final harmonization, the model design included the biological condition as a fixed effect to ensure that platform-specific biases (Microarray vs. RNA-seq) were removed without compromising the differential expression signatures across the integrated groups. This approach effectively removed technical artifacts while retaining biological variation. The integrity of the integrated dataset was rigorously validated. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) demonstrated that post-correction clustering was driven by biological conditions (Control vs. Stress) rather than the platform of origin (Fig. 3d). Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis of the resultant differentially expressed genes confirmed the recovery of biologically relevant pathways, validating the robustness of this cross-platform integrative transcriptomic analysis strategy [59].

Differential expression gene analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed on the final, integrated expression matrix, which consolidated data from both microarray and RNA-seq platforms following comprehensive pre-processing and batch correction. Using the limma package, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal and stress conditions. Genes were considered significant if they met the criteria of an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute |log2fold change| ≥ 1. The resulting gene expression profile was visualized using a volcano plot.

Functional annotation and GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

Enrichment analyses for Gene Ontology (GO) were conducted on the identified DEGs with the ShinyGO web tool (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) [60]. Pathways and terms were classified as significant based on an FDR-adjusted p-value threshold of less than 0.05.

Gene regulatory network construction and analysis

To construct a gene regulatory network (GRN) for the identified DEGs, protein-protein interaction (PPI) data were retrieved using the STRING database (version 11.5; https://string-db.org/) [61]. The DEGs were queried against the Arabidopsis thaliana interactome with a medium confidence interaction score threshold of 0.4 to ensure reliable interactions while capturing biologically relevant connections. The resulting network, comprising nodes (genes/proteins) and edges (interactions), was exported in a compatible format and imported into Cytoscape (version 3.10.4; https://cytoscape.org/) for visualization and analysis [62]. Within Cytoscape, network topology was examined using the built-in NetworkAnalyzer tool to compute node degrees, representing the number of regulatory interactions per gene. Transcription factors (TFs) were prioritized based on their out-degree centrality, with the top-ranking TFs selected as potential key regulators. Interactions involving these TFs were visualized to highlight their regulatory hubs within the network.

Cis-acting regulatory element analysis

To investigate upstream regulatory signals, the promoter sequences extending 2 kb before the transcription start sites of the hub genes were retrieved and examined through the PlantCare resource [63]. The catalogued cis-regulatory motifs were then summarized and mapped, and Python scripts were employed to construct a heatmap and other visual representations.

Machine learning (ML) methods

Applying feature selection algorithms (FSAs) directly to high-dimensional transcriptomic datasets, which often contain tens of thousands of genes but a limited number of samples (a “large p, small n” problem), poses significant computational challenges and a high risk of model overfitting. To address this and strictly avoid potential data leakage, we implemented a nested feature selection framework. First, within each training split of the resampling process, differential expression analysis (using limma) was performed independently to identify a biologically enriched pool of stress-responsive DEGs. This ensures that the test set remains completely unseen during the dimensionality reduction stage and does not inform the feature selection process. Across the iterations, this step identified a consistent subset of approximately 102 DEGs, which served as the input features for the subsequent multivariate machine learning algorithms.

Subsequently, ML techniques were employed to achieve two main objectives: (1) binary classification of samples into normal (control) and stress conditions, and (2) identification of the most predictive subset of key genes (hub genes) from within the DEG pool. While traditional statistical methods can identify individually significant genes, ML models excel at discovering complex, multivariate patterns and non-linear interactions among genes. This allows for the identification of a minimal, highly predictive gene signature that would be missed by univariate ranking methods (e.g., sorting by p-value or fold change). This hybrid approach, combining statistical pre-filtering with ML-based feature selection, is an established strategy for enhancing model robustness and biological interpretability in genomic studies [64, 65].

The dataset comprised gene expression profiles of 155 samples (73 control and 82 stress-treated samples), establishing a nearly balanced class distribution. For model training and evaluation, we employed a rigorous systematic strategy. The dataset was subjected to 100 independent iterations of a stratified 80/20 train/test split, ensuring consistent class proportions in both sets. To guarantee reproducibility, a global random seed of 42 was used, while the seed for each split was tied to the iteration index (0–99). Within each iteration, feature prioritization and hyperparameter optimization were conducted exclusively on the training partition using 5-fold cross-validation, keeping the test set entirely independent. Crucially, the initial DEG filtering was conducted separately for each of the 100 training sets, ensuring that no information from the held-out test data was utilized during the gene pre-selection phase.

The specific configurations for the three ML approaches were as follows:

SVM-RFE: Feature selection was performed using a Support Vector Machine (SVM) with a linear kernel and a balanced class-weight. The RFE algorithm recursively eliminated the least significant features with a step size of 1 to prioritize a robust gene signature.

Random Forest (RF): The model was configured with 100 trees using the Gini impurity criterion. To prevent overfitting, the max_depth was set to 5, and the min_samples_leaf was optimized via cross-validation. Feature importance was quantified by the mean decrease in Gini impurity [66, 67].

XGBoost: This approach utilized 100 weak tree learners with a learning rate of 0.1. To enhance generalization, L1 (alpha) and L2 (lambda) regularization penalties were incorporated. Feature importance was determined using the ‘Gain’ metric, and the ‘logloss’ objective function was used for binary classification [68–70].

Final performance metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Specificity, F1-score, and AUC-ROC) were summarized by averaging the results across the 100 independent runs. All machine learning analyses were performed exclusively on the integrated meta-analysis dataset encompassing the ten abiotic stress conditions and were not restricted to the seven stresses selected for experimental validation.

Plant material and growth conditions and stress treatment

This study employed the Columbia ecotype of Arabidopsis thaliana. The experiment was conducted in the Biotechnology Laboratory of the Faculty of Life Sciences, Shahid Beheshti University, Tehran, Iran. To sterilize the seeds, they were first immersed in 95% ethanol for 1 min, followed by a 5-minute treatment with 3% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO). Afterward, the seeds were rinsed five times with sterile distilled water, each rinse lasting 5 min, and then placed on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (½ MS) medium containing 1% sucrose and 0.8% agar, with the pH adjusted to 5.7. The plants were cultivated under controlled environmental conditions with a 16-hour light/8-hour dark cycle, maintaining a temperature of 22 °C during the light phase and 20 °C during the dark phase. Unless stated otherwise, all control and experimental groups were kept under identical conditions.

After 10 days of growth, seedlings were subjected to seven types of abiotic stress: abscisic acid (ABA) treatment, drought, salinity, heat, cold, nitrogen deficiency, and phosphate depletion. It is important to explicitly distinguish between the discovery and validation phases of this study. Ten abiotic stresses were included in the cross-platform integrative meta-analysis to comprehensively identify conserved stress-responsive genes. However, experimental validation by RT-qPCR was intentionally limited to seven representative environmental stresses (abscisic acid, drought, salinity, heat, cold, nitrogen deficiency, and phosphate deficiency), selected based on physiological relevance and experimental feasibility.

Stressors such as hydrogen peroxide and methyl viologen were not included in the RT-qPCR experiments because oxidative stress is a well-established secondary consequence of several of the selected primary stresses, including drought, salinity, temperature extremes, and nutrient deficiencies, all of which induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and redox imbalance [3, 72]. Thus, key oxidative stress–related transcriptional responses are inherently captured within the chosen experimental conditions. Salicylic acid treatment was excluded to prioritize direct environmental stressors over signaling molecules that plants are less frequently exposed to as standalone abiotic factors in natural growth environments.

The severity and time duration of stress conditions was selected based on previously published studies [73–76] and comprehensive review articles to ensure physiologically relevant responses in Arabidopsis thaliana.

To induce salinity stress, the seedlings were moved from ½ MS medium to a medium containing 200 mM NaCl, while control seedlings were maintained in a ½ MS medium. Samples were harvested 24 h after treatment initiation. Chilling tolerance was assessed by plating seeds on ½ MS agar plates, which were wrapped in aluminum foil and incubated in a growth chamber at 4 °C for 24 h. Heat stress was induced by exposing seedlings grown on ½ MS agar to 37 °C for 3 h in an incubator, a temperature selected to elicit mild heat stress responses as reported by. Drought stress was simulated by transferring seedlings onto ½ MS medium supplemented with 25% polyethylene glycol 8000 [77], while control seedlings were maintained on liquid ½ MS medium without PEG8000. All samples for these stresses were collected after 24 h.

To induce nitrogen and phosphate stress, seedlings were moved to modified ½ MS medium lacking nitrogen (without KNO₃ and NH₄NO₃) or phosphate (without KH₂PO₄), respectively, and subjected to these stress conditions for 48 h, with the pH and osmotic balance maintained by adjusting the medium composition accordingly. For ABA treatment, 10-day-old seedlings were transferred to ½ MS medium supplemented with 50 µM ABA and incubated for 3 h before sample collection for RNA extraction.

Validation gene responsive genes to environmental stresses by RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The measured RNA concentrations were as follows: 228.5 ng/µl for the control sample, 417.5 ng/µl for drought stress, 344.8 ng/µl for phosphate deficiency, 321.7 ng/µl for heat stress, 241.4 ng/µl for abscisic acid (ABA) treatment, 237.2 ng/µl for salt stress, 291.0 ng/µl for nitrogen deficiency, and 161.8 ng/µl for cold stress.

To evaluate RNA integrity, 1% agarose gel electrophoresis was performed. The presence of clear and distinct 28 S and 18 S ribosomal RNA bands in all samples confirmed to have high RNA integrity. To remove potential genomic DNA contamination, RNA samples were treated with DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific) prior to cDNA synthesis. Subsequently, first-strand cDNA was synthesized using the AddScript Reverse Transcriptase kit (Addgene, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Target genes, along with the reference gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, (At1g13440 (GAPC2)), were selected for primer design by using the Primer3Plus website (Table 1). The PCR amplicon lengths for targeted genes were optimized to range from 100 to 250 base pairs. RT-qPCR data normalization was conducted using the GAPC2 (Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase). The Livak method was employed for data analysis [78].

RT-qPCR was used to evaluate gene expression levels, utilizing the Azure Cielo real-time PCR system. The Maxima SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (2X) from Thermo Scientific™ was applied according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. For each stress condition and control, three independent biological replicates were collected, each derived from separately grown seedling batches. For each biological replicate, RT-qPCR reactions were performed in three technical replicates. Thus, all expression analyses are based on n = 3 biological replicates per condition, with technical triplicates used to ensure measurement precision. Data were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical differences between each stress treatment and its corresponding control were evaluated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test in GraphPad Prism 8. Multiple-testing correction was not applied because the RT-qPCR experiments were conducted as a confirmatory, hypothesis-driven validation of a limited number of pre-selected hub genes identified through prior integrative transcriptomic and machine-learning analyses, rather than as an exploratory high-throughput screening. Each gene–stress comparison represented a pre-specified and biologically independent hypothesis. In this targeted validation framework, applying global multiple-comparison adjustments would be unnecessarily conservative and could substantially increase the likelihood of Type II errors (false negatives), thereby masking genuine biologically meaningful effects.
