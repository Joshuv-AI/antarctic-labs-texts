An adaptive snake optimization algorithm incorporating Subtraction-Average-Based Optimizer for photovoltaic cell parameter identification
Authors: Chunliang Mai, Lixin Zhang, Xue Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Accurate parameter identification of photovoltaic (PV) models is essential for the optimal operation and control of PV systems. However, PV cell modeling exhibits nonlinearity and involves numerous challenging-to-solve unknown parameters, thereby reducing the utilization efficiency of solar energy in PV systems. Therefore, this paper proposes an enhanced Snake algorithm (ISASO) that integrates Subtraction Average-Based Optimization (SABO) to address the shortcomings of traditional PV model parameter identification methods, such as low accuracy, slow convergence, and susceptibility to local optima. The SABO algorithm, which updates the positions of search agents using a consistent arithmetic mean position throughout the optimization process, demonstrates high convergence. By integrating SABO's global search strategy into the exploration phase of SO, the global search capability of SO is further enhanced, mitigating the risk of early local optima in the original SO. Additionally, the Tent chaotic map initialization method is incorporated into standard SO to improve the quality of the initial population and enhance population diversity. A dynamic learning factor and adaptive inertia weight strategy are also employed to accelerate the convergence speed of the SO algorithm, balancing its exploration and exploitation capabilities. To validate the performance of ISASO, it is applied to the CEC2005 benchmark functions and employed to identify the optimal parameters of various PV models. Statistical and analytical results reveal that ISASO markedly outperforms existing methods in parameter identification accuracy and reliability, achieving the lowest Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) values between standard and simulated data. Additionally, the superior performance of ISASO is further verified by comparative analysis with existing meta-heuristic algorithms and the Friedman mean ranking statistical method. Therefore, ISASO can be considered as a reliable and effective method to accurately estimate solar PV model parameters.

Introduction

In recent years, solar photovoltaic (PV) technology, a representative of clean and renewable energy, has garnered widespread attention and intensive research in the energy sector. As the core component of PV systems, the precise identification of PV cell performance parameters is crucial for fully leveraging the efficiency of PV systems [1,2]. Precise parameter identification of PV models not only aids in optimizing system performance and improving energy conversion efficiency but also establishes a crucial foundation for system monitoring, fault diagnosis, and intelligent control [3,4]. With the rapid advancement of PV technology, research on PV model parameter identification has also become increasingly profound.

In previous research, scholars have endeavored to address numerous challenges in PV model parameter identification by introducing various mathematical models, data fitting algorithms, and machine learning techniques. However, the complexity of PV systems, the uncertainty of environmental changes, and the specificity of different cell types continue to present challenges for PV model parameter identification [5,6]. Existing research methods for extracting unknown PV model parameters primarily fall into two categories: key point-based methods and current-voltage (I–V) characteristic curve fitting methods. Key point-based methods [[7], [8], [9], [10]] are relatively simple and easy to implement but often fail to extract parameters with sufficient accuracy. These methods reduce model parameters by analyzing and simplifying model equations, then extract PV model parameters using several key points (such as short-circuit current, open-circuit voltage, and maximum power point). In contrast, I–V characteristic curve-based methods aim to minimize the overall error between simulated and measured current using numerical optimization techniques. These methods do not require complex analysis and derivation but may consume more computational resources. Commonly employed numerical optimization techniques include deterministic and metaheuristic methods. Deterministic methods [[11], [12], [13], [14]] can swiftly find optimal solutions, but their performance is highly sensitive to initial guesses. When using these methods, if the initial guess is suboptimal, they risk falling into local optima.

With the advent of intelligent computing, nature-inspired metaheuristic methods have garnered significant attention and have been extensively applied in engineering optimization, particularly in highly nonlinear and complex problems [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. For instance, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and its variants have been employed in the design of power electronic components for power systems [[20], [21], [22]]; Gravitational Search Algorithm (GSA) has been applied to the synchronous coordinated design of Power System Stabilizers (PSS) and Thyristor Controlled Series Capacitors (TCSC) as damping controllers in multi-machine power systems [23]. Chimp Optimization Algorithm (ChOA) and its improved variants have been utilized to address environmental economic dispatch and green building energy consumption optimization problems [24,25]. The notable advantages of metaheuristic methods lie in their simplicity, effectiveness, ease of implementation, and independence from the mathematical model of the system under study. Consequently, various metaheuristic algorithms have been employed for parameter identification of solar PV models in recent years. For example, Mokhtar et al. [26] introduced an enhanced Marine Predators Algorithm (MSSA) and effectively utilized it for the parameter identification of solar photovoltaic (PV) models. Behdad et al. [27] developed an improved Tunicate Swarm Algorithm (ITSA), and their research demonstrated that the enhanced ITSA significantly surpassed the original TSA in both accuracy and reliability. Wu et al. [28] introduced an enhanced Elephant Herding Optimization algorithm, integrating a fast-moving operator and elite strategy for the extraction of PV cell model parameters. Nunes et al. [29] developed a multi-population spiral-guiding Particle Swarm Optimization algorithm, which significantly enhanced the accuracy of PV parameter identification through a multi-population and exchange search mechanism. Wei et al. [30] employed a triangular search optimization algorithm for the identification of PV model parameters. Yu et al. [31] improved the local search capability of the Grey Wolf Optimization algorithm by incorporating Gaussian mutation and Levy flight strategy. Li et al. [32] introduced an adaptive teaching-learning-based algorithm hybridized with Differential Evolution, which accurately identified the unknown parameters of the PV model. Subudhi et al. [33] employed the Bacterial Foraging Optimization algorithm to extract PV module parameters under varying weather conditions. In addition to the aforementioned heuristic algorithms, various other intelligent optimization algorithms have also been employed for the extraction of solar cell model parameters. These algorithms include Shuffled Complex Evolution [[34], [35], [36], [37]], Backtracking Search Algorithm [[38], [39], [40], [41]], JAYA algorithm [[42], [43], [44]], Pattern Search [45], Simulated Annealing [46], Harmony Search [47], Artificial Bee Colony algorithm [48], Flower Pollination algorithm [49], Fireworks algorithm [50], Moth Search algorithm [51], Cuckoo Search Optimization [52], Harris Hawks Optimization [53], and Chimp Optimization [54]. However, these methods still encounter challenges such as low convergence accuracy and slow speed when identifying PV model parameters in complex environments. Consequently, researchers have introduced the concept of hybrid algorithms, which amalgamate different intelligent algorithms to enhance tracking speed and accuracy. For instance, Thira et al. [55] hybridized the Single Candidate Optimizer (SCO) and Chaotic Sand Cat Optimizer (CSCO), enhancing the algorithm's exploration capability while preventing premature convergence. Mahdiyeh et al. [56] effectively integrated Adaptive Rat Swarm Optimization (ARSO) and Pattern Search (PS), employing ARSO for global search and PS for local search, significantly enhancing the accuracy and convergence speed of PV cell model parameter identification. Amr et al. [57] introduced an enhanced Queuing Search Optimization algorithm based on Differential Evolution (DE) and boundary constraint modification (IQSODE) for PV model parameter identification. Yu et al. [58] introduced a Differential Evolution algorithm with adaptive parameters and mutation operators based on Reinforcement Learning (RLAPMDE) and successfully extracted the optimal parameters of PV models. Hybrid algorithms can fully exploit the strengths of different algorithms, thereby enhancing overall accuracy and convergence performance, making them a focal point of current research. Despite the good accuracy achieved by solar PV model parameter identification methods based on single metaheuristic algorithms or hybrid algorithms, they still encounter the following challenges.a)Insufficient optimization performance: Current PV model parameter identification methods exhibit poor performance in handling complex multimodal optimization problems, frequently falling into local optima, thereby affecting the accuracy and reliability of parameter identification.b)High computational complexity: Many current methods exhibit high computational complexity when processing large-scale data, resulting in long algorithm runtimes and making it challenging to meet real-time requirements in practical applications.c)Poor adaptability: Certain methods are sensitive to changes in initial parameters and problem scale, exhibiting limited applicability, thereby making it difficult to cope with diverse PV systems and complex environmental conditions.

The Snake Optimization (SO) algorithm [59] is an innovative bio-inspired metaheuristic technique that constructs a mathematical model by simulating the unique mating behavior of snake swarms to address various optimization problems. This method has been successfully applied across multiple fields. However, according to the “No Free Lunch Theorem,” no single algorithm can address all optimization problems. Therefore, developing a new PV model parameter identification technique based on the SO algorithm will enhance the long-term operational stability of PV systems.

This paper proposes a novel method for solar PV model parameter identification based on the Snake Optimization (SO) algorithm to tackle a series of challenges in contemporary research. Additionally, to further enhance the parameter identification accuracy of SO, we developed an improved version of the SO algorithm, termed ISASO. In ISASO, we first introduce the Tent chaotic map to initialize the population, ensuring a more uniform distribution of initial solutions. Secondly, the SABO algorithm is integrated into the exploration phase of the SO algorithm to enhance its early convergence speed, thereby preventing the snake swarm from falling into hibernation due to low temperatures, i.e., local optima. Then, the fixed learning factors in the exploration and exploitation phases of SO are adjusted to dynamic learning factors that vary with iterations, and an iteration-based adaptive inertia weight strategy is introduced to perturb the positions of the snake individuals. This adjustment provides enhanced global exploration capability in the early iterations, enabling the algorithm to search the space more broadly for potential global optima. In the later iterations, the dynamic learning factors and adaptive inertia weights switch to a local search mode to explore the space more finely and identify superior local optima. By flexibly adjusting the global exploration and local exploitation capabilities at different stages, the algorithm's convergence speed is enhanced, enabling it to identify the optimal unknown parameter values of the PV cell model more quickly. The robustness and reliability of the ISASO algorithm in PV model parameter identification are validated through the parameter identification of five different solar PV models and detailed comparative analyses with advanced algorithms reported in the literature.

The main contributions of this study are as follows.(1)Integrating the SABO algorithm into the SO algorithm to improve the early convergence speed of the SO algorithm and avoid the algorithm falling into local optima.(2)Modifying the fixed learning factors in the SO algorithm to dynamic learning factors that change with the number of iterations and introducing an adaptive inertia weight strategy to enhance the search capability at different stages of the algorithm, helping the algorithm find the optimal solution more quickly.(3)Comparing the performance of ISASO with existing advanced metaheuristic algorithms, introducing the indicators of absolute error value (IAE) and relative error value (RE) to analyze ISASO's performance more intuitively.(4)Through extensive experimental validation, the proposed method in this study demonstrated excellent performance in multiple benchmark test functions and actual PV parameter identification problems, proving its effectiveness and practicality, indicating that the proposed ISASO is an alternative method capable of accurately estimating PV model parameters.

Solar cell models

PV cells mainly convert absorbed light energy into storable electrical energy through photoelectric or photochemical effects. Electrical and optical losses are the main factors affecting the efficiency of PV cells. Accurately and effectively establishing an equivalent circuit model of PV cells can effectively ensure maximum power point tracking, performance optimization, and fault diagnosis, which is of great significance for the control of PV power generation systems. This section mainly introduces three different PV models, namely single diode model (SDM), double diode model (DDM), and PV module models [60].

Snake optimization algorithm

The snake optimization algorithm is inspired by the unique mating behavior of snakes. It achieves global optimization by simulating the fighting and mating patterns of snake groups.

Initialization

SO first generates a uniformly distributed random population, assuming an equal distribution of male and female snakes, with both genders constituting 50 % of the population. The population is then divided into male and female subpopulations.

Algorithm stage division

In the SO algorithm, when the amount of food possessed by the snake group is insufficient, the snake group will search for food, and this phase is called the exploration phase. When the snake group possesses enough food, the snake group will perform different behavioral models such as eating food, fighting, or mating depending on the temperature of the environment they are in, and this phase is called the exploitation phase. The behavior of the snake population is affected by two important parameters, the total physical quantity Q and the ambient temperature T. The formulae for both are given below:(8)Q=c1×exp(n−NN)(9)T=exp(−nN)where N is the maximum number of iterations, n is the current number of iterations, and c1 is a constant set to 0.5. It can be seen that the amount of food increases with the number of iterations and the temperature decreases with the number of iterations.

PV model parameter identification based on ISASO

The initial population of SO is generated by the random number function of the software, which may cause the initial individuals to be far from the optimal point, resulting in insufficient optimization capability of the algorithm. The tuning parameters c1, c2, and c3 in SO are set to fixed values, making it impossible to balance the exploration and exploitation phases, leading to insufficient optimization capability in the later stages of the algorithm. At the same time, the diversity of the population decreases in the later stages of SO, making it difficult to escape from local optimal points. To address the above issues, the following algorithm improvement strategies are proposed.

Tent chaotic mapping

To address the issue of reduced optimization capability due to low diversity in the initial population, Tent chaotic mapping is used to initialize the population. Tent chaotic mapping initialization has better ergodicity compared to random initialization, allowing for coverage of the entire search space. The high randomness of Tent chaotic mapping results in an initial population with a complex and irregular distribution. This randomness helps break patterns within the population, resulting in greater diversity among individuals in the initial population. This increases the diversity of the population. In the initialization phase of the ISASO algorithm, the initial population generated by Tent chaotic mapping can better cover the search space, providing more potential solutions. This lays a solid foundation for the subsequent iterative optimization of the algorithm. During the iterative optimization phase, an initial population with high diversity can explore and exploit the search space more effectively, avoiding early entrapment in local optima and thus improving overall optimization performance. The population size is set to N = 50, with a maximum iteration count of Tmax = 2000. Equations (5), (6), (7) are employed as the fitness functions during the optimization process of ISASO. Five sets of initial values are randomly generated in the optimization space, and Tent chaotic mapping is applied N times to produce an initial chaotic photovoltaic parameter matrix of size N×5. Each row in the matrix represents a feasible solution for the parameters of the photovoltaic cell model. The mathematical expression for the Tent chaotic mapping is given as follows:(19)xi(t+1)={xi(t)u,xi(t)∈[0,u)1−xi(t)1−u,xi(t)∈[u,1)

Where u is a random number in the range (0, 1), and as its value approaches 0.5, the initial solution distribution becomes more uniform. u is set to 0.499. This choice is made because when α equals 0.5, after approximately 50 iterations, the population converges to 0, losing its exploratory nature.

Dynamic learning factor strategy

To address the optimization efficiency and accuracy issues caused by fixed learning factors in the standard SO algorithm, this paper proposes a dynamic learning factor strategy. Specifically, the SO algorithm is characterized by dual populations and multi-stage optimization, using different comparison mechanisms to update the population positions at different optimization stages. Therefore, we combine these different comparison mechanisms and modify the learning factors c1, c2, and c3 in the standard SO algorithm from fixed values to variables that change with iterations, referred to as dynamic learning factors. The mathematical equation for the dynamic learning factor ci is as follows:(20)ci=1−tT

In the initial stage of the algorithm, the learning factors c1, c2, and c3 are set to larger initial values, which can enhance the exploration ability of the population and avoid falling into the local optimal solution too early. As the number of iterations increases, these learning factors gradually decrease, so that the algorithm can perform more refined local searches in the later stage and improve the accuracy of parameter identification. The dynamic learning factor strategy can automatically adjust the search strategy according to different optimization stages, so that the algorithm can achieve a balance between global search and local search, thereby improving the overall optimization efficiency. In addition, this strategy also enhances the adaptability of the algorithm in different optimization stages, thereby achieving faster and more accurate solar photovoltaic model parameter identification.

Combining subtraction-average-based optimizer

The principle of the Subtraction-Average-Based Optimization (SABO) algorithm is simple, and the optimization speed is fast [62]. The convergence curve of the subtraction optimizer is a straight line in both the early and late stages, proving that the algorithm maintains consistent convergence characteristics throughout the optimization process and does not fall into local optima due to different update methods in the early or late stages. In contrast, in the snake optimization algorithm, the small amount of food Q during the exploration phase limits the movement range of individual snakes in the search space, causing some snakes to enter a dormant state and fail to find food quickly, resulting in insufficient exploration ability in the early stage of the algorithm. Adaptive inertia weighting strategy

The adaptive inertia weighting strategy with the number of iterations as the degree of freedom parameter was used to improve the quality of individual position updates of snakes. In the early stage of iteration, the large inertia weight improves the global search ability of the snake group, so that the snake group can search for the optimal food faster. In the later stages, as the dynamics switch between multiple modes and approach the optimal solution, the weight value decreases. This allows snake individuals to perform precise searches around the optimal solution, strengthening local search capability and effectively balancing the relationship between search diversity and convergence accuracy. Evaluation of ISASO on CEC2005 benchmark test functions

To validate the performance of the proposed method, we initially compared ISASO with other extant advanced algorithms using the CEC2005 benchmark test functions. The CEC2005 benchmark test function suite comprises a series of test functions, each with distinct characteristics and challenges. For instance, certain functions feature multiple local minima, whereas others possess complex search spaces. These test functions are extensively utilized to evaluate the performance of optimization algorithms, particularly in global optimization problems. To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the experimental outcomes, we performed multiple independent runs for each test function and meticulously documented the results of each run. The specific experimental parameters were as follows: Population Size: 50; Maximum Iterations: 1000; Independent Runs: 30.

The output current of the PV module can be computed using the parameters identified by the ISASO algorithm. The closer the output current is to the measured value, the higher the recognition accuracy of the algorithm. The parameters to be identified are Iph, Isd, Rs, Rsh, and n.

The operational flow of the ISASO algorithm is depicted in Fig. 5, and the system's runtime process is outlined in Table 3. Initially, the snake population is initialized using Tent chaotic mapping, and the population is categorized accordingly. Fitness values are calculated, and the best individual is recorded. Subsequently, the two populations cooperate in optimization. When the food quantity is insufficient, the snake population initiates a global search. As iterations progress and the food quantity meets the threshold, the population transitions to local search. At this point, the snakes dynamically switch between three modes of local optimization based on the varying temperature threshold. Experiments and analysis of results

To validate the performance of the proposed ISASO algorithm, we selected five distinct solar PV models for testing. The voltage and current data parameters of these well-known standard solar cells are extensively utilized for comparing and testing algorithm performance. The five PV models include the single diode model, double diode model, Photowatt-PWP201, STM6-40/36, and SM55 models. The parameters of these cell models are sourced from the datasheets provided by the manufacturers. For the first two models, the measured current-voltage values are derived from a 57 mm French R.T.C commercial solar cell under an irradiance of 1000W/m2 and a cell operating temperature of 33 °C [58]. For the solar PV module models, three distinct solar cells were selected: monocrystalline STM6-40/36, polycrystalline STP6-120/36, and monocrystalline SM55. These measured I–V data are available in the literature [59]. The I–V data for the single diode model STM40/36 were measured at 1000W/m2 and 51 °C; the single diode model STP120/36 at 1000W/m2 and 55 °C; and for the single diode model SM55, this study tested the I–V data parameter identification results under irradiances of 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000W/m2 and temperatures of 25, 40, and 60 °C. Additionally, the upper and lower limits of the unknown parameters of these solar cells are provided in Table 6, consistent with some published literature. The computational model employed in this study is an Intel Core i9-12900H CPU at 2.5 GHz with 16 GB of RAM, and the simulation platform utilized is MATLAB 2023b.

When employing metaheuristic algorithms for parameter identification in solar photovoltaic models, the configuration of key parameters significantly influences the algorithm's performance. This study evaluates the impact of various parameter settings on the algorithm's performance through sensitivity analysis to ascertain the optimal parameter combination. The maximum number of iterations (Tmax) and population size (Pop) were chosen as key parameters and experimental analysis was conducted using data from the French R.T.C photovoltaic solar DDM cell. To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the proposed ISASO algorithm, this study designed experiments with various parameter combinations, as shown in Table 4. By analyzing the experimental results of these combinations, the optimal parameter configurations for specific applications can be determined. Table 4 presents the parameter identification accuracy (expressed as Root Mean Square Error, RMSE) of the ISASO algorithm under various combinations of population size and number of iterations. The results indicate that a population size of 50 and 2000 iterations yields the highest parameter identification accuracy and the lowest operating cost for the algorithm. Specifically, when the population size is 30, even with an increase to 3000 iterations, the final RMSE value remains higher than that with a population size of 50. With a population size of 50, the highest parameter identification accuracy is achieved at 2000 iterations, and further increasing to 3000 iterations does not significantly enhance the RMSE value. When the population size is increased to 100, the accuracy decreases at 1000 iterations. Conclusion and future research directions

This study presents an innovative application of the SO algorithm for photovoltaic parameter identification. To overcome the limitations of the SO algorithm in photovoltaic parameter identification, we have employed a synergistic combination of integrated algorithms and enhanced strategies to significantly improve its accuracy and convergence performance. The rapid and efficient global search strategy of the Subtraction-Average-Based Optimization (SABO) algorithm is employed to replace the exploratory position update strategy of SO. The Tent chaotic map is utilized for population initialization, thereby enhancing the quality of the initial population. During the development phase of the algorithm, dynamic learning factors and adaptive inertia weight strategies are introduced. These allow the step size of the algorithm to adjust adaptively with the current iteration number, thereby improving convergence speed and accuracy, and balancing the exploration and exploitation capabilities of SO. To validate the parameter identification performance of the proposed Improved Self-adaptive Symbiotic Organisms Search (ISASO) algorithm for solar PV models, comprehensive experimental analyses were conducted on the SDM, DDM, PWP201, STM6-40/36, and SM55 solar PV models. A comparative analysis of the ISASO algorithm with existing SO, SABO, WOA, EHHO, MSIDBO, and ASFSSA algorithms was conducted. The research results demonstrate that the ISASO algorithm excels in convergence speed, accuracy, and robustness, and can more effectively identify optimal values of unknown parameters in the solar photovoltaic model, ensuring the model closely approximates the measured data of the actual solar photovoltaic system. Consequently, the ISASO algorithm can be recommended as a highly accurate and reliable method for photovoltaic model parameter identification. Although the effectiveness of the ISASO optimizer has been established in this study, its performance in multi-objective optimization problems remains to be fully validated. Future research could integrate ISASO with other intelligent technologies, such as deep learning, dimensionality reduction techniques, or hierarchical search strategies, to further enhance its performance in complex optimization problems.
