Automatic maxillary sinus segmentation and pathology classification on cone-beam computed tomographic images using deep learning
Authors: Oğuzhan Altun, Duygu Çelik Özen, Şuayip Burak Duman, Numan Dedeoğlu, İbrahim Şevki Bayrakdar, Gözde Eşer, Özer Çelik, Muhammed Akif Sümbüllü, Ali Zakir Syed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaxillofacial complex automated segmentation could alternative traditional segmentation methods to increase the effectiveness of virtual workloads. The use of DL systems in the detection of maxillary sinus and pathologies will both facilitate the work of physicians and be a support mechanism before the planned surgeries.ObjectiveThe aim was to use a modified You Only Look Oncev5x (YOLOv5x) architecture with transfer learning capabilities to segment both maxillary sinuses and maxillary sinus diseases on Cone-Beam Computed Tomographic (CBCT) images.MethodsData set consists of 307 anonymised CBCT images of patients (173 women and 134 males) obtained from the radiology archive of the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology. Bilateral maxillary sinuses CBCT scans were used to identify mucous retention cysts (MRC), mucosal thickenings (MT), total and partial opacifications, and healthy maxillary sinuses without any radiological features.ResultsRecall, precision and F1 score values for total maxillary sinus segmentation were 1, 0.985 and 0.992, respectively; 1, 0.931 and 0.964 for healthy maxillary sinus segmentation; 0.858, 0.923 and 0.889 for MT segmentation; 0.977, 0.877 and 0.924 for MRC segmentation; 1, 0.942 and 0.970 for sinusitis segmentation.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that maxillary sinuses can be segmented, and maxillary sinus diseases can be accurately detected using the AI model.

Introduction

The maxillary sinus is the largest of the paranasal sinuses. It has a pyramidal anatomical shape, with its base localized on the nasoantral medial wall and its apex in the zygomatic process of the maxilla [1]. The Schneiderian membrane, a pseudo-multilayered ciliated columnar epithelium that lines the inside of the maxillary sinus, continuously transports mucus secreted by its mucociliary cells to the ostium. Blockage in the ostium or dysfunction of the mucociliary cells can cause sinus diseases [2]. The maxillary alveolar process supporting the teeth forms the lower border of the maxillary sinus. Therefore, pathologies originating from teeth can also cause maxillary sinus disorders [3]. The characteristic radiographic findings of maxillary sinus diseases include mucosal thickening (MT), mucous retention cysts (MRCs, also known as antral sinus pseudocysts), and total opacifications [4].

Depending on the indication, two- or three-dimensional imaging methods are used in the diagnosis of maxillary sinus disorders. When three-dimensional structures are examined on a two-dimensional plane, they may be magnified, distorted, and superimposed [4]. Therefore, three-dimensional imaging methods are more effective for the detailed examination of the maxillary sinus region, which has a complex anatomy. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) can offer imaging with higher image resolution, lower radiation dose, shorter scanning time, and lower cost compared to conventional computed tomography (CT), even though CT is considered the gold standard in the three-dimensional evaluation of the maxillary sinus [5, 6]. Due to these benefits, past studies have shown that when examining variations or diseases associated with the maxillary sinus, CBCT is a more popular three-dimensional imaging technique than CT [7–9].

Accurate diagnosis of pathological alterations in the maxillary sinus can be challenging for inexperienced physicians. Traditional computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) systems rely on machine learning and provide clinicians with a valuable second opinion by automatically detecting and classifying abnormal radiographic findings [10]. However, before training these systems, the specific features of the images to be defined or classified must be determined manually [10]. Deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs), a subset of deep learning (DL) algorithms, can automatically detect key features and categorize them based on acquired data [11]. A large amount of training data is required to ensure the high diagnostic performance of artificial intelligence (AI) systems based on deep CNNs. In cases of insufficient data, a pretrained CNN model with transfer learning architectures helps to transfer parameters that will allow unique adjustments to the target data according to the task being performed [12].

When the reported studies on automated segmentation of maxillary sinuses are examined, Mori et al. [13] performed the segmentation of healthy and inflamed maxillary sinuses on panoramic radiographs using the CNN-based DetecNet al.gorithm. Ha et al. [14] performed the classification of healthy maxillary sinuses, retention pseudocysts, and cyst or tumor groups with the CNN-based EfficientDet al.gorithm based on a two-feature pyramid network structure. In a study on multispiral CT images, the U-Net network architecture, which is frequently preferred in medicine and dentistry, was used for odontogenic maxillary sinusitis segmentation [15].

Although there are studies in the literature that separately segmented mucosal thickening(MT) and mucous retention cyst(MRC) [14, 16], in general, images with any of the maxillary sinus lesions were segmented as sinusitis or inflamed maxillary sinus [17–19]. Our segmentation study makes a novel research contribution to the existing literature by detecting each maxillary sinus lesion, maxillary sinuses with or without any pathological findings, and healthy maxillary sinuses. Our hypothesis is that the You Only Look Oncev5x (YOLOv5x) architecture-based model, which has not been used in previous maxillary sinus segmentation studies, can detect these lesions separately.

This study aimed to separately segment both maxillary sinuses and maxillary sinus diseases on CBCT images using customized YOLOv5x architecture with transfer learning capability, thus increasing training efficiency with limited data using systems based on DL algorithms.

Materials and methods

Study design

The Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) guidelines and the Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM) were followed in reporting this study.

Patient selection

The dataset consisted of CBCT images obtained for several reasons (impacted supernumerary teeth, temporomandibular joint diseases, implant surgery, or orthognathic surgery) in the Inonu University Faculty of Dentistry Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology. A paired 2-sample t-test with 95% power, 5% margin of error, and an effect size of dz = 0.20 yielded reliable results with 307 samples [20].The study included the images of 307 anonymized patients aged 20–52 years (173 females and 134 males, mean age of females 23.15 ± 5.44 years and males 28.42 ± 10.25 years) with clear maxillary sinus borders as a result of scanning CBCT images in the radiology archive from January 2020 to April 2022. The study was approved by the Inonu University Ethics Committee (ethics number 2021/2418) This study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Acquisition of the radiographic dataset

All scans was done with the consensus of the two oral and dentomaxillofacial radiologist(a SBD with 12 years of experience and a DCO with 8 years of experience), and it was done on standardized monitors (Dell Corp. P2719H 27inch) in a dark and quiet environment. Radiologists determined MRC, MT, total and partial opacifications, and healthy maxillary sinuses without any radiological findings from CBCT scans of bilateral maxillary sinuses. Interobserver reliability values were found by using Cohen’s kappa (0.96). Images that could not be chosen unanimously were not included in the study. Studies in the literature have reported that coronal images are the preferred initial procedure for paranasal CT imaging [21] and that coronal CT scans significantly improve visualization of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses [22]. Along with these opinions, the fact that we start to examine CBCT images from coronal slices when evaluating the maxillary sinuses led us to evaluate the detection performance of the model on these coronal sections. Although section images were available at 0.3–0.2 mm intervals, images were selected at an interval of 1 mm. This is because, for small intervals, the neighboring section images will be alike, thereby affecting the model performance.The parameters to be evaluated were determined according to the following criteria by modifying the categorization of two different studies [16, 23].

Healthy maxillary sinuses

Images with normal maxillary sinus boundaries and content.

MT

Flat/irregular and mixed thickenings less than one-third of the maxillary sinus mucosa.

MRC

Maxillary sinus mucosa thickening that is formed like a semispherical dome and does not extend over one-third of the maxillary sinus.

Sinusitis

Total or partial opacifications greater than one-third of the maxillary sinus.

All images in the archive were acquired using the same CBCT device (Newton 5G, Verona, Italy). Imaging parameters were as follows: tube voltage of 110 kVp, X-ray tube of 1–11 mA (milliamps), 360° rotation, scan time of 3.6 s, and voxel size of 0.3 mm3. CBCT images were saved as digital imaging and communication in medicine (DICOM) files. DICOM files were converted to coronal section images in JPEG (Joint Photographic Experts Group) format using the open-source software ITK-SNAP version 3.8 (www.itksnap.org), and sections in which the maxillary sinus borders were not clearly observed were extracted from the obtained images. The remaining 1377 coronal images were loaded into CranioCatch (Eskisehir, Turkey) labeling software. Of those, 635 contained at least one healthy maxillary sinus, 671 contained at least one MT, 427 contained at least one MRC, and 437 contained at least one sinusitis (Table 1).

Ground truth

Each maxillary sinus was polygonally labeled from its outer borders by two oral and dentomaxillofacial radiologists (DO with 8 years of experience and GE with 9 years of experience) with one and three years of experience to create a ground-truth label mask. The segmentation area was limited to the starting point of the sinus ostium before continuing into the infundibulum. Polygonal labeling was then performed by passing through the borders of each MRC, MT, and sinusitis lesion. Healthy maxillary sinuses were labeled at a distance of 1 mm from the maxillary sinus borders. In images in which the maxillary sinus contained more than one lesion (MT or MRC), each lesion was labeled separately.

Training

The labeled coronal images were resized to 640 × 640 pixel before being fed into the mesh, and contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) was applied for image enhancement.The dataset was divided into training (80%), validation (10%), and test (10%) subsets.

Training group

80% of the images represent the dataset used to train the model.

Validation group

10% of the images represent samples that are independent of the model training and should not be seen by the model during this time. The model is tried on this data set to stop training or revise the training variables.

Test group

10% of the images form the dataset on which the model is tested independently of the training and validation data.

The test subset consisted of images independent of the training set in each segmentation group.

In the training set, 2212 labels from 1102 coronal section images were used for maxillary sinus segmentation, 614 labels from 509 images were used for healthy maxillary sinuses, 988 labels from 671 images were used for MTs, 385 labels from 342 images were used for MRCs, and 453 labels from 350 images were used for sinusitis. In the validation subset, 269 labels from 137 images were used for maxillary sinus segmentation, 85 labels from 63 images were used for healthy maxillary sinuses, 127 labels from 83 images were used for MTs, 44 labels from 42 images were used for MRCs, and 50 labels from 43 images were used for sinusitis. In the test subset, 280 labels from 138 images were used for maxillary sinus segmentation, 77 labels from 63 images were used for healthy maxillary sinuses, 125 labels from 84 images were used for MTs, 47 labels from 43 images were used for MRCs, and 68 labels from 44 images were used for sinusitis.

Deep learning was implemented using the YOLOv5x architecture, a supervised complex-scale object detection model trained using the original COCO 2017 dataset, supported by the PyTorch library and the Python open-source programming language (v.3.6.1; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). Using this pretrained architecture, instead of training a mesh from the beginning, the weights of the lower levels were improved by recycling the weights of the higher levels using back propagation and a feedforward method to identify structures in the coronal section images. YOLOv5 Deep learning algorithm

The development of YOLOv5, which is based on YOLOv4, resulted in a significant increase in running speed, with a maximum speed of 140 frames. YOLOv5 can be implemented in embedded devices because it is smaller in size and has a weight file that is roughly 90% smaller than YOLOv4’s. When it comes to accuracy, YOLOv5 outperforms YOLOv4 [24]. YOLOv5 utilizes CSPDarknet as its backbone. In the first step, CSPNet [25] is included in the feature map with gradient changes to solve problems related to repeated gradient information in large-scale backbones. Thus, the model size is reduced, and the inference speed is increased by reducing the number of model parameters. In the next step, PANet [26], which is used in the neck region, adopts a new feature pyramid network (FPN) structure that improves the spread of low-level features. The feature grid and all feature levels are interconnected, with an adaptive feature layer that spreads important information to the next subnetwork from each feature level. In PANet’s lower layers, the use of accurate localization signals, which can enhance the objects’ position accuracy, is improved. Finally, in the head section, three feature maps of different sizes (18 × 18, 36 × 36, and 72 × 72) are created to enable the model to process small, medium, and large objects [27]. The YOLOv5 series is available in five models (YOLOv5n, YOLOv5s, YOLOv5m, YOLOv5l and YOLOv5x). These models are identical in structure, but differ in depth and width [28]. The more convolution kernels used in sub-models of this architecture, the thicker the feature map and the greater the ability of the network to learn features. YOLOv5x is the largest and most powerful variant, providing the highest accuracy among the different versions. The YOLOv5 workflow and algorithm are shown in Fig. 1 [29].

Results

The AI model showed high performance in the segmentation of the maxillary sinus and lesions (Fig. 5). Recall, precision and F1 score values for total maxillary sinus segmentation were 1, 0.985 and 0.992, respectively; 1, 0.931 and 0.964 for healthy maxillary sinus segmentation; 0.858, 0.923 and 0.889 for MT segmentation; 0.977, 0.877 and 0.924 for MRC segmentation; 1, 0.942 and 0.970 for sinusitis segmentation. mAP values of 0.5 IoU were found to be 0.994, 0.985, 0.819, 0.965 and 0.985 for total maxillary sinus segmentation, healthy maxillary sinus, MT, MRC, and sinusitis, respectively (Table 2). Discussion

In medical applications, segmentation studies using DL systems can improve diagnostic accuracy. Using DL-based AI systems to automate the comprehensive examination of maxillary sinus borders and maxillary sinus health or pathology prior to sinus-related surgical operations can greatly benefit clinicians. In this study, maxillary sinuses and their lesions were successfully segmented using a semi-automatic CNN model based on the YOLOv5x architecture. In two-stage architectures such as Faster R-CNN, region proposals are generated, location coordinates are refined in the first stage, and then these region proposals are classified in the second stage, but in one-stage architectures such as YOLOv5, classification probabilities and location coordinates are generated directly without any region proposals [28].YOLOv5x is a single-stage object detector consisting of three main stages [30]. In the backbone stage, a feature map based on the images is created for convolutional operations. In the following stage, the extracted features are gathered in the neck region, mixed, and routed to the head, where box and class estimation procedures are carried out. YOLOv5 performs well in detecting both small-sized objects and overlapping objects, while the processing speed can be so fast that the real-time requirement can be easily met [28].Because it is simpler, faster, and more reliable than other DL architectures, this architecture is frequently used for segmentation and classification purposes [31].

When training the model used in this study, the labeled coronal sections with maxillary sinus lesions were used as a whole and were not separately identified as air and lesions. A grid system was used to divide the images and perform the end-to-end regression calculations directly, significantly simplifying the computational process. YOLOv5x performed best in maxillary sinus segmentation, with an average Dice score of 0.940. This can be attributed to the fact that this group had the largest amount of data and that the labeled areas were large and properly bordered. The model performed best in the healthy maxillary sinus group (0.910), followed by the sinusitis (0.890), MRC (0.807), and MT (0.684) groups. Although the amount of data in the MRC group was lower than that in the MT group, the distinct semispherical shape of MRCs contributed to better segmentation performance. The very low number of pixels matched with each other on images in which the MT covered a very small area and the boundaries could not be clearly traced resulted in a lower average Dice score in this group.

Many studies have used CNN algorithms to evaluate maxillary sinuses using two-dimensional radiographs, such as Water’s or panoramic radiographs [13, 14, 17–19]. Kim et al. [17] reported that their DL model performed better than radiologists in diagnosing maxillary sinusitis using Water’s images. In a study comparing the performance of radiologists, dental residents, and an AlexNet-based model in diagnosing maxillary sinusitis, Murata et al. [18] found that the model and the radiologists showed similar performance, which was better than that of the dentists. In another study, the DetectNet architecture was used to detect healthy, inflammatory, and cyst-/tumor-containing maxillary sinuses on panoramic radiographs [19], achieving a maxillary sinusitis detection accuracy of ≥ 90%. In a similar study, Mori et al. [13] reported that their DetectNet-based model achieved sinusitis detection accuracies of 0.888 and 0.929 in two different test datasets. In this study, the model’s accuracy value for the sinusitis group was 0.942.

In a study using panoramic radiographs, Ha et al. [14] reported that the segmentation accuracies of a model based on the EfficientNet architecture were 98% for healthy maxillary sinuses, 81% for retention pseudocysts, and 90% for cyst or tumors. The model in our study showed accuracies of 0.931 and 0.861 for healthy maxillary sinuses and MRCs, respectively. The detection method using CBCT images had significantly higher accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity than the method based on panoramic radiographs.

Serindere et al. [32] used CNN algorithms to detect maxillary sinusitis in panoramic and CBCT images. When maxillary sinuses with MTs, fluid or MRCs were diagnosed with sinusitis, the mean AUC value obtained using CBCT images was higher than that obtained using panoramic radiographs (0.997 vs. 0.755). In our study, when MTs, MRCs, and sinusitis lesions were classified and segmented individually, the model yielded AUC values of 0.900, 0.944, and 0.974, respectively.

Using CT images and an automatic maxillary sinus segmentation technique by integrating VGG and enhanced V-Net, Xu et al. [33] obtained a Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 94.40 ± 2.07% for healthy maxillary sinuses. However, the model’s performance in segmenting maxillary sinuses with mucosal inflammation was not satisfactory. Mucosal inflammation at the base of the maxillary sinus can result from the interaction between tooth roots and the lower wall of the sinus and from the easy penetration of dental microbial flora into the sinus. Using six models based on CNN algorithms, a previous study successfully detected odontogenic maxillary sinusitis on CT images in which the positions of the tooth roots associated with the maxillary sinus were clearly observable. Among the six models, the lowest accuracy was 79.67%, while the highest was 93.78% [34]. A decision support system developed by Alekseeva et al. [15] achieved 90.09% accuracy in the diagnosis of chronic odontogenic rhinosinusitis. Another study using CT images employed a fully convolutional network (FCN) combined with a probabilistic atlas for paranasal sinus segmentation. The location of the paranasal sinus was identified using the probabilistic atlas, and then the region of interest (ROI) to be fed to the network was determined. The model yielded a DSC of 0.83, despite the presence of paranasal sinuses whose borders could not be clearly observed [35].

Studies on maxillary sinus segmentation using CBCT images are increasing due to the widespread use of CBCT for three-dimensional maxillary sinus imaging in dentistry. Morgan et al. [36] found that their 3D U-Net architecture–based CNN model for fully automatic maxillary sinus segmentation on images acquired by two CBCT devices provided more exact and consistent automatic segmentation in terms of time than semiautomatic methods. Their model was based on the creation of a three-dimensional model by applying the cube algorithm to segmented maxillary sinus images. However, in that study, healthy and pathological maxillary sinuses were segmented together, and the maxillary sinus lesions were not segmented separately. Using a customized 3D nnU-Net model, Jung et al. found that the average DSC increased after each step of maxillary sinus segmentation, with the segmentation in the last step yielding the highest value [37]. In another study in which a U-Net architecture–based model segmented healthy and hazy maxillary sinuses on CBCT images, the DSC and Hausdorff distance(HD) values increased after post-processing [38]. Kim et al. [39] created SinusNet, a DL-based model that performs label-free maxillary sinus segmentation on CBCT images. By placing pixel-based synthetic lesions on CBCT images of maxillary sinus regions, this model enables the segmentation of maxillary sinus lesions without labeling.

Using a method similar to that employed in this study, Hung et al. [18] grouped healthy maxillary sinuses and maxillary sinus diseases on CBCT images acquired at different doses and segmented them using a DL algorithm. The authors employed support vector regression (SVR), an advanced machine learning method used to solve complex regression problems, to identify MTs and MRCs from form features and used V-Net to segment the maxillary sinus air spaces. This two-stage model yielded AUC values of 0.91 and 0.84 for MTs and MRCs, respectively, for low-dose scans and 0.89 and 0.93 for MTs and MRCs, respectively, for full-dose scans. The single-stage YOLOv5x model used in this study showed similar MT segmentation performance, with an AUC of 0.90, and higher MRC segmentation accuracy, with an AUC of 0.94.

Despite its good results, this study has certain limitations. The data used for model training and detection performance evaluation were from a single center, and external data were not included. Moreover, the model’s performance was not compared with that of radiologists. Furthermore, only maxillary sinus regions with clear borders were included in the segmentation area. It is not known whether the model’s segmentation performance may change in cases in which the continuity of the sinus borders is disrupted for reasons such as maxillary sinus surgery or trauma. Future studies may classify maxillary sinus lesions and segment maxillary and other paranasal sinuses and the pathologies occurring in them.

Conclucion

In conclusion, this study shows that the AI model used can reliably segment maxillary sinuses and specific maxillary sinus lesions. The use of DL systems in the detection of maxillary sinus and pathologies will both facilitate the work of physicians and be a support mechanism before the planned surgeries.
