Enhanced ground reaction force analyses reveal injury-related Biomechanical differences in runners
Authors: Ryan M. Nixon, Kevin R. Vincent, Lydia Pezzullo, Heather K. Vincent
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This three-part study investigated alternative pre-processing techniques to better understand the differences in patterns of ground reaction force (GRF) and load rate (LR) among runners with running-related injury (RRI). 534 runners were assessed on an instrumented treadmill with 3D kinematic data capture. Participants were classified as “injured” or “uninjured” and “rearfoot” (RF) or “non-rearfoot” (non-RF) strikers. The raw net GRF is normalized by time and then averaged across at least ten steps for the left and right foot; a double Gaussian characterizes the biphasic double-mass-spring model of running gait. Six parameters from the Gaussians were used to describe the relative differences and shape change based on injury condition. LRs were calculated using a central difference numerical derivative of the raw normalized net force data. 32% of runners reached peak negative LR (unloading) within the first 20% of stance. Injured RF strikers had 18% higher peak positive LR and 6% shorter time to peak than uninjured RF strikers (p < 0.05). Injured non-RF strikers showed peak negative LR 10% earlier in normalized stance, with a 10% shorter interval between positive and negative peaks (p < 0.05). The magnitude and timing of impact and active phases of GRF waveforms differed in runners with history of tibial stress fractures and current Achilles tendinopathy (p < 0.05). LR and LR timing are important in relation to specific RRI. These alternative pre-processing methods may help improve mechanistic understanding of GRF and LR and identify gait retraining foci for specific injury diagnoses in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17773-w.

Introduction

Running is a popular form of exercise and recreation worldwide, yet it carries a substantial risk of musculoskeletal injury, with incidence rates ranging from 3% to a staggering 85%1. Despite advancements in running biomechanics research, the relationship between running-related injuries (RRIs) and kinetic variables such as peak ground reaction forces (GRFs) and load rates (LRs) is inconsistently reported2,3. The literature suggests that higher GRF and LR may contribute to overuse injuries in bone and soft tissues4,5. However, systematic reviews and meta-analyses provide mixed evidence, offering limited support for these models2,4,6,7.

The inconsistency in findings can be attributed to several factors, including the controlled laboratory conditions for treadmill running, individual variation in foot strike patterns, footwear, runner characteristics, running speed, and injury timelines across studies8–12. Recent research has emphasized the role of methodological factors, such as excluding non-vertical forces, pre-processing techniques like signal filtering, and limiting LR analysis to a narrow region of interest (ROI), in contributing to divergent conclusions drawn from kinetic data3–5,12–17. Current analyses often focus on the vertical component7,18 and simplify GRF curves using low-pass filters with cutoff frequencies ranging from 20 to 50 Hz19,20. This approach may reduce GRF metrics to discrete peaks and summarize LR values within a limited region of interest3,5,11–17,21, which can be informative but necessarily eliminates much of the complexity of the GRF waveform.

Deeper insights could be provided by a more comprehensive analysis by including anterior-posterior and medial-lateral forces with the vertical component to form the net GRF. The net GRF was calculated as the vector resultant16. Nonvertical forces are critical for neuromuscular control22 and the metabolic costs of running23. Emerging evidence suggests that nonvertical force may be linked to fatigue, age, foot strike pattern, and injury24–29. The apparent relevance of nonvertical force indicates that the current understanding of the relationship between GRF, LR, and various injuries may be incomplete.

The prevailing approach facilitates initial exploration, but studies show that there are likely underappreciated and meaningful GRF signals at frequencies above conventional cutoffs [30], and using low cutoff or unmatched lowpass filter methods can be problematic when interpreting physiological information across signals31,32. For instance, Soft tissue vibrations in the lower extremity are significant in running32,33 and can be observed in high-speed video along with time series and frequency data from force plate measurements, but these signals are often lost or significantly modified in lowpass filtered data. Improving the accuracy of GRF and LR measurements requires processing methods that preserve detailed characteristics of the GRF waveform and may include methods such as conservative data averaging and Gaussian parameter determination. Expanding the conventional ROI to capture net GRF fluctuations and LR throughout the entire stance phase may provide a more complete, unique understanding of individual responses to ground impact and energy dissipation. From the clinical perspective, clarifying the GRF and LR patterns related to specific injury diagnoses and foot strike patterns would be a breakthrough. Sports medicine specialists would have more accurate data needed to improve therapeutic program design, gait retraining cues and developing running injury prevention programs.

This study used data to explore alternative pre-processing techniques by characterizing net GRF and LR patterns across the stance phase in endurance runners. The goal was to identify differences in GRF and LR responses during running based on injury status and type [bone, soft tissue]. We hypothesized that alternative preprocessing methods will reveal significant features in the LR associated with different types of injury. Additionally, we expect that modeling the system dynamics and using descriptive mathematics will describe the shape change of the GRF and show characteristic differences with injury status and type. To accomplish these aims, this study was comprised of three main parts: (1) part 1: fidelity analysis to compare filtering cutoff effects on GRF responses in a subsample of 15 runners, (2) part 2: a cross-sectional characterization of GRF and LR using the cutoff selected from part 1 across the entire cohort of 534 runners, and (3) part 3: comparative analysis using the methods developed in above among a subset of 29 injured and non-injured matched runners of different foot strike type.

Methods

Design and participants

This study included 534 recreational and competitive runners from the UF Health Running Medicine Clinic or UF Health Sports Performance Center, assessed between January 1, 2016, and March 1, 2023. Participants aged 12 to 77 years (mean age: 35.5 ± 15.2 years), consisted of injury-free runners (39.1%) and those with recent musculoskeletal injuries (60.9%). The study protocols were approved by the University of Florida Institutional Review Board (study numbers 202101632, 201501196, and 202400001). All participants provided written informed consent to participate using approved consent forms. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Each runner underwent a comprehensive assessment, a physician examination for injured runners, functional screening, training history analysis, and 3D gait analysis.

Running and injury histories

Participants completed an electronic survey using REDCap34, collecting data on health risks, training goals, running volume, shoe wear, recent program changes, cross-training, and demographics35. In cases of injury, the running medicine physician noted the presence of musculoskeletal pain, recent diagnosis, and affected side. Injuries were defined as conditions interfering with training schedules or reduced weekly training volume. Diagnoses were classified into soft tissue [e.g., iliotibial band syndrome, Achilles tendonitis, plantar fasciitis] and bone injuries [e.g., medial tibial stress syndrome, post-recovery stress fractures]. Participants with bone injuries were cleared before testing, and injured runners resumed running before their gait assessment.

Running gait assessment

Gait data were captured in a single session using a 33-marker system to track body segments from the shoulder to the foot (bilateral calcanei, the base of the hallux, the base of 5th metatarsal, medial and lateral malleoli, tibial tuberosities, medial and lateral femoral condyles, thigh, anterior and posterior superior iliac spines, wrists, forearms, lateral elbow condyles, triceps, acromions; an offset marker was placed over the right scapula)36–39. A standing calibration trial preceded kinematic and kinetic data collection at 120 Hz using a 7-camera, three-dimensional motion capture system (Motion Analysis, Rohnert Park, CA, USA; www.motionanalysis.com/). Participants ran at a self-selected speed on a zero-grade instrumented treadmill (AMTI Inc., Watertown, MA, USA; www.amti.biz/), with GRFs recorded at 1200 Hz. Kinematic data and spatial-temporal parameters, including cadence, step length, vertical displacement of the center of mass (COM), step width, and stance times, were processed using Visual 3D v 6.0 software (C-Motion Inc., Rockville, MD, USA; www.has-motion.ca)37. These parameters were calculated based on ten consecutive strides40.

Comparative filtering and expanding the ROI (part 1)

The resultant magnitude of the net GRF was calculated as the square root of the sum of the squares of all three-dimensional force components. Net GRFs were normalized by stance time, averaging 3–4 samples for each percent of stance to preserve instantaneous rate-change information while facilitating comparisons across runners. A subset of 15 runners, randomized by foot strike type, sex, age, and BMI, underwent comparative filtering to assess fidelity loss of net GRF data across different 4th-order Butterworth low-pass filter cutoff frequencies (600, 100, 60, and 10 Hz). This subset was selected to represent the age spectrum, both sexes and presence of injury or not (bone or soft tissue). The Shannon-Nyquist theorem suggests 600 Hz as the highest lowpass cutoff to prevent aliasing. The 100 Hz cutoff is a moderately high low-pass cutoff close to a signal seen in the frequency analysis across many runners. The 60 and 10 Hz values are an order of magnitude lower and effectively bound the commonly used cutoffs in running science.

Loading and unloading were characterized by LRs and calculated for every percentage of stance using a central difference numerical derivative of the normalized net GRF with the time interval equal to 1/100th the respective left and right stance times. To compare filtering methods, peak GRF and LR values were identified for both left and right foot strikes, and a stance-wise comparison across the subset identified regions of stance that were significantly modified limbs. The magnitude and timing of peak positive and negative LR were recorded for all runners. Peak positive and negative LRs were compared across the entire dataset and further analyzed for injury subsets, including additional relative maximums and minimums.

GRF waveform modeling and musculoskeletal injury subgroups (part 2)

Specificity, exclusivity, and high injury acuity are required to differentiate GRF curve characteristics, and the Gaussian parameters of Eq. 1 provide a quantitative way to describe these differences in characteristics.

The entire sample of 534 runners was subjected to the following approach. The GRF waveform has bimodal features with primary and secondary peaks. Net GRF was modeled as a two-mass dynamic system, representing two overlapping bell-shaped impulses corresponding to the impact and active stance phases during running. The impact phase reflects the collision of the lower extremity with the ground, while the active phase involves loading and unloading the remainder of the body mass. A double Gaussian curve-fitting technique allows the characterization of each impulse by amplitude (A), peak time (B), and width or duration (C). Injury and foot strike comparison of GRF and LR Gaussian values (part 3)

Two injury subgroups, tibial stress fracture (TSF, N = 17) and Achilles tendinopathy (AT, N = 12), were case-matched by sex, age, foot strike type, and BMI to a control group for comparison. These diagnoses were chosen to represent a common bone injury and a common soft tissue injury that occurs among runners45,46. Injury subgroup LRs were evaluated more thoroughly than the entire runner cohort, with additional relative maximums and minimums within the bridge region, defined as the time of stance between the impact and active peaks.

Spatiotemporal, kinematic parameters and vertical stiffness

Standard spatiotemporal parameters, such as cadence, vertical and lateral COM displacement, step length, and stride width, were determined using established methods37,38. COM was calculated following the bone models described by de Leva41. Parameters were normalized to the gait cycle, where 0% represented initial foot contact and 100% represented subsequent foot contact of the same foot. The stance phase was defined as the time in contact with the treadmill belt. Foot strike type was identified with the angle between the foot segment and the horizontal ground at foot contact, confirmed by video review. Kinematic measures were processed using a 4th-degree Butterworth filter with a 40 Hz cutoff frequency.

Vertical stiffness, Kvert, related to bone injuries42, was estimated using the formula: Kvert = Fmax /Δy, where Fmax is the peak vertical force, and Δy is the maximum COM displacement43–48.

Statistical approach

Data were examined for normality, and non-normally distributed measures were logarithmically transformed (Log10). Variables including step width, stance time, leg stiffness, peak net GRF, and peak LR were transformed. Baseline differences in categorical variables and spatial-temporal measures between healthy and injured groups were assessed using χ2 tests and ANOVA, respectively. Repeated measures ANOVA analyzed net GRF and LR differences across filter cutoff frequencies in the subsample of 15 runners. Pearson correlations were generated for GRF and LR between the raw data and data from each cutoff frequency (part 1). One-way ANOVA was employed to test differences in peak net GRF and LR by filter cutoff frequency, with Tukey post-hoc tests applied for pairwise comparisons (part 2). Univariate ANOVA tested interactions of injury status and sidedness on Gaussian metrics, kinematic measures, and one-way ANOVA determined differences in LR by injury status and foot strike type (part 3). Significance was established at 0.05 a priori. All analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics (v.29, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY; www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics).

GRF and LR across cutoff frequencies in the fidelity analysis (part 1)

A subset of 15 The 15 participants were deliberately chosen to represent our general population of runners across the age spectrum, experience levels, sex, and injury histories in a manner that reflected the overall population. The main characteristics were as follows: age = 39.8 ± 19.8 years, 60% female, 88.9% Caucasian, BMI = 22.8 ± 3.2 kg/m2, weekly distance = 32.9 ± 23.5 km, 80% were rearfoot strikers and 60% had recent running related injury. This subset did not statistically differ compared to the overall cohort. Significant differences were detected between the raw/normalized data and the lowpass filtered data from 1 to 14% and 85–100% of the stance phase (p < 0.05). In the early stance, filtering significantly altered GRFs during the impact phase, a region critical for accurate mathematical analysis. The lowest cutoff frequencies produced pronounced edge effects, significantly altering stance time. Despite variability in waveform shapes and oscillatory frequencies among runners, the filtering effects on net GRF waveforms were consistent, resulting in artificially similar filtered curves.

Low cutoff frequencies smoothed oscillatory phenomena, delaying or eliminating the first impact peak in the net GRF and altering peak net GRF and LR values (p < 0.05). Table 2 provides the summary results of the fidelity testing. Lower cutoff frequencies were associated with greater differences in maximal GRF and LR values (both p < 0.001).

Expanding the ROI: characterization of LR for the entire stance in the whole cohort (part 2)

To explore the advantages of a broader analytical approach, we analyzed the time rate change of GRF during the whole stance, including positive and negative LR. Figure 1 overviews all participants’ loading and unloading patterns during the stance phase (N = 534), highlighting peak positive and negative LRs. The definition of ROI can vary, but the conventional ROI excludes 40% of these peak positive LRs detected here. Early in the stance phase (< 5%), 49% of runners produced their peak positive LR, while 32% produced their peak negative LR within the first 20% of the stance. Most runners (66%) exhibited peak negative LR during the push-off phase (> 50% stance).

Load rates by injury status in the whole cohort (part 2)

Across the entire sample, runners displayed positive and negative LRs throughout the complete stance phase (Table 2). In the first 5% of the stance, 49% of runners reached their peak positive LR, and 32% reached peak negative LR during the first 20% stance. RF strikers exhibited 8% higher peak positive LRs than non-RF (p < 0.05), and they have significantly larger LR fluctuations or excursions over the stance phase (p < 0.05). Injured individuals had 18% higher peak positive LR among RF strikers, occurring 6% earlier in the stance phase than uninjured RF strikers (both p < 0.05). Injury status was significantly associated with the timing of peak negative LRs; injured non-RF strikers reached their peak negative LR 17% earlier and had 18% less time between peaks than uninjured non-RF strikers (p < 0.05). This early peak unloading, observed during the impact and braking phase, indicates potential instability (27,49). 50% of RF strikers exhibited early peak negative LRs compared to 12.2% of non-RF strikers.

Speed, spatiotemporal characteristics, vertical stiffness and kinematics

Supplemental Tables 1 and 2 present data on running speed, spatiotemporal characteristics, and Kvert and joint kinematics. There were no statistical differences detected by injury status or side for joint angles at foot strike and mid-stance (Supplemental Table 1). Recently injured runners exhibited a 2% lower average cadence (p = 0.05) and 8% lower Kvert compared to non-injured runners (both p < 0.05); the TSF had lowest Kvert compared to AT and non-injured controls (168 ± 32 N/cm versus 176 ± 40 N.cm and 180 ± 36 N//cm; p = 0.014) (Supplementary Table 2).

Bilateral AT injuries displayed distinct GRF curve patterns with a pronounced impact event and shortened stance time shown in Fig. 2.B. Gaussian parameter analysis (Table 4) revealed that, while bilateral AT cases had similar impact magnitudes to controls, their impact duration was shorter and peak timing occurred earlier. Bilateral AT cases exhibited a 67% increase in the magnitude of peak positive LR, while unilateral AT cases had a 39% increase compared to case match controls (both p < 0.05). Similarly, the bridge peak positive LR was significantly elevated in injury groups, with an 85% increase in bilateral AT cases and a 29% increase in unilateral cases relative to uninjured runners (both p < 0.05).

The impact peak in bilateral AT cases occurred at 22 ms, marking the earliest impact peak time (Bimpact) and resulting in maximal early stance phase unloading, as shown in the LR graph (Fig. 2.F). Unilateral AT cases exhibited a 15% difference in stance time between the injured and uninjured sides, with the affected side’s stance time (average of 245 ms) being similar to controls, while the unaffected side had a shortened stance time (average of 212 ms), resembling bilateral AT cases. In unilateral AT, both limbs showed altered impact events, reducing overall stance time and advancing active GRF peaks (p < 0.05), which led to increased positive LR and reduced early stance unloading due to shortened time between phase peaks (Table 4, following page).

Discussion

This three-part study involved (1) a fidelity analysis to compare different filter cutoff frequencies of GRF data in a runner subsample, (2) testing the selected cutoff frequency to characterize GRF and positive-negative LR during stance in the whole study cohort, and (3) application of these GRF and LR timing and magnitude responses to injured and non-injured runners. Our alternative analytic approach showed that selecting a higher cutoff frequency for analysis compared to conventional methods produce GRF and LR results closer to raw values, and produced novel loading and unloading findings throughout stance that helped characterize a runner’s interaction with the ground beyond the conventional ROI. Gaussian patterns revealed timing and impact magnitude differences in impact and active phases of stance among runners with history of recent bone injury (TSF) and active soft tissue injury (AT). This methodology could be applied to help reveal new insight about the interactions between the runner and the ground in relation to injury status in subsequent studies.

Filtering methods

Filtering techniques exerted a significant effect on GRF and LR measurements, especially in the early and late stance phases. Studies on soft tissue vibrations suggest that frequencies around 50 Hz in the net GRF waveform may represent physiologically-relevant vibrations in running gait30,49. Therefore, low-pass filtering at cutoff frequencies near 50 Hz can artificially modulate or eliminate these vibration signals, even in non-RF strikers. The conventional ROI of the GRF waveform generally limits the LR analysis to positive values within a narrow region between 20 and 80% of the stance time before the first peak in GRF13,50,51. Unfiltered data normalized to percent stance by frame averaging showed high agreement with low-pass filtered data at the Nyquist cutoff frequency (Table 2), improving the signal-to-noise ratio by preserving the sharp response of raw data without removing any signal. Normalized, unfiltered data captures high-resolution GRFs, allowing for the characterization of intermediate LRs associated with vibrations resulting from impact. This technique has potential to: (1) preserve subtle signal features in the loading and unloading phases that could differentiate injured from uninjured runners, and to identify ‘signatures’ of bone and soft tissue injury, (2) be applied in a prospective manner to help researchers and clinicians contextualize these signals relative to onset of injury, injury type, rehabilitation programs, and pain symptoms. Through this initial dissection of the GRF and LR responses with less processing, we have new insight on the timing and signal features that differ with injury and foot strike. Subsequent steps include prospective data capture and validation to determine the mechanistic relationship between these mechanical responses and injury onset.

LR during stance

Figure 1 shows that considerable individual variability exists in stance loading, and that both maximum positive and negative LRs can occur early in stance. Efficient running mechanics involve controlled loading and unloading, facilitating a smooth transition between the stance and swing phases while the runner applies metabolically expensive propulsion24,52,53. While this unloading rate informs interpretation of impact attenuation and the propulsive phase of stance, a significant portion of this negative LR data would be excluded if traditional ROI boundaries were applied (see Fig. 1, left). Based on the theory of double-mass-spring model, one possible interpretation could be that early and erratic maximal loading or unloading rates indicate inefficiencies in transitioning from swing to stance and loading the full reaction force. This early peak unloading, observed during the impact and braking phase, indicates potential instability27,49. Another possibility is that this early unloading, or poor impact attenuation, might be the result of injury-related discomfort that triggers a partial ‘withdrawal’ of the foot from the ground to mitigate painful loading. Additional study of pain effects on the LR over time would help clarify this relationship.

After initial impact, runners make continuous neuromotor adjustments in the anteroposterior and mediolateral directions to dynamically stabilize the body across all planes of motion23,54,55. The materials and geometry of the shoe, along with the musculoskeletal tissues of the lower limb, contribute to passive impact damping56,57, whereas active damping is achieved through pre-tuning and anticipatory muscle activation changes34,58. Thus, leg muscle co-activation during late swing and after foot strike may help mitigate early unloading features and smooth transitions. The analytic approach used here would enable visualization of the runner response while interacting with the ground.

GRF and LR by injury status and foot strike

Analysis showed that LR magnitude and timing varied by foot strike and injury status, improving our understanding of loading beyond the assumptions made with traditional GRF analysis. First, non-RF strikers produced lower positive-to-negative LR excursions than RF strikers, suggesting better damping of LR during stance (Table 3). Second, injured RF strikers demonstrated an 18% higher peak positive LR and a shorter time to reach this peak than their uninjured counterparts; in contrast, injured non-RF strikers produced a peak negative LR 10% earlier in normalized stance time than in uninjured counterparts. The Gaussian values in Table 4 and net GRF waveforms in Fig. 2 indicate that runners with TSF produce different loading responses than runners with AT. The bridge region represents the mechanical transition characterized by damped oscillations as the runner shifts from the initial lower extremity impact to full bodyweight loading, and both injured groups had elevated LR (by 29–85%) compared to non-injured controls. TSF, especially bilateral, is associated with longer impact durations whereas AT, particularly bilateral, has the shortest impact duration, highest impact amplitude and shortest active phase duration.

The GRF and LR patterns of bilateral TSF could reflect the use of a ‘cautious’ loading strategy. Supplementary Table 2 indicates that Kvert is lower in the injured runners, and lowest among TSF, supporting softer, less stiff landings with history of this injury. There is potential that runners may have already adapted their loading mechanics to alleviate discomfort and prevent re-injury as they resumed training; this possibility could be studied with long-term repeated testing in a cohort of runners. In contrast, unilateral TSF cases showed GRF and LR patterns more similar to uninjured controls. With bilateral AT, we observed an impact impulse (calculated using Eq. 3) that is lower by about one-third compared to that of the control group. A high impact impulse might produce pain or discomfort with bilateral AT, so these runners may have adjusted their mechanics to cope with their condition. Unilateral AT cases were characterized by earlier active GRF peaks, increased positive LR, and reduced early stance unloading. A 15% asymmetry in stance time existed between the affected and unaffected limbs, potentially signifying minimization of strain on the injured limb.

Limitations and future directions

This cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inferences as we are not clear whether the GRF and LR signals are the cause or consequence of injury. The diversity in injury history introduces variability in GRF and LR responses, as runners were assessed at different times after recovery, and it was impossible to control for injury acuity or type. This first study has now set the stage for subsequent studies to determine which comes first, the injury or the GRF and LR responses. The reliance on treadmill data collection may differ from outdoor running patterns12,59. Moreover, the measured oscillations in the GRF represent a composite reaction, making it difficult to attribute them to specific muscle activations or tissue responses. Future investigations could build on this methodology by re-examining existing data on runners with specific injuries to assess its sensitivity compared to traditional analytic methods. Future research should aim to validate these findings in larger, more diverse cohorts and explore the longitudinal relationship between GRF/LR patterns and the development of RRI. The methods used in this study have important implications for clinical practice, especially for injury prevention and rehabilitation strategies in runners. By identifying specific GRF and LR signatures associated with different injury types and foot strike patterns, tailored interventions can be developed that address the unique biomechanical profiles of injured runners. For instance, modifying running techniques to manage early peak negative LR or reducing excessive positive LR may help runners with a history of TSF or AT. While outside the scope of this paper, we surmise that further refinement and focus on the key elements of GRF and LR signals could guide development of easy, scalable data collection methods for field use and mass testing of runner cohorts.

Conclusion

Using raw, unfiltered data and expanding the ROI to include the entire stance phase demonstrated that significant loading and unloading events occur outside traditional ROIs among endurance runners. These events are especially pronounced in runners with specific injuries, such as TSF and AT, emphasizing the importance of a more comprehensive approach to GRF analysis. Application of the double Gaussian model further differentiated injury types, revealing unique GRF waveform characteristics associated with TSF and AT.
