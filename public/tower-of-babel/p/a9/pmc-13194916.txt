Design-integrated obsolescence diagnostics for sustainable manufacturing: a multi-dimensional evaluation framework
Authors: Lesly Sierra-Fontalvo, Laura Ruiz-Pastor, Arturo Gonzalez-Quiroga, Jaime A Mesa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProactive obsolescence management from early design stages is critical to extending product lifecycles, reducing waste, and enhancing manufacturing system performance. This study introduces the Multi-Obsolescence Diagnostic Tool (MODT), a structured and scalable methodology for evaluating a product’s susceptibility to six types of obsolescence: technological, functional, psychological, economic, planned, and diminishing manufacturing sources and material shortages. MODT operates at both the product and component levels, using design attributes scoring and impact weighting to quantify obsolescence potential. A case study using an electromechanical drill demonstrates the tool’s feasibility and reveals key design aspects such as reparability and material availability that drive several types of obsolescence. Beyond supporting sustainable product development, the tool contributes to manufacturing system design and decision-making by enabling early interventions in material selection, modularity, and supply chain strategies. MODT also supports after-sales service planning, cost management, and spare part provisioning. Its structured format allows for integration with Computer-Aided Design and Product Lifecycle Management environments, enabling broader industrial applications. Results validate the tool’s relevance in minimizing product lifecycle disruptions, improving component-level robustness, and informing redesign efforts. MODT offers a practical and adaptable approach for manufacturers, designers, and engineers aiming to enhance durability and align product development with circular economy principles and strategic obsolescence management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43761-9.Keywords: Product obsolescence, Sustainable manufacturing, Product lifecycle, Spare parts planning, Circular product designSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

One challenge that product design has faced is the ongoing threat of obsolescence, where investment in an updated solution can quickly become irrelevant within months or years due to technological advancements1,2. The rapid acceleration in the consumption of new products and the disposal of older products not only contributes to the depletion of non-renewable resources and overuse of land but also shortens product lifecycles, resulting in higher replacement frequencies and increased waste3–5. As such, obsolescence represents both an environmental and an operational burden for manufacturers, affecting system availability, production continuity, and customer satisfaction.

Proactive tools introduced in early design stages can help anticipate and manage these risks, enabling more robust product development and support for circular economy goals6,7. In product development, obsolescence is commonly defined as a reduction in utility or value related to functional performance, technological compatibility, or consumer expectations8. Traditional mitigation approaches, such as last-time buys or part substitution, are primarily reactive and have limited impact across the full product lifecycle. Moreover, current forecasting models often focus on single types of obsolescence (e.g., technological or DMSMS) without addressing how specific design attributes influence the onset or severity of obsolescence. From a manufacturing science perspective, the early identification of obsolescence risks has implications that extend beyond design, affecting supply chain robustness, service logistics, spare parts provisioning, and production planning. Manufacturers require systematic approaches to assess component-level vulnerabilities and prioritize interventions that minimize lifecycle costs and complexity. A diagnostic methodology that maps obsolescence risk to design decisions offers practical benefits, including enhanced support for manufacturability, repair strategies, and the development of modular architecture.

Many existing obsolescence forecasting methods require frequent collection of product lifecycle data, such as tracking product sales or demand data6. Among the existing approaches to measuring obsolescence is the obsolescence method in manufacturing machines proposed by9, which considers economic, social, and environmental sustainability aspects using multi-criteria analysis. Other statistical approaches, such as that of10, establish a model to predict the impact of obsolescence in different market scenarios considering uncertainty through a Bayesian analysis. There are also approaches from the point of view of market analysis11, which include supply and demand relationships as a critical factor in accelerating product obsolescence. Despite these approaches, no robust methodology exists for measuring multiple types of obsolescence considering product design attributes12. Generally, the forecasting approaches focus on defining whether the scenario occurs but do not consider what type of obsolescence the product may present. At the same time, metrics can only identify one type of obsolescence and do not consider how design aspects can influence the product. As a result, determining the most effective design strategy to reduce obsolescence can be time-consuming.

Historically, product obsolescence has been deeply embedded in the Linear Economy Model, operating under a 'take-make-dispose’ paradigm that depends on shortened lifecycles to stimulate continuous consumption13. Within this linear framework, obsolescence is primarily treated as an inevitable End-of-Life (EoL) stage, intensifying resource depletion and environmental degradation14. However, the ongoing industrial transition towards circular business models demands a fundamental shift from rapid disposal to long-term value retention through strategies such as maintenance, reuse, remanufacturing, and recycling. In this context, integrating circular design principles (which prioritize lifecycle extension, modularity, and reparability) is essential. To effectively operationalize these principles, there is a critical need for multi-dimensional assessment tools capable of diagnosing obsolescence risks early in the product development phase.

This study introduces the Multi-Obsolescence Diagnostic Tool (MODT), which addresses this gap by providing a structured method to assess a product’s susceptibility to six types of obsolescence. The tool operates through a four-step process and is adaptable across different product categories. Unlike previous models, MODT quantifies the obsolescence potential at the component level based on weighted impact matrices linked to design attributes and enables prioritization of redesign efforts from a lifecycle perspective. In addition to its academic value, MODT serves practitioners in manufacturing systems by providing actionable insights that enhance product durability, minimize redesign loops, and facilitate the deployment of predictive obsolescence strategies. Its structured format allows future integration with Computer-Aided Design (CAD) and product lifecycle management systems, enhancing its applicability in industrial settings. As demonstrated through a case study of an electromechanical drill, MODT provides a replicable framework for linking design decisions to downstream impacts in production and post-market operations.

Obsolescence is a growing concern in product design and manufacturing due to its broad technical, environmental, and economic impacts. It refers to products, technologies, or parts that become outdated or unusable in a contemporary context15,16. A robust body of research has explored various types of obsolescence (namely planned, technological, functional, psychological, economic, and diminishing manufacturing sources and material shortages (DMSMS)) as defined in Cooper17, Sandborn18, and Trabelsi et al.19. These forms influence how and when products become obsolete, affecting not only consumer behavior but also product serviceability, component sourcing, and system-level sustainability.

Although obsolescence is becoming a more significant concern due to its broad impact, much work must be done to predict, evaluate, actively manage, and reduce it. The literature identifies three approaches to managing obsolescence: reactive, proactive, and strategic management15,20. Reactive obsolescence management is one of the most common approaches to addressing obsolescence problems. This strategy consists of reactionary behaviors for already obsolete components, such as lifetime or last-time buys, part substitution, uprating, and redesign12,21. Consequently, this strategy can be very costly over the life of a system, requiring time and resources to implement solutions that may not be available when needed or may not provide adequate parts support over the system’s life. This approach is usually found in a sustainment-dominated system such as aircraft and ships, where the lifecycle of the components mismatches the product lifespan22.

On the other hand, proactive management involves anticipating and preventing obsolescence problems. This approach consists of a combination of different measures to prevent or minimize the effects of obsolescence, such as obsolescence risk assessments, bill of materials management, and material risk index15,23. This strategy includes techniques, tools, and processes to detect and mitigate obsolescence at the component or system level. While these metrics can help determine the likelihood of a component becoming obsolete during its lifecycle, they do not provide information about the specific type of obsolescence it may experience. Generally, these metrics can only identify technological obsolescence or DMSMS. For an effective redesign, it is crucial to identify the types of obsolescence a system faces in order to determine the most suitable design strategy. By recognizing and understanding factors such as the robustness of materials, the flexibility of components, and the simplicity of upgrading, designers can devise strategies that prolong the lifespan of products and simplify their repair and upgrade processes.

Finally, strategic management of obsolescence focuses on minimizing the risk of obsolescence from the early design stages and over the product’s lifecycle20. Although this strategy is becoming increasingly recognized, it remains one of the least utilized. This requires proactive planning and strategic decision-making to address obsolescence throughout the product or system lifecycle15. Despite its potential to address obsolescence and environmental problems, the lack of data can be a barrier since it is often unavailable or difficult to estimate.Regarding design approaches, recent literature extensively explores DfX approaches and circular design principles aimed at extending product lifespans (Hapuwatte et al., 2024). However, as highlighted by recent systematic reviews, while there is a strong theoretical consensus on the importance of circular design, there remains a significant lack of practical, quantitative methodological support for product developers (Stölzle et al., 202334;). Most existing tools provide high-level qualitative guidelines or focus exclusively on single dimensions, such as technological decay, often neglecting the complex interplay of supply chain vulnerabilities (DMSMS) or user-driven psychological obsolescence.

While many of the methods listed in Table 1 provide robust forecasts, most of them are either focused on single types of obsolescence (e.g., technological or DMSMS), or are tailored to specific products such as electronics, smartphones, or energy systems10,30,34. Moreover, they often lack design-level diagnostics that could inform product architecture during early concept stages. Few methods integrate multiple obsolescence types simultaneously, and even fewer offer structured links between design attributes and obsolescence likelihood. Additionally, tools that do exist are rarely embedded within manufacturing workflows, making their utility limited in real-world industrial practice.

In this context, mitigating multi-dimensional obsolescence aligns with the DfX paradigm35. DfX represents a holistic approach to product development where specific lifecycle considerations are explicitly integrated into early design phases. By framing obsolescence management within relevant DfX disciplines, such as Design for Durability, Design for Disassembly, and Design for Reparability, designers can systematically evaluate critical trade-offs. For instance, ensuring that a product designed for easy disassembly also incorporates modular architectures to effectively mitigate technological and functional obsolescence36. Recognizing obsolescence as a multi-dimensional challenge highlights the necessity of these holistic DfX approaches. Extending a product’s lifecycle cannot be achieved through a single design objective,rather, it requires a coordinated balance of these specialized design disciplines to ensure long-term viability and sustainability within a circular economy37.

The theoretical foundation of this research is mainly based on the transition from linear to circular economic models. In a traditional linear economy, phenomena such as planned and technological obsolescence are often leveraged to accelerate replacement cycles. However, the paradigm shift toward a circular economy demands that products be designed for prolonged utility and value retention through strategies such as reuse, repair, and remanufacturing (Giolo Júnior et al., 2024; Stölzle et al., 2023). Within this context, product obsolescence emerges as the primary barrier to closing material loops. Therefore, understanding and proactively mitigating these multi-dimensional obsolescence risks during the early design phase is not merely an operational advantage, but a theoretical necessity for assessing the true environmental impact of products and achieving systemic sustainability.

Proposing the multi-obsolescence diagnostic tool

The proposed MODT framework comprises a four-step process designed to evaluate a product’s potential for obsolescence based on key design attributes and their impact on six types of obsolescence: technological, functional, psychological, economic, planned, and diminishing manufacturing sources and material shortages (DMSMS). The selection of the six specific obsolescence types evaluated within the MODT framework is theoretically supported by a comprehensive systematic review of product design literature12. This study analyzed the historical evolution of obsolescence classifications, ranging from absolute and relative categories17 to distinctions of voluntary and involuntary obsolescence (Amoah, 2017). The synthesis revealed that these six dimensions are consistently identified as the most prevalent and critical vectors compromising product longevity across various industrial sectors. Unlike traditional approaches, which assess obsolescence post-production or through market forecasts6,10,11, MODT enables proactive diagnosis during early-stage design, supporting strategic planning across engineering and manufacturing systems. MODT consists of four general steps: i) product characterization, ii) identification of design attributes, iii) obsolescence evaluation, and iv) consolidation of results. These steps provide a comprehensive assessment of product obsolescence, addressing technical aspects and user satisfaction through a component-product analysis. Methodology for implementing the MODT in a product.

The development of the MODT followed a Design Science Research (DSR) methodology. DSR is an established research paradigm in engineering design aimed at creating innovative, functional artifacts to solve practical problems38. In this study, the DSR process was structured into four main phases: (1) Problem Identification, which established the need for a multi-dimensional obsolescence assessment during early design stages to support circular economy goals,(2) Artifact Design and Development, which involved the conceptualization of the MODT framework, the selection of its six obsolescence dimensions, and the mathematical formulation of its evaluation matrices based on a comprehensive literature review; (3) Demonstration, where the tool is applied to a practical, mechatronic case study (an electromechanical impact drill) to illustrate its operational mechanics; and (4) Evaluation, where the feasibility, utility, and scalability of the proposed diagnostic methodology are assessed through the analysis of the case study results. The subsequent subsections detail the specific architecture and execution steps of the developed MODT approach.

Product characterization

This initial step involves collecting product-specific information, including user manuals, technical specifications, service guides, and warranty documentation. The physical product is disassembled to document all components, materials, joint types, disassembly complexity, and the use of specialized tools. For each component, the Relative Functional Importance (RFI) indicator39 is calculated using Eqs. (1) and (2), which consider connectivity and functional contribution. Components are also classified into four categories: structural, functional, user interface, and fastening/connection. Components used only for fixation are excluded from individual assessment. A second filtering step ranks components based on five binary criticality questions related to function, safety, performance, user interaction, and replacement cost. This classification enables designers to focus solely on high- and medium-priority components, thereby reducing evaluation complexity and facilitating targeted improvements.

This component classification scheme is based on recent advancements regarding circular product architecture and modular engineering, specifically utilizing frameworks that evaluate modularity and circularity trade-offs to rigorously isolate structural, functional, and connective elements36,37. This initial classification allows for the filtering out of components that do not require individual assessment, given their function in the product. In this case, components that belong to the fixing and connection category are discarded from the individual assessment, and those that relate directly to the user must be assessed. For each question, 1 point is assigned if the answer is “Yes” and 0 points if the answer is “No”. Based on these criteria, a priority can be assigned to each component based on its criticality, classifying the components as low, medium, and high. Components classified as “low” are discarded for individual evaluation. Based on the total score, high-priority (5–4 points) components are critical to the product, significantly affecting multiple key areas and playing a crucial role in its overall functionality and performance. Medium priority (3 points) components are necessary but not critical, influencing several key areas without affecting all of them. Low-priority (0–2 points) components have a minor impact and are not essential to the product’s overall functioning, with limited influence on its key areas or performance. This filter of components allows: i) to reduce the number of components, focusing the methodology on only critical components that impact the design attributes of the product, and ii) to simplify the evaluation process, making it more manageable and less prone to errors.

While the RFI and the five-question screening method efficiently prioritize components based on their structural and functional significance, this approach presents a potential limitation regarding DMSMS. Functionally low-priority components may still act as critical bottlenecks; if a minor, highly specific part is discontinued due to supply chain disruptions, it can lead to complete system unavailability or production halts. To prevent the underestimation of DMSMS risks, the MODT framework also incorporates a supplementary supply-risk override during the screening phase. Regardless of a component’s RFI score, if preliminary supply chain assessments flag the item as single-sourced, geographically restricted, or subject to high market volatility, it bypasses the screening exclusion and is automatically included in the comprehensive multi-dimensional evaluation.

Identification of design attributes

After characterizing the product, it is necessary to determine the general design attributes and parameters with which the product and components will be evaluated. The design attributes represent the combination of internal and external properties of a product, such as materials, reparability, among others. On the other hand, the parameters are the aspects that can be measured or with which each attribute can be analyzed. This methodology proposes a list of design attributes and general design parameters based on40.

Considering the product category being evaluated is crucial, as each category requires its design attributes and corresponding general parameters. Therefore, at this stage, it is necessary to identify which of the 22 design attributes from40 are relevant to the product being evaluated. Once the attributes have been selected, it is required to determine which components chosen in step 1 (subsection 3.1) contribute to each design attribute in the overall product. This attribute selection step is performed based on the criteria of the designer or the person using the methodology. Once this is done, each component is evaluated. Four sub-criteria per design attribute were defined to reduce subjectivity in the evaluation stage. These sub-criteria are based on the parameters from40. Each sub-criterion has an associated Yes/No/Neutral question, where a value of 1 point is assigned if the answer is “Yes”, 0.5 points if the answer is “Neutral”, and 0 points when the answer is “No”. Proposed questions to evaluate design attributes.

The categorization of the design attributes mentioned in this subsection is theoretically supported by contemporary DfX guidelines tailored for sustainable transitions and multi-level socio-technical systems, providing a validated basis for selecting parameters that accurately determine environmental impact and lifecycle vulnerabilities35. However, it is also possible to adjust the categorization if necessary, according to the design team criteria.

Obsolescence evaluation

After evaluating a product’s design attributes, the next step is determining their impact on the product. Each design attribute is affected differently depending on the product category. For example, in a washing machine, the durability of balancing weights is more critical than aesthetics41. In an electric drill, the power of the motor and the strength of the bit are more critical than the aesthetic design of the handle. Additionally, each design attribute has a different impact on obsolescence, which helps identify the type of obsolescence the product will likely face. Therefore, to determine this aspect, this methodology proposes six correlation matrices between design attributes and each type of obsolescence, as well as the impact of each attribute depending on the kind of obsolescence. Building upon the theoretical base established in the literature review, the six selected obsolescence types are operationalized within the MODT framework through specific evaluation matrices. Rather than treating obsolescence as a singular failure point, the framework structurally translates these six theoretical dimensions into quantifiable metrics by assessing how individual design attributes are susceptible to each specific obsolescence vector.

To ensure reliability and mitigate the subjectivity inherent in expert judgment, the scoring system for the design attributes follows a standardized protocol based on a previously developed and validated taxonomic framework40. This first study rigorously established the selection, hierarchization, and specific parameters of the design attributes used in the MODT framework. During the evaluation process, experts are provided with this standardized taxonomy, which includes specific guiding sub-criteria and defined rules for scoring each attribute. Since the assessment is based on these structured rules, the framework ensures that evaluators base their scores on empirical design characteristics rather than subjective perceptions.

Consolidation of results

In this final step, results are aggregated and interpreted. Components are ranked by their obsolescence potential, and dominant types of obsolescence are identified. Designers can then prioritize redesign actions or material substitutions. The method also highlights which design attributes, such as reparability, modularity, or support and maintenance, which drive vulnerability. This weighted aggregation method is validated by recent applications of Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis in sustainable waste management and lifecycle assessments, which demonstrate the necessity of integrating hierarchical functional weightings like the RFI to accurately resolve conflicting sustainability indicators42,43.

The framework’s component-level resolution allows manufacturers to identify failure-prone parts and plan interventions at the BOM or production planning level, improving alignment with manufacturing strategies15,44. Additionally, the tool enables predictive modeling, supports post-market services (e.g., spare part stocking), and can be scaled across multiple product categories and complexity levels.

Findings and discussion

The implementation of the MODT on a representative electromechanical product—the BOSCH GSB 13 RE impact drill—enabled a detailed evaluation of obsolescence risk from both design and manufacturing perspectives. The analysis yielded multi-level insights: at the product level (global obsolescence potential), component level (criticality and design vulnerability), and attribute level (design aspects that drive lifecycle weaknesses). These insights directly inform decisions across product development, manufacturing planning, and post-market support.

The diagnostic results indicate that the BOSCH drill exhibits a relatively low overall obsolescence potential, ranging from 19 to 34% depending on the type (see Table 9 and Fig. 5). This supports the claim that the product retains a significant degree of technical and functional durability, validating the robustness of its mechanical configuration. However, the most dominant vulnerabilities were linked to functional obsolescence (33.3%) and DMSMS (32.0%). These results highlight two critical and interdependent phenomena: i) Functional degradation due to lack of component support and poor repairability. And ii) Lifecycle disruption caused by obsolete or discontinued parts in the supply chain. From a manufacturing systems perspective, these findings raise concerns about system availability, service continuity, and warranty feasibility over time18,44. Functional and DMSMS obsolescence are particularly problematic in globalized production networks where after-sales service, part compatibility, and inventory control require proactive forecasting.

A core advantage of MODT is its ability to localize obsolescence risk to specific components. In this case, components such as the armature (C2), motor housing (C17), and field pole shoe (C1) were identified as having the highest Relative Functional Importance (RFI) and a moderate-to-high obsolescence potential. Though physically robust, these components are affected by limited modularity and repair access; non-standard part geometries that complicate substitution; and discontinued sourcing pathways, evidenced by the absence of replacement listings on manufacturer and distributor platforms. For example, the C17 motor housing presents a moderate DMSMS risk (31.8%) not because of poor material properties, but due to its integrated architecture, which makes disassembly and targeted replacement infeasible without affecting other assemblies. These insights directly support design-for-service (DfS) and design-for-disassembly (DfD) strategies, which are increasingly valued in complex electromechanical products39,45.

At the attribute level, the lowest scoring design features—specifically reparability (46%), support and maintenance (46%), and supply chain diversification (50%)—emerge as primary contributors to obsolescence. The combination of poor accessibility, lack of repair documentation, and limited supplier redundancy results in an avoidable increase in functional obsolescence. This finding has several implications: Reparability bottlenecks (e.g., glued or inaccessible parts) discourage professional or user-led maintenance; sparse after-sales support (e.g., unavailable manuals or exploded diagrams) raises the total cost of ownership; and geographic concentration of suppliers or reliance on legacy specifications increases DMSMS exposure. The MODT results enable engineering teams to prioritize design changes by quantifying which attributes degrade the most under current product conditions and which components are most affected. Moreover, the use of weighted correlation matrices ensures that such evaluations are product-type specific, rather than relying on generic checklists.

Specific contributions of the MODT approach

The insights generated by MODT stretch well beyond design diagnostics to inform lifecycle engineering and day-to-day manufacturing strategy. Because the tool quantifies how early design decisions shape serviceability, upgrade pathways, and spare-part longevity, it directly supports Design-for-Lifecycle (DfL) thinking and flags inventory or sourcing bottlenecks before they escalate. Engineers can pinpoint components whose impending obsolescence would jeopardize multi-product platform architectures or stall assembly lines, then integrate those risks into bill-of-materials reviews, warranty modeling, and service-level agreements. In multi-site production networks where a single discontinued part can trigger cascading costs, MODT offers a cross-functional language for procurement, production-planning, and after-sales teams to converge on proactive solutions—whether that means stocking strategic spares, qualifying alternate suppliers, or redesigning for modular substitution. By embedding its attribute–impact logic into CAD/PLM environments, MODT has the potential to deliver real-time diagnostics during digital prototyping, thereby shortening redesign loops and reinforcing manufacturability and maintainability targets that resonate with CIRP-JMST’s focus on integrated production science.

Equally important, MODT’s predictive mapping of design weaknesses—poor disassemblability, low modularity, or limited supplier diversification—provides a concrete pathway for circular-economy implementation. The tool highlights where products can be engineered for multiple life cycles, where component recovery and part harvesting are technically and economically viable, and how reverse-logistics flows should be prioritized to capture residual value. Although demonstrated on an electromechanical drill, the framework is intentionally product-agnostic: it can be scaled to consumer electronics, white goods, or automotive subsystems, each with distinct obsolescence profiles ranging from psychological to regulatory. Its structured scoring matrix means that, once parameterized, MODT can feed dashboards that give engineers or supply-chain managers near-instant feedback on design choices that threaten durability, compliance, or service profitability. Consequently, MODT advances the discourse from purely academic modeling to an industrial decision-support tool that harmonizes sustainability objectives with cost, risk, and performance across the full manufacturing life cycle.

The practical value of this diagnostic tool is further emphasized by the recent implementation of strict pro-circularity directives, particularly within the European Union. Regulatory frameworks such as the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation and the Right to Repair Act mandate that products be explicitly designed for durability, ease of maintenance, and extended operational lifecycles. Furthermore, initiatives like the Digital Product Passport require granular, component-level traceability, while Extended Producer Responsibility policies hold manufacturers increasingly accountable for end-of-life management and circular integration. In this regulatory context, tools like MODT offer a strategic advantage. The proposed methodology directly supports manufacturers in complying with these evolving legal requirements and transitioning their product architectures toward verifiable sustainability through proactively assessing risks related to reparability, diminishing manufacturing sources, and functional adaptability at the component level.

By bridging early-stage design with long-term lifecycle management, the MODT framework is strategically positioned within the ongoing industrial transition from Industry 4.0 to Industry 5.0. While Industry 4.0 emphasizes technological automation, data-driven efficiency, and smart manufacturing, Industry 5.0 reintroduces a critical focus on sustainability, human-centricity, and systemic resilience. The diagnostic approach of MODT inherently balances these paradigms. For instance, when evaluating a product’s modularity to mitigate functional and technological obsolescence, the framework not only accounts for the productivity gains associated with automated assembly and standardized supply chains (an Industry 4.0 priority) but also the ergonomic impact on human workers during repair and EOL disassembly (an Industry 5.0 priority). Well-designed modular architecture reduces the physical strain and cognitive load on maintenance technicians, directly supporting worker well-being while facilitating the efficient recovery of components. Therefore, by weighing human well-being and environmental impacts equally with technical obsolescence risks, the MODT framework serves as a robust methodology for designing products optimized for circular production.

Future works

To further enhance the scalability and real-time diagnostic capabilities of the MODT framework, future iterations should consider the integration of combined learning processes. In dynamic Industry 4.0 environments, predicting component obsolescence requires vast amounts of continuous data; however, manufacturers are often reluctant to centralize sensitive or proprietary supply chain information. Combined learning addresses this limitation by enabling decentralized data aggregation across multiple distributed manufacturing sites. Under this paradigm, localized models evaluate component histories and supply chain fluctuations on-site, sharing only the encrypted, updated risk weightings with the central MODT system rather than the raw data itself. This decentralized approach enables real-time updates to component obsolescence assessments, particularly DMSMS; fostering greater adaptability in rapidly changing supply chains. Based on the leverage of these privacy-preserving, data-driven insights, the methodology can evolve into a continuous learning system, providing highly resilient methodologies for designing products optimized for circular production.

Furthermore, the credibility and reliability of the diagnostics produced by the MODT framework heavily depend on the accuracy of the underlying component data. To address potential limitations in data integrity, the integration of blockchain technologies similar to frameworks such as Makerchain, offers a robust solution for future applications. By embedding blockchain-based immutable records into the product lifecycle, manufacturers can establish a transparent, decentralized history for every component. This enhanced traceability is essential not only for preventing the integration of counterfeit parts, which inherently carry high premature obsolescence and failure risks, but also for facilitating secure, trustless data sharing among diverse stakeholders across the value chain. Ultimately, ensuring that verified lifecycle information is attached by blockchain technology significantly strengthens MODT’s capability to support broader circular economy goals. It guarantees that strategic decisions regarding maintenance, component reuse, and EOL material recovery are based on reliable, tamper-proof data, thereby maximizing value retention and minimizing the overall environmental impact of the products.

Additionally, the framework’s capacity to evaluate subjective dimensions (particularly psychological and functional obsolescence) can be significantly enriched by incorporating human-centric Artificial Intelligence (AI) principles. Aligning with the emerging paradigm of Industry 5.0, which prioritizes human well-being and ergonomics alongside technological advancement, future iterations of MODT should integrate continuous user feedback mechanisms. By employing AI-driven emotion analysis on user interactions and systematically evaluating ergonomic factors, manufacturers can capture real-time shifts in consumer preferences and emerging usability challenges. This empirical data can then be used to dynamically refine the ‘Impact factor’ weightings of design attributes within the MODT matrices, replacing static expert judgments with adaptive, data-driven metrics. This human-centric approach ensures that the tool remains highly responsive to societal trends and evolving market demands. In the end, this integration raises the development of methodologies for designing products optimized for circular production that maintain both their functional relevance and psychological attraction over extended lifecycles.

While the current iteration of the MODT framework focuses on early-stage design evaluations, future research must bridge the gap between these foundational diagnostics and the operational realities of high-performance manufacturing systems. To fully realize the potential of methodologies for designing products optimized for circular production, static obsolescence metrics must eventually be linked to dynamic operational data. Through the integration of MODT’s component-level risk assessments with real-time factory floor metrics (such as production throughput, resource utilization, and system downtime) the framework can be adapted for high-performance environments. This operational integration would transform early-stage obsolescence risk scores into actionable inputs for dynamic predictive maintenance scheduling.

Furthermore, mitigating multi-dimensional obsolescence requires a deeper integration of the economic dimension of sustainability and the practical realities of component availability. Within the MODT framework, economic obsolescence extends beyond initial manufacturing and acquisition costs; it encompasses the total cost of ownership and the financial viability of circular strategies. When the cost of acquiring replacement modules or executing repairs approaches the cost of a new product, economic obsolescence is triggered, effectively neutralizing other sustainable design efforts. This economic viability is deeply linked to the availability of products, modules, and spare parts. A critical vulnerability in sustainable manufacturing is DMSMS. If supply chains for specific critical components are not maintained, or if a product lacks a modular architecture that allows for the integration of alternative parts, the entire system faces premature obsolescence. Therefore, designing standardized module interfaces and ensuring long-term parts availability are not merely supply chain concerns, but fundamental prerequisites for maintaining both the economic and operational sustainability of the product across its intended lifecycle.

Conclusion

This study presented the Multi-Obsolescence Diagnostic Tool (MODT). This design-integrated framework quantifies a product’s vulnerability to six distinct forms of obsolescence and maps those risks directly to component-level design attributes. Applying MODT to an electromechanical drill confirmed its overall durability (global obsolescence potential below 34 percent) yet exposed critical weaknesses in reparability, supply-chain diversification, and after-sales support that elevate functional and DMSMS risks. Because MODT delivers granular, weighted scores, it empowers engineering teams to target redesign resources where they yield the most significant lifecycle benefit, while simultaneously informing procurement, inventory, and service strategies that keep multi-product manufacturing systems resilient and cost-effective.

Beyond the case study, MODT advances manufacturing science in three ways. First, it operationalizes Design-for-Lifecycle principles by providing a repeatable metric that links early-stage decisions to downstream service, warranty, and spare parts demands. Second, it aligns obsolescence diagnostics with circular economy goals by highlighting design choices that hinder reuse, refurbishment, or component harvesting. Third, its rule-based architecture can be digitalized and embedded in CAD or PLM environments, enabling real-time feedback during virtual prototyping and shortening iterative loops between design and production engineering. Future research will extend validation across varied product categories such as consumer electronics, white goods, and automotive subsystems; and explore machine-learning extensions that refine impact weightings as field data accrue. Overall, MODT offers industry and academia a practical and scalable instrument to articulate durability, manufacturability, and sustainability across the entire product lifecycle.
