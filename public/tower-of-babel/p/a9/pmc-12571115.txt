Encephalomalacia from Physical Trauma in an Adult: A Case Report and Review of Literature
Authors: Aqsa Zoey Sorathia, Nitish M Khindri, Anusha Akhai, Katrina Villegas, Rouba Isshak, Patrick Michael
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEncephalomalacia is the loss of brain tissue caused by an insult to the cerebral matter. Encephalomalacia is not frequently described in literature, with most focusing on infants and children, and rarely on the adult population. When described in the adult population, encephalomalacia is most commonly described following a cerebrovascular event. Reports of trauma-induced encephalomalacia in adults are uncommon, making such cases clinically significant. Despite some identified etiology in infants and children, our understanding of adult risk factors besides a cerebrovascular event remains limited. The following report describes a case of encephalomalacia in a young adult resulting from an atypical predisposing etiology.Case PresentationOur case is of a twenty-eight-year-old male who presented with non-specific and abrupt symptoms of chest tightness, dyspnea, and aphasia after an apparent episode of alcohol intoxication. Further investigations and pertinent history-taking revealed a chronic history of similar symptoms traced back to a severe head trauma five years prior. The symptoms were ultimately attributed to left temporal lobe encephalomalacia, as evidenced by neuroimaging with CT and MRI. The patient received symptomatic treatment for possible alcohol withdrawal and was hospitalized until his symptoms improved. He was then discharged for continued outpatient follow-up care.ConclusionThis case report aims to identify the unknown cause of neurobehavioral deficits in adults and highlight physical trauma as the probable etiology of encephalomalacia in such patients. It underscores the potential for early identification through neuroimaging and appropriate symptomatic management, offering hope for mitigating the impact on the patient’s quality of life.

Introduction

Encephalomalacia is a gross pathological term derived from Greek etymology that describes the softening of the brain.1 It is characterized by loss of brain tissue due to liquefactive necrosis and degeneration. This chronic injury results from a cerebral event, most commonly ischemia.2,3 Encephalomalacia is not frequently described in literature, with most focusing on infants and children and rarely on the adult population.4,5 The following report describes a case of encephalomalacia in a young adult resulting from an atypical predisposing etiology.

Case Presentation

A 28-year-old male from a lower socio-economic background was brought to the emergency room with a sudden onset of dyspnea and chest tightness. He also had aphasia, which limited history-taking. The patient was brought in by family, who admitted that the patient was drinking alcohol at the roadside with unfamiliar individuals when his symptoms abruptly started. He displayed a paucity of speech, yet his words were incomprehensible. He repeatedly requested back thrusts to alleviate his symptoms. His family also reported the patient was displaying progressive psychomotor retardation and bradyphrenia after a physical assault five years prior. Despite sustaining reported significant head trauma, he did not seek any medical treatment for his injuries. Furthermore, the family did not pursue medical intervention for the patient’s apparent neurological compromise over the past five years. The patient’s family reported that his neuropsychiatric symptoms developed gradually. There was a slow but progressive decline in his functioning over several years following the initial trauma. This delay in seeking help was exacerbated by limited social and financial resources, which hindered timely medical evaluation and treatment. Care was only sought once his condition had deteriorated to a disabling degree. The family did not report any other known history of substance abuse, other traumatic events, underlying psychiatric illnesses, medication conditions, or seizure disorder.

On physical examination, he was afebrile, and his vitals were hemodynamically stable. He had a slender build and appeared unkempt and untidy, with disheveled hair. He had mild action tremors in his right hand and repeatedly brought his hand to his midline. He attempted to alleviate his chest discomfort using his left hand to strike his back. No abnormalities were noted in his left hand. He demonstrated no other neurological deficits. He appeared restless, and his answers to questions were irrelevant, incoherent, and not goal-directed. He had a normal affect, was otherwise cooperative, and was able to follow simple commands. Insight could not be assessed. Examination of the cardiopulmonary and gastrointestinal systems was unremarkable.

Upon investigation, his complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, and thyroid panel results were all within normal limits. The blood alcohol level was 236 mg/dL. Urine drug analysis did not uncover any illicit substance use. An electrocardiogram revealed a normal sinus rhythm with a heart rate of 91 beats per minute and the absence of any ST-segment changes. A chest x-ray did not reveal any abnormalities. Due to his presentation, a non-contrast CT scan of his head was ordered, which demonstrated no intracranial hemorrhage or evidence of an acute large territorial infarct; however, it revealed prominent encephalomalacia in the left anterior temporal lobe with mild encephalomalacia in the left inferior frontal lobe (shown in Figure 1). This was followed by a CT angiogram of the head and the neck and an MRI of the brain with contrast (shown in Figure 2) redemonstrating the previously noted left anterior temporal encephalomalacia, however, without major vessel occlusion or significant stenosis, infarction, or ischemic penumbra. An abnormal awake and drowsy electroencephalogram suggested focal cerebral dysfunction in the left hemisphere (shown in Figure 3). No epileptiform discharges or seizures were seen. A cardiopulmonary workup was unremarkable. The patient was acutely treated for alcohol intoxication with a benzodiazepine taper along with replenishment of vitamins and nutrients, which alleviated his restlessness and cardiopulmonary symptoms. An impression of post-traumatic encephalomalacia was made, contributing to his progressive neuro-psychomotor compromise. He was amenable to alcohol rehabilitation and was advised to return to the neurology clinic for continued surveillance; however, his care was lost to follow-up.

Discussion

While the patient’s admission was ultimately due to his acute alcohol intoxication, his hospitalization uncovered underlying encephalomalacia on imaging. Encephalomalacia can occur due to etiologies other than ischemic cerebrovascular accident. An intracerebral hemorrhage causes localized neurological damage and may lead to encephalomalacia. Severe inflammatory damage from infections such as meningitis or encephalitis can also lead to encephalomalacia. Additionally, traumatic events such as perinatal asphyxia in neonates can lead to severe neurological deficits, including encephalomalacia.4,6 In this case, the patient experienced a physical assault, leading to severe cerebrovascular trauma and the development of encephalomalacia, which explains the observed progressive neuro-psychomotor symptoms. While reports of post-traumatic encephalomalacia in adults have often been described in literature as incidental imaging findings or isolated neuropsychiatric features, this case illustrates a gradual, insidious neuropsychiatric decline, emphasizing the potential for delayed sequelae following head trauma. However, interpretation of this case may be limited by the patient’s lack of prior imaging and loss to follow-up, which restricts assessment of onset and prognosis. Comparable cases in adults have been described in the literature, though most present differently. For example, Harjpal and Qureshi (2023) describe incidental imaging findings of encephalomalacia in an adult with minimal neuropsychiatric symptoms, while Das and Yadav (2018) describe an adult presenting with psychosis in the setting of encephalomalacia.7,8 In contrast, our patient exhibited a progressive neuro-psychomotor compromise with aphasia and no seizures.

Given the patient’s history and presentation, alternative explanations were considered and ruled out. For example, metabolic encephalopathy was considered less likely due to the absence of laboratory abnormalities, its typical presentation with fluctuating consciousness, and diffuse EEG slowing rather than the presence of focal findings.9,10 Also, chronic alcohol-related encephalopathy, including Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome and alcohol-related dementia, more often presents with generalized cerebral atrophy and memory impairment, rather than the focal temporal and frontal lesions observed in our patient’s neuroimaging.11,12 Psychiatric disorders can also mimic the neuropsychiatric decline seen with encephalomalacia, but the presence of structural lesions on neuroimaging supports an organic cause.13,14

Pathologically, encephalomalacia is further macroscopically classified into three types based on the nature of the affected brain tissue. Red encephalomalacia is associated with hemorrhagic regions where blood has infiltrated necrotic tissue. White encephalomalacia, also known as leukoencephalomalacia, occurs in areas of white matter tissue affected by ischemic necrosis. Yellow encephalomalacia causes yellow discoloration due to prolonged degeneration seen in chronic cases.2,8,15

Encephalomalacia is best identified by neuroimaging, as illustrated by our patient. Magnetic resonance imaging is the most reliable method for detection, and in this case confirmed left anterior temporal and inferior frontal encephalomalacia. Electroencephalography showed focal cerebral dysfunction without epileptiform activity, supporting the presence of cognitive and language deficits in the absence of seizures. Affected areas appear as well-defined hypointense areas on T1-weighted images and hyperintense areas on T2-weighted images. In chronic cases, cystic changes or surrounding gliosis may occur.6,16 Although less sensitive, computed tomography can also detect encephalomalacia as hypodense areas, especially when the lesion is associated with calcification or significant structural changes.17

Patients with encephalomalacia may have varied clinical manifestations. The clinical significance will depend on the size and location of the lesion. Patients with small, localized areas might be asymptomatic, particularly in non-critical brain regions, and discovered incidentally by imaging. However, larger lesions or those occurring in critical areas, such as the motor cortex, basal ganglia, or brainstem, can lead to severe neurological compromise. These deficits may include motor and sensory impairments like hemiparesis or hemiplegia, psychiatric issues, cognitive and memory deficits if the frontal or temporal lobes are involved, seizures due to a lower seizure threshold, particularly with temporal lobe lesions, and hydrocephalus from disruption of normal cerebrospinal fluid flow.7,16,18–20

There is currently no treatment to reverse encephalomalacia, as tissue loss is permanent. Current management primarily involves managing symptoms and preventing further brain damage. Symptomatic management includes using anticonvulsants for seizures, physical therapy for motor deficits, and cognitive rehabilitation for cognitive impairments.7

Encephalomalacia can have significant long-term effects on the patient’s quality of life, affecting their ability to perform daily activities and engage in social interactions. Preventive measures, particularly in controlling underlying conditions like hypertension and infections, are vital in reducing the progression of encephalomalacia.3 There are many beneficial long-term strategies, including symptomatic management, to address neurological conditions. Neuropsychological evaluation and cognitive rehabilitation can help address deficits in memory, attention, and language.21 Psychiatric care can be used when mood disorders, anxiety, or psychosis emerge due to frontal or temporal involvement.7,22 Physical and occupational therapy can improve motor function, coordination, and independence in daily activities.23 Neurosurgical intervention can be used in select cases, such as for intractable epilepsy or ventriculoperitoneal shunting for post-traumatic hydrocephalus.6,19,20 As highlighted in a recent rehabilitation review, sustained multidisciplinary involvement in patient treatment is most effective. Outcomes improve when medical, psychological, and social supports are integrated and tailored to each unique patient’s long-term needs.24

Conclusion

Encephalomalacia is a significant neurological condition with various etiologies and pathological characteristics. Its clinical presentation and outcome largely depend on the extent and location of the lesion, with the potential for severe neurological deficits. While the condition itself is irreversible, managing symptoms, preventing further brain damage, and addressing potential complications are essential components of the management. In our case, neuroimaging and electroencephalography were crucial in linking structural findings to clinical presentation. Early identification through neuroimaging and appropriate symptomatic management can help mitigate its impact on the patient’s quality of life. Further clinical research with enhanced awareness is necessary to improve the prognosis and outlook for individuals affected by this condition, particularly in underserved populations where systemic and cultural barriers often delay diagnosis and appropriate care.
