Translocation of Foliar‐Applied Nanoparticles: A Critical Review From a Plant Science Perspective
Authors: Andrea Pinna, Søren Husted
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent advances in plant science have greatly enhanced agronomic practices involving the foliar application of agrochemicals such as fertilizers and pesticides. However, the limited phloem mobility of certain nutrients and nonsystemic pesticides reduces the effectiveness of these strategies. Nanoparticles (NPs) have emerged as promising carriers to improve nutrient use efficiency (NUE) and crop protection by enabling long‐distance transport and targeted delivery of essential nutrients and active ingredients. While increasing evidence suggests that foliar‐applied NPs can translocate within plants, a mechanistic understanding and agronomically relevant case studies remain scarce. As a result, the extent to which NP translocation can facilitate significant distribution and cargo release within plants is yet to be fully established. This review critically evaluates existing research on foliar NP translocation, emphasizing key findings within a plant science framework. Specifically, we examine how NP design can influence translocation and assess the existing quantitative data of NP remobilization within plants. Additionally, we explore how physiological processes affect NP transport and highlight alternative, often overlooked translocation pathways. Lastly, we assess current techniques used to study NP transport, discussing their applicability and limitations. This review identifies significant research gaps that must be addressed to advance nano‐enabled plant nutrition as well as crop protection and can therefore be used to inspire future research.Keywords: foliar nanoparticles, nanofertilizers, nanopesticides, phloem, translocation pathways

Introduction

Recent findings in plant science have contributed to advancing the agronomical practices and strategies related to foliar application of agrochemicals. For example, increased attention to the formulation properties and optimal application timing of foliar solutions, combined with a better understanding of the interactions of water and solutes with plant surfaces, have led to greater efficacy of foliar‐applied fertilizers and pesticides (Arsic et al. 2022; Bao et al. 2022; Fernández et al. 2021; Schmidt et al. 2013). However, despite being valuable, these advancements cannot be used to fundamentally change the inherent properties of some mineral nutrients and pesticide molecules that restrict their mobility in plants. Indeed, foliar fertilization practices for phloem immobile nutrients such as calcium, manganese, and boron, and crop protection strategies employing nonsystemic pesticides still suffer from poor efficiency (Huang et al. 2024; Husted et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2024). Hence, there is an acute need for more transformative approaches to promote the redistribution of foliar‐applied agrochemicals with limited mobility within plants.

Engineered nanomaterials have been proposed as novel carriers for nutrients and active ingredients to improve NUE and crop protection (Kah et al. 2019). Owing to their size, nanoparticles (NPs) can penetrate the leaf surface and access both the apoplast and the symplast, promoting long‐distance transport and delivery of NP payloads to unexposed plant parts (Husted et al. 2023). Furthermore, their physicochemical properties can be tuned to allow for improved leaf uptake and translocation, and more advanced functionalities can be introduced to enable stimulus‐responsive release of cargo and targeted delivery (Husted et al. 2023). Thus, harnessing the ability of NPs to translocate would enable more cost‐effective and sustainable practices by enhancing the bioavailability of NP cargoes and extending their efficacy over time, while reducing agricultural input rates and the associated risks such as loss and accumulation into the environment (Husted et al. 2023; Kah et al. 2019).

In recent years, several papers have reported the translocation of engineered nanomaterials in plants (Avellan et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2023; Jeon et al. 2024; Liang et al. 2021; Raliya et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023). These studies relate to different scientific fields, including environmental and agricultural sciences, where NPs with various morphologies and compositions were detected in unexposed organs upon foliar application. A broad range of NPs was used to study the fundamentals of NP uptake and translocation in plants or to showcase their potential as carriers for the transport of pesticides and mineral nutrients. However, in the latter case, experiments with plants were frequently conducted, for example, in the absence of a specific pathogen or without documenting NPs' ability to deliver plant essential mineral ions to restore or prevent nutrient deficiencies (Cohen et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2021, 2023; Jeon et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). Furthermore, the used NPs often contained elements nonrelevant to plant physiology and metals that are potentially toxic to plants (Jeon et al. 2024; Raliya et al. 2015). The current literature also contains several examples where the beneficial effects of foliar NP exposure on plants' growth and protection are largely overstated, and the stimulatory effects on plant metabolism appear merely observational and not supported by strong functional and mechanistic insights (Cao et al. 2021; Husted et al. 2024; Karny et al. 2018; Meier et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2021). Thus, the real benefits generated by the translocation of foliar‐applied NPs on plant growth and production have yet to be clearly demonstrated.

At the mechanistic level, studying the translocation of foliar‐applied NPs in plants has proven difficult. Most often, elemental detection of NP traces in unexposed tissues or organs has been attributed to NP presence as a result of NP translocation via the phloem (Avellan et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2021; Jeon et al. 2024; Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2021, 2023). Sometimes, qualitative evidence originating from fluorescent NP tags or elemental imaging techniques was used to back up these findings (Avellan et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2023; Jeon et al. 2024, 20; Zhang et al. 2020). In general, the poor implementation of complementary approaches and imaging techniques to investigate NP translocation leaves only a few examples providing convincing indications of NPs entering the phloem and truly translocating as intact particles, thus representing a major research gap. As described by Husted et al. (2023), to enter the phloem, NPs must cross restrictive plant cell barriers such as the cell wall and plasma membrane, possibly moving through plasmodesmata, before passing the pore‐plasmodesmata units which connect the companion cells to the sieve elements. Especially, the plasmodesmata and the branched pore‐plasmodesmata units, with their size exclusion limit of only a few nanometers, represent the two major bottlenecks to NP loading into the phloem. NP loading into the phloem represents a real conundrum in phytonanotechnology as no one has ever provided direct evidence of intact NPs in the companion cells and sieve elements, and not least of NPs translocating across relevant phloem cell structures such as the plasmodesmata and pore‐plasmodesmata units.

This review aims to highlight some key aspects related to the translocation of foliar‐applied NPs and discuss them from a plant science perspective, focusing beyond the NP transport pathways in the leaf and the phloem anatomical barriers, as these have already been covered elsewhere (Husted et al. 2023). Furthermore, the techniques used to study the transport of foliar‐applied NPs within plants are presented here, highlighting the respective strengths and limitations of each method. Given the large number of publications spanning various research areas, a limited number of papers have been selected for discussion based on two main criteria. Through the first criterion—mechanistic understanding—we identified papers providing fundamental knowledge of processes regulating phloem loading and translocation of NPs in plants, particularly those offering robust data obtained employing a combination of advanced analytical tools and imaging techniques. Studies employing model NPs such as gold (Au) NPs and quantum dots (QDs) were also included in this selection. The second criterion—relevance to the agricultural context—was applied to identify studies using NPs containing essential plant nutrients or active ingredients for plant protection. The selected studies were conducted on agronomically relevant plants, simulated agricultural conditions (e.g., nutrient deficiency and pest exposure) and appropriate NP doses. Publications involving unrealistic NP application methods, such as infiltration, were instead excluded from the selection. With these strict criteria, 15 papers were found suitable and are discussed in the sections hereafter.

Not All Plants Are the Same: Differences in NP Translocation Efficiency

In the publications selected for this review, NP translocation was studied across 8 plant species ranging from cereals to fruit trees. Interestingly, most experiments were conducted on tomato (8), followed by wheat (3). Other species included rice (1), pepper (1), canola (1), banana (1), broad bean (1), and citrus (1). Contrasting anatomical and physiological features across such different crops are expected to generate significant differences in NP leaf uptake and transport within the plant. However, comparative studies assessing the impact of plant species on NP translocation are largely missing. To date, only one study has examined and compared the translocation and distribution of NPs in monocot and dicot plant species. Zhang et al. (2023) studied how the charge and aspect ratio of gadolinium (Gd)‐doped, bottlebrush‐shaped polymeric NPs affected leaf uptake and translocation in tomato and wheat. The total transport of NPs away from the exposed leaf, measured as Gd3+ content in unexposed organs, was ∼3% to 9% in monocot wheat plants and 8 to 25% in dicot tomato plants, compared to ∼1% when ionic Gd form was applied.

Leaf anatomical features that limit or favor NP loading into the phloem may justify the variability observed across these two species. Plant leaf anatomy can also affect NP uptake and thereby the fraction of NPs available for further transport. Differences in leaf uptake efficiency have been documented for carbon dots (CDs) in maize (monocot) and cotton (dicot) (Hu et al. 2020). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) analysis revealed that 2 to 6 nm CDs coated with different ligands are taken up via both the cuticular and the stomatal pathway in cotton leaves, but only through the stomata in maize leaves. It has been hypothesized that the larger porosity of the cuticle and higher stomatal density in cotton compared to maize leaves can provide better access for the entry of NPs into the leaf (Hu et al. 2020). Reasonably, greater leaf uptake efficiency may indirectly favor translocation, as a larger number of the foliar‐applied NPs can potentially diffuse toward the vasculature, increasing the likelihood of being loaded into the phloem.

Navigating Plant Cell Barriers: Role of NP Physicochemical Properties

Except for endocytosis, plants do not possess native mechanisms that allow them to discriminate and actively take up foreign material such as NPs. Indeed, plant‐NP interactions have proven to be “passive,” largely determined by NP physicochemical properties and dependent on the plant physiological pathways. NP features such as size, surface charge, aspect ratio, and hydrophobicity, and their influence on NP interaction with plant surfaces and fate within the plant has been previously investigated (Avellan et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2021; Jeon et al. 2024; Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2021, 2023).

Size

NPs seemingly larger than the estimated size exclusion limit of the cell wall, plasmodesmata and pore‐plasmodesmata units have been reported to translocate. For example, Avellan et al. (2019) documented the translocation of foliar‐applied PVP‐ and citrate‐coated 50‐nm spherical Au NPs to young shoots and roots in wheat. Similarly, 70‐nm mesoporous silica‐coated zinc (Zn) oxide NPs (ZnO@MSN) have been detected in different parts of tomato plants, including upper leaves and roots (Gao et al. 2023). Even 135‐nm Gd‐spiked core‐shell NPs were found translocating to different organs in tomato plants (Ristroph et al. 2023). Thus far, no studies have directly observed the translocation of NPs through plasmodesmata and pore‐plasmodesmata units, and it remains unclear how this process occurs. As we hypothesized in Husted et al. (2023), NPs may indirectly promote the enlargement of these structures by, for example, altering the redox state of the surrounding cellular environment or inducing osmotic or oxidative stress.

The size of NPs can also affect their distribution in plants following phloem loading. Zhang et al. (2021) have shown that 5‐nm star‐shaped polymer NPs translocated preferentially to younger leaves, compared to larger 35‐nm NPs which concentrated mostly in the roots of tomato plants. While studying the distribution pattern of foliar‐applied 3, 10, and 50‐nm Au NPs in wheat plants, Avellan et al. (2019) observed similar results. A greater fraction of the bigger NPs (10 and 50 nm) was translocated to the roots compared to the smallest size and the ionic Au, with the largest 50‐nm NPs having the highest degree of root localization (Avellan et al. 2019). Au speciation analysis using the X‐ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) technique indicated that 100% of the Au found in the roots was metallic, suggesting long‐distance transport of intact Au NPs. It is not clear what drives NP partitioning across different plant organs, as a combination of size, surface charge, and charge density may influence it (Avellan et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2021). Nevertheless, regarding size, it has been postulated that the transport to younger leaves may be more favorable for smaller NPs, owing to their higher mobility across the symplast which is required for the phloem unloading in the leaf tissues (Zhang et al. 2021).

Surface Charge

To date, only three studies systematically investigated the effect of surface charge on NP transport, providing contrasting results (Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2021, 2023). In tomato, highly charged star polymers appeared to be less mobile compared to analogous NPs with lower charge density (Zhang et al. 2021). Conversely, spherical core‐shell NPs with high surface charge exhibited higher translocation compared to neutral NPs (−10 mV < ζ potential < +10 mV) and even ionic Gd3+, in tomato plants (Ristroph et al. 2023).

Besides the magnitude of the surface charge, also the sign (i.e., + or ‐) can influence NP mobility in plants. Zhang et al. (2023) have shown that negatively charged bottlebrush polymeric NPs are translocated more efficiently compared to their cationic counterparts in both tomato and wheat plants (Ristroph et al. 2023).

Assessing the effects of surface charge on translocation is far from trivial as sign, magnitude, and functional groups present on the surface of NPs can collectively impact their interactions with biological interfaces and ultimately determine their fate (Nel et al. 2009). For example, it has been shown that highly charged (ζ < −30 mV or ζ > +30 mV) NPs have a strong affinity toward, for example, the lipid component of biological membranes, promoting cellular or organelle uptake and thereby resulting in sequestration and/or mobility reduction of NPs (Lew et al. 2018). Positively charged NPs instead are likely to interact strongly with the negatively charged pectins of the cell wall (Hu et al. 2020). Further complicating the matter, NPs can bind to macromolecules such as organic acids, proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids while moving through the different leaf compartments (i.e., leaf apoplast, cell symplast, and phloem), altering their original surface properties and consequently their mobility inside the plant (Avellan et al. 2019; Nel et al. 2009; Zhang et al. 2021).

Aspect Ratio

The literature provides examples of spherical, elongated, and octahedral NPs found in nonexposed tissue after leaf application (Avellan et al. 2019; Liang et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023). However, only recently has the role of aspect ratio in plant‐NP interaction started to be systematically investigated, and thereby, little information is available (Zhang et al. 2023). Zhang et al. (2023) have studied how the aspect ratio affects the phloem translocation and distribution of polymeric NPs in tomato and wheat plants. In wheat, the phloem size exclusion limit appeared to be smaller than in tomato, as high aspect ratio materials (i.e., 100 nm length and 10 nm diameter) were either poorly or not translocated within the plant, depending on their surface charge, resulting in NP accumulation in the exposed leaf. Conversely, the aspect ratio of the NPs did not influence the total mass of translocated polymer in tomato plants, as low, medium, and high aspect ratio NPs were equally translocated, potentially suggesting less limiting conditions for NP transport in dicots.

NP Surface Functionality and Polarity

NPs can be endowed with new functionalities by coating their surface with polymers, organic ligands, peptides, mineral shells, and even plant‐derived molecules such as sucrose, pectin, and cellulose. Modifications of the NP surface properties allow for improved leaf penetration, assimilation of the NP cargo, and transport across the plant. For example, encapsulation of ZnO NPs in a silicon oxide shell improved the translocation efficiency of ZnO@MSN compared to pristine ZnO NPs and enabled slow nutrient release in tomato plants (Gao et al. 2021). Recent studies have indicated that by introducing sugar moieties on the NP surface, it seems possible to target the phloem tissue specifically. Jeon et al. (2024) demonstrated that coating cadmium‐tellurium (Cd‐Te) quantum dots (QDs) and CDs with sucrose molecules enabled rapid phloem loading and enhanced long‐distance transport in wheat plants. Other authors grafted materials such as cellulose derivatives and pectins onto NPs to increase their affinity toward plant cell structures. Ristroph et al. (2023) revealed 32% translocation for foliar‐applied NPs coated with a semisynthetic cellulosic polymer, exhibiting the highest translocation efficiency among the NPs utilized in the study. Finally, Yang et al. (2024) reported efficient systemic translocation of pectin‐coated iron oxide NPs following leaf immersion into the NP solution in rice plants. Owing to their biocompatibility and wide availability, as well as the opportunity for functional modifications and loading capacity, biomolecules and polymers such as sucrose, pectin, and cellulose are exceptional candidates to enhance NP mobility and translocation inside the plant. However, a clear fundamental understanding of how various coated NPs interact with the phloem tissue, given that their size is far too big to allow for translocation via specific transport proteins, and whether plant‐like ligands such as cellulose and pectin can facilitate NP transport via some hitherto unknown mechanism, constitutes an important research gap that must be resolved to advance research within this area.

The degree of hydrophobicity or hydrophilicity of the moieties displayed on the NP surface can influence the interactions of NPs with the leaf cuticle, and affect their uptake pathways and subsequent movement inside the leaf (Avellan et al. 2019; Cohen et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021). Amphiphilic PVP‐coated Au NPs have shown greater leaf uptake in wheat plants compared to their hydrophilic citrate‐coated counterparts, likely due to higher affinity toward the hydrophobic leaf cuticle, but less mobility inside the mesophyll, suggesting different reactivity of the NP coatings toward cell structures and organic molecules (Avellan et al. 2019). Conversely, citrate‐coated Au NPs were less readily taken up by the leaf but showed increased transport to the vasculature and overall translocation. Despite the differences in cuticle adhesion, uptake, and transport inside the mesophyll, PVP and citrate‐coated NPs that translocated were similarly distributed in the different plant organs. It was hypothesized that the organic corona forming around the NP surface during the transport toward the vasculature can neutralize the differences between the coatings chemistries (Avellan et al. 2019). Comparably, Zhang et al. (2021) observed that hydrophilic star polymer NPs were taken up less efficiently than the hydrophobic ones when applied without surfactant. However, by including Silwet L‐77 in the formulation, both types of NPs were taken up and translocated similarly regardless of their hydrophobicities (Zhang et al. 2021).

Going With the Flow: Plant Mechanisms Responsible for NP Translocation

The plant phloem serves the bidirectional transport of photosynthates, proteins, and amino acids from mature leaves (sources) to actively growing organs (sinks) such as newly formed leaves, fruits, and roots (De Schepper et al. 2013). The translocation of phloem‐mobile nutrients such as phosphorous and nitrogen also occurs through this pathway (De Bang et al. 2021). Fully expanded leaves are typically net sugar exporters, whereas sink leaves/organs are net sugar importers. The demand for photosynthates in sink tissues, utilized for either active growth or storage, is the main driver of phloem sap flow (Babst et al. 2022; De Schepper et al. 2013). Along the sieve elements, the longitudinal transport is achieved by mass flow owing to differences in osmotic pressure at either end of the phloem conduits, with a high sugar concentration at the source and a lower concentration at the sink (De Schepper et al. 2013).

Mobile NPs that manage to access the phloem are likely subjected to the same source‐sink forces that control photosynthate distribution within the plant (Avellan et al. 2019; Jeon et al. 2024). In fact, NPs are commonly found in, for example, newly emerging leaves and roots where the demand for photosynthates to support growth is strong (Avellan et al. 2019; Jeon et al. 2024). However, NPs have also been found in older leaves, which is puzzling as they are typically not considered sinks as they do not rely on importing photosynthates for their expansion, and on the contrary, they start translocating material toward actively growing organs (Avellan et al. 2019; Turgeon 2006; Zhang et al. 2021, 2023). It has been hypothesized that NPs could move from phloem to xylem and then translocate via the xylem to older leaves (Avellan et al. 2019, 2021; Zhang et al. 2021, 2023). This process would require NP basipetal movement along the stem toward the roots via the phloem, followed by phloem unloading and subsequent NP entry into the transpiration stream in the xylem, allowing upward translocation. The exchange of solutes between phloem and xylem has been observed for water and small molecules such as sugars and amino acids, but whether this could support the exchange of larger molecules such as proteins or even NPs has yet to be proved (De Schepper et al. 2013; Tegeder and Hammes 2018; Van Bel 1990). Recent experiments have shown that phloem transport may not be exclusively driven by sucrose consumption at the terminal sink, but that other osmolytes such as K+ can be loaded into the phloem to maintain the osmotic pressure required to drive the phloem sap flow (Babst et al. 2022). This would ensure that, for example, amino acids and signaling molecules are being delivered to organs irrespective of their demand for photosynthesis products, preserving a path of communication and coordination between, for example, younger and older leaves (Babst et al. 2022). Conceptually, the presence of a phloem flow directed to nonsink organs such as older leaves, mediated by ionic osmolytes such as K+, may explain why NPs can be found in those tissues.

Being specialized at mobilizing sucrose within the plant, the phloem contains a set of transporters designated for its loading and unloading operations (C. Zhang and Turgeon 2018). The SUT/SUC transporter family is responsible for the active loading of sucrose molecules in the phloem against the concentration gradient (Bavnhøj et al. 2023). In a recent study, Jeon et al. (2024) attempted to target the phloem transporters by functionalizing QDs and CDs with sucrose molecules, observing greater redistribution of the sucrose‐coated NPs compared to their naked and glucose‐coated counterparts. It was speculated that the sucrose moieties might promote NP interaction with the transporters, but also that NP translocation is ultimately achieved via transient disruption of the plasma membrane lipid bilayer, supposedly at the companion cell level. Despite the promising experimental data, the role of the NP sucrose coating appears to be largely overstated as no evidence of interaction between NPs and sucrose transporters was provided. Thus, further research is required to characterize the molecular mechanisms responsible for increased phloem loading of sucrose‐coated NPs and determine whether this approach would provide a viable solution for promoting systemic transport of NPs within the plant.

Despite being accessible to NPs, the xylem is typically disregarded as responsible for the translocation of foliar‐applied NPs to, for example, upper leaves as this would be against the tension gradient established in a transpiring plant (Gao et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2025). Yet, positive xylem pressure in the aerial plant parts commonly occurs, driving water flow into the xylem conduits (Schenk et al. 2021). Such a process serves different functions such as rehydration and maintenance of hydraulic functionality of tissues in trees and even in herbaceous plants, where diurnal positive xylem pressure has been observed in many species (Schenk et al. 2021). Whether this phenomenon is relevant to the long‐distance transport of foliar NPs is currently unknown. Nevertheless, it is possible that following xylem loading, NPs could passively move with water into the phloem. While this route for foliar NP translocation remains speculative, it is not beyond the realms of possibility, and it might help explain some of the remarkable results obtained on NP translocation out of the foliar application zone to roots and emerging leaves.

Timing for Studying NP Translocation

The timing for studying the translocation of foliar‐applied NPs varied widely in the analyzed studies, ranging from less than 1 h to up to 52 days (Jeon et al. 2024; Raliya et al. 2015). However, in most cases, analyses were carried out between 2 and 7 days after NP application (Avellan et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2021, 2023; Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). Early signs of NPs translocation have been reported already 10 min after foliar NP application by Jeon et al. (2024), who observed in real time 5‐nm sucrose‐QDs moving along the leaf vasculature in wheat seedlings using an epifluorescence microscope. This suggests that NPs move comparably to solutes in the phloem, translocating at a velocity between 1 and 2.5 cm min−1 depending on the plant species and developmental stage (Babst et al. 2022; Savage et al. 2013; Windt et al. 2006). As a result, following loading into the phloem, NPs may be translocated relatively fast from, for example, exposed leaves to roots.

Previous experiments have shown that NP translocation processes may take some time to fully unfold. Higher translocation was reported for 70 nm ZnO@MSNs 5 days after application on tomato leaves, compared to 2 days after NP dosing (Gao et al. 2023). It is important to note that increased caution should be exercised when interpreting translocation data obtained days after foliar application of NPs, as these are more likely to undergo extensive processes of biotransformation resulting in dissolution, NP decoupling from its cargo/tracer, and reaggregation of cargo/tracer with naturally occurring macromolecules (Husted et al. 2023; Nel et al. 2009).

Although phloem translocation of NPs may occur rapidly, foliar‐applied NPs must cross the leaf cuticle and find their way through the leaf mesophyll before reaching the phloem tissue. Thus, the time frame for studying NP translocation is likely to vary according to the specific physicochemical properties of NPs influencing their ability to cross the leaf barriers and mobility inside the leaf, as well as the convective water movements caused by transpiration and phloem loading/unloading processes that NPs may follow.

One‐Size‐Fits‐All Solution? Techniques for Studying NP Translocation

Investigating NP translocation is a complex task as it is a dynamic process occurring at different scales in space and time. To cover the fundamental aspects related to the phloem translocation of foliar‐applied NPs, involving short‐ and long‐distance transport, complementary bioimaging and analytical techniques need to be used (Table 1). Indeed, it is paramount to combine the strengths of different approaches to detect and validate NP presence at the tissue and cellular levels, and ultimately obtain a comprehensive model illustrating how NPs are taken up by leaves and subsequently redistributed across the plant.

Various approaches were utilized to study the translocation of foliar‐applied NPs. Most often, the detection of NP constituents in unexposed plant compartments such as young shoots, stems, and roots was considered a reliable marker for NP presence in the tissue. Inductively coupled plasma (ICP)‐based methods were used for the detection of NP inorganic elements in tissue analysis, whereas organic NP cargoes such as pesticides were detected using liquid chromatography (LC) techniques (Avellan et al. 2019, 201; Gao et al. 2021; Liang et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023). In some cases, bioimaging methods were also employed, either in combination with analytical techniques or as standalone methodologies to monitor NP redistribution within plants.

Simple requirements for sample preparation and wide accessibility make fluorescence imaging techniques the principal methods for studying NP uptake and short‐distance transport within the leaf (Cohen et al. 2021; Jeon et al. 2024; Ristroph et al. 2023). For example, CLSM allowed the visualization of fluorescent sucrose‐coated QDs in the leaf vasculature following NP application, whereas conventional fluorescence microscopy has been used to detect the presence of CD‐tagged polymeric NPs in the petioles of pepper plants (Cohen et al. 2021; Jeon et al. 2024). Except for QDs and CDs that possess intrinsic fluorescent properties, NPs require fluorescent tags to be visualized in fluorescence imaging applications. This represents one of the major limitations of these techniques as fluorophores may change the physicochemical properties of the NP, detach from the NP surface or diffuse as free molecules following NP dissolution, leading to false interpretations of the fluorescence patterns in plant tissues (Husted et al. 2023). To overcome this limitation, dark‐field hyperspectral imaging (DF‐HSI) is increasingly being utilized in phytonanotechnology as it allows for label‐free detection of NPs with cellular resolution (Fakhrullin et al. 2021). Similarly to CLSM, DF‐HSI has been employed to map the leaf uptake pathways and study the mesophyll distribution of foliar‐applied NPs (Avellan et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). However, the issue for both CLSM and DF‐HSI is that they do not allow resolving single NPs but clusters, and that their imaging depth is limited to the superficial layer of the sample (Chazot et al. 2020; Dumur et al. 2019). Scattering and noise from the sample are common problems that restrict their use to study processes occurring a few hundred microns below the leaf surface. Therefore, leaf cross‐section samples need to be prepared in case, for example, inspection of the vascular bundle is desired. Examples of this are provided by Etxeberria et al. (2016) and Zhang et al. (2023) who observed different NPs in the phloem tissue of exposed leaves near the NP application site using CLSM and DF‐HSI, respectively.

Elemental imaging techniques are powerful tools often used in combination with light microscopy approaches, providing high‐resolution elemental maps at the tissue and cellular level (Avellan et al. 2019; Jeon et al. 2024). For instance, Avellan et al. (2019) observed Au translocation along the vascular bundles in wheat plants upon 10 nm Au NPs leaf application using micro X‐ray fluorescence (μ‐XRF), suggesting NP translocation of NPs via the phloem. However, xylem and phloem tissues were not identifiable as fresh leaves were imaged while being attached to the plant. Employing μ‐XRF, Jeon et al. (2024) visualized the core elements of Cd‐Te QDs in the vascular tissue of wheat leaf cross‐sections. Gao et al. (2023) instead integrated laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA‐ICP‐MS) and time‐of‐flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF‐SIMS) techniques to localize Zn and Si in the phloem of tomato petioles upon foliar application of Zn@MSN, respectively. The authors provided experimental evidence suggesting that TOF‐SIMS could be used to validate the presence of intact MSN in the phloem tissue as cumulative Si signals were produced following repeated sputtering cycles using a gas beam. While enabling high‐resolution elemental mapping in plant tissues, these techniques alone can rarely be used for discriminating intact NPs and dissolved ions. Therefore, to strengthen the obtained findings, complementary techniques should be used and supplemental experiments need to be conducted to compare how NP analogue ions distribute in the tissue of interest.

Owing to its unparalleled resolving power, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) can be used to localize and validate NP existence at the organelle level. Among the papers we screened, only once TEM was used to study the translocation of foliar‐applied NPs. Raliya et al. (2015) reported the presence of iron oxide NPs in the stem of tomato plants. However, no energy‐dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis was performed on the detected material. Hyphenated TEM‐EDX is required to validate NP identity in plant tissues. Indeed, metallic aggregates generated during sample preparation or electron‐dense organelles such as ribosomes can be easily confused with NPs (Ayache et al. 2010; Husted et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2025). Even the validation by EDX analysis is not free from pitfalls as evidence shows that, for example, dissolved ions taken up by plant roots can precipitate into nanoscale aggregates inside plant tissues (Tiwari et al. 2016). This could be valid even for foliar‐applied NPs prone to dissolve, as resulting mineral ions can enter the alkaline and ligand‐rich phloem sap and reprecipitate forming small aggregates (Husted et al. 2023). Thus, morphological and elemental data should be combined to provide unequivocal proof of NP presence within plant tissue.

Alternative workflows integrating different imaging modalities, such as correlative light‐electron microscopy (CLEM), are also gaining ground in bionanotechnology as they allow capturing complementary information from the same sample and can thereby be used to localize NPs and validate their presence within biological tissues (Bischof et al. 2024; Pope et al. 2023). Furthermore, recent advances show that enhanced localization accuracy of fluorescent targets in cells can be achieved by employing super‐resolution microscopy techniques, in correlative workflows or alone (Chen et al. 2019; Franek et al. 2024). While there are no current examples of the implementation of these methods to track NPs in plant tissue, plant nanotechnology is likely to benefit significantly from these technological advancements in the future.

Most of the abovementioned techniques such as TEM, TOF‐SIMS, μ‐XRF, and LA‐ICP‐MS involve tedious and extensive sample preparation procedures to obtain the thin (μm‐thick) or ultrathin (nm‐thick) sections required for the analysis. Although more versatile, CLSM and DF‐HSI are characterized by relatively low sensitivity and do not allow resolving single NPs but only NP clusters. Consequently, these methods are better suited for studying processes occurring in the proximity of the NP deposition area, such as NP loading into the phloem or NP movement along leaf veins and petioles, where NPs are likely to be found in higher concentrations.

Long‐Distance Transport of NPs: How to Look for a Needle in a Haystack

Once NPs are loaded into the phloem, they can be diluted across the whole plant while being transported to distant compartments such as the roots or younger shoots (Figure 1B). Therefore, methodologies involving the sectioning of unexposed organs areimpracticall for studying long‐distance transport of NPs, as they may be found only in small amounts within those tissues. In this regard, analytical techniques such as ICP‐MS and LC represent viable options for detecting NP elements or cargoes in unexposed plant compartments.

In the publications we assessed, ICP‐MS was by far the most used technique for studying the long‐distance transport of foliar‐applied inorganic NPs. Avellan et al. (2019) documented greater translocation and different plant distributions for Au NPs in comparison to an ionic Au solution in wheat plants. Jeon et al. (2024) instead reported higher translocation for sucrose‐QDs compared to uncoated QDs based on increased Cd content in unexposed plant parts. Detection of the core elements of inert NPs such as Au NPs and Cd‐Te QDs in unexposed organs by ICP‐MS is commonly attributed to NP presence within those tissues owing to their relative insolubility and the absence of elemental background in plants grown in controlled conditions. Conversely, organic NPs or NPs whose elements are naturally present in plants such as hydroxyapatite NPs are typically spiked with tracer elements (Ristroph et al. 2023; Szameitat et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). Metals such as Ce, V, and Gd have a very low background in plants and can therefore be incorporated into the structure of NPs to evaluate their uptake, transport, and release in plant tissues (Ristroph et al. 2023; Szameitat et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). For example, authors have utilized Gd as a marker for studying the uptake and distribution of polymeric NPs in wheat and tomato plants (Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). It is key to highlight that when tracer‐spiked NPs are used, there are risks of detecting false positives as NP and cargo/tracer may decouple following, for example, NP dissolution, and subsequently move as separate entities within the plant. Therefore, when assessing NP element distribution in plant organs, a comparison should be made with plants treated with an equivalent ionic formulation in the same experimental settings (Avellan et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). Furthermore, phloem immobile elements should be preferred for spiking NPs as their detection in unexposed plant parts would represent a strong indication of their joint translocation with the nanocarrier. Thus, caution should be exercised even when inert materials such as Au NPs and QDs are used for fundamental studies, as also these can be degraded in biological systems through oxidizing processes releasing mineral ions (Balfourier et al. 2020; Derfus et al. 2004).

Techniques such as XANES and single‐particle ICP‐MS (spICP‐MS) have been used to back up ICP‐MS findings and confirm the presence of intact NPs in unexposed plant parts. Avellan et al. (2019) employed XANES to characterize the speciation of Au found in the roots following foliar NP application, revealing that this was in the metallic form and thereby suggesting the nanoparticulate nature of the Au detected. Gao et al. (2023) instead utilized spICP‐MS to confirm the presence of particulate material from ZnO@MSN in, for example, roots and younger leaves in tomato plants, observing that NP size was reduced during translocation.

Various LC methods have been used to characterize the distribution of pesticide‐loaded NPs (Liang et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2021, 2024). Two separate studies reported increased pesticide concentration in roots and unexposed leaves after foliar application of NPs in comparison with the free pesticide molecule treatment and thereby hypothesized that NP translocation occurred (Liang et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2021). However, it was not possible to discriminate whether NPs simply enabled better delivery of pesticide inside the leaf, or whether intact NPs and cargo were transported together following leaf uptake. Assessing foliar NP translocation with LC techniques can be complicated, as soluble cargoes such as pesticides can be released by the NPs and translocated as dissolved molecules.

Finally, CLSM was utilized in multiple publications to study distal NP transport to, for example, roots and stems. For example, Yang et al. (2024) documented the translocation of fluorophore‐labelled iron oxide NPs in roots and stems solely by CLSM. Similarly, in a separate study, fluorophore‐tagged liposomes and metal oxides were reportedly translocated as a fluorescence signal was found in the roots following leaf NP application (Liang et al. 2021). Wu et al. (2024) also reported the translocation of foliar‐applied nanogels to the roots using CLSM. However, as previously described, fluorescent tags can dissociate from NPs and possibly enter the phloem as free molecules or parts of new complexes. Thus, misleading evidence regarding NP translocation can be produced by CLSM, and thereby it should not be used for assessing the long‐distance transport of NPs unless additional methodologies are included.

Behind the Numbers of NP Translocation

Foliar‐applied NPs have been reported to translocate efficiently from the treated area to unexposed plant parts (Avellan et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2021, 2023; Jeon et al. 2024; Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). In many studies, as much as 30%–40% of the applied material, as NP core elements or elemental tracers, was reported to translocate (Avellan et al. 2019; Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). However, when converting percentages into amounts, these most often correspond to less than 1 μg (Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023), or even just a few ng (Avellan et al. 2019; Jeon et al. 2024). The underlying reason is that most publications investigated the fundamental uptake and translocation pathways of foliar NPs, where only small quantities of NPs containing non‐native elements and tracers such as Au, Cd, and Gd were applied locally (Avellan et al. 2019; Jeon et al. 2024; Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023).

While the scope of these works justifies the application of NPs on a limited portion of the leaf (e.g., versus whole‐leaf spraying), the small amounts dosed to plants do not allow us to evaluate whether NP translocation can serve meaningful redistribution of plant nutrients and other agrochemicals once these are applied at conventional rates. Furthermore, the application of such small quantities poses challenges to elemental quantification in untreated tissues, increases uncertainty around the values detected, and increases the risk of analyzing artifacts as even small cross‐contaminations between the treated area and unexposed tissues can be erroneously attributed to NP translocation. Just in a few cases, NP cargoes have been applied in quantities relevant to agronomic practices (Gao et al. 2021, 2023; Liang et al. 2021). For example, Gao et al. (2023) applied 40 μg of Zn nutrient as ZnO@MSN to tomato plants, which is in line with the physiological Zn demand of a young plant (with a fresh weight of ~10–15 g), and reported about 50% translocation to different plant parts, although likely as a combination of both ionic and nanoparticulate Zn. However, the study did not determine whether NPs could promote the restoration of Zn functionality in the Zn‐deficient plants.

Another element introducing uncertainty about the potential of turning these findings into real applications is how data are often presented. Data conversion into percentages can be informative as it provides an additional layer of information and deeper insights into NP partitioning across the plant. However, when applied to small amounts, it can produce deceptive results as small absolute changes translate into large percentual changes. In the case of NP translocation, looking at NP cargo “enrichment”, namely the increase in concentration, in the targeted tissues seems more relevant. This ultimately defines the amount of NP payload that can be used by plant tissues for their fundamental metabolic and physiological functions, provided that it remains bioavailable. However, only a few publications made available data on the concentration of NP cargoes in the different plant fractions (Gao et al. 2021; Liang et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2021), whereas most of them reported data as % of the recovered or applied amounts (Avellan et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2023; Jeon et al. 2024; Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023).

Fundamental and proof‐of‐concept studies have provided most of the evidence of NP translocation thus far (Avellan et al. 2019; Jeon et al. 2024; Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). Yet, while in relative terms the translocation of foliar‐applied NPs appears to be efficient, concrete proof that it can promote substantial and meaningful translocation of immobile agrochemicals to support, for example, plant defense and nutrition is still lacking. Thus, future efforts focused on cases of agronomical relevance are warranted to assess the real impact of foliar NP application and translocation in crop production.

Conclusions

Enabling phloem translocation of immobile nutrients and pesticides will allow for a significant improvement in the use efficiency of foliar‐applied agrochemicals. To date, however, only a small number of papers provide convincing indications of it (Avellan et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2021, 2023; Jeon et al. 2024; Ristroph et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021, 2023). More specifically, these studies show that complementing different techniques and including proper controls are required to generate robust evidence of NP translocation, overcoming the risks of analyzing and imaging artefacts generated by NP biotransformation processes leading to the unintentional release of NP cargoes and tags.

Despite being documented, some fundamental aspects of foliar NP translocation still await clarification. Proof of NP translocation from exposed leaves to other plant organs is primarily circumstantial, with no direct evidence of intact NPs in the phloem tissue or translocating across, for example, the plasmodesmata and pore‐plasmodesmata units. In this regard, future research is also needed to reveal how NPs exceeding the theoretical size exclusion limit of the phloem barriers manage to reach the sieve elements for further transport.

To promote efficient agricultural practices enabled by nanotechnology, agronomical relevant cases should be tested and phloem‐targeting strategies should be developed. Simple and scalable approaches using widely available biomolecules such as sucrose present attractive opportunities to enhance NP remobilization. To leverage these, our research field needs a better understanding of the physical features and potential molecular targets that define the phloem tissue. This knowledge will be pivotal for designing NPs capable of reaching specific biological targets within the companion cell, plasmodesmata, and pore‐plasmodesmata units.

To advance in this direction, variability across different crop species should also be studied. As of yet, we have highly concentrated evidence derived from only two plants (i.e., tomato and wheat) and little to no documentation of NP translocation in other major crops such as rice, barley, potato, soybean, and maize. Moreover, experiments are often performed at the seedling stage while comparative studies on maturing crops are generally lacking. Future studies should address this issue and explore NP translocation processes in other crops to advance effective NP design for foliar applications. Furthermore, for a deeper understanding, other aspects including differences in the cell wall composition, the chemical environment of the apoplast, mesophyll porosity, and phloem loading mechanisms should be investigated, as these are likely to influence NP mobility inside the leaf and translocation between organs in different crops.
