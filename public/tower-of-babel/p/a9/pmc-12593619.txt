Recombinant LAB vector engineered to secrete chicken IFN-λ3 induces an early but sustained activation of antiviral host responses against AIV infection
Authors: Sucharita Bhowmick, Sandeep Yadav, Aparna Varma, Subham Yadav, Samiran Mondal, Mohammadali Alizadeh, Shayan Sharif, Amirul Islam Mallick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Designing a recombinant Lactococcus lactis (L. lactis) to secrete functional chicken Type III Interferon (ChIFN-λ3).•Mucosal administration of recombinant L. lactis secreting ChIFN-λ3 (sChIFN-λ3) restricts avian influenza virus (A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966/H9N2) replication and shedding in chickens.•Histopathological analysis of AIV-infected birds pre-treated with sChIFN-λ3 showed preserved lung and intestinal tissue integrity with minimal pathological lesions.

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV) can potentially cause outbreaks in humans and avian species, resulting in high mortality rates and leading to significant economic losses worldwide (Mahmoud et al., 2024; Schmolke et al., 2009). Since 2013, several novel avian influenza virus subtypes, including H7N9, H6N1, H10N8, H5N6, H7N4, H10N3, and H5N8, have been detected in humans, highlighting their emergence beyond avian and raising significant concerns over their zoonotic potential (Bi et al., 2022). In poultry, the low-pathogenic avian influenza viruses (LPAIV), particularly H9N2, can cause subclinical infections, including coughing, sneezing, oculonasal exudates, as well as decreased egg production in domestic birds (Yehia et al., 2023). Besides poultry, H9N2 viruse pose a significant threat to humans due to their increased affinity for human-type sialic acid receptors and substantial genetic reassortment, which enhances their ability to cause human infection (Reuter et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2020). Sporadic human cases caused by highly pathogenic avian influenza virus (HPAIV) like H5N1 virus have also been reported in 23 countries over the last 20 years. Since 2020, H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4.b viruses have been widely identified in wild birds worldwide, leading to outbreaks in poultry (Xie et al., 2025). More recently, a multistate outbreak of H5N1 was detected in dairy cows, and the virus was identified in unpasteurized milk in the US (Garg et al., 2025).

Despite significant advances in understanding the molecular mechanisms of avian influenza virus (AIV) infection, host adaptation, and transmission dynamics, the development of effective control measures remains a major challenge. This is primarily due to the high mutability of the AIV genome, resulting from the error-prone replication by its polymerase complex, which leads to the accumulation of point mutations and subsequent antigenic changes (Shao et al., 2017). Consequently, existing prophylactic strategies fail to provide adequate, long-lasting immune protection against heterosubtypic AIV infections due to a lack of cross-protection (Stanifer et al., 2020). Although measures like stamping out, serological surveillance, and biosecurity measures like bio-exclusion and biocontainment remain the existing strategies for the rapid elimination of AIV outbreaks in poultry, their implementation has serious impacts on ethical, social, economic, and environmental health (Capua and Marangon, 2006; MacMahon et al., 2008; Threats et al., 2005; Zeng et al., 2024).

Although inactivated fowlpox-vectored and adjuvanted whole-virus vaccines have demonstrated some benefits, their long-term cross-protective efficacy remains limited (Guo et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2020; Swayne, 2020). In addition, various socioeconomic challenges such as poor vaccination practices, inadequate insurance coverage, and insufficient post-vaccination monitoring persist in different geographical regions, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), continuing to pose significant concerns for the effective implementation of vaccination programs (Guyonnet and Peters, 2020; Soema et al., 2015).

To this end, there is growing interest in host-directed immunotherapeutic approaches in livestock and poultry farming, attracting attention from organized poultry sectors (Brown et al., 2017). Several studies have highlighted the potential of cytokine-based biotherapeutic approaches to serve a dual role: first, as drivers of innate immune activation and second, as immunoadjuvants that enhance vaccine-induced immunoprotection. Several immunoregulatory cytokines, such as type I and II interferons (IFN-α, IFN-γ), interleukins (IL-8, IL-17A, IL-18), and toll-like receptors (TLRs), have been shown to enhance pre-immune antiviral protection in various hosts, including avians (Barjesteh et al., 2014; Jiang et al., 2011; Mallick et al., 2011; Nang et al., 2011; Yuk et al., 2016). However, the exogenous application of cytokines requires careful consideration to maintain physiological balance, as excessive systemic inflammation can lead to tissue damage and organ dysfunction, leading to a negative impact on overall performance/growth in poultry (Donnelly et al., 2009; Jarczak and Nierhaus, 2022; Kuribayashi et al., 2013). Previous studies by our group (in a mouse model) and others have demonstrated that in vitro stimulation of epithelial or primary fibroblast cells with recombinant IFN-λ3 can restrict the replication of Type A influenza virus by enhancing the expression of several interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) in a dose-dependent manner (Masuda et al., 2012; Yadav et al., 2023). Although IFN-λ signalling overlaps with Type I IFN signalling, IFN-λ3 signalling is primarily limited to epithelial tissues, such as the respiratory and intestinal mucosa, exhibiting restricted tissue tropism and thereby reducing systemic inflammation (Sommereyns et al., 2008; Stanifer et al., 2020).

In view of these exclusive features, supported by previous studies, we utilized chicken Type III Interferons, particularly IFN-λ3, to achieve targeted immune activation with minimal risk of exacerbated immune responses in vivo (Reuter et al., 2014). Interestingly, the avian IFN-λ subtypes share structural and functional similarities with their human and murine counterparts, particularly in their regulation of JAK-STAT-mediated ISGs production (He et al., 2024; Karpala et al., 2008; Shah et al., 2019).

The function of IFN-λ as a front-line defence at epithelial barriers is thus highly effective in protecting against many respiratory viruses, including influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and rhinovirus (RV) (Becker et al., 2013; Broggi et al., 2020; Kalinowski et al., 2018; Klinkhammer et al., 2018; Lazear et al., 2019). However, administration of IFN-λ3 directly at the host-pathogen interface within the respiratory or gastrointestinal mucosa presents several challenges, including the rapid degradation of cytokines in the gut environment, which necessitates frequent administration.

In recent years, live vector-based cytokine delivery platforms have been developed to facilitate the safe and effective use of cytokines by improving sustained cytokine expression, localised delivery, and reduced systemic side effects in hosts (Pires et al., 2021). Among several live bacterial-based delivery vehicles, Lactic Acid Bacteria (LAB), such as Lactococcus lactis (L. lactis), are of interest for delivering heterologous proteins due to their Generally Regarded As Safe (GRAS) status, along with their potential for targeted delivery of cytokines (Bahey-El-Din, 2012). In line with these perspectives, we previously demonstrated that oro-nasal administration of L. lactis, bioengineered to express murine IFN-λ3, can induce significant expression of ISGs at the mucosal surfaces (Yadav et al., 2023).

To investigate whether IFN-λ3 plays a similar antiviral role in chickens, we employed a live vector-based delivery platform using recombinant L. lactis engineered to secrete functional chicken IFN-λ3 (ChIFN-λ3). Following the bioengineering of rL. lactis expressing ChIFN-λ3, we demonstrated for the first time that pre-treatment of chicken embryonic fibroblast (CEF) cells with this recombinant strain using a two-layered transwell culture system can protect the cells against LPAIV subtype A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966 (H9N2) infection. We further showed that this protection was primarily mediated by the upregulation of several key ISGs and antiviral mediators, including Interferon-Inducible Transmembrane Protein 3 (IFITM3), 2′−5′-Oligoadenylate Synthetase-Like (OASL), VIPERIN, MX Dynamin-like GTPase (MX), NF-κB, IL-8, IL-1β, and Tumour Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α).

To explore the in vivo relevance of these findings, we conducted an in vivo study in which chickens were pre-treated via oro-nasal administration of live rL. lactis expressing ChIFN-λ3, followed by challenge with Influenza A/H9N2 virus. We specifically chose to use H9N2 subtypes as a prototype virus because of their ability to induce temporal changes in both the intestine and the respiratory tract of chickens (Davis et al., 2023). The data from our in vivo experiment confirmed that pre-treatment with ChIFN-λ3 conferred significant immunoprotection by preserving the integrity of the mucosal barrier. This observed immunoprotection was supported by histopathological analysis of mucosal tissues, which revealed reduced tissue damage, as well as virological assessments demonstrating a decreased viral load and viral shedding. Together, we show that the mucosal delivery of rL. lactis-expressing ChIFN-λ3 can impart strong antiviral host defence against AIV infection in chickens without compromising the mucosal health. We propose that leveraging mucosal delivery of bioengineered probiotics to induce localized, tissue-specific, controlled antiviral responses could significantly contribute to poultry production and public health by reducing the risk of zoonotic spillover.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and growth medium

The bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are presented in Table S1. Escherichia coli (E. coli) strains (Top10 and Rosetta 2 DE3) harbouring the recombinant plasmids were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB; Himedia, India) medium containing ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and chloramphenicol (20 μg/mL) (HiMedia, India), respectively, at 37°C under constant shaking. The L. lactis subsp. cremoris strain MG1363 (NZ9000), with and without the recombinant plasmids, was grown in M17 broth (HiMedia) supplemented with 0.5 % (w/v) glucose (GM17) with or without chloramphenicol (20 μg/mL), at 30°C under static conditions.

Primary cells, cell lines, and virus propagation

Chicken embryos were aseptically removed from 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs, and the head, wings, feet, and body cavity were dissected aseptically from the embryos. Finally, embryos were macerated, and the cell suspension was obtained by trypsinisation for 30 min at RT. The cell suspension was then passed through a 70 μm cell strainer. Primary CEF cells, and Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK; obtained from NCCS (Pune, India)) cells, were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Invitrogen), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin (P/S) (Invitrogen), in a humidified environment at 37°C and 5 % CO2 pressure. Furthermore, primary chicken embryo intestinal cells (CEIC) were isolated from 18-day-old embryonated chicken eggs with minor modifications (Singh and Mallick, 2019). In brief, the embryos were aseptically removed, the intestinal portions dissected and finely chopped, followed by trypsinisation. Finally, the cell suspension was passed through a cell strainer, centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min, and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin (P/S) at 37°C and 5 % CO2 pressure.

For the infection study, A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966 (H9N2) virus (NR-28661, BEI Resources, USA) was used. The virus stocks were grown and propagated in the allantoic cavity of 10-11-day-old embryonated chicken eggs for 1 to 3 days (Brauer and Chen, 2015; Guy, 2014).

The viral titer was determined based on the hemagglutinating (HA) units in the allantoic fluid harvested from the infected embryo, as evaluated according to the previously mentioned protocol (Killian, 2020). Further, the infectivity titer of the virus was determined to be 50 % Tissue Culture Infectious Dose (TCID50/mL) using primary CEF cells. Virus-infected cells were maintained in fresh serum-free DMEM medium containing 0.5 μg/mL N-tosyl-L-phenylalanine chloromethyl ketone (TPCK)-trypsin (Sigma, Germany) in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5 % CO2 supplementation.

Cloning of the ChIFN-λ3 gene in E. coli Rosetta 2(DE3) cells

The primary CEF cells were isolated and infected with 1.0 multiplicity of infection (MOI) of the A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966 (H9N2) influenza virus. At 14 h post-infection (hpi), the total RNA was extracted from the infected cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, 800 ng of total RNA was used as a template for the Superscript cDNA synthesis kit (BioBharati LifeScience Pvt. Ltd., India) and reverse-transcribed using the oligo (dT) primer, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Next, ChIFN-λ3-specific primers were used to amplify the ChIFN-λ3 sequence from the cDNA of the CEF cells. The primers were designed based on the Gallus gallus (chicken) mRNA sequence information [NM_001128496.1, 561 bp] acquired from the National Centre for Biotechnology (NCBI) database (Table S2). The PCR product obtained from cDNA amplification was then cloned into the pGEM-T Easy TA cloning vector (Promega, USA) and confirmed by gene sequencing using the 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA).

Our search in the NCBI database suggested two distinct ChIFN-λ3 mRNA sequences, which differed from each other by a single glutamic acid-to-lysine polymorphism at position 150. We confirmed that the mRNA sequence obtained after cloning and sequence verification corresponds to the Lys-150 variant, as supported by a previously published study (Reuter et al., 2014).

Expression and purification of recombinant ChIFN-λ3 (rChIFN-λ3) protein in E. coli

Recombinant pGEM-T Easy TA plasmid harbouring the coding sequence (without signal peptide) of ChIFN-λ3 was subsequently subcloned into pHis-TEV (BioBharati LifeScience Pvt. Ltd., India) expression vector having an N-terminal 6X-Histidine tag in frame. Next, the recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli Rosetta 2(DE3) cells for protein expression, and positive transformants were selected.

To express rChIFN-λ3 protein, recombinant E. coli cells were grown in LB till the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached ∼0.4 and subsequently induced with 1.0 mM Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (Sigma, USA) for 7-8 h for protein expression.

For the purification, 500 mL of induced bacterial cell pellet was resuspended in 12 mL cold lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (Merck, Germany), 0.5 M NaCl (Merck), 10 % glycerol (Merck), and protease inhibitor (1 mM phenyl methyl sulfonyl fluoride, HiMedia) and subsequently lysed by sonication at 30 % amplitude (Sonics Vibra-cell, USA). The lysate was centrifuged, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended with denaturing buffer I (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5 M NaCl, 10 % glycerol, and 6 M urea (Merck) followed by sonication and centrifugation as before. Finally, the remaining pellet was resuspended with denaturing buffer II (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5 M NaCl, 8 M urea, 0.1 mM DTT (HiMedia), 0.1 % sarcosyl (Himedia), pH 8.0). The pellet was further sonicated and centrifuged as before. The supernatant was collected, and the protein was purified by the Ni2+-NTA affinity column (Ni2+-NTA Superflow, Qiagen, USA). The fractions containing purified proteins were pooled, dialysed for desalting, and stored at −80°C till further use. The size and purity of the recombinant protein was further confirmed by SDS-PAGE, followed by Western blot analysis, as described elsewhere (Gorain et al., 2024; Mahmood and Yang, 2012). Furthermore, the 50 % cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of rChIFN-λ3 was determined using primary CEF cells and CEICs as per the standard method described elsewhere (Singh et al., 2019).

Secondary structure analysis of purified rChIFN-λ3 protein

To assess the secondary structure (protein folding) of the purified and renatured rChIFN-λ3 protein, circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was performed using a Jasco J-1500 CD spectrophotometer (Jasco Inc., Easton, MA, USA), integrated with a Peltier apparatus (Jasco CTU-100) for precise temperature control, as stated previously (Gorain et al., 2024). The spectra of the renatured rChIFN-λ3 protein (5 μM) in Tris buffer (100 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM Tris-Cl, 0.25 % glycerol, pH 8.0) were recorded at 25°C, between 190 and 300 nm using a quartz cell (Starna Scientific Ltd.) with a 1 mm optical path length and 100 nm/min scanning speed. Tris buffer was used for baseline correction, and data acquisition was done in triplicate.

Raising hyperimmune sera against rChIFN-λ3 protein

The polyclonal antibody was raised against the purified rChIFN-λ3 protein in female BALB/c mice as per the standard method described elsewhere (Huang et al., 2012). Briefly, female BALB/c mice (∼4 months old) were injected subcutaneously with 10 μg/mouse of purified rChIFN-λ3 emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma Aldrich, USA), followed by three additional injections of 5 μg/mouse emulsified in incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma Aldrich) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. On day 7 post-last immunisation, the mice were sacrificed, blood was collected, and the sera were separated. Finally, the immunoreactivity of the sera was tested by Western blot analysis. The details of the mice immunisation schedule are presented in the supplementary file (Fig. S5).

In vitro cytotoxicity by MTT assay in primary CEF cells and CEICs

3-(4, 5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2, 5- 5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was performed to evaluate the cytotoxic effects of rChIFN-λ3 on primary CEF cells and CEICs as per the published method. Further, the CC50 of rChIFN-λ3 was determined using primary CEF cells and CEICs according to the standard method described elsewhere (Singh et al., 2019). In brief, 1 × 105 CEF cells/well were seeded in a 96-well tissue culture plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 48 h at 37°C under 5 % CO2. Next, confluent monolayers of cells were treated with different concentrations of dialysed and filter-sterile protein ranging from 0.05 to 50 μg/mL (CEF cells) and 0.07 to 150 μg/mL (CEICs) in their respective medium, supplemented with 10 % FBS for 24 h. Finally, 10 μL of MTT dye was added to each well with fresh complete medium and incubated for another 3 h at 37°C under 5 % CO2 pressure. The water-insoluble formazan crystals thus formed were solubilised with dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO) (100 μL/well), and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek). The percentage of cell viability was calculated using the equation:%Cellviability=AtAcX100

where At denotes the absorbance of the treated cell, and Ac denotes the absorbance of the control (untreated cells). Therefore, the CC50 with respect to the untreated control (cells with media only) was calculated using nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism software (version 8).

Receptor binding ability of rChIFN-λ3 protein to primary CEF cells

Since all the experiments were performed in primary CEF cells, we first assessed the expression level of the cognate receptor for rChIFN-λ3 to evaluate its receptor-binding potential. Interleukin-28 receptor alpha (IL-28RA), a heterodimeric type II cytokine receptor specific for IFN-λ3, is primarily expressed in epithelial cells. Therefore, we used primary CEICs as a positive control and primary splenocytes as a negative control. Total RNA was extracted from the primary cells, and cDNA was synthesised following the method described in the supplementary file (Figs. S1, S2). RT-qPCR was then performed to analyse the transcriptional profile of IL-28RA expression in all three primary cell types using a standard set of primers (Table S2).

Confocal microscopy

To see the binding efficiency of rChIFN-λ3 protein on primary CEF cells, freshly harvested CEF cells were seeded in a 6-well tissue culture plate with coverslips and incubated till 70 % confluency (Lahiri et al., 2021). Next, cells were treated with 100 ng/mL rChIFN-λ3 protein for 1.5 h at 37°C under 5 % CO2 pressure. Following incubation, the cells were thoroughly washed with 1X PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at RT. After fixing, the cells were washed, blocked with 3 % BSA in PBS for 1 h at 37°C, and probed with mouse polyclonal anti-ChIFN-λ3 antibody (1:100 dilution) for 1.5 h at RT. Subsequently, the cells were washed and incubated with FITC-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (1:500 dilution) (Invitrogen, USA) for 1 h at RT. Further, Phalloidin-iFlour 647 (Invitrogen, USA) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole-dihydrochloride (DAPI) (Invitrogen, USA) were used to stain the cells for an additional 30 min and 15 min, respectively. The cells were then washed thoroughly and mounted on fresh glass slides using Vectashield (Vector Laboratories, USA) as the mounting medium. Imaging was then performed using the Leica SP8 confocal platform with an oil immersion 63 × objective, and the image was deconvoluted using Leica Lightning software.

Flow cytometric analysis

The cell surface binding ability of rChIFN-λ3 protein to CEF cells was further confirmed by flow cytometric analysis. Briefly, overnight-grown confluent monolayers of CEF cells were treated with 100 ng/mL and 200 ng/mL of rChIFN-λ3 protein for 1.5 h at 37°C under 5 % CO2. Next, the cells were fixed with 4 % PFA, blocked, and probed with the respective primary and secondary antibodies as described previously. Finally, the cells were trypsinized and collected in 1X PBS, and the fluorescence intensity was measured using a flow cytometer (BD LSRFortessa, BD Life Sciences) and analysed using FACSDiva software.

Construction of recombinant L. lactis secreting bioactive ChIFN-λ3 protein

To generate rL. lactis vector secreting ChIFN-λ3 (sChIFN-λ3) protein, we have used the nisin-inducible pSEC vector backbone of L. lactis, henceforth designated as pSec-ChIFN-λ3. A total of 495 bp gene sequence corresponding to the ChIFN-λ3 gene (without signal peptide) was cloned in frame with the N-terminal signal peptide of L. lactis protease USP45 (spUSP45; 81 bp) between the restriction sites NsiI and BanII, followed by electro transformation into L. lactis competent cells (Fig. 2 Panel A-a).

Expression and detection of sChIFN-λ3 in the culture supernatant of rL. lactis

Recombinant L. lactis (rL. lactis) harbouring the pSec-ChIFN-λ3 vector was grown in GM17 media with chloramphenicol (20 μg/mL) as the antibiotic selection marker, at 30 °C under static conditions. Once the OD600 reached ∼0.3, protein expression was induced with 15 ng/mL of nisin (Sigma Aldrich, USA), and the growth kinetics of the rL. lactis cells were monitored till 10 h post-induction.

Optimally induced (15 mg/mL nisin) rL. lactis cells were grown for 4 h and centrifuged to collect the culture supernatant (100 mL), and the total protein content was precipitated using trichloroacetic acid (TCA) (Sigma Aldrich, USA) as per the published method (Yadav et al., 2023). The pellet was washed with chilled acetone (Merck, USA), resuspended in 1X PBS, and finally subjected to Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) and Western blot analysis to detect and quantify the protein (Bermúdez-Humarán et al., 2003; Gorain et al., 2021).

Western blot analysis

Acetone-washed protein pellets were resolubilized in 2X sample buffer (pH 6.8) and adjusted to pH 8.0 using 1 M Tris solution, followed by incubation at 95 °C for 5 min. Routine SDS-PAGE separation and Western blot transfer were performed. After blocking with 3 % BSA for 1 h and washing with 1X PBS, probing was done with mouse polyclonal anti-ChIFN-λ3 antibody (1:500 dilution) for overnight (O/N) at 4 °C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody for 1 h at RT. The blot was developed using 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) (Sigma Aldrich, USA) as substrate.

ELISA

Washed protein pellet was quantified for the total protein content by the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) method and further used to coat (in carbonate bicarbonate buffer, pH 9.6) in a 96-well ELISA plate (Nunc, USA). After overnight incubation at 4 °C, the plate was washed three times with PBS-T (0.05 % Tween-20 in PBS) and blocked with PBS-T containing 5 % BSA for 1 h at 37 °C. Next, 100 μL of mouse polyclonal anti-ChIFN-λ3 antibody (1:500 dilution) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at RT, followed by washing and probing with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (1:2500 dilution) (Invitrogen, USA) for another 1 h at RT. Subsequently, the wells were treated with 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB; HiMedia) substrate, and the reaction was stopped by adding 50 μL of stop solution (1 M sulfuric acid) (Merck, USA). The absorbance was then read at 450 nm using an Epoch 2 microplate reader (BioTek, USA) and compared with the standard plot, which was plotted using different concentrations of purified rChIFN-λ3 protein. The purified rChIFN-λ3 protein was kept as a positive control, and the culture supernatant of empty L. lactis and only media (GM17) were served as negative controls.

In vitro functionality of sChIFN-λ3 secreted by rL. lactis in primary CEF cells

To evaluate the effects of the different forms of ChIFN-λ3 protein, CEF cells were treated either with direct administration of E. coli purified rChIFN-λ3 protein (100 ng/mL) into the DMEM medium, or the rL. lactis-secreted sChIFN-λ3 protein (400 pg/mL) was delivered using a transwell plate system according to the previously published method (Lahiri et al., 2021). Briefly, primary CEF cells were seeded in a lower chamber of a 24-well transwell system (Merck) in 1 mL of complete DMEM medium and incubated at 37 °C in 5 % CO2 for 24 h. On the next day, both empty L. lactis and rL. lactis were grown in GM17 medium until the OD600 reached 0.3 and were then immediately transferred into the upper chamber of the transwell system upon induction with 15 ng/mL nisin. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 4 h, and subsequently, the upper chamber of the transwell plate containing the bacteria was removed and incubated with culture medium containing sChIFN-λ3 for another 4 h at 37 °C in 5 % CO2 pressure. Cells incubated with 1X PBS and empty L. lactis were kept as negative controls. For E. coli purified rChIFN-λ3, the protein was directly added to the cells (100 ng/mL) and incubated for 8 h. Total cellular RNA was extracted from each treatment group using TRIzol (Invitrogen). To evaluate the expression of several ISGs, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and transcription factors, gene-specific primers for chicken IFITM-3, MX, OASL, VIPERIN, IL-8, IL-1β, TNF-α, and NF-κB were obtained from IDT Technologies (USA) (Table S2). The oligo(dT) primer was used to generate cDNA by reverse transcription with 1,000 ng of total RNA per sample, using the Superscript cDNA Synthesis Kit (BioBharati, India). The transcriptional analysis was performed by semi-quantitative PCR analysis, using Chicken β-actin as an endogenous control.

Assessing the in vitro protective efficacy in CEF cells against AIV infection

The schematic representation of the in vitro assessment of antiviral efficacy of ChIFN-λ3 pre-treatment in CEF cells against A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966/H9N2 viral infections is presented in Fig. 3, Panel A.

Cell viability assay

Briefly, primary CEF cells were treated via a transwell system with sChIFN-λ3 (400 pg/mL), and rChIFN-λ3 (100 ng/mL) was directly added to the medium for 6, 8, 12, 18, and 24 h as described previously. Subsequently, cells were infected with 0.1 MOI of A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966 (H9N2) virus, in serum-free growth medium (DMEM with 0.2 % BSA, 0.5 μg/mL TPCK-trypsin and P/S) for 1 h. Following incubation, the medium was removed and replenished with fresh serum-free growth medium, and the cells were incubated for an additional 16 h at 37°C with 5 % CO2 supplementation. Cell viability after virus infection was quantified using the standard MTT assay. The percent (%) protection was calculated as%Protection=(A−B)(C−B)X100where A, B, and C represent the absorbance of treated infected, untreated infected, and untreated uninfected cells, respectively. Cells without virus infection were used as a negative control, and cells treated with empty L. lactis as a vehicle control. In contrast, cells infected with the virus without cytokine treatment were used as a positive control. Representative cell cytopathic effects (CPE) images were then captured in the Nikon TS100 inverted light microscope (Nikon) at 40 × magnification. The residual viral titre in the culture supernatant was also checked by the standard Haemagglutination (HA) assay (Fig. S7).

Intracellular accumulation of viral nucleoprotein (NP)

An indirect immunofluorescence assay was performed using influenza NP antibody (Sino Biologicals, Japan) to detect the viral nucleoprotein (NP) in infected cells pre-treated with different forms of ChIFN-λ3 proteins. Briefly, CEF cells were grown on 10 mm round coverslips (Blue Star, India) in a 24-well transwell system, followed by ChIFN-λ3 treatment as described earlier. Next, the cells were infected with 0.1 MOI of A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966 (H9N2) virus for 1 h. After incubation, the virus medium was removed, washed with 1X PBS, and replenished with fresh serum-free growth medium. The cells were then incubated for an additional 16 h at 37 °C under 5 % CO2 supplementation. Next, the cells were fixed with 4 % PFA for 15 min at RT, permeabilised with ice-cold methanol for 30 min, and incubated in blocking solution (2 % BSA in PBS-T) at 37°C for 1 h. Rabbit polyclonal anti-NP antibody (1:1000) was then added to the cells (4 °C) for O/N, followed by 1 h incubation with FITC-labelled anti-rabbit IgG (F(ab)2, 1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific) secondary antibody in the dark. The cells were then washed with PBS-T and counter-stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole-dihydrochloride (DAPI) to detect nuclei and mounted on a glass slide using VectaShield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, USA).

Viral M-gene quantification

To quantify the viral M-gene transcripts, primary CEF cells were pre-treated with either sChIFN-λ3 (400 pg/mL) or rChIFN-λ3 (100 ng/mL) and then infected with A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966 (H9N2) virus (0.1 MOI) for 16 h. Next, viral RNA (vRNA) was extracted from primary CEF cells using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, USA). To generate cDNA, random hexamer primers were used for reverse transcription of approximately 500 ng of total viral RNA (vRNA) per sample, using MuLV reverse transcriptase and the SuperScript Reverse Transcriptase kit (BioBharati Life Science Pvt. Ltd., India), following the manufacturer's protocol. Finally, the M-gene transcript number was quantified by RT-qPCR, using the CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA) with the standard cycling mode as follows: 1 cycle at 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. The Ct values obtained were converted to the M-gene transcript number from a standard curve previously generated using the viral M-gene cloned into a pMD20 vector (Takara, Japan) (Lahiri et al., 2021).

Viral plaque assay and multicycle growth kinetics

For the multicycle growth assay, primary CEF cells were treated for 8 h with different forms of ChIFN-λ3 protein as described previously. Subsequently, the cells were infected with the 0.1 MOI of the A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966 (H9N2) virus in a serum-free growth medium (DMEM with 0.2 % BSA, 0.5 µg/mL TPCK-Trypsin, and 1 % P/S) for 1 h. After incubation, the virus medium was removed, washed with 1X PBS, and replenished with serum-free fresh growth medium containing TPCK-trypsin at a final concentration of 0.5 μg/mL. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C under 5 % CO2 supplementation. The supernatants were collected at 6, 12, 18, and 24 h post-infection and were stored at −80 °C. Finally, the virus titers in the supernatant were measured by plaque assay in MDCK cells.

To perform the viral plaque assay in MDCK cells, approximately 1 × 105 cells/well were seeded in 12-well tissue culture plates and grown until the monolayer reached approximately 90 % confluency. The culture medium was then removed, and the cells were washed with 1X PBS. They were then incubated with the respective viral supernatants for 2 h at 37°C in a humidified incubator supplemented with 5 % CO₂. After incubation, the cells were washed again with 1X PBS and overlaid with 1 mL per well of agarose overlay medium containing DMEM supplemented with 1 % BSA, 1 µg/mL TPCK-Trypsin, 1 % P/S, and 0.3 % (v/v) autoclaved agarose. The plates were then incubated for ∼40 h at 37 °C under 5 % CO₂ supplementation. Following incubation, the cells were fixed with 10 % formaldehyde for 1 h at RT. The agarose overlay was then carefully removed, and the monolayer was stained with 1 % (w/v) crystal violet in 5 % ethanol. The plaque-forming units (PFU) per mL were calculated using the equation:PFU/mL=Numberofplaques×DilutionfactorVolumeofinoculum(mL)

In vivo immunoprotection against Type A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966 (H9N2) virus challenge infection in chickens

Preparation of rL. lactis cells for oro-nasal administration

For oro-nasal administration in chickens, rL. lactis cells were cultured in GM17 medium supplemented with 20 μg/mL chloramphenicol at 30 °C under static conditions until an optical density (OD₆₀₀) reached ∼0.3. Next, protein expression was induced with 15 ng/mL nisin, followed by incubation for approximately 3 h. The wild-type (WT) L. lactis cells served as the vector control. The bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 2400 × g for 5 min, washed with 1 × PBS, and resuspended to a final concentration of 1.5 × 10⁹ CFU/mL in 200 μL of 1 × PBS. Finally, the birds were administered 100 μL orally and 100 μL nasally (50 μL/nostril) for three consecutive days per week over three weeks (Fig. 5, Panel A).

Experimental birds and housing conditions

A total of 80 one-day-old white Rhode Island Red (RIR) chickens were procured from the West Bengal Directorate of Animal Resources and Animal Health, India. Throughout the experimental period, the birds were housed under a deep litter system, which provided ad libitum access to an antibiotic-free corn-soybean-based mash diet. The detailed composition of the feed substrate used in this study is provided in Table S3.

Experimental groups, treatments, and virus infection

Following a seven-day acclimatisation period, the birds were randomly divided into four experimental groups (Groups A-D). The treatments administered were as follows: Group A received 200 μL of 1X PBS; Group B received 200 μL of 7.5 × 10⁹ CFU WT L. lactis in 1X PBS; Group C received 200 μL of 7.5 × 10⁹ CFU rL. lactis in 1X PBS, and Group D received no treatment (used as a baseline for normal tissue structure). The experiments were performed twice under similar conditions. The details of the experimental groups, sampling time points, and dosing regimens used in this study are provided in Table S4.

Pre- and post-challenge sampling

On day 24, eight birds from each group (excluding Group D) were euthanised by CO2 asphyxiation (30 %) following the guidelines approved by the Committee for the Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA), Department of Animal Husbandry and Dairying (DAHD), Ministry of Fisheries, Animal Husbandry and Dairying (MoFAH&D). Approximately 5 g of tissue (lungs and small intestine) from each bird was collected and preserved in RNA-later™ (Qiagen, USA) for transcriptional analysis, while an additional 20 g of tissue was fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin (NBF) for histological examination.

The remaining birds were challenged with A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966 (H9N2) virus via a combined ocular, oral, and intranasal route. Each bird received 6 × 10⁷ TCID50/mL units of virus in a total volume of 300 μL (50 μL per route). Challenged birds were monitored daily for clinical signs and body weight changes. Finally, oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs were collected on days 3, 5, and 7 post-infection (dpi) to assess viral shedding. The individual swab samples were collected in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes containing transport medium (DMEM supplemented with 1 % BSA and 1 % P/S). The processed supernatants were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Finally, ∼20 g of lung and small intestinal tissue samples were collected for histopathological analysis.

Transcriptional analysis of ISGs and cytokine genes in lung and intestinal tissue

Total RNA was extracted from approximately 5 g of the lung and small intestinal tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA was synthesised from 1000 ng of total RNA using the MuLV reverse transcriptase-based Superscript Reverse Transcriptase Kit (BioBharati LifeScience, India), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression of the selected cytokine genes, including IFITM-3, VIPERIN, IL-8, and IL-1β, was assessed by RT-qPCR using gene-specific primers (IDT Technologies, USA; Table S2). As the internal reference gene, chicken β-actin was used. The RT-qPCR was performed using the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA), and the relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Quantification of residual virus in oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs

Determining TCID50/mL: Viral titers in oropharyngeal and cloacal swab samples were determined using the TCID50 assay, following the WHO (2011) manual with minor modifications (World Health Organization, 2011). The assay protocol followed the previously standardised procedures in our laboratory (Bhowmick et al., 2024). Briefly, 10-fold serially diluted swab samples were added onto 75-90 % confluent monolayers of MDCK cells in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed and supplied with fresh serum-free DMEM supplemented with 1 μg/mL TPCK-Trypsin (Sigma, Germany) and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C in a humidified 5 % CO₂ incubator. The CPEs were then microscopically monitored, the viral titers were calculated using the Reed-Muench method, and the results were expressed as TCID₅₀/mL (Lei et al., 2020).

Flow virometry: To determine the residual viral load in oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs, a flow virometry assay was performed as the method published elsewhere (Bhowmick et al., 2024; Vanhulle et al., 2022). In brief, an 80 % confluent monolayer of MDCK cells was incubated with the clarified swab samples for 12 h. The cells were then fixed with 4 % PFA and permeabilized with ice-cold acetone for 20 min. After blocking with 3 % bovine serum albumin (BSA), the cells were incubated O/N at 4 °C with a rabbit polyclonal anti-nucleoprotein (NP) antibody (1:1000; Sino Biologicals, Japan), followed by incubation with a FITC-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at RT. Cells were subsequently washed, trypsinized, and resuspended in 1X PBS containing 1 % BSA. The fluorescence signals corresponding to the amount of viral NP accumulated intracellularly were measured using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Life Sciences) and analyzed with FCS Express 7 software (De Novo Software, USA).

Effect on the body weight of birds infected with A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966/H9N2 virus

To assess the effects on body weight (relative gain or loss) in the experimental birds, the gross weight of all experimental birds was recorded from 0 to 7 days post-injection (dpi). Additionally, all experimental birds were closely monitored for clinical signs associated with AIV infection, including respiratory distress, lethargy, ruffled feathers, and reduced feed intake.

Comparative assessment of histopathological changes in lungs and small intestinal tissues (pre-and post-challenge with the virus)

To see the antiviral protective effects of mucosal administration of rL. lactis secreting ChIFN-λ3 in the tissue, we collected the lungs and small intestines at pre-challenge (day 24) and post-challenge (day 31) time points. For this, tissue samples were aseptically collected from euthanised birds and fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin (NBF) for further processing as described previously (Bhowmick et al., 2024). In brief, approximately 0.5 g of each tissue (pre- and post-challenge with virus) was collected from each treatment group on day 24 and fixed in 10 % NBF solution. The samples were processed for histopathology by sectioning (∼5 μm), washing under running water, and dehydrating through a graded acetone series (70 %, 90 % and 100 %). Dehydrated tissues were cleared, infiltrated with molten paraffin at 62 °C, embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for microscopic examination.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data of the gene expression study were analysed for any significant difference by calculating the band intensity for each gene using ImageLab software (Version 3.0.1). The GraphPad Prism software (Version 8) was used to plot the graphs and analyse the experimental data using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. A P-value less than 0.05 (*P < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

rChIFN-λ3 protein exhibits receptor binding specificity

To examine the protein functionality in terms of binding to its cognate receptor, we first analysed the secondary structural conformation of rChIFN-λ3 protein, and the far-UV CD spectral data were recorded (Fig. 1, Panel A-f). A static spectrum of rChIFN-λ3 protein (5 μM) was acquired at 25 °C, and the baseline-corrected spectrum was further refined by a 10-point smoothing using the Savitzky–Golay filter. After analysing the spectrum, we found minima at 208 nm and 222 nm and a positive maxima at 192 nm. These spectral data suggest that the protein has a higher percentage (%) of alpha helices. The captured CD spectrum was further analysed by the BeStSel server for secondary structure analysis using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and an enhanced version of the SP175 dataset. The spectral magnitude was scaled by a factor of 0.5 to minimise the normalised root mean square deviation (NRMSD). From this, it could be inferred that rChIFN-λ3 protein is composed of 31.70 % helix 1 (regular), 14.50 % helix 2 (distorted), 8.20 % antiparallel, 0.70 % parallel, 7.70 % turns, and 37.20 % of other secondary structures. The spectra and secondary protein structure analysis were further compared with the CD spectra obtained from a predicted spectrum of purified ChIFN-λ3, suggesting a strong qualitative agreement (Fig. S4).

Next, we confirmed the expression of IL-28RA in primary CEF cells. We observed significantly higher expression of IL-28RA in CEF cells compared to splenocytes (representing immune cells), and it was comparable to the expression observed in primary CEICs (Fig. S3).

Finally, the cell surface binding of rChIFN-λ3 protein was assessed by incubating CEF cells with an optimised concentration of rChIFN-λ3, followed by flow cytometry and confocal microscopy analysis. Treatment of CEF cells with rChIFN-λ3 resulted in detectable fluorescence signals, as observed under confocal microscopy, and a significant rightward shift in fluorescence intensity in flow cytometry analysis, confirming effective surface binding of the protein to CEF cells (Fig. 1, Panel C-a, b).

Discussion

Interferons are a family of cytokines critical to the innate immune response, particularly in the early stage of defense against viral infections. Type I (IFN-α/β), Type II (IFN-γ), and Type III (IFN-λ) IFNs function through distinct receptors to induce antiviral states in host cells and modulate adaptive immunity. Over the past two decades, the strategic use of IFNs has revolutionised the therapeutic manipulation of host immune responses in the context of various virus infections, including Type A influenza viruses (Mertowska et al., 2023; Pandit et al., 2021; Yadav et al., 2023).

However, despite their therapeutic potential, IFNs are inherently pleiotropic, often resulting in systemic toxicity, which limits their clinical use (Balachandran and Adams, 2013; Donnelly et al., 2009). Moreover, most viruses have evolved to evade or suppress IFN-mediated signalling, primarily through the dysregulation of JAK-STAT pathways, including the premature degradation of signalling intermediates (Zhu et al., 2023). As a result, IFN pre-conditioning of the target host can lead to the risk of hyperinflammation, tissue pathology, and paradoxically impaired viral clearance, such as SARS-CoV-2, influenza virus, and certain hemorrhagic viruses (Best et al., 2005; Chen et al., 2021; Gustine and Jones, 2021; Yang et al., 2022).

Therefore, while IFNs remain a potent immunomodulator, their therapeutic application requires careful consideration of their class, timing, dose, and route of administration. In addition to their pleiotropic effects, cytokine-based biotherapies, such as IFNs, face significant pharmacokinetic challenges, including a short plasma half-life and poor tissue specificity (Pires et al., 2021). Compared to other cytokine families, IFNs, particularly Type I and III, exhibit prolonged biological effects primarily due to their ability to initiate a cascade of intracellular signaling, which often persists even after their detectable presence in circulation has ceased (de Weerd et al., 2024). However, recent studies suggest that Type III IFNs, particularly IFN-λ3, may possess a longer biological half-life than some Type I IFNs. For example, while the plasma half-life of IFN-α and IFN-β typically ranges from 2 to 10 h, the mRNA half-life of IFN-λ3 was reported to be approximately 5.4 h (Kozlowski and Milton Harris, 2001; Ramos et al., 2021).

The receptor binding ability of IFN-λ3 is also unique because both the expression of its cognate receptors and the resulting cytokine signalling are primarily restricted to epithelial cells, particularly in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts (Li et al., 2013; Mordstein et al., 2010; Sommereyns et al., 2008) and to a lesser extent, in certain non-epithelial or immune cells (de Weerd et al., 2024; Sommereyns et al., 2008). Unlike type I IFNs, IFN-λ3 binds to a distinct receptor complex composed of a heterodimer: IFN-λR1 (IL28RA) and IL-10R2 to activate the transcriptional upregulation of several ISGs at the mucosal surfaces. Generally, IFN-λ3 binds to a heterodimeric cell surface receptor (IFNLR), composed of the IFN-λ-specific IL28Rα subunit and the ubiquitously expressed IL10Rβ subunit (Lazear et al., 2015). While IL28Rα expression is largely restricted to epithelial cells, IL10Rβ is broadly expressed; together, these subunits initiate signalling that upregulates the expression of multiple ISGs through the JAK-STAT pathway (Lazear et al., 2019). In addition to ISGs, some reports suggest that IFN-λ3 can trigger the expression of various classical antiviral biomarkers, such as 2′, 5′-oligoadenylate synthetase, double-stranded RNA-activated protein kinase, and the MHC class I antigen expression (Benhammadi et al., 2020; García et al., 2006; Kristiansen et al., 2010). Although the distribution of IFN-λ3 receptors has been characterised in other species, the physiological basis of the selective IFN response in chickens remains poorly understood. The tissue-specific expression and favourable pharmacodynamic properties of IFN-λ3 led us to investigate its role in driving effective immune activity exclusively on mucosal surfaces against AIV infections in chickens.

To address this gap, our study, supported by previous findings, confirmed the elevated basal level expression of the ChIFN-λ3 receptors, IL-28RA (IFN-λR1), in CEF cells, which is comparable to that observed in primary CEICs (Zhang et al., 2015). In contrast, freshly isolated primary chicken splenocytes, a representative of classical immune cells, exhibited very low expression of the IL-28RA gene. This suggests that CEF cells may be more receptive to IFN-λ3 stimulation. Furthermore, using purified rChIFN-λ3 protein, we demonstrated its high cellular non-toxicity (up to ∼50 µg/mL) and ability to bind to primary CEF cells. These findings prompted further investigation into the physiological relevance of the selected expression of ChIFN-λ3 by non-immune cells.

For this, we first investigated the downstream signalling cascade in vitro to gain functional insight before evaluating its effects in the more complex environment of a live host. To investigate how tissue-specific activation of ChIFN-λ3 affects functional consequences in a biologically relevant and safe manner, we utilized a bioengineered food-grade rL. lactis to express and secrete functional ChIFN-λ3 protein. A co-culture experiment was then performed, using a transwell plate system with rL. lactis secreting ChIFN-λ3 was cultured in the upper chamber, and primary CEF cells were in the lower chamber. We observed significant transcriptional upregulation of several ISGs (IFITM-3, OASL, VIPERIN, and MX) and pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-8, IL-1β, and TNF-α), well-known for their roles in antiviral innate immune responses. Following binding to its cognate receptor, IFNLR recruits and activates the tyrosine kinases Tyk2 and Jak1, which phosphorylate and activate STAT proteins, primarily STAT1 and STAT2. Together with interferon regulatory factor 9 (IRF9), these STAT proteins form the heterotrimeric transcription factor complex ISGF3. ISGF3 translocates into the nucleus, where it binds to interferon-stimulated response elements (ISREs), driving the transcription of ISGs. In chickens, key ISGs include IFITM3, which blocks viral entry by preventing membrane fusion; MX1 interferes with replication of RNA viruses; OASL activates RNase L to degrade viral RNA; and Viperin, which disrupts lipid raft formation and viral budding (Husain, 2024; Smith et al., 2013). Presuming that pre-treatment timing might be critical for restricting viral replication, we performed a time-dependent antiviral protection study in CEF cells (Fig. 4, Panel A-a). By choosing six different pre-treatment time points (6, 8, 12, 18, and 24 h before virus infection), we observed no significant difference in the effects of IFN-λ3 on viral replication. These findings suggest that pre-exposure to ChIFN-λ3 triggers an early but sustained activation of the antiviral signalling cascade, maintaining an elevated antiviral state. This was further supported by a significant reduction in viral HA titre as well as viral M gene transcript levels, with a marked decrease in intracellular accumulation of viral NP at a single pre-treatment time point (8 h pre-infection). Consistent with this finding, previous studies have shown that ChIFN-λ3 induces a broad range of ISGs and delays influenza virus replication in primary chicken fibroblasts, embryonic tracheal organ cultures (TOCs), and the chicken lung cell line CLEC-213 (Arslan et al., 2019).

In contrast, a limited antiviral response was observed in the chicken fibroblast cell line DF-1 upon challenge with influenza A virus (Reuter et al., 2014). This reduced response may be due to primary CEF cells exhibiting more robust antiviral signalling, likely because they retain more intact and functional antiviral pathways (Jouan et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2009). However, in an in vivo setting, constitutive overexpression of ChIFN-λ3 in a mosaic transgenic chicken embryo model provided only partial protection against a broad range of viruses (Reuter et al., 2014). Despite the antiviral benefits, the hatched chicks exhibited high mortality within the first two weeks post-hatching. The authors suggested that this outcome was likely due to sustained expression of ChIFN-λ3, which may have disrupted the natural gut microbiota and, in turn, impaired nutrient absorption during this critical early developmental period. While these findings suggest a paradoxical role for ChIFN-λ3, they also underscore the importance of optimizing its delivery mode, dosage, timing, and the age at which birds receive the treatment. In line with our current findings, we recently demonstrated that bioengineered LAB vectors secreting murine IFN-λ3 could drive controlled expression of ISGs in a murine model (Yadav et al., 2023). To note that, Type III interferons (IFN-λ) are evolutionarily conserved and show structural similarity among mammals, birds, and amphibians. In fact, the JAK-STAT signalling pathway is also a key mediator of immune responses and inflammation in chickens, and can be activated by chicken interleukin-17A (chIL-17A), similar to the signalling observed in mammals. Recently, we demonstrated that probiotic-based delivery of chicken IL-17A can effectively reduce viral replication and shedding, while maintaining gut tissue homeostasis, preserving mucosal barrier integrity, and supporting a favorable microbial consortium (Bhowmick et al., 2024; Lahiri et al., 2021; Yadav et al., 2023).

With this background, we sought to determine whether bioengineered probiotics could serve as a delivery platform to explore similar strategies for mucosal administration of ChIFN-λ3 against AIV infections in chickens. For this, we administered the birds (oro-nasally) with an optimal dose of nisin-induced rL. lactis secreting ChIFN-λ3 over three consecutive weeks (three times/week). Following exogenous administration of ChIFN-λ3, cytokine gene expression profiles were examined in the lung and intestinal tissues of those birds. Consistent with our in vitro findings, we observed a significant transcriptional upregulation of several key ISGs in vivo, confirming the biological activity of ChIFN-λ3 secreted by rL. lactis within the complex mucosal environment. However, we want to emphasize that while the Nisin Inducible Controlled Expression (NICE) system used herein for bioengineering L. lactis is tightly regulated, there is a complex, protein-specific relationship between nisin concentration and protein yield (Mierau and Kleerebezem, 2005; Zhou et al., 2006). Although increasing nisin concentration increases protein expression, there is a risk of degradation, particularly for secreted proteins. Moreover, since nisin is also toxic to bacterial cells, we optimized the nisin concentration (15 ng/mL) as a critical step to achieve regulated expression.

Nevertheless, the key advantage of intragastric delivery of rL. lactis secreting IFN-λ3 ensures the bioavailability of cytokines at the mucosal epithelium, thereby enhancing its therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, given that cytokines typically engage their cognate receptors at low nanomolar to picomolar concentrations, the observed amount of ChIFN-λ3 secreted by rL. lactis (sChIFN-λ3; ∼ 400 pg/mL by 1.5 × 109 CFU of nisin-induced rL. lactis cells), though seemingly low, should be sufficient to trigger downstream signalling pathways (Hansen, 2020; Liu et al., 2016). This is in agreement with previous studies, suggesting that rL. lactis secreting IL-2, IL-6, or IFN-α2b, IL-17A can induce strong immune responses at mucosal sites even when secreted at low levels (Bahey-El-Din, 2012; Bermúdez-Humarán et al., 2003; Lahiri et al., 2021).

Based on these hypotheses, to finally assess in vivo immunoprotection against AIV infection, rL.lactis-secreting ChIFN-λ3 pre-treated birds were challenged with A/turkey/Wisconsin/1/1966 (H9N2) virus (6 × 10⁷ TCID₅₀/bird). Oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs were collected to evaluate viral shedding. Notably, a significant reduction in viral shedding was observed as early as 3 dpi (∼1.7 log reduction in oropharyngeal samples and ∼1.0 log reduction in cloacal samples), demonstrating the early antiviral effects of ChIFN-λ3. Considering the typical broiler production cycle of 4-5 weeks, final sampling was conducted on day 7 post-challenge (at 31 days of age), at which point we observed nearly complete suppression of viral shedding. This reduction allowed us to contemplate a sustained effect on viral replication by ChIFN-λ3.Given that H9N2 primarily spreads through the respiratory mucosa and initially targets the lungs, we specifically tried to understand the effect of ChIFN-λ3 pre-treatment on lung and intestinal tissue integrity in the context of H9N2 challenge (Yang et al., 2023). The IFN-λ3-treated group exhibited improved lung and intestinal tissue architecture compared to untreated controls, indicating that the immune responses elicited by rL. lactis secreting ChIFN-λ3 was effective yet non-damaging to host tissues. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that L. lactis can exert inherent immunomodulatory effects by promoting epithelial barrier stability, mucin-mediated barriers, modulating cytokine profiles, and supporting beneficial microbial interactions within the gut (Bhowmick et al., 2024; Padilla-García et al., 2025; Yadav et al., 2023). However, the potential of rL. lactis-mediated delivery of ChIFN-λ3 to modulate microbiota populations or promote competitive exclusion warrants further investigation.

Although the H9N2 virus is generally considered low-pathogenic, it can cause damage to both intestinal and lung tissue, as well as the tracheal epithelial cells layer in chickens (Begum et al., 2023; Davis et al., 2023; Li et al., 2018). To this end, we investigated the histopathological changes in lungs and intestinal tissue on day 31 post-infection with the H9N2 virus. In the untreated group, AIV infection led to pronounced inflammation, extensive cellular infiltration, and disruption of mucosal barriers in both the lungs and intestinal tissues. Histopathological examination of birds that received rL. lactis secreting ChIFN-λ3 revealed clear evidence of tissue recovery compared to the untreated group. These observations suggest that, in addition to the general health-promoting benefits of L. lactis, ChIFN-λ3-mediated immune modulation played a pivotal role in enhancing gut health and preserving mucosal barrier integrity, thereby creating a mucosal environment conducive to epithelial regeneration and healing. Additionally, while all birds survived throughout the study, we recorded some weight loss (only after day 5 post-infection) of control birds that received PBS or WT L. lactis (up to 21-25 %) compared to those that received rL. lactis secreting ChIFN-λ3. This further implicates that ChIFN-λ3-driven immune modulation can help alleviate the adverse effects of H9N2 infection on growth performance (Ellakany et al., 2018).

However, it is worth noting that as a non-commensal LAB vector, L. lactis (NZ9000) exhibits a short gastrointestinal transit time (Biswas et al., 2024; Boodhoo et al., 2023; Gorain et al., 2021). Furthermore, at prolonged post-administration intervals and in the absence of an inducer such as nisin, recombinant L. lactis ceases protein expression, thereby limiting sustained transgene expression. To enhance gut retention and maintain protein expression, additional modifications, either physiological or genetic, are warranted. Strategies such as applying mucoadhesive coatings or employing a commensal probiotic vector driven by an endogenous constitutive promoter could further substantiate the long-term effectiveness of this approach (Biswas et al., 2024; Ogaugwu et al., 2018).

Together, our findings demonstrate that recombinant LAB vector-mediated mucosal administration of ChIFN-λ3 can initiate early and sustained immune signalling by activating specific cell surface receptors expressed on mucosal epithelial cells. Consistent with non-avian hosts, ChIFN-λ3 likely engages the JAK-STAT signalling pathway, resulting in ISGs mediating effective antiviral defense. These results highlight the potential of ChIFN-λ3 as a prophylactic antiviral agent in poultry, offering a promising strategy to mitigate the impact of avian influenza virus infections in poultry and potentially in other susceptible species.

Conclusion

The present study provides insights into how mucosal delivery of ChIFN-λ3 via a bioengineered LAB vector can confer immunoprotection against AIV infection. By combining both in vitro and in vivo experiments, this study also offers a mechanistic understanding of the functional attributes of ChIFN-λ3 when administered directly at the mucosal interface. Given the challenges in controlling AIV infection in poultry, this epithelial-focused approach achieves targeted functions through bioengineered probiotic bacteria, providing a front-line defense against viral invasion. While minimizing systemic inflammation compared to type I interferons, this approach illustrates how type III interferons can benefit the host by enhancing gut health, preserving mucosal integrity, and facilitating antiviral defense at epithelial surfaces. However, further studies would be highly valuable to enhance the immunoprotective potential of this live vector-based delivery platform, particularly when used in conjunction with active vaccination as an adjuvant to stimulate both innate and adaptive immune responses.
