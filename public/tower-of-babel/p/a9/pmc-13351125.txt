Assessment of Large Language Models and Expert Clinicians in Grading Mandibular Third Molar Extraction Difficulty
Authors: Suk Min Yoon, Jeong Hun Yoo, Taejun Kim, Jae Hyun Kang, Bong Chul Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Pederson difficulty score (PDS) is widely used to assess mandibular third molar extraction difficulty, but is subject to interobserver variability because no universally accepted reference standard exists. This study investigates whether multimodal large language models (LLMs) demonstrate systematic grading tendencies comparable to those of human clinicians in the absence of a definitive standard.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 100 panoramic radiographs. Two LLMs (GPT and Gemini) and two oral and maxillofacial surgeons independently graded extraction difficulty using the PDS. No reference standard or gold-standard rater was designated; all raters were treated as independent assessors. Agreement was assessed using quadratic-weighted kappa and intraclass correlation coefficient values. Systematic bias was analysed using Bland–Altman plots and ordinal logistic regression. The effects of prompt language and session protocol were also evaluated.ResultsThe interexpert agreement was substantial (κ = 0.754). GPT demonstrated moderate agreement with experts (κ = 0.564-0.590), whereas Gemini exhibited fair-to-moderate agreement (κ = 0.356-0.461). Overall, the four-rater reliability was moderate (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.553). Both LLMs displayed minimal systematic bias (GPT: −0.13; Gemini: −0.08), with no significant differences in grading tendencies compared with human experts (P > .05). However, sequential-session evaluation significantly reduced the scoring consistency for both models (GPT: P = .001; Gemini: P < .001), whereas prompt language had no significant effect on total PDS scores (GPT: P = .543; Gemini: P = .386). A component-level linguistic variation was observed only in GPT’s assessment of the ramus relationship (P = .003).ConclusionThe examined LLMs showed grading tendencies partly comparable to those of human clinicians, without evidence of systematic directional bias; however, agreement remained below interexpert levels and was sensitive to conversational context. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of diagnostic accuracy or clinical reliability, as no reference standard was established.Clinical relevanceLLMs may provide supplementary grading information if used with independent-session protocols; however, their role in clinical decision-making requires further validation against defined outcome standards.

Introduction

Mandibular third molar extraction is one of the most frequently performed procedures in oral and maxillofacial surgery (OMFS). Accurate preoperative assessment of extraction difficulty is fundamental for informed consent, complication risk stratification, and referral decision-making.1,2 Among the radiographic grading systems developed for this purpose, the Pederson difficulty score (PDS) has been widely adopted owing to its straightforward application to panoramic radiographs and its structured evaluation of three anatomical parameters: depth of impaction, spatial relationship with the ascending ramus, and angulation relative to the adjacent second molar.1 By translating complex radiographic anatomy into a reproducible numerical index, the PDS directly informs surgical planning, including the selection of the surgical approach, anticipated operative time, and the level of expertise required for safe extraction.1,2 Despite its clinical utility, PDS grading remains inherently subjective, and inter-rater variability among clinicians is well documented, underscoring the need for adjunctive assessment tools.

The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has transformed artificial intelligence (AI) applications in dentistry, enabling multimodal systems to process both textual and visual clinical inputs.3, 4, 5, 6 Benchmarking studies have demonstrated that contemporary LLMs can achieve passing or near-human performance on standardized dental licensing examinations,7,8 and language-dependent performance variability has been observed across bilingual and multilingual evaluation frameworks.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Beyond examination settings, LLMs have been evaluated in diverse dental clinical reasoning tasks, including oral and maxillofacial pathology,14, 15, 16, 17 pulpal diagnosis,18 dental crowding assessment,19 dens invaginatus recognition,20 oral oncology screening,21 and OMFS-specific question generation.22 Multimodal models have shown progressively improving, yet still variable, capabilities in image-based diagnostic reasoning when provided with radiographic and clinical photographs.5,6,23,24

In parallel, deep learning–based computer vision models have achieved considerable success in dental radiographic analysis, including oral cancer detection,21 jawbone lesion diagnosis,23,25 implant treatment planning and osseointegration prediction,26, 27, 28 cephalometric analysis for orthognathic surgery,29,30 and soft tissue profile evaluation.31 For third molar surgery specifically, convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures have been developed to predict extraction difficulty and postoperative complications such as paraesthesia from panoramic radiographs,1,2 and related AI models have been applied to predict indications for cracked tooth extraction.32 However, these approaches rely on task-specific training with labelled datasets, and no study to date has evaluated whether contemporary multimodal LLMs, operating in a zero-shot setting without fine-tuning, can perform structured radiographic grading of mandibular third molar extraction difficulty.

Critically, the existing literature has predominantly adopted an accuracy-centric framework that compares AI outputs against a predetermined reference standard, with little attention to the systematic grading tendencies and scoring biases of different assessors. This distinction is particularly relevant for PDS-based extraction difficulty grading, for which no universally accepted gold standard exists.1 Rather than imposing an arbitrary reference standard in a domain where none is established, the present study characterizes the grading tendencies of all raters – both human and artificial – as independent assessors on equal footing. This approach is intended to reveal systematic differences in how different evaluators perceive surgical difficulty, without implying that any rater’s output constitutes diagnostic ground truth. Furthermore, given the language-dependent performance variation reported in LLM benchmarks,7,9,10 evaluating the effect of prompt language on grading patterns is essential for understanding the practical deployment of these tools in non-English-speaking clinical settings.

This study aimed to provide the first comparative analysis of grading tendencies among multimodal LLMs and human clinicians in assessing mandibular third molar extraction difficulty using panoramic radiographs. Specifically, we sought to: (1) characterize and compare the systematic grading patterns of two contemporary multimodal LLMs and two OMFS specialists using the PDS, without designating any rater as a reference standard; (2) evaluate the effect of prompt language (Korean vs English) on LLM grading tendency; (3) quantify the influence of session administration mode on scoring behaviour and within-model repeatability; and (4) identify specific component-level biases (depth, ramus relationship, angulation) with implications for clinical decision-making, surgical planning, and the responsible integration of AI-assisted tools into preoperative difficulty assessment workflows.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study used anonymized panoramic radiographic images obtained from clinical records. The study protocol was approved by the Public Institutional Review Board of South Korea (P01-202512-01-035). Because the dataset consisted of retrospectively collected and fully deidentified images, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Study design

This retrospective cross-sectional study compared the grading tendencies of multimodal LLMs and human clinicians in assessing mandibular third molar extraction difficulty using panoramic radiographs (Figure 1). This study was explicitly designed as a grading-tendency and agreement study, not a diagnostic accuracy study. No reference standard or gold-standard rater was designated; all four raters – two LLMs and two human evaluators – were treated as independent assessors on equal footing. The primary analytical focus was to characterize and compare systematic differences in grading behaviour across raters. Dataset

Panoramic radiographs were collected from patients evaluated for mandibular third molar extraction at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Daejeon Dental Hospital, Wonkwang University College of Dentistry, between January 2024 and August 2025. All images were acquired using a single panoramic X-ray unit (OP300, Instrumentarium Dental) under standardized acquisition protocols. A total of 100 radiographs containing a mandibular third molar adjacent to an intact second molar were included. A sample of 100 cases was selected to balance feasibility of the multirun LLM evaluation protocol (5 independent runs per model per language condition = 2000 LLM assessments total) with comparability to prior dental AI studies, which have typically employed 50 to 150 cases for rater-agreement analyses.1,2,23

To ensure data quality, specific criteria were applied. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of a mandibular third molar adjacent to a mandibular second molar and (2) completed crown and root development of the third molar. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) absence of the adjacent mandibular second molar, (2) incomplete crown or root development of the third molar, and (3) severe periodontal disease or intraosseous pathology that could affect radiographic interpretation.

All images were deidentified before analysis. Patient identifiers were removed and replaced with randomized study codes to ensure anonymity and data security.

Image processing

Radiographs were retrieved from the institutional Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) and exported in JPEG format. Each panoramic image was divided at the midline to create a hemi-panorama containing the target mandibular third molar. The images were cropped to a standardized field of view (512 × 512 pixels) that included both the mandibular third molar and the adjacent second molar. The cropping region was defined by a single experienced oral and maxillofacial surgeon (B.C.K.) who was not involved in the subsequent PDS evaluation, in order to minimize observer bias in region-of-interest (ROI) placement. To guide the evaluations, a red rectangular bounding box was added to indicate the ROI corresponding to the mandibular third molar. The standardized 512 × 512-pixel field was confirmed to include all anatomical landmarks required for PDS scoring, including the occlusal plane, cementoenamel junction of the adjacent second molar, the anterior border of the ascending ramus, and the full crown of the third molar. Cases in which cropping would have obscured these landmarks were excluded at the image-preparation stage. No additional preprocessing, enhancement, or data augmentation was performed to maintain the original diagnostic quality. These standardized images served as the primary input for both LLM assessments and human expert evaluations.

PDS scoring

The radiographic difficulty of mandibular third molar extraction was evaluated using the PDS system. This scoring method assesses three primary anatomical parameters: (1) depth of occlusion relative to the occlusal plane (C1), (2) relationship to the ascending ramus (C2), and (3) angulation relative to the long axis of the adjacent second molar (C3).

The detailed scoring criteria for each parameter, including the reference landmarks and assigned points, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The total PDS for each case was calculated as the sum of the scores from these three categories (C1 + C2 + C3), resulting in a total difficulty score ranging from 3 to 10.

Raters

Four independent raters participated in the evaluation, divided into two groups: multimodal LLMs and human clinical experts.

Human clinical experts

The human expert group consisted of two oral and maxillofacial surgeons, each with over 5 years of clinical experience specifically in third molar surgery. No formal calibration session was conducted prior to the evaluation; the two surgeons scored images independently without prior consensus training. This absence of calibration is acknowledged as a limitation (see Limitations section). To ensure the objectivity of the study, the human evaluators were blinded to the results of the LLM assessments and to each other’s ratings. They independently evaluated the 100 standardized images based on the PDS criteria.

Multimodal LLMs

The LLM group included two state-of-the-art models: ChatGPT-5.2 Thinking (OpenAI) and Gemini 3.0 Pro (Google DeepMind). These models were selected for their advanced multimodal capabilities in processing both visual and linguistic data. Both models were accessed via their official web interfaces using paid subscription accounts to ensure access to the highest performance levels available during the study period.

LLM evaluation protocol

Evaluation procedure and environment

Assessments were conducted between 14 January and 20 February 2026. All evaluations were performed from the same geographic region to maintain consistency in model response patterns. The models were utilized in their base state without fine-tuning and had no prior exposure to the study dataset. Since system-level parameters, such as temperature and sampling settings, are not accessible via user interfaces, these were kept at their default settings. Each image was evaluated using a standardized prompt that incorporated the Pederson scoring criteria, with instructions to report individual component scores (C1-C3) and the PDS. LLM responses were recorded manually for each case. Component scores (C1, C2, C3) and the total PDS were extracted verbatim from the model output. In all 100 × 2 models × 2 languages × 5 runs = 2000 independent evaluations, valid responses providing all three component scores and a total PDS were obtained. No missing, invalid, or internally contradictory responses were encountered (Supplementary Figure 1).

Prompt administration

To prevent ‘contextual carry-over’ effects, each image was submitted in a newly initialized chat session. This protocol ensured that no previous prompts or responses were visible to the model during evaluation, thereby maintaining the independence of each rating and preventing conversational memory from influencing subsequent assessments.

Comparative and reliability analysis

To rigorously validate the models’ performance, three additional experimental conditions were implemented:

Bilingual evaluation

To examine the influence of prompt language on grading behaviour, all images were evaluated using both Korean and English prompts. The two prompts contained identical instructions and scoring criteria, differing only in the language of delivery.

Sequential session analysis

To evaluate potential context effects within a single conversation, an additional experiment was performed using the Korean prompt condition. All images were evaluated sequentially within a single chat session, and the results were compared with those from the independent-session protocol.

Repeatability assessment

Within-model repeatability was assessed by repeating the full set of 100 image evaluations five times for each model under each language condition. Each run was conducted in independent chat sessions separated by several hours to ensure the consistency of grading outputs across different timeframes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Significance was set at P < .05.

Inter-rater reliability and agreement

To assess overall reliability across the four independent raters, intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) values were calculated using a two-way random-effects model (ICC 2,1) for absolute agreement. The ICC was determined for each Pederson component score (C1, C2, and C3) as well as the total PDS. Pairwise agreement between raters was evaluated using quadratic-weighted kappa statistics to account for the ordinal nature of the scoring system. Kappa values were interpreted according to the criteria of Landis and Koch: slight (0.00-0.20), fair (0.21-0.40), moderate (0.41-0.60), substantial (0.61-0.80), and almost perfect (0.81-1.00).

Systematic bias and grading behaviour

To detect systematic differences in grading behaviour between the LLMs and human clinicians, Bland–Altman analysis was performed. The mean score of the two human experts was used solely as a human-comparator score for agreement and bias quantification, not as a diagnostic ground truth or reference standard. The mean bias and 95% limits of agreement (LoA) were calculated by comparing each LLM’s total PDS with this human-comparator score. The clinical significance of observed LoA was also considered (see Results and Discussion). Furthermore, ordinal logistic regression was employed to determine whether rater type (comprising two human experts and two LLMs) significantly influenced the distribution of assigned scores, with Human Expert 1 serving as the reference category.

Contextual and language effects

To evaluate the effects of conversational context and prompt language, agreement was measured using quadratic-weighted kappa values, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed to identify any statistically significant shifts in scoring. Specifically, the independent-session protocol was compared with the sequential-session protocol to assess context-induced variations, and Korean-language prompts were compared with English-language prompts to examine the influence of prompt language.

Gemini displayed relatively lower and more varied agreement levels, ranging from 0.356 (fair) vs Human Expert 1 to 0.461 (moderate) vs Human Expert 2. Impact of prompt language (bilingual evaluation)

The influence of prompt language on the total PDS was not statistically significant for either model (GPT P = .5426; Gemini P = .3859). Both models maintained moderate to substantial agreement across language conditions (weighted kappa 0.553-0.590). Specifically, Gemini showed strong language robustness, with no significant shifts in any submetric. In contrast, GPT exhibited a statistically significant variation in ramus relationship (C2) assessment (P = .0030) between Korean and English prompts, although this did not translate into a significant shift in the total difficulty score (Table 5).

Discussion

The preoperative assessment of the difficulty of extracting impacted mandibular third molars is a fundamental yet complex task in OMFS. Although the PDS is a widely accepted standard, its clinical utility is often limited by interobserver variability due to subjective radiographic interpretation. Initial efforts to automate this assessment, including our previous work with task-specific CNNs, achieved high accuracy by benchmarking against a defined reference standard.1 However, at that time, the direct application of general-purpose LLMs to multimodal clinical reasoning was not feasible. Previous studies from our group using CNN models demonstrated high predictive performance in assessing extraction difficulty under supervised learning conditions. These approaches, however, rely on task-specific training with labelled datasets and predefined reference standards. In contrast, the present study evaluates zero-shot multimodal LLMs without prior task-specific optimization, representing a fundamentally different paradigm of clinical reasoning. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as a direct comparison in predictive accuracy but rather as an exploration of generalizable reasoning behaviour in a nonsupervised framework. This distinction is clinically important, as it addresses whether general-purpose models can provide potential supplementary assessment support in settings where task-specific training data are not readily available.

The present study, therefore, represents a distinct methodological paradigm. Unlike conventional supervised learning frameworks that require a predefined ground truth, the present study adopts a reliability-centric agreement model in which all raters – both human and AI – are treated as independent assessors. The advantage of this approach is that it reflects real clinical variability; no single expert’s assessment is assumed to be correct, and systematic differences between raters can be directly characterized. The principal disadvantage is that true diagnostic accuracy cannot be calculated, as no external outcome measure (eg, actual operative time, surgical complications) was used to validate the grading. This distinction is critical: the study can identify whether LLMs grade similarly to human clinicians, but cannot establish whether either group grades correctly. This shift is particularly significant because contemporary LLMs operate in a zero-shot setting, offering a degree of generalizability that was inherently absent from previous task-specific deep learning architectures.

In alignment with this methodological framework, our findings demonstrate that contemporary multimodal LLMs show grading tendencies partially comparable to those of human clinicians. Although agreement levels for LLMs remained below the substantial consensus observed between human specialists, GPT exhibited moderate agreement that remained below interexpert agreement. Critically, our internal analyses confirmed the absence of systematic grading bias, with neither model significantly overestimating or underestimating surgical difficulty relative to experts. These results suggest that multimodal LLMs can extract relevant radiographic information from standardized images in a zero-shot environment; however, the wide LoA and the absence of an outcome-based reference standard preclude conclusions about clinical reliability or decision-support utility at this stage.

The observed agreement patterns align with the transformative shift currently reshaping dental AI. As highlighted in a comprehensive overview by the International Dental Federation (FDI), AI in dentistry is evolving from foundational, task-specific applications towards more integrated, contemporary systems.3 Current 2026 benchmarks have already demonstrated that LLMs can achieve exceptional proficiency in high-level dental specialty examinations,11 and structured licensing assessments,7,8 showing high accuracy and logical fidelity in generating and solving complex clinical problems, particularly within the field of OMFS.22 Furthermore, recent evidence from other imaging specialties indicates that state-of-the-art models such as GPT-4o exhibit diagnostic properties comparable to those of human experts.12 Although image-based clinical reasoning in dentistry remains a complex frontier, these global trends support our findings that multimodal LLMs are transitioning into digital second-opinion tools across diverse disciplines. For instance, recent studies have shown the potential of LLMs in diagnosing oral lichen planus,5,6 and performing multimodal analysis of radiolucent jaw lesions,23 alongside broader applications in oral pathology,14, 15, 16, 17 endodontics,18,20 orthodontics,19 and oral cancer recognition.21 These advances contrast with the substantial success of task-specific deep learning architectures in dental radiology, which have long established high benchmarks for predicting surgical difficulty and postextraction paresthesia.1,2 Specialized models have demonstrated robust performance across a wide array of clinical tasks, including the radiological diagnosis of jawbone lesions,25 dental implant planning and osseointegration prediction,26, 27, 28 orthognathic surgery and cephalometric analysis,29, 30, 31 and the prediction of cracked tooth extraction indications.32 Despite these impressive benchmarks, a direct quantitative comparison between such specialized systems – including our previously reported CNN model1 – and the multimodal LLMs evaluated here is not applicable due to fundamental differences in their underlying AI paradigms. Unlike task-specific architectures that require extensive training on labelled datasets for narrow clinical indications, the present study examines the ‘zero-shot’ capabilities of general-purpose LLMs. These models perform complex diagnostic grading without any prior exposure to the study dataset or task-specific optimization. The moderate zero-shot agreement exhibited by GPT in this study suggests a growing capacity for generalizable radiographic reasoning, whereas the performance disparity of Gemini underscores both the promise and the limitations of current integration.4

A notable finding of this study was the significant influence of conversational context on grading behaviour, particularly in Gemini. When images were evaluated sequentially within a single chat session, statistically significant shifts in scoring were observed across all parameters for Gemini, whereas GPT demonstrated more localized instability. This phenomenon reflects a contextual carry-over effect, in which the internal conversational memory of the model introduces bias from preceding cases into subsequent evaluations. Such findings align with recent evidence that multimodal AI performance is highly prompt-dependent, requiring careful calibration of interaction strategies to maintain assessment quality.24 Although context retention is a strength for general-purpose interaction, our results suggest that it may compromise the independence required for objective radiographic assessment. To mitigate memory-induced bias and maintain a stable assessment standard, we recommend an independent-session protocol for AI-assisted evaluations, in which each case is initiated in a fresh session. This practical safeguard is essential to ensure that multimodal LLMs function as supplementary AI-assisted assessment tools in clinical workflows.

The results regarding linguistic influence further emphasize the robustness of current multimodal LLMs in dental clinical reasoning. Our analysis revealed no significant difference in total PDS scores between Korean and English prompts, suggesting that the core radiographic reasoning capability is largely language-independent. This stability is consistent with recent large-scale evaluations where LLMs maintained high diagnostic performance across multiple languages and demonstrated consistent proficiency in bilingual medical and dental examinations.10 The consistency of overall grading, despite the distinct linguistic structures of Korean and English, underscores the generalizability of these models for global clinical use. Nevertheless, the observation of item-specific variability, such as the interpretation of the relationship to the ramus in GPT, suggests that localized terminology and its nuances can still exert a subtle influence on the assessment of specific anatomical landmarks. These findings indicate that although LLMs offer high linguistic flexibility, ensuring the precision of translated clinical terms remains essential for the standardized interpretation of complex radiographic features across different regions.

This study has some limitations. First, and most importantly, this study did not establish a reference standard or ground-truth measure. The absence of an external validation criterion – such as operative time, postoperative complications, or surgeon-rated difficulty during surgery – means that the study can describe grading tendencies and inter-rater agreement, but cannot establish whether LLM-generated or human-generated scores are clinically accurate. This is a fundamental constraint of the study design and should be considered primary when interpreting its findings.

Second, only two human experts participated, without a prior calibration session or consensus protocol. The use of two uncalibrated raters may limit the reliability of the human-comparator score used in the Bland–Altman analysis, and the addition of a third expert or a consensus panel would have strengthened the validity of inter-rater comparisons.

Third, the single-institution nature of the dataset may limit generalizability. Although public panoramic datasets exist, they were not utilized because they lack standardized PDS annotations and sufficient metadata to verify strict inclusion criteria, such as complete root development. To avoid the interobserver variability associated with de novo labelling and ensure internal validity, a curated institutional dataset was prioritized.

Fourth, the ROI cropping procedure was defined by a single investigator. Although this investigator was not involved in subsequent PDS scoring, the standardized cropping approach may have inadvertently influenced anatomical visibility for depth and ramus-relationship assessment. This preprocessing step should be considered when generalizing these findings.

Fifth, the LLM evaluations were conducted using publicly accessible web interfaces without control over internal model parameters such as temperature or sampling strategies. These factors may introduce variability that users cannot fully standardize, and this limitation reduces the reproducibility of the specific results. Future studies should explore API-based evaluation with controlled parameters where technically feasible.

Sixth, the results should not be generalized beyond the specific LLM versions evaluated (ChatGPT-5.2 Thinking; Gemini 3.0 Pro), the panoramic radiograph modality, or the Korean patient population. LLM performance may differ substantially across model versions, imaging modalities, and patient demographics.

Finally, the study focused on radiographic difficulty grading and did not evaluate clinical outcomes such as operative time, surgical complications, or postoperative recovery. Future research integrating radiographic assessment with clinical outcome data would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the potential role of AI in surgical decision support.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides evidence that multimodal LLMs can produce difficulty grading patterns that broadly resemble those of human clinicians when evaluating mandibular third molar extraction difficulty on panoramic radiographs. At the same time, the findings emphasize that the reproducibility of LLM-based evaluations strongly depends on interaction protocols and that conversational context may influence grading behaviour. As AI systems continue to evolve, it is important to acknowledge that general-purpose LLMs may not yet have reached the precision of specialized, task-specific decision-support tools.13 Therefore, further research using larger datasets and prospective clinical designs will be essential to determine how LLM-assisted assessments can be safely and effectively integrated into clinical workflows as AI-assisted grading frameworks. In this context, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of grading-pattern similarity rather than evidence of clinically validated decision-making performance.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that two multimodal LLMs, particularly GPT, showed grading tendencies partially comparable to those of experienced oral and maxillofacial surgeons when assessing mandibular third molar extraction difficulty on standardized panoramic radiographs, without evidence of systematic directional bias. GPT achieved a level of agreement with human experts below, but closer to interexpert agreement. However, because no reference standard was established, these findings reflect grading similarity rather than diagnostic accuracy or clinical validity.

However, the reliability of LLM-based assessment is critically dependent on conversational context. The observed instability and contextual synchronization in sequential-session protocols highlight that maintaining independent chat sessions for each case is necessary to ensure reproducible and objective results. In contrast, prompt language (Korean vs English) appears to be a secondary factor, as both models maintained stable reasoning across bilingual conditions.

Although LLMs show promise as supplementary grading tools, their integration into clinical practice requires standardized interaction protocols and validation against clinical outcome measures before their utility as decision-support tools can be established. Further research involving larger multicentre datasets, additional expert raters, calibrated reference standards, and prospective clinical outcome data is warranted.
