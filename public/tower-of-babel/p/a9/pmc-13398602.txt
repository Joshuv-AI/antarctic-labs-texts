Case report of a gastric bezoar causing anterograde gastroduodenal intussusception requiring surgical intervention in a 15‐year‐old female
Authors: Olga Ostrovetsky, Abanoub Nakhla, Christopher Prien, Rachael Sundland, Srilakshmi Atthota, Kyle Glithero, Daniel Hechtman, Graciela Wetzler, Lynn Model
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastroduodenal bezoars are masses of indigestible material that accumulate within the stomach and may lead to obstruction. We present a young female who presented with abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, and was found to have a large gastric bezoar extending into the duodenum, resulting in gastroduodenal intussusception. Endoscopic removal was unsuccessful due to the size of the mass, which consisted predominantly of hair. The patient underwent surgical removal, with resolution of symptoms and uneventful recovery. This case highlights an uncommon cause of gastroduodenal intussusception and emphasizes the importance of recognizing bezoars as a potential lead point in patients with obstructive symptoms. Prompt diagnosis and intervention are essential, particularly when endoscopic management is not feasible.

INTRODUCTION

Gastrointestinal (GI) bezoars are accumulations of indigestible material in the GI tract, most frequently found in the stomach and composed of substances such as plant fibers, hair, or medications. 1 Trichobezoars are typically associated with psychiatric conditions and often present with nonspecific symptoms until complications arise. Gastric bezoars complicated by intussusception are exceedingly rare. In the pediatric population, intussusception most commonly occurs in the ileocolic region (over 90%), 2 and gastroduodenal involvement is unusual, almost always linked to a pathological lead point such as a benign gastric tumor. 3 This case describes a large trichobezoar serving as the lead point for gastroduodenal intussusception in a young female, emphasizing the importance of early recognition, prompt surgical management and evaluation of underlying psychiatric comorbidities to prevent recurrence.

CASE REPORT

A 15‐year‐old female with history significant for anxiety presented to the emergency department with 1 week of progressively worsening epigastric pain. She described the pain as being aggravated by eating and relieved by sitting up or bending forward. She denied any complaints of constipation or recent changes in bowel habits. Her psychiatric history was significant for anxiety‐associated trichotillomania and trichorhizophagia, which had not been formally treated. This behavior, though deeply connected to her anxiety, had never been formally addressed by a professional. The patient had no known surgical history. Her family history was non‐contributory. She had no history of tobacco or other substance abuse. On exam, there was a palpable fullness in the left upper quadrant with no other notable findings. Labs were significant for lipase of 1140. Pediatric gastroenterology was consulted, and upper endoscopy revealed a large trichobezoar lodged within the stomach, extending into the antrum and duodenum (Figure 2). Due to the size and extent of the bezoar, endoscopic removal was attempted but unsuccessful, and the decision was made to proceed with surgical intervention. Surgical approach proceeded with a 7‐cm incision made in the left subcostal location. Stomach was elevated to the abdominal wall through a wound protector to reduce contamination of the skin. It was noted that her stomach was intussuscepting into the duodenum with the trichobezoar as a lead point. A gastrotomy was made on the body of the stomach and the bezoar was removed piecemeal (Figure 3). Inspection of the mucosa revealed superficial ulcerations but no significant ischemic areas or deeper layer damage. The gastrotomy was closed transversely with a stapler and the wound was closed in multiple layers as per routine, with wound edges left slightly open to reduce risk of skin infection. Postoperatively, the patient was promptly evaluated and treated by the psychiatry team, and omeprazole was initiated. The patient's diet was advanced slowly as bowel function returned, and she was discharged home on postoperative day 3. The patient followed up in clinic 2 weeks after discharge with no abdominal complaints and endorsed keeping her psychiatry appointments. No further post‐operative surgical surveillance was required, and her anti‐acid medication was discontinued.

DISCUSSION

Bezoar symptoms can vary according to size, location, and type of foreign material. These may include nausea, vomiting, halitosis, weight loss, abdominal pain, abdominal distention, and upper GI bleeding. 4 In this case, the patient presented only with mild abdominal pain, highlighting the nonspecific nature of early symptoms. Despite further assessment through physical exam and imaging, the diagnosis remained unclear. Notably, the patient was found to have a markedly elevated lipase level, which was most likely secondary to mechanical duodenal obstruction and traction related to the gastroduodenal intussusception, resulting in transient pancreatic duct compression or irritation rather than true pancreatic inflammation.

A comprehensive history is crucial for diagnosing bezoars, with confirmatory tests including CT scans, upper gastrointestinal series, or endoscopy. 1 CT scans may be inconclusive as large food boluses and bezoars often have a similar appearance. Gastric bezoars may have nonspecific symptoms or be asymptomatic and be found incidentally during endoscopy or imaging studies like X‐rays or CT scans. 5 In the case discussed here, the patient initially exhibited vague symptoms along with a palpable abdominal mass, suggesting a bezoar, particularly given her known psychiatric history. The CT scan revealed an intussusception with atypical gastric contents, but it did not confirm the presence of a bezoar. Endoscopy ultimately established the diagnosis.

Treatment options for bezoars include medical, endoscopic, and surgical approaches. Medical management with enzymatic dissolution is primarily effective for phytobezoars. Endoscopic fragmentation is often ineffective for trichobezoars due to the dense, matted nature of hair conglomerates. 1 Surgical intervention is recommended when endoscopic and pharmacologic treatments fail, when bezoars are large, or when complications such as obstruction, perforation, or intussusception are present. The presence of intussusception on cross‐sectional imaging increases the urgency of intervention due to the risk of bowel wall edema, ischemia, and perforation. While laparoscopy may offer benefits such as smaller incisions and reduced postoperative pain, it is generally not recommended for large bezoars due to the risk of spillage and peritoneal contamination. 5 , 6 For a gastric bezoar surgical excision, a linear gastrotomy along the body of the stomach is recommended, with the use of a wound protector to limit contamination. In this case, removal of the bezoar resolved the intussusception, or Rapunzel syndrome, because the cause of the lead point was extracted. 7 For large bezoars, inspection of the interior of the stomach wall before gastrotomy closure is important as bezoar impaction especially in the cases of intussusception may cause ulcers or focal ischemia. The recurrence rate of bezoars can be as high as 20% due to their underlying psychiatric cause. 8 To reduce the likelihood of recurrence, it is essential for patients to manage underlying psychiatric disorders.

CONCLUSION

Trichobezoars represent rare causes of gastric intussusception and obstruction. They pose a diagnostic challenge warranting consideration as a differential diagnosis, especially in patients with known psychiatric symptoms and/or developmental delay. Additionally, collaboration with different specialties is essential to prompt diagnosis and treatment. A multidisciplinary approach involving discussion with radiology and with the gastroenterology team is crucial.
