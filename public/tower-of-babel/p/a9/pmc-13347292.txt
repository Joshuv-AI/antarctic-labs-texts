Engineering Ga-containing dendritic MFI zeolites as safe-by-design hierarchical and immunomodulatory nanointerfaces
Authors: Beatrice Fodor, Eva Romani, María del Mar Alonso-Doncel, Patricia Horcajada, David Serrano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of biocompatible and multifunctional inorganic nanomaterials is crucial in nanomedicine, requiring a balance of structural precision, safety, and therapeutic efficacy. In this work, we report the synthesis of nanometric dendritic gallium-containing MFI (d-Ga-MFI) zeolites from zeolite embryos functionalized with an amphiphilic organosilane, engineered as safe and bioactive inorganic interfaces. By systematically optimizing the synthesis, precise control over particle size, morphology, and hierarchical porosity was achieved. The final materials are highly crystalline dendritic zeolites with nanometric particle dimensions (<200 nm), high surface areas and pore volumes, with yields exceeding 90%. Optimization of the xerogel route enabled fine-tuning of gallium content and accessibility, yielding structurally balanced materials (d-Ga-MFI-X2-5) in which gallium is predominantly incorporated in tetrahedral coordination sites with a loading of 4 wt% Ga. In vitro evaluation using RAW 264.7 macrophages demonstrated excellent biocompatibility (cell viabilities above 90% across a wide concentration range), together with a marked attenuation of lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammatory responses, outperforming benchmark mesoporous silica nanoparticles. These results demonstrate that d-Ga-MFI zeolites can uniquely combine structural robustness, interconnected hierarchical porosity, and intrinsic immunomodulatory behavior. Overall, this study establishes d-Ga-MFI zeolites as a safe-by-design inorganic platform with strong potential for advanced biomedical applications.

Introduction

Designing crystalline porous nanomaterials that effectively manage particle size, hierarchical porosity, and structural stability is challenging in materials chemistry. In zeolitic systems, maintaining nanometric dimensions while ensuring crystallinity, high accessibility, and scalability is particularly difficult due to the influence of small micropores and framework composition on diffusion capacity.1

Dendritic zeolites have recently emerged as a new class of hierarchical materials, characterized by possessing a highly branched nano-architecture that originates from a central core in a ‘tree-like’ pattern. This dendritic nanostructure creates a multi-level hierarchical organization leading to materials that combine the benefits of conventional zeolites, such as crystallinity, stability, and uniform microporosity, with an outstanding accessibility through the formation of radially oriented meso/macropores.2,3 In particular, dendritic MFI (d-MFI) nano-architectures provide interconnected hierarchical networks that improve mass transport while retaining crystalline order.3,4 Dendritic zeolites provide better structural stability, reproducibility, and higher yields than amorphous porous materials or polymeric dendritic systems, making them ideal for advanced applications like catalysis, ion-exchange, and adsorption.5–7 However, controlling framework composition and heteroatom incorporation in nanometric dendritic zeolites while maintaining the accessibility remains fairly unexplored, with only the reported studies of the co-incorporation of cobalt or copper alongside aluminum (Co/Al-MFI and Cu/Al-MFI).8

Also, a key limitation for extending dendritic ZSM-5 (d-Al-MFI) materials toward other advanced applications, such as nanomedicine, is the presence of framework aluminum.9,10 This metal has been linked to different health hazards, such as neurotoxicity and embryotoxicity.11 Therefore, the isomorphous substitution with alternative heteroatoms, which preserves framework integrity while introducing additional functionality, represents a rational strategy to expand their application scope. Gallium is particularly attractive due to its well-known Lewis-acidic catalytic properties and its chemical similarity to aluminum, which enables its incorporation into tetrahedral framework sites without disrupting crystallinity.12,13 Beyond structural substitution, gallium introduces a unique opportunity to transform a traditionally catalytic material into a bioactive inorganic interface, capable of actively interacting with cellular pathways. Gallium has intrinsic biological properties, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities, making gallium materials more than passive biocompatible platforms.14,15

Engineering a well-defined bioinorganic interface requires precise control over the surface chemistry of the material, accessibility of the active sites, nanoscale dimensions, and biocompatibility.16–18 In this context, biomedical evaluation provides a stringent and highly informative validation platform. Cellular models do not merely serve as application targets, but as functional probes to assess whether the engineered zeolitic surface can operate as a safe, accessible, and intrinsically bioactive interface. Rather than designing a passive carrier, the objective is to construct a structurally precise, hierarchically accessible dendritic zeolite whose framework composition enables direct and controlled communication with biological systems.

Herein, we report for the first time the synthesis and comprehensive characterization of gallium-containing dendritic MFI (d-Ga-MFI) zeolites engineered with controlled nanometric dimensions, hierarchical porosity, and tailored framework gallium incorporation. Multiple synthetic strategies are systematically explored to optimize morphology, accessibility, gallium insertion, and yield. The most structurally balanced material is subsequently evaluated in vitro using macrophage models to assess cytocompatibility and inflammatory response modulation, providing functional validation of the material design. Altogether, this work positions dendritic Ga-MFI zeolites not simply as adapted catalytic materials, but as engineered bioactive inorganic interfaces with potential in advanced biomedical applications.

Results and discussion

Aluminosilicate dendritic zeolites have been previously prepared using the aggregative growth of protozeolitic nanounits in a liquid synthesis gel, previously functionalized with an amphiphilic organosilane.2,19 Although the starting mixture is in reality an emulsion, this methodology is here denoted as the liquid gel (LG) route. While this approach enables the formation of dendritic ZSM-5 zeolites with well-defined morphology, it leads to particles of about 1 µm in size. Such particle dimensions limit their use in nanomedical applications. More recently, a modified strategy, based on the crystallization of a functionalized xerogel (XG), has been introduced, allowing improved control over particle size and hierarchical porosity in aluminosilicate ZSM-5 zeolites.8

In this work, we extend these synthetic methodologies beyond conventional aluminosilicate compositions by substituting Al with Ga in the MFI framework, thereby accessing gallium-containing dendritic nanozeolites (d-Ga-MFI). This compositional modification not only broadens the scope of the LG and XG approaches to new heteroatom-containing zeolitic nanosystems but also introduces intrinsic biological functionality associated with gallium species.20 Thus, both LG and XG routes are systematically explored here to rationally engineer d-Ga-MFI nanozeolites. The synthetic protocols are described in detail in the Experimental section and schematically illustrated in Fig. 1.

Zeolite materials synthesis following the liquid gel (LG), xerogel-1 (XG1), and xerogel-2 (XG2) routes.

The LG route comprises: (i) hydrolysis of the Si and Ga precursors in an aqueous solution of tetrapropylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH) solution, which acts as an organic structure-directing agent (OSDA) for the MFI framework, (ii) precrystallization of the synthesis gel at 90 °C to generate zeolite embryos, (iii) functionalization of these protozeolitic units with dimethyloctadecyl[3(trimethoxysilyl)propyl] ammonium chloride (TPOAC) as a silanization agent (SIA), and (iv) hydrothermal crystallization of the synthesis gel. TPOAC plays a key role in the generation of dendritic zeolites as it acts as a micellar soft template, stabilizing the zeolite embryos into a stable mesophase formed by the interaction between the zeolitic embryos and the TPOAC micelles. This ordered structure directs the radial aggregation of nanounits during crystallization, which generates a dendritic architecture while creating hierarchical porosity.2,3

In the XG route, the gel derived from the silanization step is first dried to obtain a functionalized xerogel that contains all the inorganic and organic matter initially added in the liquid synthesis gel route (Fig. 1). This xerogel is then wetness impregnated with an aqueous TPAOH solution and subsequently subjected to hydrothermal crystallization. This new route has been called XG1. In a modified version of this methodology (XG2), the Ga source is incorporated during the wetness impregnation step of the xerogel instead of being added to the initial liquid gel preparation, allowing additional control over gallium incorporation into the MFI framework.

Preparation of dendritic Ga-MFI through crystallization of liquid gels

For the d-Ga-MFI preparation, the LG route was adapted from the reported d-ZSM-5 synthesis, using a molar gel composition of 0.01Ga2O3 : 1SiO2 : 0.11TPAOH : 15H2O : 0.03X[OH] : 0.05TPOAC. However, introducing gallium nitrate as the Ga source lowers the pH and induces gelation of the synthesis mixture. To compensate for this effect and favor zeolite crystallization, 0.03 molar equivalents of an additional base (X[OH]: TPAOH, tetramethyl ammonium hydroxide (TMAOH), or potassium hydroxide (KOH)) were added.

Initially, TPAOH was selected as the additional hydroxide source, yielding d-Ga-MFI-L1. Sample nomenclature includes “d” when dendritic features are observed, followed by the synthesis route (L, liquid gel) and sample number (Table S1).

d-Ga-MFI-L1 exhibits the characteristic MFI diffraction pattern reported by the IZA (Fig. 2a).21 The Ar sorption isotherm at −186 °C (Fig. 2b, c and Table S2) displays a hybrid type I/IV isotherm, with high uptake at both low (P/P0 < 0.01) and intermediate relative pressures characteristic of hierarchical zeolites.22 The non-local density functional theory (NL-DFT) pore size distribution (PSD) confirms the presence of well-defined MFI micropores, together with a narrow distribution of meso and macropores. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) images (Fig. S1) reveal spherical-shaped particles of ca. 600 nm with radially oriented branches formed by small nanounits (ca. 9 nm) that appear randomly aggregated. Although hierarchical porosity is introduced, the particles remain denser and less radially open than the aluminum-based dendritic analogues.3

(a) XRD patterns, (b) Ar adsorption–desorption isotherms (−186 °C), and (c) NL-DFT PSDs of the calcined d-Ga-MFI-L1, Ga-MFI-L2, and d-Ga-MFI-L3 samples.

To try to further open the nanostructure, the additional hydroxide source was varied. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was not pursued because Na+ does not promote dendritic growth in this micellar-templated MFI system.19 Instead, TPAOH was replaced with TMAOH, a basicity source, and with KOH, a mineralizing agent without SDA capacity.23 These synthesis conditions yielded samples Ga-MFI-L2 and d-Ga-MFI-L3, respectively.

The materials obtained with these additional bases retain the MFI diffraction pattern and type I/IV isotherms (Fig. 2). In comparison with d-Ga-MFI-L1, Ga-MFI-L2 shows a more compact meso/macropore contribution (Vmeso/macro: 0.27 cm3 g−1vs. 0.39 cm3 g−1, Table S2). In contrast, KOH increases the accessibility by broadening the porosity into the meso/macropore domain, giving the highest total pore volume of the LG series (0.56 cm3 g−1) and increasing the meso/macropore volume to 0.39 cm3 g−1. Morphology also changes markedly (Fig. S2). Ga-MFI-L2 forms micrometric oval particles (>900 nm), with only incipient growth of aggregated nanounits (ca. 14 nm) and no clear evidence of dendritic nano-architecture, whereas d-Ga-MFI-L3 shows clear dendritic features with radially oriented branches (ca. 600 nm) built from aggregated nanounits (ca. 7 nm) (Fig. S3).

These results denote the great effect of the cation of the basicity source on the zeolite particle size and the textural properties. It can be envisaged that these cations play a significant role in the different architectures obtained. While quaternary ammonium SDA and co-SDA (TPA+ and TMA+) favor denser morphologies, a mineralizing agent such as K+ promotes the aggregation of smaller nanounits into dendritic frameworks. Overall, within the LG series, d-Ga-MFI-L3 exhibits the most evident dendritic features. Therefore, KOH was selected as the most effective secondary basic agent to promote the formation of dendritic Ga-MFI in LG, but further methodology changes were required to lower the particle size to the sub-200 nm range targeted for nanomedical interfaces.

Synthesis of dendritic gallium-containing zeolites (d-Ga-MFI) through crystallization of xerogel precursors

While the LG route enables the formation of Ga-MFI zeolites with hierarchical porosity and dendritic features, the resulting architectures remain comparatively compact and too large for nanomedical applications (>600 nm). Then, the synthetic methodology was changed from LG (liquid-gel route) to XG (xerogel route), where the silanized gel is dried prior to crystallization and the crystallization takes place at low water concentrations (Fig. 1).

Crystallization of xerogel precursors: xerogel route-1 (XG1)

In the XG1 methodology, the precursor gels correspond to those LG formulations that displayed dendritic features (d-Ga-MFI-L1 and d-Ga-MFI-L3). After silanization, the gels are first dried, and then, the resulting xerogel is crystallized hydrothermally after incipient wetness impregnation with a TPAOH aqueous solution (20 wt%). The materials so obtained were labeled as d-Ga-MFI-X1-1 (TPAOH-derived) and d-Ga-MFI-X1-2 (KOH-derived). A summary of the synthesis conditions is provided in Table S3.

Both samples crystallize into the MFI structure (Fig. S4a), although d-Ga-MFI-X1-1 shows higher diffraction intensities, consistent with larger crystalline domains. Ar sorption isotherms display a hybrid type I/IV behavior (Fig. S4b), combining a pronounced uptake at low relative pressures (microporosity) and a second one at high P/P0, consistent with large meso/macroporosity. Accordingly, NL-DFT PSDs show well-defined microporosity together with a significant contribution from meso and macropores (Fig. S4c). Texturally, d-Ga-MFI-X1-1 displays the highest surface areas (SBET: 609 m2 g−1 and SExt: 331 m2 g−1) with a total pore volume of 0.56 cm3 g−1, of which ∼70% corresponds to meso/macroporosity. On the other hand, d-Ga-MFI-X1-2 exhibits lower surface areas (SBET: 473 m2 g−1 and SExt: 174 m2 g−1) but a markedly higher total pore volume (0.84 cm3 g−1), with ∼77% arising from meso/macroporosity (Table S4).

When comparing with their LG analogues (d-Ga-MFI-L1 and d-Ga-MFI-L3), the XG1 route enhances the hierarchical porosity in both systems. However, the nature of the pore hierarchy differs markedly depending on the basicity source used. For the TPAOH-derived sample (XG1-1), high surface areas and balanced micro- and mesoporosity are achieved, whereas for the KOH-derived material (X1-2), the XG1 methodology amplifies overall accessibility, promoting a more open architecture with extensive macroporosity.

TEM and FE-SEM images evidence a core@shell-type dendritic morphology with pronounced radial branching (Fig. S5 and S6), giving average particle diameters of ca. 475 nm (X1-1) and ca. 850 nm (X1-2). Comparing the KOH material d-Ga-MFI-X1-2 with d-Ga-MFI-L3, the larger apparent size in the XG1 material is mainly associated with the more extended branching and open morphology.

Given all of the above, the XG1 route demonstrates that xerogel crystallization can enhance dendritic features, providing a stronger control over pore hierarchy while maintaining the crystallinity. However, further optimization is required to achieve nanometric particles and a more balanced micro/external accessibility.

Crystallization of xerogel precursors: xerogel route-2 (XG2)

The XG1 route was further modified to simplify post-synthetic procedures, reduce particle size, and refine morphology, giving rise to the XG2 series (Table S5). In XG2, the gallium precursor is not present in the initial gel composition and is instead introduced during the incipient wetness impregnation step. Thus, the incorporation of the Ga source and the resulting change in the impregnation solution pH requires a further optimization of the impregnation variables.

Optimization of the TPAOH content

The effect of the OSDA during impregnation was assessed by varying the TPAOH concentration (10, 20, and 30 wt%), yielding d-Ga-MFI-X2-1, d-Ga-MFI-X2-2, and d-Ga-MFI-X2-3 samples, respectively. All three materials are highly crystalline MFI zeolites (Fig. S7a), with synthesis yields exceeding 96% relative to the initial silica content. This is a significant advantage compared to other silica-based dendritic supports previously investigated for biomedical applications.24

Ar sorption isotherms confirm hierarchical porosity through hybrid type I–IV isotherms (Fig. S7b). Notably, d-Ga-MFI-X2-3 shows a more developed hysteresis loop, consistent with an increased contribution of wider pores. Accordingly, NL-DFT PSDs reveal narrow MFI micropores along with a broad meso/macropore population (Fig. S7c), the latter being consistent with a radially oriented dendritic pore network.

The textural analysis (Table 1) shows that increasing TPAOH from 10 to 30 wt% strengthens the accessible hierarchical voids by 40% while decreasing the micropore fraction by only 6%. The total pore volume also increases from 0.57 to 0.79 cm3 g−1, while the BET surface area is maintained relatively constant, from 500 to 518 m2 g−1, indicating that a higher OSDA content promotes a more accessible hierarchical architecture without compromising crystallinity.

TEM shows the occurrence of rounded dendritic particles composed of radially oriented branches for the three materials (Fig. 3). All XG2 samples are nanometric (≈200–250 nm) and markedly smaller than LG and XG1 materials. In addition, the nanounits that form the radial branches show a slight growth with increasing TPAOH content (from ca. 7 nm in d-Ga-MFI-X2-1 to ca. 9 nm in d-Ga-MFI-X2-3).

TEM images and size distribution of particles and nanounits estimated by ImageJ (n > 100) of d-Ga-MFI-X2-1 (a and d), d-Ga-MFI-X2-2 (b and e), and d-Ga-MFI-X2-3 (c and f) samples.

Notably, d-Ga-MFI-X2-2, synthesized under the optimized XG1 conditions, exhibits an average particle size of 174 ± 47 nm (Fig. 3e), indicating a clear size reduction compared with XG1 (>400 nm, Fig. S5). The nanounits also shrink by almost 50% from XG1 to XG2 (d-Ga-MFI-X1-1, 15 nm, vs. d-Ga-MFI-X2-2, 8 nm). Beyond size, the morphology evolves from the core@shell, macropore-dominated XG1 architecture to a more uniformly dendritic nano-architecture with porosity spanning meso to macropores. In parallel, relative to the TPAOH-derived XG1 analogue, XG2 increases the hierarchical void volume (Vmeso/macro: 0.39 to 0.57 cm3 g−1) while keeping the external surface area in the same range (SExt: 331 to 297 m2 g−1), indicating that accessibility is maintained during downsizing.

In summary, the XG2 route effectively separates gallium addition from gel crystallization, leading to the formation of nanometric dendritic Ga-MFI zeolites with open structures and better hierarchical accessibility. This method significantly improves size control and pore network continuity compared to XG1.

Evaluation of the Ga content effect

Having established that XG2 enables nanometric dendritic Ga-MFI with tunable hierarchical porosity, the influence of gallium loading on framework development and pore architectures was also evaluated. The TPAOH content was fixed at 30 wt% (optimized in Section 2.2.1), and the Si/Ga ratio was varied (15, 30, and 60) using the Si/Ga = 50 material as reference. The resulting samples are denoted as d-Ga-MFI-X2-4 (Si/Ga = 15), d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 (Si/Ga = 30), and d-Ga-MFI-X2-6 (Si/Ga = 60), and compared to d-Ga-MFI-X2-3 (Si/Ga = 50).

All materials exhibit MFI diffraction patterns (Fig. 4a). Notably, the material with the lowest gallium loading (Si/Ga = 60) displays the highest diffraction intensities, evidencing larger crystalline domains. The Ar sorption isotherms display a hybrid type I/IV behavior for all materials, confirming the coexistence of micro- and hierarchical porosity (Fig. 4b). The corresponding NL-DFT PSDs evidence multimodal pore distributions, combining narrow micropores with a broader meso/macropore contribution (Fig. 4c). Interestingly, the hierarchical contribution is substantially reduced for the low-Ga sample (d-Ga-MFI-X2-6), indicating that gallium plays a relevant role in promoting secondary porosity in these dendritic architectures.

Chemical analysis by ICP shows that gallium incorporation reaches a maximum at Si/Ga = 30 (Si/Ga = 27 experimentally found in the zeolite), whereas the decrease of the Si/Ga ratio beyond this value neither enhances gallium incorporation nor affects the crystallinity of the samples (Table 2).

Regarding the textural properties of the samples, d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 provides the most balanced hierarchy within the XG2 series (Table 2). It combines a high total pore volume (0.82 cm3 g−1) with the largest meso/macropore volume (0.68 cm3 g−1), while retaining a significant micropore volume (0.14 cm3 g−1) and high surface area (SBET = 496 m2 g−1 and SExt = 266 m2 g−1). In contrast, the low-Ga sample d-Ga-MFI-X2-6 shows a pronounced loss of hierarchical porosity (VT = 0.49 cm3 g−1 and Vmeso/macro = 0.31 cm3 g−1), whereas d-Ga-MFI-X2-4 presents lower overall accessibility than d-Ga-MFI-X2-5. These trends indicate that a Si/Ga ratio of 30 (d-Ga-MFI-X2-5) gives rise to maximized accessibility without sacrificing framework microporosity.

High-resolution TEM and FE-SEM images of the optimized sample d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 reveal rounded dendritic particles (209 ± 36 nm) built from aggregated nanounits of ca. 9 nm (Fig. 5). Consistently, the intermediate-Ga materials (Si/Ga = 30–50) preserve a well-defined dendritic nano-architecture, whereas the low Ga-sample d-Ga-MFI-X2-6 exhibits a more compact core@shell architecture with larger particle sizes (∼300 nm, Fig. S8).

In summary, these results indicate that intermediate gallium contents (Si/Ga ∼30–50) are optimal to obtain fully crystalline and well-defined dendritic nano-architectures with a balanced micro/meso/macroporosity and small nanometric particle size. Among the investigated compositions, d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 provides the best compromise between crystallinity, morphology, hierarchical accessibility, and nanometric dimensions. These features make it the most suitable material for biomedical applications.

The physicochemical nature and gallium speciation within the zeolitic framework are critical determinants for its performance in biological applications. Therefore, we performed gallium solid-state magic angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (71Ga MAS-NMR), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and pyridine coupled to Fourier transform infrared (pyridine-FTIR) measurements to further understand the Ga nature in the d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 sample.

In this way, the 71Ga MAS-NMR spectrum (Fig. S9) shows a dominant broad resonance centered at ∼150 ppm, characteristic of tetrahedrally coordinated gallium species.25 The absence of a well-resolved signal in the 0–10 ppm region indicates no detectable contribution from octahedral extra-framework gallium species. Nonetheless, a weak baseline contribution near 0 ppm may arise from highly dispersed non-tetrahedral gallium species.26 Overall, these data indicate that gallium is predominantly incorporated as a tetrahedrally coordinated species in the dendritic zeolite.

To gain further insights about the nature of these minor gallium species, XPS measurements were performed (Fig. S10). Deconvolution of the Ga 2p3/2 spectrum reveals three contributions centered at 1120.6 eV (32%), 1119.7 eV (50%), and 1118.7 eV (18%). The two higher-binding-energy components at 1120.6 and 1119.7 eV are assigned to framework-associated Ga species, which together account for 82% of the total Ga signal, whereas the lower-binding-energy component at 1118.7 eV is attributed to Ga2O3-related species (18%).25,27 The presence of two framework-associated contributions likely reflects slightly different local coordination/electronic environments of Ga within the zeolitic framework, although a more specific assignment to distinct T-sites, hydration states, or Si/Ga local environments cannot be made solely from the XPS data. It should also be noted that, because XPS is a semiquantitative surface-sensitive technique, the 18% contribution assigned to Ga2O3-related species reflects its estimated relative abundance within the near-surface region and should not be taken as the bulk fraction of gallium oxide in the material. Indeed, this measurement likely overestimates the overall amount of Ga present as an oxide.

This interpretation is further supported by the pyridine-FTIR acidity measurements that were also employed to directly evaluate the acidity and accessibility of the Ga-containing sites of the d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 sample. The FTIR spectrum recorded after pyridine desorption at different temperatures is shown in Fig. S11. At 150 °C, the spectrum displays the characteristic bands of pyridine adsorbed on Brønsted acid sites (BASs) and Lewis acid sites (LASs) at 1548 and 1458 cm−1, respectively, corresponding to concentrations of 0.167 and 0.203 mmol g−1. In addition, a shoulder at ca. 1446 cm−1, together with a related contribution at ca. 1597 cm−1, is observed and assigned to H-bonded pyridine, most likely associated with the high concentration of surface silanol groups in the dendritic zeolite. Thus, these H-bonded pyridine contributions disappear upon increasing the desorption temperature and are no longer significant from 250 °C onward. The total concentration of Ga-related acid sites at 150 °C was estimated as (BAS + 2LAS), considering that dehydration/dehydroxylation of two framework-associated Brønsted acid sites generates one Lewis acid site. This gives a value of 0.537 mmol g−1, corresponding to an apparent Si/Ga ratio of ca. 31, very close to the actual one in this sample (Si/Ga = 27). This agreement suggests that most Ga atoms are associated with acid sites and are therefore present as framework or framework-related Ga species.

In addition, both BAS and LAS bands decrease progressively with increasing pyridine desorption temperature from 150 to 450 °C, as typically observed for zeolitic acid sites, characterized for showing significant acid strength. This behavior supports the assignment of measured acidity to framework and framework-associated Ga species rather than to Ga2O3-related surface species. Moreover, Ga2O3 is an amphoteric oxide, and so not expected to account for strong acidity.28,29 In conclusion, all these facts provide direct evidence that these Ga-related acid sites are accessible to this probe molecule, being originated by zeolite-associated Ga atoms.

Prior to the biological evaluation, the colloidal behavior of d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 was assessed by dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements in ultrapure water and PBS (Table S6). As expected, the hydrodynamic diameter exceeded the dry-state TEM size (∼200 nm), reaching 326 ± 19 nm in H2O and 264 ± 20 nm in human serum diluted at 10% in aqueous solution, reflecting solvation and partial aggregation under physiological ionic strength. Crucially, the measured sizes remain within the submicrometric range typically internalized by macrophages via endocytic and phagocytic pathways.30 In both media, the particles displayed a negative ζ-potential (−40 mV in H2O and −22 mV in diluted human serum), consistent with a negatively charged surface that is generally associated with improved cytocompatibility and reduced nonspecific membrane interactions.31

In vitro biocompatibility studies

To assess the suitability of the dendritic zeolite d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 as a biocompatible interface in a nanomedicine context, both its cytocompatibility and immunoresponse were evaluated. Immunogenicity is a major limitation in nanomaterial-based therapies, as recognition by macrophages can trigger inflammatory cascades that reduce efficacy and compromise safety.32–34 For this reason, the potential inflammatory reaction induced by d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 was investigated in RAW 264.7 murine macrophages, a standard model probing innate immune responses. Upon stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), these cells produce high levels of pro-inflammatory mediators such as nitric oxide (NO), which can be quantified by the Griess assay to evaluate the pro- or anti-inflammatory effects.35

For comparison, n-Ga-MFI was tested at equivalent doses to determine the effects associated with dendritic nano-architecture. The nanozeolite was prepared as previously reported, obtaining a synthesis yield of 86% with respect to the initial silica content, exhibiting high crystallinity, a particle size of 47 ± 15 nm, and textural properties consistent with a purely microporous MFI framework (see complete characterization in Fig. S12).36 Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) were included as a reference due to their established biocompatibility and were obtained in a 26% yield with respect to the initial silica content (Fig. S13).37,38 As a control of dissolved Ga species, Ga (NO3)3 was used at equivalent doses to the d-Ga-MFI material.

In LPS-activated RAW 264.7 macrophages, treatment with MSN, n-Ga-MFI, and d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 at 50–100 µg mL−1 (Fig. 6) reduced the Griess-derived inflammatory response to less than 30% of the positive LPS control, while maintaining cell viability above 80%. This strong attenuation of NO-associated inflammatory activity is consistent with the intrinsic anti-inflammatory activity described for silica, which can directly suppress inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-6, COX-2, and iNOS in activated macrophages, and for MSNs, which suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress.39,40 Notably, d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 produced the greatest decrease in the Griess-derived inflammatory response (<20%), suggesting a combined effect between hierarchical dendritic nano-architecture, providing high external surface area and enhanced cell–material interaction, and the presence of framework-incorporated gallium species, which may contribute to the modulation of inflammatory pathways. This behavior is in line with the well-documented anti-inflammatory activity of gallium and its compounds.14 Importantly, this effect was achieved without compromising cell viability.

Interestingly, Ga(NO3)3 displayed high variability in the inflammatory response normalized by cell viability values, and also low anti-inflammatory activity. This apparent lack of anti-inflammatory activity and high variability in the results is attributed to the rapid speciation that free Ga3+ species undergo in aqueous solution, rendering insoluble Ga(OH)3 and ionic Ga(OH)4− species, among others. The formation of these multiple gallium species reduces the bioavailability of this ion, thus lowering its biological activity.

These results suggest that the high anti-inflammatory activity observed for d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 is due to the accessible framework or framework-related Ga species exposed at, or near, the zeolite surface. A minor contribution from Ga2O3-like surface species cannot be excluded, but the XPS and acidity results indicate that these species represent only a small fraction of the Ga present.41 These Ga species likely interact with the macrophage via contact-mediated mechanisms or localized intracellular release after phagocytosis.

These results prompted further evaluation of the anti-inflammatory activity of the d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 at lower concentrations (10–20 µg mL−1, Fig. 6). Even at these reduced doses, the material remained effective in modulating the inflammatory response, indicating that the material not only avoids triggering macrophage activation but could also actively regulate inflammation under pathogenic stimulation. This favorable immunological profile supports d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 as a safe and functional platform for biomedical applications.

Overall, d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 combines high cytocompatibility with a marked reduction of NO-associated inflammatory response in activated macrophages, underscoring its potential as a multifunctional inorganic biointerface for biomedical applications.

Conclusions

The rational synthetic design strategies used in this work enabled the first preparation of dendritic gallium-containing nanozeolites (d-Ga-MFI) with controlled composition, hierarchical porosity, and nanometric dimensions. Progressive refinement from the liquid-gel (LG) to xerogel-based methodologies (XG1 and XG2) separated dendritic branching from excessive particle growth, allowing precise tuning of morphology and accessibility combined with high crystallinity and synthesis yields (>90%).

Within the whole series of materials, d-Ga-MFI-X2-5 (Si/Ga ≈ 30, corresponding to a Ga loading of 4 wt%) exhibited the optimal balance between structural order and hierarchical accessibility, combining a nanometric size (209 ± 36 nm) with outstanding textural properties. It features a BET surface area of 496 m2 g−1, and a total pore volume of 0.82 cm3 g−1 of which more than 80% corresponds to the meso and macropores. Advanced spectroscopic analyses confirmed predominant tetrahedral framework incorporation of gallium.

Importantly, preliminary biomedical evaluation demonstrated excellent biocompatibility, with high cell viability above 90% across different doses (10–100 µg mL−1), and a marked attenuation of inflammatory response in macrophages, outperforming reference mesoporous silica.

Overall, this work establishes a rational synthesis pathway for producing nanometric, hierarchically porous, and bioactive dendritic Ga-MFI zeolites, integrating structural precision and compositional control. This methodology also incorporates biological functionality into a single safe-by-design inorganic material nanoplatform. These findings open new opportunities for the use of dendritic zeolites as biocompatible interfaces in advanced biomedical applications, including antibacterial coatings, tissue-regenerative scaffolds, or drug delivery systems.

Experimental section

Reagents

Gallium(iii) nitrate (Ga(NO3)3·H2O, Thermo Scientific 99.99%), tetra-n-propylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH, Thermo Scientific, solution in 40 wt% in water), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, Sigma-Aldrich 98%), potassium hydroxide flakes (KOH, Thermo Scientific 85%), H2O Milli-Q, tetramethylammonium hydroxide pentahydrate (TMAOH, Sigma-Aldrich 97%), dimethyloctadecyl[3-(trimethoxysilyl)propyl]ammonium chloride (TPOAC, Sigma-Aldrich, solution 42 wt% in methanol), methanol (MeOH, Chemlab, HPLC grade), Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium-high glucose (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich), Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM/high modified, Cytiva), acetonitrile (Chemlab, HPLC grade), (3-[4,5-dimethilthyazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT, Across Organics), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Fisher Scientific 99.9%), Tripan blue stain (gibco, 0.4%), lipopolysaccharide (LPS-EB, InvivoGen)), Griess reagent (Invitrogen).

Physicochemical characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the calcined zeolite samples were recorded using an Empyrean PANalytical diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (k = 1.5406 Å) in the range of 5–50° with a step size of 0.02° and a scanning speed of 0.1° s−1 in continuous mode. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were collected using a JEOL JEM 1400 (120 kV) microscope, and the TEM/STEM micrographs on a Talos F200X Fei-ThermoFischer Scientific (200 kV), equipped with a CCD chamber 4k × 4k and a chemical analysis system. Field emission scanning microscopy (FE-SEM) images were captured with a JEOL JSM-7900F (15 kV) instrument.

Thermogravimetric TG/DTG analyses were performed using a Netzsch STA 449 F3 Jupiter instrument (30–700 °C at 5 °C min−1 with an airflow of 100 mL min−1). The hydrothermal crystallization was performed in a Memmert furnace, and the two-step calcination was performed in a Carbolite tubular furnace. The first step was using a ramp of 1.8 °C min−1 up to 400 °C under a nitrogen flow; this temperature was maintained for 4 hours. In the second step, a ramp of 1.8 °C min−1 up to 550 °C under an air flow at 100 mL min−1 was maintained for 5 hours.

The textural properties were recorded by argon physisorption at −186 °C using a 3Flex Micromeritics analyzer with previous outgassing under a primary vacuum (300 °C for 2 hours) and an in situ outgassing treatment under a secondary vacuum (300 °C for 4 hours). The surface area was calculated using the Brunauer, Emmett & Teller equation (BET) in the relative pressure interval of 0.05–0.16. Also, the NLDFT model was applied to the adsorption branch to obtain the pore size distribution in the micro-, meso-, and macro-pore ranges of the zeolite samples, assuming cylindrical pores. The total pore volume (Vt) was calculated at a relative pressure of P/P0: 0.99, and the micropore volume (Vµ) was estimated from the NLDFT cumulative pore volume versus pore size data. The micropore surface area (Sµ) was calculated using a previously described procedure.42 The non-microporous surface area (SExt) and volume (VExt) were calculated as the difference between the total surface area or total pore volume minus the microporous surface area or micropore volume.

The gallium content of the calcined samples was determined by ICP-OES with a PerkinElmer Optima 7300 DV instrument. The particle size was monitored by dynamic light scattering (DLS), and zeta potential (ζ) was registered via electrophoretic light scattering (ELS) using a Zetasizer Advance (Malvern Panalytical) instrument with a concentration of 0.1 mg mL−1 at room temperature using an ultrasound tip (10% amplitude for 20 seconds; Digital Sonifier 450, Branson). The absorbance of the in vitro assays was measured with a microplate reader (OMEGA, BMG Labtech, absorbance λ: 570 nm for the MTT assay and λ: 548 nm for the anti-inflammatory assay).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were measured using a SPECS PHOIBOS 150 MCB spectrometer, with monochromatic Al Kα (1486.6 eV) radiation and a multichannel detector. The binding energies were referenced to the C 1s line at 284.6 eV.

Solid-state gallium NMR (71Ga) spectroscopy was performed on a Bruker Avance III/HD 500WB (Wide Bore, 11.7 T) spectrometer using a 4 mm double resonance probe (IH/X) at a resonance frequency of 152 MHz, referenced to an external 1 M aqueous solution of Ga(NO3)3.

The concentrations of Brønsted and Lewis acid sites (BASs and LASs, respectively) were determined using pyridine as a probe molecule, monitored by FTIR in a self-constructed system. Self-supported wafers (15 mg cm−2) were prepared and activated in a vacuum (10−4 mbar) at 480 °C for 4 hours before measurements. After activation, pyridine was introduced into the system at 150 °C, which was then sealed for 20 minutes.

Thermal desorption was conducted under high vacuum at increasing temperatures ranging from 150 to 450 °C, with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. A 20-minute equilibrium period was allowed before recording the spectrum at each selected temperature. The spectra were collected using a Jasco-4600 instrument equipped with a TGS detector, with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 128 scans. The integrated molar extinction coefficients used for quantifying the concentrations of BAS and LAS were obtained from Zholobenko et al. for the ZSM-5 zeolite, where ξBAS = 1.09 cm µmol−1 and ξLAS = 1.71 cm µmol−1, as d-Ga-MFI zeolites possess the same dendritic framework as ZSM-5.43

In vitro studies

Cell lines and cultures

The RAW 264.7 cells, derived from murine macrophages from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), were maintained in a DMEM-high modified medium without phenol red and sodium pyruvate supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 2 mM l-glutamine at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere.

Anti-inflammatory assay (NO production) and cell viability (MTT assay)

Nitric oxide (NO) production was measured using the Griess assay in RAW 264.7 cells, which were seeded at a concentration of 8 × 104 cells per well in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 2 mM l-glutamine in a 12-well plate. These plates were placed in a fully humidified incubator with 95% air and 5% CO2 for 24 hours.

After the incubation period, the cell culture media was removed, and 0.8 mL of DMEM supplemented with 1% FBS and 2 mM l-glutamine was added to each well. To this, a dispersion of 0.2 mL of the materials (d-Ga-MFI-XG2-5, or references n-Ga-MFI, MSN, and Ga(NO3)3) with a concentration of either 100 or 50 µg mL−1, was added to each well. Note, another experiment was performed with concentrations 100, 50, 20, and 10 µg mL−1 for d-Ga-MFI-X2-5. The treatments were previously sterilized using UV irradiation for 10 minutes. Following an additional hour of incubation, 2 µL of a 500× solution of LPS antigen was added, and the cells were incubated for another 24 hours. Untreated cells were used as a positive control of inflammation induction.

Likewise, after the incubation period, the Griess reagent was prepared using a kit designed for nitrite quantification (Invitrogen). For that, N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine dihydrochloride solution (1 mg mL−1) was mixed with an equal volume of a 10 mg mL−1 solution of sulfanilic acid in phosphoric acid to create the Griess reagent. In a 96-well plate, a mixture of 130 µL of deionized water, 150 µL of the sample supernatant, and 20 µL of the Griess reagent was added to each well. The mixture was incubated for 30 minutes in the absence of light. Then, the absorbance was measured with an OMEGA microplate reader at 548 nm. Each treatment was assessed using three replicates per experiment, and each experiment was conducted three times (n = 3).

To assess the viability of the cells in this experiment, an MTT assay was performed. After 24 hours of incubation with the treatment and LPS, 20 µL of MTT solution (10 mg mL−1) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 3 hours. The crystals of formazan produced were dissolved in DMSO, and the absorbance was measured with an OMEGA microplate reader at 570 nm.

Materials synthesis

The experimental procedure and conditions for synthesizing dendritic Ga-MFI zeolites were based on the previously reported seed-silanization procedure as described.3

Liquid gel synthesis route (LG)

Ga-MFI zeolites were prepared using a molar composition of 0.01Ga2O3 : 1SiO2 : 0.11TPAOH : 150H2O : 0.03X[OH] : 0.05 TPOAC, where X[OH]: TMAOH, TPAOH, or KOH. The reported molar composition did not include the additional 0.03X[OH] molar equivalents. However, the reaction without additional base did not progress due to a lowering of the pH of the mixture.

First, the gallium source (Ga(NO3)3) was mixed with H2O Milli-Q, tetrapropylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH, Sigma-Aldrich, 40 wt% in water), and the corresponding X[OH] at room temperature until Ga(NO3)3 was completely dissolved. Then, the solution was immersed in an ice bath, and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, Sigma-Aldrich, 98%) was added dropwise. The mixture was stirred for 40 hours to ensure the complete hydrolysis of the silicon source. The alcohols produced in the hydrolysis step were then removed in a rotatory evaporator at 75 mbar and 50 °C. Then, the solution was heated at 90 °C under reflux for 20 hours as a precrystallization step, and later the solution was submerged in an ice bath to stabilize the generated protozeolitic nanounits, and dimethyloctadecyl[3(trimethoxysilyl)propyl]ammonium chloride (TPOAC, Sigma-Aldrich, solution 42 wt% in methanol) was added. This silanization agent was added in a proportion of 5 mol% with respect to the Si content, and the solution was stirred for 6 hours at 0 °C. The mixture was eventually loaded into a Teflon-lined autoclave reactor (25 mL) and hydrothermally crystallized at 170 °C for 1 day. Thereafter, the autoclave was cooled in an ice bath, and the solid was recovered, washed three times with H2O, centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 15 minutes, and dried overnight at 100 °C. The resulting samples were identified as “d”-Ga-MFI-LX, where “d” is added if the sample has dendritic features, L represents the synthetic methodology route liquid-gel, and X corresponds to the number of the sample.

In the case of the sample prepared with TPAOH (d-Ga-MFI-L1), when the autoclave was open, it was separated into two clear phases. The upper phase was a white supernatant gel, and the lower one was a white solid. The yield for the lower phase, which contained the produced zeolite, was 72% with respect to the initial silica content. However the TMAOH- and KOH-derived samples (Ga-MFI-L2 and d-Ga-MFI-L3, respectively) were obtained in a single solid phase and with high yields of ca. 96% with respect to the initial silica content.

The synthesized samples underwent a two-step calcination treatment. The first step involved heating the sample to 400 °C at a rate of 1.8 °C per minute under a nitrogen flow (100 mL min−1) and maintaining this temperature for 4 hours. The second step involved heating the sample to 550 °C at 1.8 °C per minute under an air flow (100 mL min−1) and then maintaining this temperature for 5 hours.

Xerogel-1 synthesis route (XG1)

Ga-MFI zeolites were prepared using a molar composition of 0.01Ga2O3 : 1SiO2 : 0.11TPAOH : 15H2O : 0.03X[OH] : 0.07TPOAC, where X[OH] is TPAOH or KOH. Sample nomenclature was as follows: “d”-Ga-MFI-X1-Y, where “d” is added if the sample has dendritic features, X1 represents the synthetic methodology route xerogel-1, and Y corresponds to the number of the sample. In the case of XG1 samples, alkoxide hydrolysis, alcohol elimination, and precrystallization steps are maintained the same as in the LG route. Then, for the silanization step, TPOAC was added dropwise in a 7 mol% amount with respect to the Si content, and the solution was stirred for 6 hours at 0 °C. After that, the synthesis mixture was dried with the aid of a rotatory evaporator until complete dryness, and the resulting xerogels were ground. The samples were hydrothermally crystallized using an incipient wetness impregnation method, so that the solid was impregnated with TPAOH (20 wt% solution in water) in a 1 g XG : 1.2 g TPAOH solution proportion. Later, the mixture was loaded into a Teflon-lined autoclave reactor (25 mL) and heated at 170 °C for 1 day. After the hydrothermal crystallization, the autoclave was cooled down in an ice bath, and the solid was recuperated, washed thrice with H2O, centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 15 min, and dried overnight at 100 °C. After synthesis, the samples underwent a two-step calcination treatment similar to that described above for LG materials. The resulting d-Ga-MFI-X1-1 and d-Ga-MFI-X1-2 samples were obtained in 98% yield with respect to the initial silica content.

Xerogel-2 synthesis route (XG2)

The samples were prepared using a molar composition of 1SiO2 : 0.183TPAOH : 25H2O : 0.07TPOAC. Alkoxide hydrolysis, alcohol elimination, and precrystallization steps were maintained the same as in the LG route. Then, for the silanization step, TPOAC was added dropwise in a 7 mol% amount with respect to the Si content, and the solution was stirred for 6 hours at 0 °C. After that, the synthesis mixture was dried with the aid of a rotatory evaporator until complete dryness. Then, the samples were incipient wetness impregnated with an aqueous solution containing Ga(NO3)3 (in different molar ratios: Si/Ga: 15, 30, 50, or 60, Si content referred to as TEOS) and TPAOH (with different TPAOH concentrations: 10, 20, or 30% wt in water), and a ratio of 1 g XG : 1.2 g aqueous solution. The resulting samples are identified as “d”-Ga-MFI-X2-Y, where “d” is added if the material has dendritic features, X2 represents the synthetic methodology XG2, and Y represents the number of the sample. The samples were hydrothermally crystallized in a Teflon-lined autoclave reactor (25 mL) at 170 °C for 1 day. Then, the autoclave was cooled in an ice bath, and the solid was recovered, washed three times with H2O, centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 15 minutes, and dried overnight at 100 °C. After the synthesis, the samples were subjected to a two-step calcination treatment similar to that described for LG samples. The resulting samples were obtained with yields exceeding 96% with respect to the initial silica content (TEOS).

Reference n-Ga-MFI

The n-Ga-MFI zeolite was prepared using a molar composition of 1SiO2 : 0.11TPAOH : 15H2O. First, tetrapropylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH, Sigma-Aldrich, 40 wt% in water) was mixed with H2O Milli-Q. Then, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, Sigma-Aldrich, 98%) was added dropwise while the mixture was submerged in an ice bath. The mixture was stirred for 40 hours to ensure the complete hydrolysis of the silicon source. The alcohols produced in the hydrolysis step were then removed in a rotatory evaporator at 75 mbar and 50 °C. Then, the solution was heated at 90 °C under reflux for 20 hours. Later, 0.01 molar equivalents of Ga(NO3)3·H2O were dissolved in 0.03 molar equivalents of TPAOH 40 wt% in aqueous solution, and added to the mixture, which was loaded into a Teflon-lined autoclave reactor (25 mL) and hydrothermally crystallized at 170 °C for 7 days. After the hydrothermal crystallization, the autoclave was cooled in an ice bath, and the solid was recovered, washed three times with H2O, centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 15 minutes, and dried overnight at 100 °C. The resulting sample was obtained in two phases, with an 86% yield of the lower phase with respect to the initial silica content (TEOS). The synthesized sample underwent a two-step calcination treatment using a procedure similar to that described above for LG samples. After synthesis, the material was thoroughly characterized (Fig. S12).

Reference MSN

A literature procedure was followed to prepare MSNs as a reference material, with minor variations.37 Briefly, CTAB (160 mg, 0.44 mmol, 1 eq.) was emulsified in an ethanolic aqueous solution (68 mL of ultrapure water and 10 mL of EtOH). The pH was adjusted to 12 using 1 M NaOH (820 µL). Then, the mixture was heated at 82 °C under magnetic stirring, and TEOS was added (820 µL, 3.94 mmol, 9 eq.). The reaction was stopped after 2 h on ice. The material was collected by filtration, washed with ultrapure water and EtOH, and dried under vacuum overnight. The surfactant was removed by calcinating the sample at 550 °C for 5 hours under air flow (100 mL min−1), with a preheating ramp of 2 °C min−1. The sample was obtained in a 26% yield with respect to the initial silica content (TEOS). After synthesis, the material was thoroughly characterized (Fig. S13).
