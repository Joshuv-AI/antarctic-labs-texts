Human OPN-derived peptide SV prevents BPD model through interacting with KIF5B to repair mitochondrial damage and inhibit apoptosis
Authors: Ru Yan, Honglian Zhang, Pan Zhang, Mingyue Lv, Yuhan Pu, Lihui Yu, Weilai Jin, Le Zhang, Yahui Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBioactive components in breast milk offer a new therapeutic strategy for bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD). While osteopontin (OPN) is supplemented in formula to promote neonatal growth, the therapeutic role of its derived peptide, SVVYGLR (SV), in BPD remains unexplored. This study aimed to elucidate SV’s therapeutic mechanisms in BPD. The results revealed that SV significantly enhances cell viability, inhibits hyperoxia/LPS-induced apoptosis, and repairs alveolar epithelial injury. Molecularly, SV restores mitochondrial integrity, upregulates the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2, and downregulates the pro-apoptotic protein Bax. In vivo experiments showed SV reduced hyperoxia/LPS-triggered BPD-like injury, evidenced by neonatal mice gaining weight, improved lung histology, and increased alveolar counts and septal thickness. Preliminary mechanistic studies revealed that SV interacts with KIF5B and enhances its binding to mitochondria. In conclusion, SV improves BPD by repairing mitochondrial damage and inhibiting apoptosis via KIF5B interaction. This study identified new potential targets and therapeutic strategies for BPD management.Subject areas: Natural sciences, Molecular biology, Molecular mechanism of gene regulation, Cell biology

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) continues to be a great challenge in the context of neonatology.1,2 BPD predominantly impacts preterm neonates, especially those with low birth weights, and is often associated with life-saving measures such as oxygen therapy and mechanical ventilation.3,4 BPD may lead to short-term respiratory difficulties and exert profound and long-lasting impacts on the chronic health conditions of the affected infants, including chronic lung disease, developmental delays, and other systemic issues.5,6,7

Human breast milk is the primary nutritious substance for newborns, rich in proteins, exosomes, and peptides, which have been repeatedly reported to promote neonatal growth and development.8,9,10,11 Breastfeeding, compared to formula, has been shown to significantly reduce the risk of developing BPD.12,13,14 In previous studies by our research group, the exosomes present in breast milk were demonstrated to ameliorate the alveolar damage associated with BPD.15,16 However, whether proteins, which are the most abundant nutrients in colostrum, improve BPD remains unknown to date.

Proteins, after being digested by various proteases in the gastrointestinal tract of the human body, are often broken down into short-chain peptides, which perform the intended biological functions.17 Although there have been no previous reports specifically demonstrating that the peptides derived from human milk proteins improve BPD, several studies have demonstrated the antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and antimicrobial properties of human milk peptides.18,19,20 Notably, human milk is a rich source of osteopontin (OPN) protein, which is currently added to infant formula as a nutritional supplement to promote infant growth and development.21,22,23 OPN reportedly suppresses inflammation, and OPN-supplemented formulas are currently available to support infant development. It is plausible that OPN is a crucial component of human milk in the context of preventing and managing BPD.24 A fragment of this protein, named SV, reportedly possesses strong tissue repair25 capabilities. Previous studies have confirmed the presence of this SV fragment in digested gastric fluid.26,27,28 This raises the following question: could this SV fragment be the effective component of human milk in the context of improving BPD?

In this study, SV was demonstrated to inhibit apoptosis in a BPD model and improve the AT cell damage induced by hyperoxia or LPS. Additionally, SV increased the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), which is a key protein involved in alveolar-vascular development. An in vivo investigation further demonstrated that SV improved BPD-like pathological damage. It was revealed that the key binding protein of SV is KIF5B. SV promoted the interaction between KIF5B and mitochondria and repaired mitochondrial damage. These findings are expected to offer novel theoretical insights into the contribution of human milk peptides to improving BPD. The findings may also offer novel approaches for the treatment of BPD.

Results

Basic characterization of SV peptides

OPN is a glycosylated protein present in breast milk. It is considered important as an immunoreactive protein. Its amino acid sequence 162–168 comprises a functional structural domain that has been shown to promote vascular development (Figure 1A). In this study, a permeable cellular peptide was synthesized for this sequence and termed SV. Bioinformatics analysis revealed an instability index of 13.19 and strong lipophilic activity for this SV (Figure 1B). In addition, fluorescence microscopy revealed that the GFP-tagged SV was efficiently internalized into the alveolar epithelial cells within 3 h (Figure 1C). The in vivo visualization demonstrated that the GFP-conjugated SV could effectively target multiple organs, notably the liver, lungs, and small intestine, in the neonatal subjects within a 6-h time frame after intraperitoneal administration (Figure 1D). This finding formed the basis for examining the biological functions of SV within distinct organ systems and cellular populations.

SV repairs LPS-induced cell damage in vitro may rely on the inhibition of apoptosis

It was observed that SV had a restorative effect on cellular damage in the LPS-induced model. This observation prompted an inquiry into the feasibility of the use of SV as a potential therapeutic option for managing BPD. Therefore, a CCK8 assay was performed, which showed that SV enhanced the reduction in cell viability elicited by LPS (Figure 2A). In order to eliminate experimental non-specificity, a scrambled peptide with an amino acid composition identical to that of SV but with a different sequence was prepared and designated as Src-SV. This Scr-SV was unable to enhance the decrease in cell viability caused by LPS (Figure S1A). Next, the effect of SV administration on oxidative stress in alveolar epithelial cells was evaluated. The findings revealed that exposure to LPS increased the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in these cells, whereas SV treatment mitigated the increase in ROS (Figure 2B). Flow cytometric analysis indicated that SV administration also decreased the LPS-induced apoptosis (Figure 2C). Bcl-2 and Bax are apoptosis-inhibiting and apoptosis-promoting genes, respectively. It was found that SV intervention increased Bcl-2 mRNA/protein levels and decreased Bax mRNA/protein levels (Figures 2D and 2E). Furthermore, the expression levels of surfactant protein-C (SPC), an indicator of alveolar type II epithelial cells, were assessed. It was observed that SV intervention reversed the downregulation of SPC expression induced by LPS (Figures 2D and 2F), while reducing the expressions of the inflammatory cytokines interleukin IL-1β and IL-6 in the BPD model (Figure 2D). Finally, it was found that SV also increased the expression levels of VEGFA (Figure 2F). Together, these results suggested that SV can repair the LPS-induced injury to alveolar epithelial cells.

SV repairs hyperoxia-induced cell damage in vitro may rely on the inhibition of apoptosis

In order to examine the function of SV in BPD, the SV peptide was administered to the MLE-12 cells subjected to hyperoxic conditions. It was found that SV increased cell viability (Figure 3A), whereas Scr-SV did not produce this effect (Figure S1B). Hyperoxia exposure increased the cellular ROS damage, and SV intervention could reduce ROS levels and thereby decrease this cellular damage (Figure 3B). Flow cytometry revealed that SV inhibited hyperoxia-induced apoptosis (Figure 3C). The results revealed that in the hyperoxia group, Bcl-2 expression was markedly decreased, whereas Bax expression was notably increased. In contrast, compared to the hyperoxia group, the SV+hyperoxia group presented significant upregulation of Bcl-2 expression and downregulation of Bax expression (Figures 3D and 3E). It was found that SV reduced the expressions of the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6 in the hyperoxia-induced BPD model (Figure 3D). SV also reversed the hyperoxia-induced damage to the AT2 cell markers SPC and VEGFA (Figures 3D and 3F). These results further demonstrated the capacity of SV to suppress apoptotic processes in the alveolar epithelial cells exposed to hyperoxia.

SV improves the LPS-induced BPD animal model

In order to further validate the roles of SV in vivo, an LPS-induced BPD animal model was established. The experimental design consisted of three groups: the control, LPS, and LPS+SV groups. The daily pup body weights were recorded. On postnatal day 7, neonates were euthanized for pulmonary tissue harvesting. The offspring in the LPS-treated group presented decreased body weight, whereas those in the SV-administered group presented a significant increase in weight, approximating the levels observed in the normal control group (Figure 4A). Gross examination of the lung tissues revealed marked atelectasis and volume reduction in the LPS-induced group compared to the remaining two groups. (Figure 4B). H&E staining demonstrated marked enhancement in lung architecture in the LPS+SV group, accompanied by an augmentation in alveolar count and a reduction in alveolar septal thickness (Figure 4C). TUNEL staining and Ki67 staining revealed that SV could rescue the cells from LPS-induced apoptosis and promote cell proliferation (Figure 4D). Furthermore, the findings indicated that SV can restore the expression levels of Bcl-2, SPC, and VEGF-A, while concurrently decreasing the expressions of Bax, IL-1β, and IL-6 in an LPS-induced animal model (Figures 4E–4G). These findings suggested that SV can rescue LPS-induced BPD injury in vivo.

SV improves the hyperoxia-induced BPD animal model

In order to investigate the role of SV in BPD, a BPD animal model was induced by exposing the newborn pups to 85% hyperoxia. Neonatal standard deviation (SD) rats were treated from birth and stratified into three groups: the normoxia control group, hyperoxia group, and hyperoxia+SV intervention group. The daily pup weights were recorded. On postnatal day 7, the pups were euthanized, and their lung tissues were collected for analysis. The findings indicated a decrease in pup weight in the hyperoxia group, whereas the weights were restored in the SV group (Figure 5A). Compared to those in the other two groups, the lung tissue diameter in the hyperoxia group was smaller, and the lung tissues in the hyperoxia group exhibited contraction and a reduced volume (Figure 5B). H&E staining revealed notable amelioration of the lung architecture in the hyperoxia+SV cohort, with augmentation of the alveolar count and a reduction in the alveolar septal thickness (Figure 5C). TUNEL staining and Ki67 staining revealed that SV could rescue hyperoxia-induced apoptosis and promote cell proliferation (Figure 5D). In addition, SV could restore the expression levels of Bcl-2, SPC, and VEGFA while concurrently decreasing the expressions of Bax, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the hyperoxic animal model (Figures 5E–5G). These findings suggested that SV can rescue hyperoxia-induced BPD injury in vivo.

Interaction of SV and KIF5B

In order to explore the mechanism of action of SV, its effect on mitophagy was evaluated. Elevated levels of P62 and LC3Ⅱ/LC3Ⅰ were observed in the LPS- and hyperoxia-induced BPD models; however, the addition of SV failed to induce any improvement (Figures S2A and S2B). Subsequently, pull-down assays were conducted coupled with mass spectrometry, which identified KIF5B as a robust interacting protein of SV (Figures 6A and 6B). Western blotting verified that SV and KIF5B bind in reverse and that SV combined with KIF5B but did not increase the protein levels of KIF5B under normal conditions (Figure 6C). However, it was confirmed that the interventions involving SV, both in vivo and in vitro, reversed the decrease in the KIF5B expression induced by LPS or hyperoxia. These findings indicated a reduction in the mRNA and protein expression levels of KIF5B in both LPS-treated and hyperoxia groups, and these effects were reversed upon SV intervention (Figures 6D and 6E). These findings suggested that SV may rescue from BPD through interaction with KIF5B.

SV promotes the binding of KIF5B and mitochondria

In order to investigate the mechanism underlying the interaction between SV and KIF5B, the expression of KIF5B was silenced using siRNA (Figure 7A), followed by western blotting. It was found that silencing KIF5B and then adding SV did not restore the expression of KIF5B (Figure 7B). Previous results have shown that SV inhibits ROS and modulates BAX/BCL2 levels, thereby increasing the degree of apoptosis, which occurs through a mitochondria-dependent pathway. Additionally, the JC-1 assay results indicated that SV improved the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) (Figure 7C), confirming that SV can repair mitochondrial damage and inhibit apoptosis. Morphological analysis revealed that SV alleviated mitochondrial damage in cells (Figure 7D). Furthermore, KIF5B is a transport protein that binds to the mitochondria and facilitates the transport of damaged mitochondria, thereby improving mitochondrial function. The results of this study show that SV promotes the interaction between KIF5B and cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COXIV) (Figure 7E). A specific red fluorescent probe named mitoTracker Red was used to label mitochondria, and it was observed that SV promoted the binding of KIF5B to the mitochondria (Figure 7F). These results indicated that SV may induce the binding of KIF5B with mitochondria to improve the degree of damage caused to mitochondria and inhibit the apoptosis caused by cell injury.

Discussion

BPD remains a major challenge in neonatal care, particularly among preterm infants, who require respiratory support through mechanical ventilation and supplemental oxygen therapy.29 Despite the availability of various treatments, including corticosteroids and antioxidants, the therapeutic options for BPD remain limited to date, and the long-term outcomes among the affected children often include impaired pulmonary function and growth.30,31 Given the essential contributions of oxidative stress, apoptosis, and mitochondrial impairment to the pathogenesis of BPD,24,32,33 innovative approaches targeting these cellular processes are urgently needed. In this context, this study presents a promising candidate, SV, a bioactive peptide derived from OPN,26 which has been shown to protect cells under conditions of hyperoxia and LPS-induced inflammation, the two key features of BPD.

One of the major findings of this study is that SV improved cell viability and inhibited apoptosis in both hyperoxic and LPS-treated models. Apoptosis is a key mechanism in BPD, as exposure to excessive oxygen or inflammatory mediators results in the activation of the apoptotic pathways, resulting in the depletion of alveolar epithelial cells and impaired lung development.34,35,36 The findings of this study indicate that SV markedly attenuates cellular necrosis through the modulation of vital apoptotic modulators, namely, BCL-2 and Bax. Notably, the anti-apoptotic factor BCL-2 is pivotal in preserving mitochondrial membrane stability and guaranteeing cellular survival under stressful circumstances, whereas Bax induces the permeabilization of the mitochondrial outer membrane, leading to the exocytosis of the apoptotic-inducing factor.37,38,39 The increase in the BCL-2 expression and suppression of Bax levels by SV appear to protect the alveolar epithelial cells from hyperoxia and inflammatory injury, providing a potential therapeutic avenue for alleviating BPD.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is one of the earliest events in the pathophysiology of BPD, and exposure to hyperoxia and inflammation contributes to oxidative tissue injury.40,41,42 In addition to its impact on apoptosis, SV was observed to play a notable role in mitochondrial protection in this study. Autophagic capacity is notably compromised in the lung tissues of BPD model animals. However, in hyperoxia-induced BPD models, autophagy enhancers effectively alleviate apoptosis and reverse disrupted alveolar development.43 Therefore, in this study, experiments were conducted to assess the effect of SV on mitophagy. It was found that SV failed to improve autophagic function in the BPD model. KIF5B is a motor protein that plays a crucial role in regulating mitochondrial transport and positioning, particularly in the clearance of damaged mitochondria. Damaged mitochondria are transported out of the cell through binding to KIF5B, and this process maintains cellular energy metabolism homeostasis.44,45 This study revealed that SV restores mitochondrial function by enhancing the binding of KIF5B to mitochondria, specifically to the COXIV. The detection of this interaction suggests that SV may promote mitochondrial stability and function, which are essential for sustaining the cellular energetic balance and mitigating apoptotic processes, through KIF5B.46 Given the centrality of mitochondrial dysfunction in BPD,47,48 this mechanism offers a novel and valuable target for therapeutic intervention.

These in vivo findings of this study provide additional evidence to substantiate the therapeutic potential of SV in the treatment of BPD. Neonatal mice subjected to hyperoxia or LPS-induced lung injury exhibited significant improvements in body weight, lung structure, and alveolar development following SV treatment. The H&E staining results revealed better preserved lung tissue architecture, with increased alveolar number and septation, two hallmarks of healthy lung development.49,50 These findings are aligned with the experimental evidence obtained in vitro, suggesting that SV not only protects lung cells from injury but also facilitates the restoration and regeneration of lung tissue in an environment mimicking BPD.

Limitations of the study

Our study also has several limitations. First, the exact molecular interactions between SV and KIF5B must be elucidated through further investigation, including the determination of whether SV binds to KIF5B directly or modulates the activity of the latter through indirect mechanisms. Additionally, the pathophysiology of BPD in humans may not be fully replicated by using SD neonatal mice, and the broader impact of SV on lung development and function in preterm infants requires further investigation using clinical or preclinical models. Although the effects of SV on apoptosis and mitochondrial function are compelling, the impact of SV on pulmonary arterial hypertension or neurodevelopmental outcomes requires studies with long-term follow-up periods. Additionally, further studies are needed to compare the efficacy of SV to that of other OPN-derived peptides or mitochondrial-targeted therapies. Finally, the probable interactive outcomes of SV used in conjunction with alternative therapeutic interventions, such as corticosteroids or antioxidants, warrant exploration, as a multi-target approach may further improve the therapeutic outcomes. Our team will conduct further research in these areas.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

MLE-12 cells were procured from the SUNNCELL (https://www.sunncell.com.cn) and used for in vitro experiments. The cells were cultured in the 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA) inside an incubator. The control cohort was subjected to normoxic conditions (21% oxygen), whereas the hyperoxia cohort was exposed to 85% oxygen. The peptide was administered at a dose of 100 μM, and LPS was administered at a concentration of 100 ng/mL. MLE-12 cell line was authenticated as detailed in the supplemental information (Data S1), and it was confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Experimental animals

Wild-type Sprague-Dawley (SD) pregnant mice were procured from the Animal Center of Jiangnan University. In this study, SD pups were fed for 7 days after birth without sex distinction. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), Jiangnan University (permit WXCH2022-12-080, 2022.12.20).

BPD animal model induced by LPS

Neonatal SD rats and their dams were maintained in a normoxic environment postnatally for 7 days. BPD models were induced by daily intraperitoneal injections of LPS for 7 days. The experimental design comprised three groups: the control, LPS, and LPS+SV groups. The LPS concentration was 500 μg/kg, and the SV intervention concentration was 200 μg/kg. Initially, a previously reported LPS dosage of 1 mg/mL was used for injection into neonatal SD rats.25 However, a high mortality rate was observed among the rats, and, therefore, the protocol was modified, and 500 μg/kg LPS was administered starting from the first day of birth for the next 7 consecutive days. This modified approach successfully induced the model, and the pathological features were consistent with those of BPD.

BPD animal model induced by hyperoxia

In accordance with the BPD modeling method previously used by our research group,16 the newborn SD rats were placed inside a closed transparent box for 7 days after birth along with their mothers. BPD models were established through the continuous administration of 85% oxygen. The control cohort was exposed to room air within the same environment. In order to mitigate the effects of high oxygen concentration on female rats, these rats were switched every day between the control group and the model group. Hyperoxia-exposed pups were divided into two subgroups: one received saline, and the other received SV at 200 μg/kg every day.

Method details

Basic characteristics of SV

The sequence of OPN and the physicochemical characteristic data of SV were obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/).

Peptide tracer experiment (in vitro experiment)

The peptide labeled with green fluorescent protein (GFP) was purchased from Shanghai Kopeptide Biotechnology Co. Ltd. MLE-12 cells were seeded into the wells of a 6-well plate and allowed to adhere for 24 h. The cells were subsequently treated with a GFP-labeled peptide and incubated for 6 h. A chemical system (Tanon, China) was used to monitor the internalization of fluorescent peptide into cells.

Cell viability assay

MLE-12 cell proliferation was assessed using a CCK8 test kit (Meilunbio, China). The cells were seeded into the wells of 96-well microplates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. Next, the cells were exposed to SV for 0, 24, 48, or 72 h. The absorbance values at 450 nm were then measured using a microplate spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA).

Apoptosis

Apoptosis was evaluated using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme, China). A cell suspension (1-5 × 105 cells) was harvested and centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were washed twice with chilled PBS and resuspended in 100 μL of 1x binding buffer. Annexin V (5 μL) and propidium iodide (PI, 5 μL) were added, and the mixture was gently mixed. The cells were then incubated in the dark at RT for 10 min, followed by the addition of 400 μL of 1x binding buffer. The prepared samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, USA). The results were analyzed using FlowJo version 10.8.1.

Oxidative stress

The ROS assay kit (Beyotime, China) was used for obtaining the oxidative stress index. MLE-12 cells were exposed to LPS or hyperoxia and then incubated with or without specific peptides for 24 h. DCFH-DA was adjusted to 10 mM. The cell isolates were reconstituted in DCFH-DA solution and adjusted to a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. The cell suspension was then incubated for 40 min. Subsequent triple-wash cycles with serum-free medium ensured depletion of the extracellular fluorescent probe. The experimental cohorts were stratified into positive control and untreated groups. ROS generation in the cells was quantified using flow cytometry.

Western blotting

The protein expression levels of the model-related genes were confirmed through immunoblotting. SDS-PAGE electrophoresis was performed using 30 μg of the protein lysate. Proteins were then transferred to a PVDF membrane, which was blocked with 5% milk and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies at the specified dilutions: Bax (Proteintech, 1:1000), Bcl-2 (Proteintech, 1:1000), SPC (Proteintech, 1:1000), GAPDH (Proteintech, 1:1000), VEGFA (Affinit, 1:1000), and KIF5B (CST, 1:1000). The membrane was subsequently exposed to the corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody (Sigma, 1:20000) for 1 h at ambient temperature. The target proteins were ultimately detected using a chemiluminescent kit (Western ECL substrate supplied by Bio-Rad) in conjunction with a ChemiDoc imaging system (Tanon, China). The antibodies employed in this investigation are presented in key resources table.

Peptide-protein pull-down assay

Streptavidin magnetic beads (Beyotime, China) were diluted with RIPA lysis buffer, and the mixture was placed on a magnetic rack. The resulting supernatant was discarded, and the above steps were repeated three times. Biotin-labeled Scr-SV or SV diluted to 1 mg/mL with RIPA buffer was added to the above magnetic beads, after which the mixture was placed on a rocking table at 4 °C overnight. On Day 2, the bead-peptide complexes were removed and placed on a magnetic rack. The supernatant was discarded, followed by replenishing with fresh RIPA lysate and repositioning on a magnetic stand. This step was repeated six times. After the final supernatant was discarded, the MLE-12 cell protein was added, and the mixture was incubated on a rocking table at 4 °C overnight. On Day 3, the bead-peptide-protein complex was isolated using a magnetic rack, the supernatant was discarded, and the RIPA wash was repeated six times. Following the final removal of the supernatant, SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer was added, followed by mixing and incubation in a metal bath at 95 °C for 5 min. The mixture was stored at −80 °C until use.

Silver staining

A silver dyeing kit (Beyotime, China) was used for silver staining. After electrophoresis, the gel was fixed in 100 mL of the fixing solution under agitation (RT, 3–4 h). Afterward, sequential washes were performed with 30% ethanol (15 min), ddH2O (200 mL, 15 min), silver sensitizing solution (100 mL, 5 min), and two ddH2O rinses (200 mL each). Silver staining was conducted with 100 mL of 1X silver stain solution (15 min, RT), followed by brief ddH2O washes. Development in 100 mL of silver nitrate developer was performed until band visualization (3–10 min), followed by termination using the stop solution (10 min). The final preservation step involved fresh ddH2O washes (100 mL, 10 min) and aqueous storage for mass spectrometry analysis.

Immunofluorescence

The samples were fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Then, permeabilization was induced through treatment with 0.2% Triton X-100. The samples were blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h at RT and then rinsed with PBS, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. On Day 2, the cell samples were washed three times with PBS and incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies. In order to stain with the fluorescent dye, when the cells reached the desired confluency, the medium was removed from the dish, and prewarmed (37°C) staining solution containing the MitoTracker probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added. Then, the cells were incubated in a 37°C incubator for 25 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium. The abovementioned immunofluorescence operation steps were subsequently carried out. The stained sections were visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Tracing in vivo

Neonatal animals received intraperitoneal injection of GFP-labeled SV, with a PBS-injected control group. Organs were harvested for imaging analysis using the Tanon ChemiDoc system.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

On Day 7, the rats were euthanized, and their lung tissues were harvested. Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then sectioned into three segments. Apical to basal 3 μm sections were stained with H&E. IHC analysis was conducted on the 4 μm lung tissue sections following deparaffinization. Antigen retrieval was performed by autoclaving with 10 mM citrate buffer (10 min). Endogenous peroxidase activity was inactivated through incubation with 0.5% H2O2/methanol (15 min), followed by blocking with 1.5% rabbit serum (30 min). The samples were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies, including goat anti-mouse Ki67 (CST, 1:100), and then subjected to TUNEL staining at RT for 1h. Then, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies and stained with diaminobenzidine. Finally, the samples were scanned using an Aperio scanner. The mean linear intercept (MLI) and mean alveolar number (MAN) of the lung tissue were determined as described previously.51 The MLI, which is an index for assessing the interalveolar septal distance, was determined using light microscopy at 200× magnification. For each sample, MLI was computed for 6 randomly selected fields using a cross-line grid. The MLI was then calculated by dividing the cumulative length of the cross-line by the count of alveolar septa intersecting the test lines. Next, MAN, a metric reflecting alveolar density, was calculated for each sample by counting the alveoli in 6 randomly chosen fields and dividing this count by the respective field area.43,51

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNA was isolated using an RNA purification kit (EZB, USA) and quantified using a Nanodrop2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cDNA from the extracted RNA was synthesized using a cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme, China) and amplified using the SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) on a SLAN-96S system (HONGSHI, China). RNA interference

MLE12 cells were seeded in the wells of 6-well plates in 2 mL of FBS-supplemented RPMI-1640 (antibiotic-free) and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h until 60%–70% confluency was reached. The siRNA and Lipofectamine 3000 were diluted in Opti-MEM and incubated for 5 min at RT. The transfection reagent and siRNA mixture were combined and mixed gently, followed by incubation at RT for 20 min. The transfection mixture was then added to a 6-well culture plate. The cell culture plates were transferred to an incubator for 4–6 h. Then, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and the culture was maintained for 24–48 h. The sequences of the siRNAs targeting KIF5B are listed below:

KIF5B siRNA sense: CCAAGAAUAGUGCAAGAUAUUTT; KIF5B siRNA antisense: AAUAUCUUGCACUAUUCUUGGTT.

JC-1 staining

The mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was assessed using a JC-1 staining assay kit (Beyotime, China). MLE cells were harvested, incubated with JC-1 for 20 min, rinsed, and analyzed using flow cytometry. When the MMP is elevated, JC-1 accumulates within the mitochondrial matrix as aggregates that emit red fluorescence at the excitation and emission maxima of 585 nm and 590 nm, respectively. On the other hand, when MMP is decreased, JC-1 accumulates within the mitochondrial matrix as monomeric species that emit green fluorescence at the excitation and emission maxima of 514 nm and 529 nm, respectively.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Samples on copper grids (Zhong Jing Ke Yi, Beijing) were dried using filter paper to remove excess liquid. Subsequently, 15 μL of 2% uranyl acetate staining solution was applied to the samples, which were subsequently incubated for 1 min at room temperature. After staining, the excess solution on the copper grids was blotted off using filter paper. The stained samples were then either air-dried or dried under a lamp for 10 min. Finally, the samples were examined and imaged under a transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Tecnai, USA).

Quantification and statistical analysis

ImageJ software was used to analyze the western blotting results, and FlowJo software (version 10.8.1) was utilized for the analysis of flow cytometry data. The data were analyzed statistically using GraphPad Prism 10 software and presented as means ±standard deviations (SDs). Two-tailed Student’s t tests were used to determine significant differences between two groups. Furthermore, for multiple comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. p < 0.05 was considered significant.
