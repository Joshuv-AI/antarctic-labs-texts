Adrenal hemorrhage mimicking renal bleed: A rare path to infective endocarditis
Authors: Kewen Zheng, Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous adrenal hemorrhage (SAH) is exceedingly rare and diagnostically challenging, often mimicking renal pathology. Its association with infective endocarditis (IE) remains poorly characterized. We report a case of SAH initially misdiagnosed as renal hemorrhage, which unveiled occult, culture-negative infective endocarditis (IE). A 36-year-old woman presented with acute flank pain, anemia, and coagulopathy. Initial CT suggested a perirenal hematoma. Renal angiography was non-diagnostic, but exploration of adjacent vasculature revealed active bleeding from the adrenal artery, managed with embolization. Subsequent clinical deterioration with neurological deficits led to the discovery of multifocal septic emboli and mitral valve vegetation, confirming IE per Modified Duke Criteria despite negative blood cultures. This case highlights two key points: 1) the limitation of cross-sectional imaging in definitively distinguishing adrenal from renal hemorrhage, and 2) the critical importance of angiographic exploration beyond the initially suspected site when clinical and imaging findings are discordant. Unexplained hemorrhage with persistent coagulopathy should prompt investigation for occult IE, even in the absence of classic signs or positive cultures.

Introduction

Spontaneous adrenal hemorrhage (SAH) is an uncommon clinical entity, often associated with trauma, anticoagulation, or underlying neoplasms. Its presentation as acute flank pain can lead to diagnostic confusion with more common conditions like renal hemorrhage. While modern imaging techniques like CT are invaluable, differentiating an adrenal from a renal source of bleeding can sometimes be challenging, particularly with large hematomas that obscure anatomical planes [1]. Infective endocarditis (IE) is a serious infection that may present with atypical manifestations. Although systemic embolization is a well-known complication, SAH as the presenting feature of IE is exceptionally rare. This report describes a case where SAH masquerading as renal hemorrhage was the herald sign of occult, culture-negative IE, emphasizing the diagnostic pitfalls and the need for a systematic approach to unexplained coagulopathic hemorrhage.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 36-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with acute-onset right flank pain. She reported a one-month history of intermittent cough accompanied by low-grade fever, for which she had received several incomplete courses of oral antibiotics (amoxicillin) from a local clinic. On admission, her vital signs were: blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, heart rate 80 bpm, temperature 37.4°C. Physical examination revealed right flank tenderness but no Grey-Turner's or Cullen's signs. Cardiopulmonary auscultation revealed a soft, early systolic murmur at the apex, not initially considered significant. Neurological examination was normal.

Initial laboratory investigations (Table 1) showed anemia, leukocytosis with neutrophilia, thrombocytopenia, and significantly elevated D-dimer and C-reactive protein (CRP). Coagulation studies indicated a consumptive coagulopathy. Non-contrast and contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated a large (10.5 ×12.7 cm) heterogeneous retroperitoneal mass adjacent to the upper pole of the right kidney, initially interpreted as a likely perirenal hematoma of renal origin (Fig. 1). Given the concern for active bleeding, the patient underwent digital subtraction angiography. Selective right renal arteriography showed no evidence of contrast extravasation or vascular malformation. Subsequently, angiography of the right inferior phrenic artery revealed active extravasation of contrast medium from a branch supplying the ipsilateral adrenal gland, confirming SAH (Fig. 2). Post-procedurally, the patient's hemoglobin stabilized, but coagulopathy and low-grade fever persisted.

Comprehensive endocrine evaluation, including plasma-free metanephrines, cortisol, and aldosterone, yielded normal results, making a functional adrenal tumour less likely, though this does not exclude a non-secreting adrenal tumour.

Blood cultures drawn from two separate sites at admission remained sterile after 5 days of incubation. Furthermore, evaluations of the immune system, including tests for anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), rheumatoid factor (RA), and anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), returned negative results, thereby ruling out the possibility of autoimmune disorders or systemic vasculitis. The patient was administered plasma supplementation along with prophylactic intravenous antibiotic therapy(ceftriaxone).

On hospital day 10, the patient developed acute dysphoria, followed by right-sided hemiplegia and aphasia accompanied by a low-grade fever, ranging between 37.8 and 38.3°C. Imaging studies, including a brain CT scan followed by a cranial MRI, identified multifocal infarcts in the frontal, parietal, and cerebellar areas (Fig. 3 A, B, C. Transthoracic echocardiography then demonstrated a large (19 mm x 8 mm), mobile vegetation on the mitral valve (Fig. 3D). The patient underwent urgent mitral valve replacement. Histopathological examination of the excised valve showed vegetations composed of fibrin, platelets, and neutrophils, consistent with IE, though Gram and fungal stains were negative. Postoperatively, she received a 4-week course of intravenous ceftriaxone and gentamicin, followed by a 2-week course of oral amoxicillin. Her neurological function improved gradually, and she was discharged on postoperative day 23. At a 3-month follow-up, she was clinically well with no signs of adrenal insufficiency. The clinical priority was the management and monitoring of her cardiac and neurological status post-valve surgery. In the absence of symptoms suggestive of recurrent bleeding or adrenal crisis, and given the invasive nature of her recent procedures, dedicated follow-up imaging for the adrenal hemorrhage was deferred, relying instead on clinical assessment. We acknowledge the lack of imaging confirmation of resolution as a limitation of this report.

Discussion

Spontaneous renal hemorrhage, adrenal hemorrhage, and infective endocarditis each present significant clinical challenges. Their concurrent occurrence creates complex diagnostic dilemmas due to overlapping symptoms, which can lead to misdiagnosis and delayed treatment.Spontaneous renal hemorrhage typically results from underlying conditions such as hypertension or renal tumors. Adrenal hemorrhage is often caused by trauma, neoplasms, or anticoagulant therapy and can precipitate adrenal insufficiency if not managed promptly. It has a reported incidence of 0.14–1.1%, with a predilection for the right gland [3]. When these hemorrhagic events coexist with infective endocarditis—a bacterial infection of the heart valves—clinical assessment becomes particularly difficult. Although infrequent, the co-occurrence of these conditions is a serious concern due to associated morbidity and mortality. This is especially true for patients with pre-existing cardiac conditions or recent surgical interventions, underscoring the need for heightened clinical awareness.

This case illustrates a rare and diagnostically challenging sequence where SAH was the initial manifestation of occult IE. The primary diagnostic hurdle was the misinterpretation of the hemorrhage's origin on initial CT. While CT is excellent for detecting retroperitoneal blood, distinguishing between adrenal and renal sources can be difficult when a large hematoma distorts anatomy or when the adrenal gland itself is not clearly visualized due to the bleed [1]. In our case, retrospective analysis of the initial CT shows that while the sagittal view (Fig. 1C) suggested a suprarenal location, the axial and coronal views demonstrated the hematoma's intimate proximity to the upper renal pole, and the adrenal gland was not visualized as a separate structure due to mass effect from the bleed. This led to the presumptive diagnosis of renal hemorrhage, a more common entity. Similar misclassification of adrenal hemorrhage as renal or perirenal pathology has been reported in the literature [1]. This underscores the importance of considering SAH in the differential diagnosis of spontaneous retroperitoneal hemorrhage, even when imaging seems to point elsewhere.

The subsequent angiographic evaluation was pivotal. The initial renal arteriogram was negative, which could have prematurely closed the diagnostic loop. However, proactive exploration of adjacent vascular territories, specifically the inferior phrenic artery supplying the adrenal gland, identified the true source. This step highlights a critical learning point: in the context of ongoing hemorrhage with a non-diagnostic angiogram of the suspected primary organ, systematic evaluation of surrounding vasculature is essential [4].

The association between SAH and IE is extraordinarily rare. The pathophysiology likely involves septic microembolization to the adrenal gland vasculature. In IE, septic emboli can cause focal arteritis, vessel wall necrosis, and the formation of micro-abscesses, weakening the arterial wall and predisposing it to rupture, even in the absence of a radiologically detectable mycotic aneurysm [5]. This microvascular injury, combined with the consumptive coagulopathy often seen in IE (as evidenced by our patient's elevated D-dimer and low fibrinogen), may create a 'two-hit' scenario leading to hemorrhage [5]. Although the overall incidence of mycotic aneurysms in IE patients is low (approximately 1.9%), and none was identified in our patient, this data supports the concept of systemic septic embolization in IE [6], [7]. Notably, intra-abdominal arteries are among the principal locations for extracranial mycotic aneurysms [6], [7]. This provides context for understanding a potential mechanism for IE-associated SAH: septic emboli may occlude the microvasculature of the adrenal gland (akin to the microvascular process in mycotic aneurysm formation), inciting focal arteritis and necrosis without necessarily forming a radiologically apparent aneurysm [7].

Infective endocarditis constitutes a critical condition marked by high morbidity and mortality rates, which can be further aggravated by delayed diagnosis. Inappropriate and sporadic antibiotic usage may correlate with unfavorable outcomes and misinterpretation of blood culture results.The majority of ischemic strokes associated with infective endocarditis are typically localized in the middle cerebral artery territory, featuring cortical branch infarctions, as illustrated in this case [2]. The next most prevalent finding includes numerous embolic lesions, both large and small, distributed across various vascular territories [2]. Acute encephalopathy is believed to arise from multifocal ischemia of the brain due to multiple small emboli. Currently, approximately half of the patients diagnosed with this condition do not have a known history of an underlying cardiac disease or intervention as this case reported. Blood culture-negative infectious endocarditis constitutes approximately 2.5–31.0% of all cases of infectious endocarditis [2].

Our patient met the 2023 ISCVID Modified Duke Criteria for "Definite" culture-negative IE, based on one major criterion (echocardiographic vegetation) and multiple minor criteria (fever, vascular emboli, and suggestive histological findings on valve pathology [2]. In this case, the negative blood cultures were almost certainly due to the prolonged, intermittent antibiotic therapy in the month preceding admission [8], [9]. This case reinforces that culture-negative IE remains a significant diagnostic challenge and that empirical antibiotic use prior to diagnosis can obscure microbiological confirmation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case underscores two critical clinical lessons: first, the inherent limitation of cross-sectional imaging in definitively localizing the source of retroperitoneal hemorrhage, necessitating a high index of suspicion for SAH even when initial findings suggest a renal origin. Second, it highlights that unexplained spontaneous hemorrhage accompanied by persistent coagulopathy should prompt investigation for occult infective endocarditis, including echocardiography, even in the absence of classic signs or positive blood cultures, particularly if there is a history of pre-procedural antibiotic use. A systematic angiographic approach, exploring beyond the initially suspected site, is essential when clinical and imaging findings are discordant.
