Finite Element Analysis of Implant‐Assisted Mandibular Distal Extension Removable Partial Denture: Evaluating Stress Distribution in Premolar Versus Molar Implant Positions—An In‐Silico Study
Authors: Dipayan Bhattacharya, Subhabrata Maiti, Ponnanna A.A, Pankaj Gandhi, Artak Heboyan, Narek Zakaryan, Muralidhar Gopalkrishna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimThis study aimed to compare the influence of implant support on mandibular distal extension removable partial dentures (RPD) by evaluating load distribution on implants positioned at different locations. The objective was to identify the most biomechanically suitable design for implant‐assisted removable partial dentures (IARPD).MethodsA mandibular bilateral partially edentulous model, replicating the absence of premolars and molars, was fabricated using epoxy resin. Two implants of identical diameter were vertically positioned in the edentulous ridge, one at the second molar and the other at the second premolar region on each side, to assess the biomechanical impact of different implant placements. A finite element model (FEM) was developed by taking cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan of physical model to compare two implant‐supported denture designs based on implant positioning. The geometry was created in IGS (Interactive Graphic System) format and imported into the ANSYS design modeler application. It was then transferred to the ANSYS mechanical application, where meshing was performed to generate the finite element model for load application. The occlusal surface of the prosthesis was designated as the area for applying loads of 100 N and 125 N. Von Mises stress and displacement was evaluated in premolar (Group I) versus molar (Group II) implant positions.ResultsFinite element analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in von Mises stress and displacement values between premolar and molar implant positions (p > 0.05). The observed mean values were slightly greater in the premolar group, but these differences were not significant. In premolar versus molar implant positions (p > 0.05), with RPD framework stress at 125 N being 28.71 ± 1.10 MPa (Group I) versus 25.56 ± 4.89 MPa (Group II), suggesting comparable biomechanical performance regardless of implant placement.ConclusionThe study suggests both premolar and molar implant positions are biomechanically viable for distal‐extension RPDs, with no significant differences in stress distribution.

Introduction

Bilateral distal extension removable partial dentures (RPDs) are a frequently used treatment option for patients with partial tooth loss, particularly in Kennedy Class I and II cases (distal extension situation). Despite their widespread application, traditional RPDs encounter difficulties with stability, retention, and complications over time, including alveolar bone loss and periodontal problems [1, 2]. Patients frequently report discomfort and dissatisfaction, leading to reduced usage or demands for replacement prostheses. Additionally, conditions like combination syndrome, where mandibular distal extension RPDs oppose maxillary complete dentures, exacerbate these issues with characteristic symptoms including maxillary tuberosity overgrowth, anterior maxillary resorption, and occlusal plane tipping [3]. The advent of Osseo‐integrated implants has transformed prosthetic rehabilitation strategies, providing an alternative to mitigate the limitations of RPDs. Implant‐assisted RPDs (IARPDs) combine conventional frameworks with implant support, reducing the reliance on soft tissues and anterior abutment teeth for load‐bearing [4]. By strategically positioning implants in distal edentulous regions, IARPDs can enhance stability, minimize rotational movements, and prevent tissue‐ward displacement of the prosthesis. Consequently, this approach offers improved comfort, functionality, and patient satisfaction, positioning IARPDs as a valuable option for mandibular distal extension rehabilitation [5].

Despite the advantages of IARPDs, there remains considerable debate about the optimal implant position for mandibular distal extension cases. Implants placed in the molar region are thought to provide superior posterior support and minimize cantilever forces, while premolar placement is believed to distribute occlusal loads more evenly across the arch, protecting anterior abutments and surrounding tissues. These contrasting views stem from limited and inconsistent research findings, often constrained by small sample sizes, varied methodologies, and insufficient biomechanical analysis. Finite element analysis (FEA) offers a robust and precise method to evaluate stress distribution and biomechanical behavior in complex dental structures. Previous studies using FEA have highlighted its potential in analyzing load transmission to peri‐implant bone and adjacent structures [6, 7, 8]. However, the current literature lacks comprehensive studies comparing stress distribution between premolar and molar implant positions in mandibular IARPDs. Moreover, the impact of different attachment systems, such as Locator attachments, which provide resilience, retention, and angulation compensation, remains underexplored [9, 10, 11]. This gap underscores the necessity of this study, aiming to address critical questions about implant placement and its influence on prosthetic biomechanics and long‐term outcomes.

This study employs three‐dimensional FEA to evaluate the biomechanical influence of implant positioning (premolar vs. molar) in mandibular bilateral distal‐extension removable partial dentures (RPDs). Specifically, it analyzes stress distribution patterns in peri‐implant bone, surrounding soft tissues, and prosthetic components under differing functional loads. Additionally, the research aims to translate these biomechanical insights into clinical guidelines for optimizing the design of implant‐assisted RPD (IARPD) frameworks to enhance longevity and performance. While the benefits of IARPDs are established, a critical and common clinical dilemma arises from anatomical constraints. Severe residual ridge resorption or anatomical limitations (e.g., mental foramen position, inferior alveolar nerve canal) often preclude the ideal placement of an implant in the molar region. In such cases, the second premolar site frequently remains the only viable option for implant placement. However, the biomechanical consequences of this mesial implant position remain a subject of debate and are not well‐quantified. It is hypothetically considered a biomechanical compromise, with concerns that its proximity to the rotational axis may lead to unfavourable leverage and increased stress on the implant and surrounding bone. Consequently, clinicians are often left with uncertainty: should they proceed with a premolar implant or abandon implant support altogether due to these theoretical concerns?

Therefore, a direct, controlled biomechanical comparison between these two clinically relevant positions is urgently needed. FEA is ideally suited to provide this evidence, offering a precise comparison of stress distribution that is difficult to achieve in clinical studies. To date, no study has used FEA to directly resolve this specific clinical controversy by comparing premolar versus molar implant positions for mandibular distal extension IARPDs under identical conditions. The null hypothesis states that implant location (premolar/molar) does not significantly alter stress distribution or biomechanical behavior. By systematically evaluating this hypothesis, the study addresses key knowledge gaps, providing evidence‐based strategies for implant placement in distal‐extension cases. The outcomes are expected to refine prosthodontic protocols, improve patient‐specific treatment planning, and advance prosthetic dentistry research.

Material and Method

Study Design

This in vitro study utilized a simulated model derived from a physical cast to evaluate biomechanical performance under controlled occlusal loading conditions. The experimental protocol was approved by the University Curriculum Development Cell (Registration no. 02‐D012‐72845). Two test groups were established based on implant positioning within the implant‐assisted removable partial denture (IARPD) framework: Group I‐Implant placed in the premolar region (representing mesial positioning in cases of posterior bone deficiency), Group II‐ Implant placed in the molar region (representing the biomechanically optimal distal position). The study design enabled direct comparison of von Mises stress distribution and displacement patterns between the two clinically relevant implant configurations while maintaining standardized loading parameters and material properties.

Physical Model Preparation

A mandibular bilateral distal‐extension model (missing teeth #34–37 and #44–47) was constructed using a commercially available epoxy resin (Lapox C‐51 with Lapox Hardener K‐6, India). Two 4.0 × 10 mm implants (Dentium, Korea) were placed bilaterally in the edentulous ridge—one in the second premolar area (mesial implant) and the other in the second molar region (distal implant)—positioned perpendicular to the occlusal plane to evaluate biomechanical differences in RPD performance based on implant location. The model was scanned digitally using a computer‐aided scanner (DWOS 3 Series, USA), and a virtual removable partial denture (RPD) framework was designed. proximal plates (guiding planes) were digitally placed on the distal surfaces of both canines. Minor connectors with a lattice structure were added bilaterally. An STL (Standard Tessellation Language) file of digitally designed RPD framework was then prepared and ready for model making [12, 13, 14, 15].

FEM Model Preparation

The mandibular model underwent cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT) scanning using a NEWTON system (Italy) to acquire geometric data for FEA. Following scanning, the physical model was transferred to the laboratory for subsequent processing. Serial CBCT image slices were systematically analyzed and converted into three‐dimensional reconstructions using Mimics software (Materialise, Belgium), an advanced interactive image processing system. This specialized software imports DICOM‐formatted CT data and generates comprehensive 3D visualizations through density‐based threshold segmentation algorithms [13]. Three‐dimensional point cloud datasets were reconstructed using Rapidform 2004 software to generate precise geometric models of each design variant. These comprehensive digital models captured all essential surface topography data. The STL‐formatted framework file was digitally aligned and virtually connected to the implant locator attachment system. Bilateral acrylic guidance ramps were incorporated into the framework design (Figure 1). The complete virtual assembly, representing an implant‐assisted removable partial denture system, was subsequently converted into a finite element model using Hypermesh v13.0 (2016) software with 10‐node tetrahedral elements. The final meshed configuration comprised 83,331 nodes and 451,385 discrete elements for biomechanical analysis. A mesh convergence test was conducted to ensure numerical accuracy. The global element size was iteratively reduced until the maximum observed stress in the critical region (the implant neck) changed by less than 5% between successive meshes. The final mesh density used for all simulations was selected based on this criterion.

Each finite element model (FEM) comprised numerous nodes and tetrahedral elements. To streamline the FEA, several assumptions were made: the materials were treated as isotropic, homogeneous, and exhibiting linear elastic behavior; variations in soft tissue thickness and bone density were not taken into account; and the elastic modulus (E) and Poisson's ratio (v) were kept constant throughout the analysis.

Load Application

The mechanical properties of all materials used in the FEA are detailed in Table 1 (Model). Vertical loads of 100 N and 125 N were applied at two distinct occlusal locations (premolar and molar regions). The volumetric mesh data were processed using ANSYS 12.1 FEA software to evaluate von Mises stress distribution patterns under loading conditions (Figures 2 and 3). Through numerical simulation, the software generated comprehensive von Mises stress field analyses. Post‐processing revealed several critical parameters: maximum principal stress (σmax) at the RPD‐implant interface, Displacement patterns, stress distribution contours in the resin base, and peak strain values (Figures 4 and 5).

Statistical Analysis

Data normality was confirmed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. As the data were normally distributed, stress and displacement outcomes are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent samples t‐tests were used to compare differences between Group I (premolar implant) and Group II (molar implant) for each variable under both loading conditions (100 N, 125 N). The alpha level for statistical significance was set a priori at p < 0.05 for all two‐sided tests. While p values are reported, emphasis is placed on the effect size, calculated using Cohen's d, to interpret the magnitude of observed differences. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA).

Before comparative analysis, a reliability assessment was performed to ensure measurement consistency across the finite element model. Five strategically selected nodal points were identified at regions of peak von Mises stress stress concentration (framework‐implant interface, peri‐implant bone, and major connector areas) for repeated force application simulations. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis was conducted using a two‐way mixed‐effects model with absolute agreement to evaluate measurement reliability across consecutive loads. For group comparisons, independent sample t‐tests were implemented in SPSS (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). after confirming normality (Shapiro‐Wilk test, p > 0.05). Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d to supplement p value interpretation, with all tests employing α = 0.05 significance threshold.

Result

The resulting ICC values ranged between 0.83 (95% CI: 0.82–0.95), indicating excellent inter‐cycle reliability (Cicchetti criteria: ICC > 0.75). This rigorous validation ensured subsequent force application would yield reproducible measurement outcomes. Finite element analysis revealed distinct von Mises stress and displacement patterns between premolar (Group I) and molar (Group II) implant positions. Under 100 N and 125 N loads, the RPD framework exhibited higher stress in Group I (23.24 ± 3.46 MPa and 28.71 ± 1.10 MPa, respectively) compared to Group II (21.23 ± 1.28 MPa and 25.56 ± 4.89 MPa), though differences were nonsignificant (p > 0.05). Similarly, implant stress was marginally greater in Group I (15.54 ± 1.85 MPa vs. 13.36 ± 2.83 MPa at 125 N; p = 0.14). Bone stress showed comparable trends (7.83 ± 2.22 MPa in Group I vs. 6.29 ± 1.73 MPa in Group II at 125 N; p = 0.21). Displacement magnitudes followed analogous patterns, with Group I demonstrating slightly higher values (e.g., 0.41 ± 0.01 mm vs. 0.39 ± 0.03 mm in the RPD framework at 125 N; p = 0.35). Despite numerical variations, statistical significance was not achieved in any comparison (p > 0.05 for all), suggesting implant position may influence biomechanical behavior without drastic mechanical trade‐offs. Discussion

The present finite element analysis study provides valuable insights into stress distribution patterns in mandibular distal‐extension removable partial dentures (RPDs) supported by implants placed in premolar versus molar positions. The position of the implant (premolar vs. molar) did not significantly influence stress distribution or displacement within the system (p > 0.05). Although mean values were numerically elevated for the premolar location, this trend was not statistically significant. This finding suggests that both implant positions may be clinically viable options for distal extension cases. The comparable displacement patterns observed between groups further support this conclusion, indicating that implant position influences biomechanical behavior without creating critical clinical compromises. A noteworthy observation was the consistent transfer of approximately 50% of applied loads to the Locator attachments regardless of implant position, underscoring their crucial role in stress distribution mechanics. These results align with previous finite element studies that have reported elevated but clinically acceptable stress levels in distal implant‐supported RPDs [16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

Extensive biomechanical research has established that implant support significantly enhances denture stability while reducing stress on the residual ridge [17, 18, 19, 20]. Cunha et al. [21] demonstrated that strategic implant placement near abutment teeth optimizes stress distribution patterns, a finding that informs current treatment planning approaches. Clinical practice trends identified by Bortolini et al. [22] reveal a preference for canine and premolar implant placements, primarily due to anatomical considerations involving bone volume and density. Our results complement the work of Cho et al. [16], who documented the stress‐reducing benefits of resilient attachment systems like Locators. The unique design characteristics of Locator attachments, including their self‐aligning mechanism and low vertical profile [23, 24, 25, 26], likely contributed to the balanced stress distribution observed in our study across different implant positions. However, our findings contrast with those of Matsudate et al. [27], who reported greater vertical loading with distal implants ‐ this discrepancy may stem from variations in attachment system selection or modeling methodology.

The decision to focus specifically on implant‐assisted RPDs (IARPDs) rather than tooth‐implant‐supported prostheses (TISPs) or shortened dental arch (SDA) approaches was based on several critical biomechanical and clinical considerations. While TISPs combine natural teeth and implants for prosthetic support, they carry inherent risks of differential mobility and tooth intrusion, complications that are particularly challenging to manage in removable prosthodontic applications [28, 29]. Furthermore, TISP treatment planning demands rigorous abutment selection criteria, including optimal periodontal health and favorable root morphology, which may limit their applicability in many distal‐extension cases. The SDA concept, while minimally invasive, often proves inadequate for addressing the functional demands of bilateral edentulous spans, especially in patients with significant occlusal load requirements or aesthetic concerns [30]. IARPDs offer distinct advantages in preserving remaining dentition while providing implant‐derived stability, making them particularly suitable for cases with anatomical limitations such as advanced bone resorption [22, 27].

While this FEA study provides valuable mechanical insights, its findings must be interpreted within essential biological and clinical contexts. The observed bone stress values (6–8 MPa) fell within the presumed physiological range for bone maintenance. However, the consistently lower stress values around the molar implant theoretically increase the long‐term risk of stress shielding and disuse atrophy compared to the premolar site, which may favor bone preservation. The calculated implant displacement (< 100 µm) was below the critical threshold believed to cause fibrous encapsulation (> 150 µm), suggesting both positions are mechanically conducive to osseointegration. It is crucial to note that this model assumed perfect osseointegration; in clinical practice, achieving this depends critically on factors beyond this study's scope, such as implant surface bioactivity, topography, and biocompatibility. The stresses calculated for both the titanium implant (~ 15 MPa) and cobalt‐chromium framework (~ 29 MPa) remain far below the yield strength of these materials, indicating a low risk of mechanical failure under these static conditions. A key limitation is that this model does not simulate cyclic fatigue from long‐term chewing, which is a primary cause of clinical mechanical failure.

The choice of framework material significantly influences stress distribution. The high stiffness of cobalt‐chromium (Co‐Cr) provides rigid load support but transmits higher stresses to the implant and surrounding bone. In contrast, a flexible material like polyetheretherketone (PEEK) absorbs more energy through flexure, which may reduce stress at the implant‐bone interface. However, this increased flexibility can lead to greater framework displacement, potentially compromising retention stability and increasing pressure on the underlying mucosa, presenting a different set of clinical trade‐offs.

When considering the clinical implementation of these findings, the biomechanical benefits of distal implant placement must be carefully weighed against practical anatomical constraints. Alveolar resorption patterns frequently necessitate alternative implant positions, highlighting the importance of our finding that premolar placement can serve as a viable option. A notable limitation of our study is its exclusive focus on vertical loading conditions, as lateral and torsional forces ‐ which play significant roles in clinical function ‐ were not evaluated. Existing clinical evidence indicates that IARPDs may subject abutment teeth to greater occlusal forces compared to conventional RPDs, emphasizing the need for thorough abutment assessment and thoughtful prosthetic design. Incorporating features such as reinforced major connectors can help achieve more equitable load distribution across the entire prosthetic system [29, 30, 31, 32]. Future research directions should prioritize the development of more comprehensive loading models that incorporate dynamic occlusal forces, along with long‐term clinical studies to validate these computational findings in patient populations.

Conclusion

This finite element analysis demonstrates that implant position (premolar vs. molar) in mandibular distal‐extension IARPDs influences stress distribution patterns, though without statistically significant differences. The use of Locator attachments effectively distributed functional loads across both implant positions, reinforcing their clinical utility in anatomically compromised cases. While molar placement remains biomechanically ideal, premolar positioning presents a viable alternative when posterior bone volume is limited. Future research should incorporate dynamic occlusal simulations and long‐term clinical studies to correlate these FEA findings with patient outcomes. These insights provide clinicians with evidence‐based guidelines for optimizing IARPD designs in complex edentulous scenarios.
