Dying in the Desired Location—Findings From the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging
Authors: Philip D St John, Robert B Tate, Scott Nowicki, Genevieve N Thompson, Cara L Brown, Kerstin S Roger, Shahin Shoostari, Lauren E Griffith
Published: 2026

Introduction

We analyzed the Decedent Questionnaire (DQ) from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA) [1, 2] to determine the percent of participants in this cohort study who died in their desired location as well as the factors associated with dying in the desired location, as reported by a proxy.

Methods

The CLSA consists of two related cohorts—the “tracking cohort” which is randomly sampled from representative population frames across Canada, and the “comprehensive cohort” which is limited to those living close to one of the study sites, and includes in‐person interviews and physical assessments of health. The combined cohort comprises 51,338 participants aged 45–85 years at enrolment [1]. The original sampling frame was from representative sampling frames with a goal of ensuring that the sample was as representative of community living adults as possible. The sampling frames excluded: adults living with cognitive impairment at recruitment, individuals unable to participate in English or French, individuals living in long‐term care (LTC) facilities at recruitment, active Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP) and military personnel, individuals living on First Nations reserves, the three territories and in some rural and remote regions. The DQ [3] is proxy reported after the participant died. At the time of our analysis, 3672 participants had died, and 1287 (35%) completed DQs. The proxy respondent was a husband/wife for 510 (40%); a father/mother for 97 (8%); and a son/daughter for 434 (34%); with all others making up the remainder. The proxy reported being very close to the decedent in 1178 (92%) of the participants.

For the outcome, we considered the response to the question: “In the last week of [his/her] life, do you feel that [decedent's name] died where [he/she] wanted to?” These were coded as Yes/No, Don't know or refused. We considered age at death and sex. Proxies are also asked about the functional trajectory leading to death [4]—Sudden, Terminal (a rapid decline over 1–2 months), Chronic Illness (illness over a period of years with periods of worsening followed by partial or complete recovery), and Gradual Decline (a slow, gradual decline usually over years with steadily increasing disability before dying). Proxies also report functional status [5], which we categorized as “No Disability; Mild Disability; and Moderate/Severe/Total Disability.” We used the cause of death provided by the proxy. Two investigators (PStJ and RT) reviewed the “Other Causes” of death and recoded these causes of death. We recoded some into an existing category (e.g., Valvular Heart Disease into “Cardiac”), and created two categories—Gastro‐intestinal (which includes liver disease) and Chronic Neurological (which includes Parkinson Disease, Upper Motor Neuron Disease, etc.). We also recoded “Covid‐19” deaths into “Influenza/Pneumonia.” We left some causes of death as “Other”—many of these were deaths from multiple organ failure, or deaths at very old ages. We considered place of death as: Community (their home, another private home, or other non‐institutional places such as workplace, shopping areas, or on roads/trails); Hospital; LTC (Nursing Home, Chronic Care facility or Seniors' Residence); or Palliative Care (Palliative Care Unit or Hospice).

We conducted bivariate analyses using chi square tests for categorical variables and Student's t‐tests or Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables. We then constructed logistic regression models with the participant dying in the location that they wanted as the outcome variable.

Results

Of the 1287 DQs, 764 died in the location that they desired, and 425 did not. There were 98 respondents with missing data. The results of the bivariate analyses are shown in Table 1. The results from logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, and education are shown in Table 2. Compared to those who died in hospital, those who died in the community were markedly more likely to have died where they wanted to die, as were those who died in palliative care settings. Those who died with dementia, with infection, with respiratory tract infection, or due to an accident were all less likely to die where they desired compared to those with cancer. We also found that the trajectory leading to death was associated with the desired location of death: those with a gradual or a chronic disease pattern of decline to death were less likely to die in the desired location compared to those who died suddenly.

Discussion

We have found that dying in the place that people want to die, as reported by a proxy, is dependent upon the primary cause of death and the functional trajectory to death. These factors are difficult to modify with health service delivery changes—such as increased access to palliative care, home care, or geriatric specialty services. Thus, it may prove difficult to ensure that most people die where they would like to die.
