Background

Human viruses often directly and indirectly interact with bacteria. Direct interactions involve bacteria or their products assisting viruses in infection, whereas indirect interactions occur when a viral infection creates conditions that are favourable for bacterial colonization [1]. Immunocompromised individuals, such as people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), people receiving chemotherapy, or organ transplant recipients receiving immunosuppressive drugs, are particularly vulnerable to infections because of their weakened immune system. In immunocompromised patients, coinfections with viruses and bacteria have been shown to worsen disease severity. Bacterial and HIV coinfections, which cause systemic infections in the host, are major contributors to global morbidity and mortality [2]. For example, Mycobacterium tuberculosis and HIV coinfection is considered a critical life-threatening infection in developing regions, where the loss of CD4 + T cells impairs the immune response to M. tuberculosis, leading to a greater likelihood of active tuberculosis [2]. In the context of an influenza pandemic, bacterial coinfection can be alarming and is a risk to predominantly immunocompromised or immunosuppressed patients. Influenza viruses not only damage the host epithelium (e.g., through apoptosis) but also offer potential sites for bacterial attachment [1]. Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae are the major bacteria associated with coinfection with influenza virus [3]. Other viruses, such as enteroviruses, have also been associated with coinfection. A study revealed that 20% of norovirus infections involve Salmonella coinfection [4]. Salmonella enterica and noroviruses are responsible for most gastroenteritis cases, with sporadic outbreaks and a relatively high incidence of infections [3]. Norovirus and Salmonella infections are usually resolved in a few days in healthy adults, but they can pose a life-threatening risk to immunocompromised people.

Human pegivirus-1 (HPgV-1), previously known as GB virus C (GBV-C) or hepatitis G virus (HGV), is an RNA virus belonging to the genus Pegivirus of the Flaviviridae family [5]. In recent studies, HPgV-1 has been observed in the human central nervous system, demonstrating that the virus can be present in the brain [6–13]. However, controversy exists regarding the neuropathological effect of HPgV-1. L. monocytogenes is an important zoonotic bacterium widely found in the environment and food. L. monocytogenes can survive under cold storage conditions. The main groups affected by L. monocytogenes infections are elderly individuals, pregnant women, patients with tumours, and immunosuppressed patients. L. monocytogenes is one of the major bacteria responsible for meningitis [14]. Whether HPgV-1 interacts with L. monocytogenes is unknown. Herein, we describe the case of a multiple myeloma patient with disseminated L. monocytogenes and HPgV-1 coinfection.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old patient was admitted to the West China Hospital of Sichuan University on Day 0 and presented with a 7-day history of fever. The patient was suspected of having an unclean diet before onset. One week before admission, the patient experienced limb weakness and was bedridden. The patient had a sudden high fever (body temperature up to 41 °C), accompanied by headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting of stomach contents, and urinary and faecal incontinence. The patient subsequently experienced disturbances in consciousness and showed a lack of response. The patient had been previously diagnosed with multiple myeloma in September 2020, for which the patient received multiple cycles of chemotherapy and targeted therapy. The patient then underwent autologous haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) in January 2022 and started receiving chemotherapy with bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone for one year, and bortezomib, daletumab and dexamethasone for eight months.

On physical examination, the body temperature was 40 °C, with a pulse of 83 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, and a blood pressure of 183/81 mmHg. The physical examination revealed drowsiness, occasional spontaneous eye opening, inability to answer, a bilateral pupil diameter of approximately 4 mm, a slow light reflex, neck stiffness, a positive meningeal stimulation sign, and a limb muscle strength level of 3.

The results of laboratory investigations were as follows: white blood cell (WBC) count, 12.06 × 109/L; neutrophil percentage, 86.9%; haemoglobin (Hb) concentration 119 g/L; platelet (PLT) count, 30 × 109/L; procalcitonin (PCT) concentration, 6.34 ng/ml; and CD4 + T-cell count, 198 cells/µL. Tests for liver enzymes concentrations revealed an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) concentration of 82 IU/L and an aspartate aminotransferase (AST) concentration of 72 IU/L. The results of serological and molecular tests for HIV, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, syphilis, serum cryptococcus antigen, serum (1, 3)-beta-D-glucan, serum galactomannan, and TORCH infections were negative. Epstein–Barr virus deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) quantification revealed 6.16E + 01 copies/mL, and cytomegalovirus DNA quantification revealed 1.29E + 02 copies/mL. Blood culture revealed bacterial colonies on the blood agar that were small, white, and smooth (Fig. 1). Additionally, Gram stain revealed gram-positive short bacilli (Fig. 1). The matrix assisted laser desorption/ionization biotyper microflex LT (Bruker Daltonics, Inc., Billerica, MA, USA) was performed to identify the pure colonies on blood culture; this culture was positive for L. monocytogenes (Supplementary Material). We then used Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923; American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) and Streptococcus agalactiae to perform a CAMP test. The CAMP test revealed that the haemolytic ring of the bacteria was strengthened near S. aureus, with L. monocytogenes forming a rectangular shape and Strep. agalactiae forming an arrow shape (Fig. 1). In cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), the pressure was 200 mmH2O, the protein concentration was 23.72 g/L (normal range 0.15–0.45), the glucose concentration was 8.91 mmol/L (normal range 2.5–4.4), and the chloride concentration was 128 mmol/L (normal range 120–130). The CSF glucose/plasma glucose ratio was 0.57 (normal range 0.5–0.8). The CSF was yellow and transparent. The total number of nucleated cells was 12 × 106⁄L without pus or red blood cells. The number of cells in the CSF was insufficient to determine whether they were neutrophilic granulocytes or lymphocytes. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of L. monocytogenes and HPgV-1 in CSF was conducted on the Vision Medical Center’s Illumina NextSeq 550Dx platform (Chengdu, China). A total of 1,413,134 reads were retrieved, and 1,359,341 were retrieved after cleaning. A total of 174 reads were specific for L. monocytogenes, and 4105 were specific for HPgV-1 (Supplementary Material). In peripheral blood, 1.8E + 05 copies/mL of HPgV-1 RNA were identified (Supplementary Material). Cranial MRI revealed slight thickening and enhancement of the meninges, along with inflammatory changes (Fig. 2). The patient was diagnosed with HPgV-1 and L. monocytogenes meningitis, HPgV-1 viremia, and L. monocytogenes sepsis.

The patient developed a severe illness, and the coexistence of other resistant bacterial infections could not be ruled out. Therefore, the patient was treated with meropenem (dosage: 2 g every 8 h daily) as preemptive therapy for bacterial infection. The patient’s thrombocytopenia (defined as a minimum PLT count of 21 × 109/L) was treated with a platelet transfusion. Over the following days, the patient’s body temperature returned to normal; the WBC count, PCT concentration, CSF pressure, CSF protein concentration, and glucose concentration decreased; and the results of CSF culture, a CSF HPgV-1 RNA test, and blood cultures were negative (Fig. 3). Moreover, the patient was able to respond to commands on Day 20 and was conscious on Day 24. The CSF protein concentration was 3.99 g/L (decreased by more than 5.9-fold), the CSF glucose concentration was 4.09 mmol/L, and the CSF chloride concentration was 121 mmol/L on Day 29. The CSF was colourless and transparent. The total number of nucleated cells was 20 × 106⁄L without pus. On Day 47, the patient again had a fever, with a body temperature up to 39 °C, was positive for SARS-CoV-2 infection, and was treated with Paxlovid for five days. On Day 54, the patient’s biochemical parameters were as follows: WBC count, 10.9 × 109/L; neutrophil percentage, 38%; Hb concentration, 112 g/L; PLT count, 304 × 109/L; ALT and AST concentrations, within the normal range; and PCT concentration, 0.12 ng/mL. On Day 55, the test result for SARS-CoV-2 infection was negative. The patient was discharged on Day 56. The timeline of the clinical manifestations, diagnoses and treatments of the patients is shown in Fig. 3. On Day 74, during an outpatient follow-up visit, the biochemical panel results were as follows: WBC count, 6.87 × 109/L; neutrophil percentage, 42%; Hb concentration, 107 g/L; PLT count, 155 × 109/L; PCT concentration, 0.08 ng/mL; and CD4 + T-cell count, 480 cells/µL. The patient is still alive and remains under observation.

Discussion and conclusions

Xingbing Lu et al. analysed the neuroinvasive clinical features of L. monocytogenes infection in our hospital. Compared with those in the control group, the CSF protein concentration, total nucleated cell (TNC) count, and multinucleated cell ratio were greater, whereas the glucose concentration, chlorine concentration, and mononuclear cell ratio were lower in the L. monocytogenes group [15]. However, there was no significant difference between the groups in any of the indicators (P >0.05), except for the TNC count [15]. In our patient, the protein concentration increased in the CSF, whereas the CSF glucose/plasma glucose ratio and chloride concentration were normal. The number of cells in the CSF was insufficient to determine whether these cells were neutrophilic granulocytes or lymphocytes (before treatment: 12 × 106/L; after treatment: 20 × 106/L). In addition, the CSF culture became negative. It is difficult to distinguish between bacterial and viral infections, as well as between meningitis and encephalitis. mNGS can identify a wide array of pathogens, including those that are rare, unexpected, difficult to culture or cannot be cultured [16]. In addition, its target-agnostic nature allows the detection of multiple microorganisms simultaneously, which is crucial in cases of mixed infections where no single pathogen predominates [17]. Clinicians can make more informed treatment decisions, potentially improving patient outcomes by tailoring antimicrobial therapy to the specific pathogens identified [17]. mNGS is suggested in cases involving patients with severe or complicated infectious diseases, including those who are critically ill, have immune deficiencies, or are experiencing outbreaks of unknown causes [16]. This comprehensive detection capability can significantly alter the diagnostic paradigm, shifting from a reliance on culture-based methods to a more rapid and precise molecular approach.

The infection may be missed in routine diagnostics because HPgV-1 cannot be cultured. Identification by molecular identification techniques such as mNGS is beneficial because of the ability of these techniques to identify pathogens in acutely and critically ill patients in a timely and effective manner. HPgV-1 mainly infects haematopoietic stem cells, T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, and monocytes; therefore, the rate of HPgV-1 infection in patients who have undergone with HSCT is very high [18, 19]. Vu et al. analysed the outcomes of HPgV-1-infected patients who have undergone HSCT and reported that found 41.8% of patients were infected with HPgV-1 during the follow-up period and 32% were infected before transplantation [20]. A study from Li et al. revealed that the HPgV-1 infection rate in patients who have undergone HSCT was significantly greater than that in healthy blood donors (18.6% vs. 2.3%), suggesting that blood transfusion can substantially increase the risk of HPgV-1 infection in patients who have undergone HSCT [21]. All the above studies have shown that blood transfusion is the leading risk factor for HPgV-1 infection in patients receiving an organ transplant. Although there is no evidence showing the influence of the virus on outcomes of patients receiving an organ transplant, the high rate of HPgV-1 infection caused by blood transfusion should arouse clinical attention. Our patient received blood transfusions and underwent autologous HSCT, which was the most likely route of transmission.

The pathogenicity of HPgV-1 remains controversial. At present, there are no anti-HPgV-1 therapeutic drugs available. Even though the virus is found in the saliva and blood of healthy individuals and replicates in the body at high levels, often no clear clinical symptoms are observed, nor is there significant immune activation in any cell type [5]. In approximately 80% of individuals with a competent immune system, viremia spontaneously resolves within two years following HPgV-1 infection [5]. However, HPgV-1 viremia can last for decades in individuals with weakened immune systems or other infectious conditions [19]. The continued presence of infection could be due to the ability of HPgV-1 to evade immune recognition and prevent T-cell activation [22]. HPgV-1 coinfection frequently occurs with other blood-borne viruses, such as HIV-1, hepatitis C virus, and Ebola, and this coinfection often leads to favourable clinical effects [23–25]. Specifically, HPgV-1 infection delays disease progression and extends the survival time of individuals infected with HIV-1 by directly hindering HIV-1 infection and replication and/or decreasing the immune activation of T lymphocytes [26, 27]. In addition, lymphoma has been observed in a few immunocompromised individuals who had HPgV-1 infection [28–30]. HPgV-1 infects T and B lymphocytes, causing persistent infections due to its lymphotropic nature. Continuous infection with HPgV-1 may trigger DNA mutations and possibly malignant transformation in lymphocytes, which could facilitate lymphoma development [5, 31, 32]. Furthermore, HPgV-1 has been repeatedly found in encephalitis patients’ brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid (Table 1). Pathology analysis of the brain tissue of HPgV-1infected patients has revealed prominent lymphocyte infiltration and gliosis in the white matter of the patients, and HPgV-1 could be detected in both lymphocytes and glial cells (no detection was detected in 66 controls), suggesting neurotropism of HPgV-1, indicating that the virus can replicate sustainably in cerebrospinal fluid and may have neurotoxicity [10]. A recent study supported the neurotropism of HPgV-1 by showing its ability to infect astrocytes and microglial cell lines [13]. HPgV-1 can maintain a persistent infection and facilitate the onset of neurological disorders by blocking peroxisome and antiviral signalling pathways [13]. The differences between the current case and previous case reports are that we detected bacterial and HPgV-1 coinfection in the brain, whereas in previous reports, only HPgV-1 infection was detected.

To our knowledge, this is the first report of HPgV-1 and L. monocytogenes coinfection. Healthy people with L. monocytogenes infection often present with fever, myalgia, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, vomiting, etc. It is usually difficult for bacteria to cross the gastrointestinal barrier, the infection is self-limiting, and the illness progresses to invasive L. monocytogenes infection in only a few patients. However, in patients with invasive infection, L. monocytogenes can cross the intestinal mucosal barrier through the mesentery lymph nodes and enter the blood, causing sepsis and meningitis. The fatality rate of L. monocytogenes meningitis is 30%, and 23% of cured patients experience sequelae such as hydrocephalus, brainstem encephalitis, and cerebral hemorrhage [33]. Our patient had a history of malignant disease, chemotherapy, low immunity, and a suspected unclean diet, all of which are risk factors for invasive L. monocytogenes infection.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not routinely screen for HPgV-1 before and after HSCT, making it difficult to determine the timing of HPgV-1 infection. The presence of HPgV-1 may be incidental or secondary to the patient’s underlying immunocompromised state. While HPgV-1 was detected in the CSF, its role in causing or exacerbating meningitis is speculative and was not demonstrated. Second, the invasiveness of brain biopsy restricts its application, and the lack of corresponding data limits conclusions about its neurotoxic or inflammatory effects in the brain. Third, multiple factors, including chemotherapy, autologous HSCT, and possible opportunistic infections, may have influenced the patient’s clinical presentation. Determining causality is challenging because HPgV-1 infection could have occurred before, after, or at the same time as L. monocytogenes infection. Finally, the fever and deterioration associated with COVID-19 could confound the assessment of the clinical timeline and the patient’s recovery trajectory.

In conclusion, we present the case of a multiple myeloma patient with disseminated L. monocytogenes and HPgV-1 coinfection. However, determining whether HPgV-1 contributed to meningitis in this case will require further investigation, including detailed histopathological analysis and broader clinical studies. There is limited systematic research on the pathogenic potential of HPgV-1, particularly in the context of coinfections and neuroinflammatory conditions. The pathogenesis of HPgV-1, especially its interaction with other bacteria/host cells in vivo, should be evaluated in future studies.
