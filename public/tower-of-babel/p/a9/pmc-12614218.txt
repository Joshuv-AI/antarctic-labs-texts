Bacterial natural transformation drives cassette shuffling and simplifies recombination in chromosomal integrons
Authors: Kevin Debatisse, Manon Brunie, Baptiste Darracq, Elia Bandini, Eloi Littner, Eduardo P C Rocha, Didier Mazel, Céline Loot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntegrons act as biobanks of gene cassettes conferring functions crucial for bacterial defense, including protection against phages and antibiotics. They enable bacterial on-demand adaptation through capture and shuffling of the cassettes under stress conditions. Our results underscore the significant role of horizontal gene transfer in integron cassette recombination. We discover that sedentary chromosomal integrons (SCIs), such as those found in Vibrio cholerae, efficiently excise and recruit cassettes from linear single-stranded DNA fragments acquired during natural transformation. We propose a simplified mechanism for the cassette excision process from this type of substrates, requiring only a single strand exchange at the attC recombination sites, ruling out any replicative mechanism. We also observe a higher specificity of the V. cholerae integrase for attC recombination sites from the V. cholerae repeat-type, a trait differentiating SCI integrases from the mobile integron (MI) ones. This specificity, likely stemming from a long-term co-evolution between SCI integrases and attC sites, impedes the recruitment of cassettes from phylogenetically distant integrons. Collectively, our findings may explain the greater attC site homogeneity observed in SCIs compared to MIs and showcase the role of natural transformation in driving cassette shuffling and simplifying the cassette recombination mechanism, thereby expanding bacterial phenotypic diversity.

Introduction

In their dynamic environments, bacteria are constantly challenged by a myriad of stressors. Among the most potent threats are antimicrobial agents, particularly antibiotics and bacteriophages. These pressures drive intense selective forces, pushing bacteria to evolve sophisticated defense mechanisms. A key player in this bacterial adaptability is the integron, a genetic element recognized for its crucial role in the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance genes. Integrons act as ingenious platforms able of capturing and expressing novel genetic information embedded in promoter-less cassettes [1–3]. One of the most intriguing aspects of integron biology is cassette shuffling. This dynamic process involves the precise excision and re-integration of gene cassettes, leading to changes in their order and, consequently, their expression level (Fig. 1A). Indeed, cassette expression is strongly influenced by its position within the array, as transcription depends both on the upstream PC promoter (located at the beginning of the array) and on the translation rate of the preceding cassettes [4]. This inherent ability to reshuffle cassettes provides bacteria with a powerful mechanism for fine-tuning gene expression and creating combinatorial sampling of the phenotypic diversity. Gene cassette recombination within integrons is orchestrated by the IntI integrase. Many stresses, including the action of certain antibiotics, can trigger the integrase expression [5]. The novel combinations of the array of cassettes increasing bacterial fitness in the local conditions will then rise in frequency in the population by natural selection. All these traits make integron a rapid and a stochastic system of adaptation on demand [1].

Integrons broadly fall into two categories: SCIs and MIs. SCIs can be very large, containing up to hundreds of cassettes in certain strains of Vibrio spp. [6–8]. They are nonmobile components of bacterial chromosomes, acting as biobanks of diverse genetic information, including functions for antiphage defense [9]. In contrast, MIs are typically smaller (up to 10 cassettes) and are associated with mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as plasmids, which can be horizontally transferred by conjugation [6, 7]. MIs have been grouped into five classes based on the sequence similarity of their IntI integrases and appear to have emerged recently. We hypothesized that the pool of cassettes carried by MIs did not originate de novo but rather that MIs acquired new gene cassettes from the larger and more diverse collection found within SCIs [10, 11]. As a result of massive antibiotic use, MIs are now widespread in clinically relevant bacteria and are considered as the principal agent driving the emergence and spread of antibiotic multidrug resistance in Gram-negative bacteria.

From a mechanistic point of view, the integron integrase is a tyrosine recombinase that mediates site-specific recombination between attC and attI sites. attC sites are located at the end of each cassette and the attI site at the beginning of the integron array (Fig. 1A). Cassette excision occurs through recombination between two consecutive attC sites while cassette integration happens through an attI × attC recombination event [12–14]. Excised cassettes can also be disseminated by their recruitment into attI sites from other integrons or their integration into bacterial genomes at attG sites [15]. attI sites are recombined in their ds form thanks to two inverted integrase binding sites (L and R boxes) separated by 4–5 base pairs. attC sites, by contrast, must adopt a specific ss structure where palindromic regions pair to mimic attI boxes for integrase binding [16–18] (Fig. 1B). The structured attC hairpin also exhibit three conserved unpaired features: the EHBs, the unpaired central spacer (UCS) and the variable terminal structure (VTS) (Fig. 1B) [17, 18]. EHBs directly favors integrase binding to the bs. This strand selectivity is essential for the integration of cassettes in the correct orientation relative to the PC promoter and hence to the expression of the promoter-less gene they contain. Site-specific recombination catalyzed by tyrosine recombinases generally follows a common pathway consisting of two consecutive strand exchanges. In integrons, during cassette integration event, due to the asymmetry of the ds attI × ss attC complex formed after the first strand exchange, a second strand exchange of any kind in the Holliday junction (HJ) would form linearized abortive recombination products. This atypical Holliday junction (aHJ) is subsequently resolved by a replicative step [19]. In this study, we investigated the mechanism of cassette excision. We focused on the most prevalent class I MIs and on the large SCI (179 cassettes, 130 kb) of the seventh pandemic Vibrio cholerae O1 El Tor strain (str.) N16961. We demonstrated that integrons leverage the ss nature of DNA transferred during conjugation and natural transformation to efficiently excise cassettes in a one-step strand exchange. This mechanism bypasses the more complex second strand resolution process required for recombination of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) cassettes. Furthermore, the high specificity of the V. cholerae SCI integrase for V. cholerae repeat (VCR)-type attC sites leads to the preferential recruitment of cassettes from integrons of isogenic strains, thus mimicking cassette shuffling.

Plasmids used in this study

Media

Escherichia coli and V. cholerae were grown in Luria–Bertani (LB) Lennox at 37°C. In the case of E. coli, antibiotics were used at the following concentrations: carbenicillin (Carb), 100 µg/ml, kanamycin (Km), 25 µg/ml, spectinomycin (Sp), 50 µg/ml, and chloramphenicol (Cm), 25 µg/ml. Thymidine and diaminopimelic acid (DAP) were supplemented when necessary to a final concentration of 0.3 mM. To induce the PBAD promoter, L-arabinose (Ara) was added to a final concentration of 2 mg/ml or 0.2 mg/ml; to repress it, glucose (Glc) was added to a final concentration of 10 mg/ml. To induce the PTAC promoter, isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added at 1 mM final concentration. V. cholerae strains were cultivated in the same conditions and with the same antibiotic concentrations except for Cm and Spec. Cm was added at a final concentration of 5 μg/ml and Sp at a final concentration of 100 and 200 μg/ml in absence or presence of glucose, respectively.

Strain and plasmid constructions

All PCR reactions were performed using the Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, Lafayette, CO, USA), and all diagnostic PCR reactions were performed using DreamTaq DNA Polymerase (Thermo Scientific, Lafayette, CO, USA). Oligonucleotides were synthesized by Eurofins Genomics. Gibson Assembly was performed using the master mix (New England Biolabs, NEB) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA was extracted using DNeasy® Tissue Kit (Qiagen) and Plasmid DNA using GeneJET Plasmid Miniprep kit (Thermo Scientific, Lafayette, CO, USA). PCR reactions were purified using the PCR purification kit (Fisher Scientific). Sequencing was performed by Eurofins Genomics. Natural transformations used to delete the attI1 site from the 8637 and 9156 strains and, to introduce the Y302F mutation into the intIA gene from the U411 strain were performed according to the procedure described in [20] and [21], respectively. P1 transduction used to construct the E. coli recA269::Tn10 strain was performed according to the procedure described in [22].

pSW suicide vector description

The pSW suicide vector contains a synthetic cassette inserted between a PTAC promoter and a promoter-less pir116 gene that encodes a functional π protein. This vector carries a RP4 origin of transfer and an oriVR6Kγ origin of replication. This vector cannot be maintained in the pir- recipient strains in the native cassette configuration since the π protein cannot be expressed. However, if the cassette is deleted through an att × att recombination event catalyzed by IntI1 expressed from the PBAD promoter (induced by Ara), the pir116 gene becomes expressed from the PTAC promoter (induced by IPTG), and the produced π protein is able to sustain pSW replication in the pir- recipient strain, which can be selected based on the pSW CmR marker (that is, onto Cm-containing plates).

Nonreplicative recombination assay

Experimental setup

This assay ensures the delivery by electroporation of the double stranded pSW suicide vector carrying a synthetic integron cassette into recipient strains that expresses the IntI1 integrase (see just above). Briefly, 300 ng of the pSW suicide plasmid was used to electroporate, in parallel, pir- UB5201 strains and a pir + UB5201-PI strain. After the electric pulse has been delivered, the cells were re-suspended in LB + Ara + IPTG (for pir- strains) and LB + dT (for the pir + strain) and incubated 2h at 37°C to ensure recombination and phenotypic expression. Appropriate dilutions of cells were spread onto selective plates. After overnight incubation at 37°C, the recombination frequency (RF) was calculated as the ratio of pir- strain transformants expressing the pSW CmR marker (selected onto plates containing Cm + Ara + IPTG) over the pir + strain transformants (selected onto plates containing Cm + dT). Note that Ara (IntI1 integrase inducer) was added to the culture of the recipient cells for the electrocompetent cell preparation. Note also that the transformation efficiencies of the pir- and pir + strains were calculated by transforming the p2597 replicative plasmid. Transformants were selected onto plates containing Sp (for pir- strains) and Sp + dT (for the pir + strain). The calculation of the RF was adjusted according to the transformation efficiency obtained for each strain.

Analysis of co-integrates from the nonreplicative cassette excision assay

A total of 145 clones were randomly picked from the three independent experiments, isolated onto antibiotic-containing plates and analyzed by PCR using the SWbeg and MFD primers to determine the recombination event. These primers hybridize respectively on each plasmid: SWbeg in the vicinity of the attI1 site carried by the donor plasmid and, MFD in the vicinity of the attCereA2 site carried by the recipient plasmid. A different PCR fragment length is expected, depending on whether the attI1 (∼700 bps) or the attCaadA7 (∼2000 bps) has recombined. Note that we obtained 5% of events (7/145) involving the attCaadA7 site (attCaadA7× attCereA2). Some PCR reactions were purified and sequenced to confirm the cassette recombination point.

Replicative recombination assay

Experimental setup

In this assay the pSW suicide vector (see above) is maintained in a permissive pir + strain (UB5201-PI) containing the integrase carrying plasmid. Cells were cultivated overnight in presence of Cm, Carb, dT, and Ara. After that, plasmids were extracted and, 500 ng is used to electroporate, in parallel, a pir- DH5α strain and a pir+ π1 strain. After the electric pulse has been delivered, the cells were re-suspended into LB + IPTG (for the pir- strain) and LB + dT (for the pir + strain) and incubated 1h at 37°C to ensure phenotypic expression. Appropriate dilutions of cells were spread onto selective plates. The RF was calculated as the ratio of pir- strain transformants expressing the pSW CmR marker (selected onto plates containing Cm + IPTG) over the pir + strain transformants (selected onto plates containing Cm + dT). Note that the transformation efficiencies of both DH5α and π1 strains were calculated by transforming the p2597 replicative plasmid. Transformants were selected onto plates containing Sp (for the pir-DH5α strain) and Sp + dT (for the pir+ π1 strain). The calculation of the RF was adjusted according to the transformation efficiency obtained for each strain.

Analysis of cassette excision from the donor plasmid

For each condition assay, at least eight recombinant clones (CmR) were randomly picked, isolated on antibiotic-containing plates and analyzed by PCR using the SWbeg and SWend primers to determine the cassette excision event. These primers hybridize respectively on each side of the cassette. Some PCR reactions were purified and sequenced to confirm cassette excision recombination point.

Suicide conjugation assay

Experimental setup

This assay has been previously described in [23]. It ensures the delivery by conjugation of one specific strand of a pSW suicide vector carrying a synthetic integron cassette into recipient strains that expresses the IntI1 integrase. It was previously transformed into a pir + donor strain, β2163, which contains the RP4 machinery of transfer. This later strain needs DAP to grow in rich medium. Once conjugated, the pSW vector cannot be maintained in the pir- recipient strain in the native cassette configuration except if the cassette is deleted through an att × att recombination event catalyzed by IntI1. Briefly, after overnight cultures, recipient and donor cells were diluted (1:100) and grown in LB + Ara for recipient and in LB + DAP for donor strains. When both donor and recipient cultures reach an OD600 nm of 0.7–0.8, 1 ml of each donor and recipient cultures were then mixed and centrifuged at 3000 rcf for 6 min. The obtained pellet was re-suspended in a droplet of LB and spread on a 0.45 μm filter placed onto plates containing DAP + Ara and incubated at 37°C for 3 h. After incubation, the filter was re-suspended in 5 ml of LB. After overnight incubation at 37°C, appropriate dilutions of cells were spread onto selective plates. The RF was calculated as the ratio of transconjugants expressing the pSW CmR marker (selected onto Cm-containing plates) over the total number of recipient clones (selected onto plates containing Carb + Glc).

Analysis of cassette excision from the donor plasmid

For each condition assay, at least 8 recombinant clones (CmR) were randomly picked, isolated on antibiotic-containing plates and analyzed by PCR using the SWbeg and SWend primers to determine the cassette excision event. These primers hybridize respectively on each side of the cassette. Some PCR reactions were purified and sequenced to confirm cassette excision recombination point.

Calculation of the limit of detection

When we did not detect any recombinant CFUs (Colony forming units), we considered that we obtained only one recombinant CFU and we calculated what we called the limit of detection (*). It corresponds to “1 recombinant CFU/the total number of recipient CFUs obtained for all the n replicates”. When we detected a few recombinant CFUs (α clones) but not in all n replicates, we calculated a unique RF for all replicates. This corresponds to the ratio “α recombinant CFUs/the total number of recipient CFUs obtained for all the n replicates”. Note that in the two cases, we cannot calculate the standard deviation, so the bar chart does not show an error bar and the calculation of a P-value is not applicable.

Natural co-transformation assay

Experimental setup

V. cholerae natural transformation was performed according to the MuGENT procedure described in [21]. Two different co-fragments were used to select natural transformants: one carrying an aadA7 cassette and the other a cat cassette. The aadA7 co-fragment was amplified from gDNA (genomic DNA) of the WT-aadA7 strain, while the cat co-fragment was amplified from the gDNA of V892 strain, both using primers o4743 and o4744.

For the IntIA assay, an overnight culture was diluted 1:1000 of bacteria into 5 ml of LB medium and grown at 30°C up to an OD600nm of 0.8. One milliliter of the culture was centrifuged at 2200 rcf for 10 min, and the pellet was re-suspended in 800 µl of Instant Ocean (IO). Transformation tubes containing 150 µl of chitin slurry and 750 µl of IO were inoculated with 100 µl of the washed cells, vortexed briefly, and incubated for 16–24 h at 30°C without shaking. Following incubation, 550 µl of supernatant was carefully removed without disturbing the chitin pellet. DNA fragments were then added: 50 ng of the aadA7 co-fragment (11.7 fmol) and a 30-fold molar excess (351 fmol) of the DNA fragment of interest. Tubes were gently inverted twice and incubated for an additional 24 h at 30°C without shaking. After incubation, 1 ml of LB medium was added to each tube, and cells were incubated 2 h at 37°C with shaking (180 rpm). The resulting bacterial suspensions were serially diluted and plated on LB agar supplemented with or without Sp. After overnight incubation at 37°C, the natural transformation frequency was calculated as the ratio of SpR (selected onto Sp-containing plates) clones over the total number of recipient clones.

For the IntI1 assay, the protocol was modified as follows. The co-fragment used carried a cat gene conferring chloramphenicol resistance instead of aadA7. To induce IntI1 expression, an overnight culture was diluted 1:1000 into LB medium supplemented with Sp and 0.2% Ara and, grown at 30°C up to an OD600nm of 0.8. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (2200 rcf, 10 min) and re-suspended in 800 µl of IO supplemented with 0.02% Ara. Transformation tubes containing 150 µl of chitin slurry and 750 µl of IO supplemented with 0.02% Ara were inoculated with 100 µl of the washed cells, vortexed briefly, and incubated 16–24 h at 30°C without shaking. The remaining steps were performed as described above, except that cells were plated on LB agar supplemented with Sp and 1% Glc with or without Cm. Natural transformation frequency was calculated as the ratio of CmR clones (selected onto Cm-containing plates) over the total number of recipient clones.

Analysis of cassette integration into attIA and attI1 sites

For each condition, at least 72 recombinant clones (SpR or CmR) were randomly picked, isolated on antibiotic-containing plates and analyzed by PCR.

For the IntIA assay, cassette integration into the attIA site of the SCI was assessed using the o4904 and o1374 primers, which hybridize upstream of attIA and within the first cassette (VCA0292), respectively. Some PCR reactions were purified and sequenced to confirm cassette integrations using the o1186 and o1374 primers. When sequencing with these primers was incomplete, often due to the integrations of multiples cassettes, additional sequencing was performed using primers oVCR6, oVCR7, and oVCR9. For the IntI1 assay, cassette integration into the attI1 site was assessed using the o5046 and o7691 primers, which hybridize upstream of attI1 on chromosome 1, and within the artificial first cassette (aacA1), respectively. Some PCR reactions were purified and sequenced to confirm cassette integrations using the o6802 and o7691 primers.

In each assay, clones yielding only smeared PCR products were excluded from further analysis. Specifically, for the IntIA assay, 3 out of 72 clones were discarded when using both the V. cholerae and V. fischeri fragments, and 4 out of 72 when using the V. metschnikovii fragment. For the IntI1 assay, 1 out of 72 clones was excluded when using the V. cholerae fragment.

Multiple sequence alignments and tree inference

Multiple sequence alignments was performed using MAFFT (v7.525) with default parameters [24]. The tree was inferred using IQ-TREE [25]. The software’s default parameters were retained, with the sole modification being the inclusion of FreeRate heterogeneity in the substitution model. The resulting tree was visualized using iTOL v7 [26].

In silico search of In40 attC sites homologs in Vibrionaceae

We searched for homologs of In40 attC sites within a dataset of 564 completely assembled genomes of Vibrionaceae from the NCBI RefSeq database of reference microbial genomes (downloaded on 15 May 2023). IntegronFinder 2.0.5 ([6], default options, –local-max for more sensitive detection and –gembase to preserve annotations) was used to re-predict the attC sites of In40 (Genbank identifier AF034958.3). Then, we searched for homologs of these attC sites using blastn (minimal coverage of the query sequence 80% and evalue <10–5, default parameters otherwise) against the Vibrionaceae database. To identify the complete attC matching these blastn hits, we retrieved, for each site, its complete sequence from the IntegronFinder 2.0.5 predictions on each genome (same parameters as above).

Integron cassette excision from dsDNA involves replicative pathway

Due to the ss nature of recombinogenic attC sites, the common pathway for site-specific recombination—which consists of two consecutive strand exchanges—cannot lead to a cassette excision event. We hypothesized that cassette excision process requires replicative resolution pathway, as previously demonstrated for cassette integration [19]. We therefore tested the requirement for a functional origin of replication (Fig. 2A). For this, we cloned a synthetic cassette into a pSW suicide vector, which contains a single oriR6K origin of replication dependent on the π protein. Consequently, this plasmid cannot replicate in pir- strains. The cassette excision process precisely deletes the synthetic cassette and brings a PTAC promoter and a pir116 coding sequence closer, thereby enabling pir gene expression and activating the oriR6K-dependent plasmid replication (Fig. 2A). In other words, in pir- context, the plasmid maintenance is conditional upon an att × att cassette excision event. Using this plasmid strategically enables testing whether the cassette excision step can occur in the absence of an active origin of replication. By using transformation, we delivered the synthetic cassette-containing plasmid in its ds form into pir- E. coli recipient strains, which harbored a vector expressing (or not) the IntI1 integrase, and a second plasmid devoid of any attC sites (Fig. 2A, left panel). We tested a cassette containing both an attI1 and an attCaadA7 site. The attI1 site was chosen for its efficient recombination from dsDNA, as being recognized by the integrase in this form. The attCaadA7 site was included due to its demonstrated ability to be easily extruded from dsDNA [40]. We calculated the frequency of cassette excision as the ratio of clones maintaining the plasmid (CmR clones) to the total number of clones. No excision events were detected, suggesting that an active origin of replication is required for cassette excision events (Fig. 2A, bottom panel). To confirm that the absence of excision events was not due to an intrinsic defect in the activity of the attI1 and/or attCaadA7 sites carried by the donor pSW suicide vector, we repeated the experiment in a recipient strain carrying a plasmid with an attCereA2 receptor site, into which both attI1 or attCaadA7 are expected to efficiently recombine, ensuring the suicide vector is maintained (Fig. 2A, right panel). Under these conditions, we obtained an efficient RF (∼10−3) (Fig. 2A, bottom panel), far above what was obtained without integrase expression. PCR and sequencing confirmed that these recombination events involved attCereA2 and either attI1 or attCaadA7 donor sites (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). This control shows that both attI1 and attCaadA7 sites are active; precisely that the attCaadA7 site can extrude from dsDNA. Moreover, when the same pSW plasmid was transformed into a pir + strain restoring its replication, cassette excision frequency occurs at a high level (∼10−3, Fig. 2B). PCR and sequencing confirmed that cassettes were properly excised through an attI1 × attCaadA7 recombination event. Taken together, these findings indicate that ds cassettes can only be excised if their replicon’s origin of replication is functional, demonstrating the involvement of a replicative pathway.

One-step integron cassette excision from conjugated single-stranded DNA

We now investigated whether the cassette excision process could be simplified when cassettes are carried on single-stranded DNA (ssDNA). By using conjugation, we delivered the same attI1 × attCaadA7 plasmid in its ss form into a pir- E. coli recipient strain, which harbored a vector expressing (or not) the IntI1 integrase (Fig. 2C, left panel). Under these conditions, we observed a high cassette excision rate (∼10−2), far above what was obtained without integrase expression (Fig. 2C, right panel). Notably, the recombination of the ds attI1 site likely occurs through the strand replacement resynthesis of its incoming bs [18]. We also cloned both the attCaadA7 and another attC site (that is, VCR130) and obtained a cassette excision rate of ∼10−2 (Fig. 2C, right panel). Since these assays were performed in recA− recipient strains to prevent homologous recombination events, we included a control in a recA+ background and demonstrated that this protein is not involved in the recombination process (Fig. 2C, right panel). PCR and sequencing confirmed that cassettes were properly excised through attI1 × attCaadA7 and attCaadA7× VCR130 recombination events. When conjugating the nonrecombinogenic top strand of the attI1 × attCaadA7 cassette, the excision rate dropped to ∼10−7, matching rates observed in the absence of IntI1 (Fig. 2C, right panel). These results demonstrate that cassettes can be efficiently excised in the presence of integrase from the injected ss recombinogenic bs. Given that this assay was conducted with a plasmid conjugated into a pir- strain in which the origin of replication remains inactive until the excision event occurs (see above), we suggest that a functional origin of replication is not required for cassette excision from ssDNA. Indeed, we propose that ss cassettes are simply released after a one-step single strand exchange, bypassing the need for a replicative resolution pathway.

Natural transformation drives cassette recruitment in the V. cholerae SCI

Natural transformation is a conserved mechanism of horizontal gene transfer in bacterial species. During this process, DNA is taken up from the environment and delivered into bacteria in a ss form. To further validate the cassette excision mechanism from ssDNA, we took advantage of V. cholerae’s natural competent state in the presence of chitin [41]. We PCR-amplified a fragment containing five cassettes (VCA0296 to VCA0301, Fig. 3A) from the SCI of V. cholerae O1 El Tor strain (str.) N16961. This fragment includes VCRs, the usual name given to attC sites in V. cholerae. The V. cholerae SCI fragment, along with a co-fragment containing a selectable resistance marker (SpR), were introduced by natural transformation into V. cholerae [21], in presence or absence of IntIA (or using the catalytically mutated IntIAY302F integrase) (Fig. 3A). As a control, we also naturally transformed the Sp fragment alone in presence of IntIA. We calculated the frequency of naturally transformed clones as the ratio of clones integrating the Sp fragment (SpR clones) to the total number of clones. We did not observe any significant difference between the conditions, with a transformation frequency ranging from 10−4 to 10−5 (Fig. 3B). PCR analysis of 72 randomly selected SpR clones obtained in the presence of IntIA and the Sp fragment alone did not show any integration events into the attIA site that could be attributed to shuffling of endogenous cassettes (Supplementary Fig. S1). In contrast, when using the additional V. cholerae SCI fragment, PCR analysis of 72 randomly selected SpR clones obtained in presence of IntIA, showed that 87% (60/69) of the clones carried one or more cassettes integrated into the attIA site (that is, gel bands higher than 0.8kb, Fig. 3C, left panel). PCR product sequencing confirmed the integration of up to five cassettes into the attIA site, with all cassettes providing from the SCI fragment (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Table S1). It revealed that all recombination events occurred at the expected position for integrase-mediated processes preserving the integrity of the VCR site up to the cleavage point and ruling out any RecA-dependent homologous VCR recombination during the cassette excision process. This demonstrates that natural transformation with an isogenic DNA can recapitulate the outcomes of intra-bacterial cassette shuffling. As controls, we performed a PCR analysis on 72 randomly selected SpR clones obtained in the presence of the additional VCR fragment but in absence of IntIA (or in presence of the catalytically mutated IntIAY302F integrase). The results showed no cassette integration events into the attIA site (Fig. 3C, right panel and Supplementary Fig. S1). Altogether, these results demonstrate that ssDNA delivery through natural transformation induced the endogenous integrase to a sufficient extent to promote very efficient cassette excision from the delivered fragment and subsequent cassette integration into the attIA site. Importantly, since the donor SCI fragment lacked an origin of replication, these findings confirm that cassette excision events from ssDNA can occur independently of a replicative resolution pathway consistent with our observation in the conjugation assay.

Differential cassette recruitment efficiency by the V. cholerae IntIA integrase

Our findings demonstrate that DNA fragments acquired through natural transformation can supply integron cassettes for efficient integration into SCIs. While highly efficient, a comparison of SCI cassette subsets from Vibrio species showed that most cassettes of one species were absent in others and highlighted a high homogeneity between intra-integron attC sites [36]. This points to a restriction in which attC sites are prone to recombination. To test the specificity of the IntIA integrase for SCI attC sites, we repeated the previously used natural transformation assay but using SCI fragments from two other Vibrio strains (that is, V. metschnikovii and V. fischeri). We chose V. metschnikovii based on the findings of Rowe-Magnus et al. (2003) [36], which demonstrated that 85% of VCRs showed an overall similarity of >90% while most of the remaining ones branched with V. metschnikovii repeats (VMRs). In contrast, V. fischeri repeats (VFRs) form a distantly related clade from VCRs and VMRs [36]. We also used a DNA fragment from a class 1 MI (that is, In40) as MIs are generally known for their broad range of attC sites. The three PCR-amplified fragments are described in Fig. 4A. Briefly, they all contain at least three cassettes. We obtained a natural transformation frequency of ∼10−4 for all tested conditions (Fig. 4B). PCR analysis of 72 randomly selected SpR clones obtained in presence of IntIA, demonstrated that 65% (44/68) and 35% (24/69) of the clones carried one or more cassettes integrated into the attIA site when using SCI fragments from V. metschnikovii and V. fischeri, respectively (Fig. 4C, left panel and Supplementary Fig. S2A). PCR product sequencing confirmed the integration of one up to four cassettes from the SCI fragments and revealed that recombination occurred precisely at the expected position (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Table S1). Interestingly, we found one event corresponding to the shuffling of the 133rd endogenous V. cholerae SCI cassette (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Table S1) encoding the recently described TutAB antiphage defense system [9]. When we used the In40 MI fragment, we failed to detect any cassette integration events (0/72) (Fig. 4C, left panel and Supplementary Fig. S2A) suggesting that the IntIA integrase could be inefficient at recombining cassettes from MIs. Finally, for all SCI and MI fragments, PCR analysis of 72 randomly selected SpR clones obtained in absence of IntIA revealed no cassette integration events into the attIA site (Fig. 4C, right panel and Supplementary Fig. S2B).

The V. cholerae IntIA integrase recombines phylogenetically close attC sites

To understand the differential recombination rates obtained with IntIA based on cassette source, we constructed a phylogenetic tree exploring the evolutionary relationship between all the previously tested attC recombination sites (Fig. 6A). The VCRs appeared closely related to the VMRs, with VMR5 clustering within the VCR subclade and VCR7 clustering within the VMR subclade. In contrast, the VFRs formed a distinct coherent clade distantly related to both VCRs and VMRs. VCR5 forms a distinct branch that is more closely related to the VFR clade than to the VMR or other VCR sequences. Finally, the MIs attC sites formed a highly divergent, separate clade. The phylogenetic relatedness of these attC sites correlates with the RFs previously obtained from multiples attC sites, following the hierarchy VCRRF > VMRRF > VFRRF > MIRF attC sites. Using the RNAfold program from ViennaRNA Package [42], we established the structures of all the recombinogenic bs from used VCR, VMR, VFR, and MI attC sites and calculated the probability of each attC site to fold into a recombinogenic structure (pfold) [43] (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S3).

The pfold value reflects the probability of forming a functional secondary structure. In our study, as DNA substrates were delivered through ssDNA facilitating their structuration [40, 44], we did not expect any impact of the pfold value on recombination. For example, in the V. metschnikovii SCI fragment, the most frequently recruited cassette was flanked by VMR3 with a very low pfold. This means that the folding probability is not the limiting factor. The IntIA specificity for the VCR sites can be due to one or more features of the attC site structures such as the number and positioning of EHBs, the UCS sequence, or the VTS sequence. A common feature of VCR sites is the presence of three EHBs [17]. However, VFR sites contain only two EHBs, yet they are still recruited by IntIA, making EHB configuration unlikely to explain the IntIA specificity. VCR, VFR, and VMR sites harbor very large VTS regions, which form complex branched secondary structures. They also have symmetrical UCS with an equal number of nucleotides on the 5′ and 3′ sides, and frequently contain the canonical CC-TT mismatch bubble (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S3). In contrast, In40 MI sites display a short VTS and an asymmetrical UCS generating two DNA bubbles. To investigate the role of UCS and VTS features, we modified the UCS or VTS features contained in attC1 and attC2 sites surrounding the qacF cassette in In40. In the first configuration, we replaced the UCS of the original attC1 and attC2 sites with those from VCR7 (In40 UCS-modified fragment, Fig. 7A). In the second configuration, we replaced the VTS of attC1 with that of VCR7 and the VTS of attC2 with that of VCR8 (In40 VTS modified fragment, Fig. 7A). These two synthetic In40 variants were PCR-amplified and introduced by natural transformation into V. cholerae, in presence or absence of IntIA. We obtained a natural transformation frequency of ∼10−5 for all tested conditions (Fig. 7B). PCR analysis of 72 randomly selected SpR clones obtained in presence of IntIA, demonstrated that 17% (12/72) of the clones carried one or more cassettes integrated into the attIA site when using the In40 UCS modified fragment (Fig. 7C). PCR product sequencing confirmed the integration of one up to three copy of the qacF cassette in 11 out of 12 clones, with recombination occurring precisely at the expected position. The remaining clone corresponds to the integration of the cmlA2 cassette, surrounded by the UCS-modified attC2 and the unmodified attC3 (Fig. 7D). This indicates that modifying just one of the two UCS sites is enough to allow for cassette excision. When we used the In40 VTS modified fragment, we failed to detect any cassette integration events (0/72) (Fig. 7C). This indicates that the VTS features are unlikely to account for the IntIA specificity. Finally, for all UCS and VTS modified fragments, PCR analysis of 72 randomly selected SpR clones obtained in absence of IntIA revealed no cassette integration events into the attIA site (Supplementary Fig. S4).

To evaluate the recruitment of In40 cassettes into SCIs, we searched for homologs of the four In40 attC sites across 564 Vibrionaceae genomes. We identified only eight homologs, two for attC1 and six for attC2. Of these, seven were associated with MIs and only one (that is, one attC2 homolog) was found in a SCI of an environmental V. cholerae strain (Fig. 7E and Supplementary Table S1). Accordingly, these results suggest that the specificity of IntIA for VCR sites may be primarily dictated by their UCS feature, which might constitute the main barrier to the recruitment of MI cassettes into SCIs in Vibrionaceae.

Discussion

Our previous studies, focusing on the seventh pandemic V. cholerae O1 El Tor strain, revealed a low rate of cassette shuffling into the attIA site of the SCI, even with integrase overexpression [45]. Notably, cassette excision and shuffling rates were significantly enhanced only upon artificial inversion of the entire SCI [45]. Toxin–antitoxin (TA) cassettes found all along the SCI are the main drivers of the low shuffling rate, as their presence imposes a selective pressure that drives SCI to adopt a configuration in which the excision rate of their cassettes is kept low [45]. Together, this represents a common trait of SCIs enabling silent cassettes to be accumulated and maintained making them available in a large reservoir for MIs. In return, this limits the shuffling and the evolvability of SCIs. Here, we highlighted an alternative pathway mimicking cassette shuffling and which might not primarily occur through excision of host cassettes but by using natural transformation of DNA from isogenic bacteria. DNA uptake is followed by excision of cassettes from the internalized DNA and their integration into the attIA site. Despite the existence of this pathway, our last analysis of the genomes from the recently emerged sixth and seventh pandemic V. cholerae strains, revealed little evidence of cassette shuffling toward the first position in the array [9]. This suggests that natural transformation only occurs at low levels in pandemic contexts, likely due to the absence of chitin in the human gut. Conversely, in environments such as biofilms on chitinous marine animals, frequent cell lysis and competence would ensure ample isogenic donor DNA for cassette recruitment through natural transformation as suggested in [46]. Nevertheless, our natural transformation assay revealed that the integrase also mediates cassette shuffling from the endogenous integron, indicating that both intra- and inter-bacterial shuffling could contribute to integron dynamics in natural environment. Since both processes generate the same recombinant products, it remains challenging to quantify their relative contributions.

Mechanistically, the ss nature of the incoming integron-carrying DNA piece offers a recombinational advantage in many aspects. It sufficiently induces the host integrase for recombination, facilitates attC site folding and ensures cassette excision from linear molecules. Moreover, in this ss configuration, we demonstrated that the attC × attC cassette excision events can occur by only one single strand exchange, thereby ruling out the requirement for a replicative resolution, a process otherwise essential to recombine cassettes from dsDNA (Fig. 8). By circumventing the requirement for host factors like those involved in replication, this mechanism renders the integron system less reliant on and constrained by its host. We further demonstrated that MIs delivered as ssDNA during conjugation can efficiently excise cassettes through the same one-step mechanism, potentially ensuring excision even when the incoming replicon is nonfunctional and at risk of being lost.

Despite being ancient structures, SCIs consistently show homogeneity of their attC sites compared to MIs [7, 8, 36]. This has prompted the hypothesis that cassettes are created by chromosomal integrases and serves as reservoirs for MIs [8, 11]. Given our finding that natural transformation is an efficient pathway for recruiting cassettes, we were puzzled by the conservation of this high homogeneity, especially in Vibrio strains known for their natural transformation abilities. By testing several attC-containing fragments, we demonstrated that incoming ssDNA carrying attC structures from Vibrio spp. are recombined by the host IntIA integrase. Nevertheless, among the tested VMR and VFR Vibrio spp. sites, VMRs that are closest to the VCRs in term of sequence and structure display higher recombination efficiency. We failed to detect any recombination events from MI attC sites. In contrast to the SCI IntIA integrase, we observed that the MI IntI1 integrase recombines a broader range of attC sites –from SCIs and MIs– emphasizing with previous observations [17, 47]. The high specificity of the V. cholerae SCI integrase for the VCR-type attC sites likely reflects long-term co-evolution between SCI integrases and the attC sites, which in turn limits the recruitment of cassettes from phylogenetically distant integrons. In contrast, MI integrases have lost the constraint of attC site specificity enabling them to recruit a broader diversity of gene cassettes. This selective advantage conferred by MIs has probably facilitated their dissemination and contributed to their success in clinical isolates of pathogenic bacteria, where they play a direct role in the spread of antibiotic resistance. In contrast, the high specificity of SCI integrases toward attC sites has probably restrained SCIs to a sedentary lifestyle.

By integrating DNA into their genome, naturally competent bacteria can repair damaged DNA, delete MGEs, introduce new genetic information, replace endogenous genes, or insert genomic islands, ultimately contributing to evolution [48]. Here, we uncover a novel role for natural transformation in facilitating inter-bacterial shuffling in SCIs, a process that mimics conventional cassette rearrangement while circumventing the TA-mediated low excision rate, without risking the loss of cassettes from the host’s SCI. Ultimately, this newly described shuffling mechanism guarantees enhanced phenotypic diversity, a critical advantage given that integron cassettes harbor defense systems against threats like phages and antibiotics [9, 49, 50].
