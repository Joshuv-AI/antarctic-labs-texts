Diabetes distress: Does blood sugar control affect quality of life?
Authors: Basem A.A. Alubaidi, Hawra H. Albasri, Abdulla A. Ali, Narjes E. Asad, Noora S. Selaibeekh, Abrar K. AlAnsari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Diabetes mellitus (DM), a chronic disease, can severely impact the emotional, physical, and social aspects of an individual’s quality of life (QoL). The relationship between diabetes distress syndrome (DDS) and glycemic control is critical to improve the QoL of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).Objective:The objectives of this study are to determine the prevalence and severity of diabetic dyslipidemia syndrome (DDS) in adults with T2DM and to assess its association with glycemic control by measuring glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Furthermore, determining various demographic factors that could influence the relationship between DDS and glycemic control (HbA1c) is necessary to understand how it is linked with QoL.Methods:A descriptive study was conducted between September 2024 and March 2025 involving 400 patients aged 18–75 years who had T2DM for over 1 year. It was conducted at eight health centers in Bahrain. Data were collected using a validated questionnaire, which included the Diabetes Distress Scale-17 (DDS-17) to measure emotional, interpersonal, regimen-related, and healthcare professional-related distress, as well as the European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions (EQ-5D-5LV) to evaluate QoL.Results:Preliminary data analysis revealed that the mean age of participants was 55.43 ± 10.886 years, with 56.75% being males and the majority (78.5%) earning less than 1000 BD monthly. The prevalence of moderate to high DDS was notably elevated, correlating with suboptimal glycemic control as indicated by elevated HbA1c levels. According to our study, DDS is strongly associated with self-care behaviors that ultimately lead to more unhealthy outcomes.Conclusion:The current study highlights the critical concerns of psychological health improvement, as measured by DDS, in association with QoL and glycemic control among adults with T2DM. Early detection and intervention of diabetes-related distress (DDS) will improve QoL and reduce the burden of DM. Future research efforts should focus on developing comprehensive support programs that integrate mental health into diabetes management plans.

Introduction

Diabetes Mellitus (DM) is a chronic disease influenced by numerous factors. Its control extends beyond numerical blood glucose monitoring to encompass the emotional, physical, and social dimensions of a person’s life. If an individual experiences emotional distress related to DM and does not receive support, their quality of life (QoL) may be significantly compromised.[12]

Currently, approximately 10.5% of the global population—around 536.6 million individuals—are living with DM. By 2045, this figure is expected to rise to 12.2% or 783.2 million.[3] The condition is more prevalent in urban and high-income countries than in rural or economically disadvantaged regions. The financial burden of DM is also substantial. In Bahrain, the direct medical costs are estimated at approximately USD 278 million, and treating a person with DM is more than three times the cost of treating someone without the condition.[345]

DM is frequently accompanied by mental health challenges such as depression and diabetes distress syndrome (DDS), which complicate disease management and adversely affect overall health outcomes.[67] Emotional distress may lead to poor medication adherence, unhealthy eating patterns, and sedentary lifestyles, all of which can elevate blood glucose levels and hinder glycemic control, creating a vicious cycle of deterioration.[67]

Addressing diabetes care is crucial for enhancing diabetes management and reducing the healthcare burden in countries like Bahrain. Research has indicated that approximately 30% of patients with T2DM report depressive symptoms, and 20% show poor treatment adherence, significantly impacting their health.[8]

DDS consists of a range of stress-related emotions that interfere with effective self-care.[91011] Patients with high distress often neglect physical activity, dietary management, and medication adherence, leading to worsened glycemic control and QoL.[121314]

The prevalence of DDS varies widely across countries, from 18% to 45% in wealthier nations and from 21% to 85% in developing ones.[151617] In Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, prevalence estimates range from 45.3% to 62%.[1819]

Studying DDS in depth could significantly improve the psychological wellbeing of people with DM. Understanding the contributing factors may enable more effective interventions. If diabetes is identified and addressed early, self-care behaviors and glycemic control may improve, ultimately enhancing QoL.[2021]

For those with T2DM patients as well as healthcare professionals, this study is crucial. Understanding the effects of DDS on glycemic control and overall QoL can help medical professionals develop more comprehensive, patient-centered care strategies. Clinicians can incorporate psychological support into routine DM care by identifying patients who are experiencing high levels of distress. It can increase adherence and enhance treatment outcomes. Patients may become more aware of the emotional challenges associated with DM as a result of this research, which may encourage them to get treatment and participate more actively in their self-care practices. Ultimately, the knowledge acquired can inform targeted interventions that reduce anxiety, enhance glycemic management, and improve overall QoL.

Methodology

Study type

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted between September 2024 and March 2025 across eight health centers in Bahrain. These centers were selected to cover all four governorates—Muharraq, Capital, Northern, and Southern—thus ensuring representation from diverse geographic and ethnic populations.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were adults aged 21 to 75 years who had been diagnosed with T2DM for at least 1 year and had a minimum of three clinical visits in the past year. Participants needed to be proficient in either Arabic or English. Pregnant individuals, postpartum women, and those with certain mental health disorders were excluded.

The study was explained to all participants, who then provided informed consent. The questionnaires were distributed in the language of the participant’s choice. When necessary, trained researchers aided participants. Participation was voluntary and would not affect healthcare services.

Sample size and sampling

The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 379 participants, based on a DDS prevalence of 55.7%, a 95% confidence interval (α = 0.05), and a Z-score of 1.96.[22] It was rounded to 400 to enhance precision.

A stratified multistage cluster sampling method was used. Healthcare centers were stratified by governorate, and two to three centers were randomly selected per region, resulting in a total of 10 centers. It will ensure representative sampling from a heterogeneous population.

Diabetes Distress Scale-17 (DDS-17)

The DDS-17 is a validated tool used to assess diabetes distress in adults with T2DM.[23] It includes 17 items grouped into four subscales: emotional burden, physician-related distress, regimen-related distress, and interpersonal distress. Participants rate each item on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“not a problem”) to 6 (“a serious problem”). The internal consistency is high (α = 0.93).[24]

Each subscale score is calculated by summing the responses and dividing by the number of items in that subscale. A mean score:

<2.0 indicates little or no distress,2.0–2.9 indicates moderate distress,≥3.0 indicates high distress requiring clinical attention.

Glycemic control measurement

Glycemic control was measured using HbA1c levels, which reflect the average blood glucose level over the past 3 months and are widely accepted as an indicator of diabetes management.[2]

Quality of life measurement

The European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions 5 Level Version (EQ-5D-5L) is a validated instrument for assessing QoL across five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression.[23] Each dimension is rated on a 5-point scale, where 1 indicates no problem and 5 indicates extreme issues. Higher scores reflect poorer health states. Values close to 1 suggest high QoL, values near zero imply reduced QoL, and negative values indicate states perceived as worse than death.

Ethical consideration

All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, their right to withdraw at any time, and that their participation was voluntary. Data were managed with strict confidentiality.

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using SPSS. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Chi-square tests assessed associations between categorical variables, t-tests compared two groups, and ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons.

Discussion

This study explored the prevalence and severity of DDS among individuals with T2DM in Bahrain, examining its associations with glycemic control (HbA1c), demographic factors, and QoL.

Findings revealed that 94% of participants experienced mild to moderate DDS, while 6% reported high levels of distress. The most common sources of stress were emotional issues and treatment-related concerns, consistent with findings by Zhang et al. (2022),[25] who identified emotional burden as a primary contributor to DDS across various populations.

Participants with high DDS had slightly higher average HbA1c levels (67.08 mmol/mol) compared to those with mild to moderate DDS (62.34 mmol/mol), though the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.227). This finding diverges from earlier studies, which have shown a strong correlation between DDS and poor glycemic control.[2526] Possible explanations could include cultural coping mechanisms, such as strong family support in Bahrain. Similar findings were observed in a 2023 study by Al-Banna et al.,[26] which emphasized the protective role of familial involvement in mitigating DDS in Middle Eastern populations.

A significant association was found between longer diabetes duration, insulin use, and poor glycemic control (P < 0.0001), consistent with prior evidence.[7] Hassan et al. (2021)[27] reported that individuals on insulin therapy experienced progressive worsening of HbA1c over time due to disease progression and treatment resistance.

Despite most participants (84.25%) not reporting major QoL issues on the EQ-5D-5L scale, notable proportions experienced pain/discomfort (47.75%) and anxiety/depression (32.5%), reaffirming the emotional burden among T2DM patients. Recent work by Santos et al. (2022)[28] and Elhadi et al. (2020)[29] has found that chronic pain and mental health comorbidities significantly compromise QoL in diabetic populations, particularly in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region.

QoL was notably poorer among participants with high BMI, mental health disorders, longer diabetes duration, and complications, which is consistent with studies by Sulaiman et al. (2023).[30] Women and unmarried individuals also reported lower QoL scores (P = 0.001 and P = 0.017), consistent with findings that social stressors, gender norms, and lower support networks impact disease coping in Middle Eastern contexts.[30]

Interestingly, no significant association was found between HbA1c and demographic variables, including age, gender, education, or income. Clinical variables, such as insulin use and diabetes duration, were more directly related to glycemic control, consistent with the findings of Tan et al. (2023),[31] who reported that biomedical factors were stronger predictors of HbA1c outcomes.

Elevated DDS was significantly associated with mental health conditions (P = 0.023), diabetes complications (P < 0.0001), and poor self-perceived blood sugar control (P < 0.0001). These findings align with studies suggesting that DDS impairs self-care behaviors and leads to poorer outcomes.[252628]

Overall, this study highlights the importance of integrated care models that address both the physical and psychological aspects of diabetes. WHO-EMRO (2022) recommends integrating mental health services in diabetes care pathways for improved outcomes in the region.[32]

Routine DDS screening using validated tools, such as the DDS-17, should be prioritized, particularly for long-term patients and individuals using insulin.[25] Multidisciplinary care teams involving diabetes educators, psychologists, and social workers are essential. Al-Banna et al. (2023)[26] noted that family-inclusive interventions were particularly effective in the Gulf.

QoL assessments, such as the EQ-5D-5L, should be employed routinely to personalize treatment plans and identify individuals at risk.[28]

This study expands our understanding of DDS in Bahrain, providing critical insights into the psychosocial dimensions of diabetes in a cultural context. The use of standardized instruments, such as the DDS-17 and EQ-5D-5L, enhances the reliability of the findings. However, limitations include the cross-sectional design (limiting causality), reliance on self-reported mental health data, lack of dietary assessment, and limited generalizability beyond Bahrain. The small number of high-DDS participants may also have reduced statistical power for some comparisons. By focusing on the Bahraini population, which has been frequently overlooked in the literature on DDS, this study employs a distinct methodology from earlier studies. It investigates the relationship between DDS, QoL, and glycemic control of HbA1c in a unified framework. This study did not find a significant correlation between high levels of DDS and poor glycemic control, whereas previous research found a strong correlation between the two. It implies that cultural factors, such as Bahrain’s strong family and community support, may lessen the detrimental physical effects of emotional distress. The study also highlights the significance of clinical factors by pointing out that sociodemographic factors have a limited impact on HbA1c levels. Additionally, it emphasizes the necessity of culturally sensitive, multidisciplinary care models for managing diabetes in the Gulf region.

Future research should explore the longitudinal effects of DDS on metabolic control and evaluate interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy, stress management programs, and family support models. Regional comparative studies could clarify cultural and systemic influences on diabetes outcomes across the Gulf.[2630]
