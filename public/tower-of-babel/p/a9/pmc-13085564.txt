The mediating role of pregnancy-related anxiety in the relationship between maternal family function and prenatal depression: the moderating effect of sleep latency in late pregnancy
Authors: Ye Li, Ling Ma, Chunyan Wang, Can Liu, Zexin Yu, Hao Wang, Shuqin Ma, Suzhen Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe current study aims to explore the relationship between Family function and depression, examining the mediation and moderation effects of pregnancy-related anxiety (PRA) and sleep latency, respectively.MethodA cross-sectional survey was conducted among pregnant women in the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region between February 2022 and December 2024, obtaining 7622 valid questionnaires. Model 4 and Model 7 in PROCESS were used to analyse the mediating effect of pregnancy-related anxiety and the moderating effect of sleep latency. A bootstrap method was conducted to test the mediating and moderating effects.ResultsAfter adjusting for covariates, the mediating effect of PRA explained 18.68% (95% CI: -0.094, -0.06) of the association between Family function and prenatal depression (EPD). The results indicate that the interaction term (Family function * sleep latency) has a negative effect on PRA (β = -0.096, 95% CI: -0.160, -0.032). This implies that the protective effect of good Family function in reducing PRA is weakened as sleep latency increases. Further analysis by different pregnancy periods revealed that this regulatory effect was only statistically significant in the third trimester (β = -0.127, 95% CI: -0.226, -0.028).ConclusionThis paper proved that the Family function negatively predicted depression, and PRA served as a mediator between Family function and depression during pregnancy. Besides, the protective role of sleep quality on individuals’ mental health was identified.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-07929-x.

Introduction

Depression and anxiety are the most common mental disorders experienced during the perinatal period. Previous studies have reported a high prevalence of prenatal depression, ranging from 15% to 65% [1]. An increasing number of studies have shown that anxiety and depression during pregnancy can affect the physical and mental health of both the mothers and the fetus. These conditions are associated with an increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as complications during pregnancy, preterm birth, poor fetal development, and low birth weight [2, 3]. Therefore, the pernicious and lasting consequences of prenatal depression for pregnant women and their infants present a major social public health challenge.

The existing literature indicates that the factors associated with negative emotions during pregnancy mainly include sociodemographic characteristics, such as maternal age, adverse obstetric history, educational level, and pregnancy complications, as well as social support and Family function [4–6]. As a protective and changeable factor, Family function has been widely studied in relation to negative emotions during pregnancy. Poor Family function is a key risk factor for anxiety and depression during pregnancy, whereas strong Family function can increase emotional support and communication among family members, thus helping pregnant women manage the stress and challenges of pregnancy more effectively [7]. A cross-sectional study in China found that the risk of prenatal depression symptoms with poor Family function was 3.67 times as much as that in the better-functioning group [8].

Pregnancy-related anxiety (PRA) is a unique psychological stress and physical symptom experienced by pregnant women during pregnancy due to concerns about fetal health, their own health and appearance, as well as medical, economic, and social support that may be involved in childbirth and childrearing [9]. It has been established that perceived social support and Family function are important protective factors for PRA [10]. As the basic unit of society, the family provides its members with support, resources, services, and other forms of assistance. Family function is a key variable in the psychological well-being of its members. Additionally, it should be noted that, in general, depression and anxiety are highly comorbid [3]. From the perspective of causal timing, Family function is formed before pregnancy, while PRA is the immediate psychological state under the pressure of pregnancy, and prenatal depression is the result of long-term psychological imbalance. Therefore, the level of Family function in pregnant women may indirectly affect their depressive symptoms through anxiety symptoms.

Sleep quality plays an important role in the overall health of individuals. The data show that the percentage of pregnant women with poor sleep quality is as high as 76% [11]. Increasing evidence shows that sleep quality during pregnancy has a negative effect on the mental health of pregnant women. Loret de Mola et al. [12] found that sleep latency was more likely to be associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms. Shariat [13] and colleagues reported that both state anxiety and trait anxiety were significantly associated with sleep quality during each pregnancy in a sample of 283 women. As a physiological state index, sleep quality is not directly affected by Family function. Sleep-related indicators are more suitable as regulatory variables, which indirectly affect the psychological state of pregnancy by affecting emotional regulation ability. However, few studies have examined whether sleep latency moderates the association between Family function and pregnancy-related affect.

Therefore, based on the above theoretical analysis and the theoretical model (see Fig. 1), this study aims to explore the mediating role of PRA in the relationship between Family function and prenatal depression. Specifically, we propose the following research hypotheses: (1) Family function is negatively associated with prenatal depression; (2) PRA plays a mediating role in the relationship between Family function and prenatal depression; (3) This study intends to examine the moderating effect of sleep latency on the mediating pathway, we hypothesize that sleep latency moderates the first stage of the mediation process (the link between Family function and PRA), such that the protective effect of good Family function on reducing PRA varies with the length of sleep latency. Ultimately, this study aims to suggest potential targets for future intervention studies for alleviating prenatal depression in a timely manner, thereby better safeguarding the psychological well-being of pregnant women and the health of both mothers and infants.

Method

Participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted at a tertiary hospital in the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, between February 2022 and December 2024, using a continuous sampling method. The inclusion criterion was women aged 18 years or older and no intellectual disability. The exclusion criterion was incomplete questionnaires(> 50% missing data). The survey covered general demographic information, family function, prenatal psychological conditions (including pregnancy-related anxiety and prenatal depression), and sleep quality. A total of 7630 questionnaires were collected. After 8 questionnaires were excluded because they were incomplete or all the answers were the same, 7622 valid questionnaires were obtained (Fig. 2). Prior to the commencement of the research, approval was obtained from the Ningxia Medical University (Grant number: No.2022-G007). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics

The demographic data included the following: age, residence, family per capita monthly income, occupation, educational level, duration of pregnancy (early trimester: <14weeks; second trimester: 15-27weeks; third trimester: >28weeks), character trait (introverted, biased, extroverted), pregnancy intention, only child, conceived way, adverse pregnancy, the reaction degree of early pregnancy(lighter, general, serious), pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2) was calculated based on participants’ pre-pregnancy self-reported weight.

Family functions

The Family functions of pregnant women were measured by family adaptation, partnership, growth, affection, and resolve (APGAR), compiled by Smilkstein [14]. The APGAR questionnaire consists of five items: adaptation, partnership, growth, affection, and resolve. Each item corresponds to a topic and is scored on a three-level scale: “often” is scored as 2 points, “sometimes” as 1 point, and “almost never” as 0 points. The total score ranges from 0 to 10 points, with higher scores indicating better Family functioning. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.897.

Pregnancy-related anxiety

Participants filled out the Pregnancy-related Anxiety Questionnaire (PAQ) developed by Xiao Limin [15] from Anhui Medical University. The scale consists of 13 items, related to self-concern, fetal health concern, and childbirth concern. Scores on each item range from 1= “not worried” to 4= “always worried”, and the range of the total sum score is 13 to 52. For the current study, the Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.732.

Prenatal depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using a 10-item self-reported Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [16], on a 4-point Likert scale. The total scores range between 0 and 30 points, with a score of more than 12 is a commonly used cutoff value for screening possible depression [17]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.763.

Sleep quality

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to assess individuals’ subjective sleep quality during the past month. The scale was developed by Buysse [18] in 1989. The PSQI consists of a total of 24 questions, of which 19 questions were independently assessed by the respondents in combination with their own conditions, and the remaining 5 questions were assessed by their bedmates or roommates. In this study, we mainly focused on 19 self-assessment questions directly related to each dimension of sleep quality, which are divided into seven parts, including seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medications, and daytime dysfunction, with each component is scored from 0 (no difficulty) to 3 (severe difficulty). The PSQI total score ranges from 0 to 21, where higher scores reflect poorer sleep quality. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.700.

Data analysis

The data in this study were double-entered via Epi Data 3.1 software. SPSS 23.0 and R 4.3.3 were used for statistical comparisons and analyses. The demographic characteristics and the measurement data were calculated via means ± standard deviations(SDs) or numbers(percentages). For comparisons between groups, the study adopted independent samples t-tests and analysis of variance to test the statistical significance of continuous variables. Pearson’s correlation analyses were also conducted to preliminarily assess the correlation among Family function, sleep quality, PRA, and prenatal depression. Model 4 and Model 7 in the SPSS macro program PROCESS v3.4.1 compiled by Hayes [19, 20] were used to test the moderated mediating effect. Specifically, Model 4 was selected to test the mediating role of PRA in the relationship between family function and prenatal depression. Model 7 was adopted to examine the moderated mediating effect of sleep latency, given its suitability for testing models where the moderator acts on the first stage of the mediation pathway. A bootstrap method with 5000 resamples was used to calculate the 95% CIs for the effects, ensuring the robustness of the mediation and moderation results.

The mediating effect model includes unadjusted and adjusted covariate models. With EPD as the final outcome, the adjusted covariates were statistically significant factors for EPD after univariate analysis, including residence, education, occupation, character trait, only-child, pregnancy intention, family per capita monthly income, adverse pregnancy, the reaction degree of early pregnancy, pre-pregnancy physical condition and fetal gender expectation.

Associations between sociodemographic factors and Family function, PRA, and EPD

The total score for the Family function was 8.29 ± 2.27. Univariate analysis revealed that factors such as habitancy, education, occupation, character traits, only child status, pregnancy intentions, family monthly per capita income, adverse pregnancy, degree of early pregnancy reaction, pre-pregnancy physical condition and fetal sex expectation significantly influenced the family care scores among pregnant women, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). The mean sleep quality scores for overall, subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbance, use of sleep medication and the daytime dysfunction were 4.98 ± 3.00, 1.00 ± 0.69, 0.98 ± 0.80, 0.53 ± 0.82, 0.54 ± 0.87, 1.10 ± 0.58, 0.01 ± 0.13, 0.83 ± 0.80, respectively.

Associations between Family function, sleep quality, PRA, and EPD

The correlations between variables were calculated and shown in Fig. 4. Family function levels were negatively associated with PRA (r = -0.179, P < 0.001) and EPD scores(r = -0.304, P < 0.001), suggesting that the higher levels of family dysfunction, the increased likelihood of them suffering from PRA and EPD. Meanwhile, PRA was proven to be significantly positively related to EPD (r = 0.392, P < 0.001), indicating that pregnant women experiencing PRA are more likely to develop depressive symptoms. Additionally, sleep latency was negatively correlated with Family function (r = -0.107, P < 0.001), and positively correlated with PRA (r = 0.178, P < 0.001) and EPD (r = 0.217, P < 0.001). Fig. 4A.

Further analysis stratified by different stages of pregnancy, and the results showed that during different pregnancy periods, Family functioning was negatively associated with sleep latency (r = -0.088, P = 0.002; r = -0.092, P < 0.001; r = -0.136 P < 0.001), PRA (r =-0.164 P < 0.001; r = -0.186 P < 0.001; r = -0.177, P < 0.001) and EPD (r =-0.255, P < 0.001; r = -0.306 P < 0.001; r =-0.324, P < 0.001), respectively. Additionally, the correlation analysis results for different stages of pregnancy also showed that the PRA and its various subscales were positively correlated with the EPD (r = 0.185, P < 0.001; r = 0.413, P < 0.001; r = 0.376, P < 0.001) and its respective subscales. Sleep latency was positively correlated with the PRA (r = 0.169, P < 0.001; r = 0.173, P < 0.001; r = 0.192, P < 0.001) and EPD (r = 0.253, P < 0.001; r = 0.193, P < 0.001; r = 0.230, P < 0.001). Fig. 4B-D.

Furthermore, a multiple linear regression model was constructed with EPD as the dependent variable, adjusting for all covariates identified as statistically significant in the univariate analyses. This analysis confirmed that higher levels of PRA (β = 0.204, 95% CI: 0.190, 0.218, P < 0.001) and longer sleep latency (β = 0.593, 95% CI: 0.500, 0.686, P < 0.001) were significantly associated with higher EPD scores, whereas better Family function (β = − 0.331, 95% CI: − 0.364, − 0.297, P < 0.001) was independently associated with lower EPD scores (Fig. S1).

The moderating effect of sleep quality on the mediating effect

In this study, Model 7 of the PROCESS macro was use to examine whether the interaction term (Family function * sleep latency) has a negative effect on PRA. The results are shown in Table 5. After adjusting for covariates and incorporating sleep latency into the model, it was found that sleep latency had a positive effect on PRA (β = 1.787, 95% CI: 1.245, 2.330). This finding indicates that the longer the sleep latency, the higher the level of anxiety in pregnant women. The interaction effect between Family function and sleep latency had a significant negative effect on PRA (β= -0.096, 95% CI: -0.160, -0.032), which implies that the protective effect of good Family function in reducing PRA is weakened as sleep latency increases. Specifically, longer sleep latency may exacerbate anxiety caused by inadequate Family function, further intensifying depressive symptoms. Furthermore, the differences in the mediating role of PRA between Family function and EPD among pregnant women with varying sleep scores were examined, and the results are presented in Table 6. When pregnant women had a lower sleep score of 0.182 (M − 1SD), the mediating effect of PRA was − 0.055 (95% CI: -0.075, -0.036), which did not include zero and was statistically significant. Conversely, when pregnant women had a higher sleep score of 1.779 (M + 1SD), the mediating effect of PRA was − 0.088 (95% CI: -0.106, -0.070), which was also statistically significant. Therefore, it can be inferred that as pregnant women’s sleep latency scores increase, the mediating effect related to anxiety intensifies. Fig. 6A.

Discussion

In this study, a questionnaire survey was conducted among 7622 pregnant women in a hospital in Ningxia to explore the mediating effect of PRA between Family function and EPD, and the moderating effect of sleep latency. Our cross-sectional data suggest that PRA may partially account for the association between family functioning and EPD; this pattern was observed during the COVID-19 containment and early-recovery periods (February 2022 – December 2024). Furthermore, during late pregnancy, sleep latency moderated the first half of the mediating pathway, specifically the relationship between Family functioning and PRA.

Consistent with the results of Shi et al. [21], the depression score among 7,622 pregnant women was 6.87 ± 3.71. The results of this study show that pregnant women with the following factors have higher EPD scores (P < 0.05), indicating that they are more likely to suffer from prenatal depression. This finding verifies the findings of previous studies, which have shown that unemployment, low education level, and family income are influential factors in prenatal depression among pregnant women [22, 23]. A possible reason for this observation is that pregnant women who are unemployed or have low education levels and low household incomes, typically experience financial hardship. Recent studies have also shown that the prevalence of depression in rural pregnant women is higher than that in urban pregnant women [24]. This may be related to the lack of social support in rural areas, the lack of medical resources, and the pressure of life. Consistent with this study, Bayrampour [25] reported that an introverted personality is a risk factor for depression. Pregnant women with an introverted personality may be more likely to feel lonely and helpless, thus increasing the risk of depression. In addition, this study also found that pregnant women who are not the only child in the family have higher depression scores than those who are the only child. This may be related to the fact that non-only-child pregnant women may face more challenges in terms of family support, interpersonal relationships, economic and social pressure, and pregnancy experience. Previous studies have confirmed that women with unintended pregnancies are more likely to suffer from depression than are those with planned pregnancies [26]. Unintended pregnancy imposes a significant psychological and work-related burden on the entire family, and the emphasis on offspring further exacerbates the stress associated with unintended pregnancy. Pregnant women who have experienced adverse pregnancy outcomes or severe pregnancy reactions are more prone to negative emotions like worry, anxiety, and even fear during pregnancy, which significantly predict prenatal depression [27, 28].

For pregnant women, family members constitute the primary source of social support. The correlation analysis results of this study indicate that Family functioning scores are negatively correlated with both PRA and EPD scores, which is consistent with previous research findings [10, 29]. A high level of Family function directly provides emotional support and psychological comfort, reduces the loneliness and helplessness of pregnant women, enhances their ability to cope with psychological stress, and thus reduces their levels of anxiety and depression. PRA is positively correlated with EPD, suggesting that an increase in PRA levels directly results in an elevation of depression levels. This aligns with the common psychological phenomenon that anxiety and depression frequently coexist, and furthermore, if anxiety remains unalleviated, this coexistence may result in depression [30, 31]. In addition, the results of this study found that PRA, as an intermediary variable, can partially mediate the impact of Family function on depressive symptoms. These findings suggest that insufficient family care may also indirectly aggravate depressive symptoms by increasing anxiety.

In addition to the direct impact of Family function and the mediating role of PRA, this study elucidated the moderating role of sleep during pregnancy. While most existing studies have established a close link between overall sleep quality and prenatal mental state [32, 33], few studies have specifically examined the relationships between sleep latency and Family function and mental state during pregnancy. The results of the correlation analysis in this study further indicated that sleep latency was negatively correlated with Family function and positively correlated with both PRA and EPD. Consequently, sleep latency was employed as a moderating variable to delve deeper into its moderating influence on the mediating effects’ outcomes. The results of the moderated mediation model indicated that the interaction between Family function and sleep latency exerted a significant negative impact on PRA. Further stratification by sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics indicated that this moderating effect was statistically significant, specifically among women who were urban residents, had lower educational attainment (high school or below), were younger (aged ≤ 34 years), or had no history of adverse pregnancy. This suggests that the protective effect of family function against anxiety is most vulnerable to disruption by sleep difficulties in these specific subgroups, which may relate to their distinct psychosocial resource profiles or stress contexts.

Further trimester-stratified analysis revealed that the statistically significant effect of this interaction on PRA was observed only during the third trimester. In the third trimester, the growing size of the fetus leads to increased pressure on the mother’s body, causing back pain and leg cramps, which significantly disrupt sleep [34]. These physical discomforts make it harder for pregnant women to find a comfortable sleeping position, thereby prolonging sleep onset latency. The current study also supported that sleep patterns worsen as pregnancy progresses [35], and poor sleep quality during this period was associated with an increased risk of perinatal mood and anxiety disorders [36]. In addition, women may experience significant stress and worry about the delivery process, pain, and potential complications in the third trimester, contributing to increased anxiety levels. These physiological stresses and poor family function work together to form a double burden, which puts pregnant women in a vicious circle and further aggravates perinatal mental disorders. Sensitivity analyses revealed that, after full covariate adjustment, only the interactions between family function and sleep latency or daytime dysfunction exerted a significant moderating influence on PRA. Among these, daytime dysfunction represents a non-specific, subjective appraisal that is easily confounded by general fatigue or mood state and therefore offers limited insight into the precise sleep pathophysiology characteristic of late pregnancy. In contrast, sleep latency quantifies the interval between settling in bed and sleep onset, thereby capturing the initiation difficulties that arise from third-trimester physiological perturbations. Consequently, interventions that simultaneously optimise family support and target sleep-onset latency are theoretically grounded and clinically expedient. Accordingly, to alleviate PRA and attenuate antenatal depressive symptoms, it is advisable to enhance family function through late-pregnancy family care training for spouses and main family members led by obstetric nurses or psychologists, and provide personalized sleep guidance (e.g., cognitive-behavioral techniques for insomnia, sleep hygiene education, relaxation training) for those with prolonged sleep latency.

While numerous studies have examined the relationships among Family function during pregnancy, the sleep quality of pregnant women, and their mental health status, few have investigated the mediating role of PRA and the interaction between Family function and sleep latency in prenatal depression. Drawing on a large-sample study, this research explores how pregnancy-related anxiety mediates the relationship between Family function and prenatal depression, and examines the moderating effect of sleep latency on this mediation. This study provides new insights for enhancing the mental health of pregnant women. Furthermore, it should be noted that the data collection period of this study covers the latter stages and the post-peak recovery phase of the COVID-19 pandemic. Pandemic-related social restrictions and adjustments in healthcare resources may have influenced the psychological state and family functioning of pregnant women, suggesting that the generalizability of the findings should be considered within this specific context. Therefore, future research needs to further verify the results of this study in different time contexts.

Limitations

However, a few limitations of this study also merit consideration. First, the single-center sampling and the region’s unique cultural context jointly limit the generalizability of findings. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships or temporal sequences among family function, pregnancy-related anxiety, sleep latency, and prenatal depression. Third, the reliance on self-report measures for key variables may introduce biases, which could compromise data accuracy.

Conclusion

In summary, the following conclusions can be drawn from this study: Family function has a direct effect on depression; and PRA partially mediates the relationship between family care and depression, and within this mediation model, there exists a moderating effect of the interaction between sleep latency in the third trimester and family care.
