Transfer RNAs: A treasure trove that keeps on giving
Authors: Venkat Gopalan, Karin Musier-Forsyth
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransfer RNAs are the adaptors in protein synthesis that provide the key link between the nucleic acid-based genetic blueprint and proteins. While the central role of tRNAs in protein synthesis has been known for over 60 years, recent discoveries of their many non-canonical functions and therapeutic potential have heightened interest in tRNAs. In this thematic series, we highlight some of the developments presented at the recent biennial “International tRNA Workshop”. The topics chosen reflect advances that were enabled by the latest technological breakthroughs in structure determination and small RNA sequencing and emphasize the prospects and challenges of tRNA-based medicines to treat human diseases.

Since its inception in the late 1960s in Cambridge, England, the biennial International tRNA Workshop has been held in different locations worldwide. The 28th installment of this conference was organized by Juan Alfonzo and Michael Ibba and held at The Ohio State University, Columbus, Ohio, in June 2022. We celebrate this meeting in this JBC Thematic Series with coverage by some attendees of topics that encapsulate recent exciting advances in tRNA biology spanning molecular biophysics, enzymology, evolutionary biology, and medicine.

The progress of all experimental research is typified by fitful spurts, which are often galvanized by methodological innovations that overcome major technical hurdles. The field of tRNA biology is no exception; the following reviews spotlight many advances during the last decade that were founded on two groundbreaking methods. First, single-particle cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has revolutionized structural biology, empowered by progress in detection, image processing, and three-dimensional reconstruction techniques. The recent surge in high-resolution cryo-EM structures of various enzymes bound to tRNA substrates has led to a better appreciation of structure-function relationships of different tRNA-centered cellular machineries and revealed important biophysical principles. As the most abundant non-coding RNA in the cell (based on number), tRNAs are subject to the action of various processing/modifying enzymes and continue to be fertile ground for studies of molecular recognition and catalysis. Second, RNA-seq has transformed all biology given its ability to provide in high-throughput unbiased, and quantitative profiles of all RNAs in a sample. Variants of RNA-seq have been leveraged to gain a deeper understanding of different cellular processes including translation, and thereby a firmer handle on the pathophysiology of some human diseases arising from dysfunctional tRNAs or enzymes acting on tRNAs. We preview below some of the facets covered in this special JBC collation.

Biela et al. (1) survey a gallery of recent cryo-EM structures of tRNAs bound to different enzymes and present valuable insights from their analysis. Despite the inevitable sequence variation wrought by different evolutionary forces, most tRNAs adopt a common L-shaped three-dimensional structure. Post-transcriptional modifications, which confer stability and promote self-organization, appear essential for steering divergent tRNA sequences to similar structures. The near-invariant tRNA dimensions (∼60 × 70 × 25 Å) in turn are critical because they are exploited by different enzymes as part of molecular ruler mechanisms (e.g., adenosine deaminase, RNase P). However, even within the context of a stable tertiary fold, tRNAs undergo local and global conformational changes upon binding to processing/modification enzymes, which have evolved recognition determinants that trigger structural changes obligatory for catalysis; such substrate/position selectivity considerations are likely to be broadly relevant. The new structures described by Glatt et al. (2) provide a framework for understanding tRNA dynamics and for dissecting the functional gains from specific modifications in tRNAs.

Despite the shared evolutionary origins of many archaeal and eukaryotic enzymes, eukaryotic versions often have additional subunits that are presumably related to functional diversification. This idea is exemplified in some enzymes involved in tRNA processing, notably the tRNA splicing endonuclease (TSEN) that catalyzes intron removal from tRNAs. Hayne et al. (2) describe the recently solved cryo-EM structures of the human TSEN complex, which had been hard to pin down, unlike the archaeal counterpart solved nearly 2 decades ago. Human TSEN is a heterotetramer of two catalytic and two non-catalytic subunits, while the archaeal cousin can even function as a homotetramer. The cryo-EM structures of the precursor-tRNA bound to human TSEN reveal an evolutionarily conserved mechanism where three residues flanking and including the 3′-splicing cleavage site are flipped out and held in place around a catalytic triad that ensures site-specific bond breakage. The ability of TSEN to act on mRNAs and rRNAs motivates structural studies with different substrates to decipher the functions of the additional protein subunits in the human/eukaryotic variants. Mutations in all four subunits of human TSEN are the basis for the family of pontocerebellar hypoplasia neurological disorders. Interestingly, the disease-causing mutations are distal to the catalytic site, suggesting dampened stability/folding or altered protein–protein interactions as the basis for dysfunction.

While the fluctuations between the fast and slow translation of an mRNA might make the ribosome appear capricious, Davyt, Bharti, and Ignatova (3) detail how this discontinuous translation is directed by spatiotemporal changes in the composition and abundance of individual tRNA isoacceptors/isodecoders. Results from Ribo-seq studies—a variant of RNA-seq that profiles the position of the ribosomes on translated mRNAs with codon precision—paint a compelling correlation: codons with higher ribosomal A-site occupancy are decoded by low-abundance tRNAs and vice versa. Such a framework has important implications for biotechnology, including the objective of ensuring robust recombinant protein overexpression through the choice of appropriate codons in the foreign mRNA to match the tRNAome of a heterologous host. Furthermore, the dynamics of tRNA occupancy are critical for understanding diseases, as pathologic mutations can alter rates of local translation and thus protein folding/expression. Epistasis typically refers to changes in the expression of one gene by one or more other genes; however, the review by Ignatova and co-workers draws attention to intriguing findings on intragenic epistasis when two mutations (even one synonymous and one non-synonymous) within an mRNA variably alter the local speed of translation to change the final outcome from that observed with each mutation alone. Untangling such unexpected crosstalk between intragenic mutations may help explain the phenotypic diversity reported for the same genetic mutation and lead to the design of more effective treatments for human diseases.

The expectation that methodological improvements will unveil new biological features and basic principles is embodied in the article by Chen and Zhou (4), who summarize the growing inventory of small non-coding RNAs (sncRNAs), including those derived from tRNAs. Prevalent RNA modifications (e.g., methylation) and termini with different functional groups (e.g., 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate) pose major challenges to sncRNA sequencing. These barriers have recently been circumvented by modifying RNA-seq protocols to include pre-enzymatic treatments, which allow adaptor ligation and reverse transcriptase readthrough. These newer methods (e.g., PANDORA-seq) have led to the discovery of a vast number of sncRNAs in different cells/tissues. In addition to a description of the existence of a broader sncRNA-centered regulatory RNA landscape that extends far beyond RNAi, Chen and Zhou (4) articulate the hypothesis that many of these sncRNAs function in an aptamer-like fashion and that their ubiquity in all domains of life (unlike RNAi) represent a more ancient mechanism that ultimately paved the path to RNAi. Regardless of this postulated evolutionary trajectory, reports of new sncRNA-mediated mechanisms for controlling gene expression and even protein function attest to the pervasive roles of RNAs as master regulators, a possible legacy from the RNA world.

Altering the progress and clinical outcome of heritable genetic diseases remains an important goal of modern biology. Anastassiadis and Köhrer (5) elaborate on the novel idea that a single tRNA medicine could treat a wide variety of diseases associated with a common mutation, such as premature termination due to mutation of a specific codon. This “gene-agnostic” approach differs from existing strategies because patients would be categorized based on the identity of the mutated codon and not their disease, and thus could greatly accelerate treatment modalities for rare and ultra-rare disorders. RNA therapeutics is enjoying a renaissance due to the recent overwhelming success of RNA-based vaccines, and there is growing commercial interest in RNA-based medicines. Although the authors of this review share this optimism, they emphasize the immediate need for enabling technologies to render tRNAs (or tRNA fragments) as potent drugs. Challenges for tRNA therapeutics include more economical synthesis, knowledge of sequence/chemical modifications that will enhance efficacy by fine-tuning translation dynamics and lowering degradation/immunogenicity, and better delivery vehicles. Anastassiadis and Köhrer also discuss how machine learning—an indispensable computational complement to experimental benchwork on account of its ability to deconstruct data, infer patterns, and predict outcomes—could fast-track drug discovery and optimization through a “design-build-test-learn” iterative cycle.

Collectively, this series of reviews showcases how tRNAs continue to be a treasure trove of surprises in different realms of biology. Addressing the outstanding questions enumerated in the following articles will help further unravel the astounding diversity of tRNA forms and functions, which have already far exceeded their initial description as an essential intermediary for decoding the genetic blueprint. We eagerly look forward to learning about the latest advances in tRNA biology at the 2024 International tRNA Workshop in Kanazawa, Japan!
