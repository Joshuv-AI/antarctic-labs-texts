Bioinspired recognition in metal-organic frameworks enabling precise sieving separation of fluorinated propylene and propane mixtures
Authors: Wei Xia, Zhijie Zhou, Liangzheng Sheng, Lihang Chen, Fuxing Shen, Fang Zheng, Zhiguo Zhang, Qiwei Yang, Qilong Ren, Zongbi Bao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The separation of fluorinated propane/propylene mixtures remains a major challenge in the electronics industry. Inspired by biological ion channels with negatively charged inner walls that allow selective transport of cations, we presented a series of formic acid-based metal-organic frameworks (MFA) featuring biomimetic multi-hydrogen confined cavities. These MFA materials, especially the cobalt formate (CoFA), exhibit specific recognition of hexafluoropropylene (C3F6) while facilitating size exclusion of perfluoropropane (C3F8). The dual-functional adsorbent offers multiple binding sites to realize intelligent selective recognition of C3F6, as supported by theoretical calculations and in situ spectroscopic experiments. Mixed-gas breakthrough experiments validate the capability of CoFA to produce high-purity (>5 N) C3F8 in a single step. Importantly, the stability and cost-effective scalable synthesis of CoFA underscore its extraordinary potential for industrial C3F6/C3F8 separations. This bioinspired molecular recognition approach opens new avenues for the efficient purification of fluorinated electronic specialty gases.

Introduction

Electronic specialty gases (ESGs), especially fluorinated gases (F-gases), are fundamental to the production of semiconductors, the advancement of artificial intelligence, and other high-tech applications. Among these, perfluoropropane (C3F8) is recognized for its low global warming potential and high operational efficiency attributed to its low fluorine-to-carbon (F-C) ratio. It is extensively utilized in etching and cleaning processes within the semiconductor industry1–4. Large-scale production of C3F8 typically involves the fluorination of hexafluoropropylene (C3F6), resulting in a product stream contaminated with 1 to 10% of C3F6. As the electronics industry’s standards for precision escalate, driven by rapid progress in information technology, the need for ESGs with high or ultra-high purity (5 N or 6 N) becomes imperative. Traditional separation methods, such as extractive distillation, leverage the volatility difference between C3F6 and C3F8 (ΔT = 5.9 K), but they demand energy-intensive conditions, including about 30 distillation trays and multiple towers to achieve the desired purity of C3F85. Alternatively, the use of porous materials for differential adsorption represents a promising strategy, allowing operation under milder pressure and temperature conditions6.

Current adsorbents typically differentiate molecules based on factors such as polarizability7–9, size10–12, shape13–15, and coordination affinities16,17. However, separating the C3F6/C3F8 pair within perfluorinated mixtures poses a unique challenge due to the subtle differences in their properties, unlike systems with more distinct molecular disparities, such as CF4/N218, SF6/N219–22, etc23,24. This necessitates the development of smart porous materials capable of specific molecular recognition and adaptability to guest molecules, a property that is invaluable in applications ranging from gas purification to selective catalysis25,26. Over the last decade, the field of reticular chemistry has made significant strides in creating advanced materials, particularly metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)27–29, with customizable pore architectures that are not readily achievable with traditional adsorbents like zeolites22 or activated carbons18,20,23,24. The design principles for these advanced MOF adsorbents include the functionalization of pore surfaces with selective binding sites to increase impurity adsorption, and fine-tuning pore dimensions to favor the exclusion of desired molecules, thereby achieving the pinnacle of molecular sieving30–33.

Biological systems have long demonstrated the profound utility of molecular recognition, observable in phenomena such as pathogen phagocytosis34, enzyme-substrate specificity35, and selective ion transport across bio-membranes36. Inspired by these natural processes, recent scientific initiatives have sought to replicate such mechanisms to improve the functionality and intelligence of synthetic adsorbents. These advancements are pivotal for the efficient separation and purification of important gases, including mixtures of oxygen and nitrogen37–39, methane and nitrogen40,41, and various hydrocarbons42. For instance, biological oxygen transporters, like hemoglobin, utilize a planar tetradentate porphyrin macrocycle to coordinate with a central iron atom for oxygen binding and transport43. Drawing from this, researchers have designed MOFs with similar metal centers to selectively capture oxygen over nitrogen at room temperature37. Additionally, nitrogen-fixing enzymes in nature employ metal centers capable of selectively binding and activating nitrogen44. This concept has been adapted into MOFs with specific metal sites that preferentially adsorb nitrogen over methane, offering a novel approach to their separation41.

In contemplation of the separation of fluorinated gas mixtures, we have drawn inspiration from bio-membrane ion channels with negatively charged (-COO−)-modified inner walls and sub-nanostructures, which enable selective and accelerated Na+/K+ transport through the electrostatic attraction of anions45 (Fig. 1a–c). Consequently, the construction of positive potential traps (PPTs) represents an elegant approach to enhance the capture of electron-rich F-gases. Furthermore, extensive research efforts focused on capturing and separating hydrocarbons (CxHy) provide specific guidance for addressing this challenge. In the realm of adsorbents for CxHy, MOFs equipped with O22−-modified open metal sites (OMSs)42 or high localized charge density are exemplified by the IRMOF-74 series and anionic pillared frameworks (AP-MOF), respectively. The ligand F atoms (SiF62−, GeF62−, etc.) of the latter facilitate multiple hydrogen bonding interactions with the H atoms of CxHy9,10,46, effectively restricting the guest molecules (Fig. 1d). Drawing upon these insights, we hypothesize that if atomic inversions were to occur, a multi-hydrogen host could, similarly, create an effective positive-potential transport zone for trapping fluorinated molecules with negative potential (CxFy) (Fig. 1e). Guided by this concept, we investigated the formic acid ligand (HCOOH) coupled with metal(II) cations (M= Co, Ni, Mg, and Zn) for the construction of ultra-microporous MOFs (denote as MFA). As the simplest building linker of the formula HCOOH, deprotonated formic acid exposes a sole hydrogen atom, and the shielding effect of the coordinated oxygen facilitates the formation of a porous network with multiple hydrogens. Moreover, the formic acid family of MOFs serves as an ideal platform for sub-Å editing of pore sizes through the substitution of metal species, opening up great possibilities for ideal molecular sieving separation. Indeed, the pore size (~5 Å) and pore confinement for molecules endow MFA with the desired sieving capability for separating C3F8 from C3F6. As a result, CoFA exhibits the highest C3F6 capacity (~2.0 mmol g−1 at 100 kPa) and optimal performance in dynamic breakthrough experiments (C3F6/C3F8, 10/90, v/v) under ambient conditions, surpassing other top-performing materials. Additionally, high-performing CoFA could be mass-produced with two simple, low-cost, commercially available reagents, establishing it as the benchmark material for industrial C3F6/C3F8 separations. Importantly, the separation mechanism has been confirmed through extensive theoretical calculations and experimental investigations (e.g., in situ FT-IR and 1H → 19F heteronuclear correlation solid-state NMR techniques). This bionic-derived molecular recognition approach provides valuable guidelines for future deep purification of industrially fluorinated ESGs.

Results

Structure and porosity analysis

The MFA family of compounds has garnered attention due to their intrinsic porous magnetic properties, featuring three-dimensional (3D) long-range magnetic ordering and the capacity for their magnetic behavior to be modulated by guest molecules48. Members of this compound series, such as those based on Co, Ni, Zn, and Mg, can be synthesized with high yields through a straightforward reaction of the corresponding divalent metal salt hydrate and formic acid (Fig. 2a). The crystallographic elucidation of both the as-synthesized and activated states of MFA have been determined by Kobayashi49,50 and Rood et al.51 using single crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) techniques. Structurally, MFA compounds are characterized by an open diamond-like framework comprised of M-centered MM4 tetrahedral nodes. Upon activation, MFA materials exhibit a 1D helical channel with a consistent window diameter of approximately 5 Å along the b-axis (Supplementary Fig. 1). This particular pore dimension is well-suited to the molecular dimensions of fluorinated C3 species, hinting at the potential for selective molecular sieving (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). Remarkably, the internal surfaces of these pore channels are lined with a rich array of hydrogen atoms originating from the formate linker (HCOO−). Preliminary investigations into the electrostatic potential distributions within these pore environments (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 4) have revealed positive potential regions engendered by the hydrogen-confined cavities, which are poised to offer an array of supramolecular binding sites for guest molecules through interactions such as hydrogen bonds. This is particularly pertinent for negatively-polarized F-gases, fostering the hypothesis that these MOFs, with their size-tailored lock-and-key topology, may significantly enhance the adsorptive affinity for C3F6, thus facilitating high uptake while concurrently discriminating against C3F8 to achieve the desired selectivity (Fig. 2c).Fig. 2Structural characterization and adsorption efficacy of MFA materials.a Inorganic and organic components of the MFA crystal structure, with cobalt-formic (CoFA) serving as a representative example. b, c Magnified views of the hydrogen-confined pore cavities within the framework, alongside their electrostatic potential maps, demonstrating the spatial distribution of potential sites for molecular interactions. d The adsorption isotherms for C3F6 and C3F8 across a temperature range of 273, 283, and 298 K. e A comparative analysis of the C3F6 uptake ratios and capacities at a low pressure (10 kPa) for MFA (top) against the C3F8 adsorption capacities of other renowned porous materials at 298 K (bottom). The purity of bulk MFA samples was ascertained by comparison of the powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns with those simulated from the crystal structures (Supplementary Fig. 5). The activation of MFA compounds was achieved under dynamic vacuum conditions (Supplementary Table 1), leading to materials with permanent porosity as evidenced by gas adsorption isotherms conducted at 77 K for nitrogen (N2) and 195 K for carbon dioxide (CO2). Specifically, the N2 adsorption isotherm for CoFA demonstrated a reversible type-I profile, with a total uptake capacity of 120.9 cm3 g−1 and a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of 377.5 m2 g−1. Pore size distribution analysis using the Non-Local Density Functional Theory (NLDFT) model suggested a pore size for CoFA of about 5.6 Å, marginally less than the kinetic diameter of C3F8, which aligns with the observed selective exclusion between C3F6 and C3F8. Parallel studies of the remaining MOFs in the series (Ni, Mg, Zn) corroborated these findings, underscoring the potential for selective gas separation (Supplementary Figs. 8–11).

Single-gas adsorption isotherm and selectivity

Isotherms quantifying the adsorption of C3 perfluorocarbons on MFA materials were recorded at temperatures of 273, 283, and 298 K, with pressures up to 1 bar (Fig. 2d). Owing to the plentiful hydrogen bonding sites on the hydrophobic pore surfaces, all MFA demonstrated pronounced C3F6 uptake across the examined temperatures. Specifically, CoFA exhibited a high C3F6 adsorption capacity of approximately 2.0 mmol g−1 at 298 K and 1 bar, surpassing its counterparts: NiFA (0.84 mmol g−1), MgFA (1.70 mmol g−1), and ZnFA (1.23 mmol g−1). This enhanced uptake capability is attributed to CoFA’s slightly greater pore volume. At a lower pressure of 10 kPa and at 298 K, the C3F6 adsorption capacities were recorded as 0.71, 0.44, 0.27, and 0.24 mmol g−1 for CoFA, MgFA, NiFA, and ZnFA, respectively, with CoFA maintaining a significant lead. Interestingly, all four MOFs showed negligible capacity for C3F8 adsorption at 298 K, a finding that is quite exceptional among well-known porous solids such as MOFs, activated carbon, and zeolites (Fig. 2e). For instance, the C3F8 uptake by CoFA (0.16 mmol g−1) is distinctly less than those measured for Cu-BTC (4.52 mmol g−1) and BASF-300 (MIL-100Fe, 3.33 mmol g−1). When evaluating the uptake ratio of the two gases at 10 kPa, MFA exhibited values that were substantially higher than those of the aforementioned porous materials and only marginally below our previously reported flexible molecular sieve MOF, Ca-tcpb (127)45, positioning them within the ideal range for molecular sieving. Additionally, we explored the kinetics of adsorption and desorption for MFA at 298 K. CoFA, in particular, demonstrated the most rapid adsorption kinetics for C3F6, coupled with relatively swift desorption of the adsorbed gas, a feature likely due to its comparatively larger pore size (Supplementary Figs. 12, 13). Therefore, CoFA’s superior combined performance renders it one of the most effective materials for the selective separation of C3F6 from C3F8.

Adsorption enthalpy

The differential adsorption between the two gas molecules can be partially elucidated by the measured isosteric heat of adsorption (Qst). Pursuing further insights into the selective adsorption of C3F6 over C3F8, we conducted gravimetric analyses and simultaneous calorimetric (TGA-DSC) at 1 bar. MFA materials exhibited no detectable heat of adsorption for C3F8, in stark contrast to the distinct exothermic response for C3F6 (Fig. 3a). The Qst values for C3F6 were found in descending order: CoFA (33.1 kJ mol−1) > ZnFA (31.7 kJ mol−1) > NiFA (29.1 kJ mol−1) > MgFA (27.8 kJ mol−1). The elevated Qst of CoFA corroborates its stronger binding affinity, aligning with the adsorption trends observed. Importantly, the saturated adsorbents displayed efficient regeneration, as indicated by their moderate Qst values relative to those of activated carbons, zeolites, and other MOFs (Supplementary Fig. 16). As demonstrated in Fig. 3b, we initiated with the adsorption of C3F6 using fully activated CoFA. Subsequent cycles of adsorption, from the 2nd to the 9th repetition, were conducted with room temperature activation (5 mmHg vacuum for 0.5 h), revealing only a minor decrease in adsorption capacity. Particularly, during the 10th cycle, the original C3F6 uptake was restored by applying a higher activation temperature. Dynamic breakthrough experiments

Encouraged by the MFA’s exclusion of C3F8, we performed dynamic breakthrough experiments with a binary C3F6/C3F8 (10/90) mixture in a series of packed columns filled with activated MFA at a flow rate of 1.92 ml min−1 and temperature of 298 K. CoFA demonstrated superior separation efficiency compared to Zn, Mg, and Ni based MFA (Supplementary Fig. 17). In the CoFA column, C3F8 eluted immediately, while C3F6 was retained for 44 min (Fig. 3c). During this period, the production of C3F8 from the outlet exceeded 99.999% purity, with a calculated yield of 101.2 L kg−1 per cycle. Assessing the material’s practicality, the recyclability and structural stability of the CoFA adsorbent were tested through five consecutive adsorption-desorption cycles. The CoFA bed retained its ability to exclude C3F8 without any loss in performance, consistently producing 5 N purity gas with a steady retention time of 44 min for the contaminant (Fig. 3d). The C3F6 uptake and C3F8 production values for CoFA did not exhibit any significant degradation over the cycles, affirming its exceptional recyclability. Furthermore, the column’s initial dynamic separation efficiency was preserved even after a year of storage, as validated by subsequent testing (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 18).

Computational studies and mechanism analysis

To elucidate the nature of host-guest interactions and affirm the analogies drawn to bionic mechanisms, we employed grand canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulations and first-principles dispersion-corrected density functional theory (DFT-D) calculations. The GCMC-derived adsorption density distribution revealed that C3F6 predominantly interacts with hydrogen atoms (Fig. 4a). We determined the most favorable adsorption configurations of C3F6, which are schematically illustrated in Fig. 4b and Supplementary Figs. 19, 20. Within the constrained environment of CoFA, the six fluorine atoms of C3F6 engage in multiple C-F···H hydrogen bonding interactions (ranging from 2.350 to 3.310 Å) with the free hydrogen from the formate ligand, interactions that are more compact than those in the other MFAs. The binding energies of C3F6 to the MFAs were calculated to be 56.1 kJ mol−1 for CoFA, 43.6 kJ mol−1 for MgFA, and 35.2 kJ mol−1 for ZnFA, indicating the strongest binding affinity within CoFA. Furthermore, electrostatic potential surface maps of C3F6 within the MFA cavities were generated using the VMD software, based on the Multiwfn program calculations, to identify strong binding sites and visualize electrostatic potential differences, particularly around Hδ+---Fδ- interactions (Fig. 4c, d). To further probe the specifics of C3F6 adsorption at Hδ+ sites within MFA, we recorded in situ diffuse reflectance FT-IR spectra under a pure C3F6 atmosphere at 30 °C (Fig. 4e). Upon exposure to C3F6, the infrared spectrum exhibited time-dependent changes, with two distinct C-F stretching resonances at 1120 and 1085 cm−1. The νas(C3F6) band displayed a slight downshift, indicative of hydrogen-bonding interactions, with the extent of the frequency shift corresponding to the strength of the intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Additionally, the bands associated with C-H stretching vibrations in the 2900–2800 cm−1 range and aldehyde C-H stretching at 2780–2680 cm−1 were significantly altered upon C3F6 adsorption, suggesting direct interactions with the hydrogen atoms of the formate linker. These findings are in good agreement with the theoretical predictions. In contrast, no similar spectral changes were observed upon C3F8 exposure, reaffirming the selective exclusion by the material (Supplementary Figs. 21–24). Two-dimensional (2D) solid-state NMR spectroscopy was applied to provide further evidence of the H-F interactions. Given the large nuclear spin of cobalt and nickel, which can result in broad spectral peaks that may obscure other signals, we selected MgFA for obtaining 2D heteronuclear 1H → 19F correlation spectra52. Correlation signals corresponding to interactions between C3F6 fluorine atoms and formic hydrogen atoms were evident, with robust signals at coordinates (−70.9, 9.3), (−87.9, 9.3), (−95.5, 9.3), and (−105.9, 9.3) ppm (Fig. 4f). These results provide strong support for the proposed hydrogen-fluorine interaction mechanism and reinforce the concept of positive potential channels as an effective trapping mechanism for fluorinated gases.

Scalable synthesis

The exceptional capabilities of CoFA as an adsorbent necessitate an economically viable scale-up synthesis for prospective industrial applications. A critical evaluation of cost factors associated with the synthesis of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) indicates that the expense of raw materials, especially the organic linkers, often constitutes a significant portion of the total cost. Fortunately, the raw materials required for CoFA synthesis are readily available at scale from commercial suppliers, with formic acid being an inexpensive, high-volume commodity chemical produced globally at rates exceeding 1.3 million tons annually, finding uses across various industries. The scalable synthesis of CoFA can be achieved through a simple reflux process (Fig. 4g). Previous reports have demonstrated the preparation of milligram quantities of CoFA by heating a mixture of formic acid and cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate in DMF at 100 °C. However, the limitations imposed by the size of the reaction vessel and the method of heating pose challenges for upscaling. To address these challenges and adhere to safety and environmental considerations, our focus has shifted towards developing a green, scalable synthetic approach. Following successful pilot reactions at the 10 g scale, we expanded the reaction scope, rapidly achieving production at the hundred-gram scale, which bodes well for kilogram-scale synthesis viability (Fig. 4g). Importantly, the large-scale synthesized samples maintained comparable crystallinity and gas adsorption performance to their small-scale counterparts, as confirmed by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) and gas adsorption assessments (Supplementary Figs. 25–27). Although a marginal decrease in adsorption capacity was observed, the selectivity against C3F8 was preserved (Fig. 4h). Column breakthrough experiments conducted on the CoFA synthesized at 10 g and 100 g scales consistently yielded C3F8 with a purity of 5 N from the effluent in a single step (Fig. 4i). The convergence of a mild synthesis route, high separation efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and scalability affirm CoFA’s promise as an industrially relevant agent for addressing the separation challenges of C3F6/C3F8 mixtures (Supplementary Table 2). This synthesis approach not only demonstrates the practical feasibility of CoFA for large-scale applications but also sets a precedent for the future industrial production of MOFs with similar structural and performance characteristics.

Discussion

In conclusion, our investigation into formic acid-based metal-organic frameworks (MFAs) has unveiled a biomimetic strategy for the selective adsorption of fluorinated compounds, harnessing multi-hydrogen confined spaces for targeted molecular recognition. Among these, CoFA emerges as an exemplary candidate, distinguished by its significant C3F6 adsorption capacity, effective exclusion of C3F8, and efficient dynamic separation performance under ambient conditions. The successful upscaling of CoFA synthesis to an industrial level in a cost-effective manner further underscores its potential for practical applications in the separation of fluorinated compounds. This study not only provides a blueprint for the development of advanced materials tailored for electronic specialty gases (ESGs) but also offers valuable insights into the selection criteria and design principles necessary for state-of-the-art adsorbents. The implications of this work extend beyond the immediate application to C3F6/C3F8 separation, potentially guiding future research and development efforts aimed at addressing broader challenges in environmental sustainability and industrial process optimization.

Methods

Materials

All reagents and solvents were obtained commercially and used as received without further purification. N2 (99.999%), C3F6 (99.999%), C3F8 (99.999%), He (99.999%), and mixed gases of C3F6/C3F8 (10/90, v/v) were purchased from Hangzhou JinGong Company (China).

Synthesis of MFA samples

CoFA or NiFA

Co(NO3)2 6H2O (1.309 g, 4.4 mmol), formic acid (1 ml) were dissolved in 10 ml of DMF, the resulting mixture was placed in a preheated oven at 373 K for 24 h and cooled down to room temperature, and the pink powder obtained was washed with DMF for three times. For NiFA, replace the Co(II) with Ni(NO3)2 6H2O (1.279 g, 4.4 mmol) and all else remains the same.

CoFA (large-scale)

The reactant quantities were increased by a factor of 20, and other reaction conditions remained constant, producing samples weighing over ten grams. To prepare hundred-gram samples, Co(NO3)2 6H2O (192 g, 0.66 mol) was fully dissolved in DMF (1.5 L). Subsequently, 150 ml of formic acid was added to the solution, stirring under reflux at 373 K for 24 h. The mixture was cooled to room temperature, filtered, and washed with DMF.

MgFA

Mg(NO3)2 6H2O (0.77 g, 3 mmol) was dissolved in 10 ml DMF, followed by adding 0.23 ml formic acid and mixing homogeneously, then reacting at 383 K for 40 h, centrifuge the resulting mixture and wash with DMF.

ZnFA

Zn(NO3)2 6H2O (3.0 g, 10.08 mmol) was dissolved in 25 ml of methanol, which was noted as solution A. 2.0 g of formic acid was added to 25 ml of methanol with 2.0 g of triethyl, which was noted as solution B. A was added dropwise to B and stirred at room temperature for 12 h. It was filtered and washed three times in methanol to obtain a white product.

Characterization

PXRD data were collected using microcrystalline samples on a Rigaku Miniflex 600 x-ray diffractometer (Cu-Kα, λ = 1.5418 Å). The measurement parameters include a scan speed of 10° min−1, a step size of 0.02°, and a scan range of 2θ from 5° to 30°. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was measured on a TA SDT-650 instrument, and the sample was heated under N2 flow (100 ml min−1) with a heating rate of 10 K min−1. Differential scanning calorimetry of pure components (C3F6, C3F8) were also performed on a TA SDT-650 instrument. The obtained signal is integrated to give the corresponding amount of heat in joules.

Gas adsorption measurement

At least 100 mg of sample was used for each measurement. MFAs were activated at the corresponding temperature under a dynamic vacuum (below 5 μmHg) for 24 h (See Table S1). Single-component gas adsorption/desorption isotherms were measured on an ASAP 2020 PLUS HD88 surface area analyzer (Micromeritics). The time-dependent kinetic adsorption isotherms were collected on an Intelligent Gravimetric Analyzer (IGA001, Hiden, UK), which used a gravimetric technique to accurately measure the gas adsorption on ~50 mg activated samples under constant temperature and pressure conditions. For desorption kinetics, the tests were performed at pressures from 100 mbar to 10 mbar at a rate of 10 mbar/min. For cycling experiments, the pressure of C3F6 was increased to 1000 mbar and the uptake recorded. The pressure was then reduced to regenerate the sample with no assisted heating (expect 10th test).

Dynamic breakthrough experiments

The mixed-gas breakthrough experiment was conducted under ambient conditions (298 K, 1 bar). The weights of the sample packed in the column (Φ 4.6 × 50 mm) were 0.751 (CoFA), 0.601 (MgFA), 0.783 (ZnFA), and 0.553 (NiFA) g, respectively. The outlet gas was monitored using gas chromatography (GC-2010C, SHIMADZU) equipped with FID. After each breakthrough experiment, the sample can be regenerated under a purging He gas with a flow of 10 ml min−1 at 373 K overnight.

Theoretical calculations

The surface electrostatic potential of MFAs, C3F6, and C3F8 was calculated using the DMol3 modules. The energy, force, and displacement convergence criteria were set as 1 × 10−5 Ha, 0.002 Ha/Å, and 0.005 Å, respectively. The electron density data obtained from these calculations were used to construct the 0.017 e− Å−3 electron density isosurfaces of C3F6 and C3F8, while the electron density data of both frameworks were used to construct the 0.2 e− Å−3 electron density isosurfaces. GCMC simulations. The crystal structure of the MFA (Co, Mg, Zn) was refined by adjusting the solvent molecules and then optimized using the Forcite module with the universal force field (UFF). As with all GCMC simulations, the unit cell frame comprises eight (2 × 2 × 2) unit cells treated as rigid entities. ESP charges are assigned to both the adsorbate and host frames. A cutoff radius of 15.5 Å was applied, and the Ewald & Group summation method was selected to compute the electrostatic interactions between the adsorbate and the MFA framework. Each state point in the GCMC simulations underwent 1 × 107 Monte Carlo steps for achieving equilibrium, with final data collection carried out over an additional 1 × 107 Monte Carlo steps.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

The CASTEP package in Material Studio 2019 assessed the binding energy between C3F6 and the framework, utilizing the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE)53,54. A cutoff energy of 544 eV was established, guaranteeing convergence of the total energy within 0.01 meV/atom. The non-solvent framework and C3F6 were optimized under identical parameters to achieve the lowest energy state. C3F6 was positioned at varying locations within the channel, undergoing full structural relaxation. The host-guest binding energy was calculated using the formula EB = E(guest) + E(adsorbent) – E(adsorbent + guest). Hirshfeld surface maps, essential for analyzing weak interactions in molecular crystals, were generated using Multiwfn and CrystalExplorer and integrated with VMD software55,56.

In situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (in situ FT-IR)

In situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra were recorded using a PerkinElmer Spectrum 3 spectrometer equipped with a diffuse reflection cell (Puxiang Technology) and a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector. About 20 mg activated sample was placed into a customized reaction cell. The background was recorded at the specific temperature with N2. In situ DRIFTS spectra were recorded over a duration extending from 0 to 20 min at 30 °C, flowing the pure C3F6 or C3F8.

Two-dimensional solid-state NMR spectroscopy

Solid-state NMR experiments were carried out on a Bruker Avance NEO 600WB spectrometer. The measurement was performed immediately after gas loading in MgFA to prevent any desorption caused by prolonged exposure to atmospheric pressure. The 1H → 19F heteronuclear correlation NMR experiments were conducted with a contact time of 1.5 ms and a recycle delay of 1 s for C3F6@ZnFA. The indirect dimension’s spinning speed and increment interval were 20 kHz and 50 μs. Typically, 96 scans were acquired for each t1 increment. Chemical shifts were referenced (CFCl3 δiso = 0 ppm).
