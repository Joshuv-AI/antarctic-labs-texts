CIED-Related Tricuspid Regurgitation Managed With Lead Extraction, Leadless Pacing, and Annuloplasty
Authors: Zilong Weng, Shiqiang Hou, Qinchun Jin, Dehong Kong, Wenzhi Pan, Xiaochun Zhang, Daxin Zhou, Junbo Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundManagement of cardiac implantable electronic device–related tricuspid regurgitation remains challenging, particularly when lead interference coexists with annular dilation.Case SummaryA 69-year-old woman with a single-chamber pacemaker presented with symptomatic severe lead-related tricuspid regurgitation. Echocardiography demonstrated septal leaflet impingement by the pacing lead and marked annular dilation. Because isolated lead extraction was unlikely to restore valve competence, transvenous lead extraction, leadless pacemaker implantation, and transcatheter tricuspid annuloplasty were performed. At 3-month follow-up, tricuspid regurgitation had decreased to a mild-to-moderate level, with improvement in heart failure symptoms.DiscussionThis case illustrates a feasible multimodality strategy for selected patients with cardiac implantable electronic device–related tricuspid regurgitation. Combining lead extraction, leadless pacing, and transcatheter annuloplasty may relieve mechanical leaflet interference, avoid renewed transvalvular lead disruption, and reduce annular dilation.Take-Home MessagesCompared with conventional transvalvular leads, leadless pacing causes less interference with tricuspid valve motion and may therefore reduce lead-related regurgitation. A combined approach involving lead extraction, leadless pacing, and transcatheter tricuspid annuloplasty can provide a practical therapeutic pathway for cardiac implantable electronic device–related tricuspid regurgitation.

Cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED)-related tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is increasingly recognized as an important cause of secondary TR.1 The mechanisms are often multifactorial, involving direct leaflet impingement, entanglement, or coaptation interference, eventually resulting in right-sided volume overload and heart failure. Establishing causality is essential, as TR in CIED carriers may be merely associated (CIED-associated) or directly induced (CIED-related). In patients with confirmed lead-induced TR and coexisting annular dilation, isolated transvenous lead extraction (TLE) rarely achieves complete resolution.2 After lead extraction, the choice of pacing strategy should be individualized. Leadless pacemakers have no transvalvular leads and may therefore represent an appropriate pacing option after TLE. Transcatheter annuloplasty systems, such as the K-Clip (Huihe Company, China),3 provide a minimally invasive means of restoring annular geometry in high-risk surgical candidates. Here, we present a case of severe CIED-related TR managed with TLE, leadless pacemaker implantation, and transcatheter tricuspid annuloplasty, as a feasible therapeutic option for selected patients.Take-Home Message•Compared with conventional transvalvular leads, leadless pacing causes less interference with tricuspid valve motion and may therefore reduce lead-related regurgitation.•A combined approach involving lead extraction, leadless pacing, and transcatheter tricuspid annuloplasty can provide a practical therapeutic pathway for cardiac implantable electronic device–related tricuspid regurgitation.

History of Presentation

A 69-year-old woman was admitted due to severe chest tightness and nausea. She had a permanent single-chamber pacemaker implanted 5 years ago. Her other medical history included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and liver cirrhosis. The patient had received 6 months of guideline-directed medical therapy prior to admission.

Physical Examination

On initial presentation, the patient shows a heart rate of 60 beats/min, a blood pressure of 110/62 mm Hg, a cardiac murmur with jugular vein engorgement, and NYHA class IV.

Investigation

Transthoracic echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60%, severe TR, right-heart dilation, and reduced right ventricular systolic function (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion 14 mm). Preprocedural transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed torrential TR (vena contracta width was 21.0 mm) with lead tethering of the septal leaflet during valve closure; the annular circumference 13.0 cm and the annular area 13.2 cm2 indicated marked annular dilation (Figures 1A to 1C, Video 1, Video 2). Management

Given the severity of regurgitation and confirmed lead–leaflet interference, the heart team proceeded with interventional management. The procedure followed standard protocols. Under local anesthesia, multiple attempts were first made to adjust the position and tension of the pacing lead; however, due to chronic fibrosis and fixed lead–leaflet interaction, satisfactory relief of septal leaflet impingement could not be achieved, and TLE was therefore undertaken (Video 3). Intraoperative TEE showed no reduction in TR (Figure 2A). A leadless pacemaker (Aveir, Abbott) was then implanted via the right femoral vein, but migration of the device into the pulmonary artery was observed (Figure 2B). As the device migration did not cause any hemodynamic compromise, retrieval was deferred, and the heart team proceeded with transcatheter tricuspid valve annuloplasty. Subsequently, transcatheter tricuspid annuloplasty was performed via the right internal jugular vein under intravenous combined anesthesia, with TEE and fluoroscopic guidance using the K-Clip system (Huihe Company). An 18F adjustable sheath was used to advance the 12# K-Clip delivery system to the posterior-septal portion of the tricuspid annulus. The clip arms were deployed parallel to the annulus, resulting in the reduction of TR from massive to severe (Figures 2C to 2E, Video 4). Although the annulus was reduced, regurgitation remained severe; therefore, a second 12# K-Clip was implanted at the anterior–posterior commissure (Figure 2F, Video 5), further reducing regurgitation to moderate to severe without leaflet injury or pericardial effusion. Following annuloplasty, the migrated leadless pacemaker was successfully snared from the pulmonary artery and withdrawn for retrieval. After external cleaning, it was reconnected to the delivery system and advanced again for repeat implantation. Electrical parameters at several positions were initially unsatisfactory, so the device was repositioned to a higher septal site. Repeat testing then showed acceptable parameters, and fixation was stable before the final release (Figure 2H).

Outcome and Follow-up

The patient was discharged 5 days after the intervention. She continued on rivaroxaban (15 mg orally once daily) postoperatively, along with guideline-directed medical therapy and vericiguat therapy (5 mg orally once daily). At the 3-month follow-up, the patient showed improvement in dyspnea and edema, with NYHA class improving from IV to II. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed mild-to-moderate TR (Video 6).

Discussion

Previous epidemiological studies have found that the incidence of severe TR among patients with CIEDs can be as high as 7% to 45%.2 It remains a difficult clinical problem. Establishing causality is crucial, as TR in CIED carriers can be either coincidental (CIED-associated) or directly lead-induced (CIED-related) when valvular interference is evident.3 In the present case, TR developed and progressively worsened after pacemaker implantation, accompanied by right-sided chamber dilation and reduced right ventricular systolic function. Echocardiography revealed synchronous motion of the lead and the tricuspid valve leaflets with leaflet tethering and marked annular dilation, confirming a mechanical mechanism consistent with CIED-related TR. In such cases, TLE may relieve leaflet impingement and improve valve coaptation when a direct interaction between the lead and leaflet is evident. Nevertheless, isolated TLE rarely restores valve competence in patients with significant annular dilation or right-heart remodeling, as the underlying pathophysiological substrate persists even after removal of the mechanical obstacle.

During the procedure, several attempts were made to reposition the original pacing lead, but this proved technically difficult and ineffective. Following lead extraction, a leadless pacemaker was chosen as the optimal pacing strategy. Although leadless pacemakers provide nonphysiological single-chamber pacing and prolonged right ventricular pacing may potentially impair left ventricular function, intraoperative monitoring revealed an intermittent intrinsic sinus rhythm, indicating that the patient was not pacemaker-dependent. The leadless pacemaker offers the advantage of not requiring a transvalvular lead, thus eliminating further mechanical interference with the tricuspid valve and avoiding potential adverse effects on the subsequent annuloplasty. This feature is particularly relevant in patients undergoing or having undergone tricuspid intervention. In the largest case series to date, Fink et al4 reported successful transfemoral leadless pacemaker implantation in patients with previous surgical or transcatheter tricuspid valve repair or replacement, with no periprocedural complications or progression of TR during follow-up.4

Beyond TLE, further management should be according to the patient's individual characteristics. Given the patient's high surgical risk and marked annular dilation, a minimally invasive transcatheter annuloplasty was chosen to restore annular geometry and reduce regurgitation. Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair can effectively reduce TR in selected patients but requires favorable anatomy—specifically, localized regurgitant jets, adequate leaflet grasping zones, and a coaptation gap typically <7 mm.3,5 In our patient, the regurgitant orifice was broad (coaptation gap = 15 mm) and extended beyond the anteroseptal and posteroseptal commissures, rendering transcatheter edge-to-edge repair unsuitable. The K-Clip system, a transcatheter annuloplasty device designed to plicate the annulus at variable sites including the central posterior, anteroposterior, and posteroseptal regions, was therefore considered more appropriate. One-year results from the TriStar and other multicenter studies have demonstrated sustained reduction in TR severity, reverse right-heart remodeling, and symptomatic improvement following K-Clip implantation.6 Conversely, transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement, while effective in selected patients, carries the risk of right ventricular dysfunction, particularly in those with pre-existing right ventricular impairment.7 Given our patient's moderate right-sided dysfunction, transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement was not favored.

Besides, in patients with severe TR and marked right-ventricular remodeling, geometric distortion and altered tethering forces may reduce leadless pacemaker stability.7 In such situations, performing tricuspid annuloplasty first may reduce annular dilation, improve leaflet coaptation, and optimize right-heart loading conditions, thereby providing a more favorable substrate for subsequent leadless pacemaker anchoring. Although leadless pacemaker dislodgement is uncommon, endovascular retrieval is generally feasible when required.8

Although longer follow-up and larger studies are warranted, these findings support the technical feasibility and potential clinical value of an integrated TLE-to-leadless pacing-to-annuloplasty strategy in selected patients with CIED-related TR and annular dilation.
