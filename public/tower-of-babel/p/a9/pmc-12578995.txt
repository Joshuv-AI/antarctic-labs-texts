Ecotoxicity assessment and detection of antimicrobial compounds in urban environments and AMR hotspots
Authors: Vinod Divya, Pradeep Battula, Sowjanya Pulipati, M R Veena, Akhtar Rasool, G Bhuvanesh, Mohammad Khalid, Shadma Wahab, Kotermane Mallikarjunappa Anilkumar, Shivaraju Harikarahalli Puttaiah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnvironmental pollution by antimicrobial compounds poses a serious threat to aquatic ecosystems and human health due to their persistence and ability to promote antimicrobial resistance (AMR). This study evaluated the presence of five widely used antibiotics in surface water, groundwater, and wastewater treatment plant effluents from 28 locations in Bengaluru and Mysuru, India: erythromycin, amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin, sulfamethoxazole, and trimethoprim. With concentrations ranging from 0.001 to 20.19 µg/L, ciprofloxacin and sulfamethoxazole were identified at almost every site. High-risk levels (RQ > 1) were found at 23 sites for sulfamethoxazole, 22 for amoxicillin, and 21 for ciprofloxacin, according to risk quotient (RQ) analysis. Several sites showed cumulative high-risk exposure to multiple antibiotics. Low PNEC ENV/MIC ratios were also found at many sites by AMR risk assessment, suggesting a high potential for resistance emergence and spread. In order to lower ecological and public health risks, these findings indicate at significant hotspots of contamination and emphasise the urgent need for enhanced wastewater treatment, focused pollution control measures, and regular monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24102-8.Keywords: Environmental pollution, Antibiotics, Risk quotient, Antimicrobial resistance, EcosystemSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance development and transmission in aquatic ecosystem especially AMR in human pathogens has risen throughout the world over the recent days because of the improper application of antimicrobial compounds and its derivatives in medical and animal husbandry practices. Environmental pollution by such antimicrobial compounds causes severe environmental and health risks and destruct the ecological balance by developing intense AMR1,2. Antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) are highly resilient and can be transferred to other bacteria in aquatic ecosystems, thus developing other AMR bacteria within the ecosystem3. Antibiotics enter aquatic environments through domestic sewage, hospital effluents, and agricultural runoff. Thereafter, they persist and expose microbial communities under selective pressure. In cases where resistance genes are not directly monitored, this persistence can interfere with microbial processes and indirectly promote the spread of antibiotic resistance. As a result, tracking the prevalence and levels of antibiotics in environmental waters is a vital initial step in determining the risks to the environment and public health4. Antibiotics function through imposing selective pressure with microbial populations, not by introducing new resistance genes. While resistant strains of bacteria with ARGs have the ability to survive and multiply, susceptible bacteria are harmed by this pressure. Furthermore, antibiotics may promote the spread of resistance within microbial communities by improving the horizontal gene transfer of existing ARGs in aquatic systems5. Removal efficiency of ARGs and antibiotics was between 1.0–2.0 log and 79–88% in the conventional municipal sewage treatment plants6,7. Antimicrobial compounds were regularly found in treated municipal sewage discharges to ambient environment8–10.

Municipal sewage treated effluents, which contribute to water sources, are frequently recycled through enhanced treatment in reclaimed wastewater treatment plants (RWTPs) or utilized to replace water in nearby waterbodies11. Surface water bodies with a variety of species and abundance around the world include antibiotics and abundances around the world include antibiotics and ARGs12. Therefore, in the present study, we examined five antibiotic classes i.e., macrolide, β-lactams, fluoroquinolone, diaminopyrimidine, and sulfonamide in samples obtained from two WWTPs, groundwater, and surface water bodies of Karnataka’s two most densely populated cities—Bengaluru and Mysuru. We sought to identify their concentrations, evaluate the risk quotient and AMR, and report the related environmental, health, and socioeconomic risk. To help in the development of measures for reducing the environment and public health risks posed by AMR, this study identifies contaminated hotspots.

Materials and methods

Study sites and preparation

This study targets water samples obtained from two urban centers in Karnataka, India, known as hotspots for AMR, which are Bengaluru and Mysuru, arising from high anthropogenic activities. These include effluents from hospitals, Sewage Treatment Plant (STP) discharges, and runoff from the urban water body. As a rapidly developing city with a dense population and large-scale industrialization, Bengalurus13. Mysuru, the chain of connected lakes, industrial estates, and medical facilities causes environmental pollution in the city and has moderate urbanization compared to Bengaluru14. It is characterized by historical landmarks, institutions of learning, and a combination of an urban–rural ecosystem that suffers from anthropogenic pollution15,16.

The main sources of water for residential and industrial use in Bengaluru and Mysuru are rivers such as Cauvery and its tributaries, which are subjected to treatment by municipal water treatment plants before use17,18. Borewells also act as supplementary sources of water, especially where the treated supply of river water is not accessible. Effluent from households, health care, and industrial establishments flows into urban lakes, canals, and sewage networks19,20. These emissions are treated in STPs or released without treatment into the environment, signifying the important role of good strategies for the management of wastewater to check pollution and spreading of AMR20.

56 water samples in all were taken from 28 sampling locations (W1–W28) in Bengaluru and Mysuru. To ensure reproducibility, duplicate samples were obtained at each location. Samples were collected in precleaned polyethylene bottles, kept at 4 °C, and transported to the laboratory. All the samples were preserved at − 20 °C until further processing10. Figure 1 displays the sampling points collected from three types of aquatic environments: wastewater (STP inlets and outlets), surface water (urban lakes), and groundwater (borewells and hand pumps). These classifications are also indicated in Table S2. The samples have been collected in August and September of 2024. To ensure a thorough environmental investigation, on-site measurements of physicochemical parameters, such as pH, temperature, and conductivity, were carried out for each water sample. Following APHA standard procedures, physicochemical parameters such as pH, turbidity, Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD), Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD), and chloride were measured on-site as the sample was collected. A portable turbidity meter (Hach 2100Q, USA) was used to measure turbidity, and a portable multiparameter probe (Hanna Instruments HI9829, USA) was used to measure pH, temperature, and conductivity. Titrimetric and spectrophotometric methods were used to analyse COD, BOD, and chloride. Additionally, the agar plate count method was used to evaluate the microbial quality. In brief, water samples were serially diluted, plated using the spread plate technique on nutrient agar, and then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Colony-forming units per millilitre (CFU/mL) were used to express the results.

Antibiotics and reagents

For this research, five widely used antibiotics were chosen: trimethoprim, sulfamethoxazole, ciprofloxacin, amoxicillin, and erythromycin. These substances were chosen because they are classified to various therapeutic classes (macrolides, β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, and dihydrofolate reductase inhibitors, respectively), are commonly prescribed in veterinary and human medicine, and have been found to be contaminants in surface waters and wastewater effluents on a frequent basis. They have been identified as prime targets for monitoring due to their environmental persistence, partial elimination during wastewater treatment, and potential to contribute to antimicrobial resistance. All antibiotics and reagents used were of analytical or HPLC grade. Acetonitrile (≥ 99%, Sisco Research Laboratories (SRL), India), methanol (≥ 99.9%, SRL, India), phosphate buffer (≥ 99.9%, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) and phosphoric acid (≥ 99% SRL, India) for HPLC, while the analytes: Amoxicillin, Erythromycin, Ciprofloxacin, Trimethoprim, and Sulfamethoxazole (purity ≥ 98%) are procured from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm) was obtained using a Milli-Q system (Millipore, USA). All of the reagents have been utilised without additional purification after being verified to meet analytical requirements. Physicochemical properties of targeted antibiotics is shown in Table S3.

Investigation and extraction

The extraction process and HPLC operation followed US EPA method 1694: Determination of Pharmaceuticals and Personal Care Products (PPCPs) in water Samples by Solid Phase Extraction (SPE). An autosampler, a deuterium (D2) detector, and a photodiode array detector (PDA) were all included in the AHT2010 model, HPLC (Shimadzu, Japan), which was used for the chromatographic study. For separation, the 250 × 4.6 mm Phenomenex C-18 column was employed. Before analysis, the asymmetry factor, capacitance factor, and column efficiency were calculated after the chromatographic conditions—such as the flow rate, mobile phase, solvent ratio, and pH were optimized.

Antibiotics were extracted from water samples using SPE, in accordance with US EPA Method 1694 for PPCPs21. After undergoing a 0.45 µm membrane filter, each 500 mL water sample was run through Oasis HLB SPE cartridges (6 mL, 200 mg, Waters Corporation) at a flow rate of 5 mL/min. Prior to sample loading, 5 mL of methanol and 5 mL of ultrapure water were used to precondition the cartridges. Following sample loading, the cartridges were cleaned with 5 mL ultrapure water, vacuum-dried for 10 min, and then eluted with 10 mL methanol and 5 mL acetonitrile. Table S4 provides Standardization and Calibration Summary from HPLC Measurements.

Recovery studies

In recovery experiments, known concentrations (10, 20, 50, 80, and 100 ng/L) of the target antibiotics (erythromycin, amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim, and sulfamethoxazole) were spiked into water samples to assess the analytical method’s accuracy. The same preparation and evaluation procedures used for real samples were applied to the spiked samples. Table S5 provides a summary of the recovery results. With relative standard deviations (RSDs) ranging from 4.7 to 7.4% and mean recoveries ranging from 82.1 to 95.5%, the method demonstrated good accuracy and acceptable precision. The analytes with the highest average recovery were sulfamethoxazole (95.5%) and erythromycin (85.0%).

Ecotoxicological assessments

The environmental risk assessment of analytes was determined using the RQ technique. The proportion of the measured environmental concentration (MEC) to the predicted no-effect concentration (PNEC) was applied to calculate the RQ value22. According to the previous literature the PNEC is computed with dividing the acute toxicological values (EC50 or LC50) from a safety margin of 100023. Fish, daphnia, and algae were the target organisms used in this investigation, and Ecosar software provided the acute toxicity data for them. A high level of environmental pollution is indicated by an RQ value greater than 1.0, while minimal and moderate risk to the target species are indicated by RQ values 0.01–0.1 and 0.1–1, respectively10.

Antimicrobial risk assessment

The PNEC for risk assessment of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) was determined by using the lowest of the Environmental PNECs (PNEC-ENVs) and PNECs based on minimum inhibitory concentrations (PNEC-MICs). Environmental toxicity data gathered by Tell et al.24 was utilized to calculate PNEC-ENVs. PNEC-ENVs were calculated using data based on the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development’s (OECD) recommendations. Cyanobacteria were the most antibiotic-sensitive of the many microorganisms taken into consideration for the analysis of antibiotic toxicity. The Bengtsson Palme and Larsson25 study’s PNEC-MIC data were taken into account. It should be mentioned that Bengtsson-Palme and Larsson25 used the EUCAST database from 2015 to determine the PNEC-MIC values used in this study. The risk estimates provided here might slightly change if more recent breakpoint data is used because EUCAST MIC breakpoints are updated annually. In order to guarantee conformity with current clinical guidelines, we highlight this as a limitation and advise that future environmental risk assessments take into account the most recent EUCAST values.

Using the MIC information from the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing database (EUCAST), they created PNEC-MICs26. For the RQ calculation, the lowest of the two PNECs mentioned above was chosen as the PNEC resistance.

Results and discussion

Physiochemical characterization

Table S6 illustrates the physicochemical characteristics of the water collected and analyzed in this study. Samples from Bengaluru have pH values between 6.8 and 7.8 ranging from acidic to slightly basic. Contrarily, all the samples drawn from Mysuru showed a pH in the range of 7.2–9.3. The turbidity of the water samples also had a variation with a range between 155 and 297 NTU in Bengaluru samples and between 109 and 269 NTU in Mysuru samples. The COD values also had a range between 100 and 600 mg/L for the Bengaluru samples and 100 and 900 mg/L for the samples in the Mysuru area. The chloride samples from Bengaluru were between 142.5 and 231.6 mg/L while the samples of Mysuru ranged between 22.7 and 133.3 mg/L. BOD levels ranged between 0.2 and 0.8 mg/L in Bengaluru samples and between 0.1 and 0.6 mg/L in Mysuru samples. Agar plate counts, on the contrary, showed a significantly higher microbial load, ranging from 70,000 to 90,000 CFU/mL in Bengaluru and from 60,000 to 90,000 CFU/mL in Mysuru.

Quantitative analysis of antibiotics

The HPLC analysis of antibiotic concentration in the water samples covering Bengaluru from sampling sites W1 to W15 and Mysuru from sampling sites W16 to W28 indicates a notable level of antibiotic pollution across diverse water bodies, such as groundwater, STPs, and surface water bodies. Table S7 shows the PNEC values of the targeted antibiotics while Table 1 depicts the detected Measured Environmental Concentration of antibiotics in water bodies. High levels of sulfamethoxazole were found in Bengaluru, specifically in sites (W6, W8, W10, W11; 3.34–20.19 µg/L) and STP effluents (W2, W3; ciprofloxacin 5.60–8.85 µg/L). Concerns with the safety of drinking water and groundwater were raised by the contamination found in hospital tap water (W1, W14) and borewell water (W13). Trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole contamination was found in surface waters (W16, W19) in Mysuru, while groundwater sites (W17, W18) had significant trimethoprim residues (up to 14.87 µg/L). Additionally, detectable levels of antibiotics were found in municipal tap water sources (W27, W28), indicating that drinking water may be a potential source of human exposure27,28. These levels are several degrees of magnitude higher than the safe thresholds (≤ 0.01 µg/L) recommended by WHO to prevent selection for resistance, as well as the PNEC thresholds suggested by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the AMR watch list (usually < 0.1–1.0 µg/L for environmental protection). The detected concentrations are significantly higher than values reported in previous Indian studies from surface waters (< 1 µg/L), indicating a growing contamination burden, even though India does not currently have legally enforceable antibiotic discharge limits. The potential for ecological disruption and the pressing need to include antibiotics in Indian water quality standards and discharge regulations are highlighted by this comparison. The persistent usage of antibiotics in water supplies and treated effluents draws attention to the potential risks of the development of AMR and its harmful consequences to the environment and necessitates the need for urgent infrastructure and regulation29,30.

Risk evaluation of antibiotics

Tables S8 and S9 evaluate the RQ values and risk categories of all 5 antibiotics in water samples. Figure 2 illustrates the ecotoxicological risk assessment of the detected antibiotics. The assessment indicates the widespread contamination of pharmaceutical contaminants that may pose significant ecotoxicological risks to aquatic ecosystems and public health, with antibiotic pollution levels varying from Medium Risk to High Risk. The outcomes highlight the necessity of rapid mitigation measures and the possible contribution of pharmaceutical pollution to the rise of AMR.

Ecotoxicological risk assessment of detected antibiotics.

Erythromycin was detected at High-Risk Quotient values in W1, W2, W3, W12, W14, W19–W24, and W26–W28; the maximum concentrations were identified at W2 (23.86), W14 (21.43), and W28 (21.86), while in W4–W9, W11, W15–18, and W25, it remained undetected (n.d.), exhibiting location-specific contamination fluctuation. In W15 (8.85), W16 (4.62), W17 (5.38), W20 (5.00), and W26 (10.00), amoxicillin repeatedly exhibited High-Risk Quotient levels, reflecting ongoing contamination from the pharmaceutical sector, agricultural runoff, and municipal wastewater. Due to its persistence in the aquatic environment and potential to induce AMR, the fluoroquinolone antibiotic ciprofloxacin was detected with consistent High-Risk Quotient values in all locations except one, and the levels were extremely high in W2 (8.858), W3 (5.603), and W17 (4.403). The level of contamination is further underscored by the presence of trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole. W11 (171.16), W17 (126.08), and W24 (58.57) all showed very high levels of sulfamethoxazole, which were High Risk. Trimethoprim was seen less often but showed Medium Risk at W1, W18, W20, W21, W25, and W28 and low Risk at W2, W3, and W8.

Owing to High-Risk exposure to a large number of antibiotics, many locations were confirmed as pollution hotspots. W1, W2, W3, W19, W24, and W28 were High Risk to over three antibiotics, reflecting heavy toxicity from hospital effluents, the pharmaceutical sector, and untreated wastewater outfalls. The ubiquity of antibiotics at high-risk levels gives rise to critical environmental and human health issues. Drug-resistant infections are threatened due to the environment caused by the high levels of erythromycin, ciprofloxacin, amoxicillin, sulfamethoxazole, and trimethoprim. Since such antibiotics alter microbial communities and lower biodiversity, prolonged exposure could disturb aquatic ecosystems. Also, residues from antibiotics have the potential to bioaccumulate within aquatic organisms, possibly enabling them to enter their way into the food chain and leading to individuals exposed to contaminated seafood and potable water31–33. Risk of antimicrobial resistance

One of the adverse effects of antibiotics on instinctive microbial populations is the suppression or lack of certain microbial populations engaged in key ecosystem processes owing to the bactericidal and bacteriostatic effects of antibiotics. Conversely, other populations of bacteria might become resistant through low antibiotic concentrations below the MIC34. The results indicated that water samples had an extremely high potential for drug contamination and AMR. The PNEC-ENV/MIC values of the chosen antibiotics are listed in Table S10, which provides details about their lowest PNEC values. Erythromycin, Amoxicillin, and Ciprofloxacin’s AMR evaluation is displayed in Table S11, and Sulfamethoxazole and Trimethoprim’s AMR is shown in Table S12. High AMR risk levels of Erythromycin were detected in some sites, particularly at W2, W3, W14, and W28. Ciprofloxacin also illustrated medium to low AMR risk in all the sites. Amoxicillin was also mostly placed in high AMR risk at the sites of W15, W16, W17, W20, W22, W23, and W25. Though high values of AMR risk by Sulfamethoxazole were indicated throughout the locations, including W11, W17, and W24, Trimethoprim illustrated high AMR risk values by W1, W18, W20, W21, W25, and W28. The current study found a significant spatial overlap between regions with high population density and close proximity to significant sources of antibiotic discharge and areas with high AMR risk. In Bengaluru and Mysuru, two heavily populated urban centres under significant anthropogenic pressure, hotspots of AMR risk were particularly noticeable in the urban and peri-urban areas. According to the site-specific risk mapping, these locations closely match those with substantial hospital effluent, pharmaceutical discharge, and untreated municipal wastewater input (e.g., sampling sites W1, W2, W3 in Bengaluru; W17, W18, W24 in Mysuru). MEC of antibiotics and the estimated risks of AMR development were found to be highly correlated by quantitative analysis. PNEC–MIC ratios regularly identified AMR risk hotspots as having the highest concentrations of significant antibiotic compounds, such as trimethoprim, sulfamethoxazole, and ciprofloxacin.

Environmental risks

The persistence, bioaccumulation tendency, and microbial ecological impact of antibiotics such as erythromycin, amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim, and sulfamethoxazole constitute their occurrence in aquatic environments an extreme risk to the environment35. In multiple sampling locations, the results demonstrate mid to high-risk concentrations of these antibiotics, suggesting persistent anthropogenic pollution due to pharmaceutical effluents, hospital waste, wastewater effluent, and agricultural runoff36. Through selective inhibition of susceptible bacterial populations and stimulating the development of resistant populations, antibiotics in water ecosystems can potentially disrupt microbial communities35.

High concentrations of antibiotics may exert selective pressure in favour the persistence of resistant bacteria and possibly promote the horizontal transfer of AMR genes. Although these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in our study, prior research has shown such associations37. The stability of an ecosystem can be affected by such shifts in microbial diversity since they can harm essential biogeochemical cycles, e.g., the carbon and nitrogen cycle. Antibiotic bioaccumulation at higher trophic levels threatens higher-level species in aquatic life, which transcends microbial ecosystems38,39. Trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole have also been associated with endocrine disruption in aquatic organisms, which affects growth and reproduction. Aquatic organisms exposed to sub-lethal concentrations of the drugs over a long period can suffer from physiological stress, lowered survival rates, and population reduction, all of which can contribute to a negative impact on ecosystem function and biodiversity40.

Conclusion

This study offers significant data about the prevalence and risks of antibiotic contamination in Bengaluru and Mysuru’s aquatic environments. In surface waters, borewells, tap water, and STP effluents, erythromycin, amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin, sulfamethoxazole, and trimethoprim were found in particularly high concentrations close to urban lakes and sewage treatment plant outlets. According to ecological risk assessment, a number of sites had risk levels higher than one (RQ > 1), suggesting potential adverse impacts on aquatic life. Environmental antibiotic concentrations may exert selective pressure in favour of the development of resistance, according to an AMR risk assessment based on PNEC-MIC/ENV ratios. Where cumulative risks from multiple antibiotics were observed, pollution hotspots were highlighted through location-specific variation. These results highlight the need for enhanced wastewater treatment, enhanced point source control, and routine monitoring are needed to reduce antibiotic contamination. In order to directly evaluate antimicrobial resistance genes and assess long-term ecosystem and health impacts, future research should integrate molecular tools.
