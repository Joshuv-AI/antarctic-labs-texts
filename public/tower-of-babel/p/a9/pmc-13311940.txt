Manipulating embryogenesis and testing for potential: two real problems for the regulation of stem cell-based embryo models
Authors: Jonathan Lewis, Soren Holm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStem cell-based human embryo models (SCBEMs), generated in vitro from stem cells, currently exist outside the scope of regulatory frameworks that govern in vitro embryo research in most jurisdictions. A widely discussed proposal suggests using a ‘Turing test’ framework, whereby regulatory oversight is triggered if an SCBEM is found to be ‘equivalent’ to a human embryo. In this paper, we argue that such a proposal faces two major complications. First, sophisticated laboratory techniques such as trophoblast replacement allow researchers to manipulate normal embryogenesis, obscuring whether a given SCBEM meets embryo-like regulatory thresholds. Second, attempts to assess SCBEMs’ developmental potential—especially through non-human analogues—rest on tenuous epistemic assumptions that may not align with human-specific developmental trajectories. Given SCBEMs’ potential manipulability and uncertain biological and potentiality benchmarks, we argue that reliance on equivalence-based frameworks alone is highly problematic. We conclude by urging a cautious, flexible approach that recognises both the scientific promise of SCBEMs and the normative need to prevent the circumvention of regulatory safeguards.Keywords: Stem Cell Research, Embryo Research, Embryos and Fetuses, Cloning, Ethics- Research

Introduction

Stem cell-based human embryo models (SCBEMs)—often referred to as synthetic embryos, embryoids or embryonic models—are in vitro constructs generated from stem cells that mimic certain structural and functional aspects of early human embryonic development.1 They have been derived using various techniques, including from embryonic stem cells and from the reprogramming of induced pluripotent stem cells to organise into three-dimensional structures that resemble aspects of embryo development.1 Recent advances reveal that some SCBEMs can form cell lineages parallel to those seen in natural embryos, including the formation of a trophectoderm-like layer and an inner cell mass (ICM)-like region.2 3 In some cases, SCBEMs even recapitulate peri-implantation events—an indication of increasing complexity in the modelling of embryo development.4,7

Despite these advances, SCBEMs typically lie outside the regulatory frameworks that govern in vitro embryo research.8 Laws generally focus on fertilised embryos—‘the products of sperm and egg’—and sometimes include entities derived by somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) or parthenogenesis.9 Yet, SCBEMs skirt such definitions because they are derived from cell lines or from reprogrammed stem cells. As regulatory frameworks have traditionally not been designed to address their existence, SCBEMs exist in a legal grey area in most jurisdictions. Should research on SCBEMs remain largely unconstrained, or do they deserve the same ethical and regulatory scrutiny as natural embryos?

Increasing calls to close this regulatory gap are fuelled by the gradually narrowing conceptual and biological distinctions between SCBEMs and fertilised embryos.2 10 Traditional embryo research is subject to tight governance, often capped by a so-called 14-day rule, alongside licensing and ethics review requirements. These constraints aim to balance scientific progress, public trust in biomedical research and concerns about the moral status of early human life.11 By contrast, although research with SCBEMs need not go beyond what is currently permitted in research with human embryos, experiments that produce or maintain SCBEMs beyond equivalent developmental points can take place more freely, potentially raising ethical red flags.12 Some have argued that if SCBEMs truly replicate key morphological events and generate analogous cell types or tissue structures, it becomes increasingly difficult to justify disparate treatment between SCBEMs and human embryos.13,15

A currently popular solution is to expand embryo-level protections to SCBEMs once they demonstrate ‘equivalence’—a concept that hinges on whether SCBEMs can recapitulate the developmental processes and functional capacities typically attributed to normal human embryos.13 16 According to this position, an SCBEM shown to possess comparable developmental milestones should be considered ethically and legally on par with a normal human embryo. Among the proposed criteria for equivalence are the assessment of ‘developmental potential’, often measured in such regulatory proposals by structural milestones (eg, formation of extraembryonic layers), and indirect tests involving implantation in non-human species. Some commentators liken this approach to a ‘Turing test’, in which an entity is considered to have met the threshold for equivalence when observers cannot meaningfully distinguish its developmental capacity from that of a genuine embryo.13 The European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology’s Ethics Committee Writing Group has adopted a similar approach, recommending that SCBEM research should be subject to the regulations for human embryology when SCBEMs are shown to develop in accordance with the steps of normal embryogenesis in vitro and result in live births in several non-human, mammalian species.16

As we shall argue, there are two major challenges to this equivalence-based path. First, recent breakthroughs in laboratory manipulation, particularly trophoblast replacement,17 reveal how standard accounts of embryogenesis might be circumvented, thus thwarting any rule that depends on continuous or ‘normal’ developmental processes. Second, attempts to regulate SCBEMs by testing their developmental potential assume we can reliably compare SCBEMs to non-human embryo models, or at least identify a clear tipping point for ‘embryo-like’ status. Given the potential for species-specific differences in early development—and the unique pace at which SCBEM research may advance—such assumptions are epistemically tenuous. Before adopting an equivalence-based framework, we must address these manipulative possibilities and the underlying epistemic uncertainty about how and when SCBEMs acquire embryo-like potential.

Trophoblast replacement and the undermining of ‘Turing test’ regulation

A central development underscoring the manipulability of embryogenesis is the trophoblast replacement technique, illustrated in a landmark study by Liao and colleagues.17 The researchers began with two rhesus monkey embryos at the blastocyst stage: one produced via SCNT and the other via intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). Rhesus monkey embryos produced via SCNT can, in some cases, implant, but do not develop to term. Through immunosurgery, Liao et al extracted the ICM from the SCNT blastocyst and transplanted it into the trophoblast ‘shell’ of the ICSI-derived embryo, which had its own ICM removed. After applying epigenetic regulators, the reconstructed embryo was then transferred into a surrogate mother. This technique resulted in a live-born, healthy rhesus monkey, which the team reported remains well after 2 years.

The implications for SCBEM research are profound. If one can replace the trophoblasts of two or more entities—be they fertilised embryos or SCBEMs—one can create a new construct with a new set of developmental capacities. This is not simply an abstract puzzle: an SCBEM that partially lacks a robust trophectoderm, for instance, could be ‘completed’ by a trophoblast extracted from another SCBEM. Hence, the creation of a ‘reconstructed SCBEM’ becomes something obtainable, at least in principle. As the studies by Liao and colleagues indicate,17 an embryo or embryo model can emerge from discrete parts of other constructs, with each ‘parent’ entity never completing the normal blastocyst stage on its own.

These observations naturally lead us to consider how such deliberate disruptions of embryogenesis intersect with proposals that rely on a ‘Turing test’ to classify SCBEMs. Those in favour of equivalence-based regulation typically presuppose that an SCBEM either ‘is’ or ‘is not’ a human embryo according to whether it replicates certain morphological benchmarks in an unbroken developmental path.13 16 However, if we piece together an entity from multiple SCBEMs, how does the composite pass (or bypass) regulations designed for a single, self-developing SCBEM?

The recent influential framework proposed by Rivron et al asserts that SCBEMs should be regulated similarly to human embryos once they cross certain key thresholds.13 Specifically, their ‘Turing test’ analogy adopts a two-pronged approach to testing to see whether an SCBEM has the potential to form a foetus:

If an SCBEM meets these criteria, Rivron and colleagues argue that it crosses a ‘tipping point’ that renders it ethically—and, as they propose, legally—indistinguishable from a fertilised human embryo, mandating equivalent regulatory safeguards.13 They see in vitro markers of proper embryogenesis, coupled with the capacity for successful birth in closely related species, as strong indicators of embryo-like status.

However, trophoblast replacement highlights a formidable loophole: neither ‘parent’ entity in a two-SCBEM reconstruction scenario ever completes normal blastocyst development on its own. One SCBEM might be intended as an ICM donor, and the other a trophectoderm donor. Each SCBEM, then, could be created in order to be subsequently dismantled to harvest a component from the other. Consequently, neither one is tested in its entirety to see whether it develops ‘efficiently and faithfully,’ nor do they individually undergo a live-birth test in a non-human model. If trophoblast replacement is employed, then only the newly formed, composite SCBEM will, in reality, actualise the capacity to form a fetus—the tipping point in Rivron and colleagues’ proposed framework. Since that reconstructed entity is, in effect, distinct from each progenitor SCBEM, the original ‘Turing test’ no longer straightforwardly applies; the in vitro test is designed to measure continuous, intact development, which is circumvented by the employment of trophoblast replacement.

This highlights an underlying vulnerability in such an equivalence-based system: if the regulatory classification hinges on a given embryo model’s unbroken developmental path, then researchers can exploit the regulations by, in effect, breaking up the path through interventions like trophoblast replacement. Indeed, the resulting reconstructed SCBEM (with meaningful developmental potential) is neither a simple ‘extension’ of either initial SCBEM, nor does it arise from fertilisation. Instead, it actualises its own developmental potential (ie, its own developmental path) through the novel combination of SCBEM components. As a result, the original, ‘parent’ SCBEMs, and their specific, respective developmental potentials, are destroyed forever.18 Thus, it would be conceptually impossible for these original SCBEMs to meet the tipping point for equivalence. In short, the manipulation of SCBEM developmental processes risks undercutting proposed tests for developmental potentiality.

Epistemic challenges and consequences for normative frameworks

Equivalence-based proposals often revolve around whether an SCBEM can, under suitable conditions, form a fetus. Where direct testing in humans is ethically impermissible, researchers propose that achievements in non-human species serve as a proxy for human embryo-like potential.13 16 If an SCBEM can demonstrate, in monkeys, pigs or mice, the steps culminating in a healthy birth, the logic goes that we have strong grounds to believe that the human SCBEM would similarly progress given the right environment.

Yet, two assumptions complicate this logic. First is the belief that embryogenesis in primates and other mammals parallels human biology closely enough that success in the former implies success in the latter. In reality, interspecies cloning studies frequently yield divergent outcomes with respect to gestational anomalies, birth viability and epigenetic resetting.17 19 20 Second is the notion that human SCBEM development will not exceed or diverge from the pace at which non-human embryo models can be experimentally advanced. Should specialised culture conditions or novel epigenetic tools allow human SCBEMs to reach advanced stages more readily than their non-human counterparts, it is conceivable that researchers could surpass the ‘tipping point’ without recognising it.

Philosophically, this debate centres on the concept of ‘potential,’ a longstanding theme in controversies over abortion, assisted reproduction and embryonic research.21,27 If a regulatory system is built on a threshold of equivalence that assumes the concept of potential (eg, the potential to form a fetus), then is the substantive external manipulation of an SCBEM in vitro—such as swapping its trophoblast or merging partial embryonic components—morally relevant? For some, the answer may be no: assuming the ‘fundamental influence of an instructive (uterine) environment in the realization of the human embryo’s potential to develop into a human being, rather than, for example, into a tumor’,27 it is only when a cluster of cells actualises certain embryonic properties and/or passes certain developmental milestones when situated in the right instructive environment that is what morally matters. Others, however, have questioned the need for external support by an instructive (uterine) environment as a necessary condition of equivalence between an SCBEM and embryo, arguing that developmental potential requires functional completeness, developmental autonomy and identity preservation.15 25

What such a debate illustrates is that relying on potential as the linchpin of SCBEM regulation can create vulnerabilities when that potential can be manipulated. If both ‘parent’ SCBEMs are, as a result of manipulation, prevented from fully actualising their respective capacities to form a foetus, then they will fail to be captured by regulatory frameworks that associate the ‘tipping point’ for equivalence with continuous, intact development, key morphological markers and live, fertile births in non-human analogues, and thereby remain under less stringent oversight. Of course, the risk here is that, in some cases, the ‘parent’ SCBEMs might manifest the developmental potential to form a fetus, for instance, if it turns out that embryos, without the support of a uterine environment, are complete, autonomous developmental systems, possessing active developmental potential.15 25 28 Even if these pre-manipulated SCBEMs were human embryos that were, subsequently, harmed or destroyed in a morally significant sense during manipulation, a normative framework that rests on live-birth tests as a necessary indicator of embryo-like potential would not have considered these SCBEMs to be morally equivalent to embryos. Similarly, a regulatory system that screens entities for embryo-like potential based on uninterrupted development may miss the scenario where discrete SCBEMs, individually lacking that capacity, are combined into one that does possess it.

Moreover, cross-species analogies for SCBEM viability could provide false assurance. Regulators might see no evidence of foetal formation in analogous pig or monkey embryo models and assume human SCBEMs are similarly limited. If, however, human-specific protocols enable quicker or more complete development, researchers might inadvertently or deliberately push beyond a morally significant threshold without prior recognition by animal-based tests.15 This raises the spectre of ‘accidental’ equivalence, where an ostensibly innocuous SCBEM surpasses the point at which it should have been ethically constrained.

Potential paths forward

To address these challenges, it seems inevitable that we need to consider frameworks or strategies that might more effectively handle SCBEM research. Rather than relying on a single conceptual tool—like ‘developmental potential’ or ‘normal embryogenesis’—regulations may need to incorporate multiple factors, including the nature of the scientific manipulations involved.

For instance, one conservative response might be to treat SCBEMs as embryos from the start, subjecting them to existing constraints on in vitro embryo research—such as a 14-day limit, licensing and mandated ethical review. This would eliminate any question of circumventing regulations via manipulative procedures. Yet, it may also prevent scientists from using relatively simple SCBEMs for fundamental studies that do not plausibly result in a full embryo. Such blanket rules risk stunting the potential benefits that SCBEMs might offer, including insights into early embryo development and human disease.

Alternatively, a tiered system might align regulatory scrutiny with the SCBEM’s developmental stage or biological complexity.29 For instance, an early SCBEM that only forms a rudimentary blastocyst-like structure might be subject to minimal oversight, whereas more advanced models showing definitive signs of embryonic or extraembryonic tissue differentiation could require formal ethical review and licensing. This approach is attractive because it parallels the incremental oversight commonly applied to embryo research. However, a tiered system still must account for manipulations like trophoblast replacement: if the relevant ‘threshold’ is set at a specific morphological feature, the original SCBEMs could be dissected before ever reaching it, then reassembled into an embryo-like entity that never technically crossed the threshold in a linear fashion. Hence, tiered oversight may need to consider both the final developmental stage and the means by which it was achieved.

Some have proposed a precautionary approach, suggesting that if an SCBEM might be manipulated into embryo-equivalence, it should be treated as potentially embryo-equivalent from the outset.15 Such broad coverage prevents the ‘piecemeal assembly’ loophole, yet it may sweep up large volumes of cell culture research that lack credible paths to producing a foetus. This could heavily burden scientists who merely wish to model early cell interactions or study pre-implantation processes well below embryo-like complexity.

An additional proposal could be to regulate the manipulations themselves rather than only the final form of the SCBEM. Here, any laboratory process involving reconstitution or partial assembly of SCBEM components—such as trophoblast replacement—would fall under special licensing or prohibition, similar to how some jurisdictions control nuclear transfer.18 This ensures that even if the original SCBEMs do not individually meet embryo thresholds, combining them in a way that yields embryo-like potential is recognised as a high-level ethical procedure that triggers increased scrutiny. The advantage is that such an approach closes the gap where ‘deconstructed’ SCBEMs escape regulation until too late. However, as already observed, even if regulatory frameworks seek to incorporate and account for the manipulations employed in SCBEM construction, there is still the issue that non-human species tests for human embryo-like potential rest on epistemically tenuous assumptions.

Ultimately, the puzzle surrounding SCBEM regulation parallels broader dilemmas in regenerative medicine, gene editing and other advanced biotechnologies. In each domain, emergent science challenges existing legal definitions by blurring or circumventing the lines originally set to demarcate particularly ethically relevant practices. SCBEMs are, in a sense, the embryological counterpart to organoids in neuroscience, where neural organoids spark debates about the threshold for consciousness.30,40 The deeper question is how laws that revolve around seemingly stable categories—such as ‘embryo’ or ‘organism’—can keep pace with technologies that let scientists remix, reassemble or artificially enhance developmental capacities. At the same time, there is a balance to be struck between facilitating scientific stability and promoting public trust in biomedical innovation. On the one hand, over-precaution can paralyse innovation, and unduly restrictive rules can delay clinical applications. On the other hand, an overly permissive approach to SCBEM research could undermine public trust. A legitimate and normatively satisfactory approach must, therefore, seemingly weigh at least three uncertainties: the risk of harming an entity that morally matters, the social risk of foregoing scientific and potentially therapeutic benefits and the risk of undermining public trust in research through unduly permissive regulatory action.

These discussions also re-engage a longstanding bioethical debate: do (clusters of) human cells accrue moral significance from their inherent potential to undergo continuous human development or from the environment and interventions that actualise that potential? Bioethicists have long argued about whether artificially assisting embryonic processes to overcome nature’s limitations confers real moral status, or if it remains just a high-tech form of cell culture.21,27 SCBEM research recontextualises that debate by making it possible for an entity to gain ‘organismal’ potential entirely through lab-driven assembly. Ultimately, however, the underlying issue about the correct analysis of potential, and the ethical importance of potential (if any), is probably as unlikely to be resolved now as when one of the authors of this paper wrote his Master’s dissertation on the topic in 1990, extending a discussion that was already, at that time, 20 years old.41

Concluding remarks

Efforts to regulate SCBEMs via an ‘equivalence’ paradigm—particularly those hinging on Turing-test-like definitions—encounter two formidable stumbling blocks. First, the capacity to intentionally reshape ‘normal embryogenesis’ through techniques like trophoblast replacement means that no single SCBEM needs to exhibit all the developmental hallmarks of an embryo in one linear path. By fragmenting and reassembling structures, researchers can circumvent the very benchmarks designed to identify embryo-like potential. Second, reliance on ‘developmental potential’ gleaned from cross-species analogues presumes stable biological parallels and a slower pace of human SCBEM research—assumptions that might not hold if novel culture methods or epigenetic manipulations allow human SCBEMs to reach advanced stages faster or in ways not predicted by animal models.

Given these complexities, policymakers should be extremely cautious about adopting rigid, equivalence-based frameworks prematurely. A more nuanced approach that factors in both process (ie, manipulations used) and developmental milestones (ie, degree of structural and functional complexity) may better reflect scientific realities. Nonetheless, even if regulatory regimes fully factor in the manipulations involved in SCBEM production, evaluations of human embryo-like potential derived from non-human models still currently rest on epistemically fragile premises.

Ultimately, SCBEMs offer immense promise: they could illuminate aspects of human embryogenesis that have remained elusive, shed light on pregnancy loss or developmental diseases and spur innovations in regenerative medicine.1 7 At the same time, they remind us that biological development is far more malleable than once assumed, and our normative frameworks may need to adapt. By grappling seriously with the pitfalls of manipulative techniques and the uncertain meaning and markers of potential, regulators should seek to foster responsible progress—balancing the protection of nascent human life, public trust in biomedical research and the genuine downstream benefits that SCBEM research may provide.
