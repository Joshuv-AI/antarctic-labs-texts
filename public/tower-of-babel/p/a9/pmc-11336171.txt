Risky lane-changing behavior recognition based on stacking ensemble learning on snowy and icy surfaces
Authors: Xuejing Du, Wei Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRisky lane-changing (LC) behavior adversely affects traffic safety, especially on snowy and icy surfaces. However, due to the particularity of the snowy and icy surfaces and the scarcity of data, research on risky lane-changing behavior (RLCB) under extreme scenarios is insufficient. Therefore, this study presents a novel research framework aimed at selecting key risk characterization indicators (RCIs) and identifying RLCB on highways using driving simulation data on snowy and icy surfaces. A highway LC scenario was established on snowy and icy surfaces using a driving simulator, and 1200 sets of LC sample data were extracted. From the perspectives of parameter importance and correlation, 12 key RCIs with high importance and low inter-correlation were selected using the C4.5 decision tree algorithm and Pearson correlation analysis method. The RLCB recognition model was developed using the Stacking ensemble learning method and then compared with traditional recognition algorithms. The results show that the accuracy of the recognition model based on the Stacking ensemble learning model is significantly better than that of traditional algorithms, with a recognition accuracy of 98.33%. This finding can provide the basis for developing LC warning systems for intelligent connected vehicles on snowy and icy surfaces.Keywords: Traffic safety, Snowy and icy surfaces, Risky lane-changing behavior, Risk characterization indicators, Ensemble learningSubject terms: Mechanical engineering, Civil engineering

Introduction

Lane-changing (LC) and Lane-keeping (LK) are fundamental driving behaviors1. However, LC is more complex than LK, with a greater impact on the normal driving of surrounding adjacent vehicles, potentially leading to traffic congestion and accidents2. Studies have shown that approximately 5% of traffic accidents and 7% of crash fatalities in the United States are related to LC behavior annually, causing at least 60,000 injuries3. In China, almost 14,500 traffic accidents were associated with LC, contributing to 5.89% of all accidents, resulting in 2600 deaths and economic losses exceeding 60 million yuan4. Improper LC behavior poses a serious threat to traffic safety5. In snowy and icy conditions, the accumulation of snow and ice significantly reduces the adhesion coefficient of the road surface, greatly increasing the risk of losing control and colliding during lane changes6. Therefore, accurate recognition of RLCB is crucial to mitigate accidents, improve driving efficiency, and ensure road traffic safety and stability.

In recent years, extensive research has been conducted by scholars on identifying RLCB, achieving satisfactory results. The method based on traffic conflict is one of the important directions, which primarily includes spatial distance and temporal distance as evaluation indicators7. In terms of spatial distance, Xu et al.8 considered the variable states of surrounding vehicles and developed an improved minimum safe distance model. This model identifies RLCB by calculating the safe distance between each pair of vehicles at each time point during the LC process. Li et al.9 established a minimum safe distance LC model based on the safety potential field theory, characterizing the hazard field distribution for LC behaviors under various motion parameters. Yuan et al.10 proposed an LC safety distance model based on the shortest LC time constrained by the sideslip angle to identify RLCB. In terms of temporal safety distance, Guo et al.11 introduced a new surrogate safety measure, the two-dimensional time-to-collision (2D-TTC), to identify risky behaviors during LC processes. Qi et al.12 proposed an improved post-encroachment time (PET) model to calculate LC characteristics and accurately identify RLCB in merging areas. To further improve recognition accuracy, Wu et al.13 comprehensively considered the time and space dimensions of highway vehicle LC states, applying the fault tree analysis (FTA) method to integrate the temporal risk level (TRL) and spatial risk level (SRL) into a comprehensive risk assessment index, effectively identifying RLCB. Ali et al.14 utilized extreme value theory and traffic conflict techniques to identify and quantify forced LC collision risks in both conventional and connected environments, verifying the accuracy of the extreme value model in identifying forced LC behaviors using traffic conflict data. Despite the high computational efficiency and recognition accuracy of the aforementioned recognition models, the application scenarios and identification accuracy of these methods are limited due to neglecting the uncertainty in vehicle movements.

With the advancement of technology and hardware development, an increasing number of characteristic indicators have been collected. Some studies have used natural driving tests or driving simulator tests to collect vehicle dynamic parameters and driving behavior parameters to identify RLCB from the perspectives of vehicle motion control uncertainty and driving behavior uncertainty. González et al.15 identified LC aggressiveness by modeling LC behavior classification based on parameters such as lateral and longitudinal speed and acceleration, achieving a recognition accuracy of 92%. Feng et al.16 extracted 10 factors representing LC influence from vehicle trajectory data and used the powerful nonlinear learning and high pattern recognition capabilities of support vector machines to identify RLCB. Li et al.17 extracted LC behavior segments from natural driving data to determine key indicators closely related to LC risks, including speed, acceleration, and distance to the vehicle ahead in the original lane, and established an LC risk recognition model based on LARS-Lasso and SVM algorithms. Zhu et al.18 proposed a supervised learning algorithm and Bayesian hierarchical model to identify LC risks based on multi-vehicle interaction data at highway entrances and used the XGBoost learning algorithm to classify risk levels. Shangguan et al.19 incorporated driver intention factors into LC risk recognition and used long short-term memory (LSTM) neural networks to identify driver LC intentions and the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM) algorithm to identify LC risks. Chen et al.20 utilized NGSIM data to measure instantaneous LC risks at each timestamp based on risk markers from the driving safety field theory and used the K-means clustering algorithm to classify LC risk states, revealing that LC processes with an "uphill" risk profile typically have higher risks.

Vehicle LC behavior is a complex dynamic process influenced by various factors, including the driving environment, vehicle operating conditions, and driver style. Compared to LC risk recognition methods based on traffic conflicts, models that comprehensively consider vehicle motion and driving behavior parameters are more consistent with actual situations, thereby improving the accuracy and timeliness of results. However, these studies are primarily conducted under normal weather conditions, and their effectiveness on snowy and icy surfaces remains uncertain. Current research on the recognition of RLCB shows variations in results due to different scenarios, risk characterization indicators, and model characteristics. Therefore, selecting comprehensive and accurate RCIs, overcoming the deficiencies of individual models, and integrating the advantages of multiple models to improve recognition accuracy on snowy and icy surfaces are pressing issues at present.

In summary, research on identifying RLCB under normal weather conditions has been relatively comprehensive and mature. However, the challenges associated with snowy and icy surfaces have been less thoroughly explored. The scarcity of relevant data and the unique conditions of these surfaces present a significant research gap. This study aims to address this gap by focusing on highway LC scenarios under snowy and icy conditions. By leveraging driving simulation experiments, comprehensive data on vehicle dynamics, driver behaviors, and vehicle interactions during LC events are gathered. Statistical and advanced machine learning techniques are employed to identify key RCIs and develop a robust framework based on Stacking ensemble learning to accurately recognize RLCB in these challenging environments.

Methodology

This study established a highway snowy and icy road surface scenario using a driving simulator and conducted driving simulation experiments, collecting data on vehicle operations, driver behavior, and vehicle interactions. Based on the C4.5 decision tree algorithm and Pearson correlation analysis method, the RCIs of RLCB on snowy and icy road surfaces were selected. A Stacking-based ensemble learning framework was introduced, and an RLCB recognition model was developed for snowy and icy road surfaces. The overall research process is shown in Fig. 1.

Driving simulation experiment

Experimental device

This experiment relies on a simulated driving motion simulation test platform, which consists of a driving cabin, a 210° display screen, a 6-DOF motion platform, industrial computers, and a control platform, as shown in Fig. 2. The driving cabin is modified based on a real Volkswagen Polo model, including a complete instrument panel, steering wheel, pedals, seat arrangement, seat belts, and other internal structures21. The design and layout of these components are identical to the actual vehicle, ensuring the driving cabin's authenticity and operational experience. To achieve precise simulation of vehicle dynamics performance, key dynamic parameters such as the engine, transmission, drivetrain, suspension, and steering system are controlled and output by the high-precision SILAB software. The driving cabin can simulate the translational or rotational motion in the x/y/z directions according to the real-time driving status of the vehicle, thereby truly reflecting driving experiences on snowy and icy surfaces and facilitating dynamic adjustments by drivers. Additionally, the driving simulator can authentically reproduce the sounds of the vehicle and its environment through a 3D stereo mode. This simulated experiment can effectively avoid safety hazards and testing errors encountered in real vehicle tests on snowy and icy surfaces while gathering authentic and dependable test data22.

6-DOF driving simulator.

Experiment scenario

The experimental scenario design is based on the SILAB6.0 driving simulation software, which can meticulously set the road environment, weather, traffic flow status, and vehicle dynamics. It achieves flexible control of the scenario through scripts, thus simulating highly realistic experimental scenes. For this experiment, we selected the segment from Shuangcheng to Shijia on the Jingha Expressway, with a total length of 33 km, and designed with two lanes in each direction, each lane being 3.75 m wide, with emergency lanes on both sides. The road mainly comprises straight sections and large curvature radius curves, with a curve radius all greater than 1500 m. The morphology of the snowy and icy road surface was chosen to replicate common snow-covered icy film road surfaces on expressways. To accurately obtain the friction coefficient for road surfaces, a pendulum friction tester was used to conduct on-site measurements on the test sections. Measurement points were selected every 3 km along the test sections, each point was measured three times, and the average value was taken. The average friction coefficients for the snow-covered icy film road surface and regular road surface were determined to be 0.45 and 0.76, respectively. Environment vehicles are configured using the Car and TruckMotorway components of the SILAB 6.0 software, with a traffic flow density of approximately 25 vehicles per kilometer, at a moderate density level.

Experiment process

A total of 50 participants were recruited for this experiment, including 38 males and 12 females. All participants were aged between 25 and 55 years old, with driving experience of more than 5 years and holding valid driver's licenses. Participants were required to be in good physical and mental condition, abstain from alcohol, and have no physiological or psychological issues affecting driving behavior during the experiment. Before the formal experiment, participants received instructions on skill operations and completed 10 to 20 min of adaptive driving. The formal experiment began only after confirming that participants had no adverse reactions. Participants freely chose lanes based on their driving habits during the experiment. Each experiment lasted approximately 30 min, generating real-time output of over a hundred thousand pieces of data. Participants were required to complete an experiment on both snowy and icy road surfaces, as well as on regular road surfaces. To ensure the effectiveness of the experiment, a cross-experiment method was used, and each participant was given a rest period after completing the experiment.

Definition of lane-changing

The LC process, as defined in the literature, involves a vehicle moving from its original lane to the target lane, entailing a unidirectional continuous change in the vehicle's lateral position23. This study specifically focuses on LC scenarios involving five cars, as depicted in Fig. 3. Figure 3a and b depict LC occurring from right to left and from left to right, respectively. During this process, the LC vehicle (sub) moves from its original lane to the target lane. Fol1 and Pre1 represent the following and preceding cars of sub in the original lane. Fol2 and Pre2 represent the following and preceding cars of sub in the target lane.

LC sample extraction

The extraction of trajectory data during LC processes is closely related to determining the start and end points of LC. To obtain effective LC trajectory data, we have formulated extraction rules. First, it is determined whether a vehicle has undergone LC behavior based on the lane IDs. Specifically, if the lane ID of the vehicle at time t is different from the previous time, then we can infer that the vehicle has undergone an LC behavior. Second, we further identify intervals of monotonic lateral displacement of vehicles and mark the start and end points of lane changes. Finally, to exclude vehicles that do not intend to change lanes or have weak LC intentions, we introduce a constraint: the distance of the lateral coordinates of the vehicles at the start and end points of the LC from the lane lines on both sides should be greater than or equal to half of the vehicle width. Key RCIs selection

Definition of risky LC behavior

RLCB is an adverse event or potential hazard that may occur when making a lane change during the driving process24. The first type of RLCB is Lane-Changing Clearance Insufficient (LCCI), which means that the distance between the subject vehicle and surrounding vehicles falls below the safety threshold. In this situation, due to the close proximity of the vehicles to each other and the low coefficient of adhesion on snowy and icy road surfaces, any unexpected braking or acceleration actions by surrounding vehicles may cause collisions.

The second type of RLCB is Vehicle Lateral Instability (VLI). When the vehicle performs a lane change maneuver, the risk of lateral instability occurs due to excessive steering wheel angle rate or high driving speed, coupled with the low adhesion coefficient on the snowy and icy road surface. This risk solely concerns the vehicle's operating status and does not conflict with surrounding vehicles.

Key RCIs selection

Due to the complexity of LC operations on snowy and icy surfaces, there exist numerous RCIs for LC. To reduce the computational complexity of the model, it is necessary to select indicators that accurately depict the characteristics of RLCB. The key RCIs were selected in this study using decision tree principles. The specific process is shown in Fig. 4.

Flowchart for selecting key RCIs.

Statistical feature of RCIs.

The LC behavior parameters in Table 1 were discretized using a statistical value construction method to generate corresponding statistical indicators. 13 statistical features were extracted from the time series of each feature indicator, including the mean, maximum, minimum, mode, 0.25 quantiles, median, 0.75 quantiles, variance, standard deviation, range, variable coefficient, skewness, and kurtosis. The lane number(X27), not being a time series parameter, was excluded. Finally, the remaining 26 LC behavior parameters were selected as RCIs, totaling 338 statistical features. The importance of each RCI was objectively reflected by analyzing the importance of its statistical features.

Importance analysis of RCIs.

The importance of feature indicators was quantified using the information gain rate by the C4.5 algorithm. The C4.5 algorithm, an improvement over the ID3 algorithm, is used to generate decision trees for classification purposes. It analyzes the features of the data to create classification rules, enabling the prediction of the class of new data. This algorithm uses the concept of information entropy to split the data at each node of the tree, selecting the feature that provides the highest information gain at each step25. A greater information gain rate indicates a higher degree of orderliness of the resulting set after splitting, leading to better classification effects and higher importance of feature indicators. RCIs with information gain ratio rankings exceeding 50% are relatively less important and are eliminated. The statistical features of the selected RCIs were used as split nodes of the decision tree to split the original data. If the information gain ratio of the dataset after splitting is high and the level of confusion decreases significantly, it indicates that the selected RCIs have a better classification effect in identifying RLCB, meeting the requirements of the LC RCIs.

Correlation analysis of RCIs.

To reduce the computational complexity and improve the recognition efficiency, it is necessary to ensure that the input feature indicators are as independent as possible in the model. In this study, the Pearson correlation coefficient was used to analyze the correlation of the screened RCIs, and the feature parameters with strong correlation were excluded to avoid redundant information and multicollinearity problems. Hohlfelder26 pointed out that a Pearson correlation coefficient greater than 0.6 is considered a strong correlation between two feature parameters, which should be excluded to reduce feature redundancy.

Based on 1200 sets of LC sample data on snowy and icy surfaces, the samples were classified into three types according to the definition in section "Definition of risky LC behavior": Normal lane-changing (LCN), LCCI, and VLI. These classified samples will serve as the dataset for training the model. According to the key RCIs screening steps in section "Key RCIs selection", the key RCIs of RLCB on icy and snowy surfaces were identified, which will be used as inputs for the RLCB recognition model. Additionally, to ensure the efficiency and accuracy of the model, the original time series data were processed to maintain the optimal input sequence length in the model training and validation process.

Framework of the stacking ensemble learning

Ensemble learning is a machine learning method that improves classification and recognition performance by constructing multiple models and combining them27. The three common frameworks of ensemble learning are Bagging for parallel computation, Boosting for sequential computation, and Stacking for hierarchical integration. Compared with Bagging and Boosting, the Stacking algorithm can train multiple different classification models on the same dataset, thus taking advantage of the strengths of different models and improving the recognition accuracy28.

The framework of the Stacking ensemble learning.

Three different types of base-classifiers were constructed using a Stacking ensemble learning framework to generate preliminary recognition results. These preliminary recognition results were then fed into the meta-classifier to obtain the final recognition results. If one of the base- classifiers makes errors when learning specific regions of the feature space, the meta-classifier can correct these errors by integrating the learning results of other base-classifiers. This approach effectively takes the advantages of each base-classifier and compensates for their limitations in different regions, thereby improving the overall model performance and generalization ability.

ROC and AUC

The ROC curve is used to depict the trade-off relationship between the True Positive Rate (TPR) and the False Positive Rate (FPR). The horizontal axis of the ROC curve represents the False Positive Rate (FPR). The vertical axis represents the True Positive Rate (TPR), which is also known as recall35.

The higher convexity of the ROC curve represents better model performance. AUC (Area Under Curve) is the area enclosed by the ROC curve, which can effectively quantify the model's performance. The AUC value is between 0 and 1, and a higher AUC value indicates better model performance. When the value of AUC is greater than 0.7, it indicates that the expected effect of the model is better.

Results

Key RCIs

Based on 1200 sets of LC sample data on snowy and icy surfaces, the information gain ratio of 338 statistical features derived from 26 RCIs was calculated. Then, the rankings of the information gain ratios of the 13 statistical features for each RCI were plotted as a box plot, as shown in Fig. 6. In the box plot, each box represents the ranking of the information gain ratio of the 13 statistical features corresponding to each RCI. The smaller the vertical coordinate value of the box, the higher the importance of the feature parameter. RCIs with information gain ratio rankings exceeding 50% were eliminated. Therefore, the RCIs selected after the importance assessment are as follows: X1(δs), X2(ωs), X6(v), X7(vx), X8(ax), X9(vy), X10(ay), X13(θroll), X14(βs), X15(ryaw), X19(dySub&Pre2), X20(vySub&Pre2), X21(dySub&Fol2), X22(vySub&Fol2), X23(dySub&Pre1).

The statistical features of the selected RCIs were used as the splitting nodes of the decision tree to split the original data. The information gain ratio of the split data set was significantly increased, and the level of confusion was noticeably reduced. This indicates that the 15 RCIs have a good classification effect in identifying RLCB, meeting the requirements of LC RCIs.

To identify key RCIs with high importance and low intercorrelation, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted on the 15 RCIs, as shown in Fig. 7. Therefore, by integrating the importance of RCIs and Pearson correlation analysis, ωs, vx, ax, vy, ay, βs, ryaw, dySub&Pre2, vySub&Pre2, dySub&Fol2, vySub&Fol2, dySub&Pre1 were identified as key RCIs, providing input parameters for the subsequent LC risk behavior recognition model training.

Optimal time window length

The length of the LC time window represents the time series length of the model parameter input, and selecting an appropriate LC time window is crucial for accurately identifying RLCB. If the time window is too large, it may result in sequence data containing multiple behavioral features, reducing the model's accuracy and efficiency. Conversely, if the time window is too small, critical feature parameters may be excluded, severely reducing the model's recognition accuracy. Based on the LC dataset, the duration of LC on snowy and icy surfaces was determined to be approximately 4.2–11.6 s using statistical methods. In this study, the optimal time window length was identified by analyzing variations in RLCB using neural networks with different time window lengths. As shown in Fig. 8, the highest recognition accuracy was achieved when the time window length was 7.6 s. Therefore, 7.6 s was adopted as the optimal time window length for LC on snowy and icy surfaces.

Identification of RLCB and model evaluation

The method of manual selection was employed to screen 643 LCN samples, 323 LCCI samples, and 234 VLI samples from a dataset of 1200 LC samples. All data samples were randomly divided according to a 7:3 ratio, with 70% used as the training set and 30% as the test set. Using the five-fold cross-validation method, the training dataset was randomly divided into 5 subsets. To ensure better recognition, the original time series data were processed to maintain a time series length of 7.6 s for model input.

On snowy and icy surfaces, a Stacking ensemble learning model for RLCB recognition was developed. The model input parameters include ωs, vx, ax, vy, ay, βs, ryaw, dySub&Pre2, vySub&Pre2, dySub&Fol2, vySub&Fol2, dySub&Pre1. Discussion and conclusions

In this study, simulated highway driving experiments on snowy and icy surfaces were conducted using a driving simulator, and a large amount of LC data was collected. The key RCIs of LC behavior were extracted, and an RLCB recognition model applicable to snowy and icy surfaces was established. The conclusions are summarized as follows:

Based on the characteristics of LC behavior on snowy and icy surfaces, two risk LC behaviors were defined: vehicle lateral instability and LC clearance insufficient. A total of 1,200 LC samples were extracted from the driving simulation data on snowy and icy surfaces, including 643 LCN samples, 323 LCCI samples, and 234 VLI samples. The optimal LC time window length on snowy and icy surfaces has been determined to be 7.6 s.

The importance analysis of 26 RCIs during the LC process on snowy and icy surfaces was analyzed based on the C4.5 algorithm. Subsequently, Pearson correlation analysis was applied to eliminate highly correlated RCIs. Finally, 12 key RCIs, ωs, vx, ax, vy, ay, βs, ryaw, dySub&Pre2, vySub&Pre2, dySub&Fol2, vySub&Fol2, dySub&Pre1 were extracted and used as input variables for identifying RLCB on snowy and icy surfaces.

An RLCB recognition model was developed based on Stacking ensemble learning, integrating three algorithms: SVM, RF, and Bi-LSTM. The research results indicate that the Stacking ensemble learning model has the highest recognition rate for RLCB, with a comprehensive recognition accuracy of 98.33%. Moreover, the model demonstrates superior performance in terms of P, R, and F1 values. This illustrates that the Stacking ensemble learning algorithm is more suitable for identifying RLCB in the research on LC trajectory data extracted from driving simulators.

It is crucial to accurately identify RLCB on snowy and icy surfaces. For micro-traffic systems, identifying RLCB on snowy and icy surfaces can effectively prevent traffic accidents and enhance overall transportation safety. For macro-traffic systems, an in-depth understanding of the vehicle's operating status can help traffic management departments make effective decisions, reducing traffic congestion and accident rates. For ADAS systems, accurately identifying RLCB on snowy and icy surfaces can assist drivers in taking timely safety measures, thereby enhancing driving safety under adverse road conditions. For V2X systems, sharing and transmitting the recognition results of RLCB among vehicles can help surrounding vehicles make driving decisions in advance, further improving the safety performance of autonomous driving.

This study is based on driving simulation data to conduct research on RLCB recognition. In future studies, we will conduct real vehicle experiments at professional testing sites to improve data reliability and model accuracy. Furthermore, in the context of intelligent connected vehicles, the Stacking ensemble learning algorithm and its parameters optimization should be further expanded. Additional factors, such as vehicle dynamic parameters, vehicle interaction data, and traffic environment data, should be considered to achieve more accurate recognition of RLCB.
