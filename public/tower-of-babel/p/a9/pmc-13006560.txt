Role of Liver Biopsy in Biliary Atresia: Impact of Histological Fibrosis on Kasai Portoenterostomy Outcomes and Decision Making
Authors: Tawfiq Taki Al Lawati, Omar Saadah, Mohammed Jaafer Al Sajwani, Fatma Ali Ramadhan Al Lawati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of histological fibrosis on liver biopsy at presentation on the decision-making and outcomes of Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE), as well as the subsequent timing of liver transplantation.MethodsA retrospective, cross-sectional study of children <13 years old with biliary atresia (BA) seen at the Royal Hospital between January 2007 and December 2021.ResultsForty children were diagnosed with BA, of whom 31 (77.5%) underwent a liver biopsy, and 26 (65.0%) underwent KPE. Thirty children (96.8%) had either complete or impending cirrhosis. Eleven children (42.3%) who underwent KPE received a liver transplantation at a median age of 26 months (interquartile range [IQR]=33–34). Four children did not undergo KPE, and seven (50.0%) of these underwent liver transplantation at a median age of 22 months (IQR=13–25). The age of liver transplantation did not differ statistically between the KPE group and the primary liver transplantation group (p=0.311). Three children (11.5%) were alive with their native liver following KPE at 5 years. The mortality rate was similar in the KPE and non-KPE groups (p=0.171).ConclusionOf the 40 children with biliary atresia, 31 had a liver biopsy, with 30 (96.8%) exhibiting either complete or impending cirrhosis. Hepatic fibrosis did not impact the decision to perform KPE. The age of liver transplantation did not differ significantly between the KPE and non-KPE groups (median ages of 26 vs. 22 months, p=0.311).

INTRODUCTION

Biliary atresia (BA) is an idiopathic, rapidly progressive fibrosing and obstructive cholangiopathy that occurs in infancy and typically presents with neonatal jaundice and pale stools [1]. If left untreated, BA is fatal by 2–3 years of age. Timely surgical intervention with Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE) remains essential for survival [1]. The gold standard for the diagnosis of BA is intraoperative cholangiography [1]. However, several noninvasive and minimally invasive diagnostic modalities are commonly used to support or confirm the diagnosis prior to surgery. These include magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) [2], hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scanning [3], liver ultrasonography demonstrating the triangular cord sign [4], and liver biopsy [5]. Among these, liver biopsy is considered a reliable diagnostic tool in many centers [1].

Management of BA primarily involves timely KPE. When performed within the first 60 days of life, KPE results in early bilirubin clearance in approximately 70–80% of patients and can significantly delay the need for liver transplantation. However, the success of KPE declines with increasing age at surgery, with bilirubin clearance rates decreasing to approximately 50% when performed between 60 and 90 days and to 25% when performed after 90 days of life [6]. Reported native liver survival following KPE is approximately 35% at 5 years and 30% at 10 years [7], with the best reported native liver survival reaching 51% within the first 4 years of life [8]. In contrast, post–liver transplantation survival for BA is substantially higher, with reported survival rates of 91.5% at 5 years and 87.1% at 10 years [9]. Consequently, some authors have suggested liver transplantation as a primary treatment strategy, particularly given the higher post-transplant mortality observed in children who undergo transplantation after failed KPE compared with those receiving primary liver transplantation [10].

Beyond the timing of surgery, several additional factors have been investigated for their influence on the success of KPE. These include the pediatric end-stage liver disease (PELD) score, the extent of liver fibrosis, the presence of extrahepatic congenital anomalies, and recurrent cholangitis. Each of these factors has been shown to adversely affect KPE outcomes [910]. Interestingly, bilirubin clearance itself has not been found to significantly influence long-term native liver survival or the eventual need for liver transplantation [10].

The role of liver fibrosis in predicting KPE outcomes remains controversial. A recent meta-analysis by Jahangirnia et al. [11] demonstrated that advanced liver fibrosis is associated with lower odds of native liver survival. Similarly, Kumar et al. [12] reported poorer survival outcomes in children with advanced fibrosis when KPE was performed at a mean age of 96 days. Gunadi et al. [13] further supported these findings by demonstrating a significant correlation between the extent of liver fibrosis at the time of KPE and reduced patient survival in a cohort of 50 infants. In contrast, Czubkowski et al. [14] found no association between the extent of fibrosis and age at surgery, reporting a native liver survival rate of 96% at 2 years of age.

Despite evidence suggesting a potential impact of advanced cirrhosis on surgical outcomes, many surgical units do not routinely require preoperative liver biopsy to assess the extent of fibrosis or bile duct size prior to KPE [1516]. Therefore, the present study aims to evaluate the impact of histological liver fibrosis at presentation on the decision to perform KPE and on the subsequent timing of liver transplantation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A cross-sectional retrospective review was conducted on all electronic charts of children diagnosed with BA between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2021. The electronic databases of patients at the Royal Hospital (RH) in Oman were used to retrieve data for all children ≤13 years who presented at the RH with neonatal conjugated hyperbilirubinemia. Bio-demographic, clinical, radiological, and laboratory data, along with the age of KPE and the age of liver transplantation, were obtained from the electronic medical records.

Exclusion criteria

The following categories of children were excluded: those older than 13 years, those who underwent KPE prior to the study period, non-Omanis, and patients with causes of neonatal jaundice other than BA. Non-Omani patients were excluded as they were diagnosed, operated on, and followed up outside the RH.

Case identification

Children with conjugated hyperbilirubinemia at the RH underwent a battery of serological, radiological, and histopathological investigations. Serological investigations included metabolic, infective, and autoimmune workups. Radiological investigations included an abdominal U/S and could include HIDA scans. Both the U/S and HIDA scans were carried out and interpreted by specialized pediatric radiologists.

All liver biopsies were examined and interpreted by a dedicated liver histopathology team at the RH. Complete cirrhosis was defined by mature nodules observed on liver histology, along with stage 4 fibrosis on the METAVIR scoring system. METAVIR fibrosis F3 or the presence of incomplete nodules was categorized as impending cirrhosis [17]. Any infant with conjugated hyperbilirubinemia (>17 µmoL/L), elevated gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and two of the following three findings was considered for an intraoperative cholangiogram to confirm BA and subsequently KPE:

• U/S liver with features suggestive of BA.

• U/S liver with features suggestive of BA.

• HIDA scan showing no intestinal excretion.

• HIDA scan showing no intestinal excretion.

• Liver biopsy demonstrating features consistent with BA.

• Liver biopsy demonstrating features consistent with BA.

Post-operative management protocol of BA

Medical management for post-KPE patients, particularly regarding the use of steroids, antibiotics, and ursodeoxycholic acid, was implemented based on the preferences of the surgeon and the gastroenterologist. The antibiotics used by the surgeons included a fixed combination of amoxicillin, metronidazole, and gentamicin for 7 days. Prednisolone was administered at a dosage of 1 mg/kg for 1 month, followed by a tapering schedule. Ursodeoxycholic acid was dosed at 10–15 mg/kg per dose, twice a day, for up to 3 months.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Co.). Prior to analysis, all continuous data underwent a normality test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test. Continuous data were expressed as mean±standard deviations or as median and interquartile ranges (IQR). Statistical associations were tested using Student’s t-test, ANOVA, and regression analysis for normally distributed data. The Mann–Whitney U-test was applied for non-parametric and non-normally distributed samples. The strength of associations was expressed as p-values, with statistical significance considered for p-values less than 0.05. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to compare the outcomes of KPE based on the timing of liver transplant.

Ethical approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was obtained from the research committee at the RH, and a written letter of approval was waived by the ethical committee since the study was anonymous.

Investigations

1. Abdominal ultrasound (U/S)

Abdominal U/S results were available for only 35 of the 40 children (87.5%). Twenty children (57.1%) had an atrophic gallbladder along with the triangular cord sign. Seven children (20.0%) had an isolated atrophic gallbladder, and one child (2.9%) had an isolated triangular cord sign. In five children (14.3%), the U/S features indicated nonspecific hepatitis, while two children (5.7%) had normal U/S findings.

2. HIDA scan

HIDA scans were performed in only 18 children (45.0%), and all showed no secretion of the radioisotope into the duodenum.

3. Liver biopsy

Liver biopsy results were available for 31 out of the 40 children (77.5%). Of these, 30 children (97.0%) had either established (METAVIR F4) or impending liver cirrhosis (METAVIR F3). One child (2.5%) had stage 2 fibrosis on liver biopsy.

4. Kasai portoenterostomy

Out of the 40 children with confirmed BA, 26 (65.0%) underwent KPE surgery, while 15 (35.0%) received no surgical intervention. The bilirubin level after KPE significantly decreased from a mean of 177.8±38.8 µmoL/L before surgery to 166.8±133.2 µmoL/L at 3 months (p<0.001). However, this reduction was not sustained, as the mean bilirubin level rose back to 190±141.65 at 6 months. The differences in the age of presentation and diagnosis between the KPE and non-KPE groups were statistically significant (p=0.015 and p=0.020, respectively).

5. Medical management

Regarding the medical management of post-KPE children, 3 children (11.5%) received prednisolone, 18 children (69.2%) were given antibiotics (a combination of metronidazole, gentamicin, and amoxicillin), and 13 children (50.0%) received ursodeoxycholic acid. There was no significant statistical association between the use of antibiotics (p=0.862) or ursodeoxycholic acid (p=0.433) and the need for a subsequent liver transplant.

Outcomes

1. KPE group

Out of the 26 children who underwent KPE, three children (11.5%) had bilirubin levels <20 µmoL/L at 6 months. Bilirubin measurements were unavailable for 14 children (53.8%) at this time point. Eleven children (42.3%) underwent liver transplantation at a median age of 26 months (IQR=33–34). All transplanted children, except for one who died shortly after transplantation due to sepsis, were alive at the time of the study, with a median age of 6 years (IQR=3–8). Among those who underwent KPE and did not require liver transplantation by 26 months, 15 children (57.7%) were alive at a median age of 4.0 years (IQR=2–7). One child was lost to follow-up and was unreachable by phone, and two children (7.7%) were listed for liver transplantation during the study period at ages 10 months and 112 months, respectively. Five children (19.2%) died (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This is a retrospective study describing the clinical features, liver biopsy findings, and KPE outcomes of 40 children with BA seen at a tertiary hospital over 15 years. Our study highlights a high percentage (96.7%) of advanced liver fibrosis in children with BA who presented at a mean age of 54 days of life. However, despite the presence of advanced fibrosis, the extent of fibrosis had no impact on the decision to perform KPE. Moreover, liver transplantation in the KPE group occurred only four months later than in the group that did not undergo KPE, with both groups undergoing transplantation at approximately two years of age. The study also shows that in only a small proportion of patients (27%), KPE was able to delay the need for liver transplantation to around four years of age.

Regarding liver fibrosis, our findings are in line with other studies that have emphasized the negative association between the extent of hepatic fibrosis and the age at liver transplantation. Hence, primary liver transplantation might be an option. Unfortunately, many surgical units do not consider the stage of fibrosis, and the primary factor guiding management decisions remains confirmation of BA on biopsy. Not surprisingly, native liver survival post-KPE in our study was only 11.5% at five years, which is far lower than the figures reported from Japan and Nordic countries (52–58%) [1819]. The low native liver survival post-KPE may be related to multiple factors; however, liver fibrosis may be an important contributing factor in our cohort, with borderline statistical significance (p=0.05). Similarly, and consistent with our findings, Al-Hussaini et al. reported a low two-year native liver survival rate of only 35% in a cohort of 204 children, while in our population, two-year native liver survival was only 27% [20]. In that study, KPE prolonged survival by only 1.2 years before liver transplantation, a finding that again raises the question of primary liver transplantation.

The similarity in the timing of liver transplantation between the KPE group and the primary liver transplant group, coupled with the short duration of native liver survival, mandates careful selection of children for KPE, particularly those with cirrhosis. It is possible that, as liver transplant facilities and outcomes improve, more physicians may opt for primary liver transplantation in selected cases [21]. We propose that in selected patients with advanced cirrhosis, parents should be counseled regarding the short interval between KPE and liver transplantation observed in many centers. Therefore, liver biopsy findings should be incorporated into shared decision-making when considering KPE versus primary liver transplantation in selected patients. The study also identified a single patient with BA and normal GGT, highlighting the need for comprehensive clinical assessment, including stool color and ultrasound findings, even in the presence of normal GGT.

The limitations of this study are mainly related to the small sample size, particularly the very limited number of patients without cirrhosis. In addition, the single-center design and missing bilirubin levels at six months of age represent further limitations. Missing data were substantial (26 out of 40 patients) and were due to the absence of a standardized protocol for monitoring bilirubin levels post-KPE. It was deemed inappropriate to use conventional imputation methods; therefore, only available data were analyzed. Nevertheless, the main outcome of the study was not affected. Despite these limitations, the methodology and sample size are comparable to many published studies, allowing our findings to contribute to the broader BA literature.

Future directions

With growing experience in the long-term outcomes of KPE and liver transplantation, larger multicenter studies are needed to further validate and standardize fibrosis scoring systems in BA and to clarify their impact on outcomes.

In conclusion, despite presentation at approximately 60 days of age, 30 out of 31 children with BA had advanced fibrosis or established cirrhosis. The extent of fibrosis had no impact on the decision to perform KPE. Liver transplantation was performed at 26 months in the KPE group and at 22 months in the non-KPE group (p=0.311). Only 27% of children who underwent KPE retained their native liver by four years of age. The status of liver fibrosis should be considered when planning KPE, and families should be counseled accordingly. Larger multicenter studies are needed to validate and standardize fibrosis scoring systems for BA patients planned for KPE.
