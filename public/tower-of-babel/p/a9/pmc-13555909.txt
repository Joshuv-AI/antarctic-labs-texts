Alexithymia in nurses: a context-specific concept analysis using rodgers’s evolutionary methodology
Authors: Wu Feng, Lv Yiting, Sun Yanwen, Jin Yinji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlexithymia, a personality construct characterized by difficulties in identifying and describing emotions, as well as a tendency toward externally-oriented thinking, has significant implications for psychological well-being and interpersonal functioning. In the nursing profession, where emotional awareness and communication are essential, alexithymic traits may contribute to psychological distress and compromised patient interactions. However, its contextual manifestations and professional relevance within nursing remain insufficiently clarified. Rather than treating alexithymia in nurses as a wholly independent concept from general alexithymia, this study aimed to clarify how the concept is contextually specified within nursing practice using Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis, thereby enhancing psychological understanding and informing support strategies.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted across CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to January 17, 2026. Following Rodgers’ evolutionary framework, we analyzed the concept’s evolution, surrogate terms, related concepts, attributes, antecedents, consequences, and exemplars. A total of 52 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis.ResultsThe analysis identified six antecedent conditions associated with alexithymia in nurses, including demographic characteristics, occupational environment, emotional intelligence, social support, psychological resilience and self-esteem stability.Three core attributes emerged: difficulties in identifying and describing emotions, externally-oriented thinking, and somatic manifestations of emotional experience. Consequences associated with alexithymia in nurses were identified at multiple levels, including individual psychological outcomes (e.g., burnout, reduced well-being), interpersonal effects (e.g., impaired nurse-patient communication), and organizational implications (e.g., increased turnover intention). The findings also indicate that the practical impact of alexithymia in nurses extends to therapeutic engagement and relational care within clinical settings.ConclusionsThis concept analysis provides a comprehensive conceptualization of alexithymia in nurses, clarifying its context-specific manifestations and professional relevance within healthcare settings. The findings offer a theoretical foundation for future research, context-sensitive assessment in nursing practice, and targeted interventions to support nurses’ emotional processing and mental health. For healthcare administrators and psychologists, these insights highlight the importance of addressing occupational factors associated with alexithymia and implementing structured, evidence-informed strategies to strengthen nurses’ psychological resilience and emotional awareness, ultimately supporting better mental health outcomes and quality of patient care.Keywords: Alexithymia, Nurses, Concept analysis, Emotional experience, Emotional expression

Introduction

Nursing is a profession that provides holistic care to individuals based on continuous interaction and mutual relationships. Nurses are the healthcare professionals who interact most frequently with individuals in medical settings [1]. As the core workforce within the healthcare system, nurses’ mental health and professional efficacy directly impact nursing quality and patient safety. Among the numerous factors influencing nurses’ psychological adaptability, alexithymia has been recognized as a significant psychological variable, yet it is often overlooked [2]. Alexithymia, also referred to as difficulties in emotional awareness and expression, describes deficits in emotional cognition, emotional processing, and emotional regulation [3]. Alexithymia is relatively common among nurses. Research indicates that 38.5% of nurses exhibit alexithymic tendencies toward affective disorders in clinical practice, while 26% have already developed such disorders [4]. Alexithymia generally regarded as a stable personality trait; some studies associate it with psychopathological disorders such as depression or anxiety, or with somatic illnesses [5]. Nurses with higher levels of alexithymia often struggle to recognize, express, and release their emotions in a timely and effective manner. This tendency can contribute to negative emotional experiences such as loneliness and apathy, while simultaneously exacerbating occupational stress levels. Such challenges undermine nurses’ mental health and heighten their desire to leave their positions. More importantly, the impact of alexithymia extends far beyond the individual level, profoundly affecting the interpersonal relationships central to nursing care. In nurse-patient interactions, nurses who struggle to understand their own emotions also find it difficult to effectively recognize and respond to patients’ emotional needs. This can lead to less effective empathic communication, potentially reducing patient satisfaction and contributing to workplace conflicts. In China, research on alexithymia has progressed slowly, with descriptive studies dominating while prospective research remains insufficient [6]. However, research attention to alexithymia in nursing populations remains limited. Nursing leaders must pay close attention to alexithymia among nursing staff to help them express personal feelings and release stress. This is particularly crucial for enhancing nurses’ work experience, preventing burnout, and alleviating occupational stress. Although the significance of alexithymia in nursing is increasingly recognized, the nursing community’s understanding of this concept remains ambiguous. Its definition, characteristics, and specific implications within the nursing context—including its causes and consequences—have yet to be systematically explored. Compared to psychological perspectives, nursing science should place greater emphasis on the practical manifestations of this concept within high-pressure clinical settings and its impact on nursing outcomes. Importantly, the present study does not assume that alexithymia in nurses constitutes a wholly independent concept from alexithymia in the general population. Rather, it examines how this broader construct is contextually expressed within nursing, where emotional labor, therapeutic communication, and repeated exposure to suffering may shape its manifestation, relevance, and consequences. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a clear and precise conceptual analysis of nurses’ alexithymia. Given this, this study employs Rodgers’ evolutionary conceptual analysis method to conduct an in-depth examination of nurses’ alexithymia. The aim is to clarify its essential characteristics, antecedent variables, and subsequent effects, while distinguishing it from similar concepts. This analysis seeks to provide a reference for subsequent research on nurses’ alexithymia. Using Rodgers’ evolutionary approach, this study aims to clarify the context-specific attributes, antecedent conditions, and consequences of alexithymia in nurses, while also examining how the concept has been understood and used within the nursing literature over time. By doing so, this analysis seeks to provide a theoretical reference for future nursing research, context-sensitive assessment, and psychologically informed interventions targeting nurses’ emotional processing and mental health.

Research methods

Literature review

Keywords such as “Nurse/Alexithymia,” “Clinical nurse/Alexithymia,” and “Nursing staff/Alexithymia” were utilized to search the databases, including China Knowledge, Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform, Wipo Database, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library. The search was conducted from the inception of each database until January 17, 2026.

Inclusion of literature

Tofthagen ot al [7] suggest that to draw reliable conclusions, a study should include approximately 30 articles, representing about 20% of all cited references. In this study, initial database searches yielded 1,889 articles. After removing duplicates, 1317 articles remained. Two researchers independently screened the literature; disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher to reach consensus. Results

Evolution of the concept of alexithymia in nursing contexts

The concept of alexithymia was first proposed by psychiatrist Peter Sifneos in the 1970s to describe psychological characteristics associated with patients suffering from psychosomatic illnesses [8]. Its classic definition encompasses three core dimensions: (1) difficulty identifying and describing one’s own emotions; (2) lack of imagination; (3) externally-oriented thinking, a concrete cognitive style characterized by a tendency to focus on external details while avoiding internal emotional experiences [9]. For a considerable period, alexithymia was primarily viewed as a cross-cultural, relatively stable individual psychological trait. However, with the shift in medical paradigms toward the bio-psycho-social model, the nursing discipline began to place heightened emphasis on practitioners’ emotional labor and mental health. Researchers have progressively discovered that this concept demonstrates strong explanatory power in understanding unique challenges faced by nursing populations, such as empathy fatigue [10] occupational burnout [5], and nurse-patient communication barriers [1]. Research by Shen Jie et al. revealed that operating room nurses exhibit mild emotional difficulties and severe empathy fatigue, with higher levels of emotional disorders correlating with greater empathy fatigue [10]. Emotional dysregulation is significantly correlated with occupational burnout. Research indicates that high emotional dysregulation prevents nursing staff from timely releasing internal emotions, leading to psychological depression. This chain of reactions contributes to the onset of occupational burnout [5]. A study of Turkish nurses revealed that developing communication skills and emotional expression abilities is crucial, directly impacting nurses’ mental health. Emotional dysregulation is closely linked to communication competence, with nurses possessing strong communication skills exhibiting lower levels of emotional dysregulation [1]. Taken together, these findings suggest not that nursing has generated a wholly separate parent concept of alexithymia, but that the concept has gradually acquired context-specific salience within nursing research and practice. In this disciplinary context, alexithymia is increasingly discussed not only as an intrapersonal trait, but also as a phenomenon closely related to emotional labor, therapeutic communication, occupational stress, and exposure to suffering. Accordingly, its meaning in nursing has evolved from a predominantly trait-based interpretation toward a more context-sensitive and practice-relevant understanding. In China, the application and interpretation of alexithymia in healthcare settings also developed progressively. In 1991, Yao Fangchuan et al. [11] introduced the 26-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-26) into Chinese and examined its psychometric properties, which facilitated the early use of the construct in Chinese health-related research. Yao et al. described alexithymia as a pattern of difficulties in emotional expression observed in individuals experiencing various physiological or psychological problems [11]. In 2003, Yi Jinyao et al. [12] revised the Chinese version of the 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20), establishing a three-factor structure consisting of difficulty identifying feelings, difficulty describing feelings, and externally-oriented thinking. This version has since been widely used in subsequent nursing studies. In 2014, Wu Aifen et al. first reported a moderate positive correlation between alexithymia among nurses and occupational stress, thereby linking the construct more explicitly to occupational health in nursing [13]. Multiple cross-sectional studies confirmed alexithymia as an independent risk factor for occupational burnout, depression, and somatization, expanding the concept from a “personality trait” to an intervenable occupationally relevant psychological risk factor within nursing contexts [14–16]. Since 2016, research has increasingly focused on mediating variables such as psychological resilience, cognitive-emotional regulation, and social support [6]. Post-2020 research has broadened the populations examined beyond practicing nurses to include nursing students and nurses at different stages of training [16, 17]. In parallel, emerging evidence suggests that emotion-focused educational interventions and mindfulness-based approaches may help reduce alexithymia levels or improve related emotional processing capacities [18, 19]. Overall, the evolution of this concept within nursing lies less in the creation of a new conceptual entity than in the contextual reinterpretation of a broader psychological construct. As nursing research increasingly emphasizes relational care, emotional demands, and occupational mental health, alexithymia has become more clearly recognized as a clinically and professionally meaningful construct in nursing science.

Substitute terms and related terms

Substitute terms refer to alternative words used by researchers during their studies to express the same concept beyond their selected term [20]. No clear substitute terms were consistently identified in the reviewed literature .Related terms refer to words that share commonalities with a specific concept but do not possess identical characteristics [20]. Through the literature review, related terms associated with alexithymia in nurses included emotional regulation difficulties, compassion fatigue, emotional labor, emotional exhaustion, and communication difficulties [21–23]. These terms overlap with alexithymia in their focus on emotional functioning and occupational burden, but they do not represent equivalent constructs.

Attributes of nurse alexithymia

Difficulties in identifying and describing emotions

Difficulty identifying and describing emotions is a core dimension of alexithymia and is highly relevant in nursing contexts. Nurses with alexithymic tendencies may struggle to recognize their own emotional states and to verbalize them appropriately. This limitation may reduce emotional self-awareness and constrain their ability to communicate emotional experiences effectively. Existing literature suggests that impaired emotional awareness is associated with affective disturbances and reduced empathic responsiveness [24, 25]. In nursing practice, such deficits may indirectly increase the risk of empathy fatigue and workplace conflict by intensifying emotional exhaustion [26]. Sharafkhani et al. [27] found in a study of 365 nursing undergraduates in northern Iran that communication skills were negatively associated with alexithymia, and students with lower alexithymia tended to show stronger empathic ability; Thus, they concluded that communication skills should be a priority intervention target in nursing education. A study of nursing undergraduates also indicated that empathy influences depersonalization [28]. Consequently, strengthening communication competencies and emotional literacy may help reduce emotional identification and expression difficulties among nurses and nursing students.

Externally-oriented thinking style

Externally-oriented thinking primarily processes thoughts and information through interaction, discussion, and action with the external world. Individuals with this style exhibit “outward-directed” thought processes, requiring expression, communication, and practical application to clarify and complete their ideas. Clinical nurses, due to the unique nature of their work environment and role, endure prolonged high-intensity stress. Sustained high-risk conditions may lead them to avoid external interactions, resulting in externally-oriented thinking style disorders. A study of 1,480 nurses revealed that psychological distress among clinical nurses primarily manifests as extroverted thinking patterns, indicating nurses rarely open up in daily practice [29]. A study of clinical nursing students similarly found this pattern. Overwhelmed by demanding clinical tasks, students habitually direct cognitive resources toward external procedures and protocols, forming an “external cognitive orientation” that correlates with elevated levels of alexithymia [2]. Zhang Chunyu et al. [30] proposed that, mechanistically, individuals with high alexithymia exhibit reduced medial prefrontal cortex activation on MRI scans. This suggests alexithymia individuals possess cognitive characteristics favoring external facts and avoiding introspection. For nursing students, externally-oriented thinking may not only result from poor emotion recognition but also represent a pre-cognitive style stemming from insufficient interpersonal-emotional processing resources.

Somatization of emotional experiences

Somatization of emotional experiences refers to the unconscious conversion and expression of psychological distress, stress, or conflict into physical symptoms [31]. Long-term work stress among nurses may not manifest as psychological complaints of anxiety or depression, but rather as persistent chronic headaches, back pain, gastrointestinal discomfort, or prolonged fatigue [32]. They genuinely believe they are merely “unwell physically,” unaware of the connection to unprocessed emotional burdens. A cross-sectional study of 810 registered nurses in Turkey revealed that alexithymia—characterized by difficulty identifying and describing emotions—can lead to somatic discomfort (e.g., headaches, fatigue) through immature defense mechanisms like anger suppression [33]. Nurses in operating rooms, ICUs, and emergency departments exhibit higher scores for both alexithymia and somatization compared to nurses in other departments [4, 34, 35]. A quantitative review of literature revealed correlations between alexithymia and somatization, with impaired emotional cognition and difficulty describing emotions emerging as significant predictors of somatization symptoms [15]. Basak et al. found that alexithymia can trigger tension headaches and is associated with chronic tension-type headaches, which may lead to symptoms such as insomnia, reduced adaptability, and depression [36].

Antecedent variables of alexithymia in nurses

Antecedents refer to events or situations that exist before the formation of a concept [20]. Based on the reviewed literature, factors associated with alexithymia in nurses were identified at the individual, interpersonal, and occupational levels. In this study, these antecedent conditions are grouped into six categories: general demographic factors, occupational environment and exposure to trauma or chronic stress, emotional intelligence, social support, psychological resilience and self-esteem stability.

General demographic factors

The literature suggests that alexithymia in nurses may vary according to several demographic and work-related characteristics, including years of service, gender, marital status, educational attainment, income, living status, and department assignment. Ruiling Nan et al. [4] suggest that younger nurses with less than five years of experience exhibit poorer coping abilities toward external stressors, which may place them at higher risk of alexithymic tendencies. A study of male nurses in Zhejiang Province found a 32% alexithymia prevalence among males, higher than the 22% among females [37]; potentially linked to men’s limited socialization of emotional expression. Hiirola et al. [38] suggest that higher educational attainment and income may serve as protective factors against alexithymia. Nurses with advanced education possess greater comprehension skills, psychological resilience, and the ability to accurately recognize and express their emotions [39]. Research indicates that unmarried individuals are more prone to alexithymia than married individuals, with divorced individuals showing higher prevalence than unmarried individuals [4]. Nurses in high-stress departments such as ICUs, operating rooms, and emergency departments exhibit higher alexithymia rates than those in general wards [40].

Occupational environment

Nurses frequently experience chronic occupational stress and repeated exposure to emotionally challenging events because of the nature of their work environment. From developmental psychology and sociocultural perspectives, the onset of alexithymia is believed to be associated with childhood experiences and developmental factors [30]. Nan et al. [4] suggest that early emotional neglect or prolonged ICU exposure to patient mortality events can generate unresolved grief, subsequently inhibiting emotional verbalization. Such chronic stressors suppress individuals’ emotional expression, gradually leading to diminished capacity for emotional articulation over time. Research by Kooiman et al. [41] suggests that parental indifference or overprotection increases the risk of emotional disorders in adulthood. The influence of parental behavior during childhood persists into adulthood, potentially contributing to the development of alexithymia. Within nursing contexts, these findings indicate that both distal developmental experiences and proximal occupational stressors may shape vulnerability to alexithymic features.

Emotional intelligence

Emotional intelligence refers to the ability to perceive and regulate one’s own emotions, serving as a crucial competency in clinical communication within the nursing profession. Individuals possessing higher emotional intelligence are more likely to experience job satisfaction and efficiency, whereas those unable to manage their emotions may become entangled in internal conflicts, ultimately undermining their work performance and clear thinking abilities [42]. Mohamed and Ahmed [43] found that emotional intelligence negatively correlates with alexithymia. It indicates that nurses with higher emotional intelligence tend to show stronger psychological resilience, better adaptation to stressful workplace events, and greater ability to identify and express inner emotional states, which may reduce vulnerability to alexithymia.

Social support

Alexithymia in nurses is influenced by social support. According to the social support buffering model, social support acts as a buffer between stressful events and their evaluation [44]. Higher levels of social support correlate with a lower incidence of alexithymia among nurses [45]. This occurs because abundant social support alleviates nurses’ stress, reduces tension and anxiety stemming from nursing duties, and lowers alexithymia severity by maintaining positive psychological states [6]. Amid feelings of helplessness and avoidance during stressful situations, sufficient social support provides nurses with care and encouragement, restoring their sense of personal worth. Moreover, social support encompasses not only assistance from family, friends, and partners but also depends on individuals’ utilization of such support. Liu et al. [46] suggest that effective social support utilization provides coping strategies, enhances problem-solving abilities, boosts personal accomplishment and job satisfaction, reduces perceived stress, and shields individuals from negative emotions.In this sense, social support may function as an important contextual buffer against the development or exacerbation of alexithymic tendencies in nursing staff.

Psychological resilience

Research indicates that psychological resilience mediates the relationship between alexithymia and stress. Low resilience may exacerbate difficulties in emotional processing. By enhancing emotional regulation capabilities, psychological resilience helps individuals more effectively identify and describe emotions, thereby alleviating core symptoms of alexithymia [47]. Nurses frequently encounter stressors such as workplace violence and high job demands. Those with high psychological resilience exhibit lower risks of psychological distress (e.g., depression, anxiety) and higher sleep quality. Research indicates that workplace injustice and psychological resilience jointly influence nurses’ mental health. Nurses with insufficient psychological resilience are more likely to develop alexithymia when confronting unfair treatment due to deficits in emotional processing [48]. Overall, the reviewed evidence suggests that psychological resilience may operate as a protective internal resource, moderating the impact of occupational adversity on alexithymic experiences in nurses.

Self-esteem stability

In addition to resilience, other relatively stable psychological resources may also be relevant to nurses’ emotional functioning. A Turkish methodological study involving 305 hospital nurses demonstrated that the Turkish version of the Self-Esteem Stability Scale had good psychometric properties, including a clear one-factor structure, excellent model fit, high internal consistency, and strong four-week test–retest reliability. Although this study did not directly examine alexithymia, it underscores the importance of assessing stable self-evaluative characteristics in nurses, as such characteristics may be relevant to psychological well-being and vulnerability to emotional difficulties [49].

Consequences of alexithymia in nurses

The reviewed literature suggests that the consequences of alexithymia in nurses can be understood at three interrelated levels: personal, interpersonal, and occupational.

Personal level

Research indicates that alexithymic characteristics and related difficulties in emotional processing are closely related to personal health and significantly influence an individual’s anxiety and depressive mood [29]. Li Shuwen et al. [50] found that nurses with higher levels of perceived stress were more likely to experience greater psychological burden. Such nurses exhibited immature coping tendencies such as avoidance, blame, and rumination, along with diminished stress management abilities, which could trigger or exacerbate depressive and anxiety symptoms. The reviewed literature also suggests that alexithymia is associated with negative emotional experiences such as loneliness and apathy, as well as reduced well-being. Emotional dysregulation is significantly associated with sleep disorders, and chronic sleep problems may impair nurses’ work performance and health [51]. Post-traumatic stress and reduced resilience exhibit a bidirectional relationship with affective disorders and traumatic experiences [52]. Consequently, nurses exposed to traumatic events in their profession—such as patient deaths—may struggle to restore normal emotional processing capabilities due to impaired emotional regulation. Overall, at the personal level, alexithymia may contribute to poorer mental health, reduced psychological adjustment, and increased vulnerability to burnout-related distress.

Interpersonal level

Difficulties in identifying and describing emotions may hinder nurses’ ability to understand both their own emotional states and the emotional needs of patients, thereby weakening emotional support and empathic communication. Nurses with alexithymic tendencies may fail to respond promptly to patients’ emotional needs, leading to feelings of isolation or misunderstanding. This exacerbates psychological distress and diminishes patient satisfaction with care. The reviewed literature further indicates that impaired emotional awareness may be accompanied by reduced emotional expression and difficulties verbalizing feelings [53]. This may hinder nurses’ ability to effectively explain medical information, provide emotional reassurance, or discuss sensitive topics (such as prognosis), thereby diminishing patient comprehension and treatment adherence. In this sense, the consequences of alexithymia at the patient level are reflected mainly in compromised therapeutic interaction, lower perceived empathy, and potentially poorer care experiences.

Occupational level

Research by Pei Juhong et al. revealed that emergency nurses exhibit significant difficulties in expressing emotions, with a positive correlation between this emotional dysregulation and occupational burnout. Addressing these issues in emergency nurses can facilitate interventions to alleviate their occupational burnout [54]. Nurses working under prolonged high-intensity conditions gradually lose sensitivity to emotional experiences, thereby increasing their risk of burnout. Research indicates that nurses’ alexithymia is a significant factor influencing occupational stress. Nurses with alexithymia struggle to express their emotions, leading to emotional accumulation and the development of negative psychological states, which ultimately contribute to occupational stress [13]. A study involving 116 clinical nurses revealed that communication barriers are closely associated with stress stemming from the nursing work environment, workload, career planning, and demanding job content [40]. He Hongying et al. further indicated that nurses with higher levels of alexithymia experience greater occupational stress, with emotional cognition, emotional description, and externally-oriented thinking serving as independent predictors of occupational stress [55]. In addition to occupational stress and burnout, the literature suggests that alexithymia may also be associated with reduced job satisfaction, interpersonal conflict, impaired team collaboration, and increased turnover intention. At the occupational level, therefore, alexithymia has implications not only for individual work experience but also for workforce stability and nursing quality.

Assessment tools for alexithymia in nursing contexts

Because this study does not regard alexithymia in nurses as a wholly independent construct, the assessment of this phenomenon in nursing populations has primarily relied on established general alexithymia instruments rather than nurse-specific scales. The most commonly used tools in the reviewed literature were the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20) and the Perth Alexithymia Questionnaire (PAQ).

Toronto alexithymia scale (TAS-20)

The Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS), particularly its 20-item version (TAS-20), is currently the most widely used measure of alexithymia globally. Developed by Taylor et al. [56] as a simplified version of the original TAS, the TAS-20 is a self-report measure widely used among healthcare professionals, students, and other groups. It is particularly suitable for screening nursing students and clinical nurses for alexithymia. In nursing research, it has frequently been used to assess alexithymia among nursing students and clinical nurses.The scale comprises 20 items organized into three dimensions: difficulty identifying feelings, difficulty describing feelings, and externally-oriented thinking. It employs a 5-point rating scale, with total scores ranging from 20 to 100. Higher scores indicate more severe alexithymia. Scores between 20 and 51 indicate no alexithymia, scores between 52 and 60 indicate a borderline level, and scores above 60 suggest the presence of alexithymia.The scale’s Cronbach’s α coefficient is 0.83, with Cronbach’s α coefficients for Factors 1, 2, and 3 ranging from 0.581 to 0.739. Reliability is good. Yi, JinYao ot al 12 validated the Chinese version of the scale, which yielded a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.83 and a test-retest reliability coefficient of 0.87, demonstrating good reliability and validity.

Perth alexithymia questionnaire (PAQ)

The Perth Alexithymia Questionnaire (PAQ) is a tool developed by Australian scholar Dr. Preece and colleagues based on the Alexithymia Attention Evaluation Model [57]. It measures alexithymia by focusing on emotional valence. The scale comprises five dimensions: Negative Emotion Recognition Difficulty, Positive Emotion Recognition Difficulty, Negative Emotion Description Difficulty, Positive Emotion Description Difficulty, and generalized externally-oriented thinking. It consists of 24 items. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (7), yielding scores between 24 and 168. Higher scores indicate greater alexithymia severity. The PAQ has undergone reliability and validity testing across multiple countries and languages, demonstrating sound psychometric properties. According to the reviewed document, Qin Rongzhen et al. [57] conducted a Chinese adaptation of the PAQ for nursing students in 2024. The results showed a total Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.910, with dimension-specific Cronbach’s α coefficients ranging from 0.739 to 0.916, and a test-retest reliability coefficient of 0.905, supporting its reliability and validity in this population.

Typical cases

A comprehensive description of concepts through case studies enables a deeper grasp and recognition of their essence [20].

Nurse Zhang, female, 34 years old, married with one child, has worked in the emergency department of a tertiary hospital for 10 years. The emergency department operates on a rotating shift system (3 days of day shifts + 2 days of night shifts), with an average daily work duration of 12 h. Due to high patient volume, she frequently handles trauma and critically ill patients, resulting in high work intensity and frequent emotional stress. Recently, Nurse Zhang has noticed that when colleagues or patients express anxiety or anger toward her, she often feels “perplexed” and struggles to recognize their specific emotions (difficulties in identifying and describing emotions). This has led to frequent misunderstandings of colleagues’ criticism or patients’ complaints, causing interpersonal friction. During routine work, even when feeling tense or uneasy, Nurse Zhang rarely expresses her emotions verbally or through facial expressions, often appearing “expressionless.” She tends to focus on nursing assessment results and workflow procedures while neglecting her own internal emotional experiences (externally-Oriented Thinking Style). Over the past six months, Nurse Zhang has also developed chronic headaches, shoulder and back soreness, stomach bloating, and persistent fatigue. However, hospital physical examinations revealed no organic diseases (somatization of emotional experiences). Nurse Zhang feels she has lost enthusiasm for her work, suffers from insomnia, and has a low mood. When she observes colleagues chatting and laughing with patients daily or sharing complaints about life’s trivialities among themselves, she remains silent, feeling unable to engage in their conversations.

Conceptual framework of alexithymia in nurses

In summary, this study defines alexithymia in nurses as a contextually expressed emotional processing difficulty characterized by difficulties in identifying and describing emotions, externally oriented thinking, and somatic manifestations of emotional experience. Within nursing contexts, these features are shaped by factors such as years of service, occupational environment, emotional intelligence, social support, and psychological resilience, and may significantly affect nurses’ professional functioning and mental health. Discussion

In this study, we applied Rodgers’ conceptual analysis framework to elucidate the conceptual characteristics, causes, consequences, and related concepts of alexithymia in nurses. We further supported our understanding through representative case illustrations. The findings suggest that alexithymia in nurses should not be understood as a wholly separate concept from general alexithymia. Rather, it represents a contextually shaped expression of a broader psychological construct within the nursing profession, where emotional labor, therapeutic relationships, and repeated exposure to suffering may influence how alexithymic features are manifested and experienced. After reviewing the included literature, the defining attributes most consistently identified in nursing contexts were difficulties in identifying and describing emotions, externally-oriented thinking, and somatization of emotional experience. Huang Lei [58] and Han Yaping [59]. They discovered that adolescents with alexithymia and coronary heart disease patients share deficits in emotional description. This comparison suggests that alexithymia in nurses shares important commonalities with alexithymia in other populations, particularly in relation to difficulties in emotional awareness and expression. At the same time, the nursing context may place distinctive emphasis on empathic functioning and the bodily burden of prolonged emotional suppression, because nurses work in settings characterized by sustained interpersonal demands and repeated exposure to suffering. Accordingly, the distinctiveness of alexithymia in nurses lies less in the existence of entirely unique attributes than in the way common alexithymic features are shaped by professional role expectations and occupational stressors. For example, nurses may retain functional communication abilities in routine clinical interactions, yet still experience difficulty processing, integrating, or safely expressing emotional experiences under conditions of chronic emotional labor. Over time, this may weaken empathic responsiveness, reduce emotional attunement to patients, and contribute to somatic distress or burnout-related manifestations. The reviewed literature further indicates that the consequences of alexithymia in nurses extend beyond the individual level and affect the interpersonal and organizational domains central to nursing practice. At the individual level, alexithymia is associated with burnout, psychological distress, reduced well-being, and somatic symptoms. At the interpersonal level, nurses who have difficulty understanding and articulating their own emotions may also find it harder to recognize and respond effectively to patients’ emotional needs, which can impair empathic communication and potentially reduce patient satisfaction. At the organizational level, alexithymia may contribute to occupational stress, interpersonal conflict, reduced job satisfaction, impaired team collaboration, and increased turnover intention. These findings are consistent with the reviewed document, which emphasizes that the impact of alexithymia in nursing extends from personal mental health to nurse-patient relationships and workforce stability. In nursing—a profession characterized by high emotional labor—alexithymia may therefore be understood not merely as an individual psychological characteristic, but also as an occupationally relevant mental health concern for healthcare administrators and nursing leaders, this suggests that alexithymia should be addressed not only through individual coping approaches, but also through organizational strategies that reduce chronic emotional overload and promote emotional awareness and resilience. According to the reviewed literature, evidence-based approaches such as psychological support [60] mindfulness therapy [61] and group interventions [62]may help alleviate alexithymic tendencies. In addition, organizational measures such as rational scheduling and guaranteed rest periods may help regulate workload and reduce cumulative emotional strain.Future interventions should therefore focus on three priorities: (1) fostering a psychologically safe culture that permits emotional expression; (2) integrating emotional literacy training into continuing nursing education to strengthen emotional resilience and reflective capacity; and (3) designing interventions tailored to how alexithymic characteristics are expressed in nursing populations. At the same time, caution is warranted in interpreting the current evidence base, as much of the existing literature remains cross-sectional and descriptive, particularly in China, where prospective and explanatory studies are still limited. Future research should further clarify the boundary between general alexithymia and its contextual manifestations in nursing practice. This may be achieved through experimental, longitudinal, and qualitative studies, as well as through interdisciplinary collaboration integrating perspectives from psychology, sociology, and nursing science. Such efforts would help deepen understanding of the mechanisms through which occupational demands interact with alexithymic tendencies and would support the development of more targeted assessment and intervention strategies.
