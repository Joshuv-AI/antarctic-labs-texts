The prevalence of HPV in Chongqing, China from 2017 to 2022: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Shu Li, Xiao He, Siyue Li, Yan Su, Xiaotong Wang, Chunli Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsHuman papillomavirus (HPV) infection is widespread. The regional differences in infection rates and dominant HPV types are a significant aspect of the virus’s epidemiological characteristics. In China, these differences are further influenced by the country’s diverse ethnic composition, as well as varying economic and cultural conditions across regions. Additionally, factors such as population migration contribute to the exacerbation of these regional disparities in HPV infection rates.MethodsWe conducted an analysis of the current prevalence of HPV among 108,863 patients in Chongqing from 2017 to 2022, along with an investigation into the interaction between HPV and vaginal microbiota. Additionally, we explored the relationship between HPV clearance time and disease outcomes.ResultsOur findings revealed an overall HPV positive rate of 30.14% (32,813/108,863) in Chongqing, China. The top five HPV positive rates were as follows: HPV52 (7.78%), HPV16 (5.85%), HPV58 (4.41%), HPV53 (3.17%), and HPV39 (2.47%). The age-related trend of HPV infection exhibited a bimodal curve. Importantly, the positive rate of high-risk HPV (BV: 24.74%, AV: 24.38%, VVC: 18.19%, TV: 22.81%) was significantly higher than those in the control group (all P<0.05). Follow-up for 4 years, we observed an overall clearance rate of HPV at 92.20%, with a median clearance time of 13 (1-61) months.ConclusionThese findings hold significant implications for the Chinese government in evaluating the prevalence of HPV in the Chongqing region and formulating effective HPV prevention strategies. Moreover, these findings could contribute to the enhancement of public health initiatives related to HPV in the region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74588-x.

Introduction

Malignant tumors have become one of the leading health threats globally1. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), tumors were the primary or secondary cause of death in most countries as of 20192. In China, malignant tumors are the top cause of death, accounting for 24.09% of all resident deaths, as reported in the 2019 Chinese Cause of Death Surveillance Report3. Gynecological malignancies, which include cervical cancer, endometrial cancer, and ovarian cancer, represent 15–20% of all female cancers3,4. The incidence of these cancers is rising, with cervical cancer showing a particularly sharp increase3. Endometrial cancer is also on the rise, with both cancers increasingly affecting younger women3,4. According to the 2020 global cancer statistics, cervical cancer ranked fourth in both the number of new cases (6.5%) and deaths (7.7%) worldwide in 20205.

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a non-enveloped, small DNA virus known for its ability to disrupt the cell cycle and cause uncontrolled proliferation of squamous epithelial cells in the cervical mucosa6. It is a prevalent pathogen in the vaginal environment7. When HPV infects epithelial cells, it can persist in the nucleus or integrate into the host genome8. In its non-integrated state, HPV typically leads to low-grade lesions. However, if the virus integrates into the human genome, it can progress to high-grade lesions or cancer8. HPV infection can cause various diseases in humans7. HPV can cause not only benign diseases such as inflammation and condyloma acuminatum (CA), but it can also induce malignant tumors such as cervical cancer, oral cancer, and ovarian cancer9. The carcinogenic risk of HPV varies by type. Based on the different carcinogenic risks, HPV can be divided into high-risk HPV (HPV16, HPV18, HPV31, HPV33, HPV35, HPV39, HPV45, HPV51, HPV52, HPV56, HPV58, HPV59, HPV68, etc.), moderate-risk HPV (HPV26, HPV53, HPV66, HPV73, HPV82, etc.), and low-risk HPV (HPV6, HPV11, HPV81)10. Persistent infection with oncogenic HPV subtypes has been a critical factor in the development of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and its potential progression to cervical cancer over the past three decades11. Without treatment, approximately 25–30% of patients with CIN3 may develop invasive cancer12. Therefore, early screening for cervical cancer is crucial in preventing the progression of precancerous lesions.

China plays a significant role in the global burden of newly diagnosed tumors and tumor-related deaths5. In 2022, the country recorded 111,820 new cases of cervical cancer and 61,579 deaths from the disease5. By comparison, the United States reported 13,740 new cervical cancer cases and 5,830 related deaths5. The considerably higher numbers in China underscore a substantial tumor burden in the country. Furthermore, China’s vast territory, coupled with its diverse environmental factors and ethnic groups, may contribute to regional differences in susceptibility to various HPV types4. Understanding the prevalent HPV genotypes in specific regions and their association with tumor development is crucial for optimizing HPV vaccination strategies and improving cervical cancer screening programs. Consequently, we are planning a large-scale, long-term study to assess the prevalence of HPV in women with different cervical pathological conditions in Chongqing, the largest city in western China. This research aims to provide essential data to refine HPV-targeted vaccine strategies and enhance cervical cancer prevention efforts.

Materials and methods

Inclusion criteria and reagents

The study subjects are patients who sought medical treatment at the Chongqing Maternal and Child Health Hospital from January 1, 2017 to December 31, 2022 in Chongqing, China. Inclusion criteria: (1) No sexual activity in the 3 days prior to the examination; (2) No vaginal treatment performed. Exclusion criteria: (1) External genitalia wash within 48 h prior to the examination; (2) Vaginal use of antibiotics within the past month prior to the examination; (3) Co-existing immunological pathological changes; (4) Vaginal use of immunological-related drugs within the past year. The present study was approved by the Ethics Association of Chongqing Health Center for Women and Children (approval no. cstc2020-jyk2.0), and all patients signed the informed consent form. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

HPV nucleic acid genotyping test kit (Fluorescence PCR method) and the combined vaginitis detection kit (Dry chemistry enzyme method) were purchased from BioPerfectus Technologies in Jiangsu, China. The ABI7500 fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument and SLN-96P fully automated medical PCR analysis system were used for HPV nucleic acid detection.

Subgroup

Our study will categorize the population into 14 groups: normal control (NC), others, benign pelvic lesions (BPL), inflammation, LSIL, HSIL, ICC, CA, atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance or inability to exclude HSIL (ASC-US/-H), benign tumor (BT), malignant pelvic tumors (MPT), aciesis, abnormal pregnancy (AP), and pregnancy. CIN can be divided into three levels: LSIL corresponds to CIN1, indicating mild dysplastic changes in the lower one-third of the epithelium. HSIL includes CIN2 and CIN3. CIN2 refers to moderate dysplastic changes restricted to the lower two-thirds of the epithelium. CIN3 refers to severe dysplastic changes that extend beyond the lower 2/3 layer, even involving the full thickness of the epithelium13. BPL includes polyps, uterine leiomyoma, menstrual disorders, etc. MPT includes endometrial cancer and vulvar cancer, etc.

Specimen collection and preservation

Before collecting the sample, gently wipe off excessive secretions around the cervix using a cotton swab. Insert the cervical sampling brush into the junction of the squamous and columnar epithelium at the cervix opening, and rotate it clockwise or counterclockwise for 3–5 turns to collect cervical exfoliated cells. Place the collected cells into a sampling tube labeled with the patient’s identification number and seal it tightly for submission. After specimen collection, it should be promptly tested. Specimens stored at a temperature of 2–8 °C should be tested within 3 days, while specimens stored at -20 °C should be tested within 12 months. The specimen should be avoided from repeated freezing and thawing, with a maximum of 5 cycles. If using an ice pack for transportation, it should not exceed 3 days.

HPV genotyping

Real-time fluorescent PCR technology is used in this process. The L1 region of the human papillomavirus genome is targeted, and 21 subtype-specific primers and probes are designed. FAM, HEX, and ROX are used to label the corresponding subtypes, respectively. The probe consists of an oligonucleotide with a reporter group at the 5’ end. During PCR amplification, when the primers are intact, the fluorescence emitted by the reporter group is absorbed by the quencher group, resulting in no fluorescence signal. As the primers extend, the probe bound to the template is cleaved by Taq polymerase (5’→3’ exonuclease activity). This separation of the reporter group from the quencher group generates a fluorescent signal. A real-time fluorescent PCR instrument automatically plots a real-time amplification curve based on the detected fluorescence signals. This enables qualitative detection of HPV at the nucleic acid level. Additionally, reference genes are used to monitor and exclude false-negative results caused by factors such as instrument malfunctions, reagents, improper operation, or inhibitory substances in the specimen.

Vaginal microenvironmental imbalance and HPV

The status of vaginal microecology can be categorized into five groups: bacterial vaginosis (BV), trichomonas vaginitis (TV), vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC), aerobic vaginitis (AV), and normal control (NC). The cleanliness of vaginal discharge, Candida, and Trichomonas is evaluated by preparing wet mounts and observing them under 10 × 40 microscopy. The pH level is determined using a vaginal inflammation five-parameter detection kit. Lactobacilli, microbial diversity, and microbial density are evaluated by preparing vaginal smears, staining them with Swiss-Heimsa, and observing them under 10 × 100 microscopy. Candida is indicated by the visibility of spores and hyphae under the microscope, while Trichomonas is identified by the presence of vaginal flagellates. Cleanliness is assessed by evaluating the quantity of white blood cells, bacteria, cocci, and epithelial cells.

HPV clearance rate

The HPV clearance time is defined as the interval between the detection of HPV positivity and achieving two consecutive HPV-negative results. In this study, we included 8,642 patients who tested positive for HPV, without restriction on HPV genotypes. The follow-up period lasted for 48 months. HPV testing was conducted at least once a year during the follow-up, and participants who acquired new HPV genotypes during the follow-up period were excluded.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 25.0 statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data analysis. The HPV positivity rate was expressed as a percentage. Count data was used to calculate the number of positive and negative cases for each type of HPV. A chi-square test was performed to determine if there were statistically significant differences between the two groups. Heatmaps were used to represent the HPV positivity rates for each disease group. Heatmaps were also used to represent the HPV positivity rates for each age group. Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, Massachusetts, USA) and GraphPad Prism 6 (Boston, USA) were used for data visualization. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

The overall distribution of HPV in the population

From 2017 to 2022, a total of 108,863 patients were tested for HPV. Among them, LSIL accounted for 2.64%, HSIL accounted for 6.03%, ICC accounted for 1.47% (Fig. 1A; Table 1). The overall HPV positivity rate was 30.14% (32,813/108,863). The results revealed that ICC had the highest positive rate at 80.36%. This was followed by HSIL (78.15%) and LSIL (69.78%), and CA with a positive rate of 52.93%. Compared to the control group, there were statistically significant differences in the positive rates of ICC, LSIL, HSIL, CA, ASC-US/-H, Inflammation, and malignant pelvic tumors (all P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B; Table 1). Over the course of 5 years, the total number of HPV screenings increased annually. Compared to 2017, the positive rates of HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 45, 52, 58, 59, 6, and 11 showed a significant decrease in 2022 (all P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1).

Prevalence of HPV types in diseases

A heatmap was used to illustrate the prevalence of HPV in each disease group. It was found that among ICC, HSIL, and ASC-US/-H, HPV16 had the highest positive rates of 31.58%, 28.47%, and 9.05%, respectively, with significant statistical differences compared to the control group (all P < 0.001). In LSIL, HPV52 had the highest positive rate at 18.58%. Interestingly, during the progression from LSIL to ICC, the positive rate of HPV16 gradually increased (12.41–31.58%). Moreover, the positive rate of HPV16 in ICC is the highest among all HPV types in all groups. In CA, HPV6 (18.18%) and HPV11 (15.19%) had the highest positive rates (all P < 0.001). In other groups, HPV52 consistently had the highest positive rates, such as in malignant pelvic tumors (12.8%, P < 0.001) and aciesis (5.35%, P < 0.01). It is worth noting that HPV58, HPV33, HPV39, HPV51, HPV56, HPV53, and HPV18 also had relatively high positive rates across all groups (Fig. 2; Table 1).

HPV and cervical lesions

In LSIL, the top 8 HPV types in terms of positive rate are: HPV52, HPV16, HPV58, HPV51, HPV53, HPV18, HPV39, and HPV56 (all P < 0.001). In HSIL, the top 8 HPV types in terms of positive rate are HPV16, HPV52, HPV58, HPV33, HPV53, HPV39, HPV51, and HPV18 (all P < 0.001). In ICC, the top 8 HPV types in terms of positive rate are: HPV16, HPV52, HPV53, HPV58, HPV18, HPV39, HPV33, and HPV56 (all P < 0.001). In malignant pelvic tumors, the top 5 HPV types in terms of positive rate are: HPV52, HPV16, HPV81, HPV53, and HPV66 (all P < 0.001). It is noteworthy that HPV81, considered a low-risk HPV, shows a relatively high positive rate in LSIL, HSIL, ICC, and malignant pelvic tumors, with rates of 4.04%, 3.28%, 2.94%, and 5.85%, respectively. Additionally, HPV showed slightly different trends in the prevalence of malignant pelvic tumors, HPV81 (5.85%), HPV66 (3.95%), and HPV59 (2.84%) exhibit higher positive rates (Fig. 2; Table 1).

The median age of patients is 34 years, with ages ranging from 1 to 89 years. The patients are divided into 6 age groups: ≤19 years, 20–29 years, 30–39 years, 40–49 years, 50–59 years, and ≥ 60 years. A heatmap is used to show the percentage of patients in each age group for each disease. In several diseases, the age group with the highest number of patients is 30–39 years old. However, in ICC, the age group with the highest number of patients is 40–49 years (35.46%). In CA, the age group with the highest number of patients is 20–29 years (44.65%) (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 2).

HPV and vaginal microenvironmental imbalance

From 2017 to 2022, a total of 7,996 patients underwent both vaginal microbiota testing and HPV DNA testing. Based on the results of the patients’ vaginal microbiota, they were divided into five groups: BV, AV, VVC, TV, and NC. In these groups, the overall HPV positivity rates (BV: 29.67%, AV: 29.28%, VVC: 21.68%, TV: 26.32%) were significantly higher than those in the control group (all P < 0.05). Similarly, the positive rates of high-risk HPV (BV: 24.74%, AV: 24.38%, VVC: 18.19%, TV: 22.81%) were also significantly higher than those in the control group (all P < 0.05). However, the positive rates of moderate-risk HPV and low-risk HPV were only significantly higher than those in the control group in BV (moderate-risk HPV positive: 6.42%, low-risk HPV positive: 4.05%) and AV (moderate-risk HPV positive: 6.16%, low-risk HPV positive: 3.98%) (all P < 0.05). The positive rate of HPV58 (10.53%) in TV is the highest among all HPV genotypes in patients with vaginal microbiota imbalance. HPV16, HPV52, and HPV58 showed relatively high positive rates in BV, AV, VVC, and TV. However, compared to the control group, HPV16 showed statistically significant differences in all four groups (all P < 0.01). HPV52 showed statistically significant differences compared to the control group only in BV and AV (all P < 0.01), and HPV58 showed statistically significant differences compared to the control group only in AV and TV (all P < 0.05). There were no statistically significant differences in the positive rates of nine HPV genotypes (HPV18, HPV31, HPV68, HPV26, HPV66, HPV73, HPV82, HPV6, and HPV81) among the four vaginal microenvironmental imbalances compared to the control group (Fig. 5; Table 3).

Clearance rate of HPV genotype

In this follow-up study of 8,642 HPV-positive patients, we excluded those who experienced vaccine failure or acquired other HPV types during the follow-up process. To avoid the impact of surgical treatment on the analysis results, we excluded patients with a history of surgical treatment. A total of 1936 HPV-positive patients were included. Among the 1936 HPV-positive patients, HPV52 (442 / 1936) and HPV16 (365 / 1936) were the most prevalent types. After a 48-month follow-up, the overall HPV clearance rate was 92.20% (1785 / 1936), with a median clearance time of 13(1–61) months. After analyzing the clearance rates of high-risk, moderate-risk, and low-risk HPV types, we found no statistical differences among the three groups. In addition, in the five most prevalent HPV types among the 1936 HPV-positive follow-up patients, included four high-risk HPV types (HPV52 : 84.16%, HPV16 : 85.21%, HPV58 : 95.05%, HPV39 : 94.81%) and one moderate-risk HPV type (HPV53 : 90.96%) (Table 5).

Discussion

Annually, approximately 19.3 million people worldwide are diagnosed with cancers related to HPV14. Among them, around 604,127 individuals are identified with cervical cancer, and roughly 341,831 people succumb to the disease5. HPV infection is the primary cause of most cervical cancers15–17. Consequently, cervical cancer is currently the only cancer with a well-established cause that can be effectively prevented, detected early, treated, and potentially eradicated9,18,19.

In the case of HPV, there are geographical variations in infection rates and dominant types, which is one of its main epidemiological characteristics20. Additionally, the diverse ethnic population, economic and cultural differences in different regions, as well as population migration, which further contribute to the geographic features of HPV infection in China10. Lifang et al.21studied the HPV infection rate in different regions of China through meta-analysis and found that there were significant regional differences in HPV infection (P < 0.05). The highest infection rate was observed in Central China (19.00%), followed by Southwest China (15.67%), and the lowest infection rate was found in Northwest China (9.96%)21. The epidemiological characteristics of HPV infection varied significantly among different regions. The most commonly detected high-risk HPV types varied as well. In Central China, the most frequently detected types were HPV16/52/58/33/53/56, while in Southwest China, they were HPV52/16/58/68/39/1821. Considering these regional differences, we conducted a study on the prevalence of HPV in Chongqing, China, which is located in Southwest China. Our findings differ from those of Lifang’s study. We found an overall HPV positivity rate of 30.14% (32,813/108,863), significantly higher than the 15.67% reported by Lifang. This discrepancy may be attributed to the smaller sample size in Lifang’s study (63,242 cases) compared to ours, which could lead to a falsely low estimate. Additionally, the vast territory and diverse ethnic groups in Southwestern China might contribute to variations in HPV positivity rates.In our study, the top five HPV types in terms of positivity rate were HPV52, HPV16, HPV58, HPV53, and HPV39. Notably, although HPV81 is classified as a low-risk type, its overall HPV positivity rate is high (2.40%), and the top five diseases associated with HPV81 are MPT (5.85%, P < 0.05), CA (4.49%, P < 0.05), LSIL (4.04%, P < 0.05), HSIL (3.28%, P < 0.05), and ICC (2.94%, P > 0.05). Li et al.22 reported a positivity rate of 0.33% for HPV81 in Guangdong Province, China, while Yuping et al.23 found a positivity rate of 0.16% for HPV81 in Jiangxi Province, China. However, our findings suggest that the positivity rate of HPV81 in Chongqing is significantly higher than in these other regions. After HPV81 infection, it primarily leads to chronic cervicitis. In a small percentage of cases, it may progress to LSIL, with an even lower likelihood of progressing to HSIL, and rarely leads to the development of cervical cancer.

Some studies have shown a bimodal age-related trend in HPV infection24–26. The first peak occurs among young individuals, mostly below 25 years of age25. Following this, the age curve of HPV infection tends to flatten out but gradually increases after age 41, eventually reaching a second peak26. The timing of this second peak varies across different geographical areas, ranging from 45 to 65 years24–26. In this study, we found that the highest HPV infection positivity rate was among individuals aged ≤ 19 (59.74%). Among females aged 30–39, the HPV infection rate remained relatively low (25.81%). From age 40 onwards, the positivity rate gradually increased, reaching a second peak among those aged ≥ 60 (49.38%). When analyzing different diseases by age group, we observed that most conditions were concentrated in the 30–39 age group, while ICC and BT were more prevalent among those aged 40–49, and CA was concentrated in the 20–29 age group.

HPV-related carcinogenesis is a complex process involving multiple factors and steps27. HPV E6 and E7 play a critical role in the formation and progression of cervical cancer27,28. During the HPV integration process, the abnormal activation of the E6 and E7 genes enhances the carcinogenic potential of cells29,30. Numerous studies have demonstrated a close relationship between HPV16 infection and the development of HSIL and cervical cancer, with approximately 70% of cervical cancers attributed to infections with HPV16 and HPV1831–33. However, our findings present a slightly different picture. In Chongqing, China, the positivity rate of high-risk HPV in cervical cancer cases reached 75.11%. The highest positivity rate was observed for HPV16 (31.58%), followed by HPV52 (17.01%), HPV53 (11.51%), and HPV58 (8.88%). In HSIL cases, the positivity rate for high-risk HPV was 56.61%. These results suggest that the distribution of HPV genotypes in Chongqing may differ from that in other regions. In addition to the high prevalence of HPV16, there is also a significant prevalence of HPV52, HPV58, HPV53, HPV33, HPV51, and HPV39 in Chongqing. The widely used nine-valent HPV vaccine covers a broad range of HPV types, but it does not include HPV53, HPV51, and HPV39. Notably, in cervical cancer (ICC) cases, HPV53, HPV51, and HPV39 rank third, ninth, and sixth in terms of positivity rates, respectively. In HSIL cases, these three HPV types rank fifth, seventh, and fifth, respectively. This finding underscores the potential need for the development of vaccines that provide coverage for HPV53, HPV51, and HPV39, given their prevalence in this region.

Healthy women harbor a diverse array of microorganisms in the vaginal environment that coexist, proliferate, and restrain each other, collectively forming the vaginal microbiota34. The normal vaginal microenvironment consists of various microorganisms, including aerobic bacteria, facultative anaerobic bacteria, and obligate anaerobic bacteria, with Lactobacillus being the dominant genus35. Lactobacilli produce acidic substances, such as lactic acid and fatty acids, which help maintain the vagina’s acidic environment and preserve the balance of the vaginal microenvironment36. This microbiota plays a crucial role in maintaining the normal morphology of cervical cells37. Disruptions in the vaginal microenvironment, particularly in cases of bacterial vaginosis, vulvovaginal candidiasis, and mixed infections, have been linked to persistent HPV infections37. The possible mechanism is that the overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria reduces the number of lactobacilli, resulting in an elevated vaginal pH, prolongs the metaplasia time of squamous epithelial cells, and promotes epithelial cell dysplasia38. Additionally, the metabolites such as propionic acid and butyric acid causes epithelial cell damage39. This series of reactions create a favorable environment for the infection of pathogenic factors such as HPV and contributing to the development of squamous intraepithelial lesions in the cervix and vagina40. Research also suggests that an imbalanced vaginal microbiota can disrupt the inherent immune defense mechanisms of the vaginal environment, increasing HPV’s abnormal adhesion and further damaging epithelial cells40. In our study, we recruited patients to detect HPV-DNA and evaluate their vaginal microbial status. The results showed that compared to the control group, patients with microbial imbalances had significantly higher rates of total HPV positivity and high-risk HPV positivity, indicating that microbial imbalance may contribute to the persistence of HPV infection. In cases of BV, AV, and VVC, HPV52 had the highest positivity rate, followed by HPV16, while in TV, HPV58 had the highest positivity rate. This suggests that microbial imbalances may not lead to the persistence of all HPV types and that different types of microbial imbalances may result in different HPV prevalence patterns. Further changes in cellular and microenvironmental processes, including disruption of the extracellular matrix (ECM), are necessary for tumor establishment and progression41. Alterations in the ECM can affect the tissue microenvironment of HPV infection, promote the recruitment of inflammatory factors such as IL-1 and IL-6, and ultimately contribute to disease progression41,42. Consequently, we investigated the cervical disease status of patients with normal and abnormal vaginal microbiota. We found that in patients with HSIL and ICC, the total HPV positivity rate and high-risk HPV positivity rate were significantly higher in those with microbial imbalances compared to those with normal vaginal microbiota. This phenomenon was not observed in LSIL patients, suggesting that microbial imbalances may interact with HPV and collectively promote the occurrence and progression of cervical malignancy.

HPV infection is common, but only a small percentage of individuals infected with high-risk HPV ultimately develop invasive cancer43. In most cases, HPV can be cleared by the immune system43. However, HPV has developed mechanisms to evade the host immune surveillance to complete its lifecycle and replication, increasing the chance of long-term infection and cellular transformation44. Persistent infection with high-risk HPV has been identified as a major cause of HSIL and cervical cancer44. Previous studies have shown that the 5-year clearance rate of HPV is 89.94%, with a median clearance time of 16 (9–31) months45. In our study, we found that after a four-year follow-up, the overall clearance rate of HPV was 92.20%, with a median clearance time of 13 (1–61) months, and the clearance time for most HPV types exceeded 90%.Significantly, the clearance rates of HPV16, HPV18, and HPV52, which are highly associated with the risk of cervical cancer, are low. This indicates that these high-risk HPV types are more difficult to clear by the body after infection, thereby increasing the risk of developing cervical cancer. Therefore, it is necessary to further increase public awareness of the prevention and treatment of these high-risk HPV.

In conclusion, our research has yielded some compelling insights into the prevalence of HPV in Chongqing, China. The HPV infection rate in this region is relatively high at 30.14%, with the infection exhibiting a bimodal age-related trend. Both the overall HPV positivity rates and the positivity rates for high-risk HPV types were significantly higher compared to the control group. Although the HPV infection rate in Chongqing is notably high, the majority of infections are likely to be cleared spontaneously. However, our study does have certain limitations. First, it is a retrospective study conducted within a single center, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, we did not explore the relationship between certain behaviors (such as smoking, sexual behavior, and dietary habits) and HPV prevalence, due to a lack of relevant data. Lastly, while our data includes HPV prevalence among some pregnant women, we lack information on pregnancy outcomes, and thus did not investigate the relationship between HPV prevalence and pregnancy outcomes. Addressing these gaps will be the focus of our future research.
