Effect of ambient temperature on dietary energy partitioning and utilization in broilers as measured by closed-circuit indirect calorimetry system
Authors: Hansuo Liu, Changlin Guo, Xiaomeng Ye, Jingjing Xie, Yuming Wang, Bing Lei, Feng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of ambient temperatures on growth performance and energy partitioning in broilers using a computer controlled closed-circuit indirect calorimetry system. Sixty 21-day-old male Arbor Acres broilers (1,004 ± 11 g BW) were randomly allocated to four ambient temperature treatments (23°C, 25°C, 27°C, 29°C), with five indirect calorimetry chambers per treatments and three broilers per chamber. Broilers were fed the same test diet, and growth performance, gas exchange, dietary energy partitioning, and substrate oxidation were determined from d 25 to 28. Increasing ambient temperature from 23°C to 29°C resulted in a linear increase (P ≤ 0.036) in BW, metabolic BW, and ADG, whereas ADFI/ADG decreased linearly (P = 0.019). Daily O2 consumption and CO2 production also decreased linearly with increasing ambient temperature (P ≤ 0.003). Compared with broilers maintained at 23°C, those maintained at 29°C exhibited lower heat increment (HI) and HI/AME (P ≤ 0.017), but greater net energy (NE), retained energy (RE), NE/GE, NE/AME and RE/AME (P ≤ 0.047). Regression models incorporating NE or RE intake, together with CP and metabolizable CP (MCP) intake, produced higher R² for predicting ADG than the corresponding model based on AME, CP, and MCP intake. Ambient temperature had no significant effects on substrate oxidation or the contribution of carbohydrate, fat, and protein oxidation to total heat production. In conclusion, increasing ambient temperatures within the tested range improved dietary energy utilization efficiency, as reflected by higher NE and RE, lower HI, and a linear decrease in ADFI/ADG. The superior predictive performance of NE- and RE-based models further supports the use of NE and RE as more physiologically relevant indicators of dietary energy value than AME. These results demonstrate that closed-circuit indirect calorimetry is an effective approach for evaluating dietary energy partitioning under different thermal environments.

Introduction

Net energy (NE) is widely regarded as the most accurate estimate of available energy in feed for broilers (Liu et al., 2017; Noblet et al., 2010). It was calculated by subtracting heat increment (HI), defined as the difference between total heat production (THP) and fasting heat production (FHP) from AME (Zuidhof, 2019). Thus, fluctuations in HI may have a considerable impact on the overall efficiency of dietary energy utilization in broilers.

Previous study has shown that HI is influenced by ambient temperature when broilers were fed the same diet (Morillo et al., 2023). However, fluctuations in ambient temperature may affect THP, primarily by altering the energy required for thermoregulation, which constitutes a major component of the NE requirement for maintenance (Koh and Macleod, 1999a). Broilers expend energy for thermogenesis at low ambient temperature and for evaporative heat loss under hot conditions (Hossain et al., 2026; Pishnamazi et al., 2015). Consequently, broilers maintained outside their thermoneutral zone must adjust their metabolic activity to either increase THP or enhance heat dissipation. These thermoregulatory responses affect the efficiencies of energy utilization for protein and fat deposition, thereby altering body composition. Sakomura et al. (2005) reported that broilers reared at ambient temperatures of 13, 23, and 32°C from 25 to 28 days of age exhibited marked differences in body protein deposition (3.66, 2.53, and 2.62 g, respectively) and fat deposition (1.52, 2.32, and 1.52 g, respectively). These differences were attributed to increased feed intake and greater THP at low temperatures, whereas higher temperature reduced energy intake and THP, thereby changing the partitioning of dietary energy between THP and body energy retention (Belhadj Slimen et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2022; Sakomura et al., 2005). Consequently, ambient temperature plays a critical role in influencing energy loss as heat and dietary energy utilization in broilers (Liu et al., 2022; Reyes et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2024). However, quantitative information describing how ambient temperature affects the partitioning of dietary energy into THP, HI, NE, and retained energy (RE) remains limited.

The indirect calorimetry technique provides a reliable approach for quantifying THP by simultaneously measuring oxygen (O2) consumption and carbon dioxide (CO2) production. These measurements allow the partitioning of AME into THP, HI, NE, and RE as protein (REp) or fat (REf) to be determined (Barzegar et al., 2020; Gerrits and Labussière, 2015). By controlling ambient temperature within the indirect calorimetry chamber, differences in THP and the partitioning of AME can be evaluated under different thermal conditions. Therefore, this study used indirect calorimetry to investigate the effects of four ambient temperatures (23, 25, 27, and 29°C) on the growth performance, O2 consumption, CO2 production, dietary energy partitioning, and energy utilization efficiency in broilers. The results will improve our understanding of how ambient temperature influences energy utilization and energy partitioning in broilers.

Materials and methods

All experimental procedures related to the use of live broilers were approved by the animal care and welfare committee of the Institute of Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Beijing, China). The code of ethical inspection was IAS 2024-61.

Experimental design

A total of sixty 21-day-old male Arbor Acres (AA) broilers with similar BW (1,004 ± 11 g) were selected and randomly allocated to four environmental temperature treatments (23, 25, 27 and 29°C), with five closed-circuit indirect calorimetry chambers per treatment to compare the dietary energy partitioning differences in broilers under varying temperature conditions. Each chamber contained one wire-mesh metabolic cage (70.5 cm length × 58.0 cm width × 50.0 cm height) equipped with an individual feeder and drinker. Each cage housed three broilers and served as one experimental unit.

Broilers management and experimental diets

The broilers were fed a starter diet from 0 to 21 days of age, and an experimental diet from 22 to 28 days of age, with free access to water. Broilers were acclimated to the calorimetry chambers from 21 to 24 days of age and were evaluated from 25 to 28 days of age at a stocking density of 7.36 kg/m² based on initial BW of broilers. Relative humidity and ventilation were managed according to the guidelines for Arbor Acres broilers (Aviagen, 2018). Experimental diets were formulated to meet or exceed nutrient recommendations for broilers in China (MOA, 2004) (Table 1). Measurements of O2 consumption and CO2 production

The O2 consumption and CO2 production of broilers were measured using an automatic closed-circuit indirect calorimetry system (Model CRS-1), developed by the Institute of Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Beijing, China). The design, operating principles, and validation of this system were described by Zhao et al. (2022) and Liu et al. (2025). The indirect calorimetry system consisted of four subsystems: (1) an animal chamber system; (2) an environmental control system for regulating chamber temperature, humidity, and airflow; (3) an O2 supply and consumption quantification system; and (4) an air filtration and CO2 absorption system to purify incoming air and remove CO₂ from exhaust air. The procedure for quantifying O2 consumption and CO2 production was as follows: a 1-L bottle containing 0.70% (vol/vol) sulfuric acid solution, two 6-L bottles each containing 350 g/kg KOH solution, and a 1-L bottle of deionized water were connected in sequence by silicon tubing. The bottles were placed in a stainless-steel box (49.5 cm length × 43.5 cm width × 49.5 cm height) with a sealed door, which was connected to the air inlet and outlet of the box. Subsequently, an automatic airtightness self-test was initiated using the control software (Copyright 2024SR0019052).

The airtightness of each respiration chamber was verified before the experiment using a pressure decay test. Each chamber was connected to a high-pressure oxygen cylinder (≥ 10 MPa) and pressurized with oxygen at 0.05 MPa. Chamber pressure was continuously monitored by a pressure sensor connected to the closed-circuit indirect calorimetry system. When the pressure differential between the chamber and ambient air reached 300 Pa, the oxygen supply was automatically shut off. The pressure differential was then recorded 3 min after valve closure (A) and again 30 min later (B). Chamber airtightness was considered acceptable when the pressure decrease over the 30-min period did not exceed 50 Pa (i.e., B ≥ A − 50 Pa). Under this condition, the system automatically passed the self-test; otherwise, the test failed and an alarm was triggered.

After the airtightness test, broilers were transferred to the chamber and provided ad libitum access to diet and water during a 3-day adaptation period. Throughout this period, the chamber door remained open while the environmental temperature was maintained at the preset level (23, 25, 27, or 29°C). Air was continuously circulated through each chamber by a computer-controlled air pump and solenoid valves without passing through the air filtration and CO2 absorption system.

Experimental parameters, including chamber temperature, relative humidity, O2 concentration, differential pressure, feeding schedule, and test duration, were preset in the control software. Before each run, the feeder was emptied, refilled with sufficient test diet for the 3-day experimental period, and weighed. The chamber door was then closed, and the bioassay was initiated through the control software. During the experiment, the closed-circuit indirect calorimetry system automatically monitored chamber temperature, relative humidity, O2 concentration, CO2 concentration, differential pressure in the chamber, BW, and O2 consumption of the broilers. Oxygen consumption was calculated as the difference between the weight of the oxygen cylinder at the beginning and end of each run. Oxygen consumption (g) was converted to volume (L) using an O₂ density of 1.331 g/L under standard conditions (20°C and 101.325 kN/m2). All data were automatically recorded by the control software and used to calculate average BW, O₂ consumption, and ADG.

Upon completion of each bioassay, the residual feed was automatically discharged from the feeder by opening the feeder base plate. The air filtration and CO2 absorption system were then deactivated, and the solenoid valve connected to the respiration chamber was opened to allow atmospheric air exchange. All excreta were collected into aluminum foil boxes and stored at −20°C for subsequent analysis. After three consecutive 24 h bioassays had been completed in each chamber, the CO2-absorbing KOH solution was weighed and sampled to determine the total amount of CO2 produced during the test period. The amount of CO2 absorbed in the KOH solution was quantified using BaCl2 precipitation method as described by Wu et al. (2019). The density of CO2 at standard conditions (1.842 g/L, at 20°C and 101.325 kN/m2) was used to convert CO₂ weight (g) to volume (L).

Determination of AME, THP, and NE

The dietary AME was determined based on the total feed intake and total excreta collected from broilers aged 25 to 28 days over three consecutive 24 h calorimetry bioassays. Excreta from each experimental unit were pooled, dried in a forced-air oven at 65°C for 72 h, and analyzed for chemical composition and gross energy (GE). The O2 consumption and CO2 production were determined using computer-controlled closed-circuit indirect calorimetry throughout the three consecutive 24 h bioassays. The THP was calculated based on the volume of O2 consumed and CO2 produced. The respiratory quotient was calculated as the ratio of the volume of CO2 production to the volume of O2 consumption. The HI was calculated as the difference between THP and FHP. The dietary NE content (kcal/kg DM) was calculated by subtracting HI from AME.

Chemical analysis

Feed and excreta samples were finely ground using a laboratory mill (model BJ-150, Deqing Baijie Electrical Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China) and passed through a 0.42 mm mesh screen prior to chemical analysis. The DM content was determined according to the AOAC (2007) method 925.10. GE was measured by adiabatic calorimeter (Parr 6400, Parr Instrument Co., Moline, IL), with benzoic acid as the calibration standard. The CP content was determined by the AOAC (2007) method, using a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (model KDY-9820, Shandong Haineng Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Dezhou, China). The acid detergent fiber was determined by AOAC (2005) method 973.18. Uric acid content in excreta was determined according to the method of Marquardt (1983).

Calculation and statistical analysis

Dietary AME (kcal/kg DM) = (energy intake – energy output) / feed intake

THP (kcal) = 3.866 × O2 consumed (L) + 1.200 × CO2 exhaled (L) (Brouwer, 1965)

FHP (kcal/kg BW0.70 per day) = 107.55 (Noblet et al., 2015)

HI (kcal/kg DM) = (THP – FHP × BW0.70 × day) / feed intake in which THP is in kilocalories, O2 is oxygen consumption in liters, CO2 is carbon dioxide production in liters. A 107.55 kcal/kg BW0.70 per day for broilers reported by Noblet et al. (2015) to calculate the FHP.

NE (kcal/kg DM) = AME − HI

RE (kcal/kg DM) = AME – THP / FI

Retained nitrogen (RN) = (CP intake − CP excretion)/6.25 where CP intake (g/d) is the daily CP intake from the diet, and CP excretion (g/d) is the total of CP excreted in feces and urine. CP content in feed, feces and urine was determined by the Kjeldahl method (nitrogen content × 6.25).

RE as protein (REp, kcal/kg DM) = RN × 6.25 × 5.70

RE as fat (REf, kcal/kg DM) = RE – REp

Where RE is retained energy; REp is RE as protein; REf is RE as fat; 6.25 is the protein equivalent of 1 g nitrogen, and 5.70 is the energy equivalent of 1 g protein (kcal), as reported by Sharma et al. (2021).

Net substrate oxidation (g) was calculated from gas exchange measurements (L) and urinary nitrogen excretion (g) according to the equations described by Brouwer (1957, 1958).

Urinary nitrogen (g) = excreta (g) × uric acid (%) × 33.33%

Protein oxidation (g) = 6.25 × urinary nitrogen (g)

Carbohydrate oxidation (g) = – 2.965 × O2 (L) + 4.170 × CO2 (L) – 2.438 × urinary nitrogen (g)

Fat oxidation (g) = 1.718 × O2 (L) – 1.718 × CO2 (L) – 1.969 × urinary nitrogen (g)

Heat production from substrate oxidation was calculated using energy equivalents of 4.40, 9.50, and 4.19 kcal/g for protein, fat, and carbohydrate, respectively, as described by Gerrits and Labussière (2015).

The experimental unit was the group of broilers housed within each calorimetry chamber. Descriptive statistics for chamber environment variables (temperature, humidity, O2 concentration, and CO2 concentration), growth performance (BW and ADG), energy partition (daily O2 consumption, daily CO2 production, AME, THP, HI, RE, REp, and REf), and energy utilization indices (AME/GE, NE/GE, THP/AME, HI/AME, NE/AME, RE/AME, REp/AME, and REf/AME) were generated using JMP Pro 19.1 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Data were analyzed by ANOVA using the Fit Model platform in JMP Pro 19.1. BW was included as a covariate to adjust the daily O2 consumption and CO2 production. The least squares means were compared using the Tukey honestly significant difference test. Polynomial orthogonal contrasts were applied to evaluate linear and quadratic responses to increasing ambient temperature during respiration calorimetry on all measured variables. Linear regression models were developed using the Fit Model platform in JMP Pro 19.1 to evaluate the relationships of ADG with AME, NE, or RE intake, together with CP and metabolizable CP (MCP) intake, and to evaluate the relationships of THP, HI, NE, RE, and REf with ambient temperature. The significance level was set at P < 0.05, whereas 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10 was considered a trend.

The growth curve of broilers maintained at 25°C was consistently lower than those of broilers maintained at 27°C and 29°C groups, whereas broilers maintained at 23°C exhibited the lowest growth trajectory throughout the experimental period. Discussion

Accurate measurement of O₂ consumption, CO₂ production, and THP by indirect calorimetry depends on control of the chamber environment, particularly temperature, relative humidity, and gas concentrations (O₂ and CO₂). Previous research has demonstrated that ambient temperature markedly influences the logarithmic value of THP, highlighting the importance of maintaining stable environmental conditions during calorimetric measurements (Brown-Brandl et al., 2004). In the present study, the actual chamber temperature closely matched the preset values throughout the 72-h measurement period, with a maximum deviation of only 0.15°C. This level of precision is particularly important in broiler studies because minor changes in ambient temperature can influence feed-to-gain ratio (Bottje and Harrison, 1985). Relative humidity was also maintained within a narrow range (48.00%−52.17%) across treatments. Excessive humidity, particularly when combined with ambient temperature, has been reported to impair feed intake, carbohydrate metabolism, protein synthesis, fat deposition, and oxidative status (Kpomasse et al., 2021). The stable humidity observed in the present study reflects the ability of the closed-circuit indirect calorimetry system to automatically regulate both temperature and humidity, thereby avoiding environmental conditions that could compromise calorimetric measurements. Stable regulation of temperature and humidity also ensured reliable measurement of respiratory gases. Throughout the experiment, O2 concentration remained constant at 20.84% to 20.89%, minimizing potential errors associated with changes in chamber during closed-circuit operation. Furthermore, the CO₂ concentration remained below 3,614 ppm, well below the 5,000 ppm limit recommended by the healthy farming technical specification for multi-layer caged white broiler (T/SDAA 0041-2021). Collectively, these findings indicate that this closed-circuit indirect calorimetry system provided a stable and well-controlled environmental suitable for accurately evaluating energy metabolism in broilers.

Accurate BW measurements are essential in indirect calorimetry because O2 consumption and CO2 production are commonly expressed relative to per kg of MBW when comparing energy metabolism among treatments (Ning et al., 2022; Tay-Zar et al., 2024). Moreover, FHP is required for NE calculations, is estimated from MBW using the value of 450 kJ/kg BW0.70 proposed by Noblet et al. (2015). Consequently, abnormal growth during indirect calorimetry may bias measurements of O2 consumption, CO2 production, THP, and NE. Previous respiratory calorimetry studies have generally reported only the initial and final BW of broilers (Tay-Zar et al., 2024), whereas continuous BW changes during the measurement period have rarely been monitored. In the current study, real-time BW was continuously recorded throughout the 72-h calorimetry period, allowing automatic calculation of O2 consumption per kg of MBW. The resulting growth curves showed that broilers maintained at 27°C and 29°C exhibited similar growth trajectories, whereas those maintained at 25°C grew more slowly and those at 23°C showed the lowest growth trajectory. Accordingly, BW, MBW, and ADG increased linearly, whereas ADFI/ADG decreased linearly from days 25 to 28 as ambient temperature increased from 23°C to 29°C. Ambient temperature directly influences heat exchange between the broiler and the environment, thereby affecting nutrient utilization and growth performance (Kim et al., 2021). The improved growth performance observed at higher ambient temperatures in the present study likely reflects a reduction in the energy required for thermoregulation, allowing a greater proportion of dietary energy to be directed toward tissue deposition and body weight gain.

Respiratory calorimetry quantifies substrate oxidation by measuring O2 consumption and CO2 production, making these variables, together with the respiratory quotient, reliable indicators of nutrient utilization in broilers. Both ambient temperature and feed intake have been shown to influence daily THP (Apalowo et al., 2024; Yunianto et al., 1997). Previous study has reported that increased feed intake and lower ambient temperature are associated with greater daily THP (Koh and Macleod, 1999b). In the present study, broilers maintained at 23°C exhibited greater daily O2 consumption per broiler, daily O2 consumption per kg of MBW, daily CO2 production per broiler, and daily CO2 production per kg of MBW than those maintained at 29°C. These findings indicate that decreasing ambient temperature increased metabolic activity and oxygen demand, reflecting greater energy expenditure for thermoregulation. Consequently, more dietary energy was dissipated as heat at lower temperatures, which is consistent with the greater THP and HI observed in the 23°C group. This response agrees with the established concept that broilers increase metabolic heat production under cooler conditions to maintain body temperature (Pishnamazi et al., 2015).

Energy utilization efficiency in broiler diet is determined by the partitioning of ME between THP and energy retention. Ambient temperature influences this partitioning by altering metabolic rate and the allocation of energy to protein and fat deposition (Sakomura et al., 2005). Previous study reported that the ME required for fat and protein deposition differs with ambient temperature, indicating that the efficiency of utilizing dietary energy for growth is temperature dependent (Sakomura et al., 2005). In the present study, broilers maintained at 23°C exhibited greater HI and THP but lower RE than those maintained at 29°C, resulting in a lower RE/AME. These findings indicate that a greater proportion of dietary ME was dissipated as heat under cooler conditions, whereas less energy was retained for tissue deposition. In contrast, the lower thermoregulatory demand at 29°C group reduced HI and favored energy retention, particularly as fat deposition, leading to greater RE and RE/AME. These findings are consistent with those of Zuidhof (2019), who reported that ambient temperature is a major determinant of the proportion of dietary energy retained in body tissues, and with Sakomura et al. (2005), who demonstrated that the energy cost of tissue deposition varies with ambient temperature. Furthermore, regression analyses showed that replacing AME intake with either NE intake or RE intake in models including CP and MCP intake progressively increased the R² for predicting ADG. This finding suggests that NE or RE more accurately represent the biologically available energy supporting growth than AME. Together with the observed changes in energy partitioning across ambient temperatures, these results demonstrate that the closed-circuit indirect calorimetry system provides a robust approach for evaluating dietary energy utilization in broilers under different thermal environments and further support the use of NE and RE as more physiologically relevant indicators of dietary energy value than AME.

The oxidation of carbohydrates, fat, and protein provides the energy released as THP in farm animals (Chwalibog, 2002). Although indirect calorimetry has been widely used to estimate the THP from respiratory gas exchange, relatively few studies have applied this approach to quantify individual substrate oxidation in broilers (Chwalibog, 2004; Liu et al., 2025). In the present study, substrate oxidation was estimated from O₂ consumption, CO₂ production, and uric acid nitrogen excretion, allowing the contributions of carbohydrate, fat, and protein oxidation to THP to be evaluated. The results showed that ambient temperature did not significantly affect uric acid nitrogen excretion, oxidation of carbohydrates, fat, and protein, or the contribution of individual substrates to THP. Carbohydrate oxidation accounted for the largest proportion of THP, followed by protein oxidation, whereas calculated fat oxidation was negative across all treatments. These findings agree with those of Chwalibog (2004), who reported that carbohydrate oxidation is the primary contributor to heat production in broilers and that negative fat oxidation values are commonly observed when carbohydrate is converted into lipid. Thus, the negative fat oxidation values observed in the present study likely reflect net lipogenesis rather than fat catabolism.

Conclusion

In summary, increasing ambient temperature from 23 to 29°C reduced THP and HI while increasing NE and RE values, thereby improving dietary energy utilization efficiency in broilers. The superior performance of NE- and RE-based models for predicting growth further supports the use of NE and RE as more physiologically relevant indicators of dietary energy value than AME. These results demonstrate that the closed-circuit indirect calorimetry system provides a reliable approach for evaluating dietary energy partitioning under different thermal environments and offers a useful tool for improving dietary energy evaluation and environmental management in broiler production.
