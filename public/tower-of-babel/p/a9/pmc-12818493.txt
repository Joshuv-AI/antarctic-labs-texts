Effects of Aircraft Noise on Pilots’ Auditory Perception
Authors: LingLing Song, MengMeng Liu, ShunShun Li, YinZi Piao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to investigate the effects of aircraft noise on pilots’ auditory perception and develop evidence-based control strategies.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted between March 2021 and May 2024. Participants were divided into two main groups: a pilot group (n = 92) and a control group of 67 healthy individuals without occupational noise exposure. For subgroup analyses, the pilot group was further categorised into three subgroups according to aircraft type: helicopter (n = 28), fighter (n = 30) and passenger aircraft (n = 34). The Results section presents comparisons across four groups in total (control + three pilot subgroups). Hearing test data were used to evaluate the effects of aircraft noise on auditory perception and propose effective prevention and control strategies.Results:The control group included more females than the pilot group (P < 0.05). No significant difference in baseline data was observed among the three groups (P > 0.05). Significant differences were detected in the pure tone audiometry results of 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 4 kHz among the four groups (P < 0.001). The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the 2–6 kHz band was significantly lower in fighter jet crews compared with controls (median difference range: 2.0–9.0 dB), with the largest difference at 6 kHz (Δ9.0 dB, χ2 = 91.37, P < 0.001). Helicopter crews also observed SNR reductions of approximately 5.5 dB at 4 and 6 kHz (P < 0.001). Only a slight decrease in the 2 kHz threshold was observed for the passenger aircraft crew (Δ0.5 dB, P = 0.002). We found no significant difference between the groups at 1 kHz (P = 0.794). Significant differences were noted in the Weinstein Noise Sensitivity Scale scores of the four groups (P < 0.001).Conclusion:Aircraft noise threatens pilots’ auditory health and flight safety, requiring integrated technical, protective and managerial control measures.

INTRODUCTION

Noise levels in the aviation industry usually exceed the safe limit. The sources of noise include aircraft engines and ground service equipment, which pose a crucial risk to pilots, aircrew, maintenance engineers and ground crew.[1] Studies have shown that the noise level in fighter cockpits can reach approximately 110 dBA.[2] Prolonged exposure to noise in fighter cockpits can lead to a high-frequency hearing loss rate of up to 53.6%, which is aggravated by the increase in flight time. Noise not only damages the auditory system but also threatens flight safety by affecting core competency indicators, such as communication barriers and decreased situational awareness.[3] Therefore, a pilot’s health must be ensured, and flight safety must be improved to further investigate the multidimensional effects of aircraft noise on pilots’ auditory perception and develop effective prevention and control strategies. By revealing the multisystem influence mechanism of aircraft noise on auditory perception and proposing the integrated prevention and control strategy, this study aimed to reveal the multisystem influence mechanism of aircraft noise on pilots’ auditory perception and propose integrated prevention and control strategies to ensure flight safety and occupational health.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Subjects

This retrospective observational research was conducted and approved by the Ethics Committee of Qingdao Hospital, University of Health and Rehabilitation Sciences (Qingdao Municipal Hospital) (batch number: 2025-KY-066). This cross-sectional study retrospectively analysed ear, nose and throat examination data collected between March 2021 and May 2024. A total of 170 individuals were initially identified, of whom 11 were excluded (5 due to incomplete hearing test data, 4 due to a history of ear disease and 2 due to prior use of ototoxic medications). A total of 159 participants met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis, comprising 92 pilots and 67 controls. The control group consisted of healthy individuals recruited from the hospital health examination centre, with normal hearing and no history of occupational noise exposure. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines, and all pilots provided written informed consent to participate.[4]

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria included the following: (1) accumulated flight time ≥300 hours in the last 12 months and average flight time ≥20 hours per month; (2) the control group comprised of all healthy people with normal hearing (0–25 dB), no vestibular symptoms such as tinnitus and vertigo; and (3) aged 18–45 years old.

The exclusion criteria comprised the following: (1) history of ear trauma; (2) previous history of ear surgery; (3) combined with ear diseases, such as otitis media and external auditory canal; (4) history of use of ototoxic medications (e.g., aminoglycoside antibiotics and chemotherapy drugs); (5) family history of noise deafness; and (6) missing data records.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was estimated using G*Power (Heinrich-Heine-Universitat Dusseldorf, Dusseldorf DE, 3.1.9.7) based on an independent-samples t-test comparing two groups (α = 0.05, power = 0.80 and effect size d = 0.54).[5] At least 61 participants per group were required. Although the final analyses involved four groups and employed Kruskal–Wallis non-parametric tests, this initial calculation provided a conservative estimate to ensure adequate statistical power. The exact power for multi-group non-parametric comparisons may differ.

Data Collection and Related Definitions

Baseline Data

The baseline data of participants, including demographic information, clinical characteristics, and audiological test results, were obtained from online health records. Demographic information included gender, age, body mass index (BMI), hypertension (according to the World Health Organization [WHO] definition: subjects with systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher, diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or higher or taking medication for hypertension), current smokers (self-reported to be smoking at least one cigarette per day for 6 months or more), alcohol drinkers (defined as drinking alcohol ≥ once a week in the past year), triglycerides and total cholesterol levels.[678]

Pure Tone Audiometry Test

A pure-tone audiometer (model AD-4A, Oshda Hearing Technology, Xiamen Co., LTD.) was used to measure air conduction hearing thresholds at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 kHz. The instrument was operated strictly in accordance with the international standard ISO 8253-1:2010. Its sound pressure level output was calibrated using a class 1 sound level meter in accordance with IEC 61672, ensuring regular traceability to the national metrological standard. Biological calibration was performed before each day’s experiments. A threshold >25 dB HL was defined as hearing loss, consistent with the WHO criteria (WHO, 2021) and the guidelines of the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA, 2005).[910]

Otoacoustic Emission (OAE) Measurement

OAE: All participants were tested in an anechoic testing room. Distortion product otoacoustic emission (DPOAE) was performed using a Maico ERO · SCAN otoacoustic emission analyser (Maico, Berlin, GER, Germany). The DPOAE test consisted of presenting two primary sounds at the f1 and f2 frequencies and L1 and L2 levels, with the frequency ratio f2/f1 fixed at 1.22, the stimulus sound pressure level set at L1 = 65 dB/L2 = 55 dB and with testing frequencies ranging from 1 to 6 kHz. These parameters were expected to yield the optimal DPOAE amplitude.[11] Insertable transducers were placed in each participant’s ear to deliver pairs of primary sounds, which were automatically stopped after 30 seconds of running through frequencies in each ear. Only responses with SNR > 6 dB were considered ‘pass’ for all f2 frequencies.

Weinstein Noise Sensitivity Scale (WNSS)

Noise sensitivity was measured using the WNSS (sourced from public academic resources, consistent with fair use principles), which consisted of 21 rating items, each on a 6-point (0–5) Likert scale. The scale ranged from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’, and the greater the consistency of the statement, the higher the noise sensitivity. It’s Cronbach α coefficient was 0.86, indicating high reliability.[12]

Statistical Methods

Statistical Product and Service Solutions 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyse and process the data collected in this study. Categorical variables were expressed as (n [%] using the Chi-square test or Fisher exact test. For continuous variables, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Data that did not meet the normal distribution were expressed as median (P25, P75). For comparisons among more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. For comparisons between two groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher exact test as appropriate. Sex was included as a covariate in the linear regression model (coded: male = 1, female = 0), whereas confounders such as flight duration and age were adjusted for to assess the independent effect of noise exposure on hearing. Noise Sensitivity Questionnaire

The fighter group had the highest WNSS scores, followed by the helicopter and passenger aircraft groups, whereas the control group had the lowest scores (all P < 0.001; Figure 1).

Correlation between Cumulative Noise Exposure and Hearing Threshold

Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between flight time and hearing thresholds at different frequencies. No statistically significant correlations were observed at 1, 2 and 4 kHz (ρ = 0.096, −0.046 and −0.045, respectively, all P > 0.05), suggesting that the crude association between flight noise exposure and hearing loss was not significant, possibly due to sample size limitations and heterogeneity in flight characteristics [Table 5]. However, when potential confounders such as age, gender, smoking history and hypertension were adjusted for in a multivariate linear regression model, flight time showed a significant positive correlation with the 4 kHz hearing threshold (β = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.18–0.66, P = 0.001). Thus, the independent effect of cumulative flight time on high-frequency hearing loss may be masked in univariate analysis but becomes evident after controlling for confounding variables.

Multivariate Regression Analysis

To further explore the independent effects of noise exposure on hearing, a multivariate linear regression model was constructed using the 4-kHz hearing threshold as the dependent variable and cumulative flight hours as the primary independent variable. After adjusting for sex, age, smoking status and hypertension, flight hours were significantly associated with an elevated 4-kHz threshold (β = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.18–0.66, P = 0.001). This indicates that for every additional 100 hours of flight time, the hearing threshold increased by an average of 0.42 dB HL. Sex was also a significant factor (β = −1.25, P = 0.022), with female participants exhibiting lower thresholds than males. Age approached, but did not reach, statistical significance (P = 0.071), while smoking status and hypertension showed no significant associations (both P > 0.05). DISCUSSION

This study systematically evaluated how different aircraft noise types affect pilots’ auditory function and identified characteristic patterns of noise-related hearing impairment. The findings confirmed that aviation noise causes significant auditory damage and highlighted distinct mechanisms under different exposure modes, providing a scientific basis for targeted protective strategies.

Hearing threshold differences across 0.25–4 kHz were highly significant among occupational groups (all P < 0.001, η2 = 0.43–0.48), exceeding Cohen’s large-effect criterion (>0.14).[13] This confirmed a strong link between occupational exposure and hearing loss, consistent with Blair et al.[14] The hearing loss of fighter and helicopter pilots showed a ‘high-frequency priority’, with maximal threshold elevation at 3–4 kHz, aligning with their respective noise spectra (3–6 kHz for fighters; 2–5 kHz for helicopters).[15] This correspondence suggests that the spectral composition of noise determines the hearing loss pattern.

This finding was further reinforced by signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) analysis. In the frequency bands of 2, 4 and 6 kHz, the SNR of the exposed group was significantly reduced (P < 0.001), and no significant difference was observed at the frequency band of 1 kHz (P > 0.05). This highlighted the clinical significance of frequency specificity, with η2 of 0.46 and 0.47 for the 4 and 6 kHz bands, respectively (95% CI: 0.35–0.56), exceeding Cohen’s ‘big effect’ threshold (>0.14). Thus, occupational exposure accounted for 46%–47% of the individual variance in SNR in these two high-frequency bands. By contrast, η2 of 2 kHz was 0.10 (95% CI: 0.04–0.17), suggesting a moderate effect size and indicating that the SNR difference in this frequency band was less affected by occupational factors. Moreover, η2 of 1 kHz was only 0.006 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.00–0.02), representing a ‘minor effect’, with P = 0.825 after FDR correction, further confirming the absence of a significant difference in low-frequency SNR between groups. This result may reflect two mechanisms. Firstly, the cochlear basilar membrane is sensitive to high-frequency acoustic waves, and the hair cells are highly susceptible to damage.[16] Secondly, the middle ear sound transmission system provides optimal protection for frequencies around 1 kHz, which correlates with the evolutionary adaptation to the main frequency range of human speech. Although the passenger aircraft group showed hearing impairment at all tested frequencies, the severity was significantly lower than that observed in the combat aircrew and helicopter groups; this finding may be related to the effective noise control measures implemented in the cabins of commercial aircraft.[17]

Univariate Spearman correlation analysis found no statistically significant correlations between flight time and hearing thresholds at 1, 2 or 4 kHz (all P > 0.05). However, in the multivariate linear regression model, after adjusting for age, sex, smoking history and hypertension, flight hours were significantly positively associated with the 4 kHz hearing threshold (β = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.18–0.66, P = 0.001). This result indicated that the independent effect of cumulative flight time on high-frequency hearing loss may be masked in univariate analysis but became evident after controlling for confounding variables.

The WNSS score results revealed the psychological effect of noise exposure. Combat aircrew showed the highest sensitivity to noise, which was closely related to their long-term exposure to high-intensity (>110 dBA) and impulsive noise environments. Neurophysiological studies have shown that this type of noise can induce nonspecific activation of auditory pathways and elicit excessive responses in the limbic system.[18] By contrast, the airliner group had significantly lower WNSS scores than the combat group but higher WNSS scores than the unexposed group. This difference reflected the cumulative effect of long-term moderate-intensity noise exposure. A possible mechanism is that cockpit noise from fighter jets, typically reaching 100 dBA, may cause damage to inner ear hair cells with prolonged exposure, triggering sensorineural deafness, particularly affecting high-frequency hearing loss. This auditory impairment affects the pilot’s perception of commands and alerts, increasing cognitive load and raising WNSS scores. This finding has important implications for the psychological evaluation of pilots: noise sensitivity may serve as a sensitive index for the early identification of occupational hearing fatigue.[12]On the basis of the identified damage patterns, graded protection strategies are recommended. For fighter and helicopter pilots, combining earmuffs and earplugs with high attenuation at 3–6 kHz should be prioritised. Previous studies on turboprop aircraft showed that active noise-cancelling headphones and earplugs effectively improve flight comfort and protection.[19] Real-time noise monitoring using dosimeters should trigger mandatory rest once cumulative exposure exceeds 85 dBA, which is a threshold also required by the European Aviation Safety Agency guidelines.[20] In addition, systematic desensitisation with biofeedback training may help individuals with high WNSS scores adapt well to noise environments.

Although this study has achieved some results, it also encountered limitations. A major limitation of this work is its cross-sectional design, which prevents the determination of temporal or causal relationships between flight noise exposure and auditory outcomes. Moreover, this study exhibited a significant gender imbalance, with a very low proportion of female pilots (only 2.17% in the pilot group, n = 2; 14.93% in the control group, n = 10). This phenomenon primarily reflects the inherent gender distribution of the aviation profession rather than a flaw in the study design. Statistical analysis revealed a significant gender difference (P = 0.003); however, the extremely small sample size of female pilots led to unstable odds ratio (OR) estimates. Additionally, the high proportion of female medical examiners in the general population and the likelihood of occupational exposure factors in skewing the results further diminished the precision of the estimated sex effect (95% CI: 0.03–0.60), limiting the efficiency of stratified analyses. However, sex was included as a covariate in the multivariate regression analysis to mitigate potential bias. The results showed a relatively limited effect size related to sex, whereas noise exposure-related indicators contributed more significantly to changes in hearing thresholds. Therefore, the robustness of the overall conclusions was not substantially affected. A priori power analysis indicated that the current sample size (n = 28 for the helicopter group, n = 30 for the fighter group and n = 34 for the passenger aircraft group) was sufficient to detect a between-group effect of d ≥ 0.8. However, the overall sample size was still relatively limited, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Future studies should further expand the sample size, include additional female pilots, or adopt a sex-matched study design to reduce potential bias caused by sex differences. The results of this study may be affected by confounding factors, such as differences in the performance of individual protective equipment, inconsistent use behaviours and genetic susceptibility. Furthermore, the precise noise exposure doses of various aircraft types were not measured. Finally, WNSS assessment may be affected by subjective factors. Future studies should use a longitudinal design, combined with precise monitoring of noise dose and extended frequency audiometry (8–16 kHz), to fully assess noise damage. In addition, exploring the association between genetic factors (such as GSTM1 gene polymorphism) and noise susceptibility will help in achieving individualised protection.

Using flight time as a proxy for noise exposure fails to accurately measure the actual noise dose across different aircraft types or flight phases. Although imperfect, this metric is widely accepted in aviation medicine and occupational health studies and is correlated with cumulative noise effects. The observed association between flight time and high-frequency hearing thresholds reinforces its validity. Future research should adopt portable dosimeters to record real-time exposure for enhanced individual assessments. Important confounders, such as the use of protective equipment and pre-employment auditory levels, were unavailable due to the retrospective design based on routine health data. Despite adjusting for key covariates (sex, age, smoking and hypertension), residual confounding cannot be excluded. Future prospective studies with detailed baseline data and monitoring of protective behaviours are essential for robust causal inference. Moreover, the initial sample size estimation was based on a two-group t-test, whereas four-group nonparametric analyses were used; thus, statistical power may vary. Differences in sex composition and occupational background between pilots and controls may also introduce selection bias. Variability in the use of hearing protection device (HPD) remains a potential confounder. Subsequent studies should employ sex-matched controls and quantify HPD usage to accurately isolate the effects of aircraft noise exposure.

CONCLUSION

The effects of different types of aircraft noise on the auditory system of pilots vary. The high-frequency noise of fighters and helicopters mainly leads to an increase in hearing threshold and a decrease in SNR at the frequencies of 3–6 kHz, and the effects of aircraft noise are relatively mild but extensive. The differences in WNSS scores reflect the psychological influence of noise exposure. On the basis of these findings, a protective strategy was proposed for various noise exposure modes, emphasizing the critical protection against high-frequency noise, dynamic management of exposure dose and a comprehensive intervention model for psychological adaptability training. The implementation of these measures will effectively protect the auditory health of pilots and improve aviation safety.
