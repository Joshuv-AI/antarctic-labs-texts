Early fecal metabolomic profiling for predicting acute graft-versus-host disease following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation
Authors: Da Jung Kim, Yoo Jin Lee, Jung-Won Choi, Hong Sub Lee, Won Sik Lee, Han-Seung Park, Eun-Ji Choi, Hyunkyung Park, Jung-Hee Lee, Je-Hwan Lee, Seung Bum Lee, Yunsuk Choi, Seong-Joon Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). Reliable early, noninvasive biomarkers for GVHD prediction remain limited. We conducted targeted fecal metabolomic profiling using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry in 77 allo-HSCT recipients at two time points: before conditioning (S1) and immediately after conditioning but prior to donor T-cell engraftment (S2). We assessed changes in metabolite profiles and their association with subsequent GVHD development. A multivariate logistic regression model and receiver-operating characteristic analysis were used to evaluate predictive performance. Conditioning induced significant alterations in neurotransmitters, bile acids, purine metabolism, tryptophan pathways, and phospholipase A2 (PLA2) activity. Post-conditioning, patients who developed GVHD showed elevated xanthine synthesis, reduced indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase activity, and increased PLA2 metabolites. Reflecting the metabolic balance between anti-inflammatory omega-3 and pro-inflammatory omega-6 fatty acids, the eicosapentaenoic acid to arachidonic acid and docosahexaenoic acid to arachidonic acid ratios were consistently higher in the GVHD group. A five-metabolite-ratio panel demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability (area under the curve: 0.752, 95% confidence interval: 0.584–0.909). Early fecal metabolomic profiling after conditioning may capture metabolic patterns associated with later GVHD risk. This noninvasive approach shows potential to aid early risk stratification and improve preventative strategies in allo-HSCT patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-38818-8.

Introduction

Acute graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) is a major complication arising within the first 10 to 100 days following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) and remains a leading cause of post-transplant morbidity and mortality1,2. Despite prophylactic immunosuppressive therapies, acute GVHD still occurs in approximately 30%–50% of recipients3,4. Among the target organs, GI tract involvement is particularly critical, affecting up to 40%–70% of patients with acute GVHD5,6. Recent evidence highlights the role of intestinal microbiota in the pathogenesis of acute GVHD7–9. A previous study involving 16 S rRNA sequencing of fecal samples from allo-HSCT recipients demonstrated that those with acute GVHD exhibit marked reductions in microbial diversity, particularly a loss of Clostridia class bacteria, a decrease in butyrate-producing organisms, and a reduction in the ratio of strict to facultative anaerobes (S/F ratio)6. These alterations are associated with increased severity of acute GVHD and related mortality, suggesting that the gut microbiome might serve as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target against acute GVHD. Recently, metabolomics has emerged as an important tool for profiling gut-associated metabolites, reflecting influences from host genetics, environmental factors, and microbial activities10,11. Indeed, a longitudinal study by Kim et al. highlights that dynamic shifts in the gut metabolome are closely linked to clinical outcomes, including GVHD and overall survival, in HSCT recipients12. In the context of allo-HSCT, interventions such as conditioning regimens, antibiotic therapies, and antiviral treatments can substantially alter the gut microenvironment and potentially modulate the risk of acute GVHD. In the field of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) research, including research on ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, which share pathophysiological features with GI-GVHD13, metabolomics has revealed disturbances in short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, and tryptophan derivatives, implicating them in immune regulation and disease progression14,15. However, metabolomic investigations on patients with acute GVHD remain limited16. Given the shared inflammatory pathways between GI-GVHD and IBD, we hypothesized that metabolic alterations implicated in IBD could serve as early warning signals for GVHD. Accordingly, the present study aimed to (1) characterize early fecal metabolomic reprogramming induced by conditioning and (2) discover a mechanism-based metabolite panel capable of predicting acute GVHD in a real-world cohort.

Results

Study population

A total of 77 consecutive patients who underwent allo-HSCT across the three institutions were enrolled in the present study (Table 1). The median age of the cohort was 51 years (interquartile range [IQR], 37–62 years), and 59.7% of the patients were male. The most common underlying disease was acute myeloid leukemia (AML) (36.4%), followed by acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) (20.8%) and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) (20.8%). Among the patients, 42 (54.5%) were in remission at the time of HSCT. When restricting the analysis to acute leukemia (AML and ALL) other than MDS, aplastic anemia, and other hematologic malignancies, 39 patients (89.7%) with acute leukemia were in remission status at the time of HSCT. Around 49.4% of the patients underwent allo-HSCT from matched unrelated donors. There were no significant differences in the underlying diseases between the patients who developed acute GVHD (n = 29, 37.7%) and those who did not (n = 48, 62.3%). The incidence of acute GVHD tended to mildly differ by donor type. Patients with matched sibling donors showed a relatively lower incidence of acute GVHD (acute GVHD vs. no GVHD: 22.2% vs. 77.8%), whereas those with unrelated donors were more likely to develop acute GVHD (acute GVHD vs. no GVHD: 44.7% vs. 55.3%).

There was no significant difference in the incidence of acute GVHD based on conditioning regimen. However, patients receiving anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) exhibited a significantly higher incidence of acute GVHD compared to those who did not receive ATG. To investigate this further, we stratified patients by ATG dose intensity. We found that the incidence of acute GVHD was significantly higher in the high-dose ATG group (> 4.5 mg/kg) compared to the low-dose group (53.3% vs. 21.4%). Crucially, this disparity was driven by donor type: high-dose ATG was exclusively administered to patients with high-risk donors (unrelated or haploidentical), whereas low-dose ATG was predominantly reserved for HLA-matched sibling donors (P < 0.001, Table S1). Consistent with the ATG findings, the higher GVHD incidence observed in the methotrexate (MTX) group aligns with our institutional protocol, where MTX is standardly co-administered with high-dose ATG for high-risk transplants. Consequently, these incidence patterns appear to be driven by the inherent immunological risks associated with the donor type.

Intravenous (IV) broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered to 61 patients (79.2%) between D-8 and D0 of HSCT, with no significant difference observed between the GVHD (24, 82.8%) and non-GVHD groups (37, 77.1%), respectively (P = 0.773).

Study outcomes

Among those with acute GVHD, 13 patients exhibited GI involvement, denoted as GI-GVHD patients. Most acute GVHD cases (n = 23/26) were classified as grades II–IV. The median onset of acute GVHD was 26 days post-transplantation, with a longer median onset observed in GI-GVHD patients (37 days) compared to those with non-GI GVHD (26 days) (Table 2). Neutropenic fever occurred in 54.5% of patients (median onset: day 5), with a higher incidence frequency in the non-GVHD group (62.5% vs. 41.4%), although the difference was not significant (p = 0.071). During the 1-year follow-up period, the cumulative incidence rate of underlying disease relapse was 22.1% (n = 17), with no significant difference observed between the GVHD and non-GVHD groups. Nine patients died within 100 days after allo-HSCT, and the cumulative incidence rate of non-relapse mortality within the 1-year follow-up period was 6.5% (n = 5), with no significant differences between the GVHD and non-GVHD groups (Table 3).

Metabolomic changes before and after conditioning

We conducted a paired analysis of fecal metabolite profiles before (S1) and after (S2) conditioning in 70 allo-HSCT recipients, excluding the remaining seven patients due to sample insufficiency or technical limitations (Fig. 1 and Table S2). PCA was used to evaluate global metabolic shifts. No overt clustering was observed between the two groups in the PCA score plot. However, the Volcano plot analysis identified 34 and 31 metabolites that significantly upregulated and downregulated, respectively, after conditioning. Pathway analysis comparing samples before and after conditioning revealed distinct alterations across multiple metabolite pathways (Fig. 2). The synthesis pathways of various neurotransmitters, including dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA), gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and serotonin, were significantly downregulated after conditioning. Contrarily, the bile acid conjugation pathways, particularly glycine and taurine conjugation of primary and secondary bile acids, were markedly upregulated. Purine metabolism was also activated. Additionally, tryptophan metabolism tended to shift toward the indole pathway, whereas the phospholipase A2 (PLA2)-related inflammatory activity was elevated across multiple isoforms.

Metabolomic differences in patients with and without acute GVHD

The post-conditioning fecal samples (S2) from 70 patients (27 with GVHD and 43 without GVHD) were compared (Fig. 3 and Table S3). PCA and Volcano plot analysis showed partial separation between the GVHD and non-GVHD groups. Pathway-based comparisons revealed multiple metabolite alterations distinguishing the two groups (Fig. 4). The eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) to arachidonic acid (AA) ratio was markedly elevated in the GVHD group (fold change = 2.74), along with the activities of PLA2 subgroup VI (PLA2-VI) and PLA2 subgroup II (PLA2-II), with fold changes of 2.34 and 1.68, respectively. The docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) to AA ratio and xanthine synthesis were also higher in GVHD patients (fold change = 1.89 and 1.45, respectively). Contrarily, the indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) activity was notably reduced in the GVHD group (fold change = 0.38), suggesting a shift in tryptophan metabolism. Notably, although the neurotransmitter-related and bile acid conjugation pathways markedly altered post-conditioning, they did not differ significantly between GVHD and non-GVHD patients. Contrarily, the EPA: AA and DHA: AA ratios remained stable before and after conditioning; however, they were consistently elevated in the GVHD group (Figure S1).

Biomarker panel to distinguish GVHD from non-GVHD

To identify potentially useful biomarkers for GVHD formation, a binary logistic regression analysis was employed to construct an optimal model. Ultimately, five metabolic ratios involving diacylglycerol (DG) and triacylglycerol (TG) species were identified. The panel of metabolic ratios is listed in Table S4. Subgroup analysis based on organ involvement

The comparison between the GI-GVHD group (n = 13) and the non-GVHD group (n = 43) did not yield statistically robust metabolic signatures distinguishing GI-GVHD from uncomplicated cases, likely due to the limited sample size. Thus, specific predictive markers for GI-GVHD were not included in the primary model. However, to address potential metabolic differences within the GVHD cohort, we compared patients with GI involvement (n = 13) to those with non-GI GVHD (n = 16). This subgroup analysis revealed that patients with GI-GVHD exhibited a widespread downregulation of lipid species, particularly DG and TG (e.g., TG 14:0_34:1, fold change = -3.38), compared to the non-GI group (Table S7).

Impact of antibiotics on fecal metabolic profiles in patients with acute GVHD

Given that broad-spectrum antibiotics can alter the gut microenvironment, we investigated their influence on the identified metabolic signatures. We compared the metabolic profiles of patients with acute GVHD who were receiving intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics at the time of sampling (S2) versus those who were not. As detailed in Table S8, antibiotic treatment induced alterations in certain metabolites. However, the core metabolic signatures identified as predictors of GVHD did not show significant differences based on antibiotic usage. Furthermore, Figure S2 reveals no distinct clustering or significant separation between the antibiotic-treated and non-treated subgroups.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated early fecal metabolic alterations to identify predictive biomarkers for acute GVHD in a real-world allo-HSCT cohort. A key distinguishing feature of our approach is the analysis of samples at a unique early time point (day + 1 to + 3), specifically immediately after conditioning but prior to neutrophil engraftment. Unlike previous studies that typically collected samples around day + 21,8 this timing was strategically chosen to capture metabolic signatures prior to both the median onset of acute GVHD (~ day + 26) and the initiation of empirical antibiotics (~ day + 6), thereby minimizing confounding factors. Indeed, recent machine-learning data from Shtossel et al. confirm that stool microbiome features collected in this early post-transplant phase are superior predictors for acute GVHD compared to later time points17.

This study uniquely integrates metabolomics with pathway analysis to elucidate metabolic networks reprogrammed in GVHD, offering mechanistic insights beyond isolated biomarkers. Among the changes observed post-conditioning, the most prominent were a decrease in neurotransmitter-related metabolites, such as DOPA, GABA and serotonin, and an increase in the bile acid pathways, with both of them reflecting gut dysbiosis induced by conditioning. A recent study revealed specific GABA-modulating bacteria within the human gut microbiota, such as the Bacteroides, Parabacteroides, and Escherichia species, using a combination of co-culture isolation, metabolite purification, and transcriptomic analyses18. Given that Bacteroides spp. are major GABA producers, the observed reduction in GABA levels in the current study further validated gut dysbiosis post-conditioning. A previous meta-analysis showed that reduced Bacteroides abundance is associated with IBD, which indicates that the post-conditioning gut environment becomes more susceptible to inflammation19. The reduction in serotonin levels also suggested potential gut dysbiosis because gut microbiota are known to regulate host serotonin biosynthesis, particularly via microbial metabolites that stimulate enterochromaffin cells in the intestinal mucosa20. In our study, the pathways associated with the primary bile acids were enhanced post-conditioning. According to a previous systematic review, such an increase is related to gut dysbiosis, particularly a decrease in Firmicutes, which play a key role in the microbial conversion of primary to secondary bile acids21,22. Similar patterns of dysbiosis have been reported as a universal signature of gut GVHD in a recent systematic review of pediatric cohorts,9 suggesting that these alterations reflect a fundamental pathogenic process. A reduction in Firmicutes has also been reported to be associated with IBD, further suggesting that the post-conditioning gut microenvironment might be more susceptible to inflammatory changes23,24. The alterations in bile acid metabolism of the patients with acute GVHD were also demonstrated in a previous French study analyzing plasma metabolomic profiles of patients undergoing matched sibling allo-HSCT16. In the present study, which analyzed stool metabolites at a preclinical stage, we observed a notable increase in the primary bile acid-related pathways in acute GVHD patients, suggesting that microbial dysbiosis affecting bile acid transformation might precede clinical onset.

Our analysis of the post-conditioning metabolites revealed specific metabolic pathways that may serve as potential predictors of subsequent acute GVHD development. Among these, the pathways that showed changes at the S2 compared to S1 and were more prominently altered in the GVHD group included elevated purine metabolism, particularly xanthine synthesis; a shift in tryptophan metabolism with elevated indole levels and reduced IDO activity; and increased PLA2 activity across multiple subgroups. Xanthine synthesis was more prominent in the GVHD group, and given its role in enhancing Th17 cell activity, our findings indicated a link between altered purine metabolism and gut inflammation25,26. This aligns with a recent longitudinal study, which identified elevated uric acid, a downstream product of xanthine metabolism, as a predictive marker for GVHD onset12.

In the present study, conditioning led to an increase in indole synthesis, whereas IDO activity was specifically decreased in patients who developed acute GVHD, suggesting that an imbalance between the microbiota-mediated indole and host-driven IDO/kynurenine pathways may be critical in disease development27. Metabolic shift from the host-driven IDO/kynurenine pathway toward the microbiota-mediated indole synthesis post-conditioning might contribute to the loss of immune tolerance and the development of acute GVHD28. As IDO activity supports immune tolerance by promoting regulatory T cell differentiation and suppressing effector T cell responses, its downregulation might lead to impaired tolerance and heightened inflammatory reactivity29. A previous study has shown that mucosal IDO expression is upregulated in established GI-GVHD lesions30. However, our findings suggested that a decrease in fecal IDO activity precedes the onset of acute GVHD. This discrepancy might reflect distinct spatial dynamics, where early luminal IDO suppression contributes to immune tolerance loss, contrasting with the compensatory upregulation often observed in inflamed mucosal tissues later in the disease course. However, further studies are warranted to elucidate the specific roles of IDO in the pathogenesis of acute GVHD.

PLA2 plays a key role in inflammation by catalyzing the hydrolysis of membrane phospholipids to release AA, a precursor for various pro-inflammatory lipid mediators31. Following conditioning, various PLA2 subgroups showed an increased activity in the present study, with PLA2-II being particularly elevated in patients who developed acute GVHD. PLA2-II has been identified as a modulator of the gut microbiota, and its overexpression in Pla2g2a-null C57BL/6 mice induces systemic inflammation and worsens autoimmune conditions32. In the present study, PLA2-VI was significantly increased after conditioning and was further elevated in the GVHD group. Although there is a lack of direct evidence linking PLA2-VI to gut inflammation, a previous study has shown that group VIB Ca2+-independent PLA2γ can function as a gut-derived mediator, entering the systemic circulation via the mesenteric lymph and contributing to acute lung injury by regulating pro-inflammatory lipid mediators33. These findings indicated that PLA2-VI might act like an inflammatory modulator in the pathogenesis of acute GVHD.

Interestingly, the EPA: AA and DHA: AA ratios were consistently elevated in our patients, both before and after conditioning. Previous studies have shown that AA acts as a pro-inflammatory fatty acid stimulating ICAM-1 expression, whereas EPA lacks such inflammatory potential34. Therefore, the consistently elevated EPA: AA or DHA: AA ratios observed before and after conditioning in the current study might reflect an intrinsic characteristic of hosts susceptible to acute GVHD; however, the detailed underlying mechanisms require further investigation.

Based on these pathway-level findings, we constructed a stool-based metabolite panel to predict the risk of GVHD. This model demonstrated a moderate discriminative ability (AUC: 0.752) in the ROC analysis. This finding further supports the feasibility of metabolite-based models for early prediction of acute GVHD in a noninvasive manner. Previous metabolomic studies have primarily focused on characterizing metabolic signatures after the onset of acute GVHD in allo-HSCT recipients. However, the present study uniquely analyzed stool metabolites at an earlier, pre-GVHD time point, immediately after conditioning but before clinical GVHD manifestation. Notably, despite this earlier sampling period, our results revealed metabolic alterations that were similar to those reported in the GVHD group, including changes in tryptophan metabolism, purine pathways, and bile acid profiles16,35. These findings suggested that the metabolic shifts associated with GVHD begin to emerge before the onset of overt clinical symptoms and may be captured through early metabolomic profiling. The derivation of our data from real-world patients strengthens the clinical relevance of our results and supports the potential utility of fecal metabolomics as a predictive tool for acute GVHD in clinical practice.

The present study had several limitations. First, the absence of gut microbiome profiling restricted our ability to establish a direct causal relationship between the observed metabolite alterations and dysbiosis. Second, because only a relatively few studies have examined metabolomic changes specific to GVHD, the clinical relevance of our findings had to be interpreted primarily by comparison with data from IBD patients. Although IBD and GVHD share overlapping pathophysiological features, such cross-disease comparisons may not fully align with the objective of identifying GVHD-specific metabolic pathways. Third, although we observed distinct lipidomic alterations in patients who subsequently developed GI-GVHD, characterized by a marked reduction in fecal glycerolipids compared to non-GI GVHD patients, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size (n = 13 vs. n = 16). Due to this limitation, we were unable to establish a robust predictive marker panel exclusive to GI-GVHD (versus the non-GVHD group) in the current cohort. Larger-scale validation studies are required to confirm whether these early lipidomic shifts can serve as reliable organ-specific predictors. Fourth, the stool samples were not collected after the onset of GVHD, which prevented us from evaluating whether the metabolite changes observed prior to GVHD persisted during the disease course or whether additional alterations emerged post-GVHD. Finally, serum metabolite analysis was not performed in the present study. While our primary focus was to characterize the local gut metabolic landscape, we acknowledge that the absence of concurrent serum data limits the direct comparison of our findings with established systemic markers. Future prospective studies integrating these fecal metabolic profiles with established blood-based biomarkers are warranted to construct a comprehensive ‘gut-systemic’ prediction model, thereby enhancing the accuracy of early GVHD detection.

Additionally, a significant proportion of patients in our cohort received broad-spectrum antibiotics for febrile condition between D-8 and D0 of HSCT, which inevitably influences the gut microbiome and metabolomic landscape. While we purposefully included these patients to reflect real-world clinical practice, the potential confounding effects of antibiotics on specific low-abundance metabolites cannot be fully ruled out. However, the persistence of our core metabolic signatures despite this heterogeneous antibiotic exposure underscores the robustness of the identified biomarkers for clinical application.

Despite the abovementioned challenges, this study provides preliminary evidence that metabolic patterns preceding GVHD onset can be detected in the early post-conditioning phase. While these findings suggest the potential of fecal metabolomics as a predictive tool, this work represents a discovery phase. Large-scale validation studies in independent cohorts are essential to confirm the clinical utility and generalizability of these candidate biomarkers.

Methods

Study design and patient enrollment

This prospective, multicenter observational study was conducted at the Department of Hematology or Hematology-Oncology of the following three tertiary care centers in South Korea: Asan Medical Center, Ulsan University Hospital, and Inje University Busan Paik Hospital, via which all biological samples were procured. Patients aged ≥ 19 years undergoing allo-HSCT were recruited consecutively. A total of 77 patients were enrolled, and their fecal samples were successfully obtained at two predefined time points: prior to conditioning (day − 7 to − 6: S1) and on the day of transplantation following conditioning (day + 1 to + 3: S2). The fecal samples (~ 5 g, collected at S1 and S2) were immediately frozen at − 80 °C and transported under cold chain to Seoul National University Hospital for metabolomic analysis. The samples were processed in a blind manner after removing patient identifiers. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all three participating centers. All patients provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Antimicrobial strategy

According to the protocols of the three institutions, prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended to prevent antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections in allogeneic HSCT. To control high fever (≥ 38.1 °C), IV broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered without restriction, irrespective of the timing of stool sample collection.

Sample collection and Preparation

The fecal samples were collected and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. For metabolite extraction, approximately 200 mg (± 10 mg) of the frozen fecal samples were weighed and mixed with extraction buffer (HPLC-grade 100% ethanol/20 mM phosphate buffer, 7:3, v/v) at a buffer: sample ratio of 3:1. The mixture was homogenized using an ultrasonic sonicator (KBT, Korea) at 100 W for 2 min, 4 °C. The homogenate was first centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to a new tube. A second centrifugation was performed at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove residual particulates. The final supernatant was collected for metabolomic profiling.

Metabolite quantification

Targeted metabolomic analysis was conducted using the MxP® Quant 500 Kit (BIOCRATES Life Sciences AG, Austria), which quantifies up to 630 metabolites across diverse classes. The platform integrates liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) and flow injection analysis (FIA)–MS/MS using isotope-labeled internal standards. Sample preparation involved loading 10 µL of each sample into a 96-well plate preloaded with internal standards, followed by drying under nitrogen. The samples were derivatized with 5% phenyl isothiocyanate and extracted with 5 mM ammonium acetate in methanol. The eluates were used for LC–MS/MS (diluted 1:1 with water) and FIA–MS/MS (10 µL eluate mixed with 400 µL FIA solvent).

LC–MS/MS

LC–MS/MS was used to quantify the amino acids, biogenic amines, bile acids, indoles, and other small molecules in each sample using a Biocrates UPLC column. Chromatographic separation employed a binary gradient (water and acetonitrile with 0.2% formic acid) at 0.8 mL/min and 50 °C. FIA–MS/MS was used to profile lipid species using a methanol-based solvent under a 0.03 mL/min flow. All analyses were conducted on a 5500 QTRAP Plus mass spectrometer (AB Sciex) in positive electrospray ionization mode with multiple reaction monitoring.

Data analysis

Raw data were processed using Analyst 1.5.1 software and Biocrates MetIDQ®. Quantification was based on 7-point calibration curves and internal standards. Inter- and intra-plate normalizations were performed using QC2 replicates and their expected target concentrations. The observed concentration of each metabolite was normalized to the amount of protein.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 software (IBM Corp., New York, USA). Continuous variables were presented as the median (IQR) and compared using Wilcoxon rank sum tests. Categorical variables were presented as numbers with percentages and analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Paired comparisons (S1 vs. S2) were evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Group comparisons (GVHD vs. non-GVHD at S2) were assessed using Mann–Whitney U tests. For metabolomics data analysis, multivariate and univariate analyses were conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 tool. Normalized data based on the total sum of all detected ions were entered, and a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to examine the differences in the overall metabolite profiles among the groups. The multiple test problem was corrected by adjusting the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini and Hochberg method. Binary logistic regression was employed for biomarker discovery. Volcano plots were constructed based on the adjusted p-values (FDR < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| > 1). For the diagnostic modeling of GVHD, a logistic regression model was built using five metabolite ratios involving DG and TG species. The model was assessed via 100 times stratified shuffle cross-validation. The area under the ROC curve and 95% CIs were calculated using the resampled folds.
