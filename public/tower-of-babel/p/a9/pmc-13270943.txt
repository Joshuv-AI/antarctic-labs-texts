Introduction

The use of an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) can prevent sudden cardiac death as a result of malignant arrhythmia. Traditionally, devices have used a transvenous approach; however, there is a cumulative risk of infection and vascular complications as the patient ages. The novel extravascular ICD system (EV-ICD) provides lifesaving therapy while avoiding the intravascular space via a tunneled lead into the retrosternal space.1 To the authors’ knowledge, there are no published data regarding the behavior of preexisting devices poststernotomy. We are the first to describe the perioperative course and postoperative behavior of a patient with an incumbent EV-ICD who required surgery via a sternotomy.

Case report

A 37-year-old woman was diagnosed with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) after the identification of a murmur during pregnancy in October 2022. At diagnosis, her interventricular septum measured 19 mm, with dynamic left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction, a resting gradient of 23 mm Hg increasing to 79 mm Hg under Valsalva maneuver, and mild mitral regurgitation associated with systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated diffuse patchy fibrosis of the anterior interventricular septum, with severe focal fibrosis of the insertion of the inferior right ventricular free wall. Genetic testing revealed a pathogenic heterozygous mutation of MYH7, associated with familial HCM.2

Cardiac monitoring in 2024 revealed nonsustained ventricular tachycardia. There was an 11.9% calculated 5-year risk of sudden cardiac death, alongside previously described unheralded syncope. A Medtronic Aurora EV-ICD was implanted successfully in March 2024.

The patient described progressive breathlessness through late 2024. A repeat echocardiogram demonstrated progression of her LVOT obstruction, with a Valsalva maneuver-provoked gradient of 178 mm Hg. There was now significant SAM with severe mitral regurgitation. The left ventricle had near-complete obliteration during systole and demonstrated grade 3 diastolic dysfunction. Her estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure was 69 mm Hg. A subsequent right heart catheter study demonstrated a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 56 mm Hg, a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 25 mm Hg, and a pulmonary arterial resistance of 9.8 Wood units.

She was discussed by a cardiac surgical multidisciplinary team for consideration of surgical intervention, given her rapidly progressive HCM. Septal ablation was considered; however, given her young age and mitral valve functional involvement, it was felt unlikely to provide robust long-term result. As such, a definitive surgical myectomy and possible mitral valve replacement was determined to be the most appropriate strategy. As part of preoperative planning, change of device to an alternative strategy such as transvenous implantation was discussed, including the benefit of atrial pacing and discrimination; however, the patient’s preference was to retain the preexisting EV-ICD system, with the option of extraction and replacement with a transvenous system if there was failure of the device postoperatively.

Preoperatively, the computed tomography scan demonstrated the entry point of the lead to the left of the xiphoid process and tunneled lateral to the left lateral border of the sternum. The EV-ICD was deactivated. A standard midline sternotomy was performed, with subsequent sternal retraction to facilitate cardiac access. The lead was noted to move en bloc with the chest wall. Although it was palpable to the surgeon, it remained protected by chest wall tissue and did not enter the surgical field.

The patient was placed on cardiopulmonary bypass and the heart arrested in the usual fashion. Septal myectomy was performed via a transaortic approach. The post–cardiopulmonary bypass transesophageal echocardiogram demonstrated a reduction in the LVOT gradient from 68 to 6 mm Hg. Stress testing of the residual mitral regurgitation was undertaken in a staged fashion with isoprenaline, hypovolemia, and in a hypotensive state (systolic blood pressure 60 mm Hg). This induced only mild SAM with mild-to-moderate mitral regurgitation. Given the reduction in gradient and improvement in transvalvular function, the decision was made to preserve the native valve. The sternotomy was closed carefully with a combination of sternal wires and a fiber tape, ensuring the lead was not captured.

The postoperative transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated normal left ventricular systolic function, with an interventricular septal thickness of 9.7 mm. There was the identification of a small diastolic jet on color flow Doppler originating from the interventricular septum into the left ventricular cavity, likely consistent with an unroofed small septal perforator artery.

There was a 12-hour period of complete heart block after the procedure, with eventual recovery of her native conduction system. Her discharge electrocardiogram demonstrated a new rightward axis and incomplete left bundle branch block when compared with her preoperative electrocardiogram. The patient’s recovery was otherwise unremarkable, and she was discharged home 9 days after surgery. Her histological analysis returned after discharge, with findings consistent with HCM.

The patient presented for defibrillation threshold testing 3 months later. Ventricular fibrillation was induced with burst pacing and was successfully defibrillated by the EV-ICD with a single 30-J shock to sinus rhythm. Pacing parameters remained similar to the immediate postoperative check.

Discussion

We present the first published case of a preserved EV-ICD after a standard sternotomy. Although there have been case reports on EV-ICD implantation at the time of sternotomy, to date, it has been unclear whether the lead could be preserved in the setting of a sternotomy, given its proximity to the surgical site. We demonstrate, with evidence of pre- and postsurgical device parameters and successful defibrillation threshold testing, that cardiac surgery has been safely performed without important alteration of device function. In our case, this was likely facilitated by the location of the lead to the left of midline.

Choice of ICD type has become more complex with the advent and approval by various regulatory bodies of subcutaneous ICD (S-ICD) and EV-ICD in 2012 and 2023, respectively.3,4 These systems promote a reduced risk of infection, which is an important and deleterious complication of the classical transvenous ICD systems.5, 6, 7 There is also the important preservation of the subclavian vascular architecture, which can become stenotic after intravascular lead placement, leading to difficulties with lead replacement and provoked deep vein thrombosis in patients.5 This has further implications for patients who may require a change in device therapy, such as change from ICD therapy to cardiac resynchronization therapy. Intravascular leads are also prone to complication, with up to 8% of leads developing a mechanical complication at 5 years and up to 25% at 10 years.8

Younger patients with indications for defibrillator therapy are subject to these risks over a long lifespan, with subsequent cumulative harm. The advent of S-ICD systems alleviated some of these concerns, but they have technical limitations.6 This system is able to provide bradycardia pacing only for a short period after defibrillation, and because of the high current required for capture through skeletal tissue, this can be uncomfortable.9 It is also unable to deliver antitachycardia pacing (ATP) without the assistance of leadless pacemaker implantation, which requires a second implantation procedure and a secondary device, which also requires monitoring and maintenance.10 Given that ATP may reduce high-voltage therapy by up to 50%, this drawback is clinically relevant.11

The EV-ICD system overcomes this limitation with the ability to deliver ATP, which reduces the number of high-voltage therapies delivered through the device.1 The device also advertises a longer predicted generator lifespan, and as such, there is likely a reduction in the frequency of generator changes compared with the SC-ICD system.12,13 Both these factors, alongside the removal of cumulative vascular risks associated with transvenous systems, have made EV-ICD systems an attractive option for patients requiring an ICD.

Some patients with arrhythmia will develop an indication for cardiac surgery through their lifespan, including progression of their index disease that poses an arrhythmic risk, alongside the background population rate of coronary artery disease and age-related valvular disease.2 There are little data regarding S-ICD or EV-ICD performance in the context of sternotomy. 2 small studies included subgroups of patients who underwent a sternotomy after S-ICD implantation. These have suggested that sternotomy does not appear to interfere with device function, though these subgroups had only 30 and 5 patients, respectively.14,15 There have been no publications regarding sternotomy and EV-ICD. This case has demonstrated that a sternotomy can be performed, with continued satisfactory functioning of the EV-ICD in the early postoperative period.

One potential concern is that the performance of a sternotomy may compromise the ability to subsequently extract an EV-ICD lead, as there may be postoperative fibrotic tissue or adhesions that adhere the EV-ICD lead in place. The leftward and anterior mediastinal placement of the lead may be protective against this. This location lay outside the surgical field, given the lead was palpable but not visualized intraoperatively, making both post–device implantation adhesions and postsurgical adhesions less likely to interfere with either device function or extraction. However, we note that the lead has only been in situ for 1 year, and adhesions may form over a longer period of time. Further postsurgical publication may provide further valuable insight into the outcomes of a longer dwell time.

Conclusion

We present the successful preservation of an incumbent EV-ICD system during cardiac surgery via a midline sternotomy. This provides important information to help guide decision making regarding device choice in young arrhythmic patients who may require future cardiac surgery.
