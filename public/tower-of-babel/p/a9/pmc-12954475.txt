Facilitating Family‐Centred Care at the Paediatric Intensive Care Unit: Implementation of a Co‐Created Bedside Family Board
Authors: Lisa‐Maria van Klaveren, Barbara Geven, Rien de Vos, Job van Woensel, Vincent Geukers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBedside boards are used in various healthcare settings to support communication but their additional value in facilitating family‐centred care (FCC) in the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) has not been systematically studied.AimsTo evaluate the implementation process of a co‐created bedside family board (FB) and assess its effectivity and effectiveness to guide FCC six months following its implementation in the PICU.Study DesignHybrid effectiveness–implementation design type 2, using validated questionnaires, structured observations and self‐report surveys complemented by two focus groups and an interview.ResultsValidated questionnaires indicated high acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of the FB. Qualitative data highlighted the need for ongoing awareness and integration into daily workflows. Effectivity measures showed that the FB accurately reflected child and family needs, although some items of the FB were used inconsistently, and their relevance varied by developmental stage. Effectiveness results demonstrated that the FB facilitated the inclusion of family perspectives in care planning and shared goal setting, thereby promoting FCC. Professionals and families valued the FB for making care more personal, aligning care with the child's preferences and family experiences, guiding family education and supporting care transitions.ConclusionThe FB demonstrated to be a practical and effective tool for enriching FCC in the PICU. It standardises the recording and sharing of child and family needs while allowing personalisation. Embedding the FB into standard workflows, sharing responsibility across the interprofessional team including families, and tailoring it to the child's developmental stage, condition and family context are essential for sustained use.Relevance to PracticeA FB empowers families, fosters shared understanding of care goals and enriches collaboration in paediatric critical care settings. Successful implementation requires co‐creation, contextual tailoring, integration with existing processes and ongoing training to maintain engagement.Keywords: bedside board, family participation, family‐centred care, implementation research, PICU

Introduction

Admission to a Paediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) is a stressful experience for critically ill children and their families [1]. Families commonly report feelings of helplessness and loss of control, altered family roles (e.g., caregiver, sibling or relative), when a child is admitted to the PICU [2, 3]. They are asked to navigate complex medical decisions and witness their child undergoing invasive procedures and treatments [2, 3]. To address these challenges, Family‐Centred Care (FCC) has become a cornerstone of PICU practice [4]. FCC recognises families as integral to care and emphasising collaboration between families and healthcare professionals [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Key principles of FCC include respect and dignity, information sharing, participation and collaboration [7, 10, 11]. FCC is associated with greater emotional resilience, reduced parental stress, increased family's confidence and improved satisfaction and quality of life [12, 13]. While professionals value FCC, barriers persist [14, 15, 16]. Families and professionals frequently report communication breakdowns, particularly when coordinating care across disciplines [13]. Also, families often feel that their expertise, especially regarding children with medical complexity or special needs, is insufficiently valued [13, 17]. High workloads, limited workforce and insufficient training opportunities often constrain FCC in daily practice [12]. Interventions have therefore focused on structuring FCC rounds, promoting family participation in daily care, providing education and psychosocial support and improving communication through tools such as personalised bedside boards [12].

Background

Typically, bedside boards are white boards that are present in the patient's room and contain relevant information for the patient, their family and professionals. Existing boards typically focus either on personal aspects (e.g., comfort measures, personal preferences, important people) [18] or on care planning (e.g., care and safety plan, team member names) [19]. Evidence suggests that bedside boards can improve professionals' understanding of what comforts the child, enhance their recognition and increase family understanding of care plans in the PICU [18, 19].

However, the integration both personal aspects and care planning would facilitate a more holistic approach to supporting the child and their family. The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) serves as a biopsychosocial framework that extends the understanding of health and functioning beyond medical diagnoses, providing a more holistic framework to inform and guide care [20, 21]. To open up possibilities for thought and action that should benefit children, families and professionals beyond classification, Rosenbaum and Gorter [22] propose a series of Favourite or ‘F‐Words’ embedded in the ICF‐domains. Developed to promote modern perspectives on childhood disability that extend beyond concepts of ‘fixing’ and ‘normality’, the F‐Words are increasingly seen as a guide for clinical practice and rehabilitation as well as for FCC, communication and the positive management of childhood disability [23]. The words include Function (what the child can do), Fitness (physical and mental health), Fun (enjoyable and meaningful activities), Family (support and relationships at home), Friends (social connections) and Future (goals and aspirations) [22]. Embedding a bedside board into daily care, while using the F‐Words as guidance, offers a structured approach to enhance FCC in the PICU. Therefore, we developed such a family bedside board (FB) in co‐creation together with families and professionals and systematically evaluated its implementation process as well as its effectivity and effectiveness.

Aims

The aim of this study was twofold: (1) To evaluate the implementation process of the co‐created bedside family board (FB). (2) To assess the effectivity and the effectiveness of the FB over a six‐month period following implementation in the PICU. The study was pre‐registered prior to data collection on the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/cakq5).

Design and Methods

Design

This study followed a hybrid effectiveness‐implementation design type 2 [24], allowing for the simultaneous evaluation of both the implementation process and the effectivity and effectiveness of the innovation. We employed a multi‐method quantitative design complemented by qualitative data to evaluate both implementation outcomes and the (perceived) impact of the FB.

Setting and Sample

The study was conducted at the PICU of the Emma Children's Hospital, Amsterdam UMC. This 11‐bed PICU with single‐patient rooms provides care for critically ill children aged 0–18 years with a mixed patient population. The interprofessional PICU team comprises paediatric intensive care nurses (including trainees) and assistants, paediatrician‐intensivists and paediatric anaesthesiologist‐intensivists, residents/fellows in speciality training, child life specialists, social workers and one family supporter at the time of the study. Additional professionals such as doctors from other specialties (e.g., ENT‐specialist, paediatric pulmonologist), specialist nurses (e.g., nurse ventilation practitioners), allied health and social professionals (e.g., physiotherapist, occupational therapist, psychologist) are part of the interprofessional care team on a consultation basis.

This study included all professionals working within the interprofessional PICU team. Professionals who declined to participate were excluded from the study. All patients admitted to the PICU and their families were eligible for participation, with the exclusion criterion being a PICU stay of 24 h or less. Both professionals and family members had the option to withdraw from the study at any time. A convenience sampling method was employed, supplemented by purposive sampling that included personal invitations to underrepresented professionals and families to participate in self‐report surveys.

Innovation

The development process of the FB was guided by monthly innovation group meetings to co‐create, implement and evaluate the FB. The innovation group included families, nurses, doctors, family supporters, child life specialists and researchers. The perspective of families was represented by a parent of a child who was regularly admitted to this PICU at various stages of his life. At least one representative of each group participated in structured meetings during both the development phase (March–November 2024) and implementation phase (November 2024–May 2025). The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) was used to inform the development and implementation process [25]. CFIR provides a comprehensive structure to facilitate the understanding and evaluation of factors influencing the implementation of innovations in various settings. It encompasses five key domains: outer and inner setting (i.e., physical and work infrastructure, shared values), characteristics of the individuals involved (i.e., leaders, implementation leads and team members, innovation recipients), characteristics of the innovation (i.e., its source and design) and the implementation process (i.e., tailoring strategies) [25].

Outer and Inner Setting

Prior to study initiation, a context analysis conducted by the FCC work group (comprising PICU‐team members dedicated to improving FCC) demonstrated a shared value of FCC within the PICU and generated enthusiasm among staff and families for the co‐creation of a bedside family board aimed at enhancing FCC. This analysis identified barriers such as workload concerns, redundant documentation and communication interruptions. To address these barriers, it was essential to integrate the FB in existing work infrastructure already established to support FCC. This infrastructure included daily rounds involving family members and support from the designated family supporter to assist family members during their PICU stay. The physical infrastructure of single‐patient rooms offered an ideal space for the FB as a tangible resource. Moreover, available funding facilitated the implementation of the FB, ensuring it could be a sustainable addition to the existing resources.

Bedside Family Board

Based on the context analysis and literature review, the innovation group co‐created the item list for the FB that would include relevant care planning information (e.g., team member names) and provide a comprehensive overview of the child's situation, as well as the workflow that would integrate the FB in work infrastructures. Specifically, the item list encompassed all aspects of the F‐Words, while complementing the electronic patient records that contain Fitness‐related and partial Family‐ and Future‐related information. The workflow specified both the timing (e.g., morning round) and responsibility (e.g., interprofessional team) for completing each item. Through iterative cycles of design, piloting and refinement, the innovation group collaboratively developed a definitive version of the FB that was user‐friendly and tailored to the local context and needs. This culminated in a whiteboard placed in the single‐patient's room, featuring co‐created content (i.e., item list), clear visual organisation (i.e., layout) and integration into daily work infrastructures (Table 1, Figure 2).

Individuals

High‐ and mid‐level leaders, including the head of department and both medical and nursing leaders, supported the innovation by allocating time and space to inform and educate all PICU‐team members, as well as by providing budgetary resources as needed. Implementation was led by a senior nurse who was also a member of the innovation group. The process was further facilitated by ambassadors from the FCC work group. The PICU team and family acted equally as innovation deliverers and recipients by filling in the FB individually (e.g., professionals adding their name or family outlining their child's sleeping ritual) as well as jointly (e.g., adding the ‘goal for today’ during the morning round). The family supporter explained the FB to the family and assisted them with filling out the FB, simplifying or translating the items (using a translator app) if needed. The implementation lead, most members of the innovation group and ambassadors also comprised the innovation deliverers and recipients.

Implementation Process

Based on the context analysis and the co‐created innovation, the innovation group formulated and executed the implementation strategy. This strategy comprised three interrelated components: (1) Building upon the shared beliefs and enthusiasm among staff to enhance FCC, the innovation group selected ambassadors, distributed posters as visual reminders and organised a festive launch event as a motivational strategy; (2) In alignment with the characteristics of the innovation, professionals received education about the purpose and usage of the FB. This was achieved through a combination of informative posters, clinical teaching sessions and one‐on‐one explanations provided by the implementation lead or innovation group members, serving as a key educational strategy; and (3) Using the available budget, all ensured necessary resources were made available, including whiteboards including the FB logo, markers and pre‐scripted FB items as a facilitation strategy. To support evaluation and reflection, the implementation process was continuously monitored and, if necessary, adapted to promote optimal uptake and integration into daily practice. Examples included newsletter updates, refresher clinical lessons and informal feedback conversations with professionals and families.

Implementation Outcomes

Implementation outcomes were conceptualised using the taxonomy of Proctor et al. [26, 27] and included penetration, acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, adaptation, sustainability and fidelity. The Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM) and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) [28] were used to assess acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility, respectively. The validated implementation questionnaire was completed digitally by professionals immediately after the clinical teaching session or one‐on‐one explanation.

Adaptation and sustainability were defined as the proportion of FB use during the six‐month implementation phase. Fidelity was operationalised as correct application of items. They were assessed through structured observation of FBs conducted by the researchers as well as through self‐report surveys completed by professionals and families via on‐site QR codes. Structured observations took place on a randomly selected day each week. To ensure inter‐observer reliability, two researchers (LK and BG) conducted observations jointly during the first month and discussed any discrepancies that arose. After one month, as no further discrepancies were identified, subsequent observations were performed by one of the researchers.

Innovation Outcomes

Innovation outcomes focused on two measures: effectivity and effectiveness. Representing the FCC principle ‘respect and dignity’, effectivity in this study referred to the extent to which the FB reflects the needs and preferences of the child and family regarding the content recorded on the board [11]. It was assessed by two items on a self‐report survey completed by professionals and families via an on‐site QR code. Aligned with the ‘participation’ principle of FCC, effectiveness in this study described the extent to which the FB facilitated FCC in daily care. It was measured through three items on a self‐report survey addressing (1) the shared goal formulation, (2) the inclusion of the respondent's perspective and (3) the perceived contribution of the FB to FCC.

Qualitative Data

Qualitative data were collected to contextualise and provide deeper insights into implementation and innovation outcomes. This included two audio‐recorded focus groups with professionals conducted at three and six months, one audio‐recorded semi‐structured interview with the family supporter at six months, and field notes from observations (including ‘goal for today’ entries and conversations with families). The focus groups took place during workhours and replaced a regular clinical lesson. All professionals working at that moment were invited to participate.

Descriptives Variables

For respondents, we recorded the type of respondent for family members (e.g., parents, siblings) and professionals (nurses, doctors, social professionals: family supporter, child life specialist). For patients, descriptive variables included admission day at the time of data collection.

Data Analysis

To describe the characteristics of the sample and to summarise the outcomes, we calculated descriptive statistics including frequencies, medians and interquartile ranges as appropriate. All quantitative data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.2) [29]. Statistical significance was determined at an alpha level of 0.05.

Implementation Outcomes

To assess whether professionals perceived the FB as acceptable, appropriate and feasible, we conducted one‐sided one‐sample Wilcoxon tests comparing the observed mean scores of the AIM, IAM and FIM against the predetermined threshold value of 3, which indicates a positive evaluation. To evaluate whether the use of the FB is adapted and sustainable, we performed binominal tests of the observed and self‐reported proportions of FB use against the predetermined value of 0.80. To assess whether the use of the FB corresponds with the intended use (fidelity), we performed one‐sided one‐sample Wilcoxon tests comparing the observed proportion of used items as intended per FB against the predetermined value of 0.80.

Innovation Outcomes

To evaluate whether the FB effectively represents the needs and preferences of the child and family (effectivity), we conducted one‐sided one‐sample Wilcoxon tests comparing the observed scores of the two items against the predetermined threshold value of 3 as an indicator of a positive evaluation. To assess the effectiveness of the FB to facilitate FCC in clinical practice in terms of establishing a shared goal, we performed one‐sided Wilcoxon tests comparing the observed scores of the three items against the predetermined threshold value of 3 as an indicator of a positive evaluation. This considers the degree to which their own perspective and the perceived role of the FB as a facilitator (effectiveness).

Qualitative Data

Qualitative data analysis involved deductive coding of interview and focus group discussion transcripts based on four codes using the definitions of implementation and innovation outcomes (Table 2): (a) acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility; (b) adaptation, sustainability and fidelity; (c) effectiveness; and (d) effectivity. The ‘goal for today’ entries from structured observations were coded using the F‐Words. Following the initial round of coding (LK), a consensus meeting (LK, BG) was conducted to address any discrepancies and uncertainties in the coding. During this meeting, the coding team collaboratively reviewed the coded segments, engaged in discussions to clarify differences in interpretation and reached a unified agreement on the final coding.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board Amsterdam UMC (approval number: #2022.00287, approval date: 03 September 2023). All data were handled in accordance with institutional data management policies and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). All respondents were informed about the study prior to participation. Data collection was conducted by two of the researchers (LK, BG). Validated questionnaires, self‐report surveys and structured observations were administered and recorded anonymously via Qualtrics (Provo, UT, USA). Upon entry into the self‐report surveys, professionals and families gave informed consent; no personal data were collected. Focus groups and the interview were conducted with written informed consent.

Results

Sample

Fifty‐five professionals (69.6%) answered the validated implementation questionnaires in the pre‐implementation process. Forty‐two families (95% parents, 5% siblings) and 49 professionals (57% nurses, 27% social professionals and 16% doctors) completed the self‐report survey. During 23 structured observation sessions, we recorded 119 FBs for 66 patients (admission days: 0–165 days). Twenty professionals participated in the focus group interviews after three and six months. One family supporter was interviewed after six months.

Implementation Outcomes

Penetration

Fifty professionals (43 nurses, 7 doctors) participated in onsite clinical teaching lessons; additionally, five nurses received one‐on‐one education. This comprises a total engagement of 69.6% of the target group (66 nurses, 13 doctors) with the educational strategy. The clinical lesson was subsequently made available online for all professionals.

Acceptability, Appropriateness and Feasibility

The AIM, IAM and FIM showed high internal consistency in this study (α = 0.932, α = 0.961, α = 0.973, respectively). Professionals rated the FB as highly acceptable, appropriate and feasible. Scores on acceptability (AIM; median = 4, IQR = 0.5; V = 1231.5, p < 0.001), appropriateness (IAM; median = 4, IQR = 0.25; V = 1138.5, p < 0.001) and feasibility (FIM; median = 4, IQR = 0.5; V = 1186.5, p < 0.001) were all significantly higher than the predefined threshold value of 3.

Qualitative data indicated that professionals valued the FB but also emphasised the need for robust evidence on its effectiveness in all targeted families including specific subgroups (e.g., families with children with medical complexity or mental disabilities). They also identified further clarification and increasing awareness of the FB's aims as key to successful implementation. Professionals noted time and workload pressures as barriers to completing the FB and recommended a redistribution of responsibilities for maintaining the FB to family supporters, nurse assistants and families themselves. The exclusive availability of the items in Dutch language was perceived as a limitation, with English or bilingual formats (e.g., Dutch/Arabic or Dutch/Polish) as potential solutions. Adaptation, Sustainability and Fidelity

FB adaptation was high in the first three months, with observed use significantly exceeding the pre‐defined threshold of 0.80 (p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.96, 1.00]) and a median of seven items used per board (IQR = 3). Also, fidelity was strong, with a correct use above threshold (median = 1, IQR = 0.14; V = 5588, p < 0.001). The sustained use over the six‐months period was similarly high (p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.98, 1.00]), again with a median of 7 items (IQR = 3). Most frequently completed items were ‘nurse’ (94.1%), ‘goal’ (84.3%) and ‘family’ (78.2%) (Figure 3). Professionals reported partial adaptation, with self‐reported use remaining below the predetermined threshold value of 0.80 (95% CI [0.71, 0.94]).

Qualitative findings highlighted that the integration of the FB in daily workflow, such as setting common daily goals during morning rounds, was perceived as key to sustainability. FB use was reported to have become standard among new nursing staff and was partially adopted by experienced medical and nursing staff. The family supporter reported integrating the FB fully into her workflow, using it to initiate contact and enrich conversations with families, particularly those who did not speak Dutch fluently or had children with disabilities. For example, she might ask ‘If you suddenly had to leave the hospital for an emergency, hypothetically, what are the most important things about your child and their needs that you would want us to know?’ On the other hand, professionals raised concerns about information overload and uncertainty regarding the currency of information. A digital version was suggested as a potential solution, provided that the FB's ease of use and accessibility would be maintained. Table 3 provides selected example quotes.

Innovation Outcomes

Effectivity

Families and professionals agreed that the FB effectively captured the needs and preferences of the child and family. Scores on both self‐report items for both the child (median = 4, IQR = 1; V = 3121.5, p < 0.001) and family (median = 4, IQRfam = 0.75, IQRprof = 1; V = 2806, p < 0.001) were significantly higher than the predetermined threshold value of 3 (Figure 4). Professionals observed that families engaged with the FB in diverse and meaningful ways. Examples included a child awaiting PICU admission completing the FB with his siblings to help him feel ‘at home’; families documenting the number of epileptic seizures to create a shared understanding with the care team; and families transforming the FB into a memory board during end‐of‐life care, adding drawings, wishes and photographs. Effectiveness

Family and professionals perceived the FB as an effective facilitator of FCC in daily care. Scores on the items whether a clear and shared goal was formulated during the morning round (median = 4, IQR = 1; V = 1737.5, p < 0.001), whether respondents felt their own perspective was taken into account during integrated care planning (medianfam = 4, medianprof = 3, IQR = 1; V = 1424.5, p < 0.001) and regarding the perceived contribution of the FB to FCC in daily practice (medianfam = 4.5, medianprof = 4, IQR = 1; V = 2246, p < 0.001) were significantly higher than the predetermined threshold value of 3 (Figure 4). Daily goals (n = 102) primarily focused on Fitness (84%; e.g., extubation, medication reduction), followed by Family (15%; e.g., going home), Fun (1%; e.g., winning against grandma) and Function (1%; e.g., sitting upright). In 10% of cases, Fitness goals were complemented by Family goals.

Based on the qualitative data, we found that professionals valued the FB to make care more personal, pleasant and welcoming by enabling them to address the child, siblings or even a favourite cuddly toy by name. Families likewise noted that knowing the names of the professionals caring for their child helped them relate more easily to the care team. The FB also supported the care process by aligning with the child's preferences, such as favourite media or bedtime rituals. The ‘goal for today’ helped ensure that families and professionals shared the same expectations and understanding. For example, the FB was used as an overview of steps in family education for learning nursing procedures; as a place to make meaning, parents wrote ‘playing the hockey match on [date]’ as a shared aspiration or ‘saving lives and going home’ for a deceased child; and as a tool to prepare families for care transitions to peripheral hospitals or regular wards. Discussion

While bedside boards are used in various healthcare settings, this study is the first to examine their implementation and effectiveness in facilitating FCC in a PICU. The F‐Words served as holistic approach for supporting children and their families in the development of the FB. The use of the FB was embedded in existing FCC processes (e.g., recording the ‘goal of today’ together during daily FCC rounds) to encourage integration into routine practice [22, 23]. Implementation outcomes from validated questionnaires indicated high acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility. Qualitative data, however, highlighted the need for ongoing motivational and educational strategies, such as targeted clinical lessons, to sustain information‐ and experience‐sharing and ensure its full integration into daily care.

Family and professionals judged the FB to meaningfully reflect child and family needs. By inviting families to record their knowledge, values and preferences, the FB made their contributions visible and legitimate within the care environment, especially for children with disability. This reflects gaps that are well documented in the literature: Greenway et al. [13] reported that families of chronically ill children perceive their expertise as undervalued; the FB counters this by prompting and documenting family input, thereby acknowledging and integrating family knowledge, values and cultural backgrounds into daily care. However, not all items were used consistently. Optimising its uptake may require further balancing the number of mandatory items with space for flexible personalisation, such as drawings, photographs and shared updates. Item relevance varied by developmental stage: some items (e.g., cuddly toy) were more appropriate for younger children, whereas others (e.g., sleeping routine) were not applicable to newborns. Future additions tailored to the developmental stage, such as Friends for older children and adolescents could support and sustain social connections and relationships during admission [30].

Beyond being a visual aid, the FB functions as a relational tool that supports partnership and mutual understanding between families and professionals. Both families and professionals perceived the FB as a meaningful tool for family participation in care planning, facilitating shared goal setting and creating a more personal, welcoming care environment. Analysis of the ‘goal for today’ entries showed that the FB supported the alignment of expectations between families and professionals, guided family education, created space for emotional expression and facilitated preparation for care transitions. These findings echo Ashcraft et al. review [31] documenting unmet informational needs among families in the PICU and the value of structured tools to support information sharing and continuity of care, also during care transitions [31, 32, 33]. Similarly, Jiménez et al. [33] reported that poor familiarity with a child's baseline health status and uncertainty regarding long‐term care plans amplify family stress and worry about the care quality. The FB scaffolds family participation by making involvement in daily care explicit and visible, which can empower and engage families. This capacity maps onto the four key dimensions of family participation identified by Hill et al. [34]: parents as experts, modes of participation, the influence of the care environment and professionals and rounds as a participatory forum and suggests that the FB can facilitate each domain [35].

Nevertheless, our observations revealed a predominance of Fitness‐related goals. This pattern raises questions about the extent to which a truly holistic approach including all F‐Words is both desirable and feasible in a critical care setting. Acute care priorities, workflow constraints and perceived relevance bias use towards immediate clinical aims, potentially crowding out broader social, developmental and emotional domains.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this work lies in its co‐creation approach, which actively involved all stakeholder groups in both the innovation development and implementation. Methodological triangulation further strengthened the study by complementing quantitative measures with qualitative insights, thereby providing robust and contextualised findings. This study has several limitations. First, it was a single‐site study which may restrict the transferability of findings to other paediatric critical care settings. Second, doctors were underrepresented among respondents, meaning that self‐reported outcomes reflect the perspectives of families, nurses and social professionals. Third, the number of focus groups and interviews conducted with professionals was determined pragmatically rather than based on data saturation and therefore did not fully meet the methodological standards of qualitative research. However, these data were intended to complement the quantitative findings, and no additional themes were generated.

Looking ahead, digitalisation and integration of the FB with the electronic patient record could further enhance acceptability among innovation deliverers and recipients. This would strengthen FCC by providing an up‐to‐date and comprehensive overview that encompasses all F‐Words, along with clear goal setting. In this digital or virtual format, the FB would also be accessible beyond the single‐patient room, increasing its utility when FCC rounds are held in different locations or online. Furthermore, it could serve as a valuable tool during multidisciplinary team meetings or family conferences. Such integration would enrich interprofessional collaboration by ensuring real‐time updates and optimising the FB's role in aligning care between professionals and families. Additionally, using the F‐Words as standard language, both on the FB and during rounds, would provide a consistent, family‐friendly framework for discussing child and family needs and support family participation. Thereby, it facilitates the realisation of FCC principles in daily care.

Implications for Practice and Further Research

For sustained impact, bedside family boards should be co‐created and embedded into routine workflows, with shared responsibility across the interprofessional team including families. Co‐creation with stakeholders is essential to ensure relevance, ownership and cultural fit. Flexibility in tailoring the FB to the child's developmental stage, disability and family context is key, allowing inclusion or exclusion of specific items and providing free space for personalisation, which can foster engagement and emotional connection.

Future research should evaluate the FB's effectiveness in other and across paediatric critical care settings as well as for specific populations, such as children with mental disability, chronic illnesses or traumatic injuries. Especially for older children and adolescents, the potential benefits of including Friends to capture peer relationships could be examined. Longitudinal studies could further assess its adaptability, further integration of all ICF‐domains/F‐Words as well as its long‐term contribution to FCC beyond critical care settings. Finally, digital or virtual FB formats extending beyond the patient room could be developed and assessed to further integrate FCC practices and principles into daily care.

Conclusion

The FB is an acceptable, feasible, effective and valued tool for empowering families and facilitating FCC in the PICU. It offers a structured yet personalised framework that captures the needs and preferences of children and their families, providing a shared understanding for goal setting and care planning among the interprofessional care team including family.
