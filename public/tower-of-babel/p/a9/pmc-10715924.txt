Time to Redefine Optimal Medical Therapy With Contemporary Therapies for Secondary Tricuspid Valve Regurgitation
Authors: Ali Vazir
Published: 2023

Severe secondary tricuspid valve regurgitation (sTR), also described as functional tricuspid valve regurgitation, accounts for 90% of cases of tricuspid valve regurgitation. It is predominantly attributable to dilation and dysfunction of the right ventricle or atrial dilation from a long-standing history of atrial fibrillation, leading to annular dilatation and leaflet tethering. Concurrently, sTR may exist alongside cardiac device leads, which may interfere with tricuspid valve function or have no effect. The prevalence of sTR is approximately 0.5% among the general population,1 with a rising prevalence in older age, affecting 1.5% and 4% of men and women, respectively, at >75 years of age.2 Notably, surgical interventions for isolated tricuspid valve disease are infrequent and associated with a mortality risk of 8.8%, with higher mortality observed with tricuspid valve replacement surgery compared to tricuspid valve repair. Advancements in transcatheter valve interventions for sTR have been substantial, encompassing both repair and implantation procedures. The latter may be heterotopic or orthotropic. Despite these developments, few studies on transcatheter-based tricuspid valve interventions for sTR have included a control group receiving optimal medical therapy.3 In such studies, optimal medical therapy is generally defined according to guidelines, which provide a Class 2a recommendation with Level of Evidence: C recommendation. In the most recent version of the guideline, they recommend diuretic use in cases of severe tricuspid regurgitation accompanied by heart failure (HF) signs and in patients with signs and symptoms of right-sided HF attributable to severe sTR and use therapies to treat the primary cause of right-sided HF (eg, pulmonary vasodilators to reduce elevated pulmonary artery pressures, guideline-directed medical therapy for HF with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, or rhythm control of atrial fibrillation).4 The TRILUMINATE pivotal study explored the safety and effectiveness of tricuspid valve edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) using the TriClip device (Abbott Vascular) compared to guideline-directed optimal medical therapy for treating severe tricuspid valve regurgitation in 350 patients.3 The T-TEER patients experienced symptomatic relief compared to those on optimal medical therapy, albeit without improving the 6-minute walk distance. In the optimal medical therapy cohort, 91% of patients were prescribed diuretics; 39% were prescribed angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or angiotensin receptor blocker–neprilysin inhibitors, and 66% were on beta-blockers. Notably, a subset of TRILUMINATE study patients likely met the criteria for HF with preserved ejection fraction or had chronic kidney disease and might have benefited from sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors. Nevertheless, the study did not report on the use of these medications, and at the time, SGLT-2 inhibitors were not approved for the treatment of HF with preserved ejection fraction or chronic kidney disease. Ongoing clinical trials, such as the Edwards CLASP II TR pivotal trial (NCT04097145) and the TRISCEND II pivotal trial (NCT04482062), are currently exploring treatment options for patients with severe tricuspid regurgitation. In these studies, patients are randomized to receive either optimal medical therapy, as determined by the local heart team, or to undergo transcatheter interventions. In the CLASP II TR (Edwards PASCAL Transcatheter Valve Repair System Pivotal Clinical Trial), patients receive T-TEER using the PASCAL device (Edwards Lifesciences). In contrast, in the TRISCEND II (Transfemoral Tricuspid Valve Replacement in Patients With Tricuspid Regurgitation) pivotal trial, patients are treated with tricuspid implantation using EVOQUE (Edwards Lifesciences). Thus, the current definitions of optimal medical therapy in these transcatheter tricuspid valve intervention studies appear to be vague and are dependent on the discretion and performance of the local heart team. This definition of optimal medical care for severe sTR can potentially overestimate the quality of life benefits of these interventions, which are limited by their open-label designs and lack of sham controls. Moreover, tricuspid regurgitation patients represent a heterogeneous group, with varying pathophysiological mechanisms underlying sTR. Assessing these mechanisms requires right heart catheterization data because echocardiography alone can provide unreliable and discordant estimates of pulmonary pressures in severe sTR patients.5 Approximately 50% of patients with sTR have concomitant left heart disease,1 and data suggest that 40% of patients have improvements in the severity of sTR when significant mitral valve disease is effectively treated with transcatheter-based therapies.6

Given this observation, a multidisciplinary approach to patient care is essential. The inclusion of a pulmonary hypertension specialist, alongside an HF specialist, should be considered a cornerstone of the heart team, which is already composed of an imaging specialist, cardiac surgeon and a structural interventionalist. Such a composition equips the team to effectively leverage invasive pulmonary hemodynamic data for the individualized optimization of medical therapy.

For example, a patient with severe sTR, a dilated right ventricle with reduced systolic function, preserved left ventricular systolic function, and right heart catheter data revealing combined pre- and post-capillary pulmonary arterial hypertension. For such a patient, a tailored medical regimen targeting both postcapillary (via agents such as SGLT-2 inhibitors, sacubitril/valsartan, mineralocorticoid antagonists, beta-blockers, and loop diuretic agents if needed) and precapillary (by addressing increased pulmonary vascular resistance with pulmonary vasodilators) components may be beneficial.

Nonetheless, the efficacy and safety of such targeted therapy in driving reverse remodeling of the right ventricle and tricuspid valve annulus, ameliorating sTR severity, and improving outcomes, including reduced hospitalizations and mortality, remains a topic of ongoing inquiry. The ultimate success of this approach hinges largely on the degree of irreversible damage inflicted on the right ventricle and tricuspid valve annulus because of sTR, which may limit the responsiveness to medical therapy or tricuspid valve interventions.

Future investigations should endeavor to assess the safety and effectiveness of personalized, targeted medical regimens informed by a synthesis of echocardiographic and right heart catheterization data. If demonstrated to alleviate sTR severity and improve clinical outcomes by facilitating reverse remodeling of the right ventricle and tricuspid valve annulus, such an approach holds promise for reshaping the paradigm of optimal medical therapy for sTR.
