CFD-based numerical investigation of convective heat transfer in multi-channel micro-exchangers using MWCNT–water nanofluid
Authors: G. Anjaneya, S. Sunil, M. B. Hanamantraygouda, N. K. Manjunatha, K. Ravikiran, C. Solaimuthu, H. Ramesha, S. Harish, B. P. Dileep, C. Durga Prasad, Aymen Adem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient cooling is a key factor influencing the performance of compact electronic systems, which requires advanced thermal management. Microchannel Heat Exchangers (MCHEs) are increasingly becoming popular due to their high surface area-to-volume ratio and ability to dissipate high heat flux. The current study aims to explore the performance of MCHEs using a water-based, multiwalled carbon nanotube-blended nanofluid as coolant under different channel counts and geometries. A 3D CFD model was developed in ANSYS Fluent using the finite volume method, assuming constant thermophysical properties and steady-state, laminar flow, with uniform heat flux boundary conditions. An aluminum MCHE of dimensions 45 mm × 45 mm × 7 mm was analyzed with five channel geometries (square, circular, sawtooth, cross, and curved sawtooth) at three channel counts (5, 8, and 11). Two concentrations of multi-walled carbon nanotube (MWCNT)–water nanofluid (0.1 and 0.2%) were considered. Results exhibited that the baseline configuration (Square channel, 5 channels, water as coolant) achieved a convective heat transfer coefficient of 2008.24 W/m²K, overall heat transfer coefficient (U) of 1258.75 W/m²K, and thermal effectiveness (ε) of 0.102. The optimum configuration, curved sawtooth geometry with 5 channels and 0.2% MWCNT nanofluid, yielded h = 8271 W/m²K, U = 6590 W/m²K, and ε = 0.4765. ANOVA analysis confirmed channel geometry as the most influential factor, contributing 70.6% to effectiveness and 78.2% to the overall heat transfer coefficient.

Introduction

In modern electronic devices, the biggest challenge encountered is effective thermal management. The modern devices, such as compact processors, graphics processing units and power electronics, generate high heat fluxes that need to be efficiently dissipated to ensure performance and lifespan. Further miniaturization of these devices, coupled with high processing rates, magnifies the challenge of heat dissipation. The Microchannel heat exchangers (MCHEs) are a promising solution to the challenge faced in modern electronics due to their compactness, high surface area to volume ratio and precise thermal management at higher loads1–3.

The applications of MCHEs extend to data centres, consumer electronics, electric vehicles, and avionics systems, owing to their efficient heat dissipation capabilities. As MCHEs employ forced convection to enhance heat dissipation, they are superior to heat pipes that operate passively, and their application is limited by capillary action and orientation constraints. The adaptable design of MCHE enables accurate adjustment of geometric and flow parameters4–6, making them suitable for thermal management of high-density heat flux in electronic circuits. The efficiency of MCHE is mainly influenced by three critical factors, i.e. configuration/shape and number of channels, thermophysical properties of coolant, and flow regime maintained within the system7,8.

Earlier studies have proven that channel geometry is a major factor in determining heat transfer efficiency4–6 as well as pressure drop across the device. Mushtaq et al.9 and Saleh et al.10 studied the effect of channel shape on Nusselt number. It was found that a trapezoidal triangular channel resulted in a higher Nusselt number in comparison with a rectangular section. This was attributed to the higher wetted perimeter and ability to destabilize the laminar flow structures in the case of a trapezoidal triangular channel. The findings of Barhatte et al.7 and Kalbasi et al.8 were also similar and were backed by analytical and numerical investigations. Qu and Mudawar11 demonstrated the effect of channel number to minimize peak surface temperatures and enhance thermal spreading until a point post which maldistribution of flow was noticed12,13.

Apart from varying the geometry, performance improvement is also possible by altering the coolant used10,14,15. Nanofluids are the dispersion of engineered nanoparticles in a base fluid16–19. Replacing the coolant with nanofluids results in an improvement in thermal conductivity and overall efficiency of the system20–22.

Multi-Walled Carbon Nanotubes (MWCNTs) have high stability at low volume fractions and possess very high thermal conductivity23–25. S Chatterjee et al.26 studied the effect of MWCNT-dispersed water as a coolant on heat transfer rate in microscale domains and inferred a notable increase in heat transfer. Similar studies27,28 proved an increase in heat transfer with varied concentrations of nanofluids but with a minor increase in viscosity. Studies by various scientists on microchannel heat sink (MCHS) in the recent past, with varied geometrical configurations, have resulted in enhanced thermal efficiency and stability29–31. Studies have proven that wavy microchannels can increase the heat transfer coefficient due to increased surface area to volume ratio32. Uniform temperature distribution is one of the important parameters for future generation thermal management. Fractal microchannels lower thermal resistance and promote a uniform temperature distribution33–37.

The thermal performance of MCHEs can be improved by using nanofluids as coolants. Especially, MWCNT as a dispersant in nanofluids is helpful in improving the efficiency38–42. MWCNT dispersed nanofluids exhibit a high aspect ratio and good surface interaction with the base fluid, resulting in enhanced stability and a very low possibility of residue formation under laminar flow conditions43–46. The dispersion technique is an important parameter that contributes to the stability. Ultra- sonification is a reliable method, and the usage of surfactants further supports promoting homogenous stability47–49.

The other nanoparticles, like titanium dioxide (TiO₂)50–56 and aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃)57–60, have been extensively studied in the recent past. TiO₂ particles have good stability and improvement in thermal efficiency, and Al₂O₃ nanoparticles as dispersants in coolants have resulted in reduced thermal resistance and enhanced heat flux capability. Topology optimization56–63 is a useful technique in the design of MCHEs, especially when it is intended to be used with nanofluids as coolants. Numerical optimization is an effective technique63–67 in the optimization of geometry and flow pathways of MCHEs. The combined optimization of flow geometry, pathway and Nano fluid concentration results in lower pressure drops and uniform heat distribution68,69. These studies conclude the importance of optimized geometry, pathways and nanofluid concentration in overall MCHEs performance. Compact, high-performance electronic devices can now improve heat transfer more effectively by means of microchannel heat exchangers (MCHEs). Their improved surface area-to-volume ratios and ability to regulate temperature under varying heat flux conditions make them superior to passive heat pipes.

Review of literature1–17 indicates that MCHEs leverage forced convection to boost thermal performance while minimizing pressure drops. Channel geometries—such as trapezoidal, triangular, wavy, and fractal significantly influence heat transfer rates and pressure losses. However, increasing the number of microchannels can improve the Nusselt number, but excessive channel density within a limited surface area may lead to flow maldistribution, elevated pressure drops, and higher operational and fabrication costs. Recent studies41–45 highlight the use of low-concentration multi-walled carbon nanotube (MWCNT) nanofluids as promising alternatives to conventional base fluids, offering notable improvements in thermal conductivity and convective efficiency. Recent research studies highlight the importance of a low volume fraction of CNT in nanofluids to improve the thermal properties in compact exchangers. Studies on the automotive radiator using MWCNT–Al₂O₃ hybrid nanofluid revealed 28.5% higher convective heat transfer coefficient with up to 22% improvement in effectiveness. The Nusselt number increased up to 23.7% as in comparison with plain water as a coolant. There was a nominal increase in pressure drop, which was justifiable70. Investigation on the use of MWCNT/water nanofluids in a flat tube radiator with louver fin with 0.2 to 1.6% vol concentrations resulted in up to 13.7% increase in Nusselt number in comparison with distilled water71. Studies on shell and tube heat exchangers using MWCNT/ Graphene hybrid nanofluid with water in 0.01–0.03 vol% demonstrated significant improvement in performance. The overall heat transfer coefficient was increased by up to 34%72. Comparative studies on the geometry of tubes using MWCNT/ water nanofluid revealed that the use of flat plate geometry resulted in a better heat transfer coefficient (up to 9%) in comparison with circular geometry. This study highlighted the importance of co optimizing the flow geometry for better performance73.

Many studies in the literature have focused on either nano fluid usage or geometric variation in the micro channel heat exchangers. The novelty of the current work lies in an integrated approach. In the current study, the modelling incorporates water-based MWCNT nano fluid into CFD simulations across five different channel geometries (square, circular, sawtooth, cross, and curved sawtooth) with multiple channel counts. This approach enables a comparative framework that has not previously reported. Methodologically, the current study combines CFD with statistical ANOVA analysis to quantify the relative influence of channel number, geometry, and nanofluid concentration on thermal performance.

Analytically, the current work identifies geometry as the predominant factor, which contributes to over 70% for overall heat transfer coefficient and effectiveness. This threefold novelty in modelling, methodology and analysis advances the current study of optimization of MCHEs for compact electronic cooling applications. Therefore, the current investigation explores the individual and combined effects of channel geometry, number of channels, and MWCNT–water nanofluid concentration on thermal performance of a compact aluminium MCHE for improved processor-level cooling. Simulations were performed with five-channel geometries (square), each comprising a number of channels of 5, 8, and 11 and two concentrations of MWCNT nanofluid (0.1 and 0.2% volume) on the effectiveness of heat exchange, considering a constant laminar flow regime with a mass flow rate of 0.00623 kg/s and an imposed heat flux of 143 kW/m². The selection of MWCNT as dispersant for producing a nano fluid was based on the exceptionally higher thermal conductivity of 3000 W/m·K and large aspect ratio, which promotes the heat transfer coefficient as compared with conventional spherical nanoparticles such as Al₂O₃, CuO, or TiO₂. From the literature, it is understood that the conventional metal-based nanoparticles typically enhance the overall heat transfer coefficient up to 30%, whereas MWCNT suspensions have exhibited 40 to 50% enhancement in the overall heat transfer coefficient at low volume fractions. It is also understood that the stability of MWCNT at low concentrations is better in comparison with metal based Nano particles74,75.

Geometry of MCHE

In the current study, a steady state laminar flow model is used with the selection of an inlet velocity to ensure a Reynolds number below 2000. The energy equation was enabled to capture conjugate heat transfer between aluminum walls and the coolant. For Nano fluids, a single-phase homogeneous model was adopted, treating MWCNT–water suspensions as effective fluids. A pressure-based solver with SIMPLE coupling was applied. These choices balance computational efficiency with physical accuracy. A 3D solid model of the MCHE, measuring 45 mm × 45 mm × 7 mm, was developed using CAD software. This model featured a central cutout on the top surface to represent the heat source. The core of the investigation involved varying two primary design parameters: the cross-sectional geometry of the channels and the number of channels. Five distinct channel geometries were investigated: Circular, Square, Sawtooth, Cross channel, and Curved Sawtooth, as mentioned in Table 1, along with the dimensions. Each geometry was simulated with three different channel counts: 5, 8, and 11, as shown in Figs. 1, 2 and 3, respectively (samples shown for circular geometry). A constant cross-sectional area of 7.5 mm2 is maintained for each channel across all configurations to isolate the effects of shape and number. The heat sink body was modelled as aluminium, with its thermophysical properties defined accordingly.

The channel shape was modified with the objective of increasing the perimeter without changing the same area. The idea behind doing this was to raise the wetted perimeter and hence provide for a greater area for heat transfer and hence a more uniform temperature distribution. As the perimeter increases, the effectiveness of the MCHE also increases proportionally. This happens due to the increase in wetted area, thereby increasing surface for heat transfer and ultimately bringing the maximum temperature of the solid down, which increases effectiveness.

Justification for parameter selection

The selection of five distinct geometries was done considering the fact that non-rectangular geometries demonstrated enhanced convective performance76,77. The number of channels was finalized based on the previous studies, which reported optimal spreading in the 5–8 range with diminishing results at a higher number of channels. At a high number of channels, a severe pressure drop was observed in the literature78,79. The concentration of nanoparticles was selected based on the fact that a higher concentration results in an increase in viscosity and lower settling times. Aluminum was selected for the study due to its high thermal conductivity and manufacturability. The boundary conditions were selected to ensure physical relevance and realistic operation of MCHE. The inlet velocity was selected to maintain a Reynolds number below 2000 and ensure a laminar flow regime80. The inlet temperature and heat flux were decided based on realistic electronics cooling scenarios to represent CPU and GPU operating environments. The outlet boundary condition was set to atmospheric pressure, which is widely adopted in CFD studies to replicate open discharge conditions. Collectively, the selected boundary conditions ensure the numerical model reflects practical applicability in microchannel heat exchanger design.

Mesh generation and boundary conditions

Mesh generation was done through ANSYS Meshing. A hex-dominant mesh is used in the fluid domains in order to better capture flow behavior, and a tetrahedral-dominant mesh was used for the solid aluminum body in order to capture complex geometries. High mesh density with inflation layers was used at fluid-solid interfaces and close to the heat flux area to capture steep thermal gradients and the development of boundary layers. Orthogonality angles were strictly controlled at over 20º. Inflation layers were applied at fluid-solid interfaces to resolve steep thermal gradients near the walls. Mesh independence studies were conducted to ensure solution accuracy and reliability. Meshing parameters are then defined to establish baseline resolution, followed by mesh refinement and face sizing to accurately capture boundary layer effects. The quality of the generated mesh was evaluated using key metrics such as orthogonality angle (> 20°), aspect ratio (< 100), and skewness (< 1), which are essential for ensuring numerical stability and physical fidelity. If these criteria are not met, the mesh is coarsened and regenerated. Finally, a mesh independence test is conducted to validate that simulation results are not sensitive to further mesh refinement. Mesh independence and quality controls

Hex‑dominant meshing in the fluid domain (with inflation layers) and tetrahedral meshing in the solid were used with strict quality limits (orthogonality > 20°, skewness < 1, aspect ratio < 100). A three‑level mesh‑refinement study showed that further refinement changed outlet temperature, average solid temperature, and surface heat‑transfer rate by less than 0.5%, confirming grid independence for the reported metrics (ε, h, U).

Governing equations

Fundamentally, the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy governs the fluid flow and heat transfer properties in the microchannel heat sink (MCHE). In microchannels, these concepts are quantitatively stated by the following equations under steady-state, incompressible, and laminar flow conditions.

The continuity equation ensures mass conservation in the fluid region. For an incompressible flow, in which the fluid density is constant, it is written as:

CFD analysis

ANSYS Fluent was used to run CFD simulations. Consistent with common microchannel applications, the numerical model assumes steady-state, laminar, and incompressible flow conditions. A discretized computational domain was used to solve the governing equations for energy, momentum, and mass conservation. Key thermal performance indicators, such as the fluid’s bulk mean temperature, the maximum and average solid temperatures, and the overall and convective heat transfer coefficients (U and h, respectively), were extracted from the simulation data after post-processing. These collected variables were then used to compute thermal effectiveness (ϵ).

To statistically determine the individual and interconnected contributions of channel geometry and number of channels to the overall MCHE performance, a two-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed on thermal effectiveness, heat transfer coefficient, and heat transfer.

Results and discussion

Convergence

Convergence in CFD simulations means that the numerical solution has stabilized, whereby the computed variables no longer vary much with additional iterations. This implies that the solution is a good representation of the physical system behavior. The simulations in this research were started by means of a hybrid initialization scheme. The solution was deemed to be converged whenever the residuals of the governing equations were met: 10− 6 for the energy equation and 10− 3. The condition of convergence being achieved has been shown in Fig. 6 for continuity and momentum equations. In addition to these residual goals, the stability of important output parameters, such as outlet temperature and surface heat transfer values, was also tracked. Convergence typically occurred within 100 cycles for most simulated configurations. This quick convergence, combined with the small residual tolerance and parameter stabilization, guaranteed the stability and integrity of the simulation solutions for further analysis and prediction.

Numerical convergence criteria used in the study

Residuals were monitored until reaching 10⁻⁶ for the energy equation and 10⁻³ for momentum and continuity equations, which aligns with standard CFD best practices for steady, laminar microchannel simulations. Although the continuity residual remained comparatively higher than the energy residual, we verified physical convergence through stabilization of key output quantities, including outlet temperature, maximum solid temperature, surface heat-transfer rates, and average heat-flux balance, all of which stabilized and showed no further changes with additional iterations. This dual validation numerical and physical ensures that the final solution is fully converged and reliable.

Overall heat transfer coefficient (U) and convective heat transfer coefficient (h)

A measure of thermal efficacy at the system level, the overall coefficient of heat transfer (U) considers both the fluid and solid side thermal resistances. However, the heat transfer between a fluid and a solid at a specific location is expressed by the heat transfer coefficient (h), which is a point characteristic.

Fig. 12Variation of convective heat transfer coefficient vs. perimeter across 5, 8 and 11 channels.

Variation of convective heat transfer coefficient vs. perimeter across 5, 8 and 11 channels.

Figure 12 predicts variation of convective heat transfer coefficient vs. perimeter across 5, 8 and 11 channels. For the coolant as water, higher values of h were noted for channels with more complex perimeters, like sawtooth and cross channels, because there was greater surface area and flow disturbance near the walls (Fig. 12). But when the number of channels increased, there was a decrease in the fluid flow rate per channel, it prompted the coefficients of convective heat transfer (h) values to fall along with the Reynolds number. Because of the additional thermal resistance that the solid heat sink’s body offered, the overall heat transfer coefficient (U) values were lower than the heat transfer coefficient (h). It is observed that the curved saw tooth profile exhibits the highest heat transfer coefficient in comparison with the other profiles studied. This may be attributed to the larger wetted perimeter, which in turn increases the contact area between fluid and wall. Curved saw tooth geometry enhances the mixing and flow disturbance, which in turn reduces the thermal boundary layer. Complex geometries like saw tooth reduce the thermal resistance due to strong fluid-wall interactions compared to simple profiles.

Effect of nanofluids

Thermal analysis is performed further for the curved sawtooth with the addition of MWCNT-water nanofluids of 0.1% and 0.2%. The reason is that it has shown improved effectiveness compared with other shapes. Addition of 0.1% and 0.2% MWCNT in water on a volume basis showed improved thermal performance in 5, 8, and 11 channel curved sawtooth configuration compared with water as coolant, as shown in Fig. 14. Both 0.1% and 0.2% MWCNT nanofluid concentrations showed improved convective heat transfer coefficient and overall performance of MCHE as against water as coolant. The 0.2% MWCNT-water nanofluid showed better improvement, decreasing the maximum wall temperature by more than 8 K than water in 11 channels, curved sawtooth and improving the heat transfer coefficient by more than 100% (Table 4). This improved performance is due to the higher thermal conductivity and better thermal dispersion properties of MWCNTs in the coolant.

While nanofluids overall offered improved performance, the channel number increasing trends for the MWCNT nanofluids offered complexities. Effectiveness tended to increase between 5 and 8 channels with better fluid-wall interaction, but indicated a plateau or decrease between 8 and 11 channels, especially for the 0.2% concentration (as depicted in Fig. 14). This behavior is related to the trade-off between greater heat transfer surface area and lower individual channel flow rates, which can result in greater viscosity-induced resistance to flow in denser arrangements. The total heat transfer coefficient (U) and convection heat transfer coefficient (h) also exhibited comparable trends (Table, with greater values for nanofluids but reducing levels as the number of channels rose beyond an optimal value. The thermal effectiveness increases with the number of channels; this can be attributed to increased surface area and convective interaction, which improves the heat transfer. When the channels are increased beyond 11, the reduced flow per channel lowers the Reynolds number, which in turn diminishes the convective efficiency. Nano fluids improve the thermal conductivity and dispersion, boosting effectiveness. At higher channel counts, viscosity effects introduce flow resistance, slightly reducing gains.

Discussion

Simulation studies were carried out on an aluminum MCHE of dimensions 45 mm × 45 mm × 7 mm, using ANSYS Fluent in electronic cooling applications. Five different channel shapes, Sawtooth, circle, square, curved sawtooth and cross were selected with 5, 8, and 11 channels. Two different concentrations of MWCNT nanofluid, 0.1% and 0.2%, were selected for analysis. The Effectiveness, Overall heat transfer coefficient (U), and Convective heat transfer coefficient (h) of MCHE were analyzed. Simulations were performed using CFD analysis assuming steady state laminar flow under uniform inlet temperature, mass flow rate of 0.00623 kg/s and uniform heat flux of 143,000 W/m2.

Influence of channel geometry

Five different channel shapes, Sawtooth, circle, square, curved sawtooth and cross were selected for the study. Amongst them Curved Sawtooth profile showed the best thermal performance. The Curved Sawtooth profile, having an area of 7.5 mm2 and a perimeter of 19.89 mm, enabled enhanced thermal distribution, which resulted in higher overall heat transfer coefficients and reduced peak temperature. Application of MWCNT-water nanofluids

The addition of 0.1% and 0.2% MWCNT-based nanofluids significantly improved the convective heat transfer coefficient and overall MCHE performance. The addition of 0.2% MWCNT nanoparticles in water as a coolant resulted in the highest improvement in performance. The peak wall temperature was reduced by more than 8 K in comparison with water, and the heat transfer coefficient was enhanced by more than 100%. Considering a five-channel setup, the heat transfer coefficient in the case of water, 0.1% MWCNT and 0.2% MWCNT were 1258.75 W/m2K, 3544 W/m2K and 6590 W/m2K, respectively. The performance improvement may be attributed to increased thermal conductivity and better dispersion ability of MWCNT.

Statistical analysis (ANOVA)

Two-way ANOVA analysis using water as a coolant indicated the channel geometry as a prime factor that impacts the performance of MCHE. The ANOVA results represented a variance of 70.6% in thermal effectiveness, 50.8% in the convective heat transfer coefficient and 78.1% in the overall heat transfer coefficient. The number of channels is minor yet crucial in enhancing the performance by up to 26.8% of performance and 49.1% of the convective heat transfer coefficient. The overall heat transfer coefficient contributes to 10.2%, which proves the significance of optimizing the channel geometry for improving the performance of MCHE. The top-performing configuration was found to be a curved sawtooth with eight channels using 0.2% MWCNT nanofluid as coolant. This configuration had the highest heat removal efficiency, best convective performance, and most even thermal distribution. It can be concluded that channel geometry is the top priority in increasing the performance of MCHE, followed by the number of channels and coolant type. Significant performance improvements can be achieved through careful design, even with small geometries and relatively low nanofluid concentrations.

Practical and industrial applicability

The study advances beyond traditional water-cooled microchannel heat exchangers by demonstrating that geometry optimization combined with low-concentration nanofluids yields substantially higher thermal performance. Specifically, the curved sawtooth geometry with 0.2% MWCNT nanofluid achieved a significant increase in overall heat transfer coefficient compared to the baseline water-cooled square channel. This represents a clear innovation over conventional designs, where improvements are typically sought by simply increasing channel density or flow rate. By quantifying the relative contributions of geometry, channel count, and nanofluid concentration through ANOVA analysis, the study provides design guidance rooted in evidence, showing that geometry optimization offers the greatest engineering leverage.

The results are directly relevant to electronics cooling, EV power electronics, and compact heat sink applications, where high heat flux removal is critical. Because the study uses aluminum substrates, laminar flow regimes, and nanofluid concentrations below.

0.3% all consistent with manufacturable and stable operating conditions. The main steps required before industrial adoption include experimental validation under transient loads, long-term nanofluid stability testing, and pressure drop versus pumping power analysis. With these refinements, the optimized geometries and nanofluid formulations can be translated into a commercial cooling module.
