Rapid replacement of blaKPC variant in ST11 carbapenem-resistant and hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae contributed to ceftazidime/avibactam resistance during severe in vivo infection
Authors: Ping Yang, Chao Liu, Jiajia Zheng, Juan Yi, Zhenchao Wu, Yun Tian, Pengcheng Du, Ming Lu, Ning Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesHypervirulent ceftazidime/avibactam (CAZ/AVI)-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (Kp) has emerged; however, its dynamic within-host evolution and competitive features are uncharacterized. This study aimed to clarify the systematic microevolution characteristics of the rapid transformation of blaKPC variants during long-term infection.MethodsThirty-nine Kp strains were isolated from a single patient with severe recurrent osteomyelitis during a 2-year period. Whole-genome sequencing and in vitro evolution assay was performed. Microbiological characteristics were examined through antimicrobial susceptibility testing, plasmid stability, growth curve, in vitro competition and Galleria mellonella larvae lethality assays.ResultsAmong all the clinical Kp isolates, 37 were carbapenem-resistant Kp (CRKP), including 25 CAZ-/AVI-resistant Kp. All isolates belonged to the ST11-K47. During in vivo evolution, the blaKPC variant and its amplification emerged. Twenty-four isolates (24/39, 61.5%) harboured a novel blaKPC variant, blaKPC-144. All five Kp isolates carried blaKPC-2 in 2021. Surprisingly, 24 blaKPC-144-harbouring isolates (70.6%, 24/34) and 10 blaKPC-2-harboring isolates were identified in 2023, indicating rapid changing of blaKPC. Kp4 carried two copies of blaKPC-2, and Kp10-1 exhibited a 1.94-fold increase in the blaKPC-144 copy number. Similarly, in vitro, the blaKPC copy number increased upon exposure to low CAZ/AVI concentrations. However, at higher concentrations (4/1 mg/L), the blaKPC copy number increased significantly, and blaKPC mutations emerged simultaneously. The competition assay indicated that the blaKPC-144-harboring isolates exhibited a superior competitive capacity.ConclusionsThe blaKPC amplification and mutation emerged simultaneously or sequentially during in vivo and in vitro evolution. Kp isolates harbouring blaKPC-144, conferring resistance to CAZ/AVI, exhibited a competitive advantage, promoting the rapid replacement of blaKPC-2.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (Kp) is an important community-acquired and nosocomial Gram-negative bacterial pathogen that can cause fatal infections, including pneumonia, urinary tract infections, pyogenic liver abscesses and bacteremia.1 The emergence of carbapenem-resistant Kp (CRKP), which is resistant to the majority of antimicrobial agents, has led to a clinical dilemma, owing to the limited number of effective treatment options. Hypervirulent Kp (hvKp) is a common pathogen causing community-acquired pyogenic liver abscesses.2 The recent emergence and epidemic spread of carbapenem-resistant hypervirulent Kp (CR-hvKp) has attracted the attention of public health experts and clinicians.2–4

The main mechanism of carbapenem resistance in Kp is the production of carbapenemases, which predominantly include Class A carbapenemases (mainly KPC), Class B metallo-β-lactamases (mainly NDM) and Class D OXA-48-like serine carbapenemases.5,6 Consequently, novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, such as ceftazidime/avibactam (CAZ/AVI), have been developed and have shown promise for the treatment of CRKP infections. Clinical studies have also indicated that treatment regimens containing CAZ/AVI are more effective than traditional treatment approaches against CRKP infections.7,8 However, rapid adaptive mutation leads to treatment failure during CAZ/AVI therapy. Recently, various new blaKPC gene subtypes derived from blaKPC-2 and blaKPC-3 have been discovered.9–13 Compared with wild-type, the blaKPC variants have one or more amino acid substitutions, insertions or deletions, resulting in modifications in the amino acid sequence at the carbapenemase active site, resulting in CAZ/AVI resistance and posing new challenges for clinical treatment.14–18

The immune microenvironment and antibiotic selection pressure complicate the process of within-host evolution.19 The KPC-producing Kp strain has diversified over 4.5 years at a rate of 7.5 substitutions per genome per year, resulting in broad phenotypic modifications.20 In addition, Kp strains isolated from the same patient presented heteroresistance to tigecycline, possibly because of differential expression due to dynamic genetic heterogeneity.21

However, the systematic microevolution characteristics of the rapid transformation of blaKPC variants during long-term infection are currently unclear. In this study, blaKPC variant and its amplification were found to emerge individually or concurrently under CAZ/AVI selection pressure both in vivo and in vitro. Importantly, Kp isolates carrying blaKPC-144, conferring CAZ/AVI resistance, rapidly replaced blaKPC-2-harboring Kp isolates during the 2-year evolution.

Materials and methods

Patient and Kp strains

A total of 39 Kp strains were isolated from the wound drainage fluid of a 38-year-old male patient with severe traumatic osteomyelitis. On 17 February 2021, the male patient had a traffic accident and underwent surgery at an external hospital. The patient was transferred to our hospital on the fifth postoperative day due to a suspected infection in the right lower extremity. From March to June 2021, the patient underwent a series of 10 surgical interventions, after which his condition improved and he was discharged home. The patient was subsequently readmitted to our hospital due to a prolonged infection. He was treated with tigecycline and CAZ/AVI in 2021 and gradually improved. In February 2023, recurrent osteomyelitis emerged, and the causative pathogen was again identified as Kp. The patient received CAZ/AVI separately or in combination for 10 days, isepamicin for 10 days and vancomycin for 8 days (Figure 1a). Additionally, despite many active drainages and debridements, the patient’s condition did not improve. Ultimately, the patient withdrew from treatment.

(a) Medical history of the patient; (b) Phylogenetic relationships and genomic characteristics of the clinical K. pneumoniae isolates.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed with the VITEK-2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France). The antimicrobial agents included ticarcillin/clavulanic acid, piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefoperazone/sulbactam, cefepime, aztreonam, amikacin, tobramycin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, doxycycline, minocycline and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. Moreover, the MICs of ceftazidime/avibactam, meropenem, imipenem, ertapenem, ceftriaxone, cefiderocol, tigecycline and rifampin were assessed via the Etest method. The breakpoints were interpreted according to the CLSI guidelines, and the breakpoints for tigecycline were based on the EUCAST recommendation (http://www.eucast.org/).

WGS and bioinformatic analysis

DNA was extracted and sequenced with the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end, 150 bp) and the QNome-3841hex nanopore sequencing platform (QitanTech, China). To assemble the complete genomes, two strategies were employed to combine highly accurate short reads and long reads: processing with Raven v1.8.1 followed by polishing with Polypolish v0.5.0 and de novo assembly with Unicycler v0.4.4. Sequence types (STs), serotypes, resistance genes, virulence genes, insertion sequences (ISs) and plasmid replicon types were determined as previously described.22 HvKp was defined as the presence of peg-344, iroB, iucA, rmpA or rmpA2 positivity as described previously.18

For phylogenetic analysis, the Kp HS11286 (accession no. NC_016845) was used as the reference as previously described.23 In brief, the single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of each isolate were compared, and the polymorphic sites among all the isolates were combined. The high-quality SNPs were retained, and the recombination sites were removed. Finally, the concatenated sequences of the filtered polymorphic sites were identified to construct the phylogenetic tree via the maximum likelihood method.

Quantitative PCR analysis

Kp DNA and total RNA were extracted with the Magnetic Universal Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, China) and the RNAprep Pure Cell/Bacteria Kit (Tiangen, China), respectively. For analysis of qPCR data, the 2−ΔΔCT method was used for the relative quantitation of blaKPC gene expression normalized to that of a plasmid replication protein gene (rep) and for relative quantitation of the plasmid copy number (rep) normalized to the single-copy housekeeping gene rpoB. In addition, the relative expression of blaKPC was calculated via reverse transcription–quantitative (qPCR) (RT-qPCR; the primers are listed in Table S2 (available as Supplementary data at JAC Online)). The mean CT value was calculated from triplicate reactions, and the data are presented as the means ± SDs.

Growth curve assay

LB broth and M9 minimal medium were used as culture media. A bacterial suspension of 0.5 McF was prepared, and then 10 μL was added per well of a 96-well plate in triplicate. Then, 190 μL of culture media was added to each well, and the cells were grown at 37°C. Finally, the OD590 nm was recorded with a Tecan Infinite 200Pro microplate reader hourly for 12 h, and the growth curve was plotted by GraphPad Prism v8.

Competition assay

Strains carrying different blaKPC variants and blaKPC-negative isolates were used for the evaluation of the competitive capacity. Three mixtures were tested: Kp6-2/Kp1-1 (blaKPC-144/blaKPC-2), Kp6-2/Kp20 (blaKPC-144/lacking blaKPC) and Kp1-1/Kp20 (blaKPC-2/lacking blaKPC). Each mixture was prepared at a 1:1 ratio and incubated overnight. The three mixtures were subsequently inoculated into LB broth at a 1:100 ratio and subcultured for three consecutive days. Colony counts on MH agar with or without antibiotics (16/4 mg/L CAZ/AVI for Kp6-2/Kp1-1 and Kp6-2/Kp20; 8 mg/L meropenem for Kp1-1/Kp20) were performed via dilution plating every 24 h to calculate the competitive index (CI).

Plasmid stability

The strains Kp6-2 (blaKPC-144) and Kp1-1 (blaKPC-2) were passaged continuously in LB broth for 10 days. At 24-h intervals, 100 µL of bacterial suspension was withdrawn, diluted and spread onto MH agar plates with and without antibiotics for enumeration. In brief, Kp6-2 was selected with 16/4 mg/L CAZ/AVI, and Kp1-1 was selected with 8 mg/L meropenem. The plasmid retention rate was estimated by dividing the colony counts on the antibiotic-containing plates by those on the antibiotic-free plates.

Transformation assay

To assess the relationship between the CAZ/AVI MIC and the presence of blaKPC-144, a transformation assay was performed. In brief, the blaKPC-144 gene was amplified via PCR and then ligated into the pCR2.1 vector. Chemical transformation was performed by using Escherichia coli DH5α as the recipient strain, and transformants were selected on MH agar plates containing 50 mg/L kanamycin. Subsequently, recombinant plasmid DNAs were extracted from E. coli DH5α transformants and electroporated into the CAZ/AVI-susceptible recipient K. pneumoniae 1524 (CAZ/AVI MIC = 0.19 mg/L). Sanger sequencing was conducted to confirm successful transformation. The antimicrobial susceptibility of the recipients to CAZ/AVI and meropenem was determined.

Galleria mellonella larvae lethality assay

The G. mellonella larvae lethality assay was performed as described previously.24,25 In brief, a bacterial concentration of 1 × 106 cfu/mL was prepared, and 10 μL of this solution was injected into 10 larvae per group. The injected larvae were incubated at 37°C, and mortality was observed every 12 h for 72 h.

In vitro evolution assay

The index CAZ/AVI-susceptible isolate (MIC = 2 mg/L), Kp1-1, which carries one copy of blaKPC-2, was selected for an evolution assay under CAZ/AVI pressure in vitro. This strain was inoculated into 5 mL of fresh LB broth containing varying concentrations of CAZ/AVI (1/0.25, 2/0.5 and 4/1 mg/L) and passaged for 3 days. Later, serial passaging under CAZ/AVI-free conditions was performed for 10 days.

Results

Genomic features of clinical Kp isolates derived from one clone

All 39 isolates belonged to the ST11-K47 and carried the IncFIB and IncFII plasmids. Seventeen strains harboured the Col156 plasmid replicon, and 30 strains harboured ColRNAI (Figure 1b). Notably, all the blaKPC-carrying plasmids were identified as IncFII replicon type, and all the virulence plasmids were IncFIB replicon type. Thirty-four strains carried blaKPC plasmids with similar sizes and structures (73–75 kb), whereas pKp10-1-111, pKp20-56 and pKp27-1-55 showed variations in size (111, 56 and 55 kb, respectively). Compared to pKp1-1-73 (from the first isolate Kp1-1), pKp10-1-111 harboured a dual copy of blaKPC-144 and presented additional 38 kb segment carrying resistance genes fosA3, blaTEM-1B, rmtB and blaKPC-144. pKp20-56 had a 17 kb blaKPC-carrying segment missing. pKp27-1-55 lacked resistance genes fosA3, blaTEM-1B and rmtB, presenting only blaKPC-144 (Figure S1). The virulence plasmids varied between 211 and 265 kb in size, with similar structural configurations. All the virulence plasmids carried the same virulence genes aerobactin-related genes (iutA-iucABCD), peg-589 and rmpA2 (Figure S2).

For blaKPC-carrying plasmid (pKp1-1-73) and virulence plasmid (pKp1-1-256) in this study, we searched for the similar plasmids in the online NCBI GenBank database. We found that pKp1-1-73 showed high similarity to other blaKPC-2-carrying plasmids, such as pKPC_KP0714 (accession no. CP128193) and pKP1034 (accession no. KP893385) (Figure S3). Additionally, pKp1-1-256 was highly similar with plasmid pVir_KP0714 (accession no. CP128192) and pKP13-Vir_OXA (accession no. CP154634) (Figure S4).

Additionally, all 39 isolates carried the resistance genes mphA, dfrA1, catB3, arr-3, sul1 and tet(A). Thirty-five carried aph(3’)-Ia and 36 harboured fosA3, and 38 harboured rmtB and qnrS1. Importantly, all the strains were identified as hvKp except for Kp25-2, which lacked iutA-iucABCD and rmpA2. One strain (Kp20) lacked yersiniabactin cluster (irp1-ybtUTE-fyuA) (Figure 1b).

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the 39 isolates were divided into three clades that differed by only 14 SNPs, indicating that these isolates originated from the same ancestral clone. Notably, three main mutations were detected in the chromosome genes gcvP, dgcM and potF during evolution. Specifically, the nonsynonymous point mutation G416426A was identified in the gcvP gene in Clade 1 isolates. Clade 2 isolates harboured the intergenic mutation C4043243T in the dgcM gene. Additionally, Clade 3 isolates harboured the nonsynonymous mutation T3060447A in the potF gene (Figure 1b and Table S3). Notably, in 2021, five isolates harboured variations in one to three SNPs. In 2023, 27 isolates exhibited three SNPs, and the chromosome was more stable than in 2021 (Figure S5).

The emergence and expression of various blaKPC subtypes contributed to changes in CAZ/AVI resistance

Notably, 25 CAZ/AVI-resistant strains exhibited different MICs ranging from 16 to >256 mg/L. WGS analysis revealed that 24 isolates (24/39, 61.5%) harboured a novel blaKPC variant, blaKPC-144, which differed from blaKPC-2 by a single-nucleotide substitution at Position 172 (A172T, alanine to threonine). Interestingly, 13 isolates carried blaKPC-2 (13/39, 33.3%), and two isolates lacked blaKPC (2/39, 5.1%), indicating rapid changes in blaKPC during severe in vivo infection.

qPCR analysis revealed that the increased expression of blaKPC-2 contributed to CAZ/AVI resistance (MIC = 32) in one blaKPC-2-harboring isolate (Kp19). In addition, the blaKPC-144 expression levels of Kp10-1, Kp6-2, Kp13 and Kp27-2 (CAZ/AVI MIC = 64 mg/L, 96, 192 and 256 mg/L, respectively) were significantly higher than that of Kp-9 (CAZ/AVI MIC = 32 mg/L) (Figure 2f and Table S1). These results indicated that the variability in the MIC of CAZ/AVI among clinical strains may be attributed to differences in the expression of the blaKPC gene (Figure 2a–f).

Rapid transformation of blaKPC amplification and mutation during in vivo and in vitro evolution

Among the 39 clinical Kp isolates identified during the period of in vivo evolution, blaKPC variants and their amplification emerged. Kp4 carried two copies of blaKPC-2, one located on the chromosome and the other on the plasmid, as confirmed by WGS and qPCR analysis. Notably, isolate Kp10-1 presented a 1.94-fold increase in the copy number of blaKPC-144 (Figure 2d).

We used three different concentrations of CAZ/AVI to evaluate the evolution of the index strain in vitro. The copy number of blaKPC increased 2.2-, 2.4- and 2.5-fold on Days 1, 2 and 3 under 1/0.25 mg/L CAZ/AVI pressure, respectively (Figure 2g and Figure S6). Furthermore, the plasmid copy number did not change greatly (Figure 2h). Interestingly, the MIC of CAZ/AVI increased to 12 mg/L on Day 1 without CAZ/AVI pressure (Table 1). Although the copy number of blaKPC did not increase significantly, the expression level of blaKPC was markedly elevated, by 3.2-fold, on Day 1 without CAZ/AVI pressure (Figure 2i). The expression level of blaKPC subsequently decreased under CAZ/AVI-free conditions.

Under 2/0.5 mg/L CAZ/AVI selection pressure, increases of 2.2-, 3.0- and 2.6-fold in the copy number of blaKPC were also noted on Days 1, 2 and 3, respectively, and the increase was sustained under CAZ/AVI-free conditions (Figure 2j and Figure S7). Similarly, the plasmid copy number did not change greatly (Figure 2k), and the expression level of blaKPC corresponded to the MIC of CAZ/AVI (Figure 2l and Table 1).

Under 4/1 mg/L CAZ/AVI selection pressure, a significant increase in the blaKPC copy number occurred, and various random blaKPC mutations emerged simultaneously (Figure 2m, Figure S8 and Table 1). Significant increases in the MIC of CAZ/AVI (32, 32 and 48 mg/L) were detected on Days 1, 2 and 3, respectively (Table 1). In addition, 1.5-fold and 1.7-fold increases in plasmid copy numbers were observed on Days 2 and 3, respectively (Figure 2n). Moreover, the MIC of CAZ/AVI was decreased on Days 1, 5 and 10 without CAZ/AVI pressure (24, 16 and 12 mg/L, respectively). Notably, the relatively high level of CAZ/AVI resistance conferred by increased blaKPC expression and blaKPC mutation decreased slowly without CAZ/AVI pressure (Figure 2, Figure S8 and Table 1).

The superior competitive capacity of blaKPC-144 promoted the rapid replacement of blaKPC-2

Among all the clinical Kp isolates, most carried blaKPC-144. All five Kp isolates carried blaKPC-2 in 2021. Surprisingly, 24 blaKPC-144-harboring isolates (70.6%, 24/34) and 10 blaKPC-2-harbouring isolates were identified in 2023, suggesting a general trend of transformation from blaKPC-2 to blaKPC-144.

We performed a growth assay to clarify the fitness cost of nine isolates possessing different blaKPC subtypes and SNPs (Table S4). Interestingly, the growth curves revealed no significant difference among these isolates in LB broth, indicating no significant fitness cost in blaKPC-144-carrying isolates (Figure 3a). However, in M9 minimal medium, strain Kp4 and Kp13 grew significantly slower (Figure S9). Additionally, the results of the competition assay indicated that Kp6-2 (carrying blaKPC-144) exhibited a distinct competitive advantage over Kp1-1 (carrying blaKPC-2) and Kp20 (lacking blaKPC). In contrast, Kp1-1 (carrying blaKPC-2) exhibited a weaker competitive edge than did Kp20 (lacking blaKPC) (Figure 3b). These results indicated that the blaKPC-144-harboring isolates exhibited superior adaptability and competitive advantages in vivo and in vitro.

In addition, the results of the plasmid stability assay indicated that after 10 days of passage, both the plasmid carrying blaKPC-144 and the plasmid harbouring blaKPC-2 remained stable (retention rate > 80%) (Figure 3c). Notably, the blaKPC-144 gene could be successfully transformed into E. coli DH5α (CAZ/AVI MIC = 8 mg/L; meropenem MIC = 0.5 mg/L), suggesting that blaKPC-144 could lead to an elevation of the MIC for CAZ/AVI. Importantly, the blaKPC-144 gene could be successfully transformed into K. pneumoniae 1524 (CAZ/AVI MIC = 16 mg/L; meropenem MIC = 1 mg/L), indicating that blaKPC-144 could confer resistance to CAZ/AVI.

The results in the G. mellonella infection model revealed that strains Kp6-2 (blaKPC-144), Kp1-1 (blaKPC-2) and Kp20 (lacking blaKPC) exhibited similar virulence levels, which was significantly lower than that of the hypervirulent-positive control strain (NTUH-K2044). Additionally, these strains exhibited greater virulence than the Kp25-2 strain (lacking both the virulence plasmid and blaKPC) and the negative control strain (Kp1302) (Figure 3d).

Discussion

To our knowledge, we are the first to confirm that an increased copy number and mutation of blaKPC can occur during both in vivo and in vitro evolution. Additionally, the blaKPC-144-positive isolates exhibited superior competitive capacity, which promoted the rapid replacement of blaKPC-2.

Gene amplification and mutation can lead to the emergence of novel antibiotic resistance characteristics. Notably, increased copy number and mutation of blaKPC can lead to CAZ/AVI resistance in vitro. The increased expression of the KPC carbapenemase can lead to increased hydrolysis of ceftazidime, as avibactam may not be able to completely inhibit relatively high amount of KPC.26,27 Additionally, a previous study revealed that blaKPC mutation was the dominant mechanism of CAZ/AVI resistance and that combined increases in the gene expression and copy number of blaKPC could lead to a higher level MIC of CAZ/AVI.28 Similarly, Zhang et al.29 reported that blaKPC-2 point mutation and high KPC expression are key factors contributing to CAZ/AVI resistance. In addition, increased gene expression of blaKPC-51 contributes to high-level CAZ/AVI resistance.28 A previous study revealed that in vitro, the blaKPC-2 multicopy-containing population was increased among the original single-copy blaKPC-2-harboring population under ceftazidime, meropenem or moxalactam selection, generating a low-level CAZ/AVI resistance phenotype. Moreover, blaKPC-2 mutants increased in the blaKPC-2 multicopy-containing population, generating high-level CAZ/AVI resistance and reducing cefiderocol susceptibility.30 Interestingly, our results indicated that during in vitro and in vivo evolution, increases in blaKPC copy number and expression and mutation of blaKPC emerged, contributing to CAZ/AVI resistance. Additionally, the results of the evolution assay indicated that blaKPC mutations occurred randomly in this study, suggesting that real-time phenotypic and genomic surveillance is essential in clinical practice. Fortunately, the amplification and mutation of resistance genes and the resistance phenotype were reversible under CAZ/AVI-free conditions.

In this study, blaKPC-144 replaced blaKPC-2 as the dominant variant within the patient, especially when the host was not exposed to antibiotics. Notably, strains harbouring blaKPC-144 did not exhibit a significant fitness cost, and the competitive growth assay confirmed that blaKPC-144-harboring Kp isolates exhibited a competitive advantage. This finding was consistent with a previous study, which reported that fitness costs accompanied by CAZ/AVI resistance acquisition were compensated rapidly and that bacterial virulence was almost completely restored.31 In general, resistance acquisition is accompanied by reductions in bacterial fitness and virulence.32 Xu et al.33 reported the within-host acquisition of CAZ/AVI resistance and virulence attenuation in ST11 CRKP during CAZ/AVI treatment. However, our data revealed that isolates carrying blaKPC-144 or blaKPC-2 or lacking blaKPC presented similar virulence levels, suggesting no significant change in virulence during in vivo evolution.

This study revealed that minor variations in SNPs presented within the Kp chromosome under antibiotics selective pressure and host factors from 2021 to February 2023, whereas the chromosome presented more stable when the patient unexposed to antibiotics (March–May 2023). Importantly, two main chromosomal nonsynonymous mutations were identified in the clinical isolates. Mutation of the glycine-associated gene gcvP might be linked to tigecycline and minocycline resistance,34,35 but further research is needed. Additionally, the potF mutation may be associated with solute sensing and nutrient uptake.36,37 The potential impacts and mechanisms of chromosomal mutations need further investigation.

In addition, the acquisition of CAZ/AVI resistance in blaKPC variants is tightly correlated with the restoration of carbapenem susceptibility.38–40 However, Patricia et al. reported that the presence of two copies of blaKPC-2-Tn4401b and the D179Y mutation were not associated with loss of resistance to carbapenems.41 A previous study revealed that the simultaneous presence of porin alterations resulted in carbapenem resistance in KPC-109 producers.12 Our results showed that the blaKPC-144-positive Kp clinical isolates did not exhibit a trade-off in carbapenem resistance, which needs further investigation. These strains might be reservoirs of CR-hvKp for severe infection and further spread, highlighting the need for improved surveillance. The main limitation of our study is that the relative contribution of plasmid copy number versus blaKPC allele to the phenotype needs further studies. Additionally, further investigations are needed to identify whether other factors such as T6SS activity could influence the competitive growth of strains besides different blaKPC subtypes and the potential mechanisms.

In summary, blaKPC amplification and mutation emerged simultaneously or sequentially during in vivo and in vitro evolution. The Kp isolates harbouring blaKPC-144, conferring resistance to CAZ/AVI, exhibited rapid adaptability and a competitive advantage during in vivo evolution, suggesting that real-time genomic surveillance is essential for the appropriate application of antibiotics.
