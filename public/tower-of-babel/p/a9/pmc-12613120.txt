Recent Advances in Hydrate-Based CO2 Capture: Energy Consumption, Cost Analysis, Policy Perspectives, and Machine Learning-Driven Simulation of CO2 Hydrate Formation
Authors: Manjusha Anipeddi, Bhajan Lal, Baldeep Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gas hydrate-based carbon capture and storage (CCUS) technologies represent a promising approach to mitigating climate change by efficiently capturing and storing carbon dioxide (CO2). This review examines the technical and economic aspects of gas hydrate-based CCUS, including the latest developments related to CO2 hydrate formation studies. With special attention to flue gas decarbonization, this paper discusses the challenges faced and the focus areas that need improvement in advancing and scaling up hydrate-based CCUS. The review also provides a systematic approach to understanding the techno-economic aspects of CCUS plants and concisely discusses the technical opportunities and the associated cost dynamics within various CCUS technologies. The review also discusses the integration of hydrate-based capture with broader decarbonization strategies, underscoring the critical role of supportive policies and technological advancements in scaling up these solutions for global impact.

Introduction

Carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) are vital strategies for mitigating carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions and combating climate change. Gas hydrate-based carbon capture is a promising approach within the CCUS framework, offering a potentially efficient and long-term storage solution for captured CO2. In the context of CCUS, gas hydrate-based carbon capture involves the formation of CO2 hydrates, where CO2 molecules are trapped within a lattice of water molecules under high pressure and low temperatures. − One of the key advantages of gas hydrate-based carbon capture within the CCUS framework is its potential for long-term storage. Once the CO2 is captured and converted into hydrates, it can remain stable for extended periods, reducing the risk of leakage of the CO2 into the atmosphere.

Techno-economic analysis (TEA) is essential for evaluating gas hydrate-based CO2 capture technologies, focusing on their technical potential and economic feasibility within the broader context of carbon capture and utilization (CCU). This evaluation method meticulously examines both the attainable benefits and the challenges associated with these technologies, offering a systematic approach to assessing their commercial viability. Gas hydrate-based CO2 capture involves complex processes that have garnered significant interest owing to their climate change mitigation potential. The technical assessment within TEA explores the operational intricacies and efficiency of various CCUS technologies, evaluates the technical readiness, and identifies the areas requiring research and development. Economic assessment complements this by analyzing costs associated with implementation, potential revenue streams, and overall economic sustainability. − The development of gas hydrate technologies for CO2 capture is at various stages, with a few advancing toward demonstration phases. This progression necessitates substantial investment, emphasizing the importance of TEA to guide stakeholders in making informed decisions on funding allocations. The commercial trajectory of these technologies is critically dependent on their ability to offer competitive advantages over conventional methods, both in terms of cost and environmental impact.

The environmental aspect of CCUS technologies is often scrutinized through life cycle assessments (LCA), which quantify the environmental impacts throughout the lifecycle of the technology. This includes the energy required for CO2 conversion and any ancillary processes, such as the preparation stages in hydrate formation. These assessments help in understanding the broader environmental implications of deploying such technologies at scale. Despite the promising outlook, hydrate-based CCUS possesses challenges that need to be addressed to attain commercial success. Many gas hydrate-based CO2 capture technologies require significant energy inputs and involve high operational pressures, leading to elevated capital and operational costs. Additionally, the scale of CO2 that can be processed through these methods is relatively small when compared to the global CO2 emissions, which may limit their impact on a global scale unless deployed extensively. − TEA provides a structured framework for evaluating gas hydrate-based CO2 capture technologies, offering insights into both their technical potential and economic viability. This comprehensive analysis is crucial for advancing these technologies beyond the laboratory and pilot stages to full-scale industrial applications, thereby contributing to global efforts in mitigating climate change through innovative CCU strategies.

Process of Hydrate-Based Carbon Capture

Gas hydrate nucleation is a sophisticated microscopic process that begins with the initial aggregation of hydrate nuclei and continues with their expansion to a critical size that supports sustained growth. This complex sequence is influenced by various thermodynamic and kinetic factors and is characterized by stochastic and time-dependent properties.

A critical metric in assessing hydrate nucleation is the induction time, which measures the period from the onset of gas–water cluster formation to the point where these clusters stabilize into a critical size. Induction time is a vital indicator of the occurrence of nucleation and is subject to influence by several variables, including chemical composition, system configuration, and mixing conditions. − The shorter induction time suggests more favorable thermodynamic and kinetic conditions for rapid hydrate formation.

The stochastic nature of hydrate formation is attributed to the system’s metastability, defined as the ability of a nonequilibrium state to persist in a seemingly stable condition over prolonged durations. This metastability is crucial in hydrate nucleation, as it allows for an understanding of the variability and unpredictability in the timing and rate of nucleation events.

In the nucleation phase, water molecules assemble around guest atoms or molecules to form either incomplete or complete crystalline embryos. These embryonic formations are dynamic, continuously undergoing formation and dissolution due to localized fluctuations in mass, temperature, and pressure. This introduces a degree of stochasticity that is dependent on free energy, rendering the nucleation process fundamentally probabilistic. −

Upon attaining a critical size, the hydrate nuclei are capable of surmounting the associated free energy barrier, thus marking a pivotal transition to further growth or aggregation. This overcoming of the energy barrier is a crucial juncture in the nucleation phase.

Several factors significantly influence this phase transition. Among these, the critical nucleus size denotes the minimum size at which a hydrate nucleus becomes thermodynamically stable and can facilitate further growth. The energy barrier represents a thermodynamic threshold that the system must surpass to transition from a metastable state to a more stable configuration. − The driving force for nucleation is often linked to the chemical potential difference between phases, which may be altered by gradients in temperature, pressure, and composition, further affecting the dynamics of hydrate nucleation.

Structure of Gas Hydrates

Gas hydrates, in general, form three types of structures. These include sI, sII, and sH structures, as can be seen in Figure . The type of structure hydrate forms depends on the type of molecule, size, and shape of the gas molecule, which is known as the guest molecule that can fit into the cage that is formed by the host molecule, i.e., water molecule. The size and shape of the cavity of the hydrate in the cages help to differentiate structures, whereas the shape and type of gas molecules accommodated by water show the type of hydrate formed.

Hydrate crystal unit structures: sI, sII, and sH. Adapted with permission from Sloan. Copyright 1998, American Chemical Society.

Structure I is formed with guest molecules having diameters ranging from 4.2–6 Å, such as methane, ethane, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen sulfide. CO2 typically forms sI hydrates, characterized by high stability and relatively straightforward formation compared to other hydrate structures. Smaller molecules, such as hydrogen, with a diameter less than 4.2 Å, form structure II as single guests. Larger single guest molecules like propane or iso-butane form structure II with a diameter ranging between 6 Å < d < 7 Å. Furthermore, molecules within the range having diameters 7 Å < d < 8 Å form structure H in the presence of small molecules such as methane, hydrogen sulfide, or nitrogen.

Gas Hydrate Nucleation Process

Gas hydrate nucleation process is a microscopic phenomenon that refers to the conformation and growth of hydrate nuclei into a critical size for additional growth. It is characterized by the determination of induction time (the time at which nucleation occurs, which includes the formation of gas–water clusters and their growth into stable bases with a critical size). Hydrate nucleation is a stochastic process and time-dependent that can last from hours to days depending on the composition, outfit, and mixing conditions, etc. This stochastic nature of hydrate formation results from the degree of metastability (the capability of a nonequilibrium state to persist for a long time). During hydrate nucleation, water particles gather around the guest atoms, forming incomplete or complete crystal embryos. These embryos form and shrink continuously due to changes in local mass, temperature, and pressure. This medium makes hydrate nucleation a free-energy-dependent and stochastically arbitrary process. Upon attaining a critical size, the hydrate cores overcome the free energy barrier and move to the succeeding stage, which involves additional growth of the nuclei. In the nucleation process, factors like critical size, energy barrier, and driving force are veritably important.

Gas Hydrate Growth Process

Following the completion of hydrate nucleation, hydrate growth takes place. This growth process depicts the growth of stable hydrate nuclei into solid hydrates. The growth of hydrate depends upon temperature, pressure, agitation, interfacial area, and degree of subcooling. When there is a sudden sharp drop in the pressure caused by the consumption of gas molecules, it shows hydrate formation and growth, and the constant pressure in the system depicts the completion of the hydrate formation process. In addition to this, a sudden rise in temperature of the system represents hydrate formation as it is an exothermic process, while a constant temperature marks the completion of the hydrate formation process. There are key kinetic parameters that are studied for hydrate growth, which include1.Hydrate formation rate (growth rate): quantifies how quickly hydrate crystals grow after nucleation, typically measured as gas consumption rate (mol/min) or the rate of crystal mass increase.2.Gas uptake: represents the rate and total amount of gas molecules entrapped within hydrate cages.3.Degree of subcooling (ΔT): defined as the temperature difference between equilibrium hydrate formation temperature and operating temperature; greater subcooling enhances hydrate growth kinetics.4.Activation energy (Ea): represents the energy barrier to hydrate crystal growth. It can be experimentally determined using Arrhenius-type kinetic modeling from rate data.

Gas Hydrate Promoters

Hydrate formation is a stochastic and time-dependent process. It may take seconds to hours or days for hydrate formation, depending on the mixing conditions, composition, and apparatus. This stochastic nature is because of the degree of metastability (the ability of a nonequilibrium state to persist for a long period of time) that exists during the formation process. Subsequent research has been done to improve the hydrate formation process by employing chemical methods as well as mechanical methods. The mechanical methods include enhancement of the contact area and mass transfer between gas and water by using a fixed bed crystallizer with porous media, temperature fluctuation, a stirrer, or liquid spraying by a nozzle. While the chemical methods refer to the use of certain additives (promoters) that facilitate hydrate formation at milder conditions by increasing the formation rate and improving the selectivity of hydrates. ,

Research on the CO2 hydrate formation kinetics with the use of various additives has been ongoing due to its application in carbon capture. There are two types of additives, generally termed as promoters, which include kinetic and thermodynamic promoters. Thermodynamic promoters are utilized in hydrate formation as they shift the equilibrium, which means they reduce the high pressure to lower required for hydrate formation. In contrast, kinetic promoters (commonly “surfactants” or “amino acids”) alter the interfacial properties during gas/liquid contact, due to which increased hydrate formation rates are achieved. These kinetic promoters have no effect on the phase equilibrium curve. The thermodynamic and kinetic promoters can be used together, which is termed the synergistic effect of promoters on hydrate formation. Thermodynamic promoters shift equilibrium conditions to milder temperature–pressure regimes, while kinetic promoters accelerate hydrate formation rates by improving interfacial characteristics and reducing induction times. This results in significant enhancement of hydrate formation kinetics, lowering the required pressure, improving hydrate selectivity, and reducing overall energy costs.

Thermodynamic promoters (THPs) substantially reduce the hydrate formation pressure or increase the hydrate formation temperature, delivering milder conditions for hydrate formation. There are two types of THPs. The first group are the one that consists of small molecules enwrapping the water concavities as guest atoms. , Tetrahydrofuran (THF) is a prominent hydrate-forming guest that has been studied extensively as a thermodynamic promoter for different guest gases like methane, hydrogen, and CO2 hydrates. It is a water-miscible cyclic ether that has the capability of forming a hydrate on its own by enwrapping the large cages of sII. Other considerably researched THPs include 1,4-dioxane, propylene oxide, cyclopentane, propane, neohexene, acetone, and methylcyclohexane. Still, the maximum of these chemicals is largely unpredictable and hence are delicate to use. Meanwhile, the alternate group is organic phosphonium salts and nonvolatile organic quaternary ammonium salts that served as ionic hydrate formers. These can form semiclathrate hydrates (SCHs) at ambient pressure and temperature. These are a unique group of clathrates that can form hydrate by being blended with the host cavities, replacing the water molecules.

Kinetic hydrate promoters are additives that allow a faster rate of hydrate formation by reducing the induction time, increasing the gas uptake as well as enhancing hydrate growth kinetics. The main advantages of kinetic hydrate promoters are that they do not occupy the water cages and are used at low concentrations of <10,000 ppm. These are mainly high surface materials, also called surfactants, that facilitate hydrate formation primarily by reducing the surface tension between gas and liquid. Surfactants are materials containing both hydrophobic and hydrophilic components that are capable of dissolving polar as well as nonpolar substances. They are mainly categorized into four groups depending on the moieties they contain, namely anionic, cationic, nonionic, and zwitterionic surfactants. Zwitterionic surfactants consist of both cationic and anionic centers attached to the same molecule. A widely used example is the anionic surfactant sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), which exhibits excellent kinetic promoting effects. It enhances the hydrate formation kinetics significantly compared to a pure water system. Experimental work shows that the use of an optimum concentration of SDS acts as a promoter, but an excess amount of the same can inhibit hydrate growth. SDS was found to be most effective in enhancing the rate of hydrate formation as well as reducing the induction time for CO2 hydrate formation.

Amino acids are another group of organic molecules that are found to be implicit gas hydrate kinetic promoters. These are environmentally friendly, fairly cheap, and do not support froth formation, and hence they can be applied in hydrate-based marketable operations. Amino acids contain introductory amino groups (−NH2), acidic carboxyl groups (−COOH), and organic R groups (or side chains) that are unique to each amino acid. The actions of amino acids can change for CO2 or methane hydrates. For illustration, histidine shows kinetic promotional effects on the CH4 hydrate. Still, it is reported to kinetically inhibit CO2 hydrates, indicating that the kinetic creation/inhibition effect of amino acids is dependent on the type of guest molecule. Interestingly, tryptophan and methionine can promote both CO2 and CH4 hydrates. On the other hand, some other investigators reported that certain amino acids, similar to l-methionine, l-norvaline, and l-norleucine, have a large CO2 gas storage potential in the form of hydrates with faster gas uptake, under a nonstirred configuration. l-methionine (0.5 wt %), a natural amino acid, has been used as an effective kinetic promoter to dramatically enhance CO2 uptake kinetics in CO2 hydrate conformation. The optimal performance of l-methionine was attained at a concentration of 0.2 wt %, the gravimetric capacity reached up to 356 mg g–1 in 1000 min (81 mg g–1 for the bulk water system), and t 90 was only about 15 min. It is one of the most effective promoters to enhance CO2 hydrate formation. When the concentration of l-methionine increased to 0.5 or 1.0 wt %, the capacities dropped to 346 and 321 mg g–1, respectively, but the t 90 remained to be about 15 min. In addition, the recyclability of the 0.2 wt % l-methionine system is also good.

Gas Hydrate Reactors

The most commonly utilized reactor setup for lab experimentation is a continuous stirred tank reactor for hydrate formation. They’re the formal choice for closed system, small-scale batch processes, due to the simplicity of operation. Nevertheless, stirrer-tank reactors are not recommended for large-scale hydrate formation, as numerous specialized challenges may arise during the scale-up, fabrication, and operation of this type of reactor. The use of a stirrer for agitating the gas and water phases is the first and most important issue in operating stirrer-tank reactors. Although mixing of gas and liquid phases is necessary for hydrate formation, veritably fast mixing will help the accumulation of the hydrate core, which is essential for the nucleation part. Hence, stirrer-tank reactors suffer from long induction time.

The alternate common type is bubble column reactors. Like stirrer-tank reactors, the dominant phase is liquid water, which has a high heat capacity. Thus, the heat of hydrate formation can be removed more efficiently from reactor walls, and a stable thermal profile can be maintained in this type of reactor. Likewise, the movement of bubbles in the water phase agitates the system. Yet, supplying atomic, uniform bubbles in the entire column cross section and length is not a simple task. Thus, bubble columns cannot give enough contact area for continued output of gas hydrate, unless they are equipped with a stirrer system, which has its own downsides, as mentioned earlier. Still, some hydrate reactors are designed and reported in the literature based on bubble column technology. −

The third common type of gas hydrate reactor is the spray tower. By scattering water into a continuous gas phase region, the gas/water interfacial area can readily be increased. Likewise, as the tiny water drops have a large speed in the gas phase of the reactor, the necessity of a mechanical stir system is ruled out for this type of reactor. Consequently, the design of the reactor is simple, and scaling up can be done readily.

However, as the heat capacity of the gas phase is small and a large portion of the spray tower is occupied by the gas phase, the heat of hydrate formation may not be removed throughout the reactor walls efficiently. This would easily increase the reactor temperature. By increasing the temperature, the system's supersaturation condition, which is essential for crystallization, would decrease, and this would result in decreasing the rate of hydrate formation or even stopping it. The simplicity in both fabrication and operation of any reactor is essential in developing hydrate-based technology, especially for the separation. Thus, spray towers seem to be the best option for industrial applications. However, the problem of heat transfer should be resolved for this purpose. In the hydrate-based CO2 separation system, as shown in Scheme , the process begins with flue gas (mainly CO2 entering the cooler at 140 °C and 0.101 MPa (step 1). Simultaneously, cooling water at 25 °C and 0.101 MPa flows into the cooler (step 13). The cooler reduces the flue gas temperature to 50 °C (step 2), and the gas, still at 0.101 MPa, is directed to the first stage compressor (COMP1). Here, the gas is compressed to 0.5 MPa, increasing its temperature to 241.5 °C (step 3). This high-temperature gas enters the heat exchanger (HX1), where it is cooled to 90 °C before proceeding to the second-stage compressor (COMP2) (step 4). In COMP2, the gas is further compressed to 2.5 MPa, raising the temperature to 218.5 °C (step 5). The compressed gas then flows into the turbine, where it is expanded to 0.39 MPa, resulting in a temperature drop to 58.1 °C (step 6). The expanded gas at 58.1 °C and 0.39 MPa continues to the next process stage. Meanwhile, the cooling water, after absorbing heat in the cooler, exits at 50 °C and 0.101 MPa (step 14). This sequence of cooling, compressing, and expanding prepares the flue gas for further CO2 separation steps involving hydrate formation and dissociation.

Costs in CO2 capture plants are categorized as capital expenditures (CAPEX) and operational expenditures (OPEX). CAPEX includes all initial investments like land, materials, and construction, divided further into bare erected costs (BEC) and total plant costs (TPC), which incorporate engineering, labor, and contingencies. − Additionally, owners' costs cover feasibility studies, permits, and insurance, contributing to the total overnight cost. OPEX is split into variable costs (energy, consumables, and CO2 treatment) and fixed costs (staff, maintenance, and taxes), with the operation typically managed by existing staff without significantly impacting costs. Due to limited data from large-scale operations, these figures are often estimated from broader industry data.

The cost of the captured CO2 is calculated by comparing the cost of electricity with and without the captured CO2 technology and adjusting for the net CO2 captured. This metric excludes costs related to CO2 transport and storage, focusing solely on capture costs given by eq : CO2avoidancecost=LCOECCS−LCOErefEFref−EFCCS 1

LCOECCS and LCOref denote the levelized costs of electricity generation for plants equipped with CCS and for standard reference plants without CCS, respectively. EFref and EFCCS represent their respective emission factors, measured in tons of CO2 per megawatt hour of electricity produced.

The cost of electricity generation (COE) in power plants, particularly when considering carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies, is influenced by a variety of factors. This cost can be calculated using the following formula mentioned in eq : COE=TCC×FCF+FOMCF×8766×MW+VOM+HR×FC 2 where COE represents the cost of generating electricity, measured in dollars per megawatt hour, TCC is the total capital cost of the plant in dollars, FCF stands for fixed charge factor, which is a yearly fraction, FOM denotes fixed operating and maintenance costs in dollars per year, VOM refers to variable nonfuel operating and maintenance costs, in dollars per megawatt hour, HR is the net power plant heat rate in MJ/MWh, FC indicates the unit fuel cost, in dollars per one megajoule, CF is the plant capacity factor, a fraction representing the ratio of the actual output to the maximum possible output, 8766 is the total number of hours in an average year, and MW signifies the net plant capacity in megawatts.

The cost per ton of CO2 captured can be calculated using the following formula in eq : CostofCO2Captured(tonsofCO2capturedforeachdollar)=(CostofElectricitywithCapture−CostofElectricityfortheReferencePlant)/(TotalCO2CapturedperNetMWhforthePlantwithCaptureCostofCO2Captured) 3

“Cost of Electricity with Capture” refers to the electricity cost for the plant that includes CO2 capture technology.

“Cost of Electricity for the Reference Plant” represents the cost of electricity for a similar plant but without CO2 capture technology. ,

“Total CO2 Captured per Net MWh for the Plant with Capture” is the net amount of CO2 captured by the plant, calculated as the total CO2 produced minus the CO2 emitted.

Cost Dynamics in Advancing CO2 Capture Technology

Challenges and Progress in Emerging CO2 Capture Technologies

While emerging technologies in CO2 capture show promise in enhancing capacity and longevity, significant breakthroughs in cost reduction are not expected to be dramatic. Innovations such as improved sorbent materials and efficiencies brought about by mass production may lead to cost savings, but these are likely to be incremental. The most substantial cost reductions are projected to arise from the expansion of operations from pilot-scale to full-scale facilities, leveraging the operational insights gained from existing projects.

Impact of Scaling and Learning Curves on Cost Efficiency

The lack of detailed cost data from large-scale, commercially viable CO2 capture installations indicates that the financial outlay for initial demonstration plants is typically higher than that for subsequent ones. To understand the evolution of these costs, industry experts use terms like “first of a kind” (FOAK) and “nth of a kind” (NOAK). A method commonly used to assess the learning curves across various energy technologies involves a power function, which is calculated by eq : − y=ax−b 4

In this equation, y signifies the capital cost for the nth unit per unit capacity, a represents the capital cost per capacity of the initial unit, x denotes the cumulative capacity, and b is the learning rate exponent. The learning rate (LR) reflects the percentage decrease in cost with each doubling of capacity and is calculated from eq : b=log(1−LR)log2 5

Scaling up from pilot projects to larger, full-scale operations is also crucial for cost reduction. Costs related to different unit sizes are typically estimated using an exponential scale-up law, characterized by a scaling factor (sf) as shown in eq: C=Cref(SSref)sf 6 Here, C stands for the capital cost of a component, S is the size parameter (usually linked to capacity, like fuel power for boilers or mass flow rates for pipelines), and ref denotes the reference values. The scaling factors, which generally range from 0.5 to 1, vary across different components of energy systems. For instance, reactor vessels and boilers often use a scaling factor of 0.67, but this can differ based on more specific data. In scenarios where economies of scale do not influence cost components, such as variable operating and fuel costs, the scaling factor is set to 1. −

These strategic measures in scaling and learning provide a framework for reducing costs in CO2 capture technology deployment, essential for making these technologies economically viable and scalable. ,

Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence for CO2-Based Gas Hydrate Formation

Carbon dioxide (CO2) hydrate formation simulation is a critical area in the advancement of carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies. Gas hydrates, which are crystalline water-based solids physically resembling ice, form when gas molecules, such as CO2, are trapped within cages of hydrogen-bonded water molecules. CO2 hydrates have gained considerable attention for their application in carbon sequestration due to their capacity to stably store CO2 under high-pressure and low-temperature conditions.

Machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) present powerful tools for improving the accuracy, scalability, and efficiency of hydrate formation simulations. By training on historical data sets and experimental outcomes, these models can predict formation conditions, design novel materials, detect anomalies in real time, and optimize process parameterssignificantly reducing computational burden and enabling broader deployment of CCS technologies, which is as shown in Figure .

Predictive Modeling of Hydrate Formation Conditions

Accurate prediction of hydrate phase boundaries and formation kinetics is vital for the design and operation of hydrate-based CCS systems. Traditional models like van der Waals–Platteeuw theory or equation-of-state (EOS)-based approaches often face challenges in capturing real-world conditions, especially in the presence of impurities, salinity variations, and complex geological settings.

Shaik et al. pioneered an approach using artificial neural networks (ANNs) to predict CO2 hydrate formation kinetics under pipeline conditions. Their model incorporated critical variables, including gas composition, temperature, pressure, and water cut, yielding an R 2 value of approximately 0.98 and outperforming conventional thermodynamic predictions. The ANN architecture enabled real-time performance suitable for dynamic pipeline operations.

In another study, Mohammadi developed a support vector regression (SVR) model for predicting the equilibrium temperature of CO2 hydrates using a data set comprising pressure, temperature, and gas impurities. The model achieved a root-mean-square error (RMSE) below 0.5 °C, indicating high reliability for industrial applications.

Deep learning has also shown promise in this domain. A recent study by Tan proposed a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based architecture that combined thermophysical property embeddings with temporal series inputs to predict hydrate induction times. The CNN model demonstrated superior generalization across varied compositions and thermal conditions.

Surrogate Modeling and Simulation Optimization

Surrogate models offer efficient alternatives to high-fidelity numerical simulators, particularly for complex multiphysics hydrate systems. Leperi et al. demonstrated the use of ANNs as surrogates for simulating pressure swing adsorption (PSA) systems, analogous in complexity to hydrate separation processes. Their model yielded CO2 recovery and purity estimates within 1.42% of high-fidelity simulations, reducing simulation time by 90%.

Jianchun proposed the surrogate flow model search (SFMS), which uses neural architecture search (NAS) to automatically construct optimal neural networks for CO2 injection flow simulation. Trained on finite-element outputs, the SFMS predicted pressure and saturation distributions with <0.4% average error, showcasing its potential for hydrate-bearing reservoir simulations.

Moreover, a novel Gaussian process regression (GPR) approach by Lee was employed to model hydrate growth rates in deep-sea conditions. GPR offered not only accurate predictions but also quantified uncertainty, facilitating robust design under incomplete data scenarios. Advanced Feature Engineering for Material Selection

Machine learning has revolutionized the identification and design of materials conducive to CO2 hydrate formation. Anderson et al. used decision trees trained on molecular simulation outputs of metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) to predict CO2 adsorption capacity and selectivity. Functional groups such as hydroxyl, cyano, and amine, combined with specific topological motifs, were identified as key contributors to enhanced hydrate-like interactions.

Dureckova et al. built a large-scale ML framework using gradient boosting to model adsorption performance across a diverse library of 358,400 MOFs. By integrating atomic property-weighted radial distribution functions (AP-RDFs), they achieved R2 values above 0.94 for CO2 uptake and 0.87 for CO2/H2 selectivity. These descriptors can be translated to hydrate nucleation surfaces, allowing for the design of materials that promote efficient CO2 encapsulation in hydrate lattices.

A more recent example includes the work by Shamsudin, who used XGBoost and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to analyze 1,200 porous organic frameworks (POFs) for CO2 hydrate facilitation. Their work emphasized the role of hydrophilic functionalization and nanoconfinement in enhancing hydrate growth.

Risk Analysis and Uncertainty Quantification

Incorporating ML into risk analysis improves the reliability of hydrate-based storage systems. Chen et al. utilized multivariate adaptive regression splines (MARS) to develop monitoring frameworks for CO2 sequestration. The model enabled optimal well placement and early detection of migration anomalies under uncertain geological scenarios.

Amar et al. constructed an ensemble of RBFNNs optimized via bioinspired algorithms to predict CO2 solubility in saline aquifers. Their work achieved high accuracy (R 2 > 0.996) across a range of NaCl concentrations and pressures, providing essential inputs for hydrate formation simulations in deep aquifers.

Rahimi et al. reviewed the role of feature selection in enhancing model robustness, highlighting that ML models trained with irrelevant features often misrepresent risk factors. They recommended techniques such as recursive feature elimination (RFE) and Lasso regularization for identifying influential geological and operational variables.

Experimental Efficiency and Adsorbent Optimization

Reducing the experimental burden through ML-guided material design and process simulations is a key advancement. Fathalian et al. developed a multilayer perceptron (MLP) model to predict CO2 adsorption on graphene oxide (GO)-based adsorbents. Achieving R 2 > 0.99, the model identified surface area, pore volume, and defect density as dominant variables.

Gu et al. used a deep learning framework to evaluate CO2/H2O/N2 selectivity in metal-zeolites. The model screened 208 zeolite frameworks and identified Sc-, Zr-, and Y-zeolites as optimal for selective CO2 adsorption, setting the stage for hydrate-promoting surface development.

In another study, Eslam used ensemble ML models to derive interpretable rules for designing high-performance adsorbents. Their decision-tree-based models achieved classification accuracy above 98% and offered practical guidelines for designing low-pressure, low-temperature hydrate adsorbents.

Process Optimization Using AI and Hybrid Models

AI can streamline process design in hydrate-based CCS. Aliyon et al. applied XAI methods to identify critical process parameters for reducing reboiler duty in absorption-based CO2 capture. This approach is applicable to hydrate formation processes, where phase transitions require significant energy input.

Narimani introduced ML models to predict solvent degradation in carbon capture, which directly impacts the hydrate stability in hybrid CCS schemes. Their ANN and random forest models, trained on Mongstad pilot plant data, provided degradation predictions with R 2 > 0.90, enabling proactive maintenance and reducing hydrate destabilization risks.

Real-Time Monitoring and Leakage Detection

Machine learning plays a pivotal role in the real-time monitoring of CCS sites, particularly for early detection of hydrate dissociation and CO2 leakage.

Wang et al. developed classifiers using seismic and downhole data to predict CO2 saturation and leakage in saline aquifers. Trained on 6000 synthetic scenarios, their model achieved high concordance between predicted and actual saturation levels, offering a template for hydrate leakage detection.

Sun et al. modeled the contact angle behavior of CO2-brine-mineral systems using gradient tree boosting and extreme learning machines. Accurate contact angle predictions under varying pressure and salinity were essential for assessing caprock wettability and hydrate nucleation potential.

Integration with Digital Twins and Industrial Deployment

The concept of digital twinsvirtual representations of physical systemshas recently been adopted in CCS. Yamada et al. integrated ML with physics-based simulations to assess hydrate formation risks in depleted gas reservoirs. Their deep neural network predicted hydrate risks near wellbores with a 95% recall rate, enabling dynamic injection control.

Ak et al. conducted a patent landscape analysis revealing a surge in AI-driven CCS innovations, particularly in digital monitoring and automated control systems. Their work emphasized the synergy between real-time ML models and industrial SCADA systems for hydrate-based CCS. Conclusions

By effectively capturing and storing carbon dioxide (CO2) over an extended period of time, gas hydrate-based carbon capture and storage (CCUS) technologies represent a promising solution to the urgent problem of climate change. Recent developments in CO2 hydrate formation research, the techno-economic aspects of CCUS plant operations, and the incorporation of hydrate-based capture into more comprehensive decarbonization plans have all been methodically reviewed in this review. Notably, the study also examined the new models and simulations of CO2 hydrate formation processes using machine learning (ML) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques, highlighting their potential to speed up design optimization and lower experimental costs. Although hydrate-based CCUS has clear benefits, such as high selectivity and the potential for energy-efficient operations, a number of technical, financial, and computational issues must be resolved before its full potential can be realized. Gas hydrate-based CCUS, enhanced by data-driven methods, has great potential to help achieve global carbon reduction goals with continued innovation, strategic policy support, and integration into current energy systems.

Limitations and Future Recommendations

Although the process mechanisms, general cost considerations, and the use of ML and AI for hydrate simulation are all covered in this review, it is devoid of scale-dependent CAPEX and OPEX, probabilistic analyses of economic uncertainties under dynamic energy and carbon pricing scenarios, and detailed quantitative assessments of process-specific cost drivers. To more accurately assess the operational robustness and economic viability of hydrate-based carbon capture and storage systems at the industrial scale, future research should concentrate on rigorous techno-economic modeling that combines sophisticated multiphase simulations with stochastic cost analyses and machine learning-driven optimization.
