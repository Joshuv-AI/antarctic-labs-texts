A Unique Case of Metastatic Paraganglioma in a Patient With Parkinson Disease
Authors: Soe Theingi Aye, Theik Di Oo, Nyein Aye Lwin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe the case of a patient in his 70s with Parkinson disease (PD) who had an incidental pheochromocytoma with mesenteric secondaries. Although the literature contains a few case reports of patients with PD who have incidental pheochromocytoma, this is the first case report of a patient with PD and metastatic paraganglioma (PPGL). The diagnosis was confirmed by a metaiodobenzylguanidine radioisotopic scan, along with increased levels of catecholaminergic products and byproducts in the urine. Genetic predisposition to PD and PPGL appears paradoxical, as PPGL is associated with the pathologic stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α. In contrast, PD involves the disruption of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α activity. Thus, the coexistence of these 2 conditions should be very rare, and, based on the paucity of such cases in the literature, this appears to be the case.

Introduction

Paraganglioma (PPGL) is a rare neuroendocrine tumor that is usually benign and unilateral. It has an annual incidence of 2 to 8 per million people [1] and typically presents at a mean age of 43 years [2]. PPGL originates from neural crest-derived cells located in the adrenal medulla, the sympathetic paraganglia, and the parasympathetic paraganglia. The risk of malignancy in PPGL varies; about 15% to 20% of these tumors become metastatic. The current assessment of metastatic risk involves multiple factors, including tumor size (≥5 cm), extra-adrenal location, the presence of a succinate dehydrogenase complex subunit B mutation, dopaminergic phenotype (eg, plasma 3-methoxytyramine exceeding 3 times the upper limit of normal), and a high Ki-67 proliferation index [3]. Regarding Parkinson disease (PD) prevalence, a 2018 report from Parkinson UK states a figure of 286.5 per 100 000 person-years.

Although PPGL and PD are both influenced by genetic factors, their pathogenic mechanisms appear to act in opposite directions. In PPGL, pathologic stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α drives pseudohypoxic signaling and tumorigenesis [4], whereas in PD, disruption of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α activity contributes to dopaminergic neuronal vulnerability [5]. This paradox may partly explain the extreme rarity of their simultaneous occurrence [6].

In the literature, only 5 case reports discuss the coexistence of pheochromocytoma and PD. The first case report involves a patient in his 50s with a history of PD who showed elevated metanephrines and a left adrenal nodule. The diagnosis was confirmed after an I-123 metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scan. A subsequent left adrenalectomy revealed adrenal medullary hyperplasia [7]. Four similar cases have been reported in patients with PD in their 60s or 70s. In all cases, adrenal masses were incidentally found or identified during evaluation of nonspecific symptoms. Similarly, elevated catecholamine metabolites were observed, and I-123 MIBG scans confirmed increased uptake in the adrenal mass [6, 8-10]. However, our case is unique as it involves an adrenal nodule and mesenteric secondaries in a patient with PD.

Case Presentation

This case describes a 75-year-old man with a five-month history of PD, for which he was receiving dopaminergic therapy. He also has type 2 diabetes mellitus, an overactive bladder, hypertension, and gout. His social history includes living alone, typically walking with a cane, a performance status of 2, and no family history of PD or PPGL. The patient was admitted after experiencing a fall likely caused by postural hypotension along with respiratory symptoms. Initially, the patient was treated with intravenous antibiotics for suspected community-acquired pneumonia and cellulitis of both legs. However, during hospitalization, a chest computed tomography (CT) pulmonary angiography was performed due to the patient's persistent oxygen requirement, despite ongoing treatment for community-acquired pneumonia. This revealed a diagnosis of pulmonary embolism in the right lung.

Diagnostic Assessment

On assessment, the patient had supine hypertension of 205/120 mm Hg with a postural drop to 69/45 mm Hg, which contributed to another inpatient fall. To evaluate for possible adrenal insufficiency, morning serum cortisol was measured at 12.2 µg/dL (SI: 336 nmol/L) (reference range, 6-23 µg/dL [SI: 275-555 nmol/L]. Subsequently, a short Synacthen test was performed, confirming adequate adrenal function. Additionally, necrotic lesions were observed on both lower limbs, and a CT angiogram was requested. Incidentally, we found a 40-mm heterogenous mass in the body of the left adrenal gland, demonstrating avid arterial enhancement of 296 Hounsfield units (HU) with washout on the delayed phase of 68 HU, a 54-mm irregular, heterogenous central mesenteric mass with marked arterial enhancement of 189 HU with washout on the delayed phase of 78 HU, and a 16-mm mass in the gastric pylorus demonstrating intense arterial enhancement of 181 HU with washout on the delayed phase of 65 HU (Fig. 1).

Computed tomography (CT) angiogram demonstrating (A) a 40-mm heterogeneous mass in the body of the left adrenal gland, (B) a 54-mm irregular, heterogeneous central mesenteric mass, and (C) a 16-mm nodular focus centered on the wall of the gastric pylorus.

Initially, based on imaging findings of a mesenteric nodule, a lesion in the gastric pylorus, and an adrenal mass, the differential diagnosis included gastrointestinal stromal tumor or another primary gastrointestinal malignancy. At that stage, PPGL was considered less likely due to the unusual locations of the extra-adrenal lesions. An esophagogastroduodenoscopy was recommended to further evaluate the gastric lesion. However, the results showed nonspecialized gastric mucosa with normal architecture and no evidence of inflammation. Tumor markers were unremarkable, and paraneoplastic antibodies were negative. Following the hormonal workup, which revealed markedly elevated urinary metanephrines, 3-methoxytyramine, and chromogranin A, suspicion shifted toward a neuroendocrine origin, consistent with a pheochromocytoma and possible metastatic PPGL. Treatment

Blood pressure management in this patient was particularly challenging due to the coexistence of orthostatic hypotension and supine hypertension. Orthostatic hypotension in PPGL is often worsened by hypovolemia; however, the patient remained euvolemic throughout admission, and, given normal renin activity, volume expansion was not necessary. Although α-adrenergic blockade is standard in PPGL to prevent hypertensive crises, its use was deemed high risk in this patient. Even low-dose α-blockers would likely have worsened orthostatic hypotension, increasing the risk of syncope and falls. To address supine hypertension while minimizing standing hypotension, evening-dose amlodipine of 5 mg was initiated. However, following discharge, amlodipine was replaced with ramipril at 2.5 mg daily. This was subsequently discontinued during follow-up due to the recurrence of postural hypotension. Management of postural hypotension included pyridostigmine at 30 mg 3 times daily, which resulted in moderate improvement by enhancing autonomic ganglionic transmission.

Outcome and Follow-up

Following review at the multidisciplinary team meeting, a diagnosis of metastatic PPGL was confirmed. The patient remained under active monitoring by the endocrine team and was referred to the oncology team for further management. After serial CT scans to monitor disease progression, the identified masses remained stable over a 6-month period. Consequently, taking the patient's frail state into account, a decision was made to adopt a palliative management approach.

Discussion

PPGL is a rare condition, mainly seen in young people, with its peak occurrence between the third and fifth decades of life [1]. Catecholamine-producing PPGL may present with headache, diaphoresis, palpitations, and episodic hypertension [11]. Most PPGLs are benign, and 50% are hereditary [12], associated with certain genetic syndromes such as neurofibromatosis type 1, multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2, and Von Hippel-Lindau disease [13]. Laboratory evaluation involves measuring plasma and urinary metanephrine, normetanephrine, and 3-methoxytyramine (the breakdown product of dopamine) using a 24-hour urine collection [14]. However, false-positive results are common during severe illness, stress, intense physical activity, and with certain medications, including dopaminergic drugs (as in this case), tricyclic antidepressants, and selective noradrenergic reuptake inhibitors [15]. Patients taking dopaminergic medications are especially likely to have false positives, which raised initial concerns for the endocrinology team in this case.⁠ In patients treated with levodopa, plasma and urine levels of 3-methoxytyramine can rise dramatically, reported as 22- to 148-fold higher than reference values [16]. The significant increase (∼10-fold above the upper limit) in urinary 3-methoxytyramine, along with a mild rise (<3-fold above the upper limit) in urinary normetanephrines, may partially be explained by dopaminergic therapy [16].

Radiological evaluations for PPGL include CT and magnetic resonance imaging for anatomical staging and 123I-MIBG scintigraphy to confirm the diagnosis. Additionally, 68 Ga-DOTATATE positron emission tomography/CT or 18F-FDOPA positron emission tomography/CT could have been considered to further evaluate metastatic or extra-adrenal lesions, especially in the context of multifocal PPGLs or succinate dehydrogenase complex subunit B-associated disease [17]. Although genetic testing is recommended for all patients with PPGL, it was not performed in this patient due to his advanced age and the absence of a family history, which makes the hereditary form less likely.

Management involves both medical and surgical approaches. It is essential to control hypertension perioperatively using α-blockers (nonselective α-blockers or selective α-1 receptor blockers). For PPGL, surgical intervention involves resection, either minimally invasive adrenalectomy or open resection for most PPGLs [18]. For metastatic cases, treatment options include chemotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, immunotherapy, and high-specific-activity 123I-MIBG treatment, with ongoing research on personalized treatment plans [3]. The prognosis (10-year survival) can range from 0% (without any treatment) to 73% with multimodal therapy [19].

PD is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder [20]. Predisposing factors include advanced age [21], genetic factors related to synuclein and LRRK2/dardarin [22], and environmental factors such as exposure to toxins, including smoking, excessive coffee intake, and pesticides [23]. Clinical diagnosis and imaging tests, such as magnetic resonance imaging or single-photon emission CT, may be used to rule out other conditions [24]. Treatment involves increasing dopamine levels with dopaminergic agents (either Levodopa plus a dopa decarboxylase inhibitor or dopamine agonists like ropinirole) or drugs that inhibit dopamine destruction (monoamine oxidase inhibitors, catechol-o-methyltransferase inhibitors), and amantadine [25].

Orthostatic hypotension is a well-recognized nonmotor feature of PD, resulting from impaired autonomic regulation. In this patient, blood pressure management was likely complicated by excess catecholamine secretion from the PPGL, creating a paradoxical combination of supine hypertension and postural hypotension.

Learning Points

Although the coexistence of metastatic paraganglioma and PD in the same patient is very rare, this can happen, and such an occurrence could make diagnosis and management complicated.
