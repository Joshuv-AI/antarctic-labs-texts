Utilizing Dynamic Liquid Crystal Elastomer Dressings to Enhance Early Dermal Wound Closure
Authors: Clifford Jiajun He, Jeffrey D Bernstein, Benjamin Bernard, Devyansh Agrawal, Gaoweiang Dong, Jennifer Lee, Shengqian Cai, David B Hom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo evaluate whether a novel body temperature‐activated liquid crystal elastomer (LCE) bandage with contraction properties can accelerate wound closure in a porcine model.MethodsAdult pigs underwent creation of thirty‐six 8‐mm circular full‐thickness dermal wounds. LCE strips were assembled into bandages and applied to half of the wounds, while the remaining wounds received conventional dressings within the same animal. Wound size was measured over 14 days and expressed as percent wound area relative to baseline. A total of 144 wounds were analyzed across 4 pigs (LCE group, n = 72; control group, n = 72).ResultsLCE‐treated wounds demonstrated accelerated early reduction in wound area compared with controls (postoperative day [POD] 3: 88.7% vs. 101.5%; POD7: 56.2% vs. 75.0%; POD10: 36.1% vs. 66.7%; all p < 0.001). Although the magnitude of the treatment effect was reduced by POD14, a significant difference remained (44.0% vs. 53.7%; p = 0.001). No adverse effects were observed.ConclusionsLiquid crystal elastomer materials show promise as a novel approach for enhancing early dermal wound closure. LCE‐based dynamic bandages may have future clinical utility as a noninvasive mechanically active wound dressing platform.Level of EvidenceNAKeywords: body temperature actuation, liquid crystal elastomer, porcine model, skin, wound closure, wound healing

Introduction

Dermal wound healing is a complex, multistep physiologic process requiring coordinated cellular and molecular responses. Numerous tissue‐specific and patient‐specific factors influence both the rate of wound closure and the final healing outcome [1, 2, 3]. Among these, mechanical tension is an important determinant of wound healing dynamics and scar formation [4].

In surgical practice, meticulous tissue handling, thoughtful wound design, and appropriate closure techniques remain the most reliable methods for minimizing wound tension [5, 6, 7]. Nonetheless, strategies that further modulate mechanical forces across healing tissue may improve wound closure. Dynamic wound dressings that reduce tension without requiring sutures represent a promising noninvasive approach to support early healing [6].

Recent literature has explored multiple strategies to accelerate wound healing and reduce pathologic scarring [8, 9, 10]. Mechanotransduction pathways, including integrin‐FAK, Wnt, YAP, PI3K, and Rho GTPase signaling, have been identified as key mediators through which mechanical forces influence tissue repair, with dysregulation contributing to fibrosis and hypertrophic scar formation [9]. In parallel, biomaterials such as silicone, polyurethane, and elastomeric dressings have been developed to offload tension through viscoelastic deformation, although most are passive systems with effects that diminish over time [10].

Liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) are a unique class of active polymers capable of programmable, reversible contraction in response to temperature changes. This behavior arises from phase transitions between ordered (nematic) and disordered (isotropic) states, enabling meaningful actuation forces when heated above the transition temperature [11, 12, 13, 14]. Importantly, LCEs can be engineered to respond at physiologic temperatures, allowing autonomous contraction in vivo [15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

Because of their tunable mechanical properties, shape programmability, and potential biocompatibility, LCEs are emerging as promising materials for biomedical applications, including wound healing, tissue scaffolds, and implantable devices [20, 21, 22, 23].

The objective of this study was to evaluate whether LCE bandages that contract at body temperature can enhance early dermal wound closure when applied across full‐thickness skin wounds. We hypothesized that this dynamic system would generate controlled mechanical forces across the wound bed and thereby accelerate wound size reduction during the early healing period.

Materials and Methods

Animal Model

A porcine model was selected because of its close resemblance to human skin in structure and healing characteristics [24]. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of California San Diego (Protocol #S24027) and were conducted in accordance with established animal care guidelines.

Animal Subjects

Four adult Yorkshire pigs weighing 55 to 60 kg were included. All procedures were performed under general anesthesia using ketamine, dexmedetomidine, midazolam, and isoflurane. Local anesthesia consisted of 1% lidocaine with epinephrine (1:100,000). Postoperative analgesia included buprenorphine and carprofen. Animals were monitored closely throughout the study and euthanized at study completion using intravenous pentobarbital.

Bandage Design and Assembly

The bandage consisted of a patterned LCE layer embedded within a transparent adhesive dressing. Upon exposure to body temperature, the LCE contracted to approximately 50% of its original length within 60 s, generating tensile forces across the wound (Figures 1 and 2). Study Design

Thirty‐six 8‐mm full‐thickness dermal wounds were created per animal along the paraspinal dorsum. Wounds were assigned in an alternating pattern to receive either LCE bandages or standard transparent dressings (Tegaderm, 3 M).

Wounds were assessed on POD3, POD7, POD10, and POD14. At each time point, dressings were removed, wounds were cleansed, photographed, measured, and re‐dressed. (Figure 3 Timeline).

Time line for wound measurements and photographic documentation in the porcine dermal model.

Outcome Measures

Wound surface area was calculated at each time point and expressed as percent wound area relative to baseline, with baseline defined as 100%. Lower values therefore indicated greater wound size reduction. Directional contraction was measured along axes parallel and perpendicular to the LCE orientation.

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons between treatment groups were performed using Student's two‐tailed paired t‐tests, with significance defined as p < 0.05. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen's d. Within each animal, each LCE‐treated wound was paired with an adjacent control wound, such that the pig effectively served as its own control, inherently accounting for between‐animal differences in baseline wound‐healing capacity.

To address within‐animal wound‐level correlation and properly account for repeated measurements on individual wounds, a linear mixed‐effects model was fit using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. This nested random‐effects structure included a random intercept for each pig (capturing between‐animal variability in baseline wound‐healing) and an additional random intercept for each wound nested within pig (capturing within‐animal, wound‐level clustering across repeated measurements). Degrees of freedom were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation. Post hoc pairwise comparisons between treatment groups at each time point were performed with Bonferroni correction, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for all pairwise contrasts. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.1) using the lme4, lmerTest, and emmeans packages.

Results

Safety and Tolerability

All animals tolerated the procedures without complications during this time frame. No infections, fevers, or adverse wound‐healing responses were observed during the study period.

The mixed‐effects model confirmed these findings, which revealed a significant main effect of treatment (F (1, 457) = 78.24, p < 0.001), indicating that LCE‐treated wounds had substantially smaller wound area than control wounds across the study period. A significant main effect of time was also observed (F (3, 458) = 114.18, p < 0.001), reflecting progressive wound area reduction in both groups over the 14‐day study period. Importantly, a significant treatment and time interaction was identified (F (3, 457) = 4.20, p = 0.006), demonstrating that the magnitude of the LCE treatment benefit varied significantly across time points, greatest at POD10 and attenuated by POD14. The intraclass correlation coefficient for pig was 0.23, with between‐animal variance (0.014), wound‐level variance (0.008), and residual variance (0.038) indicating that the treatment effect was consistent across all four animals. Results from the mixed‐effects model were consistent with those from the paired t‐test analysis, supporting the observed findings.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that LCE‐based dynamic bandages enhance early dermal wound closure in a porcine model [24]. The treatment effect was most pronounced from POD3 through POD10, which is consistent with the period during which healing tissue may be most responsive to externally applied mechanical modulation.

The directional nature of wound contraction supports the conclusion that wound size reduction was driven by the programmed actuation properties of the LCE material rather than by a nonspecific effect of dressing coverage alone. This finding is important because it suggests that the observed benefit was linked to the mechanical behavior of the LCE construct itself.

Although the magnitude of the treatment effect decreased by POD14, a significant difference between groups remained. This attenuation over time may reflect the evolving biomechanical properties of the healing wound, including increasing tissue stiffness and resistance to externally applied forces as healing progresses [25].

These results suggest that LCE bandages may have greatest utility during the early phase of wound healing, when modulation of mechanical tension is most likely to influence wound geometry and rate of closure [26, 27, 28]. The present study was designed to assess wound size reduction rather than scar quality or histologic remodeling. Accordingly, although improved early closure could potentially translate into downstream benefits in healing quality, those outcomes were not directly measured here and require further investigation.

Compared with traditional closure‐support strategies [5, 6, 7], LCE dressings offer a noninvasive and self‐actuating alternative. Their ability to contract at body temperature without an external power source is a notable advantage [20, 23]. In addition, preliminary observations suggested that the material retained functional behavior during repeated application [17], although formal durability testing was not performed in this study.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was performed in an animal model, and porcine skin, although similar to human skin, is not identical. Second, dressing adherence may have varied because of movement, moisture, and the anatomical challenges of the dorsal skin surface specifically in a porcine model. Third, this study focused on wound size reduction and directional contraction rather than histologic endpoints, scar quality, or long‐term functional outcomes. Fourth, the study included four animals, which limits the number of clusters available for estimation of between‐animal variance in the mixed‐effects model. However, the large number of wounds per animal and the consistency of treatment effects across animals support the robustness of the observed findings. Finally, although paired comparisons were used based on matched wound placement within animals, future studies using larger samples would further strengthen the precision of between‐animal variance estimates.

Conclusion

LCE‐based dynamic bandages improved early dermal wound closure in a porcine model, with the greatest effect observed during the early postoperative period. These findings support further investigation of LCE technology as a novel noninvasive, mechanically active wound dressing platform for wound healing applications.
