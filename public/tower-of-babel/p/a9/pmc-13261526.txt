METTL14 inhibits atherogenesis by epigenetically activating PPAR-α/γ transcription and fatty acid oxidation in VSMCs
Authors: Lei Cui, Cheng Kiu Ho, Panhong Liang, Wentao Gao, Jitao Liu, Xiao Yu Tian, Randolph Wong, Bin Zhou, Kathy O Lui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsN6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification can govern cell fate by co- or post-transcriptionally regulating gene expression. VSMCs can undergo phenotypic switching, contributing to other cells within atherosclerotic plaques, including foam cell- and macrophage-like cells. However, the role of VSMC m6A in atherosclerosis development remains unclear. While PPAR-α and PPAR-γ have been extensively studied in macrophages for their roles in atherosclerosis, the epigenetic regulation of these nuclear receptors under high cholesterol conditions remains poorly understood.Methods and resultsWe utilized murine and human atherosclerotic aortas, along with VSMC-specific Mettl3 and Mettl14 knockout mice, to evaluate the role of VSMC m6A in atherosclerosis. Lineage tracing was used to assess macrophage-like VSMCs. The epigenetic regulation of Ppara and Pparg transcription by methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14) was investigated through a variety of methods, including histological, cellular, genomic, transcriptomic, metabolomic, lipidomic, computational, and pharmacological approaches. The therapeutic potential of VSMC Mettl14 in atherosclerosis was analysed using adenoassociated virus-mediated expression in ApoE−/− mice. We showed that the METTL3/METTL14 methyltransferase complex was reduced in both murine and human atherosclerotic VSMCs. The levels of METTL3, and consequently m6A, were regulated by METTL14, which was in turn influenced by oxidized low-density lipoprotein. Notably, while VSMC METTL3 or m6A did not contribute to atherosclerosis, VSMC-specific Mettl14 knockout mice exhibited accelerated foam cell formation, enhanced vascular inflammation, and exacerbated atherosclerosis. These effects were driven by impaired beta-oxidation and reduced mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Replenishment of Mettl14 significantly attenuated these adverse effects. Specifically, METTL14 regulated phenotypic switching of VSMCs and modulated the number of VSMC-derived macrophage-like cells, rather than infiltrating macrophages, within atherosclerotic plaques. Furthermore, we demonstrated that METTL14 regulates the transcription of Ppara and Pparg, master regulators of lipid metabolism that promote cholesterol efflux, by enhancing SETD1A-mediated H3K4 trimethylation in an m6A-independent manner. Activation of PPAR-γ with rosiglitazone restored impaired mitochondrial OXPHOS in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs, leading to reduced lipid accumulation. Lastly, recapitulating Mettl14 expression in atherosclerotic vessels through AAV gene therapy effectively inhibited atherosclerosis progression without compromising liver function.ConclusionWe have unveiled that METTL14 promotes lipid metabolism and inhibits atherogenesis through activating PPAR-α/γ expression. These experiments highlight the therapeutic potential of the endogenous METTL14/PPAR-α/γ axis for treating atherosclerotic and metabolic diseases.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease, primarily resulting from atherosclerosis and its complications, is the leading cause of mortality worldwide. Atherosclerotic plaques not only narrow blood vessels but also obstruct blood flow through spontaneous rupture, ultimately leading to myocardial infarction or stroke. The retention of lipoprotein cholesterol in artery walls is recognized as a key driver of plaque formation,1 and its removal is essential for plaque regression.2 Notably, accumulating evidence from genetic lineage tracing studies has demonstrated that up to 70% of the cells within an atherosclerotic plaque, including foam cells, are derived from vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs).3 Lipid uptake into VSMCs occurs through specialized scavenger receptors, such as scavenger receptor class A (SR-A)4 and CD36,5 while cholesterol efflux is mediated by transporters such as ATP-binding cassette transporter (ABCA1). These lipid metabolic pathways are crucial for regulating foam cell formation.

PPARs are ligand-activated transcription factors that belong to the nuclear receptor superfamily. They play essential roles in regulating glucose and lipid metabolism across multiple organs, including the liver, adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and vascular wall (for review, see Mark et al.6 and Montaigne et al.7) Specifically, PPAR-α governs the expression of genes that are crucial for lipid and lipoprotein metabolism, while PPAR-γ regulates genes that are important for glucose homeostasis and adipogenesis. As physiological lipid sensors, PPAR-α and PPAR-γ can be activated by endogenous ligands, such as oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL), so as to increase cholesterol efflux, lipolysis, beta-oxidation, and adipocyte differentiation.8 The identification and development of PPARα agonists (e.g. fibrates) and PPAR-γ agonists (e.g. glitazones) have led to their clinical use in the treatment of dyslipidaemia and type-2 diabetes. In addition to their presence in adipocytes, these nuclear receptors are also expressed in macrophages, endothelial cells (ECs), and VSMCs,6 all of which participate in vascular atherosclerosis. While extensive research has centred on their roles of macrophages and ECs in atherosclerosis, VSMCs are a primary source of plaque cells and contribute to all stages of atherosclerosis progression (for review, see Grootaert and Bennett,3, Allahverdian et al.,9 and Basatemur et al.10) However, it remains unclear whether PPAR-α and PPAR-γ expression in VSMCs contributes to atherogenesis. Gaining insights into the upstream mechanisms that regulate PPAR-α and PPAR-γ expression in the vasculature is crucial for improving the therapeutic potential of targeting this signalling pathway in the treatment of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

Emerging evidence suggests that N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification, the most prevalent modification in eukaryotic mRNA,11 governs cell fate determination by epitranscriptomically regulating gene expression across various biological processes, including embryonic stem cell differentiation12 and haematopoietic stem cell specification.13 m6A RNA modification is a dynamic and reversible process, primarily catalyzed by a methyltransferase-like complex consisting of the writer METTL3 and the RNA-binding scaffold protein METTL14.14 Loss of either Mettl3 or Mettl14 significantly reduces m6A RNA modifications both in vitro and in vivo15 and further studies have demonstrated their interdependent effects on each other’s stability.16 Recently, the role of m6A RNA methylation in the regulation of atherogenesis has garnered attention. Studies have reported that the m6A writer METTL3 mediates atherosclerosis by controlling gene expression in ECs, although findings have been contradictory.17,18 In one study, METTL3 expression was found to be upregulated by oscillatory stress, promoting atherosclerosis by enhancing NLR1 transcripts and reducing KLF4 transcripts through m6A methylation in ECs.17 Conversely, another study reported that METTL3 expression is reduced by oscillatory stress both in vitro and in atheroprone regions of ApoE−/− mice in vivo.18 Atherosclerosis is accelerated after EC-specific Mettl3 ablation in Cdh5CreER;Mettl3fl/fl;Apoe−/− mice fed on high cholesterol diet for 12 weeks, primarily by preventing Egfr mRNA degradation.18 This activation of the EGFR pathway promotes EC inflammation in response to oscillatory stress. Beyond ECs, METTL3-mediated m6A RNA methylation also contributes to atherosclerosis by modifying gene expression in myeloid cells, including macrophages.19 Myeloid cell−specific deletion of Mettl3 results in reduced atherosclerotic plaque formation in Lyz2Cre;Mettl3fl/fl;Apoe−/− mice fed a high-fat diet for 14 weeks. Mechanistically, METTL3-mediated m6A RNA methylation increases Braf translation via the m6A reader YTHDF1 in macrophages, thereby promoting ox-LDL-induced ERK phosphorylation and facilitating the inflammatory response during atherosclerosis. In VSMCs, silencing Mettl3 via lentivirus alleviates ox-LDL-induced phenotypic switching in vitro by limiting miR-375-3p expression, which is necessary for targeting Pdk1.20 We recently reported that VSMC METTL3 regulates vascular resistance and blood pressure in mice by dynamically modulating the expression of Mylk2 and Tgfb2 through m6A mRNA modifications.21 Nevertheless, it remains to be determined whether METTL3-mediated m6A RNA methylation in VSMCs plays a role in regulating atherosclerotic plaque formation.

In the present study, we investigate the role of the m6A writer complex, consisting of METTL3 and METTL14, in VSMCs and their contribution to atherogenesis. In Myh11CreER;Mettl3fl/fl;Apoe−/− mice, we observed no significant changes in atherosclerotic plaque formation. However, Myh11CreER;Mettl14fl/fl;Apoe−/− mice exhibited increased plaque formation along the aorta. Additionally, glycerophospholipid levels were significantly elevated in VSMC-specific Mettl14 but not Mettl3 knockout aortas, suggesting that METTL14 may regulate the atherosclerotic lipid profile in an m6A-independent manner. We also provided lineage tracing evidence showing that VSMC Mettl14 deficiency results in the increased accumulation of macrophage-like VSMCs but not infiltrating macrophages within the plaque. Further metabolomic and functional analyses highlighted METTL14’s crucial role in lipid metabolism by enhancing beta-oxidation. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that METTL14 functions as a novel chromatin regulator that promotes the transcriptions of Ppara and Pparg in VSMCs independently of its RNA m6A methyltransferase activity, as demonstrated by restored m6A levels through METTL3-mediated mRNA methylation after Mettl14 knockout or catalytic mutation of METTL14. Notably, rosiglitazone was able to compensate for the loss of Mettl14 in VSMCs, promoting beta-oxidation, reducing lipid accumulation, and inhibiting foam cell formation. Our findings also reveal the therapeutic potential of upregulating VSMC METTL14 to delay atherosclerotic plaque formation without compromising liver function, thereby minimizing the off-target effects associated with rosiglitazone. In summary, this study demonstrates that the loss of Mettl14, but not Mettl3, in VSMCs leads to reduced beta-oxidation, increased lipid accumulation, and accelerated atherosclerosis development. These findings provide novel insights into therapeutic strategies and uncover METTL14 as a previously unrecognized epigenetic regulator of PPAR-α and PPAR-γ in the context of atherosclerotic and metabolic disorders.

Methods

The data and analytical methods that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. An expanded experimental procedures section is provided in the Supplementary material online.

Human samples

All procedures were approved by The Joint CUHK-NTEC Clinical Research Ethics Committee. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to the inclusion of subjects in the study. Atherosclerotic tissues and proximally adjacent tissues were collected from patients who underwent aortic repair surgeries. The baseline characteristics of the patients are detailed in Supplementary material online, Table S1.

Mice

The care and use of mice were in accordance with the US NIH guidelines and were approved by the CUHK Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee. The C57BL/6 and Apoe−/− lines were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Mettl14fl/fl was kindly provided by Professor Chuan He (University of Chicago, U.S.A.). Myh11CreER,22 Mettl3fl/fl23 and Rosa26YFP/YFP24 were previously reported by us. As Myh11-CreER is Y-linked, only male mice (aged 8–10 weeks) were used in this study. To induce the activation of Cre recombinase, tamoxifen (MedChemExpress, Cat No. HY-13757A) was dissolved in corn oil (10 mg/mL) and administered at a dosage of 100 μg/g body weight in five doses to males aged 8–10 weeks for five consecutive days through intraperitoneal injection. To induce an atherosclerosis-prone background in non-ApoE−/− mice, 1.0 × 1011 vg/mouse of adenoassociated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) vector containing Pcsk9 was intravenously injected through the tail vein. To induce atherosclerosis, ApoE−/− or AAV8-Pcsk9 treated mice were fed on a high cholesterol diet (Research diets, Cat. No. D12336i) for 12 weeks. Mice were randomized before their assignment to treatment groups. The analyses were performed in a blinded manner. To activate PPAR-γ in vivo, mice were treated with rosiglitazone (MCE, Cat. No. HY-17386) at a dosage of 20 mg/kg per day in 0.9% saline or with 0.9% saline as vehicle control by oral gavage for 12 weeks. To rescue METTL14 expression in vivo, mice were intravenously injected with AAV9 vector containing Mettl14 driven under the control of a Tagln (encoding SM22α) promoter. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under anaesthesia with 2.5% isoflurane.

Results

The METTL3/METTL14 complex is reduced in VSMCs during mouse and human atherosclerosis

To investigate the overall m6A profile in key cell types involved in vascular atherosclerosis, infiltrating macrophages, intimal layer ECs, and medial layer VSMCs, we quantified the percentage of m6A in mRNA isolated from these cells derived from the aortas of ApoE−/− mice fed either a chow diet or high cholesterol diet (HCD) for 12 weeks. m6A modification levels were significantly increased in the mRNA of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) in HCD-fed mice compared with chow diet-fed mice (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1A). In contrast, m6A modification levels remained unchanged in aortic intima (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1B). However, in aortic media, m6A levels were significantly reduced under HCD conditions (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1C). Since m6A modifications are installed, removed, and recognized by its writers, erasers, and readers, respectively,25–28 we performed quantitative RT-PCR to examine the mRNA levels of various methyltransferases and demethylases.29 In BMDMs, the mRNA levels of the writer complex Mettl3 and Mettl14 were significantly increased after 12 weeks of HCD, while those of other writers, including Mettl4, Mettl16, and Wtap, remained unchanged (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1D). Moreover, the levels of the erasers Fto and Alkdh5 were significantly reduced. In aortic intima, the mRNA levels of the writer complex Mettl3 and Mettl14, as well as the cofactor Wtap, remained unchanged (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1E). However, the mRNA levels of other writers, Mettl4 and Mettl16, were significantly reduced. Similarly, the levels of the eraser Fto remained unchanged, while Alkdh5 was significantly increased. In aortic media, the mRNA levels of the writers Mettl3 and Wtap were unchanged, while the transcript level of Mettl14 was significantly reduced (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1F). Furthermore, the mRNA levels of other writers, such as Mettl4 and Mettl16, were also significantly reduced, whereas the levels of the erasers Fto and Alkdh5 remained unchanged. These findings suggest that METTL3/14-mediated m6A modifications in ECs may not play a critical role in the development and progression of atherosclerosis. On the other hand, m6A modifications in infiltrating macrophages and VSMCs, driven by METTL3/14, appear to play a distinct role in this process. Notably, our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that METTL3-mediated m6A modifications in macrophages promote atherosclerosis, as its specific ablation inhibits the pathological process.19 However, the role of METTL3/14-mediated m6A modifications in VSMCs during atherosclerosis development remains unclear.

Given that METTL3 is responsible for over 95% of m6A modifications in mRNA modification,30 we focused on the METTL3/METTL14 methyltransferase complex in this study. We further examined the protein levels of METTL3 and METTL14 in aortic media of ApoE−/− mice after 8 and 12 weeks on HCD by Western blot analysis. Consistent with its mRNA level, the protein level of METTL14 was significantly reduced after HCD (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2A). In contrast, while the mRNA level of Mettl3 was unchanged, its protein level was significantly reduced after HCD (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2A). Next, we explored the mechanisms behind the reduction of METTL3 and METTL14 during atherosclerosis. Aortic VSMCs derived from ApoE−/− mice were treated with 0–200 μg/mL ox-LDL for 48 h. We found that ox-LDL did not affect Mettl3 mRNA levels but significantly reduced Mettl14 mRNA levels in a dose-dependent manner (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2B). Moreover, both METTL3 and METTL14 protein levels were significantly reduced after ox-LDL treatment compared with control (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2C). To examine the clinical relevance of VSMC m6A in human atherosclerosis, we recruited 12 patients with atherosclerosis (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). We collected their aortas and purified human VSMCs by surgically removing the adventitial and endothelial layers. We analysed mRNA and protein expression levels in the atherosclerotic core and the proximal adjacent region of the same blood vessel, focusing on KLF4, a master regulator of atherosclerosis (for review, see Shankman et al.31) as well as METTL3 and METTL14 in human VSMCs. Both mRNA and protein levels of KLF4 were significantly increased, while METTL14 mRNA and protein levels were significantly reduced in the atherosclerotic core compared with the proximal adjacent region (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2D, E). However, METTL3 mRNA levels remained unchanged, but its protein levels were significantly reduced in the atherosclerotic core (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2D, E). Immunostaining revealed a significant reduction in %MYH11+ and %α-SMA+ cells, alongside a significant increase in %CD68+ cells within the atherosclerotic core (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3A–D). Furthermore, both METTL3 and METTL14 were significantly reduced in α-SMA+ VSMCs in the atherosclerotic core (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2F, G). Overall, our findings demonstrate that the METTL3/METTL14 methyltransferase complex is reduced in VSMCs during atherogenesis in both mice and humans. METTL3 is post-transcriptionally regulated, whereas METTL14 is transcriptionally regulated in the presence of ox-LDL.

Ablation of Mettl14, but not Mettl3, promotes phenotypic switching of VSMCs and aggravates atherogenesis

To examine whether VSMC METTL3 and METTL14 contribute to atherogenesis in mice, we generated VSMC-specific Mettl3 and Mettl14 knockout models, Myh11CreER;Mettl3fl/fl (referred to as VSMC-Mettl3cKO) and Myh11CreER;Mettl14fl/fl (referred to as VSMC-Mettl14cKO), by crossing Myh11CreER with Mettl3fl/fl and Mettl14fl/fl, respectively. Myh11CreER (referred to as VSMC-Mettl3WT or VSMC-Mettl14WT) was used as a control to eliminate the confounding effects of tamoxifen (Tam)-induced toxicity in group comparisons. To examine the knockout efficiency, we collected aortic media 2 weeks after Tam (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4A). Expression levels of METTL3 and METTL14, along with global m6A RNA methylation, were significantly reduced in VSMC-Mettl3cKO and VSMC-Mettl14cKO mice compared with controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4B–D). To investigate the effect of HCD on METTL3/14 expression and m6A RNA modification, mice were intravenously injected with adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) containing Pcsk9, which promotes LDL receptor degradation,32 and then fed an HCD to induce atherosclerosis (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4E). After 12 weeks on the HCD, aortic media were harvested for analysis. Again, METTL3, METTL14, and m6A levels remained significantly reduced in both knockout models (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4F–H). In terms of atherosclerotic development, VSMC-Mettl3cKO mice showed no significant difference in plaque formation along the aortas compared with controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4I). In contrast, VSMC-Mettl14cKO mice exhibited a significant increase in aortic plaque burden (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4J). Quantification of H&E and oil red O staining of frozen aortic root sections showed no significant difference in plaque area or lipid content between VSMC-Mettl3cKO and controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4K, L). Similarly, Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated no significant differences in collagen content or fibrous cap area between the two groups (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4K, L). In contrast, in VSMC-Mettl14cKO mice, both plaque area and lipid content were significantly increased, while collagen content and fibrous cap area remained unchanged (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4M, N). These findings suggest that METTL14, but not METTL3, plays a crucial role in inhibiting atherosclerotic plaque formation in VSMCs.

Next, we further examined whether VSMC-specific METTL3 and METTL14 contribute to atherogenesis using an additional model: ApoE−/− mice. To this end, we crossed VSMC-Mettl3cKO, VSMC-Mettl14cKO, and control mice with ApoE−/− mice. To assess the effect of a HCD on METTL3/14 expression and m6A RNA modification, we collected aortic media 2 weeks after Tam (see Supplementary material online, Figure S5A). Both METTL3 and METTL14 expression, along with global m6A methylation levels, were significantly reduced in VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/− and VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice compared with controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure S5B, C). Following a 2-week Tam washout, mice were placed on an HCD for 12 weeks, after which aortic media were harvested (see Supplementary material online, Figure S5D). METTL3, METTL14, and m6A levels remained significantly reduced in both knockout models (see Supplementary material online, Figure S5E, F). In terms of atherosclerotic development, Sudan IV staining, which detects lipids including fatty acid (FA), lipoproteins, and triglycerides, showed no observable difference in plaque formation along the aortas of VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/− mice compared with controls (Figure 1A, Supplementary material online, Figure S6A). Similarly, quantification of H&E and oil red O staining of aortic root sections revealed no significant differences in plaque area or lipid content between VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/− and controls (Figure 1B, C). Masson’s trichrome staining also demonstrated no significant differences in collagen content or fibrous cap area between these groups (Figure 1B, C). In contrast, VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice exhibited significantly increased plaque formation along the aortas compared with controls (Figure 1D, Supplementary material online, Figure S6B). Quantification of H&E and oil red O staining further confirmed a significant increase in plaque area and lipid content in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice (Figure 1E, F). However, Masson’s trichrome staining showed no significant differences in collagen content or fibrous cap area in this group (Figure 1E, F). Notably, METTL3 expression was significantly reduced in aortic media of VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice, whereas METTL14 remained detectable in media of VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/− mice, regardless of HCD treatment (see Supplementary material online, Figure S5B, E). These findings suggest that Mettl3 deficiency alone does not promote atherosclerosis, highlighting a distinct and critical role for METTL14 in regulating vascular atherosclerosis progression.

To evaluate potential contributions from other metabolic organs, we analysed lipid content in the liver and adipose tissues of VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− and control mice 12 weeks after HCD (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7A). H&E staining showed no observable changes in hepatic morphology, and quantification of Oil Red O staining indicated no significant differences in hepatic lipid content between groups (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7B). Serum ALT and AST levels remained unchanged, consistent with preserved liver function in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7C). Furthermore, quantification of H&E staining showed no significant differences in lipid content in epididymal and inguinal white adipose tissues or in brown adipose tissue between VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− and control mice (see Supplementary material online, Figure S7D). These results indicate no evidence of hepatic or adipose tissue remodelling in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice.

One of the key features of VSMCs during atherosclerosis is phenotypic switching. We found that the loss of Mettl14 in VSMCs led to a significant reduction in contractile markers, such as MYH11 and SM22α, but a marked increase in synthetic markers, including COL1A1, and the phenotypic switching activator KLF4, particularly after a HCD (see Supplementary material online, Figure S8A, B). Notably, HCD itself significantly reduced METTL14 expression in aortic media compared with a chow diet (see Supplementary material online, Figure S8A, B), confirming the in vitro findings (Figure 1E). Moreover, the loss of Mettl14 enhanced the migration ability of VSMCs under both n-LDL and ox-LDL conditions (see Supplementary material online, Figure S8C, D). To investigate this further, we analysed a scRNA-seq dataset from Myh11CreER;Rosa26ZsGreen;Ldlr−/− mice fed a HCD for 0–26 weeks.33 Through lineage tracing, VSMCs and their derivatives were marked as ZsGreen+. At baseline (week 0), Mettl14 expression was predominantly observed in ZsGreen+ VSMCs, ZsGreen+ minor VSMCs, and ZsGreen- fibroblast populations (see Supplementary material online, Figure S9A, B). However, during atherosclerosis progression, Mettl14 expression gradually decreased in VSMC populations but increased in ZsGreen+ macrophage-like VSMCs or ZsGreen- infiltrating macrophages (see Supplementary material online, Figure S9A, B). These observations suggest that METTL14 may play a critical role in the phenotypic switching of VSMCs into macrophage-like VSMCs. To validate this hypothesis, we generated lineage-tracing VSMC-Mettl14cKO;Rosa26YFP;ApoE−/− and control VSMC-Mettl14WT;Rosa26YFP;ApoE−/− mice and fed them a HCD for 12 weeks after Tam induction (Figure 1G). Immunostaining for YFP, Ki67, and TUNEL revealed a significant increase in Ki67+ cells per YFP+ area without changes in TUNEL+ cells per YFP+ area in the aortic roots of VSMC-Mettl14cKO;Rosa26YFP;ApoE−/− mice compared with controls (Figure 1H–K). Next, we examined whether METTL14 contributes to the phenotypic switching of VSMCs into macrophage-like cells, previously characterized as VSMC-lineage + CD45 + CD68+.34 Immunostaining of aortic root sections showed a significant increase in YFP + CD68+ within the total CD68+ macrophage population in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;Rosa26YFP;ApoE−/− mice compared with controls (Figure 1L). Consistently, quantification by flow cytometry further confirmed a significant increase in YFP + F4/80+ cells among the CD45+ population in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;Rosa26YFP;ApoE−/− mice (Figure 1M, gating strategy shown in Supplementary material online, Figure S10). These results indicate that loss of Mettl14 in VSMCs promotes the accumulation of macrophage-like VSMCs within atherosclerotic plaques. To further validate the role of VSMC METTL14 in vascular inflammation, we performed cytokine immunoassays, which revealed significantly elevated levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in aortic media from VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− aortas compared with controls (Figure 1N). Altogether, our results indicate that the ablation of Mettl14, but not Mettl3, in VSMCs exacerbates atherogenesis in both LDL receptor-deficient and ApoE-deficient mice. Loss of Mettl14 promotes lipid accumulation and atherosclerotic plaque formation, with lipid deposition primarily occurring in blood vessels, rather than in the liver or adipose tissue. Moreover, METTL14 is essential for the phenotypic switching of VSMCs into macrophage-like VSMCs, which may further drive inflammation and plaque progression during atherosclerosis.

Ablation of Mettl14, but not Mettl3, reduces PPAR-α/γ signalling and OXPHOS in VSMCs

To elucidate the regulatory role of METTL3 or METTL14 in VSMCs during atherogenesis, we focused on the cell-autonomous effects of Mettl3 or Mettl14 loss and subsequently validated these findings under HCD conditions. We performed a genome-wide RNA-seq analysis using aortic media isolated from VSMC-Mettl3cKO, VSMC-Mettl14cKO, and control aortas. Our analysis revealed that among the 350–400 genes significantly upregulated in either the VSMC-Mettl3cKO or VSMC-Mettl14cKO aortas compared with their respective controls, only 32 genes were commonly upregulated after the ablation of Mettl3 or Mettl14 (Figure 2A). Similarly, we identified approximately 650 genes that were significantly downregulated in the VSMC-Mettl3cKO or VSMC-Mettl14cKO groups, with only 45 genes being commonly downregulated after VSMC ablation of Mettl3 or Mettl14 (Figure 2A). These data suggest that METTL3 and METTL14 shape the unique molecular landscape of aortic media, with METTL14 indicating a potential for m6A-independent gene regulation. Next, we investigated the differential protein-coding gene expression profiles in VSMC-Mettl14cKO mice (see dataset Supplementary material online, Table S1). Among the 383 genes that were significantly upregulated in VSMC-Mettl14cKO compared with controls, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the most significantly upregulated gene-sets were associated with diabetic cardiomyopathy and HIF-1 signalling pathway (Figure 2B). On the other hand, among the 695 genes that were significantly downregulated in VSMC-Mettl14cKO compared with controls, KEGG pathway analysis displayed that the most significantly downregulated gene sets were associated with PPAR signalling pathway, FA metabolism, FA elongation, biosynthesis of unsaturated FA, regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, FA degradation, adipocytokine, and AMPK signalling pathways (Figure 2B). We validated several of the downregulated genes identified in Figure 2C, including Ppara and Pparg, along with some of their target genes, using aortic media isolated from VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/−, VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/−, and control mice fed a HCD for 12 weeks after Tam induction. RT-qPCR analysis showed that the transcript levels of Abca1, Cpt1b, Lpl, Pck1, and Ucp1 were significantly downregulated in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− aortas but not in VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/− aortas, compared with their controls (Figure 2D). We further confirmed whether METTL14 regulates PPAR signalling cascades by retrieving publicly available bulk RNA-seq data35 from mouse aortas treated with rosiglitazone, a PPAR-γ agonist, and its solvent control for comparative analyses. We identified 56 significantly differentially expressed genes in rosiglitazone-treated aortas that were also differentially expressed in aortic media from VSMC-Mettl14cKO mice (see Supplementary material online, Figure S11A). Consistent with the downregulated gene sets found in VSMC-Mettl14cKO aortas, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the most significantly upregulated gene sets in rosiglitazone-treated aortas were associated with PPAR signalling pathway, regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, adipocytokine, and AMPK signalling pathways (see Supplementary material online, Figure S11B). These findings confirm that METTL14 regulates PPAR signalling and lipid metabolism in VSMCs.

To further understand how the ablation of Mettl14 in VSMCs contributes to atherogenesis, we functionally assessed its regulation of the metabolic state of VSMCs, considering that PPAR-α and PPAR-γ are crucial regulators of lipid and glucose metabolism.6,7 We analysed the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of VSMCs derived from VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/−, VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/−, and control aortas using Seahorse technology in a glucose-based medium (Figure 2E). Quantification of the data revealed that basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP-linked respiration were significantly reduced in VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− aortas compared with controls, while no such reductions were observed in VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/− aortas (Figure 2F). Additionally, we measured the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR, Figure 2G), which indicates lactic acid levels formed from the conversion of glucose to lactate during glycolysis. Our results showed that glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve were significantly increased in VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− aortas compared with controls, whereas no such elevations were observed in VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/− aortas (Figure 2H). Given that both FAs and glucose ultimately generate energy through OXPHOS in the mitochondria, we attempted to investigate which of these substrates is more important for VSMCs. We analysed the mitochondrial OCR of aortic VSMCs cultured in either n-LDL or ox-LDL for 48 h. Following this, we treated the cells with and without etomoxir, a potent CPT1 inhibitor that irreversibly inhibits long-chain beta-oxidation,36 for 30 min (Figure 2I). Our results revealed that etomoxir reduced basal respiration by 59.48%, maximal respiration by 62.69%, and ATP-linked respiration by 65.94% in n-LDL-treated VSMCs (Figure 2J). In ox-LDL-treated ones, etomoxir led to reductions of 74.04% in basal respiration, 76.86% in maximal respiration, and 72.77% in ATP-linked respiration (Figure 2J). These data indicate that FAs are the preferred substrate for mitochondrial ATP production in these cells. Moreover, we found that mitochondrial OCR was significantly lower in ox-LDL-treated VSMCs compared with n-LDL-treated VSMCs (Figure 2J), suggesting that ox-LDL is less efficient than n-LDL for OXPHOS. We also measured energy generation from aortic media and confirmed that ATP production was significantly reduced in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− aortas compared with controls after 12 weeks of HCD (Figure 2K). Altogether, our findings suggest that the ablation of Mettl14, but not Mettl3, leads to decreased PPAR-α/γ signalling, reduced lipid metabolism, increased glycolysis, diminished OXPHOS, and lowered energy production in VSMCs.

Ablation of Mettl14 results in impaired beta-oxidation leading to the accumulation of glycerophospholipids in aortic media

Next, we investigated how VSMCs metabolize FAs in the absence of Mettl14 by studying the metabolic profiles of aortic media derived from VSMC-Mettl14cKO; ApoE−/− and control aortas 4 weeks after HCD using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, where applicable (Figure 3A). In the glycolytic pathway, the levels of glucose, glucose-6-phosphate, and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GA3P) remained unchanged (Figure 3B–D). However, the downstream metabolites pyruvate and lactate were significantly elevated in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− aortas compared with controls at both 4 and 12 weeks after HCD feeding (Figure 3E, F, Supplementary material online, Figure S12A–C). These findings, consistent with the elevated ECAR (Figure 3G, H), suggest that glycolysis is enhanced in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− aortas. Furthermore, RNA-seq analysis did not reveal any significant changes in the levels of glucose transporters Glut1 or Glut4 after ablation of Mettl14 in VSMCs. To further investigate, we measured the uptake of the fluorescently labelled glucose analog, 2-NBDG, in aortic VSMCs derived from both VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− and control aortas, using flow cytometry. The results showed no significant difference in glucose uptake between the two groups (see Supplementary material online, Figure S12D). Compared with controls, VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice exhibited no differences in fed glucose levels, fasting glucose levels, or glucose tolerance 12 weeks after HCD (see Supplementary material online, Figure S12E–G). These observations indicate that the increased glycolysis observed in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− was not due to enhanced glucose uptake, reduced glucose metabolism, or diabetes development.

In addition to lactate, pyruvate can also be converted into acetyl-CoA, allowing for entry into the TCA cycle. We observed reduced levels of acetyl-CoA, citrate, and malic acid in VSMC-Mettl14cKO; ApoE−/− aortic media at both 4 and 12 weeks after HCD (Figure 3G–I, Supplementary material online, Figure S12H, I), indicating decreased TCA cycle activity. Acetyl-CoA can also be generated from the mitochondrial conversion of long-chain FAs during beta-oxidation. To gain further insights into the influence of Mettl14 ablation on lipid metabolism, we assessed lipid levels in aortic media at 4 weeks after HCD by non-targeted metabolomics. According to the LIPID MAPS classification system, lipids can be divided into eight categories, including FAs, glycolipids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, saccharolipids, polyketides, sterol lipids, and prenol lipids (for review, see37) We observed significant changes in the levels of FAs, glycolipids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and sterol lipids in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice compared with controls, with the most notable changes in FAs and glycerophospholipids (Figure 3J). Specifically, we observed distinct changes in long-chain unsaturated FAs in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice, including reduced levels of linolenic acids (e.g. γ-linolenic acid, linoelaidic acid) and palmitoleic acid (e.g. 16-hydroxyhexadecanoic acid). Conversely, we found increased levels of polyunsaturated (e.g. FFA (22:5), docosa-4,10,13,16-tetraenoic acid) and monounsaturated (e.g. elaidic acid, 13-azaprostanoic acid) FAs (Figure 3K). Additionally, we identified 22 significantly elevated metabolites involved in glycerophospholipid synthesis (Figure 3L), which occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum (for review, see Coleman38). Compared with controls, we found significantly increased levels of glycerophospholipid precursors, such as lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) and phosphatidic acid (PA), as well as significantly elevated levels of glycerophospholipids, including phosphatidylcholine (PC), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI), and lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE) in VSMC-Mettl14cKO; ApoE−/− mice. These findings suggest that Mettl14 ablation in VSMCs reduces beta-oxidation and increases lipid accumulation.

To further validate the role of VSMC METTL14 in lipid metabolism, we examined lipid accumulation in VSMCs isolated from VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− and control mice using BODIPY and Dil-ox-LDL. Confocal microscopy and flow cytometry demonstrated significantly increased BODIPY and Dil-ox-LDL in METTL14-deficient VSMCs compared with controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure S13A–D). In contrast, VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/− mice exhibited no significant differences in lipid accumulation (see Supplementary material online, Figure S13E–H), suggesting that METTL14, but not METTL3, regulates neutral lipid and cholesterol accumulation in VSMCs. Given that impaired beta-oxidation is often indicative of mitochondrial dysfunction, which can lead to reduced TCA cycle activity, diminished OXPHOS, and increased ER stress, we next assessed mitochondrial function. Mitochondrial membrane potential and reactive oxygen species levels were evaluated using Mito-Tracker and dihydroethidium (DHE) staining, respectively. Both confocal microscopy and flow cytometry showed significantly reduced Mito-Tracker staining and elevated DHE fluorescence in METTL14-deficient VSMCs (Figure 3M–P), suggesting compromised mitochondrial function and increased oxidative stress due to lipid overload. To further confirm mitochondrial dysfunction, we used tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE) as an additional mitochondrial potential indicator, which also showed a significant reduction in METTL14-deficient VSMCs (see Supplementary material online, Figure S14B, C). In contrast, VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/− mice exhibited no changes in TMRE staining (see Supplementary material online, Figure S14D, E). To determine whether mitochondrial mass was affected, we measured mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number for genes encoding core subunits of the respiratory chain complexes (Complex I: Nd1-Nd6; Complex III: Cytb; Complex IV: Cox1-3; Complex V: Atp6, Atp8) and found no significant differences (see Supplementary material online, Figure S14F). Consistently, Western blot analysis showed no significant changes in TOM20 or PGC1-α, a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis (see Supplementary material online, Figure S14G). These findings indicate that loss of METTL14 impairs mitochondrial function without affecting mitochondrial mass or biogenesis, while METTL3 deficiency has no significant impact on mitochondrial lipid metabolism or function in VSMCs.

Our lineage tracing revealed that, at 3 months after HCD, approximately 40% of plaque cells originated from VSMCs (YFP+), while about 7.5% were CD45+ cells, including infiltrating macrophages (see Supplementary material online, Figure S15A). To determine whether the dysregulated lipid metabolism in atherosclerotic plaques was a secondary effect driven by infiltrating macrophages, we analysed BMDMs isolated from VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− and control mice fed a HCD for 12 weeks after Tam induction. RT-qPCR analysis showed no changes in the transcript levels of Mettl3, Mettl14, Ppara, Pparg, Abca1, Cpt1b, Lpl, Pck1, and Ucp1 in BMDMs from VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice (see Supplementary material online, Figure S15B). OCR measurements revealed no significant differences in basal respiration, maximal respiration, or ATP-linked respiration in BMDMs from VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice (see Supplementary material online, Figure S15C, D). Similarly, ECAR measurements showed no changes in glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, or glycolytic reserve (see Supplementary material online, Figure S15E, F). Confocal microscopy demonstrated no significant changes in BODIPY or Dil-ox-LDL levels in BMDMs from VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice (see Supplementary material online, Figure S15G, H). Collectively, the results suggest that METTL14 regulates lipid metabolism, and its deficiency in VSMCs leads to increased glycolysis, reduced TCA cycle activity, reduced mitochondrial OXPHOS, and enhanced lipid accumulation, particularly of glycerophospholipids. However, the dysregulated lipid metabolism in atherosclerotic vessels was not directly driven by infiltrating macrophages.

VSMC METTL14 activates Ppara and Pparg transcription through H3K4 trimethylation by recruiting SETD1A along with RNA pol II to the promoter

The above findings indicate that the Mettl14 deficiency leads to the inhibition of the PPAR-α/γ signalling pathway in VSMCs. To gain deeper insights into the regulatory mechanisms by which METTL14 influences PPAR- α/γ activity, we performed m6A-seq using VSMCs purified from VSMC-Mettl14WT aortas. This analysis revealed no significant peaks of m6A methylation marks within the 5′ untranslated region (UTR), coding sequence, or 3′ UTR of Ppara and Pparg genes (Figure 4A, B), indicating that METTL14 may regulate PPAR-α/γ signalling through mechanisms other than direct m6A modification of these transcripts. We then retrieved publicly available METTL14 ChIP-seq data from mouse stem cells39 and histone ChIP-seq data from mouse VSMCs.40 Our analysis showed that METTL14 binds to the Ppara promoter, where it is located adjacent to the histone mark, histone 3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3, Figure 4A). Similarly, METTL14 was found near the Pparg region, adjacent to both H3K4me3 and H3K27ac histone marks (Figure 4B). Based on these observations, we hypothesized that METTL14 activates the transcription of Ppara and Pparg through histone modifications. To explore the mechanism by which METTL14 mediates transcriptional activation of Ppara and Pparg, we examined whether METTL14 influences the epigenetic landscape in aortic VSMCs. We performed coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays targeting active marks, including H3K4me3, H3K27ac, and H3K36me3, as well as the repressive mark H3K27me3. The results indicated that METTL14 particularly enriched H3K4me3 in VSMCs (Figure 4C). Considering that METTL14-binding sites were near regions marked by H3K4me3, we investigated how METTL14 contributes to H3K4 trimethylation at the Ppara and Pparg promoters. In mammals, several Set1-like factors, including SETD1A, SETD1B, MLL1, and MLL2, are known to possess histone methyltransferase activity.41 We performed Co-IP assays to determine if METTL14 interacts with any of these histone methyltransferases in aortic VSMCs. Our results revealed METTL14 interacts specifically with SETD1A (Figure 4D). Notably, the N-terminal domain of SETD1A can also interact with RNA polymerase II (pol II),42 prompting us to investigate whether METTL14 could enhance the transcription initiation complex by forming a METTL14-SETD1A-RNA pol II complex. Indeed, we found that METTL14 directly interacted with RNA pol II in VSMCs (Figure 4D). Next, we compared these interactions in aortic VSMCs from ApoE−/− mice fed a HCD vs. a standard chow diet for 12 weeks. After normalizing to levels of β-ACTIN and H3 (Figure 4E), we observed significantly reduced METTL14-mediated enrichments of SETD1A and H3K4me3 in VSMCs from HCD-fed mice compared with those on a chow diet (Figure 4F). This reduction is likely due to decreased METTL14 expression after HCD. We further confirmed that SETD1A interacts with METTL14, but not METTL3, in VSMCs through reciprocal Co-IP assays (Figure 4G). To assess whether METTL14 is essential for SETD1A-mediated H3K4me3 deposition on the genome, we conducted ChIP-qPCR targeting specific regions of the Ppara and Pparg promoters (highlighted regions in Figure 4A, B) in aortic media from VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− and control mice after 12 weeks of HCD. Compared with controls, H3K4me3 enrichment at the Ppara and Pparg promoters was significantly reduced in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice (Figure 4H, I). Similarly, the enrichment of SETD1A at these regions was also significantly lower in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice (Figure 4J, K). Furthermore, we evaluated whether the increased activity of SETD1A after VSMC-specific Mettl14 depletion was a global or gene-specific effect. Western blot analysis revealed no significant changes in overall SETD1A or H3K4me3 levels in VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl3cKO;ApoE−/− or VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice (see Supplementary material online, Figure S16B), suggesting that global histone methylation is largely unaffected. To assess gene-specific regulation, we performed SETD1A knockdown in VSMCs using SETD1A siRNA, which led to a significant reduction in PPARA and PPARG expression (see Supplementary material online, Figure S16C). ChIP-qPCR assays further confirmed that SETD1A and H3K4me3 enrichment at the promoter regions of PPARA and PPARG was markedly reduced after SETD1A silencing (see Supplementary material online, Figure S16D, E). Correspondingly, lipid accumulation was significantly reduced after SETD1A knockdown, as evidenced by decreased BODIPY and Dil-ox-LDL staining (see Supplementary material online, Figure S16F, G). Collectively, these findings suggest that METTL14 regulates SET1A-mediated H3K4me3 in a gene-specific manner, particularly influencing lipid metabolism-related genes in VSMCs. To further confirm whether METTL14 regulates PPAR-α/γ signalling independently of its m6A methyltransferase activity, we performed a series of experiments. First, we overexpressed METTL3 in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs using modified mRNA (modRNA) technology.43 Western blot analysis confirmed a significant increase in METTL3 protein levels after modRNA treatment, accompanied by a corresponding increase in global m6A levels; however, the increase was notably less pronounced in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs compared with controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure S17A–C), suggesting that METTL14 may be required for efficient METTL3 expression or function. Importantly, METTL3 and m6A levels in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs treated with modRNA were comparable to those in wildtype controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure S17A–C), indicating that m6A activity was effectively restored. Next, we assessed whether restored m6A activity could rescue SETD1A-mediated PPAR-α/γ transcription. Mettl3 modRNA treatment did not affect global SETD1A or H3K4me3 levels (see Supplementary material online, Figure S17A, B). qPCR analysis revealed that expression of Ppara and Pparg remained significantly reduced in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs, even after METTL3 overexpression (Figure 5A). Furthermore, co-IP assays showed that Mettl3 modRNA did not restore the interaction between SETD1A and METTL14 in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs (Figure 5B). ChIP-qPCR analysis also demonstrated that METTL3 overexpression failed to rescue SETD1A or H3K4me3 enrichment at the Ppara and Pparg promoter regions (Figure 5C, D). In line with these findings, lipid accumulation remained elevated in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs following Mettl3 modRNA treatment, as indicated by BODIPY and Dil-ox-LDL staining (Figure 5E–G). These results suggest that restoring m6A activity alone is insufficient to rescue SETD1A-mediated PPAR-α/γ transcription or lipid metabolism in the absence of METTL14. To further determine whether m6A RNA modification is necessary for SETD1A-METTL14 interaction, we performed co-IP assays with RNase treatment, as previously described.44 The interaction between SETD1A and METTL14 was preserved after RNase treatment, and ChIP-qPCR analysis showed no difference in SETD1A or H3K4me3 enrichment at the Ppara and Pparg promoter regions in the presence or absence of RNA (see Supplementary material online, Figure S18), suggesting that the functional interaction between SETD1A and METTL14 is independent of m6A-modified RNA. Lastly, to directly test the role of METTL14’s methyltransferase activity, we generated a catalytically inactive METTL14 mutant (R298P), alongside wild-type METTL14, as previously described,44 and overexpressed both in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs. Western blot confirmed successful expression of both wild-type and R298P METTL14 proteins (see Supplementary material online, Figure S19A, B), but only the wild-type restored m6A levels (see Supplementary material online, Figure S19C). Despite lacking catalytic activity, the R298P mutant restored Ppara and Pparg expression to levels comparable with wild-type METTL14 (Figure 5H). Co-IP assays showed that both wild-type and R298P METTL14 restored interaction with SETD1A (Figure 5I) without affecting global SETD1A and H3K4me3 levels (see Supplementary material online, Figure S19A, B), and ChIP-qPCR analysis confirmed rescue of SETD1A and H3K4me3 enrichment at the Ppara and Pparg promoters (Figure 5J, K). Additionally, both constructs significantly reduced lipid accumulation in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs (Figure 5L–N). Taken together, these three independent lines or evidence demonstrate that METTL14 regulates SETD1A-mediated PPAR-α/γ transcription and lipid metabolism in an m6A-independent manner.

Rosiglitazone reduces lipid accumulation in aortic media and inhibits atherosclerosis after VSMC loss of Mettl14

Although transcript levels of Ppara and Pparg were reduced in VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl14cKO aortas, we asked whether activating the remaining nuclear receptors could rescue the dysregulated lipid metabolism caused by Mettl14 ablation. To test this, we purified aortic VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− and control mice and treated them with pemafibrate (a PPAR-α agonist) or rosiglitazone (a PPAR-γ agonist) for 48 h. Pemafibrate significantly reduced BODIPY and Dil-ox-LDL signals in VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl14cKO aortas, restoring levels back to those observed in controls, as shown by confocal microscopy (see Supplementary material online, Figure S20A–C) and flow cytometry (see Supplementary material online, Figure S20D, E). While the mRNA levels of Ppara and Pparg were not significantly altered by treatment, downstream target genes, including Abca1, Cpt1b, Lpl, Pck1, and Ucp1, were significantly upregulated in both control and VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− VSMCs (see Supplementary material online, Figure S20F). Similarly, rosiglitazone significantly reduced BODIPY (Figure 6A, B) and Dil-ox-LDL (Figure 6A, C) signals in VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl14cKO aortas, as demonstrated by confocal microscopy, normalizing levels to those of controls. The OCR data indicated significant increases in basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP-linked respiration in rosiglitazone-treated VSMCs from both VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− and control groups, with OCR levels in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− group normalizing after treatment (Figure 6D, E). Moreover, ECAR analysis demonstrated significant drops in glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve in rosiglitazone-treated VSMCs from both groups, with ECAR levels also returning to normal in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− following treatment (Figure 6F, G). These experiments functionally confirmed that lipid accumulation, along with the dysregulated OCR and ECAR observed in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− can be restored through activation of PPAR-γ.

Next, we examined whether PPAR-γ activation could delay atherogenesis in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice. Two weeks after Tam washout, both VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− and control mice were treated daily with rosiglitazone while being maintained on a HCD for 12 weeks (see Supplementary material online, Figure S21A). Sudan staining showed that rosiglitazone significantly reduced plaque formation along the aortas of both groups (see Supplementary material online, Figure S21B, Figure 6H, 6I). Consistently, quantification of H&E and oil red O stained frozen aortic root sections revealed that rosiglitazone significantly reduced plaque area and lipid content in both VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− and control mice (Figure 6J–L). However, Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated no significant differences in collagen content or fibrous cap area between the groups with or without rosiglitazone treatment (Figure 6J, M, N). RT-qPCR analysis showed no changes in the transcript levels of Mettl14, Ppara, Pparg, and Cpt1b, but rosiglitazone significantly increased the expression of Abca1, Lpl, Pck1 and Ucp1 in both groups, restoring their levels in VSMC-Mettl14cKO;ApoE−/− mice to those seen in controls (see Supplementary material online, Figure S21C). Additionally, rosiglitazone significantly enhanced glucose metabolism but impaired liver function in both groups, as evidenced by significantly increased serum ALT and AST levels, which were independent of VSMC METTL14 (see Supplementary material online, Figure S22A–F). Moreover, rosiglitazone did not affect lipid metabolism in the liver or adipose tissue of either group (see Supplementary material online, Figure S22G). Taken together, these results suggest that PPAR-γ activation effectively inhibits atherogenesis after the ablation of Mettl14 in VSMCs, highlighting the potential dysregulation of the METTL14/PPAR-α/γ axis in the context of vascular atherosclerosis.

Harnessing VSMC Mettl14 for therapeutic interventions in vascular atherosclerosis

Given the side effects of systemic rosiglitazone use, including liver damage, consistent with previous report,45,46 we investigated whether Mettl14 overexpression in VSMCs could provide atheroprotection in vivo. To test this hypothesis, ApoE−/− mice were intravenously injected with either control AAV (AAV9-Tagln-Control) or AAV-Mettl14 (AAV9-Tagln-Mettl14), both driven by the VSMC-specific Tagln promoter. Four weeks after AAV-mediated gene delivery, the mice were placed on a HCD for 12 weeks (Figure 7A). Western blot analysis confirmed a significant increase in METTL14 levels in VSMCs derived from the aortas of AAV-Mettl14-treated mice compared with those treated with control AAV (Figure 7B). Sudan staining showed a marked reduction in plaque formation along the aortas of AAV-Mettl14-treated mice compared with controls (Figure 7C, Supplementary material online, Figure S23). Quantification of H&E and oil red O staining of aortic root sections indicated a significant reduction in both plaque area and lipid content in AAV-Mettl14 treated mice compared with controls (Figure 7D, E). However, Masson’s trichrome staining did not show significant differences in collagen content or fibrous cap area between the two groups (Figure 7D, E). RT-qPCR analysis showed that AAV-Mettl14 treatment significantly increased the transcript levels of Mettl14, Ppara, Pparg, Abca1, Cpt1b, Lpl, Pck1 and Ucp1 in aortic media compared with controls (Figure 7F). Liver function remained unaffected after AAV-Mettl14 treatment, as indicated by unchanged ALT and AST levels (Figure 7G). To further elucidate the effects of Mettl14 overexpression on atherosclerosis, we conducted immunostaining for Ki67 with α-SMA and TUNEL with MYH11 on frozen sections of the aortic roots (Figure 7H, I). We observed a significant reduction in Ki67 + α-SMA+ cells with no change in TUNEL + MYH11+ cells (Figure 7J, K), indicating decreased proliferation and reduced phenotypic switching of VSMCs within the atherosclerotic lesions of AAV-Mettl14 treated mice compared with controls. Additionally, immunostaining for CD68 and α-SMA revealed a marked reduction in the number of CD68+ macrophages per α-SMA+ area within the atherosclerotic plaques of AAV-Mettl14 treated mice (Figure 7L, M). Further validation of vascular inflammation through cytokine immunoassays demonstrated significantly reduced levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, IFN-γ and TNF-α in VSMCs of AAV-Mettl14 treated aortas compared with controls (Figure 7N). Altogether, these results suggest that Mettl14 in VSMCs serves as an effective therapeutic target for inhibiting the development and progression of atherosclerosis.

Discussion

METTL14 has been studied in ECs, where it promotes atherosclerosis by enhancing endothelial inflammation47 and pyroptosis.48 Nevertheless, its functional role in VSMCs during atherosclerosis development has not been investigated in vivo. PPAR signalling pathways have also been extensively examined in macrophages and ECs in the context of atherosclerosis. These studies highlight crucial roles for PPAR-α and PPAR-γ in regulating lipid metabolism and inflammation, processes central to atherogenesis. Several PPAR-α and PPAR-γ agonists, such as fibrates and thiazolidinediones, have been tested in clinical trials to reduce cardiovascular events,49,50 but results have been largely disappointing, indicating our understanding of PPAR signalling remains incomplete. Additionally, thiazolidinediones have been associated with adverse effects, including hepatotoxicity, weight gain, and fluid retention,46,49 limiting their clinical utility. Cell-type specific targeting of METTL14/PPAR activity could overcome these caveats by selectively modulating signalling in defined cell populations. Nevertheless, whether PPAR-α and PPAR-γ expression in VSMCs, which contribute to all stages of atherosclerosis progression, directly regulates atherogenesis remains unclear. Here, we unveil a novel epigenetic mechanism by which METTL14 regulates PPAR-α and PPAR-γ expression in VSMCs, thereby conferring protection against atherosclerosis (Figure 8). Using both PCSK9-mediated LDLR deficiency and ApoE knockout mouse models, we demonstrate that VSMC-specific Mettl14 ablation impairs beta-oxidation, reduces mitochondrial OXPHOS, increases lipid accumulation, accelerates foam cell formation, enhances vascular inflammation, and exacerbates atherosclerosis. In contrast, VSMC-specific Mettl14 overexpression reverses these effects, reducing atherosclerosis development and progression. Lineage-tracing studies reveal that METTL14 regulates VSMC phenotypic switching, increasing macrophage-like VSMCs but not infiltrating macrophages. Lipid metabolism in infiltrating macrophages and lipid remodelling in the liver or adipose tissues was unaffected. Importantly, METTL14 directly drives vascular atherosclerosis protection through activating PPAR-α and PPAR-γ expression in VSMCs. Under healthy conditions, METTL14 forms a transcription initiation complex with SETD1A and RNA pol II, activating Ppara and Pparg transcription through H3K4 trimethylation in VSMCs. However, ox-LDL directly downregulates METTL14. In both mouse and human samples, METTL14 expression was significantly reduced in VSMCs from atherosclerotic tissues compared with non-atherosclerotic controls. The mechanisms underlying this loss of METTL14 in atherosclerosis remain unclear, warranting future investigation. Activation of PPAR-α and PPAR-γ with rosiglitazone restored impaired mitochondrial OXPHOS in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs and reduced lipid accumulation. Furthermore, rosiglitazone inhibited atherosclerosis development in VSMC-Mettl14cKO; ApoE−/− mice. No difference in atherosclerotic plaque burden was observed between the two groups of rosiglitazone-treated mice, which may be due to PPARγ expression in multiple vascular cell types; therefore, the target cell of rosiglitazone is not limited to VSMCs. Our findings are opposite to those seen in ECs, highlighting these cell type-specific effects, the therapeutic use of rosiglitazone or METTL14 modulators warrants careful consideration, as their impact may differ across cell populations critical to atherogenesis. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of the endogenous METTL14/PPAR-α/γ axis in VSMCs for treating atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

The hypothetical model of the study. METTL14 plays a crucial role in recruiting SETD1A and RNA pol II to the promoter regions of the genes Ppara and Pparg. This recruitment is essential for facilitating the transcription of these genes, primarily through the actions of SETD1A, which mediates the trimethylation of histone 3 lysine 4 (H3K4me3). This modification is known to enhance transcriptional activity. Under conditions of high ox-LDL, the expression of METTL14 in VSMCs is significantly decreased. This reduction in METTL14 leads to diminished PPAR-α and PPAR-γ expression, which are critical transcription factors involved in lipid metabolism. Consequently, lower expression of their target genes is resulted, including ABCA1, which is essential for cholesterol efflux; LPL, which is important for the breakdown of triglycerides; CPTB1, which facilitates the transport of long-chain fatty acids (FAs) into mitochondria for beta-oxidation. The reduction in these processes results in reduced activity of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), leading to diminished energy production within VSMCs. Additionally, the expression of PCK1, another target gene of PPAR-α and PPAR-γ, is significantly reduced. This suppression likely contributes to decreased gluconeogenesis, further impacting energy metabolism. In response to these metabolic changes, VSMCs adapt to utilizing glucose as their primary energy source.

Children with inborn defects of mitochondrial beta-oxidation enzymes are among the most common inherited metabolic diseases, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 10 000 to 1 in 15 000 live births.51 These children are often asymptomatic under normal feeding conditions, but short-term fasting can trigger severe and fatal clinical symptoms, including hypoglycaemia, liver dysfunction, and cardiomyopathies. The global knockout of PPAR-α in mice serves as a valuable model for studying both inborn and acquired abnormalities in human FA utilization. Similar to affected patients, PPAR-α knockout mice exhibit hypoglycaemia, myocardial and hepatic lipid accumulation, and reduced mitochondrial beta-oxidation in response to fasting.52 Additionally, a HCD promotes atherosclerosis development in Ppara/Apoe double knockout mice.53 Moreover, PPAR-γ plays a crucial role in regulating lipid metabolism, with higher expression levels often found in macrophages. PPAR-γ agonists have been shown to inhibit macrophage foam cell formation and reduce atherosclerosis in LDLR−/− mice.54 Consistent with these findings, our results indicate that VSMC-specific Mettl14-deficient mice display lipid accumulation, decreased beta-oxidation, and accelerated atherosclerosis. RNA-seq analysis demonstrated significantly reduced expression of PPAR-α and PPAR-γ target genes, such as Abca1, Cpt1b, and Lpl, in aortic VSMCs derived from VSMC-Mettl14cKO mice. Although specific effects in VSMCs have not been reported, dysfunction in these metabolic pathways in other cell types has been associated with impaired lipid metabolism and the development of atherosclerosis. For instance, ABCA1, the rate-limiting protein in cholesterol efflux facilitates the transfer of cholesterol to HDL and is found to be reduced in VSMCs from both atherosclerotic mice and humans.55,56 PPAR-α and PPAR-γ activators can induce ABCA1 transcription, thereby increasing cholesterol efflux in human macrophages.57 Cpt1b, which encodes the muscle isoform of carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1, regulates beta-oxidation by controlling the mitochondrial uptake of long-chain FAs. Deficiency of Cpt1b has been reported to worsen pressure overload-induced hypertrophic cardiomyopathy due to lipotoxicity in the heart.58 Furthermore, PPAR-α and PPAR-γ activators respectively induce lipoprotein lipase expression in hepatocytes and adipocytes, contributing to hypotriglyceridaemia in treated mice.59

In addition to the downregulated genes involved in cholesterol efflux and mitochondrial uptake for beta-oxidation, metabolomic profiling further identified dysregulated levels of metabolites potentially associated with the development of atherosclerosis. For example, omega-6 γ-linolenic acid and omega-7 palmitoleic acid were significantly reduced in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs. Previous clinical studies have shown that dietary consumption of linolenic acid leads to lower blood pressure, reduced serum triglycerides, increased HDL-c, and the production of anti-inflammatory compounds such as prostaglandin E1, which is inversely associated with coronary atherosclerosis in both men and women.60 Additionally, dietary intake of palmitoleic acid has been linked to reduced serum triglycerides and fewer atherosclerotic lesions in LDLR−/− mice.61 In contrast, trans FAs such as elaidic acid were significantly elevated in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs. Excessive intake of trans FAs is associated with increased serum LDL-c, reduced HDL-c, and impaired vascular function in healthy men and women.62 Furthermore, we identified 27 significantly elevated metabolites involved in glycerophospholipid synthesis. The initiation of glycerolipid biosynthesis begins with the esterification of long-chain FAs that do not enter mitochondria for beta-oxidation via CPT1, forming LPA, PA, and subsequently various glycerophospholipid species within the endoplasmic reticulumn.38 Increased intracellular PC and PG species were also observed in CPT1a-deficient alveolar epithelial cells, confirming the regulation of glycerophospholid synthesis by CPT1.63 These findings suggest that the increase in glycerophospholipid synthesis as seen in Mettl14-deficient VSMCs is likely a result of reduced mitochondrial beta-oxidation. While LPA is known to destabilize atherosclerotic plaques and trigger thrombosis by activating platelets and mast cells,64,65 and LPC has been identified as a major component of ox-LDL that plays a critical role in its atherogenic activity,66 the specific functions of different glycerophospholipid species in atherosclerosis remains to be determined in future studies.

Furthermore, we observed decreased levels of TCA cycle intermediates, including acetyl-CoA, citrate and malate, which are essential for generating NADH and FADH2. These co-factors drive mitochondrial OXPHOS and ATP production through the electron transport chain. Consequently, VSMCs from Mettl14-deficient mice exhibited lower OCR measurements, reduced mitochondrial potential, and diminished ATP production, likely due to decreased beta-oxidation and reduced TCA cycle activity. In contrast, levels of pyruvate and lactate were increased, and the ECAR was elevated in these mice. Besides, RNA-seq analysis revealed that Pck1, a target gene of PPARα/γ and a rate-limiting enzyme in gluconeogenesis,67 was the most significantly downregulated gene following VSMC loss of METTL14. The supply of glycerol, a substrate for glucose synthesis during gluconeogenesis, is likely reduced due to increased glycerophospholipid synthesis. These findings suggest that Mettl14 deficiency leads to inhibited gluconeogenesis and increased glycolysis in VSMCs (see Figure 8). In line with our observations, increased rates of glucose oxidation and glycolysis have also been reported in hearts isolated from PPAR-α knockout mice, despite unchanged glucose uptake.68 Moreover, both PPAR-α knockout mice53 and adipocyte-specific PPAR-γ knockout mice69 exhibit lower serum glucose levels when fed a high fat diet. The increased glycolysis observed in Mettl14-deficient VSCMs likely results from a secondary metabolic rewiring mechanism, allowing for energy generation from glucose to compensate for reduced beta-oxidation.

Mechanistically, we demonstrate that METTL14 functions as an epigenetic regulator controlling Ppara and Pparg transcription in VSMCs. ChIP-seq analyses confirmed overlapping peaks of METTL14 and H3K4me3 at the promoters of Ppara and Pparg. Our reciprocal Co-IP assays revealed that METTL14 interacts with SETD1A. Additionally, METTL14 was found to interact with H3K4me3 and RNA pol II. Knocking out of Mettl14 in VSMCs significantly reduced H3K4me3 and SETD1A binding at the Ppara and Pparg gene promoters, indicating METTL14’s crucial role in mediating SETD1A-dependent H3K4 trimethylation. Our findings position METTL14 as a key component of the transcriptional initiation complex involving SETD1A and RNA pol II. In this complex, SETD1A facilitates efficient H3K4 trimethylation, promoting gene activation. We hypothesized that in the absence of METTL14, the stability of the SETD1A-RNA pol II complex at the Ppara and Pparg promoters is compromised, leading to reduced H3K4 trimethylation and diminished transcription. To determine whether METTL14 regulates PPAR-α/γ signalling independently of its m6A methyltransferase activity, three complementary experiments were performed. First, overexpression of METTL3 in METTL14-deficient VSMCs restored global m6A levels but failed to rescue PPAR-α/γ expression, SETD1A interaction, promoter occupancy, or lipid accumulation, indicating that m6A restoration alone is insufficient. Second, RNase treatment showed that the SETD1A–METTL14 interaction and chromatin binding are RNA-independent, suggesting their function is not mediated by m6A-modified RNA. Lastly, overexpression of a catalytically inactive METTL14 mutant (R298P) restored PPAR-α/γ transcription, SETD1A recruitment, and lipid homeostasis, despite lacking m6A activity. Collectively, these results demonstrate that METTL14 regulates SETD1A-mediated PPAR-α/γ transcription and lipid metabolism through an m6A-independent mechanism. Mettl14 activates Ppara and Pparg transcription through H3K4 trimethylation by recruiting the SETD1A-RNA pol II complex to chromatin. This unveils a novel m6A-independent mechanism by which METTL14 regulates gene expression in VSMCs, with significant implications for understanding and treating vascular atherosclerosis.

In this study, we provide compelling evidence that Mettl14 regulates gene transcription in VSMCs in an m6A-independent manner. First, VSMC-specific ablation of Mettl14, but not Mettl3, resulted in dysregulated lipid metabolism and accelerated atherosclerosis development. Second, the majority of differentially expressed genes in VSMC-Mettl14cKO mice were not observed in VSMC-Mettl3cKO mice. Third, METTL14-binding sites on the transcripts of target genes, such as Ppara and Pparg, did not exhibit m6A modifications. Fourth, SETD1A interacts with METTL14, but not with METTL3, within the transcription initiation complex. Notably, while METTL3 protein levels significantly reduced in VSMCs from ApoE−/− mice after a HCD, its mRNA levels remained unchanged, suggesting post-transcriptional regulation under high cholesterol conditions. We further demonstrated that METTL3 was absent in VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl14cKO; ApoE−/− aortas, whereas Mettl14 was still expressed in VSMCs from VSMC-Mettl3cKO; ApoE−/− aortas. Given that METTL3 and METTL14 form a stable complex and that METTL14 is essential for supporting METTL3 structurally,70 we hypothesize that the reduced METTL3 protein levels are secondary to reduced expression of its scaffold protein. Both mRNA and protein levels of Mettl14/METTL14 were significantly reduced in VSMCs after a HCD. Consequently, m6A levels were also markedly decreased in HCD-fed mice compared with those on a chow diet, likely due to the destabilization of METTL3 in the absence of METTL14. These findings suggest that METTL3 is regulated post-transcriptionally, whereas METTL14 is transcriptionally downregulated in response to ox-LDL, although the underlying mechanisms require future investigation. Importantly, unlike METTL14, METTL3 and m6A modifications do not regulate the metabolic state of VSMCs, and their loss does not contribute to atherosclerosis development.

Last but not least, our findings from etomoxir treatments demonstrate that VSMCs primarily utilize FAs as the main substrate for energy production through mitochondrial OXPHOS, underscoring that VSMCs, similar to cardiac muscle, are metabolic tissues with high oxidative energy demands. Taken together, our study uncovers that METTL14, an RNA-binding protein, functions as a novel chromatin regulator of Ppara and Pparg transcription in VSMCs. This finding highlights a new therapeutic axis for treating atherosclerotic and metabolic disorders.

Coronary heart disease largely arises from atherosclerosis. Although PPAR-α/γ govern lipid metabolism and inflammation, their agonists have demonstrated limited anti-atherosclerotic efficacy, suggesting incomplete understanding. We reveal a VSMC-specific METTL14-PPAR axis: VSMC METTL14 is reduced within mouse and human lesions. VSMC Mettl14 loss drives lipid accumulation, foam cell formation, inflammation, and atherosclerosis, while overexpression reverses these effects. Mechanistically, METTL14 forms a SETD1A/RNA Pol II complex to H3K4-trimethylate and activate Ppara/Pparg, sustaining β-oxidation and mitochondrial OXPHOS. Rosiglitazone limits atherosclerosis in VSMC-Mettl14cKO mice, highlighting cell-specific therapeutic potential. These findings uncover a genome-regulatory role of the RNA-binding protein METTL14 in atherosclerosis prevention.
