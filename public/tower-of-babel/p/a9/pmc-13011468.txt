Background

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is the most common cause of peripheral vertigo, characterized by brief episodes of vertigo triggered by changes in head position relative to gravity. The posterior semicircular canal (PSC) is most frequently involved, producing an upbeat–torsional nystagmus during the Dix–Hallpike test [1]. An apogeotropic PSC-BPPV variant can present with downbeat–torsional nystagmus, resembling anterior canal (AC)-BPPV [2]. Distinguishing between these is essential because maneuvers differ: the Epley maneuver for PSC-BPPV and the Yacovino maneuver for AC-BPPV [2, 3]. Misdiagnosis between posterior and anterior canal BPPV may result in prolonged symptoms and unnecessary investigations. The key educational message of this case series is that apparent anterior canal BPPV may represent an atypical posterior canal variant, revealed by diagnostic transformation of nystagmus following repositioning maneuvers.

Case presentation

Between January and October 2024, 14 patients (mean age 56.2 years, 71% female), all of Kosovar ethnicity, presented to the vestibular diagnostics unit of RINO Clinic with positional vertigo and downbeat nystagmus on positional testing. Discussion

Our findings show that a substantial proportion of patients initially suspected to have AC-BPPV actually have apogeotropic PSC-BPPV, consistent with prior reports [2]. From a practical standpoint, clinicians should avoid definitive labeling of anterior canal BPPV at initial presentation when downbeat–torsional nystagmus is observed and instead perform early reassessment after repositioning maneuvers.

Pathophysiology

In apogeotropic PSC-BPPV, debris may adhere to the cupula or reside in the non-ampullary arm of the PSC [2]. Repositioning maneuvers can mobilize debris toward the ampullary arm, producing the classic PSC pattern and enabling successful Epley treatment.

Comparison with literature

Several published series have documented similar transformation rates, typically ranging between 55% and 67%, with the Epley maneuver being the most consistently effective treatment once conversion occurs [2]. These reports, along with our current data, highlight that many presumed AC-BPPV cases are in fact atypical PSC variants.

Recurrence

Recurrence rates in our series (28%) match published rates for BPPV (15–30%) [4]. Systemic risk factors such as vitamin D deficiency, osteoporosis, and thyroid disorders may contribute [4, 5]. Screening for these conditions is advisable in recurrent or atypical cases.

Lay summary

Sometimes vertigo looks like a rare form called anterior canal BPPV but is actually a common type that appears unusual at first. Careful follow-up after treatment can reveal the true cause and ensure the right therapy, leading to faster recovery.

Limitations

This study has limitations, including its single-center design and ethnic homogeneity of the patient population, which may limit generalizability to other clinical settings. In addition, the modest sample size precludes definitive conclusions regarding recurrence risk factors and adjunctive pharmacologic therapy.

Conclusion

Atypical PSC-BPPV should be considered when positional downbeat–torsional nystagmus is observed. Recognizing transformation patterns after initial treatment and using VOG can prevent misdiagnosis and ensure effective therapy.
