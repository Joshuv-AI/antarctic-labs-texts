Effect of amiodarone, diltiazem, or both to achieve strict heart rate control in dogs with atrial fibrillation
Authors: Sonja S Tjostheim, Heidi B Kellihan, Rebecca L Stepien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDogs with atrial fibrillation (AF) live longer if heart rate is strictly controlled (SHRC, 24-h mean heart rate ≤ 125 beats per minute [bpm]). Amiodarone and diltiazem are used to lower ventricular response rate (VRR) in dogs with AF, but information about their relative efficacy for achieving SHRC is lacking.Hypothesis/ObjectivesDescribe heart rate response with sequential escalation of amiodarone or diltiazem monotherapy to combination therapy.AnimalsThirteen client-owned dogs with AF.MethodsProspective cohort study with an adaptive treatment protocol. Dogs with AF with mean daily heart rate (MDHR) > 125 bpm (baseline, 24-h Holter) were randomized to receive amiodarone or diltiazem at standard doses. Dogs were reassessed 21 days later, and the other drug (amiodarone or diltiazem) was added if SHRC was not achieved. Dogs were then reassessed at 42 and 63-147 days after starting treatment. If MDHR >125 bpm at any repeat assessment, the second drug was added. Timing of final assessment was dependent on whether treatment was escalated.ResultsMonotherapy with amiodarone and diltiazem was initially used in 7 and 6 dogs, respectively. SHRC was achieved in 3 dogs (amiodarone, n = 2; diltiazem, n = 1) at first follow-up. At last follow-up, 11 of 13 dogs were receiving both drugs (median [range] 50 [20-153] days). Combination therapy of amiodarone and diltiazem did not result in SHRC (139 [94-160] bpm) in most dogs (7/11, 64%).Conclusions and clinical importanceMonotherapy with amiodarone or diltiazem or combination therapy of amiodarone/diltiazem did not achieve SHRC in most dogs in this study.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a supraventricular tachyarrhythmia associated with an increased risk of cardiac-related death in dogs.1 It is often a consequence of structural heart disease in dogs, termed secondary AF.2 Dogs with AF are commonly treated with antiarrhythmic medications that lower ventricular response rate (VRR). VRR is the term used to describe heart rates in people and animals with AF. Inadequate VRR control can lead to low-output heart failure or congestive heart failure (CHF).3 Dogs with secondary AF live longer when the ambulatory mean daily heart rate (MDHR) is ≤ 125 beats per minute (bpm), providing a tangible therapeutic target.4,5

Diltiazem is a calcium channel blocker used to treat dogs with AF to control VRR. Despite its negative inotropic effects, it is generally well-tolerated in dogs with CHF and it has not been associated with a significant reduction in left ventricular systolic function.6,7 Combination therapy with extended-release diltiazem and digoxin is the most effective and well-tolerated option for rate control investigated to date.7,8 This combination results in superior VRR control in dogs with AF compared to monotherapy with either drug, but 46%-50% of dogs receiving combination therapy still had median VRR > 125 bpm, indicating that alternative treatment regimens are required to achieve SHRC for some individuals.7,8

Amiodarone is a broad-spectrum antiarrhythmic medication with potassium, sodium and calcium channel blocking effects and non-competitive β-receptor antagonism.9 It has unusual pharmacokinetics, resulting in large variability in individual dog response, and it interacts with many other drugs.10 Amiodarone has been used for rate control in dogs with AF, though there is limited information available about its efficacy in dogs with naturally occurring disease.7,11,12 A retrospective study showed that amiodarone decreased VRR >20% in dogs with AF based on in-clinic ECG-derived VRR, which might overestimate MDHR.11,13 Amiodarone has minimal effects on left ventricular function and is well-tolerated by dogs in CHF, but might cause increases of liver enzymes without overt liver failure, hyporexia, vomiting, diarrhea, and thyroid dysfunction (expressed as changes in thyroxine or thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH]), or both; moreover, it can rarely cause keratopathy, neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia.11,12,14–19 Clinically relevant adverse effects seem to be infrequent, dose-dependent and reversible with dose reduction, but drug discontinuation might be required in some dogs.11,12,14,15,18 Some breeds (eg, Doberman pinschers) might be more susceptible to amiodarone-induced liver injury.12,15,19

The primary objective was to describe heart rate response with sequential escalation of amiodarone or diltiazem monotherapy to combination therapy in dogs with AF. Our secondary objective was to report adverse outcomes associated with chronic amiodarone therapy in dogs with naturally occurring heart disease.

Materials and methods

This was a cohort study with an adaptive treatment protocol. All procedures were approved by the institution’s animal care and use committee (IACUC protocol ID V006161-R01). Informed consent was obtained from owners before participation. Target enrollment was 10 dogs in each monotherapy treatment group (20 total). Sample size calculation appears in Supplementary material.

The study population was recruited by a board-certified cardiologist or supervised cardiology resident from client-owned dogs presented to the University of Wisconsin Veterinary Care Cardiology Service. Dogs diagnosed with AF were eligible if the in-hospital ECG VRR was > 160 bpm (indicating a likely MDHR > 125 bpm13) and baseline (untreated) 24-h ambulatory (Holter)-ECG MDHR was >125 bpm. Exclusion criteria included: (1) In-hospital ECG-derived VRR ≤ 160 bpm, (2) presence of clinically significant ventricular arrhythmia (defined as couplets or triplets, or ventricular tachycardia with heart rates > 180 bpm),20 (3) Doberman pinschers, (4) presence of known hepatic disease or serum concentrations of alanine transaminase [ALT] or alkaline phosphatase [ALP] > twice the maximum reference range value, (5) evidence of hemodynamic impairment (eg, weakness resulting in an inability to rise) requiring immediate therapy of AF, or (6) dogs already receiving antiarrhythmic drug therapy.

Dogs with persistent AF were identified by 6-lead ECG; the VRR was calculated from 3 different ECG segments and the averaged value was recorded. For dogs with an ECG VRR > 160 bpm, a CBC, biochemistry profile, thyroid profile, and non-invasive Doppler or oscillometric systolic blood pressure (BP) measurements (average of 5 measurements) were obtained, and a corneal exam performed. Additional testing (eg, echocardiography or radiographic examination) was performed at the discretion of the attending clinician. Signalment, echocardiographic diagnosis, presence of echocardiographic left ventricular systolic dysfunction, and presence of CHF were recorded. Systolic dysfunction was identified by measuring left ventricular volumes at end diastole and end systole using a single plane Simpson’s method of discs and was defined as a calculated ejection fraction <40% or as left ventricular end systolic volume indexed to body weight of >1.6, using the most recent echocardiogram.21 The client was asked to fill out a questionnaire validated to assess health-related quality of life in dogs with cardiac disease (functional evaluation of cardiac health [FETCH]22). A digital 3-channel Holter-ECG was performed using a commercially available system (NAVIX Diagnostics Inc., Taunton, MA; ALBA Medical Systems, Inc., Locust Valley, NY) and recordings were reviewed by a board-certified cardiologist or cardiology resident. The MDHR was recorded. If ventricular arrhythmias were present, the ventricular load (percent ventricular ectopic complexes in 24 h) and the complexity of the ventricular arrhythmia were evaluated to determine eligibility.

Qualifying dogs were assigned to an antiarrhythmic drug treatment using a random number generator (Random Number Generator for Excel, Microsoft Office 2011, Microsoft Corporations, Redmond, WA), with order of allocation established before the beginning of the study. Dogs were treated with either diltiazem (2-3 mg/kg PO q8h)8,23 or amiodarone (10-15 mg/kg PO q12h ×7 days, then reduced to 7.5 mg/kg PO q12h ×7 days, then reduced to 7.5 mg/kg PO q24h).11,12 Chronic oral administration of amiodarone results in a long plasma half-life (11 h) in dogs and therefore it was administered at a higher loading dose over the first 2 weeks before establishing a maintenance dose.24 Dogs with AF that were also in CHF received appropriate treatment of CHF at the direction of the attending clinician.

Enrolled dogs were evaluated at 3 subsequent time points (Figure 1). Recheck laboratory testing at each re-evaluation (CBC, biochemistry profile, thyroid profile), BP assessment, corneal exam, in-hospital ECG, and Holter-ECG were performed. The client was asked to fill out a FETCH questionnaire. The first recheck evaluation (R1) was scheduled 21 days after starting diltiazem or amiodarone monotherapy. If MDHR was ≤125 bpm, no change was made to the antiarrhythmic treatment protocol. If MDHR was >125 bpm, the second drug was added to the initial randomized drug therapy and combined therapy was continued for subsequent rechecks. Each dog was scheduled for re-evaluation (R2) 21 days after R1. If MDHR was >125 bpm and the dog was receiving monotherapy, the second drug was added to the initial randomized drug therapy as outlined for R1. Clinicians were allowed to escalate the diltiazem dose in dogs that were already receiving combination therapy at R2 if MDHR remained >125 bpm and there was concern that a fast VRR was contributing to persistence of clinical signs of CHF or low cardiac output. The last recheck evaluation (R3) was scheduled for either 21 days after starting the second antiarrhythmic drug (if applicable) or approximately 90 days after R2 if the antiarrhythmic treatment regimen was unchanged at R2.

Statistics

Data analysis was performed with commercially available statistical software (R Statistical Software, v4.4.0, R Core Team 2024). Significance was set at P < .05 for all analyses. Baseline descriptive statistics are reported as a median [range or 95% confidence interval, as indicated] because of small study sample size. Differences in baseline variables between the 2 monotherapy treatment groups were examined using the Mann–Whitney test for continuous data and Fisher’s exact test for categorical data. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the proportion of dogs with systolic dysfunction and CHF between treatment groups at baseline.

Fisher’s exact test was used to test for association between SHRC and treatment (amiodarone or diltiazem) at R1. A Wilcoxon matched pairs signed rank test was used to compare MDHR between baseline and R1 and baseline and final visit. The reported p-value is derived from the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while the 95% confidence interval represented the Hodges-Lehmann estimate of the median difference. Final visit was defined as R3 or R2 in cases where the dog was removed from the study before the R3 evaluation.

Total bilirubin and selected hepatic enzymes ([ALT] and [ALP]) and thyroid hormones (free thyroxine [FT4], total triiodothyronine [TT3], [TSH]) were defined as abnormal if outside of the hospital reference range. Fisher’s exact test was used to determine if the proportion of dogs with abnormal values differed between baseline and final visit. The same biochemical values and selected CBC results (hematocrit, white blood cell count, platelet count) were observed over time for dogs treated with amiodarone, and a linear mixed effects model was used to estimate the population rate of change in these measures, accounting for a random intercept effect. A Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used to compare BP between baseline and R1 and baseline and final visit for each dog.

Quality of life (FETCH) scores were tallied after every visit. The total score was the sum of responses ranging from 0 to 5 from 17 questions, with lower scores indicating a perceived improvement in clinical condition. A linear mixed model was used to evaluate the mean total FETCH score with MDHR ≤125 bpm and >125 bpm, accounting for within-subject correlation. Most dogs were in CHF at baseline; this might have confounded this analysis and therefore “visit” was added as a covariate to the regression model with R1 being the reference category.

Results

Thirty-two dogs with AF were screened between February 2019 and June 2023 (Figure 2). Eleven dogs were ineligible and an additional 3 dogs were not enrolled when owners declined to participate. Seven of 11 ineligible dogs had MDHR ≤ 125 bpm despite having an in-clinic HR > 160 bpm. Eighteen dogs were enrolled after the screening examination; 13 dogs completed the study.

Fourteen breeds were represented in the 18 enrolled dogs (Table S1) and all had structural heart disease. The primary echocardiographic diagnosis was myxomatous mitral valve disease in 12 of 18 dogs (67%), dilated cardiomyopathy in 4 of 18 dogs (22%), tricuspid valve dysplasia in 1 dog (6%), and right-sided cardiomyopathy of unknown etiology in 1 dog (6%). Fifteen dogs (83%) had CHF at baseline. Nine dogs (50%) had systemic disease (Table S2). This was the first documented episode of AF in 17 of 18 dogs. One dog was diagnosed with AF with a controlled VRR (in-clinic ECG VRR 100-120 bpm) without therapy 5 months before enrollment. This dog was screened and subsequently enrolled when the dog was presented with a rapid VRR at recheck examination.

After enrollment, 10 dogs were randomized to start amiodarone treatment, and 8 dogs were randomized to start diltiazem treatment. There was no difference in baseline characteristics between these initial groups (Table 1). Five dogs were removed before R1; 3 dogs receiving amiodarone (sudden death, n = 1; acute anorexia and diarrhea, n = 2), and 2 dogs receiving diltiazem (euthanasia, n = 1; lost to follow-up, n = 1; Figure 2).

There was a median [range] of 23 [20-49] days between baseline and R1 (n = 13), 29 [21-90] days between R1 and R2 (n = 13), and 64 [25-124] days between R2 and R3 (n = 9). One dog was in sinus rhythm by R2 after amiodarone was added at R1 and was in sinus rhythm for the remainder of the study. All other dogs were in AF for study duration. At R1, SHRC was not achieved in most dogs (10/13; 77%), necessitating combination therapy. Diltiazem was added to therapy in 5/7 dogs (71%) in the amiodarone treatment group and amiodarone was added to therapy in 5/6 dogs (83%) in the diltiazem treatment group after R1. Three dogs were adequately controlled at R1 with either amiodarone monotherapy (n = 2) or diltiazem monotherapy (n = 1). Two dogs (n = 1 amiodarone, n = 1 diltiazem) remained adequately controlled with monotherapy for the remainder of the study, while 1 dog receiving amiodarone required the addition of diltiazem at R2 to regain SHRC. Four dogs were not returned for R3. Of these, 2 dogs were lost to follow-up and 2 dogs were euthanized before R3.

A minority of dogs had CHF at recheck (R1: 5/13 dogs, 38%; R2: 4/13 dogs, 31%; R3: 3/9 dogs, 33%; Table S3). The MDHR was > 125 bpm in 5/5 dogs at R1, 3/4 dogs at R2, and 2/3 dogs at R3.

The median [range] loading dose of amiodarone was 24.2 [18.8-27.8] mg/kg/day for the first week and 15.0 [12.4-18.6] mg/kg/day for the second week. Median [range] maintenance doses of amiodarone and diltiazem were 7.5 [6.2-9.3] mg/kg/day and 7.3 [6.3-11.9] mg/kg/day respectively. One dog received an extended-release formulation of diltiazem (6.7 mg/kg/day) at the owner’s request; the remaining dogs treated with diltiazem received the standard formulation. Dose escalation of standard formulation diltiazem occurred in 2 dogs at R2; the dogs were receiving combination therapy and remained inadequately controlled (MDHR 144 and 151 bpm, respectively) at R2. Diltiazem dose was increased from 5.86 to 8.79 mg/kg/day in the first dog, and from 7.24 to 9.05 mg/kg/day in the second dog.

Heart rate analyses

Strict heart rate control was not achieved with monotherapy in most dogs (10/13; 77%) by R1 (Table 2). There was no difference between treatment groups in the proportion of dogs that achieved SHRC by R1 (amiodarone—diltiazem = 0.12, 95% CI, −0.40 to 0.56, P = 1.00). Both drugs resulted in a decrease in VRR despite not achieving SHRC in most cases. The MDHR was significantly lower after initial therapy with either amiodarone (7 dogs; 95% CI, −62 to 4, P = .03) or diltiazem (6 dogs; 95% CI, −67 to −5, P = .03; Figure 3).

Laboratory values

There was no difference between baseline and final visits in the proportion of dogs with abnormal hepatic enzymes (normal [ALT] − abnormal [ALT] = 0.07, 95% CI, −0.29 to 0.42, P = 0.73; normal [ALP] − abnormal [ALP] = 0.34, 95% CI, −0.07 to 0.63, P = .13) or abnormal [FT4] (normal [FT4] − abnormal [FT4] = 0.28, 95% CI, −0.15 to 0.60, P = .23) or [TSH], (normal [TSH] − abnormal [TSH] = 0.51, 95% CI, 0-0.74, P = .06). There was a difference in [TT3] (normal [TT3] − abnormal [TT3] = 0.52, 95% CI, 0.32-1.04, P = .03), but it was not considered significant after correcting for multiple comparisons (using a Bonferonni-corrected α of 0.05/5 = 0.01). Six of 18 dogs had abnormal [TT3] at baseline; [TT3] was below the reference range in 5 dogs and above it in 1 dog. The [TT3] was within reference limits by the final visit in all dogs. Low [TT3] at baseline was suspected to be euthyroid sick syndrome as the baseline [TSH] was within reference limits and because the [TT3] normalized by R1 in all affected dogs. Total bilirubin was measured in 16 of 18 dogs at baseline, and 12 of 13 dogs had at least 1 measurement at subsequent recheck evaluations. Total bilirubin was within reference limits at all time points; however, it was not measured again after starting amiodarone in 3 of 12 dogs. All dogs had red blood cell and white blood cell counts within reference limits at the final visit. One dog receiving amiodarone had a decreased platelet count (138 × 103/μL, reference interval 175-500 × 103/μL) at R3. This was attributed to enlarged platelets that were not included in the automated count. The platelet count was normal for all other dogs at the final visit.

Longitudinal modeling showed that the average rate of change was not different from 0 for total bilirubin, [ALT], [ALP], [FT4], [TT3], [TSH], hematocrit, white blood cell count, or platelet count after starting amiodarone.

There was no difference in BP between baseline and R1 visits in dogs treated with amiodarone (95% CI, −37 to 61, P = .22) or diltiazem (95% CI, −45 to 12, P = .31), or baseline and the final visit (95% CI, −23 to 22, P = .92), when most dogs (11/13, 85%) were receiving combination therapy (Table S4).

The mean FETCH score was 13.8 points higher at baseline than at R1 (P < .001), with small decreases at subsequent visits. With all visits included in the linear mixed model, the total FETCH score was estimated to be 10 points higher for visits where the dog had a MDHR > 125 bpm (P = .02). Including a covariate in the regression model for visit (with R1 being the reference category) showed that the association between FETCH score and uncontrolled VRR (MDHR > 125 bpm) is likely being driven by the baseline visit when all dogs had uncontrolled VRR and most dogs were in CHF. Adjusting for visit, the estimated FETCH score for dogs with MDHR > 125 bpm was 0.6 points higher than the FETCH score for dogs in which SHRC had been achieved (P = .90).

Adverse drug effects

Two dogs were removed from the study before R1 after starting amiodarone. The first dog was hyporexic and had vomiting and diarrhea within 2 weeks of starting amiodarone. Amiodarone was discontinued for 1 week and these signs resolved. Amiodarone was restarted at a lower dose and within 1 week the dog became hyporexic again. Amiodarone was discontinued and the dog was removed from the study. The second dog developed diarrhea and anorexia after receiving a single amiodarone dose. Amiodarone was discontinued and the dog was removed from the study at the owner’s request. The cause of the anorexia, diarrhea, and vomiting was not determined as blood work was declined by the owners of both dogs in favor of drug discontinuation and supportive care.

Two dogs receiving amiodarone had a [FT4] below the reference range coupled with an elevated [TSH]. Neither dog showed clinical signs typical of hypothyroidism. The first dog had a decreased [FT4] and an elevated [TSH] at R2. The dog was not treated for hypothyroidism and the [TSH] was within reference at R3. The second dog had a decreased [FT4] and an elevated [TSH] at R3. Amiodarone was discontinued at that time.

A small circular corneal plaque was reported in one dog at R2; prior corneal exams were normal. This lesion was not identified at R3. Corneal examinations were normal in all other dogs.

Discussion

In this small sample, monotherapy did not result in SHRC in most dogs (10/13; 77%). Additionally, there was no difference between monotherapy treatment groups in the proportion of dogs in which SHRC was achieved. Most dogs were receiving both drugs by the time of the last follow-up (11/13; 85%). Combination therapy of amiodarone and diltiazem resulted in greater decreases in MDHR compared to monotherapy with either drug, however, this should be interpreted cautiously because of the adaptive study design. Combination therapy still did not lead to SHRC in most dogs.

It is more difficult to attain SHRC than lenient rate control in people with AF. The RACE II clinical trial, which evaluated differences in outcomes when the target VRR was lenient (resting VRR < 110 bpm) or strict (resting VRR < 80 bpm), showed that lenient rate control was achieved in 98% of people while SHRC was achieved in only 67%.25 In the current study, SHRC was achieved in only 46% (6/13) of dogs by last visit. A prospective study where dogs with AF were treated with diltiazem, digoxin, and the combination of the two drugs showed that combination therapy resulted in SHRC in approximately 50% of treated dogs after 2 weeks (median VRR 126 bpm, n = 18).8 A more recent retrospective study showed that the combination of diltiazem and digoxin resulted in SHRC in 54% of dogs.7 In the current study, there were 12 dogs with CHF at R1, R2, or R3 and SHRC was achieved in only 2 of 12 (17%) dogs. Sympathetic nervous system activation is a compensatory response to both systolic dysfunction and CHF and might counter the negative dromotropic effects of antiarrhythmic drugs.26 Alternative drug or dosing strategies might be warranted in these cases. In some dogs, SHRC can be achieved after escalating drug doses, and the survival benefit is still apparent regardless of when it occurred.27 Diltiazem dose escalation occurred in only 2 dogs in this study, so no conclusions can be drawn about efficacy of dose escalation of diltiazem, and amiodarone dose escalation was not attempted due to concern for dose-related adverse effects.12,15

Randomized clinical trials have evaluated the efficacy of antiarrhythmic medications, including β-blockers, nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, digoxin, and amiodarone, in achieving rate control in people with symptomatic AF.28–37 These medications slow conduction and prolong atrioventricular nodal refractoriness by different electrophysiologic mechanisms, with the common effect of decreasing the VRR.9,38 Combination therapy appears to result in greater decreases in VRR in people compared to monotherapy,28–30,34,37 however, there is no consensus on which drug combinations are most successful in achieving adequate rate control.37 Some studies suggest that β-blockers result in greater rate control in human AF,34 but dogs might not tolerate β-blockers.39,40 Amiodarone might be an option to attain rate control in these dogs as it is in people when β-blockers are not tolerated.38

Amiodarone decreases VRR in dogs with AF, and dogs experiencing CHF appear to tolerate this medication clinically.11 Due to its broad spectrum of antiarrhythmic effects, amiodarone might be the preferred therapy for AF with concurrent ventricular tachyarrhythmias—a clinical picture that might be more frequent than previously thought.11,12,15,18,41 A recent study demonstrated that dogs with secondary AF typically show a high number of ventricular ectopic complexes on 24-h Holter-ECG (median: 2606), often organizing into clinically relevant allorhythmias.41 This is the first study to investigate amiodarone’s efficacy for achieving SHRC. The results showed that SHRC was not achieved in most dogs with monotherapy with either drug or with combination drug therapy. These findings might be due to drug and dog factors. Amiodarone is predominately a potassium channel blocker and sodium channel blocker. The negative dromotropic effects result from ancillary L-type calcium channel inhibition and weak non-competitive β-receptor antagonism; these relatively weaker effects might be insufficient to achieve SHRC.9 It is also possible that the amiodarone or diltiazem doses used were not the optimal dose needed to achieve SHRC. The median maintenance dose of amiodarone was 7.5 mg/kg/day, which falls within the range of median maintenance doses reported in dogs with naturally occurring cardiac diseases (4.3-12.1 mg/kg/day).11,12,15,18,19 The median amiodarone dose reported here is lower than the optimal antiarrhythmic dose of 10-40 mg/kg/day, which was designed to achieve therapeutic serum concentrations between 1.0-2.5 μg/mL in dogs.14,42,43 The dose used in the study was purposefully chosen with safety in mind as many of the adverse effects of amiodarone are dose-dependent.11,14,15,18,19 The median diltiazem dose (7.3 mg/kg/day) also fell within the reported ranges (3.2-11.3 mg/kg/day)7,8 used to treat dogs with secondary AF. Amiodarone significantly increases the plasma concentration of diltiazem in dogs, so plasma concentrations might have been higher than expected based on dose.44 Therapeutic drug levels were not performed for either amiodarone or diltiazem to confirm adequate dose or proper absorption. Lastly, amiodarone has unusual pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties which might result in variability in individual therapeutic response.24,45

There are other factors that might have contributed to an inability to attain SHRC. Most of the dogs had advanced heart disease and 83% were in CHF at enrollment. Drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics can be altered in patients with CHF by changes in neuroendocrine, inflammatory, and metabolic mechanisms, concurrent drug therapies, and impacts of co-morbidities.46 Chronic atrial dilatation and CHF lead to myocardial ischemia, inflammation, calcium overload, and interstitial fibrosis, which can result in myocardial cell death and replacement fibrosis. Tachyarrhythmias can result in or further exacerbate these molecular and structural changes, some of which can be reversed with adequate arrhythmia control.47 Reversible cardiac remodeling has been documented in dogs with AF with rate control.27,48 Degree of rate control and the amount of reverse remodeling are correlated; a recent study showed that dogs had greater reverse cardiac remodeling when SHRC was achieved compared to when it was not.27 The current study was not designed to follow cardiac dimensions after enrollment, and therefore it is unknown if dogs experienced reverse cardiac remodeling. It is possible that disease severity contributed to inadequate rate response, leading to worsening cardiac remodeling.

The combination of amiodarone and diltiazem resulted in a smaller percent decrease in MDHR compared to the combination of digoxin and diltiazem previously reported (Table 2).8 The combination of amiodarone and diltiazem did not demonstrate clear superiority at the doses used in this study. This combination might not provide additional benefit compared with published digoxin and diltiazem data, but direct comparisons cannot be made across studies.

Twenty-two percent of screened dogs (7/32) were not enrolled because their MDHR was ≤ 125 bpm on Holter-ECG despite having an ECG-derived VRR > 160 bpm in hospital. Six of these dogs (6/7; 86%) had abnormalities on echocardiogram at baseline and none were in CHF; the elevated VRR in hospital was tentatively attributed to anxiety. Based on these observations, a Holter-ECG is recommended before starting antiarrhythmic treatment in dogs that have AF and in-clinic ECG-derived VRR > 160 bpm but no signs of CHF.

Potential adverse drug effects were reported in 2 of 15 dogs after starting amiodarone. Both experienced decreased appetite and diarrhea, one with concurrent vomiting. These signs resolved after amiodarone was discontinued. Anorexia, vomiting, and diarrhea associated with amiodarone treatment have been described in people, dogs, and cats, and these signs might be a consequence of liver injury, acute pancreatitis, or direct gastrointestinal irritation.11,12,14,15,18,19,49–51 Clinical improvement is reported after amiodarone dose reduction or discontinuation.11,12,15,18,19,50,51 Dose reduction was attempted in one dog, however, the dog became anorexic again and amiodarone was permanently discontinued. The remainder of the study population (n = 13) did not experience clinical signs attributed to amiodarone toxicity. Selected laboratory values screening for increases in liver enzymes, thyroid dysfunction, and cytopenias were followed during the study, and there were no significant changes in these values over time. Two dogs developed biochemical changes suggestive of hypothyroidism while receiving amiodarone, but neither dog showed clinical signs of this disease. In people, adverse effects of amiodarone are both time-dependent and dose-dependent.52 The biochemical changes suggestive of hypothyroidism did not appear until near the end of the study, and therefore it is possible that they might have developed syndromic hypothyroidism with continued amiodarone exposure. Regular surveillance for biochemical and clinical evidence of hypothyroidism is suggested for all dogs receiving amiodarone.

Diltiazem appeared to be well tolerated in all study dogs with no change in BP and no reported adverse effects when receiving diltiazem alone or in combination with amiodarone. This is consistent with previous reports of dogs with AF.7,8

The FETCH scores were improved at R1 compared to baseline, likely due in part to better rate control after starting antiarrhythmic treatment.53 Many dogs also had CHF at baseline and treatment for CHF was either initiated or adjusted at that time. Improvements in FETCH scores were likely multifactorial in most dogs, resulting from both better rate control but also improvement of congestive signs if present. Once visit number was considered, there was no difference in FETCH scores between dogs with and without SHRC.

There are several limitations. The study is underpowered for comparative effectiveness between the treatments because of the small population size and high attrition rate. The progressive attrition results in diminishing sample size across time points and therefore limits interpretation of longitudinal outcomes. An unexpected number of dogs did not qualify for enrollment, highlighting both variability in presentation in dogs with AF and the value of performing a Holter-ECG prior to initiation of antiarrhythmic drugs, especially if they are not in CHF. The study used MDHR as the only metric to determine whether SHRC was achieved since MDHR has been correlated with survival.4,5 Other Holter-ECG metrics (number of hours in which VRR remained ≤ 125 bpm, minimum or maximum VRR) were not investigated, and therefore periods of poor rate control might have been missed. The amiodarone dose was lower than the dose recommended to achieve therapeutic drug levels in dogs.14,42,43 Drug plasma concentrations were not measured so some dogs might not have received therapeutic doses of amiodarone. However, MDHR was significantly lower at R1 compared to baseline in individuals treated with amiodarone, potentially indicating some clinical effect. The unusual pharmacokinetics of amiodarone might have increased variability in individual response, and this might have resulted in a disproportionate effect on the results in this small population. Most dogs were in CHF at baseline evaluation. Sympathetic nervous system activation secondary to CHF might have impacted the baseline MDHR,26 and treatment of CHF might have contributed to the initial rate response. The study was not designed to investigate changes in echocardiographic measurements of systolic function and therefore it is unknown if there is a correlation between systolic function and VRR, or if functional parameters were impacted by the antiarrhythmic drugs. Dogs with ventricular arrhythmias of low complexity were enrolled. The frequency or complexity of ventricular arrhythmias documented on Holter-ECG at R1, R2, or R3 were not analyzed and therefore it is unknown if amiodarone influenced this arrhythmia. Systolic BP values were highly variable with inaccurate heart rate detection, raising concerns that the data was unreliable. Oscillometric BP measurements can be inaccurate in patients with AF due to the beat-to-beat changes in stroke volume and high VRR.54 Oscillometric monitors designed for human use are less accurate at heart rates > 100 bpm, and dogs with AF in this study had in-clinic median VRR > 200 bpm.54,55 Lastly, measurements of ECG waveforms, segments, and intervals were not analyzed. While the risk of amiodarone-associated QT prolongation in dogs with AF is reported to be low,18 the incidence of QT prolongation in this study is unknown.

Conclusion

In this study, neither monotherapy with amiodarone or diltiazem, nor combination therapy with both drugs achieved SHRC in most dogs with AF. Given the limited number of dogs enrolled and the high attrition rate, future studies are needed to confirm and expand upon these preliminary findings.
