Risk factors for ocular complications in HIV/AIDS patients: a retrospective analysis of ophthalmology consultations in a tertiary hospital in Central China
Authors: Renfan Zhang, Tian Zheng, Xiaomin Chen, Lin Yang, Wen Zeng, Min Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) affects 39.9 million people worldwide. Despite antiretroviral therapy, sight-threatening ocular complications, such as cytomegalovirus retinitis (CMVR), remain common, yet contemporary incidence data from Asian populations are scarce.PurposeTo quantify the incidence of fundus lesions and identify independent predictors of ocular lesions among HIV-positive inpatients referred for ophthalmologic evaluation at a tertiary center in Central China.MethodsAll consecutive HIV-1-positive inpatients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent standardized ophthalmic screening from 19 November 2019 to 9 June 2023 were included. Data on demographic, systemic, and ocular characteristics were collected. Binary logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated with fundus lesions.ResultsOf 345 eligible individuals, 117 had fundus lesions, classified into three main types. The distribution was 35.9% for opportunistic infections, 59.8% for HIV-related microangiopathy, and 4.3% for syphilis-related uveitis. The opportunistic infections group was common among the youngest age group [median age: 41.5 years, IQR: 32–54 years], with 38.1% reporting blurred vision. Patients in the microangiopathy group had the lowest CD4+ T cell count [median count: 18.5 cells/µL, IQR: 5–40.25 cells/µL] and the shortest duration of HIV/AIDS [median month: 0.7 months, IQR: 0–6.5 months]. The syphilis-related uveitis group had the longest duration of HIV/AIDS [median month: 24 months, IQR: 8.125–54 months], the highest CD4+ T cell count [median count: 166 cells/µL, IQR: 41–224.5 cells/µL], and the most frequent complaint of blurred vision (80%).ConclusionIn this contemporary Central-Chinese cohort, CMVR remains the leading ocular sequela despite widespread HAART. Age, nadir CD4+ < 50 cells/µL, and symptomatic visual loss are independent red flags. We propose routine dilated fundus examinations every 3 months for patients with any of these predictors and immediate ophthalmic referral at the onset of visual symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04916-4.

Introduction

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), caused by human immunodeficiency virus type-1 (HIV-1), affected an estimated 39.9 million people worldwide by December 2023 [1]. Despite widespread use of combination antiretroviral therapy (cART), immune dysfunction persists in a substantial minority, increasing the risk of sight-threatening ocular sequelae [2]. Retinal microangiopathy, cytomegalovirus retinitis (CMVR), immune recovery uveitis (IRU), and syphilitic posterior uveitis remain the principal manifestations, yet population-based incidence data from contemporary Asian cohorts remain scarce.

Ocular involvement is often asymptomatic until irreversible visual-field loss occurs; prior work from sub-Saharan Africa reported fundus lesions in up to 78% of treatment-naïve patients [2]. These complications can present with early clinical symptoms, such as sudden-onset blurred vision or visual field loss, or with milder symptoms, such as mild eye discomfort, floaters, or no symptoms [3]. Furthermore, studies have indicated that AIDS patients face a substantial risk of retinopathy progression within 5 years [4], and early immunotherapy has been shown to aid in vision restoration [5]. Whether these risk relationships hold in central China, where late presentation and delayed cART initiation are common, remains unknown. Clarifying region-specific risk profiles would inform screening intervals and conserve limited ophthalmology resources.

We therefore conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive HIV-positive inpatients who underwent standardized ophthalmic consultation at a 1,500-bed tertiary center in Wuhan, central China, from 19 November 2019 to 09 June 2023. The primary objective was to determine the incidence and independent predictors of fundus-confirmed lesions in the cART era. We hypothesized that nadir CD4+ T cell count < 50 cells/µL, increasing age, and symptomatic visual loss would confer an elevated hazard, and that early cART exposure would be protective. The findings are intended to inform evidence-based, risk-stratified screening algorithms for PLWH in resource-constrained settings.

Methods

Data collection

We conducted a single-center, observational study of all inpatients with laboratory-confirmed HIV-1 infection referred for ophthalmologic assessment from the Department of Infectious Diseases at Zhongnan Hospital (a tertiary referral center with 1,500 beds) from 19 November 2019 to 9 June 2023. Cases were identified through the hospital’s electronic medical record (EMR) system. Demographic, systematic, and ocular data were extracted and entered into a predefined, locked database.

Baseline: Age, gender, self-reported ethnicity, date of HIV confirmation, AIDS-defining event, nadir CD4⁺ T cell count, and antiretroviral history.

Referral: Indication for ophthalmic consultation (free text mapped to the pre-specified categories by two independent clinicians).

Ocular examination: Aqueous humor PCR for herpesvirus DNA was performed when clinically indicated; positivity was defined as ≥ 10³ copies/mL. A comprehensive ophthalmologic examination was conducted, including visual acuity, intraocular pressure, visual field testing, fundus photography, optical coherence tomography (OCT), fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA), and other relevant ophthalmic tests. Three investigators independently assessed all data to ensure accuracy and reliability.

Definitions

The baseline visit was defined as the first ophthalmic encounter within the study window. Any of the following findings on dilated examination was considered an ocular complications related to HIV: (i) cotton-wool spots consistent with HIV microangiopathy; (ii) necrotic retinal lesions with extensive bleeding and positive aqueous PCR for cytomegalovirus, varicella-zoster, or herpes simplex virus; (iii) posterior uveitis with positive syphilis serology after exclusion of toxoplasmosis and viral retinitis by OCT/FFA; (iv) rhegmatogenous retinal detachment secondary to (ii) [3]. To minimize potential observer bias, two independent ophthalmologists graded all ocular images based on standardized CMVR diagnostic criteria, and a third senior ophthalmologist resolved discrepancies.

We excluded eyes with prior intraocular surgery within 90 days, prior trauma, or concomitant retinal pathology (age-related macular degeneration or diabetic retinopathy) that could confound lesion attribution, as well as records with insufficient imaging or clinical data.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA, www.graphpad.com) and SPSS (IBM Corp., released 2011. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 20.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Group differences in continuous variables were assessed with the Mann-Whitney U test and the Kruskal-Wallis test. Differences in categorical variables were assessed with the chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test.

Generalized linear mixed models were fitted to assess the crude association between lesion type and Blurred Vision. To preserve the total effect of lesion type, covariates such as age and duration of HIV/AIDS were not adjusted for in this primary analysis.

Binary logistic regression was used to assess associations between ocular lesions and potential factors, including age, gender, duration of HIV/AIDS, CD4+ T cell count, reasons for ophthalmic consultation, and HIV-related systemic diseases. Risk ratios and corresponding p-values were computed for each factor. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant baseline characteristics

Between 19 November 2019 and 9 June 2023, 781 HIV-positive inpatients underwent screening for ophthalmic referral. Patients were excluded if they lacked a confirmed HIV/AIDS diagnosis, were unavailable for ophthalmic consultation, had repeated visits, or lacked fundus photography data. A total of 345 participants were included in the final analytic cohort (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of AIDS patients

The cohort’s age distribution was bimodal, with peaks at 25–35 and 45–60 years. The median CD4⁺ T cell count was higher among patients ≥ 40 years than among those < 40 years (Fig. 2A and B).

A: Age distribution of 345 people we enrolled. B: Comparison of CD4+T cell level between Two Age Groups

Six indications for ophthalmic referral were recorded. A documented history of previous HIV-related eye disease was associated with a higher prevalence of fundus lesions. Among patients referred for blurred vision, 41.18% (28/68) displayed fundus lesions, compared with 54.41% (37/68) who did not. Likewise, among those referred specifically for fundus screening, 26.62% (78/293) exhibited lesions, while 59.39% (174/293) did not. Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia and hematological manifestations of HIV were positively correlated with fundus pathology, whereas systemic disorders unrelated to HIV were linked to a lower frequency of lesions.

HIV-related systemic disease burden was used to estimate overall systemic status (Supplementary Table 1). Individuals with HIV-related systemic comorbidity were more likely to be male, had a shorter duration of HIV/AIDS, and had lower CD4⁺ T cell counts. They also experienced blurred vision and fundus lesions more often, although no absolute causal link can be inferred.

Group features of patients with different ocular lesions

Table 2 presents the ocular complications observed in this study. The opportunistic infections group included 42 patients (median age: 41.5 years, IQR: 32–54 years; 80.95% male), of whom 40 had cytomegalovirus infection, and 2 had Epstein-Barr virus infection. The microangiopathy group included 70 patients (median age: 50.5 years, IQR: 35.75–58.25 years; 90% male), while the uveitis caused by syphilis group included 5 patients (median age: 48 years, IQR: 34–55.5 years; 80% male). Age distributions varied slightly across groups, with the opportunistic infection group showing a more even distribution between 30 and 60 years, whereas the microangiopathy group was more concentrated between 50 and 60 years (Supplementary Fig. 1). Males predominated among patients aged < 40 years (proportion significantly higher; p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

The CD4+ T cell count was significantly lower in the microangiopathy group than in the no fundus lesions group (p = 0.041; Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 3A). The median CD4+ T cell count was lowest in the microangiopathy group (18.5 cells/µL, IQR: 5–40.25 cells/µL) and highest in the uveitis caused by syphilis group (166 cells/µL, IQR: 41–224.5 cells/µL). The duration of HIV/AIDS was shortest in the microangiopathy group (median duration: 0.7 months, IQR: 0–6.5 months) (p = 0.034; Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 3B). Patients with uveitis caused by syphilis had the highest prevalence of blurred vision (80%), whereas only 38.1% of patients with opportunistic infections reported blurred vision.

Among the 42 patients with cytomegalovirus retinitis, 17 (40.48%) had bilateral involvement, and seven progressed to rhegmatogenous retinal detachment or massive retinal necrosis. One individual was monocularly blind, and another was bilaterally blind.

2. Mixed-Effects Model Analysis of Unilateral Blurred Vision Across Disease Types

2. Mixed-Effects Model Analysis of Unilateral Blurred Vision Across Disease Types

Mixed-Effects Model Analysis of Unilateral Blurred Vision Across Disease Types

To eliminate unit-of-analysis ambiguity, eyes were treated as separate observations. 38.9% of eyes with CMVR listed blurred vision as the primary concern; the remainder were detected through surveillance for prior AIDS-related ocular disease.

Generalized linear mixed models were fitted to assess the crude association between lesion type and blurred vision. To preserve the total effect of lesion type, covariates such as age and duration of HIV/AIDS were not adjusted for in this primary analysis. Random-effects analysis revealed significant inter-patient variability in baseline risk (random-intercept variance = 3.575, p < 0.001). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.521, indicating that 52.1% of the variance in blurred vision risk was attributable to between-subject heterogeneity, confirming the need to adjust for bilateral correlation.

Fixed-effects analysis showed that lesion type was significantly associated with blurred vision (p = 0.001), whereas eye laterality was not (p = 0.958). Compared with eyes without lesions, opportunistic infections conferred a 3.13-fold increased risk (adjusted OR = 3.13, 95% CI: 1.24–7.93, p = 0.016). Uveitis caused by syphilis showed a markedly elevated risk (adjusted OR = 41.90, 95% CI: 3.05–578.6, p = 0.005). Microangiopathy showed no significant association (adjusted OR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.23–1.47, p = 0.255). The lack of a significant difference between left and right eyes (adjusted OR = 1.01, p = 0.958) indicates a symmetric pattern of visual impairment, suggesting that fellow eyes share similar susceptibility to lesion-related visual dysfunction (see Table. 3).

3. Logistic Regression of Lesion-Related Visual Impairment

3. Logistic Regression of Lesion-Related Visual Impairment

Logistic Regression of Lesion-Related Visual Impairment

Patient-level analysis was conducted, with fundus lesions in either eye defined as the outcome of interest. Standard binary logistic regression was used to estimate the independent effects of age, gender, duration of HIV/AIDS, CD4+ T cell counts, reasons for ophthalmic consultation, and systematic diseases related to HIV on the risk of developing fundus lesions.

Univariate logistic regression identified longer AIDS duration (OR = 0.992, 95% CI: 0.986–0.998, p = 0.005), lower CD4⁺ T cell count (OR = 0.992, 95% CI: 0.998–1.000, p = 0.015), and the presence of HIV-related systemic disease (OR = 2.4, 95% CI: 1.118–5.152, p = 0.025) as potential predictors of fundus lesions.

Because the duration of HIV/AIDS and CD4+ T cell counts were not normally distributed, natural logarithmic transformations were applied to these continuous variables. Due to small sample sizes in some subgroups, reasons for ophthalmic consultation, previous HIV-related eye disease, previous non-HIV-related eye disease, and ocular operations without HIV-related eye disease were combined into a prior eye history/operation category.

Multivariable logistic regression was conducted to identify independent factors associated with fundus lesions (Table 4). The model included age, log-transformed duration of HIV/AIDS (LN_duration), log-transformed CD4+ T cell counts (LN_CD4), gender, reasons for ophthalmic consultation, and systematic diseases related to HIV.

As shown in Table 4, older age was associated with a marginally increased risk (OR = 1.021, 95% CI: 1.002–1.040, p = 0.028). A higher LN_CD4 level was a significant protective factor (OR = 0.737, 95% CI: 0.617–0.882, p = 0.001). Compared with the reference category of o for fundus examination, blurred vision was associated with fundus lesions (OR = 2.603, 95% CI: 1.356–4.999, p = 0.004), whereas other discomforts, aside from blurred vision and prior eye history/operation, did not differ significantly (both p > 0.05). None of the other variables, including duration of HIV/AIDS (LN_duration, p = 0.646), systematic diseases related to HIV (p = 0.210), or male gender (p = 0.286), reached statistical significance.

Discussion

This five-year retrospective review of 345 HIV-positive inpatients at a tertiary center in central China characterizes the clinical spectrum and predictors of AIDS-related fundus disease. The findings underscore the need for early, systematic ophthalmic screening to preserve vision and quality of life in this vulnerable population.

Multivariable analysis confirmed that low CD4⁺ T cell count, advancing age, and self-reported blurred vision are independent risk factors for fundus lesions—findings consistent with the literature linking immunosuppression and aging as drivers of HIV-related ocular disease. Stratification by lesion subtype revealed distinct phenotypes: opportunistic infections (primarily CMV retinitis) occurred in younger patients and were associated with a moderate prevalence of visual symptoms; HIV microangiopathy predominated in older individuals with the shortest known duration of AIDS; and syphilitic posterior uveitis was associated with the longest duration of HIV infection, the highest CD4⁺ T cell counts, and the greatest proportion of blurred-vision presentations. These disparities underscore the heterogeneity of AIDS-related retinal pathology and support adopting risk-tuned, subtype-aware screening algorithms rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

The demographic profile, with 83.5% male and a bimodal age distribution peaking at 25–35 and 45–60 years, mirrors national HIV epidemiology. The younger peak aligns with incident infections attributable to unsafe sexual practices, whereas the older peak reflects cohorts exposed to unregulated commercial blood-collection practices in the early 1990s [5, 6]. These findings underscore the need to consider the broader socioeconomic and cultural landscape when formulating strategies to address the HIV/AIDS epidemic [7].

Our observations corroborate prior reports linking ocular complications to CD4⁺ T cell count. Microangiopathy, reflecting early blood–retinal barrier compromise, was associated with advanced age and a shorter recognized duration of AIDS [8, 9]. This breach may also serve as a preclinical substrate for cytomegalovirus retinitis [10], as emerging evidence implicates subclinical endothelial injury sustained during systemic CMV infection in the pathogenesis of subsequent retinal necrosis [11]. The two entities may act synergistically, each accelerating the other’s progression.

Nevertheless, CMVR was not limited to the lowest CD4⁺ T cell count in our cohort, suggesting recognition during the transient window of delayed anti-CMV immune reconstitution [6]. A meta-analysis of 23,614 patients across 236 studies identified blurred vision as the most common presenting symptom of HIV-related ocular disease [12]. CMVR was slightly more often classified as a “peripheral type” than as a “central type”, indicating that over 50% of CMV infections manifest in retinal areas outside the central visual zone [12]. Among our cohort with confirmed CMVR, only 38.9% reported blurred vision as the principal complaint, indicating that infection is often established before symptomatic recognition. Lesions outside the macular arcade preserve central acuity, so early retinitis can remain clinically silent, predisposing to under- or delayed diagnosis and underscoring the imperative for systematic screening.

A study of 21 individuals with syphilis found that concurrent AIDS significantly increases the likelihood of ocular involvement [13]. In these patients, CD4⁺ T cell counts were significantly higher in those with ocular syphilis than in the other cohorts [6, 14]. Reduced visual acuity is a cardinal feature of ocular syphilis, yet substantial recovery occurs with prompt anti-syphilitic therapy [15, 16]. Consistent with published series, our syphilitic-uveitis group was characterized by higher CD4⁺ T cell counts, a longer documented duration of AIDS, and prominent blurred vision.

After HAART initiation, some patients develop immune recovery uveitis (IRU) driven by restored intraocular immune responses, whereas the incidence of CMVR remained unchanged over the subsequent 3–6 months [17–19]. Notably, a study in eastern China reported that 16% of CMVR patients experienced IRU [20]. Contrary to the reported 16% IRU rate, no patient in our cohort met the criteria for IRU. Several individuals exhibited vitreous haze consistent with immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS), yet dilated fundus examination excluded necrotizing retinochoroiditis typical of IRU. The principal explanation is methodological: our protocol precluded enrollment of eyes with dense vitreous opacification that precluded high-quality photography; moreover, such eyes are usually managed directly by the ophthalmology service rather than through inpatient consultation, thereby escaping our surveillance net.

A South China cohort study found that instituting routine ophthalmic screening increased the detection of HIV-related eye disease 9-fold [21]. Moreover, published work shows that symptom-led assessment alone often misses early HIV-related eye disease [22]. A European series of 21 patients with syphilis found that presentation within 15 days of visual symptoms predicted better anatomic and functional outcomes [13]. Our findings, derived from generalized linear mixed models, were consistent with previous results. Despite regional and economic disparities, our findings echo this message: systematic, early ophthalmic evaluation of all AIDS patients, before symptoms emerge, remains the most effective strategy to minimize preventable visual loss.

Our study is limited by its single-center, retrospective design and restricted dataset; larger, multicenter prospective cohorts are needed to define the full epidemiological spectrum of HIV-associated ocular disease. Moreover, the analysis focused on fundus lesions, with no systematic documentation of ocular surface pathology. Future work should encompass the entire ocular phenotype and validate predictive algorithms using external resampling.

In conclusion, systematic ophthalmic screening of HIV-positive adults, especially those with a nadir CD4⁺ T cell count < 100 cells/µL, age ≥ 40 years, or complaints of blurred vision, enables early detection and sight-preserving intervention. Integrating these risk factors into standardized follow-up protocols will ensure that fundus monitoring is targeted and timely, thereby preventing severe, irreversible ocular sequelae in this vulnerable population.
