Sensitivity Study of the FDEM Model of Brazilian Splitting for Weiyuan Shale Considering Mineral Components
Authors: Yizhen Li, Yumeng Jiang, Wei Jiang, Bao Li, Yadong Yang, Yafei Luo, Mingyang Wu, Yintong Guo, Jialing Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To design and optimize hydraulic fracturing technology for shale gas production, it is crucial to understand the impact of mineral composition on shale mechanical properties. In this study, based on the Brazilian splitting experiment results of Weiyuan shale, a heterogeneous mechanical numerical simulation model of shale is established, and Brazilian splitting tests are conducted . Then, the influence of mineral size, mineral spatial distribution, critical failure displacement, and the stiffness of interface elements on fracture propagation is analyzed. The results indicate that with the increase of the mineral size, the tensile failure ratio decreases from 0.5 to 0.4, and a more complex fracture path is formed. Meanwhile, the changes in the spatial distribution of minerals also lead to certain differences in the simulation results. This phenomenon may be caused by the nonuniform distribution of mineral components in heterogeneous shale, which leads to a significant difference in the stress path and stress concentration location. Therefore, the crack initiation and propagation trajectories show certain differences. With the increase in critical failure displacement of the interface element, the failure stress of the Brazilian split specimens gradually increased from 6.2 kN to 7.92 kN, and the tensile strength increased. When the stiffness of the interface element is high, local shear failure dominates, and the failure mode is manifested as single brittle failure. This result indicates that the mechanical properties of the fracture interface inside and outside the mineral have a significant impact on the Brazilian splitting process of shale. Therefore, further consideration of the influence of the interface on the propagation law of Brazilian splitting fractures in shale is an important way to clarify the propagation law of Brazilian splitting fractures in shale. This study can provide important reference for understanding the deformation and fracture characteristics of shale under the influence of minerals.

Introduction

Shale gas, as an unconventional natural gas resource, possesses characteristics of clean and efficient energy. China’s recoverable shale gas reserves amount to 315.7 billion cubic meters, which can supplement the gap in conventional oil and gas resources and reduce the country’s dependence on imported energy. The large-scale development of shale gas holds strategic significance for achieving “energy independence”. −

Shale gas, as an important unconventional natural gas resource, relies on a deep understanding of shale mechanical properties for efficient development. In recent years, researchers at home and abroad have make significant progress in the field of shale mechanics through a combination of experiments and numerical simulations. In terms of experimental research, scholars have systematically reveal the anisotropic laws of shale mechanical properties: Scholars quantitatively analyzed the tensile and compressive strength characteristics of shale through Brazilian splitting and triaxial compression tests as a function of bedding angle, − establish an evaluation system for the tensile brittleness index of Longmaxi formation shale. At the same time, the regulatory mechanisms of moisture content and confining pressure on strength and elastic modulus are clarified, the structure–property relationship between clay mineral content and mechanical properties is discovered, and a moisture damage constitutive model is constructed. , Hydraulic fracturing physical simulation further reveals the regulatory mechanisms of fracture initiation and propagation controlled by injection parameters, in situ stress, and formation depth. − The development of a true three-axis seepage device have make it possible to quantitatively characterize crack propagation behaviors. However, due to the constraints of experimental costs and scale effects, numerical simulation has gradually become a key tool for analyzing the complex mechanical responses of shale.

In terms of numerical simulation methods, attempts have been made by researchers to use the discrete element method (DEM), the finite element method (FEM), and elastoplastic models (such as the brittle cracking and Drucker-Prager models) to simulate the rock fracturing process. A rock fracture simulation method based on the bonded particle model (BPM) have been proposed and is widely used in the study of rock crack propagation. For example, the DEM have successfully revealed the mechanism by which bedding planes inhibit the propagation of hydraulic fractures. The finite element-discrete fracture network (FEM-DFN) coupled model effectively analyzes the mechanism of natural fractures deflecting hydraulic fractures. However, while the DEM excels at characterizing stratigraphic discontinuities and crack propagation dynamics, it struggles to depict heterogeneity of multiple mineral components. Similarly, the FEM excels at multifield coupling analysis but has limitations in capturing the dynamic evolution of microcracks. Combining the advantages of both methods, the Finite Discrete Element Method (FDEM) is developed: this method integrates the continuous medium stress analysis capabilities of the FEM with the fracture discontinuity simulation advantages of the DEM, becoming a breakthrough tool for dynamic rock fracture simulation. For example, Song et al. have verified the influence of mechanical parameters on the deformation-cracking mechanism through 3D-printed layered samples. The precise simulation of the evolution process of kuru granite cracks, along with the visualization of the stress and velocity fields and discrete cracks, has deepened our understanding of how crack patterns form. − The study combines digital image processing and grain boundary models to reveal the regulatory patterns of microcrack initiation, propagation, and coalescence in the microstructural heterogeneity of Beishan granite.

Recently research trends have focused on multiscale modeling and dynamic evolution simulation. For example, some studies have integrated CT scan data to construct mineral composition distribution models, while others have utilized the cohesive element method to analyze the nonlinear effects of texture orientation on tensile strength. , A three-dimensional lattice algorithm hydraulic fracturing simulator is successfully employed to investigate the impact of perforation location and length on the expansion of hydraulic fractures in the coal seam of the Linxing block. The simulation effectively has captured the interlayer expansion of fractures within the coal seam. Despite the achievements made with existing methods, the mechanical response in deep shale has remained highly intricate due to the complexity of mineral composition, making it challenging for traditional models to effectively represent mineral interface effects and cross-scale fracturing mechanisms. By performing registration and segmentation processing on the images obtained from microcomputed tomography and energy-dispersive scanning electron microscopy, a multimineral digital rock model could be constructed for numerical simulations of various rock physical properties. This approach could be applied to a range of tight reservoirs. The FDEM, due to its advantages in simultaneously analyzing the stress of continuous media and simulating the propagation of discontinuous fractures, have become a breakthrough approach to addressing this issue. This method has effectively integrated the mechanical responses of continuous materials with the dynamic extension of discrete fractures, allowing for the accurate capture of crack formation, propagation, and their effects on the overall mechanical behaviors of geological materials.

Although the anisotropic mechanical properties of shale have been revealed through laboratory experiments, such as Brazilian splitting and triaxial compression tests, these experiments are costly, and scale effects are significant. They also struggle to replicate the true reservoir conditions of deep shale and fail to fully capture the microscopic mechanisms of multimineral components. DEM is effective in modeling stratigraphic discontinuities and crack propagation dynamics; however, it struggles with the heterogeneity of multiple mineral components. In contrast, FEM is suitable for multifield coupling analysis but does not effectively model the dynamic evolution of microcracks. Although some studies have attempted to combine these two methods, no specialized models have been developed to represent the mechanical response of “multi-mineral shale” (particularly for specific formations like Weiyuan shale). Furthermore, a systematic analysis of the effects of mineral size, distribution, and interface elements on the fracture process remains lacking.

Based on this, the present study utilized the FDEM to simulate the tensile mechanical properties of multimineral shale, constructing a heterogeneous model in conjunction with X-ray diffraction (XRD) mineral composition data. The system investigates the regulatory patterns of “stress evolutioncrack propagation pathfailure mode” in the Brazilian splitting process of Weiyuan shale, focusing on mineral size, mineral composition distribution, critical failure displacement, and the influence of different interface units. This provides quantitative evidence linking micro- to macro-scale phenomena for the study of fracture mechanisms in multimineral shales. First, mineral composition analysis and Brazilian splitting experiments on Weiyuan shale are conducted to provide data support for the subsequent numerical simulation model, as presented in Section . The application of the FDEM, along with the creation and validation of the heterogeneous numerical model for Weiyuan shale during Brazilian splitting tests, is described in Section . The feasibility of the numerical model is demonstrated through comparison with physical laboratory experiments. The influences of mineral size, distribution of mineral components, critical failure displacement, and various interface elements on stress evolution, fracture propagation characteristics, and failure modes during the Brazilian splitting tests of Weiyuan shale are analyzed in Section . Finally, a comprehensive analysis of the results is provided in Section , where the limitations of the current simulation model are outlined, along with potential future solutions. The conclusion is presented in Section

Laboratory Experiments

To create a Brazilian splitting FDEM model for Weiyuan shale that take mineral composition into account, mineral analysis and Brazilian splitting experiments on Weiyuan shale have been conducted as the first step. There, the XRD test have been presented in Section , while the corresponding procedures and conditions for the Brazilian splitting experiment have been detailed in Section .

Specimen Preparation

Shale samples are selected from the Longmaxi Formation at a depth of 1828.7 m in Weiyuan. This rock type is characterized by long-term high in situ stress conditions, making it a high-strength and dense brittle rock. The obtained core samples are processed into standard circular discs with a diameter of 25 mm and a thickness of 12.5 mm for the Brazilian splitting tests, which are aimed at analyzing the tensile characteristics of the shale. The waste material generated during the processing is collected for qualitative and quantitative XRD mineral composition analysis. Some of the drilled core samples are shown in Figure .

Sample schematic diagrams ((a) brazilian disc specimen (b) powder sample).

The mineral composition of the core samples is analyzed using a Bruker AXS D8-Focus X-ray diffractometer (Figure ). The experimental analysis system works by irradiating X-rays onto the sample positioned at the center of the goniometer, which generates diffraction from the sample. The mineral composition of the sample is analyzed through the diffraction of X-rays within the crystal structure. To ensure the accuracy of the XRD experiments and minimize the effects of shale heterogeneity, as well as to reduce particle size effects and the potential for mineral phase changes or crystal structure damage due to excessive grinding, the fragmented shale is dried at 110 °C for 24 h prior to analysis. After drying, the shale samples are cut or crushed into uniform small particles. The rock samples are then ground into a fine powder using a grinder, and powders that passed through a 200-mesh sieve are selected for XRD analysis.

X-ray diffractometer.

It can be seen from Figure that the main mineral components of Weiyuan shale are quartz (41.7%), calcite (32.35%), dolomite (8.7%), Illite (8.17%), kaolinite (2.45%), and sodium feldspar (6.63%). Among them, the total content of brittle minerals (quartz, calcite, dolomite, and sodium feldspar) is as high as 89.38%, while the proportion of clay minerals (Illite and kaolinite) is only 10.62%. Given that brittle minerals may provide higher mechanical strength and exhibit more significant brittle failure. Therefore, it can be inferred from the mineral testing results that Weiyuan shale may have high mechanical strength and is highly prone to brittle failure.

Brazilian Disc Test

To eliminate the influence of the shale’s stratification characteristics, horizontal stratified shale samples are selected for drilling and preparation. The samples are processed into standard disk specimens with a diameter of 25 mm and a thickness of 12.5 mm, ensuring a flatness deviation of the end faces within ±0.05 mm. The testing is conducted using the RMT-150C (Figure ) multifunctional rock mechanics testing system, independently developed by the Wuhan Institute of Rock and Soil Mechanics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. An indirect tensile test is performed under displacement-controlled loading, with a loading rate of 0.002 mm/s.

RMT experimental apparatus diagram.

By applying radial compression to the disk, the tensile strength of the rock is indirectly measured. Concentrated loads are applied to the upper and lower surfaces of the disk, and the tensile stress at the center of the disk is calculated to determine the tensile strength of the rock (Figure ). Fundamental Theory of the FDEM Model

To comprehensively understand the mechanisms by which mineral components influence the tensile behavior of Weiyuan shale, a numerically simulated method based on the FDEM is developed. This method integrates finite element and discrete element techniques to analyze the entire process of the Brazilian splitting test, from continuous deformation to discontinuous failure of the shale specimen. Initially, the material is divided into a finite number of discrete elements. Cohesive elements of zero-thickness are inserted at the interfaces between adjacent discrete elements, creating a complete material model that integrates both discrete and cohesive elements. The deformation and displacement of the discrete elements are controlled by the forces at their nodes, while the bonding characteristics of the cohesive elements assess the initiation and propagation of cracks. Their failure indicates the progression of cracking. This approach synergistically combines the precision of continuous numerical methods for material deformation under stress with the versatility of discontinuous numerical methods, enabling effective simulation of crack paths. Therefore, the FDEM model using zero-thickness cohesive force elements is particularly suitable for studying the dynamic evolution of fractures in Weiyuan shale under the influence of mineral components.

In the simulation model, zero-thickness cohesive elements are used to represent the contact interactions between the boundaries of discrete mineral phase entities. Previous studies confirm that the “relative displacement-stress” criterion effectively captures this type of interaction. Therefore, a traction-separation model is employed to define the constitutive behavior of these cohesive elements. Before the occurrence of damage, the deformation behavior of interface elements is governed by the traction-separation law, which adheres to a linear elastic relationship. The stress–strain relationship in the elastic phase prior to material damage is described by the following equation: 5 t={tntstt}=[KnnKnsKntKnsKssKstKntKstKtt]{εnεsεt}=Kε where ϵn = δn/T0, ϵs = δs/T0, ϵ t = δt/T0. ε n represents the normal strain; ε s , ε t denote the transverse shear strains in two orthogonal directions; T 0 corresponds to the initial thickness of the interface element, m; δ n indicates the normal separation displacement, m, and δ s , δ t represent the shear separation displacements in two transverse directions, m.

In this numerical simulation study, a displacement-based damage evolution criterion is primarily employed. Effects of Mineral Size

To analyze the effects of mineral grain size on numerical simulation the fracture propagation during the Brazilian splitting process, different mineral components are embedded into the model grid. The grid size is adjusted to indirectly simulate the size of the minerals. The mineral size of the shale model is set to 0.33 mm, 0.58 mm, and 0.994 mm, respectively. The corresponding element quantities for different mineral sizes are 1,542, 3,824, and 14,630, respectively (Figure ). The simulation results are presented in Figures –.

Figure illustrates the variation over time of the number of fractured elements, tensile failure examples, crack length, maximum width, and area due to differences in mineral size at different experimental time points, as well as the relationship between stress and displacement. Under the condition that the total interface area of the sample remains unchanged, the number of crack elements during the fracturing process decreased with the increase in mineral size, as shown in Figure a. This result may be explained by the fact that with the total area of the sample remaining constant, an increase in mineral size results in a reduction in the number of grid size elements, leading to fewer fractured elements in samples with larger grid sizes during the experiment. Figure c–e demonstrate that with the increase of the mineral size of the sample, the total crack length, maximum crack width, and overall crack volume gradually increased, while the proportion of tensile failure gradually decreased (the tensile failure ratio changs from 0.5 to 0.4), as shown in Figure b. These phenomena may be explained by the energy required at the same failure displacement decreases, resulting in lower stress and lower tensile strength (Figure f). These results suggest that during the Brazilian splitting experiment, as mineral size increased, the stress conditions required for crack failure in the sample became lower, leading to a larger crack area.

Figure illustrates the stress distribution during the crack initiation, development, and failure stages for different mineral sizes, clearly indicating the changes in stress throughout the crack propagation process. It shows how stress varied at the same moment under different mineral sizes. With the decrease of the mineral grain size, stress becomes more concentrated at both ends of the sample. The end stress of samples with smaller mineral grain size is significantly greater than that of samples with larger mineral grain size. With the increase of the mineral grain size, the stress distribution within shale samples becomes more dispersed. In the crack development phase, as shown in Figure , the location of stress concentration in the middle section shifts, which promoted a change in the crack propagation path. For samples with larger mineral sizes (1542 elements), three large cracks developed with a curved morphology, while for smaller mineral sizes (14630 elements), there is a primary crack with a simpler shape. These results indicate that as mineral size increased, the positions of stress concentrations became more dispersed, leading to more complex failure paths and crack shapes. This complexity may arise from the influence of mineral size in heterogeneous shale models. In larger mineral sizes, the heterogeneity is stronger, dominated by locally stronger mechanical properties of the minerals, resulting in greater strength dispersion. Consequently, cracks in the sample are more influenced by the mineral grains during propagation, evolving along grain boundaries, thus resulting in more complex crack morphologies.

Figure shows the differences in crack morphology and failure modes of samples with varying mineral components during the progression from crack initiation to failure. With the decrease of the mineral size, the behavior of cracks changes. When the mineral grain size is large, the crack morphology mainly manifests as single, curved inclined lines, and the crack propagation path is random. With the decrease of the mineral size, more small cracks initiate during the crack initiation phase. However, with further stress loading, these cracks gradually develop along the central axis until failure occurred. The failure mode of the samples shifts from predominantly shear failure in larger mineral samples to tensile failure in smaller mineral samples. This result may be explained by the fact that the expansion of cracks is significantly controlled by the boundaries of stronger minerals, leading to a higher number of cracks in all three sample types during this phase in the early stages of crack initiation. Figure c shows that, at the initial crack stage, the total length of cracks in samples with larger mineral sizes is greater than that in samples with smaller mineral sizes. With further stress loading, the cracks in samples with smaller mineral sizes develop primarily along a single main crack direction, resulting in a reduction in the total crack length by the end of the experiment.

Through the numerical simulation of Brazilian splitting with different mineral size gradients, it is found that the size of the minerals significantly influences the crack propagation paths and failure modes in the Brazilian splitting tests of heterogeneous shale samples. With the increase of the mineral size, the crack propagation paths become more complex. The failure mode transitions from being predominantly tensile failure to predominantly shear failure. As indicated in Figures f and , the impact of size effects in the numerical simulation suggests that the stress distribution in samples with larger mineral sizes in the heterogeneous model might have been dominated by local defects, leading to a higher degree of variability in tensile strength, more dispersed locations of stress concentration, and increased fluctuations in the stress-displacement curves. However, the specific reasons for the transition of the crack failure mode from tensile failure in small-sized minerals to shear failure in large-sized minerals remain unclear.

Effect of Mineral Spatial Distribution

To investigate the effect of differences in mineral composition distribution on the tensile properties of Weiyuan shale, three different mineral spatial distribution models are established (Figure ) by keeping the mineral composition content, critical failure displacement, interface element stiffness, and mineral particle size constant. The stress transfer path and crack propagation changes are observed during the Brazilian splitting numerical simulation. The results are presented in Figures –.

Figure displays the mineral component distribution difference graphs. With changes in the distribution of mineral components, the number of fracture elements, total crack length, maximum crack width, total fracture area, and tensile strength of Model 1 are significantly lower than those of models 2 and 3. However, no substantial differences are observed in the data between models 2 and 3. As illustrated in Figure b, the onset of tensile failure in model 1 occurs later than in the samples of models 2 and 3. At the final moment of failure, the tensile failure ratio of model 1 is higher than that of models 2 and 3. This indicates that the distribution of mineral components in Weiyuan shale has a certain impact on crack propagation, failure modes, and tensile strength in shale samples.

Figure displays the stress evolution process of different mineral distribution samples at the same time during the Brazilian splitting numerical simulation tests. The stress distribution for model 1 is found to be more dispersed compared to models 2 and 3 throughout the experiment. At the end positions, the stress is found to be more concentrated in the end regions of models 2 and 3, primarily focusing on the main cracks and the terminal areas. Model 1 exhibits a longer crack development cycle than models 2 and 3, demonstrating better ductility characteristics, while models 2 and 3 show superior brittleness compared to model 1. This indicates that the differences in mineral distribution led to variations in stress distribution in the Weiyuan shale samples during the Brazilian splitting tests, resulting in differences in the fracture development cycles and consequently affecting the rock mechanical properties of the samples. The underlying reason for this phenomenon is that model 1 featured a more uniform alternation in the distribution of clay minerals (kaolinite, Illite) and nonclay minerals, with a higher content of brittle materials like quartz and calcite being more dispersed in this sample. In contrast, models 2 and 3 accumulated nonclay minerals (quartz and calcite) primarily along the center-line of the sample, while the clay minerals exhibit good plasticity. The greater mechanical strength of the brittle nonclay minerals dominates the stress distribution, leading to a higher tensile strength. , This results in models 2 and 3 having higher tensile strength compared to model 1. The study indicates that direct links between different minerals could have led to variations in tensile stress between them. Feldspar and quartz constitute the majority of the load-bearing capacity of the samples, which allow them to endure higher stress during the loading process. Furthermore, the interface between feldspar and quartz, in relation to other minerals, generates higher tensile stress.

Figure demonstrates the crack propagation paths and failure modes of shale during the Brazilian splitting tests under varying mineral component distributions. In the models 2 and 3 are found to predominantly exhibit a simple vertical crack primarily along the center-line, whereas model 1 displays a complex distribution of cracks with intricate shapes during the fracturing process. While all three samples show different crack initiation at the early stage of crack nucleation, notable differences in the crack propagation paths emerged as the tests progressed. Models 2 and 3 develop cracks along the center-line toward a primary crack, whereas model 1 exhibits a more complex and irregular crack evolution pathway. Regarding the failure modes, model 1 experiences a longer crack development cycle from initiation to failure, with tensile failure predominating throughout the process. In contrast, models 2 and 3 demonstrate shorter development cycles and exhibit prominent shear failure characteristics at both ends of the cracks compared to model 1. This indicates that the differences in mineral distribution significantly influenced the crack propagation behavior and failure mechanisms of the shale samples.

Through numerical simulation of the Weiyuan Brazilian split test based on differences in mineral distribution, it is found that differences in mineral composition distribution significantly affect stress transfer in the Brazilian split test, altering the location of stress concentration. This, in turn, influences the crack initiation locations, crack propagation paths, and failure modes of the samples during fracturing. Some researchers suggest that the primary cause of stress redistribution in heterogeneous models is the differing mechanical responses between mineral phases. − The content of brittle minerals, the structure of mineral aggregates, and grain-to-grain contact models all control stress distribution. Brittle mineral grains often have weak grain boundaries, and hydraulic fractures tend to cross grain boundaries during reservoir modification, inducing complex fractures. The particles are physically connected and cannot deform independently. Differences in the distribution of mineral components result in different fracture forces required for different mineral phases, which causes changes in stress concentration locations, crack propagation paths, and failure modes during the experiment.

Effect of Critical Failure Displacement

To investigate the impact of interface critical fracture displacement on the Brazilian splitting numerical simulation, four variable conditions for the critical fracture displacement are established at values of 0.0005, 0.001, 0.003, and 0.005 m, while interface element stiffness, mineral size, and mineral distribution are maintained consistent. These varying conditions allow for a gradual change in the critical displacement values within the numerical simulation. The results are presented in Figures –.

It is clearly observed that with the increase of the critical fracture displacement, the number of fractured elements gradually increased. However, when the critical fracture displacement is set at 0.001 m, the number of fractured elements is significantly higher than in the other three groups. This discrepancy might have been influenced by the grid size in the experiment: when the critical fracture displacement is either too small or too large relative to the grid size, it exhibits a certain dependence on the element grid size, thereby affecting the fracture behavior. Figure b demonstrates the proportion of tensile failure that occurred within the sample throughout the fracturing process. The ratio of tensile failure gradually increased during the experiment, with the sample experiencing a drop before fracture. This is likely due to the high content of brittle quartz and calcite in the Weiyuan shale numerical simulation sample, which results in greater brittleness. Specifically, a shear failure event occurred before fracture in all experiments. Notably, in the tensile failure curve, the curve corresponding to a critical fracture displacement of 0.0005 m preceded the other three groups, exhibiting a greater degree of drop and a lower tensile failure value. This phenomenon could be attributed to the more pronounced brittle characteristics of the sample when the critical fracture displacement is too small, manifesting as greater shear failure during the experiment. Figure c–e shows the changes in total crack length, maximum crack width, and total crack area over time during the sample testing process, respectively. Clearly, as the critical fracture displacement increased, the total crack length, width, and area all exhibit upward trends. However, Figure c, the total crack length for a critical fracture displacement of 0.001 m is higher than that of the other three groups. This is likely due to the dependency between the size of the critical fracture displacement and the grid size, resulting in a reduction in the number of fractured elements during the Brazilian splitting experiments. These factors may result in a decrease in the total crack length of the sample. Figure f presents the stress-displacement curve for the shale sample during the Brazilian splitting experiment. With the increase of the critical fracture displacement, the energy threshold required for damage between elements gradually rose. Therefore, as the critical fracture displacement increased, the stress required for the shale sample to fail during the experiment also increased. It is noteworthy that when the critical fracture displacement is set to 0.0005 m, the stress-displacement curve shows a drop when the vertical displacement reached 0.2 mm, while the other three curves remained relatively stable. In Figure b, the sample experienced a similar drop, signifying that a shear failure occurred at 100 s. This demonstrates that the drop in the red line of the stress-displacement curve is caused by a shear failure event occurring in the sample with a critical fracture displacement of 0.0005 m at that moment.

Figure shows the variation of stress in shale samples at different moments for various critical displacements. As the critical fracture displacement increased, the stress concentration at the loading ends of the samples with larger critical displacements became more pronounced. In the middle of the main fracture, the stress variation gradually decreased with the increase in critical displacement. When the critical fracture displacement is at its minimum of 0.0005 m, the stress at the middle end is more concentrated. The stress contour plots clearly indicated that as the critical fracture displacement gradually increased, the time taken for the sample’s fracture to develop and open also increased. These phenomena indicate that the two samples with smaller critical fracture displacements exhibit pronounced brittle characteristics, while the shale samples demonstrated enhanced ductility as the critical fracture displacement increased.

It is evident that as the critical fracture displacement increased, the time taken for the sample’s cracks to evolve from initial formation to failure became longer, suggesting that larger critical fracture displacements enhanced the ductility of the shale samples. For the sample with a critical fracture displacement of 0.0005 m, the time required for the crack to progress from initial development to failure is shorter, and the entire development process is primarily characterized by shear failure, exhibiting noticeable brittle characteristics. In the experiments with critical fracture displacements of 0.003 and 0.005 m, the main cracks also predominantly showed shear failure at both ends during the fracturing process. Notably, the shale sample with a critical fracture displacement of 0.001 m shows a greater occurrence of tensile failure throughout the development of the main crack, although there is still a shear failure process at the two ends of the crack. In fact, during the failure phase, one end of the main crack is primarily characterized by tensile failure, while the other end shows shear failure. This result may be explained by the fact that the heterogeneity in the material and local stress distribution is involved during the fracturing process.

Through numerical simulation of Weiyuan Brazilian split test and analysis based on differences in critical fracture displacement, it is found that differences in critical fracture displacement have a significant impact on tensile strength in the Brazilian split test. As critical fracture displacement gradually increases, the energy threshold required for cracks to propagate from initiation to failure also gradually increases. This phenomenon is manifested in the experimental simulation as a gradual increase in stress values, indicating an improvement in tensile strength. As the critical fracture displacement increases, the stress distribution at the loading end of the Brazilian split test becomes more concentrated, while the stress distribution around the main crack in the central region remains relatively uniform. This indicates that changes in the critical fracture displacement alter the stress distribution and crack initiation location in the Brazilian split test. It is worth noting that the experiment used Weiyuan shale samples, which contain a large amount of brittle minerals such as quartz and calcite, thus exhibiting obvious brittle characteristics. During the crack failure process, the failure mode primarily manifests as tensile failure.

Effect of Stiffness

To investigate the effects of progressively increased interface element stiffness (10 times, 50 times, 100 times, and 200 times the baseline values) on Brazilian splitting test outcomes in Weiyuan shale, this section has been systematically analyzed using numerical simulation results under rigorously controlled conditions: constant critical crack displacement, invariant mineral grain dimensions, and preserved mineral distribution patterns throughout all comparative simulations. The results are shown in Figures –.

As shown in Figure , with the increase in interface element stiffness, the number of fractured elements, tensile failure ratio, crack length, maximum width, and fracture area all exhibit an increasing trend, with a longer duration of failure. These relationships may partly be explained by the higher interface element stiffness, which requires more force during the failure process, thereby enhancing the material’s resistance to deformation. Thus, in the stress-displacement curve presented in Figure f, for the same fracture displacement, samples with higher interface stiffness requires greater stress, indicating an increase in tensile strength. Notably, as the tensile failure data is displayed in Figure b, the sample with 50 times interface stiffness shows a lower tensile failure ratio compared to the other three sets of samples. For the shale samples with interface stiffness values of 50 times and 100 times, the tensile failure ratio curves exhibit a drop prior to failure, indicating a decrease in the tensile failure ratio at that moment. Furthermore, the curve for the 100 times stiffness drops earlier than that for the 50 times stiffness. In contrast, the tensile failure ratios for the interface stiffness values of 200 times and 10 times remain linearly increasing until failure. This phenomenon is attributed to excessively high interface stiffness, which may be caused by the inefficiencies in stress transfer through the sample cross-section. Consequently, this leads to stress concentration at the crack tip, promoting an increase in the tensile failure ratio. On the other hand, if the interface stiffness is too low, the material exhibits insufficient resistance to deformation, resulting in plastic behavior during the process. Early softening at the interface leads to tensile failure being the predominant mode of failure, even when the significant deformation occurred.

Figure displays the stress variation process over time under different interface element stiffness conditions, and primarily highlights the stress distribution at three key moments: crack initiation, crack development, and failure. It is clear that at an interface element stiffness of 10 times, the stress distribution in the shale experiment is relatively complex. With the increase of the interface elements stiffness, the stress progressively concentrated toward the central primary crack. During the crack development phase, it is evident that samples with higher stiffness exhibit earlier crack initiation and shorter development times, and the stress concentration at the crack tip is more pronounced. These results indicates that under conditions of higher interface element stiffness, stress becomes more concentrated, resulting in faster crack development and a more pronounced brittle behavior of the material.

Figure presents the morphological changes of cracks at different moments, as well as the tensile and shear evolution processes during crack development. In the sample with an interface element stiffness of 10 times, the cracks are distributed more extensively and exhibit complex patterns, with tensile failure being the predominant mode throughout the entire process. With the increase of the interface element stiffness, the number of cracks gradually decreased, and the crack morphology began to converge toward a single main crack location. This trend indicates that with increasing interface element stiffness, the crack morphology reduced to a single main crack. In terms of tensile and shear failure, as interface element stiffness increased, the samples displayed more pronounced brittle fracture characteristics, and the failure mode remained predominantly tensile.

Through numerical simulation of Weiyuan Brazilian split test and analysis based on differences in interface element stiffness, it is found that with the increase of the interface element stiffness increased, the material’s ability to resist deformation gradually improved, with samples exhibiting higher interface element stiffness demonstrating better tensile strength. Increased interface element stiffness enhanced the efficiency of interfacial stress transfer, resulting in a more concentrated stress distribution, particularly at the crack tip (Figure ). This leads to higher stiffness samples experiencing crack initiation and development at earlier stages (Figure ), with crack morphology becoming more singular and exhibiting brittle fracture characteristics. This result confirms that curves with higher stiffness exhibit earlier changes, as shown in Figure a–e. Specifically, in Figure b, tensile failure occurs earlier under the condition of 100 times the stiffness compared to that under 50 times the stiffness. The drop in the tensile failure curves for samples at both 100 times and 50 times stiffness is attributed to transient stress concentrations appearing at the ends of the cracks during the crack development process (Figure ), which leads to minor shear failure during crack propagation. Discussion

Considering the extremely complex factors influencing the propagation process of shale Brazilian splitting cracks under the influence of minerals, this study may have certain limitations. Therefore, a detailed discussion based on comprehensive factor analysis and model limitations and improvements is presented in Sections and

Comprehensive Analysis of Simulation Results

Considering the influence of mineral composition, a simulation model for the Brazilian splitting test of Weiyuan shale is established. The effects of different mineral particle sizes, mineral composition distribution, critical failure displacement, and interface element stiffness on the mechanical properties of Weiyuan shale are analyzed. The results indicate that the primary factors influencing its mechanical properties are mineral composition distribute ion and particle size, followed by critical failure displacement, while interface unit stiffness has the least influence among the four factors. Although interface unit stiffness has the weakest influence, it should not be overlooked. The distribution of different mineral components alters the stress transmission pathways within the model during testing, thereby influencing the locations of stress concentration, crack initiation, and propagation. The cause of this issue lies in the differences in mechanical properties between different mineral phases within the heterogeneous Weiyuan shale model. This results in varying forces required for fracture between mineral phases, which in turn alters stress transmission pathways and ultimately affects crack initiation locations and propagation trajectories. , Based on the above findings, future studies on rock mechanical properties should focus on the distribution of mineral components and the mechanical property relationships between mineral phases.

Model Limitations and Improvements

Previous studies , have conducted extensive numerical modeling research on the mechanical characteristics of Brazilian splitting tests. The numerical models established in these studies primarily focused on factors such as the size effect of the samples used in the Brazilian splitting test and the angle of layering. These investigations have contributed significantly to understanding how these parameters influence the results of the tests and the mechanical behavior of shale under tensile loading conditions. By examining these aspects, researchers have enhanced the comprehension of crack initiation, propagation, and the overall fracture characteristics of shale materials. , Based on the FDEM method, further considered the mineral components to construct a numerical simulation model for the Brazilian splitting test of Weiyuan shale. The simulations revealed that there are numerous influencing factors on the model. Although the influences of mineral size, the distribution of mineral components, etc. have been taken into account currently, the sizes of minerals in real rocks may be randomly scattered without fixed dimensions, and there are limitations in size simulation. Critical failure displacement and stiffness are actually more microscopic mechanical parameters, and it is still difficult to obtain them in practice. In contrast, indoor physical experiments reveal that the distribution of shale minerals exhibits three-dimensional characteristics. Therefore, from the perspective of experimental equipment in the future, should we pay more attention to how to set up corresponding experimental equipment to directly obtain these parameters? However, existing literature does not consider numerical simulation studies of rock mechanics based on mineral composition, The ability of two-dimensional models to predict crack propagation and the direction of main cracks has been proven. , Although numerical simulations can currently achieve three-dimensional modeling, shale models that consider mineral composition are more complex due to the complexity of the spatial distribution of mineral composition and the size and shape of minerals. Second, three-dimensional models require significant computing power during the simulation process and are limited by costs. Nevertheless, despite the challenges of achieving three-dimensional models under current conditions, this will be our next research plan.

Conclusions

In this study, the FDEM is used to establish a heterogeneous simulation model of Brazilian splitting tests of Weiyuan shale considering mineral components. Then, the influence of different mineral sizes, mineral component distributions, critical failure displacements, and interface unit stiffness on the stress evolution patterns, crack propagation behavior, and failure modes is discussed. The results are as follows:(1)In the Brazilian splitting tests of heterogeneous Weiyuan shale, larger mineral sizes lead to local defects dominating the stress distribution, resulting in stress concentration dispersion and variability in tensile strength. With the increase of the mineral size, the shale tensile failure ratio decreases from 0.5 to 0.4, which causing the failure mode to shift from tension-dominated to shear-dominated, thereby forming a more complex fracture path.(2)Under the influence of the spatial distribution of different mineral components, the stress transfer path varies during the test, causing changes in the stress concentration location, which in turn alters the crack initiation point and crack propagation path. The differences in mechanical properties between different mineral phases result in distinct failure modes for these phases, leading to variations in the failure modes of shale specimens.(3)In the experiments with heterogeneous models, with the increase of the critical failure displacement, the failure stress of the Brazilian split specimens gradually increased from 6.2 kN to 7.92 kN, and the tensile strength increased. With the increase in critical displacement, stress concentration mainly occurs at the loading end, while stress dispersion occurs in the middle section, indicating that stress distribution and crack initiation are affected by displacement control.(4)The stiffness of the interface elements affects the stress transfer at the interface, leading to more concentrated stress at the crack tip, which in turn increases the material’s tensile strength and promotes earlier crack initiation. Higher stiffness results in more uniform failure characteristics, typically manifesting as a singular brittle fracture. Under varying stiffness, there exists a dynamic competition between stress concentration and failure modes (tensile/shear). At high stiffness, local shear failure may briefly dominate during crack propagation.(5)In this study, a relatively ideal simulation model considering the heterogeneity of minerals is established. The results indicate that differences in mineral grain size and spatial distribution of mineral components do indeed influence the stress transfer path, stress concentration location, and crack propagation path during Brazilian splitting tests. In nature, the size and distribution of minerals in real rocks exhibit significant randomness and irregularity. Therefore, to more accurately predict and reveal the deformation and failure characteristics of real rocks, future rock mechanics research should focus on the distribution of mineral components and the mechanical interactions between mineral phases.
