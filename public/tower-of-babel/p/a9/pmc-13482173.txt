INTRODUCTION

As emerging evidence highlighted the role of cerebromicrovascular mechanisms in cognitive decline, the term “vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia” (VCID) was coined 1 . Although progress has been made toward understanding VCID, there is a critical need to expand studies of heterogeneous populations because certain populations are at higher risk of vascular disease and related dementias 2 , 3 . Specifically, Black/African and Hispanic/Latino Americans have a higher prevalence of VCID at 13.8% and 12.2% compared to their non‐Hispanic White peers (10.3%) 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 . To address the significant gaps in scientific knowledge, the Diverse VCID (DVCID) project aimed to enroll 2,250 at‐risk Americans, evenly distributed among Black/African, Latino/Hispanic, and non‐Hispanic White backgrounds 8 . The goal of DVCID is to better understand the impact of incidental white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) on cognitive impairment outcomes across individuals with a different prevalence of risk factors (Figure 1). The designed data collection encompasses cognitive assessment, blood‐based analysis, and neuroimaging 8 . The Repository Core (RC) of the coordinating center at the University of California, Davis (UCD) oversees blood processing, banking, and in‐house blood‐based biomarker measurements and analyses. As vascular brain injury and Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology frequently coexist in older adults and jointly contribute to cognitive impairment and dementia, the DVCID study measures a panel of plasma biomarkers reflecting neurodegeneration, astroglial activation, and AD pathology at the UCD lab, including amyloid beta 40 (Aβ40), Aβ42, phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217), p‐tau181, neurofilament light chain (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and total tau (t‐tau) 9 , 10 . These plasma biomarkers are complemented by advanced imaging‐based assessments of vascular brain injury collected from magnetic resonance imaging, including WMHs, cerebral infarcts, cerebral microbleeds, and hippocampal and cortical volumes. Other components of the DVCID framework evaluate vascular biomarkers, including endothelial dysfunction and innate immune activation. Finally, the study incorporates genetic analyses in contribution to the susceptibility to VCID via genome‐wide genotyping to provide insight into molecular pathology. While these measurements are performed at other labs (Figure 1), for the purposes of this manuscript, we focus specifically on the in‐house plasma biomarkers measured at the UCD lab and do not report findings from imaging, vascular, or genetic analyses.

Blood‐based biomarkers are increasingly recognized as critical tools due to their advantages with respect to cost, access, and lower invasiveness for disease screening, diagnosing, and monitoring across the clinical spectrum 11 . Quantification of these plasma biomarkers could better inform the underlying pathologies driving cognitive impairment and dementia in individuals with higher vascular risk 12 , 13 , 14 .

To ensure the reliability and validity of the biomarker results in this multicenter study, the RC recognizes the importance of standard operating procedures (SOPs) 15 , 16 . Here, we summarize the body of work conducted by the RC to ensure uniformity across sites, including overseeing biofluid collection, processing, storage, and data management. We also outline the analytical strategies employed to harmonize data generated at UCD, including pre‐, intra‐, and post‐analytical quality control (QC) measures. These efforts not only enhance the reproducibility of biomarker measurements but also establish a strong foundation for the DVCID study, ensuring high‐quality data for downstream analyses. By documenting these practices, this work aims to inform future biomarker studies by improving study design and enhancing data comparability across diverse clinical and research settings.

METHODS

Blood collection and processing

Blood specimens are collected via venipuncture in the morning after an overnight fast. To account for physiological factors that may affect sample quality, detailed records are maintained for each participant, including participant ID, age, sex, race, ethnicity, medications, time of last meal, and time of blood collection. To standardize procedures across sites, only approved vacutainers are used (Table 1). Sample collection is performed in accordance with the guidelines from the Seventh Edition of the Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute 17 . Study sites select either maximum (59.0 mL) or minimum (24.5 mL) collection volume upon joining the study, based on practical considerations and site Institutional Review Board (IRB) regulations.

Biospecimens collected in the DVCID study include plasma, erythrocytes, platelet‐poor plasma (PPP), and serum. Blood samples were processed and frozen within 2 h of collection using pre‐barcoded cryovials provided by the RC. The blood processing protocol was adapted from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) and MarkVCID protocols (Appendix SA) 18 . Briefly, blood specimen quality is evaluated (Figure SA.1) and centrifuged, followed by aspiration and aliquoting into the corresponding cryovials, and then immediately stored at −80°C. Specific centrifugation parameters and processing timelines are listed in Table 1; the aliquot schemes for maximum or minimum collection volumes are outlined in Table SA.1. The PPP buffer – used to prevent platelet activation and enable the isolation of PPP samples 19 , 20 – is prepared, aliquoted, and provided by the RC following the MarkVCID1 protocol (Appendix SB) with materials listed in Table SB.1.

Case report form and biospecimen shipment

Case report forms (CRFs) are used to document key information regarding sample collection procedures and are designed to be clear, concise, and consistent. Any deviations from the blood collection and processing SOPs must be reported and recorded. In DVCID, all CRFs have now been fully transitioned to electronic format (eCRFs), with study sites entering information through online forms developed by the RC and Data & Statistical Core. The eCRFs are then bulk imported into the study database on a weekly basis, with the data team overseeing the process and continuously enhancing validation features. Problematic eCRF submissions are flagged and reviewed by the RC, which either corrects the issues or contacts sites for further clarification. Once imported, CRF data are integrated into the database and made accessible to participating study sites.

Collected samples are shipped overnight in accordance with the MarkVCID Human Biospecimens Shipping Guidelines (https://markvcid.partners.org/markvcid1‐protocols‐resources). Samples are stored at −80°C, with contingency plans in place to preserve the long‐term integrity of biomarkers, including protein stability and enzymatic activity 21 . At RC, inventory, allocation, and freeze‐thaw cycle data are managed using OpenSpecimen, which is tailored to our specific needs and for seamless integration with the DVCID database.

Sample allocation

As the RC for the DVCID study, we oversee the allocation of biofluid samples to associated research sites, including University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston, Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, and University of California, Los Angeles for analyses of genetic, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory markers, respectively.

Biomarker quantification

Quanterix Single Molecule Array (Simoa) technology overview

Blood‐based biomarker discoveries drive the development of ultra‐sensitive platforms 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 . In DVCID, plasma biomarkers are quantified using the Quanterix Simoa HD‐X™ Analyzer (Quanterix Corp., MA, USA), a bead‐based immunoassay platform, where analyte molecules bind to antibody‐coated beads and biotinylated detector antibodies, enabling colocalization of streptavidin‐β‐galactosidase enzyme within individual micro‐wells. This technology allows detection of analytes at sub‐femtomolar concentrations with high accuracy, making it suitable for blood‐based biomarker measurements 26 .

Plasma biomarkers measured in‐house at the RC lab at UCD include Aβ40, Aβ42, NfL, GFAP (Human Neurology 4‐Plex E Multiplex Assay), p‐tau217 (p‐tau217 Simoa ALZpath Assay), p‐tau181 (Quanterix Simoa p‐tau181 Advantage version 2.1 Assay), and t‐tau (Simoa Tau Kit).

Maintenance of Quanterix Simoa HD‐X™ analyzer

Instrument maintenance is essential for proper functionality, extended instrument lifespan, reduced downtime, and reliable data quality. All daily, weekly, and monthly maintenance tasks are performed according to the manufacturer's guidelines. The Simoa® Qualification Test is conducted after monthly maintenance to assess instrument performance. Additionally, preventive maintenance by a Quanterix field service engineer is scheduled every 6 months.

Data sharing and tracking

All study data are uploaded to a centralized DVCID database, accessible to authorized researchers via a secure web portal compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996. Biomarker datasets include participant ID, visit number, specimen label, assay version, biomarker name, raw and bridged concentrations, and relevant notes. By default, each study site can access only its own participant data. Request for cross‐site data must be submitted to the DVCID executive committee for review and approval.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

As of November 2025, a total of 1,928 participants are enrolled across 19 study sites, including 34% Black/African (N = 664), 29% Hispanic/Latino (N = 560), and 37% White (N = 704). The cohort has a mean age of 73.36 years (standard deviation [SD] = 5.81; range 56 to 91 years).

Across all visits, 199,451 biospecimen aliquots were collected by participating sites and then accessioned and stored by the RC. A total of 17,591 biofluid aliquots from 1,726 unique participants have been distributed to affiliated research sites for analysis.

Preanalytical variable conditions, their impact, and resolution strategies.

During sample processing, hemolysis, lipemic, or insufficient blood volume are considered unacceptable, as they can adversely affect laboratory results. Samples are processed within 2 h of collection time to minimize blood cell lysis and protein degradation 18 . Proper centrifugation settings are essential, since excessive speeds could cause hemolysis or structural damage of cells, whereas insufficient speeds could result in incomplete separation and mixing of sample components 31 . For serum tubes, complete clotting at room temperature for 30 to 60 min is required, while excessive (> 60 min) and inadequate (< 30 min) clotting time could adversely affect serum protein profiles and lead to incomplete coagulation 36 .

To further reduce labeling errors during processing, the study employed a machine‐readable barcoding system (i.e., QR codes). Each aliquot tube is pre‐labeled with a unique barcode encoding information such as site ID, participant ID, visit time, sample type, and aliquot number. This system streamlines processing workflows and facilitates accurate sample identifications. For study sites where sample collection and processing occur at different locations, proper transportation with a secondary container is necessary to maintain sample integrity.

Analytical measures at UCD lab of RC

Sample selection and randomization

Plasma samples are batched for analysis based on quarterly collection periods. Approximately 10% of samples in each batch consist of previously analyzed samples to support data bridging across production lots (Section 3.4.2). Sample randomization is performed to eliminate selection and plating bias. A balanced, stratified randomization approach is essential to assign processing order, ensuring a representative distribution of stratification factors on each plate.

In DVCID, all factors – including sex, age, and diagnosis, for example – are blinded during sample selection, except for collection site and quantification status (unquantified vs bridging rerun). Incorporating quantification status as a stratification allows even distribution of samples across plates to mitigate intra‐batch effects. Sample plates are designed to approximate the distribution of factors in the overall sample population within the collection period. When batch sizes do not divide evenly into plates, partial plates are created with sample selection aimed at maintaining proportional representation. Certain low‐frequency groups may not be represented on these partial plates but are adequately represented across the remaining plates.

To validate assay performance, manufacturer‐provided calibrators and controls are included in every analytical run on each sample plate. Calibration establishes the relationship between raw instrument data and known reference concentrations, accounting for batch effects. Lot‐specific controls are used to assess within‐ and between‐run variabilities.

Sample preparation

Sample preparation follows the manufacturer's instructions for each assay kit 21 . Reverse pipetting is used during plating to avoid air bubbles, and care is taken to avoid aspirating any cellular debris. Remaining sample aliquots are promptly returned to −80°C storage, with freeze‐thaw cycles monitored and recorded in the database, as freeze‐thaw cycles could significantly influence blood biomarker levels 35 .

Post‐analytical QC procedures at UCD lab of RC

Within‐ and between‐run variability

For each experimental session, two sample plates are analyzed simultaneously on the Quanterix instrument. Within‐run variability is evaluated by comparing values and coefficients of variation (CVs) of calibrators and controls between the paired plates, reflecting the repeatability and precision of results under identical conditions. Between‐run variability is assessed by comparing values and CVs of calibrators and controls across different experimental runs performed using reagents from the same production lot, capturing performance stability over time and identify potential data shifts.

Across all performed assays, both within‐run and between‐run variability are assessed, with CV ≤ 20% considered acceptable 37 , 38 . The values of technical replicates for each biomarker are presented in Figure S3 showing the plate‐to‐plate and lot‐to‐lot variabilities, and the average CVs of technical controls are summarized in Table S3, ranging from 4.63% to 18.63%. Although some biomarkers may exhibit higher variability than others, all assays remained within predefined acceptance criteria. Assay performance is continuously monitored through longitudinal QC tracking, and any QC metrics exceeding established thresholds will trigger investigation and corrective efforts, including repeat analysis of affected sample if necessary to ensure data quality and support the reliability of future longitudinal analyses.

Between‐lot variability

Between‐lot variability is a major source of analytical error, arising from differences among production batches of reagents or materials. Each new reagent lot is rigorously evaluated by comparing values and CVs of re‐run (bridging) samples across lots. Linear regression analyses are performed using re‐run bridging samples, and the coefficient of determination (R 2) is assessed with a pre‐defined acceptance threshold of 0.7 37 , 38 . These criteria were based on the literature on biomarker analyses and commonly used standards, as outlined in the US Food and Drug Administration Bioanalytical Method Validation for Biomarkers (https://www.fda.gov/regulatory‐information/search‐fda‐guidance‐documents/bioanalytical‐method‐validation‐guidance‐industry). As shown in Figure 2, biomarker concentrations measured from the subsequent reagent lot prior to bridging may deviate from those obtained from the original lot. Following application of the linear regression‐based bridging method (Table S1), adjusted values are more closely aligned with the line of identity (y = x), indicating improved agreement between reagent lots and enabling reliable data comparison and subsequent analyses along study phases.

Plasma biomarker measurements from RC‐UCD lab

To date, a total of 7,663 plasma biomarker measurements from 1,105 participants across 1,343 unique visits have been acquired using the Quanterix Simoa HD‐X™ Analyzer. Observed baseline concentrations in DVCID are broadly consistent with ranges reported in prior studies using similar platforms (Table S2A) 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , though direct comparisons are limited by differences in study populations, specimen handling, and analytical conditions. For example, some reference cohorts included participants with AD, which may contribute to differences in biomarker levels, particularly Aβ. Additionally, differences in assay versions may influence reported concentrations. Notably, updates to the Quanterix p‐tau181 assay have improved analytical sensitivity and contributed to higher values than earlier studies.

In our interim analysis, plasma biomarker levels at the baseline visit were evaluated using general linear models. Older age was consistently associated with higher levels of Aβ40, Aβ42, GFAP, NfL, p‐tau217, and p‐tau181 (Figure 3A). After adjusting for age, body mass index (BMI), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) calculated from serum creatinine using the 2021 CKD‐EPI eGFRcr equation, several biomarkers exhibited significant differences across demographic variables. As shown in Figure 3B, plasma biomarker concentrations differed by sex, with Aβ40, Aβ42, GFAP, and t‐tau significantly higher in females and p‐tau181 lower (Aβ40: p = 0.0033; Aβ42: p = 0.0330; GFAP: p < 0.0001; t‐tau: p = 0.0001; and p‐tau181: p < 0.0001); other biomarkers did not differ significantly. Additionally, Aβ40 and Aβ42 demonstrated significant variations across race and ethnicity groups, with Hispanic/Latino participants exhibiting the highest levels, whereas the non‐Hispanic White group showed the highest NfL values (all p < 0.0001) (Figure 3C). Detailed biomarker concentrations and p values derived from general linear models are presented in Table S2B. Importantly, p‐tau181 and p‐tau217 measurements were generated in non‐overlapping participant subsets and are presented as independent descriptive analyses rather than directly comparable measures. Although additional covariates (e.g., physician‐assessed diabetes and hypertension status) were not included, these interim findings highlight heterogeneity in participant characteristics and associated differences in vulnerability across populations. This underscores the importance of accounting for demographic and clinical factors in subsequent analyses and in the design of future clinical studies.

DISCUSSION

Large‐scale multicenter studies inherently face challenges in maintaining consistency across participating sites. With the DVCID study now in steady operation, the RC reflects on practical considerations and valuable experience throughout its development that may inform similar large‐scale clinical studies.

In addition to harmonizing laboratory procedures, consistent documentation is equally important. Early use of hard‐copy CRFs introduced errors and inefficiencies, including illegible handwriting, delays in scanning, and manual data entry. In response, the eCRF was developed to streamline data entry and standardize documentation. While errors can still occur, the eCRF system allows timely detection and correction, improving the overall quality of records.

Additionally, while barcoding systems bring convenience and efficiency, encoding meaningful information rather than using randomly generated numbers can be a double‐edged sword. While it allows processers to quickly identify sample type, minimizes manual entry errors, and streamlines workflow during processing – particularly for visits with high aliquot counts (up to 89) – it also has potential drawbacks. The complexity of meaningful barcodes can increase the risk of misreading, which occasionally requires correcting.

To date, RC has accumulated an inventory of almost 200,000 aliquot cryovials for storage and coordinated distributions for both on‐site and off‐site analyses. Across the cohort, 133 participants did not complete baseline blood collection due to logistical and operational factors, including an inability to obtain blood, insufficient volume following clinical draw, and missed timing of research visit. Median time from collection to processing across sites ranged from 11 to 76 min, followed by immediate freezing, with no evidence of freeze–thaw events or issues. Overall, protocol deviations are infrequent and predominantly minor pre‐analytical events that do not result in specimen exclusion. Inventory workflows and distribution records are documented through OpenSpecimen. Ultra‐low‐temperature freezers are monitored 24/7 by UCD Plant Operations and Maintenance, with contingency plans in place and laboratory personnel notified in the event of power outages or temperature deviations. The RC also implemented a real‐time monitoring and alerting system, Minus80 Monitoring (Nellysford, VA), providing remote alerts and reports for freezer‐related issues, further safeguarding sample integrity.

The robust biorepository structure established a strong foundation for reliable downstream biomarker analyses. In our interim analysis at the RC‐UCD lab, quantified plasma biomarker levels significantly differed across race/ethnicity and sex while adjusting for age, BMI, HbA1c, and calculated eGFR derived from serum creatinine. While these findings emphasize the importance of accounting for participant characteristics and clinical factors, additional analyses are needed to determine whether they reflect variation in underlying risk profiles or chronic conditions across these demographic groups. As limitations exist for this interim analysis, we currently do not have a complete dataset of physician‐assessed diabetes and hypertension status, direct measures of kidney function measures, such as eGFR, are not collected. These factors may influence plasma biomarker levels and, when available, should be incorporated into future analyses. Together, these considerations highlight the importance of carefully accounting for demographic and clinical variables when analyzing and interpreting results. It is important to note that as part of our biomarker analytical strategy, we transitioned from measuring p‐tau181 initially to p‐tau217, as p‐tau217 has been proven to be more sensitive and specific for identifying AD pathology and clinical phenotypes compared to other p‐tau isoforms, including p‐tau181 and p‐tau231 44 , 45 , 46 . It is important to note that in the current dataset, p‐tau181 and p‐tau217 measurements correspond to different sample collection periods in the study and were obtained from entirely separate participant subsets. Therefore, no overlap exists between samples measured for these two assays. To ensure the consistency of p‐tau217 quantification following this transition, previously collected baseline samples (for which only p‐tau181 values are currently available) will be re‐analyzed for p‐tau 217, enabling harmonized p‐tau217 measurements across the full cohort. As additional p‐tau217 data generated over time, lot‐to‐lot bridging will be applied across p‐tau217 assays to ensure comparability across study phases. Recent studies suggest that while p‐tau217 strongly reflects amyloid plaque burden in early‐stage AD, p‐tau205 may better correlate with the neurofibrillary tangle burden in individuals with brain amyloidosis, making it a promising biomarker for mid‐ to late‐stage tau pathology and clinical severity. Importantly, with our standardized processing, stringent intra‐ and post‐analytical strategies, we are well positioned to efficiently implement new biomarkers, adapt to evolving assay forms, and continue to follow and contribute to innovations in ADRD research.

CONCLUSION

Blood‐based biomarkers are a rapidly growing field in cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative diseases due to their potential accessibility and scalability. In the DVCID study, we successfully collected substantial amounts of biofluid aliquots across four sample types. The collection, processing, distribution, and storage of the biofluid specimens are well documented to support downstream analyses.

As outlined in Figure 4, the RC implemented a workflow encompassing pre‐analytical, analytical, and post‐analytical phases. This structured approach helps minimize avoidable variability, enhance sample integrity, and improve research efficiency. Importantly, the integration of harmonized protocols, ongoing data capture, and longitudinal QC provides a reproducible and reliable framework for ensuring data consistency across a large, multicenter study. The biorepository generated by the DVCID study thus provides a robust resource for longitudinal clinical and biomarker research.

Overview of DVCID Repository Core workflow.

Looking forward, blood‐based biomarkers are expected to undergo validation and clinical implementation, though ongoing updates to harmonization practices will be necessary to incorporate emerging findings. This work highlights the importance of harmonized strategies for sample management and data acquisition in large‐scale clinical research and demonstrates how such integration can support reliable and scalable biomarker investigations. Additionally, interim findings suggest that population‐tailored reference ranges should be considered when interpreting blood biomarker data. Together, these efforts lay a strong foundation for future pooled analyses. Through this work, the DVCID RC aims to facilitate access to high‐quality samples and data, supporting large‐scale investigations and accelerating biomarker discoveries.
