Association of Overactive Bladder With Fall Risk Among US Adults Aged 20−69 Years
Authors: Brian J Linder, Larry E Miller, Samir Bhattacharyya, Karen L Noblett, Erin Turner, Shelley Copeland, Melanie N Ketchandji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveFalls are a leading cause of morbidity and loss of independence in adults. The association between overactive bladder (OAB) and fall risk has not been thoroughly investigated. This study evaluated the association between OAB and fall history in a nationally representative sample of US adults aged 20−69 years.MethodsWe performed a cross‐sectional analysis of adults aged 20−69 years from the 2021−2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). OAB was classified using a composite symptom severity score that assessed urge urinary incontinence and nocturia. Fall history was assessed by self‐reporting (only in participants aged 20−69 years), where a recent fall was defined as experiencing at least one fall within the past year. Survey multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) for the association between fall history and OAB after controlling for sociodemographic and clinical covariates.ResultsAmong 4118 participants, the population‐weighted prevalence of OAB was 19.6% (standard error [SE]: 1.1%), and the prevalence of a recent fall was 28.3% (SE: 1.3%). The prevalence of a recent fall was higher among those with OAB (39.7% [SE: 1.6%] vs. 25.7% [SE: 1.5%]). The prevalence of a recent fall increased progressively with OAB symptom severity (24.3% in those with no symptoms vs. 52.4% in those with severe symptoms). In the fully adjusted model controlling for sociodemographic and clinical covariates, OAB was independently associated with a 42% higher odds of experiencing a recent fall (aOR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.10 to 1.83; p = 0.01).ConclusionOAB is independently associated with an increased risk of a recent fall in US adults aged 20‐69 years, with a strong positive relationship between OAB severity and fall risk. These findings suggest that screening for OAB may be a useful adjunct to fall risk assessment. Future studies are needed to determine whether OAB management reduces the risk of falls.Keywords: falls, NHANES, nocturia, overactive bladder, urge urinary incontinence

Introduction

Falls represent a major public health concern, affecting over 216 million individuals worldwide annually and resulting in over 800 000 deaths [1]. Falls are also a leading cause of disability, with lower extremity fractures and traumatic brain injuries accounting for the majority of fall‐related morbidity [1, 2]. The cumulative burden of death and morbidity makes falls one of the leading causes of disability‐adjusted life years worldwide [3]. Given the aging population and escalating healthcare costs, fall prevention has become a national health priority in the United States, with the Department of Health and Human Services establishing fall reduction as a core objective in the Healthy People 2030 program [4]. Identification of risk factors and implementation of effective prevention strategies are imperative for mitigating the rising burden of falls.

Overactive bladder (OAB) represents a physiologically plausible risk factor for falls through multiple mechanisms. Urgency symptoms that necessitate rapid ambulation toward toilet facilities alter gait patterns that may increase fall vulnerability [5, 6]. Nocturia increases this risk via ambulation during periods of reduced lighting and decreased alertness. Additionally, sleep fragmentation due to frequent nighttime voiding may impair daytime cognitive function and motor coordination. Finally, anticholinergic burden is associated with a higher risk of falls and fractures in patients with OAB [7, 8].

Previous studies have reported an increased fall risk among individuals with OAB [9, 10, 11]; however, cohorts were restricted to middle‐aged and older adults with minimum ages ranging from 40 to 65 years. A systematic review found a 1.3‐ to 2.3‐fold higher fall risk among those with OAB [8], but did not adjust results for the influence of demographic and clinical confounders. Overall, prior research on this topic has been limited by narrow demographic representation and insufficient statistical adjustment for potential confounding variables.

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a national survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the US. While urologic symptom assessment has been an integral component of each survey cycle since 1999 [12], fall history was ascertained for the first time in the 2021−2023 NHANES cycle [13]. This provided the first opportunity to examine the association between OAB and fall risk in a nationally representative US population. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the association between OAB and fall history in a nationally representative sample of US adults aged 20−69 years. We hypothesized that OAB would be independently associated with increased fall risk after controlling for established fall risk factors.

Materials and Methods

Source Population

This cross‐sectional study analyzed data from the 2021−2023 NHANES cycle administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) [13]. The NHANES uses a complex, stratified, multistage probability sampling design to select participants from the noninstitutionalized civilian US population. Data collection consisted of personal interviews conducted in‐home or by telephone, and health examinations and laboratory tests performed in mobile examination centers (MEC). All data used in this analysis were publicly available through the NCHS [13]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the NCHS Ethics Review Board approved the study protocol (Protocol #2021‐05). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [14].

Determination of Fall History

Fall history was assessed in participants aged 20−69 years using the Balance section of the 2021−2023 NHANES. Participants were asked, “During the past 12 months, how many times have you fallen?” Response options included “Never,” “1 or 2 times,” “3 to 4 times,” “5 to 9 times,” “10 or more times,” or “Don't know.” Participants who reported at least one fall during this period were categorized as having a recent fall history, while those who reported “Never” were classified as non‐fallers. Responses of “Don't know” were treated as missing.

Determination of OAB

OAB was assessed during MEC visits among participants aged 20 years and older using items from the Kidney Conditions‐Urology section of the 2021−2023 NHANES. OAB status and severity were determined using a two‐item symptom assessment that collectively evaluated urge urinary incontinence (UUI) and nocturia severity on a 0−7 scale, which was adapted from the validation study of Homma and colleagues [15]. UUI was assessed based on the responses to two questions. The first was: “During the past 12 months, have you leaked or lost control of even a small amount of urine with an urge or pressure to urinate and you couldn't get to the toilet fast enough?” Among participants who responded affirmatively, they were subsequently asked: “How much were daily activities affected”? Responses were scored as: No UUI episodes or “Not at all” affected = 0 points; “Only a little” or “Somewhat” = 1 point; “Very much” = 2 points; and “Greatly” = 3 points. These response options were updated from previous NHANES surveys for the 2021−2023 NHANES cycle by shifting from frequency‐based scoring to evaluating impact of symptoms on daily activities (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). Nocturia frequency was determined with the question, “In the past 30 days, during a typical night, how many times did you wake up and urinate?” Responses were scored as: “0” = 0 points; “1” = 1 point; “2” = 2 points; “3” or “4” = 3 points; and “5 or more” = 4 points. Based on the combined UUI and nocturia scores, OAB severity was categorized as none (0 points), mild (1−2 points), moderate (3−4 points), or severe (5−7 points). Participants with a combined score of ≥ 3 were classified as having OAB.

Covariates

Sociodemographic and health‐related covariates were selected a priori based on established associations with fall risk and data availability in the 2021–2023 NHANES. Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, employment status, marital status, household composition (living alone or with others), and family income‐to‐poverty ratio using the Department of Health and Human Services poverty guidelines. Health‐related covariates included obesity, smoking status, sleep duration, alcohol use, sedentary behavior, prescription medication use, and functional limitations. Obesity was defined as a body mass index ≥ 30 kg/m2. Smoking status was categorized as never smoker (fewer than 100 cigarettes lifetime), former smoker (≥ 100 cigarettes lifetime but not currently smoking), or current smoker (≥ 100 cigarettes lifetime and currently smoking). Sleep deficiency was defined as an average of less than 7 h of sleep per night across weekdays and weekends [16]. Heavy alcohol use was defined as an average of ≥ 15 drinks per week for men or ≥ 8 drinks per week for women during the previous year [17]. Excess sedentary time was defined as at least 8 h per day of leisure‐time sitting [18]. Polypharmacy was defined as the concurrent use of five or more prescription medications daily [19]. Functional limitation was assessed using the Washington Group Short Set on Functioning, which evaluates difficulty across six functional domains, including vision, hearing, mobility, communication, cognition, and self‐care [20]. For each question regarding functional limitation, response options included “no difficulty,” “some difficulty,” “a lot of difficulty,” or “cannot do at all.” Participants who responded “a lot of difficulty” or “cannot do at all” to at least one question were considered to have a functional limitation. This validated instrument was selected over individual comorbidity assessment because it assesses the functional consequences of multiple conditions simultaneously and provides a single measure of overall disability that is most directly associated with fall risk.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses incorporated the complex multistage sampling design of the NHANES using sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units to generate nationally representative estimates. Because OAB assessment occurred during MEC visits, MEC sample weights were applied to adjust for differential selection probabilities, non‐response, and age group over‐sampling. Variance estimation was performed using Taylor series linearization. Participant characteristics were reported as percentages with standard errors (SE) and compared between individuals with and without OAB using chi‐square tests. Survey multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine the association between OAB and falls. An unadjusted model was first estimated and reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). This was followed by sequential modeling adjusted for age; age, sex, and race/ethnicity; and all covariates in the fully adjusted models, with estimates reported as adjusted ORs (aOR) with 95% CI. All tests were two‐sided, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Stata v19.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, United States).

Results

Of the 11 933 NHANES participants, 6070 adults aged 20−69 years completed interviews that included sociodemographic characteristics, health measures, and fall history. Among these, 4771 completed an MEC visit, during which 4118 provided data on urinary symptoms relevant to OAB. When survey weights were applied, this sample was representative of approximately 180.2 million US adults aged 20−69 years, with an OAB prevalence of 19.6% (SE: 1.1%).

Most baseline characteristics statistically differed between participants with and without OAB, except marital status, sleep deficiency, and sedentary leisure time. Participants with OAB were more likely to be older, female, of non‐White race, live alone, have lower educational attainment, employment rates, and socioeconomic status, and have a higher frequency of health‐related risk factors, particularly functional limitation (27.3% vs. 11.6%) and polypharmacy (28.4% vs. 12.0%) (Table 1).

Age‐ and sex‐stratified analyses demonstrated a higher prevalence of a recent fall among participants with OAB across most demographic strata, with the greatest differences observed in those aged 55−69 years. In addition, the prevalence of a recent fall increased with increasing OAB symptom severity, ranging from 24.3% in those with no symptoms to 52.4% in those with severe symptoms (Figure 2).

Discussion

This nationally representative observational study examined the association between OAB and the prevalence of a recent fall among US adults aged 20−69 years using data from the 2021–2023 NHANES. After controlling for established fall risk factors, OAB remained independently associated with an increased fall risk. Specifically, participants with OAB had a 42% higher odds of a recent fall. These findings suggest that OAB may contribute to fall susceptibility and highlight the clinical relevance of OAB beyond urinary symptoms alone.

The biological mechanisms underlying the association between OAB and increased fall risk likely involve interactions among bladder dysfunction, cognitive load, and motor control. The urgency component of OAB creates competing cognitive demands that may interfere with the attention and executive function required for safe ambulation [6]. Additionally, anxiety surrounding potential incontinence episodes may lead to altered movement patterns, including shortened stride length and hesitant gait, which increase fall susceptibility [5, 6]. The neurophysiological overlap between bladder control and postural stability, which are both mediated by similar brainstem and cortical regions, suggests that dysfunction in one system may compromise the other via shared neural pathways. The inflammatory processes associated with bladder overactivity may also contribute to a decline in neuromuscular coordination [21]. These interconnected physiological processes provide plausible explanations for the observed independent association between OAB and falls, which warrants further investigation.

A previous meta‐analysis examining OAB and falls reported mixed findings [22], making it difficult to establish definitive conclusions regarding this association. Meta‐analyses are generally unable to adequately control for confounding variables, which may partly explain the heterogeneous results. This pattern is reflected in our results, where the association between OAB and falls was somewhat attenuated after controlling for all fall‐related risk factors. Interestingly, age was not a significant predictor of falls in our study. This finding challenges the conventional assumption that fall risk increases primarily due to aging and instead suggests that clinical conditions such as polypharmacy, functional limitations, and OAB have a stronger influence. This suggests that age‐related fall risk is largely mediated by such conditions and supports a shift in clinical focus from age alone to specific conditions that accumulate with aging and more directly impact fall susceptibility.

The clinical implications of the study findings are relevant for healthcare providers involved in fall prevention and urological care. Incorporating routine screening for OAB symptoms during fall risk assessments may help identify previously under‐recognized yet treatable risk factors, particularly since OAB affected nearly 20% of our study population. Primary care physicians should consider evaluating for OAB in patients with recurrent falls, especially when conventional risk factors do not fully explain their fall patterns. Patient education regarding fall risks associated with urgency episodes may promote safer toileting behaviors, such as the use of assistive devices, ensuring adequate nighttime lighting, and considering the use of bedside commodes. Furthermore, interventions to reduce nighttime voiding, such as fluid management strategies, optimized medication timing, and treatment of underlying sleep disorders, should be actively encouraged.

The findings of the current study also suggest that effective management of OAB may help reduce fall risk, although existing evidence on this relationship remains limited and has yielded conflicting results. Most studies have demonstrated no difference in fall‐related injury between OAB patients treated with pharmacotherapy and those who remain untreated [23]. Moreover, an increased fall and fracture risk has been associated with increasing anticholinergic burden [8, 24]. Despite their modest efficacy in alleviating urinary symptoms, OAB medications are associated with high discontinuation rates [25] and may contribute to increased fall risk due to side effects, including dizziness, somnolence, visual impairment, and orthostatic hypotension. Our finding that polypharmacy was strongly associated with fall prevalence supports this concern and highlights the need for judicious anticholinergic use in this population, including potential withdrawal of oral medications in fall‐prone adults [26]. This recommendation is provided in the STOPP/START criteria and endorsed by numerous international medical organizations [27]. Interestingly, one study of patients with OAB reported a lower fall risk following treatment with surgery, minimally invasive procedures, or pharmacotherapy [10]. This suggests that future research should investigate non‐pharmacological OAB interventions to determine whether adequate symptom relief and fall prevention benefits can be achieved without the risks associated with these medications.

Several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of the current study. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes the establishment of temporal relationships or causal inferences between OAB and falls. Second, OAB classification relied on questionnaire responses rather than urodynamic testing, which may have resulted in misclassification bias. Third, fall frequency was assessed by self‐reporting of falls in the past year, which may have introduced recall bias, particularly in participants with cognitive impairment. Fourth, unmeasured confounding factors, such as medication‐specific effects, physical performance measures, or environmental hazards, could influence the observed associations. In particular, the NHANES does not specify individual‐specific medication classes such as anticholinergic agents or beta‐3 adrenergic agonists that are commonly prescribed for OAB. Since anticholinergic medications are associated with increased fall risk, the inability to adjust for these specific pharmacologic exposures may contribute to residual confounding that impacts the interpretation of the study findings. Fifth, the study population was limited to adults aged 20−69 years because fall history was not ascertained in participants aged 70 years and older in the 2021−2023 NHANES cycle, thus limiting the generalizability of our findings to the population segment at highest risk for both OAB and falls. Although our results are consistent with prior studies on this topic in older adults [28], future NHANES cycles should aim to include fall history data for all adult age groups to better inform prevention strategies in this high‐risk population. Sixth, the causes of falls were not reported in this study. This distinction is clinically important because the contribution of OAB to fall risk may differ substantially between age groups, as younger individuals frequently fall due to accidents or during recreational activities, whereas older adults tend to fall due to gait and balance impairment [29]. Future studies should report fall circumstances to better understand the different types of fall events across age groups. Finally, while this study provides important epidemiologic findings, prospective longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal relationships and confirm whether interventions aimed at reducing OAB symptoms result in measurable reductions in fall risk. Interventional trials comparing pharmacologic and non‐pharmacologic management strategies would also help clarify the safest and most effective approaches in fall‐prone populations.

Conclusion

This nationally representative study demonstrated that OAB is independently associated with an increased risk of a recent fall in US adults aged 20–69 years, with a strong positive relationship between OAB severity and fall risk. These findings suggest that screening for OAB may be a useful adjunct to fall risk assessment. Future studies are needed to determine whether OAB management reduces the risk of falls.
