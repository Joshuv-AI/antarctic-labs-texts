Elevated Driving Pressure in Pressure-Controlled Ventilation: An Independent Risk Factor for Adverse Outcomes in Mechanically Ventilated Neonates
Authors: ShaoCong Zheng, Ya Sun, YuanYi Dai, XueJiao Miao, Shan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDriving pressure (DP) has recently gained attention as a meaningful variable in lung-protective ventilation, particularly in studies of adult acute respiratory distress syndrome. Its role in neonates, particularly regarding bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), is poorly defined.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 145 neonates who received invasive conventional mechanical ventilation (CMV) for ≥ 72 h between January 1, 2020 and December 31, 2024. In this study, DP was obtained by subtracting the applied positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) from the peak inspiratory pressure(PIP), following common clinical practice in pressure-controlled ventilation. Patients were grouped into High DP (≥ 10 cmH2O) and Low DP (< 10 cmH2O). The primary outcome was a composite of BPD, severe intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH grade ≥ III), need for surgical intervention, or death. A subgroup analysis of preterm infants < 32 weeks with neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) assessed DP and the Clinical Risk Index for Babies II (CRIB II) score as predictors of BPD.ResultsThe composite adverse outcome rate was higher in the High DP group than in the Low DP group (44.7 vs. 21.7%; P = 0.03), primarily driven by increased BPD incidence (25.0 vs. 8.7%; P = 0.009). Multivariate analysis identified High DP (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 5.30; 95% CI, 2.20–12.74) and gestational age < 32 weeks (aOR = 11.11; 95% CI, 4.35–28.36) as independent risk factors. In the NRDS subgroup (n = 61), both High DP (aOR = 5.80; 95% CI, 1.61–20.90) and higher CRIB II score (aOR = 4.44; 95% CI, 1.28–15.42) independently predicted BPD, with comparable discriminatory ability (DP AUC = 0.707; CRIB II AUC = 0.733). Lower DP was protective against progression to more severe BPD (aOR = 0.20; P = 0.008).ConclusionElevated DP is an independent, modifiable risk factor for adverse outcomes—particularly BPD—in mechanically ventilated neonates. The effect is most pronounced in very preterm infants. Routine DP monitoring combined with clinical scoring (e.g., CRIB II) may improve early risk stratification and facilitate individualized lung-protective ventilation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00408-026-00880-4.Keywords: Driving pressure, Bronchopulmonary dysplasia , Mechanical ventilation, Neonate, Preterm infant, CRIB II, Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation is frequently life-saving for critically ill neonates but can cause ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI), especially in preterm infants with immature lungs [1, 2]. Increasing evidence suggests that driving pressure (DP) plays an important role in lung-protective strategies [3, 4], with adult ARDS studies reporting associations between elevated values and worse outcomes [5]. Although DP has been extensively studied in adults and children with ARDS [6–8], data on neonatal populations are limited. Preterm lungs—characterized by structural and functional immaturity [9]—may be particularly susceptible to elevated DP, yet gestational age–specific DP guidance is lacking.

Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) [10, 11], driven by surfactant deficiency, frequently necessitates mechanical ventilation and is a major contributor to bronchopulmonary dysplasia(BPD) [12], a leading cause of long-term respiratory morbidity in preterm infants. Current neonatal ventilation guidelines emphasize limiting tidal volumes and optimizing positive end expiratory pressure(PEEP) [13], but DP-specific thresholds for neonates are not well established.

This study aimed to evaluate the association between DP and adverse outcomes in neonates receiving invasive conventional mechanical ventilation(CMV). In pressure-controlled ventilation—a mode widely used in neonates, especially for those with low birth weight(BW) or poor lung compliance—direct and continuous measurement of tidal volume is often less reliable or unavailable. In this context, DP serves as a readily available bedside surrogate, reflecting the pressure gradient applied to deliver each breath and correlating with the stress applied to the respiratory system. We hypothesized that elevated DP is independently associated with an increased risk of composite adverse outcomes, especially BPD, and that this association is stronger in more premature infants due to their greater lung vulnerability.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This single-center retrospective study enrolled neonates who received invasive CMV for respiratory failure at the Department of Neonatology, the First People's Hospital of Yunnan Province, between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024.

Inclusion criteria: ① Neonates receiving invasive CMV; ② CMV duration ≥ 72 h.

Exclusion criteria: ① Receiving high-frequency or non-invasive ventilation within the first 72 h of life; ② CMV duration < 72 h; ③ Severe lethal congenital anomalies; ④ Incomplete medical records or parental withdrawal of treatment.

The study protocol received ethical approval from the hospital’s institutional review board. Informed consent requirement was formally waived owing to the retrospective design of this research.

This broad initial cohort allowed for an exploratory assessment of DP across the spectrum of ventilated neonates. To address the expected heterogeneity in lung mechanics and ventilation strategies, pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed, stratified by gestational age(GA) (< 32 weeks vs. ≥ 32 weeks) and focused on the more homogeneous subgroup of preterm infants with NRDS, where lung-protective ventilation strategies are most critical.

Ventilator Parameters and Data Collection

All neonates received pressure-controlled ventilation using a Stephan Stephanie Ventilator (Stephanie Medical Systems, Germany). To minimize patient-ventilator asynchrony and pain related to endotracheal intubation, a standardized sedation and analgesia protocol was applied during the first 72 h of mechanical ventilation, consisting of continuous Midazolam and Fentanyl infusion. The ventilator is equipped with a flow sensor located at the proximal end of the endotracheal tube (Y-piece), which measures pressure and flow directly at the airway opening, ensuring accurate recording of peak inspiratory pressure (PIP) and PEEP. Ventilator parameters, including fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), PEEP, PIP and mean airway pressure (MAP), were recorded by trained nursing staff beginning after endoentracheal intubation and stabilization of breathing, defined as the absence of respiratory distress (no chest retractions, cyanosis or oxygen desaturation) for ≥ 1 h. For analysis, DP was derived by subtracting PEEP from the PIP( D P = PIP − PEEP). Parameters were documented every one hour. For every 24-h interval, the median recorded value was used to represent the day’s ventilator settings. This process was repeated for the first three consecutive full calendar days of CMV (Days 1–3), and the final analyzed ventilator parameters were calculated as the arithmetic mean of the three daily median values.

The following other parameters were extracted: baseline characteristics (including GA, sex, BW, mode of delivery, Apgar score, hospitalization duration, mechanical ventilation duration[MVD] and oxygen therapy duration), prenatal high-risk factors(including nuchal cord, fetal distress, placental abnormalities, premature rupture of membranes, preeclampsia, turbid/blood-stained amniotic fluid, and maternal diabetes) and admission status(Clinical Risk Index for Babies II [CRIB II] score [14], temperature[T], Heart rate[HR], Respiratory rate[RR], blood glucose, PH, severe birth asphyxia, meconium aspiration syndrome [MAS] and NRDS).

Outcomes

The adverse outcomes were defined as the occurrence of any one of the following in neonates [15]: BPD (along with its severity grading [12]), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) [16] of grade III or higher, severe retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) [17] stage 3 or above, severe neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) [18] stage IIB or higher, or death.

Other outcomes were documented, including purulent meningitis (PM), persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN), ROP managed non-surgically, NEC, hospitalization duration, mechanical ventilation duration and oxygen therapy duration.

Grouping and Rationale

We initially compared DP between neonates with and without the composite adverse outcome. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the distribution of DP values differed between the two groups, with the adverse-outcome group showing a higher median DP (10.23 [IQR 9.50–10.67] cmH2O) compared to those without adverse outcomes (9.67 [IQR 8.92–10.67] cmH2O; P = 0.042). Based on this distribution, clinical relevance (round number for ease of clinical use), and prior observational data suggesting increased risk around this level in immature lungs, a cutoff of 10 cmH2O was selected to define High DP (≥ 10 cmH2O) and Low DP (< 10 cmH2O) for subsequent analyses. This threshold was then tested for its association with outcomes in the independent, pre-specified subgroup analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses used SPSS version 26.0. Continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared by Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed data are reported as median (IQR) and compared by Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and compared using χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were < 5. Univariate logistic regression screened potential predictors; variables with statistical significance were entered into multivariate logistic regression models to identify independent predictors of adverse outcomes and BPD. Ordinal logistic regression assessed factors associated with BPD severity. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and AUC values evaluated discriminatory performance. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Phase One: DP and Composite Adverse Outcomes

Cohort and Ventilation Parameters

Of 172 neonates screened, 145 met inclusion criteria and were analyzed. Patients were grouped into High DP (≥ 10 cmH2O; n = 76) and Low DP (< 10 cmH2O; n = 69) (Fig. 2). The DP ≥ 10 (n = 76) and D P < 10 (n = 69) cohorts demonstrated balanced baseline demographics, including GA, BW, sex, delivery mode, twins or triplets, Apgar score and surfactant-treatment showed no statistically significant disparities. Prenatal risk profiles were comparable except for significantly higher preeclampsia incidence in the D P < 10 group (20.3% vs. 7.9%, P = 0.031). Admission parameters (vital signs and pH) and diagnoses (NRDS, MAS and severe asphyxia) showed no intergroup differences (Table 1).

Ventilator profiles differed: the High DP group had higher FiO2 (0.38 ± 0.09 vs 0.34 ± 0.06; P = 0.010) and higher PIP (16.6 ± 1.5 vs 14.8 ± 1.7 cmH2O; P < 0.001). PEEP was slightly lower in the High DP group (median 5.7 [5.0–6.4] vs 6.0 [5.5–7.0] cmH2O; P = 0.014), but the small effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.179) suggested limited clinical relevance.

Outcomes and Risk Stratification

Composite adverse outcomes occurred in 44.7% of the High DP group versus 21.7% of the Low DP group (P = 0.03). This difference was mainly due to higher BPD incidence (25.0% vs 8.7%; P = 0.009) and higher PPHN (11.8% vs 2.9%; P = 0.04). No significant differences were detected for mortality, surgical interventions, or severe IVH. MVD was longer in the High DP group (6.29 ± 4.98 vs 4.86 ± 3.56 days; P = 0.024); hospitalization and oxygen therapy durations did not differ significantly (P > 0.05) (Supplement 1).

To identify the independent risk factors for adverse outcomes related to neonatal mechanical ventilation, we first conducted a univariate analysis. Although there was no statistically significant difference in GA between the two groups (P = 0.485), due to its well-established importance in influencing neonatal outcomes and the fact that a GA stratification analysis will be conducted next, we still consider it a key covariate. Eventually, the variables with P < 0.10 from the univariate analysis and GA were jointly included in a multivariate binary Logistic regression model for correction analysis. In multivariate logistic regression both High DP (aOR = 5.30; 95%CI, 2.20–12.74; P < 0.001) and GA < 32 weeks (aOR = 11.11; 95%CI, 4.35–28.36; P < 0.001) were independent predictors of the composite adverse outcome (Fig. 3A).

Gestational Age Stratification

Subgroup analysis based on GA revealed that the detrimental association between elevated DP and adverse outcomes was primarily concentrated in infants born at < 32 weeks of gestation. As presented in Fig. 1B, among infants < 32 weeks, the incidence of adverse outcomes was significantly higher in the High DP group compared to the Low DP group (75.0 vs 31.7%; P < 0.01). In contrast, among infants ≥ 32 weeks, differences in outcome incidence between DP groups did not reach statistical significance (17.5 vs 7.1%; P = 0.192). This pattern underscores the heightened vulnerability of very preterm infants to elevated driving pressure.

Phase Two: DP and BPD in NRDS Cohort

Baseline Characteristics

A focused analysis included 61 preterm infants < 32 weeks diagnosed with NRDS (Fig. 2). The BPD group (n = 23) had lower GA (28.78 ± 1.28 vs 30.10 ± 1.09 weeks; P < 0.01), lower BW (1168.48 ± 207.3 vs 1352.63 ± 227.17 g; P = 0.02), higher CRIB II scores (median 8 [6–10] vs 6 [5–8]; P = 0.002), and higher DP (median 10.00 [9.5–10.33] vs 9.33 [8.58–10.03] cmH2O; P = 0.007) compared to controls (Supplement 2). All infants in this subgroup received surfactant replacement therapy immediately after intubation. Therefore, surfactant use was universal in this cohort and was not analyzed as a variable.

Multivariate Modeling

Given the linear correlation between GA and BW, we utilized the CRIB II score—a validated assessment tool that incorporates both parameters—for subsequent analyses. Univariate logistic regression models were applied to evaluate the associations of CRIB II score, DP, MVD, and oxygen therapy duration with the outcome. The results demonstrated statistically significant associations for CRIB II score (P = 0.004), DP(P < 0.001), and MVD(P = 0.021), while the oxygen therapy duration(P = 0.998) showed no statistical significance. To dissect the independent contribution of DP to BPD risk, we constructed a multivariate logistic regression model incorporating CRIB II score, DP and MVD. The CRIB II scores were originally categorized into four groups [14]: CRIB 1 (scores 1–5), CRIB 2 (scores 6–10), CRIB 3 (scores 11–15), and CRIB 4 (scores > 15). However, due to the absence of scores exceeding 15 in our dataset, the final analysis included three groups (CRIB 1, CRIB 2, and CRIB 3). Additionally, DP was stratified into two groups based on a threshold of ≥ 10 cmH2O, and MVD was stratified into two groups based on a threshold of ≥ 7 days. The analysis in Fig. 3B unequivocally identified both DP (aOR = 5.80, 95%CI: 1.61–20.90, P = 0.007) and CRIB II score (aOR = 4.44, 95%CI: 1.28–15.42, P = 0.019) as independent predictors of BPD development. MVD showed a trend toward significance (aOR = 3.07, 95%CI: 0.84–11.19, P = 0.089) but did not reach statistical significance. The results underscore the critical role of DP and CRIB II score in BPD pathogenesis, independent of ventilation duration.

Predictive Accuracy and Clinical Stratification

The ROC analysis demonstrated moderate discrimination for DP (AUC = 0.707; 95%CI, 0.570–0.844; P = 0.007) and CRIB II (AUC = 0.733; 95%CI, 0.603–0.863; P = 0.003) (Fig. 1C). These results highlight the clinical utility of integrating DP and CRIB II scores for early risk stratification in neonates.

We further collected data on the severity grading of BPD among affected infants, categorizing them into three groups: no BPD, Grade I/II BPD, and Grade III BPD (based on the 2018 NIH consensus criteria [12]). An ordinal logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the associations of DP and CRIB II score with BPD severity progression. The results in Fig. 3C, D further established that lower driving pressure served as a protective factor against BPD severity progression (aOR = 0.20, 95%CI: 0.06–0.66, P = 0.008). CRIB II stratification confirmed significantly reduced severe BPD incidence in the lowest risk category (CRIB 1 vs CRIB 3, P < 0.05). These findings suggest that lower DP values and milder baseline clinical risk (CRIB 1) are protective against severe BPD progression, reinforcing the clinical importance of minimizing DP and early risk stratification in neonatal care. However, the validity of these findings is questionable due to the limited sample size (only 5 cases) in the group CRIB 3.

To validate the accuracy of the ordinal logistic regression results, we further analyzed the association between the three CRIB II groups and the occurrence of BPD. The findings are presented in Fig. 1D, neonates in CRIB 1 exhibited markedly reduced odds of severe BPD compared to both CRIB 2 (scores 6–10) and CRIB 3 (scores 11–15) (P < 0.05), aligning with the regression model (CRIB 1: aOR = 0.095, P = 0.033). And no significant difference in BPD risk was detected between CRIB 2 and CRIB 3 (P > 0.05), aligning with the regression model too. Therefore, the results of the ordinal logistic regression analysis provide meaningful insights and hold significant reference value.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort demonstrates that elevated DP (≥ 10 cmH2O) in pressure-controlled ventilation is independently associated with composite adverse outcomes—most notably BPD—in neonates receiving invasive mechanical ventilation, with the strongest effect observed in infants < 32 weeks GA. In the NRDS subgroup, DP and CRIB II provided comparable predictive accuracy for BPD, and lower DP was associated with reduced BPD severity.

A similar pattern has been described in adult ARDS research [19, 20], where higher DP has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes. The lower DP threshold observed here (10 cmH2O vs 15 cmH2O often reported in older children [21, 22]) likely reflects neonatal lung vulnerability: fewer alveoli, surfactant deficiency, and high chest wall compliance increase susceptibility to mechanical stress and VILI.

An important observation in this study is that the association between DP and adverse outcomes was most pronounced in infants born before 32 weeks of gestation. This GA–dependent pattern is consistent with the biological understanding that immature lungs are more vulnerable to ventilation-related injury [23, 24]. These findings support previous experimental observations suggesting that mechanical stress may interfere with normal alveolar and vascular development—the fundamental pathological processes underlying BPD [12].

Clinically, these results suggest that incorporating DP into ventilatory decision-making may be relevant in neonatal practice. The comparable predictive performance of DP(AUC = 0.707) and the validated CRIB II score (AUC = 0.733) for BPD development suggests that these parameters offer complementary information. While CRIB II captures baseline physiological vulnerability [25], DP provides real-time assessment of ventilator-induced lung stress. Their combined use enables dynamic risk stratification that could guide preemptive interventions.

A methodological consideration in our study pertains to the calculation of DP. In this study, pressure-controlled ventilation was used, and DP was therefore determined using PIP and PEEP, consistent with routine neonatal ventilation methodology [26, 27]. Because plateau pressure is not available in pressure-controlled ventilation [19, 28], this value represents working pressure rather than a plateau measurement. Consequently, our calculated DP represents the total DP required to overcome both the elastic recoil of the respiratory system and the airway resistance.

The focus on DP in this study stems from its practical utility in pressure-controlled ventilation, which remains the primary mode for initiating ventilation in many neonatal intensive care units worldwide, particularly for very low BW infants. While volutrauma is a key mechanism of VILI, in the absence of reliable, continuous tidal volume monitoring in all neonates, DP provides a valuable surrogate metric. It represents the dynamic pressure applied to overcome the elastic recoil of the respiratory system to deliver a breath. Our finding of an independent association between higher DP and adverse outcomes, especially in very preterm infants with low compliance, suggests that even within “safe” peak pressure limits, excessive pressure swings (high DP) may be deleterious. This supports a ventilation strategy aimed at minimizing DP through optimization of PEEP and careful titration of inspiratory pressure, alongside attention to clinical gas exchange targets.

Our data support a paradigm shift from uniform ventilation approaches toward individualized strategies based on pulmonary mechanics and developmental stage. The stark contrast in DP effects between preterm and more mature infants underscores the inadequacy of one-size-fits-all ventilation strategies. For clinicians, this implies that DP reduction should be prioritized in preterm neonates through careful titration of PEEP and tidal volume, even when traditional parameters like peak pressure appear acceptable.

We acknowledge the physiological and clinical diversity within our cohort, encompassing a range of GA and diagnoses. To address this, our analysis was stratified, and the most robust findings emerged specifically in the more homogeneous subgroup of very preterm infants (< 32 weeks) with NRDS. Regarding the DP threshold of 10 cmH2O, it was derived from our data distribution and aligns with a rounded value feasible for clinical vigilance. While not based on a prior physiological model, its strong independent association with outcomes in the preterm subgroup suggests clinical relevance. Future prospective studies are needed to validate if this or other GA-specific thresholds can serve as targets for intervention.

Although both duration and intensity of oxygen exposure are recognized contributors to BPD [29, 30], mean FiO2 over the first three days of ventilation was not significantly associated with adverse outcomes in our cohort. In univariate analysis of the Phase One population, FiO2 showed no predictive value for the composite outcome. Similarly, in the NRDS subgroup, FiO2 did not differ between infants who developed BPD and those who did not. These negative findings may reflect the relatively narrow range of FiO2 values in our study population, or they may suggest that the deleterious effect of DP outweighs the impact of moderate hyperoxia in the context of invasive mechanical ventilation.

An unexpected finding in our study was the higher prevalence of maternal preeclampsia in the Low DP group. Several explanations may account for this observation. First, preeclampsia often necessitates medically indicated preterm delivery, and infants born to preeclamptic mothers may have relatively more mature lungs for their GA due to the absence of intra-amniotic inflammation, which could reduce their vulnerability to ventilator-induced lung injury. Second, clinicians might adopt a more conservative ventilation strategy in these often growth-restricted infants, intentionally using lower inspiratory pressures and thereby achieving lower DP. Third, given the multiple comparisons performed, this could be a chance finding, especially because the absolute number of preeclampsia cases was small. Importantly, despite this imbalance, multivariate analysis confirmed that DP ≥ 10 cmH2O remained an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes after adjusting for preeclampsia and other confounders, supporting the robustness of the association.

In addition, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. As the study was retrospective and conducted at a single center, further multicenter prospective research will be required to confirm these findings. Then, The absence of long-term pulmonary and neurodevelopmental outcomes limits our understanding of the lasting impact of early DP exposure. Third, the calculation of DP as PIP-PEEP in pressure-controlled ventilation, while clinically standard, incorporates both elastic and resistive components, unlike plateau pressure-derived DP in volume-controlled modes. Fourth, the selection of the DP threshold was data-derived; its generalizability and optimal value require external validation. Fifth, our study included only infants who received invasive mechanical ventilation for ≥ 72 h; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to non-ventilated preterm infants who may also develop BPD from other mechanisms such as antenatal inflammation, postnatal infection, or oxygen toxicity. The pathogenesis of BPD is multifactorial, and our results specifically highlight the role of a modifiable ventilator-associated risk factor in the subset of infants who require invasive support.

Prospective, multicenter studies are needed to validate GA–specific DP thresholds and test whether DP-targeted ventilation protocols reduce BPD incidence. Incorporating advanced bedside monitoring (e.g., electrical impedance tomography) could allow regional assessment of lung stress and better guide individualized ventilation.

Conclusion

In summary, higher DP during pressure-controlled ventilation appears to function as a potentially modifiable risk factor for adverse outcomes, particularly BPD, among ventilated neonates. The effect is most pronounced in very preterm infants. Incorporating DP monitoring alongside clinical risk tools such as the CRIB II score may help identify high-risk infants earlier and support more individualized ventilation strategies.
