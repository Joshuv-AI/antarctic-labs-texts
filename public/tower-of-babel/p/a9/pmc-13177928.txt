Time to Initiation of Antibiotic Therapy in Critically Ill Patients with Suspected Sepsis and Its Effect on Intensive Care Unit Mortality: Analysis Using Bayesian Approach
Authors: Amarja Ashok Havaldar, Aswathi Saji, Tinku Thomas, M Selvam, Janin Josy, CS Nandhini, N Madhammal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsSepsis is a leading cause of intensive care unit (ICU) admission and mortality. Although early antibiotic therapy in the emergency department improves outcomes, the clinical impact of delays in ICU-based antibiotic escalation is not well established. This study evaluated the association between time to ICU-based initiation of newly prescribed antibiotics and ICU mortality using a Bayesian analytical framework.Patients and methodsA prospective observational study (2019–2021) was conducted in a tertiary care hospital, including adult patients admitted to the ICU. New antibiotic administration represented an escalation of therapy for suspected new-onset or worsening infection. Delay was defined as the interval between prescription and actual administration. Repeated antibiotic escalation events within patients were analyzed using a Bayesian generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) accounting for within-patient correlation.ResultsA total of 695 antibiotic escalation events occurred in 467 patients (mean age 50.6 ± 17.7 years; 61.7% male). The majority of patients received antibiotics within 3 hours of sepsis recognition (90.6%), and 42.8% within 1 hour. Intensive care unit mortality was 25.9%. Each hour delay in administration of newly prescribed ICU antibiotics was associated with increased odds of ICU mortality [posterior odds ratio (OR): 1.13, 95% credible interval (CrI): 1.11–1.15]. Higher mortality was also associated with septic shock, hospital-acquired infection, higher delta sequential organ failure assessment (Δ SOFA), and higher Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II) Score.ConclusionDelays in ICU-based antibiotic escalation are associated with increased mortality. Timely escalation may represent an important, modifiable target to improve outcomes in critically ill patients with suspected sepsis.How to cite this articleHavaldar AA, Saji A, Thomas T, Selvam M, Josy J, Nandhini CS, et al. Introduction

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction with dysregulated immune response as per the sepsis 3 definition.1 Sepsis remains the leading cause of in-hospital mortality worldwide.2,3 There are several critical interventions, including fluid therapy, antibiotic administration, vasopressors, and source control, to improve patient outcomes.4–6 Among these interventions, timely antibiotic administration and adequate source control remain crucial in the management of sepsis.

From the initial inception of sepsis guidelines from 2004 till date, various changes have been made in the guidelines, but the important armamentarium in control of sepsis is antibiotics and source control.4–6 The timing of antibiotics is considered the key deciding factor, and each hour delay is associated with an increased mortality.7

The landmark retrospective cohort study by Kumar et al., of 2,731 patients, showed a strong correlation between delay in antibiotic administration and increased mortality. Each hour delay in antibiotic administration was associated with a 7.6% increase in mortality.8 In this study, the median time to effective antibiotic therapy was 6 hours [interquartile range (IQR): 2–15 hours]. Liu VX et al. observed similar results, with 1.8% increase in mortality with each hour delay in septic shock [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.8–3.0; p = 0.001] with a median antibiotic administration time of 2.1 hours (1.4–3.1 hours).9 Peltan et al. studied the association of delay in antibiotics on long-term mortality and observed that a delay of >3 hours was associated with an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 1.27 with a 95% CI, 1.13–1.43.10 A retrospective cohort study based on culture positivity determined the number needed to treat to prevent mortality was four.11

The impact of early antibiotic administration on mortality remains controversial. A meta-analysis of 11 studies by Sterling et al. questioned the practice of early antibiotics. This study did not find a difference between antibiotics given within 3 hours or 1 hour of septic shock.12 Conversely, the meta-analysis by Johnston et al., published in 2017, showed a 33% reduction with administration of antibiotics within 1 hour [OR: 0.67 (95% CI: 0.59–0.75)].13 Additionally, a Japanese study of 1,124 patients found no association between timing of antibiotics and hospital mortality.14 These diverging results highlight ongoing uncertainty.15 Most studies are conducted in emergency department settings and focus on the initial administration of antibiotics. There is a lack of data on the timing of subsequent antibiotic administration during hospitalization and on outcomes, particularly for community-acquired vs hospital-acquired infections. This variability highlights the limitations of conventional frequentist methods, which typically yield point estimates and p-values without fully accounting for uncertainty, prior knowledge, or heterogeneity across patient populations. Recent methodological advances suggest that the Bayesian approach may enable the integration of prior evidence with observed data, providing probabilistic interpretations (e.g., the probability that earlier antibiotic initiation reduces mortality). It is also better equipped to handle sparse data, hierarchical structures, and complex relationship features common in ICU sepsis datasets. Importantly, Bayesian analysis yields complete posterior distributions, facilitating a richer understanding of effect sizes and uncertainty.

Given these advantages, a Bayesian framework offers the potential to reconcile conflicting evidence on antibiotic timing by explicitly modeling uncertainty and incorporating prior findings. This approach may help determine not only whether early antibiotics reduce ICU mortality, but also the magnitude and credibility of such effects across diverse patient subgroups. A recent study modeled ICU deaths in Malaysian ICUs using the Bayesian approach, demonstrating its applicability in this context.16

Accordingly, our study employs a Bayesian approach to evaluate the association between time to antibiotic initiation and ICU mortality. By leveraging this framework, we aim to provide more robust and clinically meaningful evidence to guide antibiotic timing in critically ill patients with sepsis.

Patients and Methods

A prospective observational study was conducted from February 5, 2019, to January 24, 2021, after institutional ethical committee approval (IEC: Institutional Ethics Committee 385/2018, Clinical Trial Registration India: CTRI 2019/02/017409). All procedures were followed in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional or regional) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed (Strobe checklist) (Supplementary material, STROBE Checklist). A waiver of consent was given as this study was examining the process of care.

The primary objective was to examine whether delay in antibiotic administration in critically ill patients was associated with increased ICU mortality. We also examined the association between the administration of appropriate antibiotics and ICU mortality. To evaluate the timing of antibiotics, baseline characteristics and admission diagnosis were collected. Information about previous hospitalization within 90 days, prior use of antibiotics, and the name of the antibiotics administered, if available, was captured. The reason for administering antibiotics was captured. The time and date of antibiotic prescription and antibiotic administration were captured. Any ICU-based escalation of antibiotics was considered as adding a new antibiotic to the existing therapy. Delay in new antibiotic administration was defined as the difference between the time of antibiotic administration and the time of diagnosing sepsis based on clinical parameters. The prescription of new antibiotics was considered as a new onset of sepsis or as a worsening of existing infection. On the day of prescription of the new antibiotics, data on PaO2/FiO2 ratio, the requirement of vasopressors, and creatinine were collected to assess the severity of illness. SOFA and APACHE II scores were calculated. We calculated Δ SOFA as the difference between baseline SOFA and SOFA calculated on the day of prescribing a new antibiotic to suggest new-onset sepsis or worsening of existing sepsis and to minimize the bias due to differences in treating physicians’ decisions to initiate a new antibiotic. The number of days from ICU admission to antibiotic administration was captured. The type of infection was classified as community-acquired if an antibiotic was administered within <48 hours of hospital admission and as hospital-acquired if >48 hours. Baseline characteristics of the patients were collected. During ICU stay, whenever new antibiotics were prescribed, details were collected. The reason for adding new antibiotics was classified as empirical and based on the culture report.

To evaluate the appropriateness of antibiotics, we conducted a survey of critical care experts using questionnaires to determine the factors they consider when assessing antibiotic appropriateness. The parameters considered were age, comorbidities, previous hospitalization or previous antibiotic usage, presence of shock requiring vasopressors, hypoxia, associated organ dysfunction, and the day when the antibiotic was administered.

The primary outcome was ICU mortality. When participants had received two or more antibiotics with the same prescription date and time, and there was less than an hour delay between their administrations, it was considered a single observation.

The sample size was calculated based on 20% of the patients receiving antibiotic within 1 hour, and the estimated sample size was 246.

Statistical Analysis

The association between delay in antibiotic administration and ICU mortality was evaluated using a Bayesian generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). The use of this model was prespecified to account for the repeated measures of antibiotic administration within individual patients and to incorporate uncertainty more transparently than traditional frequentist approaches. More details on the model have been explained in the supplementary document (Supplementary Material, Bayesian approach).

In this model, the delay in antibiotic administration was treated as a fixed effect, while individual patients were modeled as random effects to account for within-patient correlation. The analysis adjusted for key clinical covariates identified a priori based on clinical relevance, including age, gender, presence of shock, PaO2/FiO2 ratio, serum creatinine, day of ICU stay when the antibiotic was administered, APACHE II score (within 24 hours of ICU admission), SOFA score at baseline, and Δ SOFA on the day of antibiotic administration (Supplementary Material, Figs 2 to 4).

Informative priors were specified for key predictors (delay in antibiotic administration and presence of shock) based on consistent effect estimates reported in observational studies.8,17 These priors were intentionally conservative, centered on previously reported effect sizes with clinically plausible variance, to reflect existing knowledge without overwhelming the observed data. All other variables used weakly informative priors.

Importantly, we recognize the potential for confounding by severity, whereby sicker patients may both receive earlier antibiotics and have a higher baseline risk of mortality. To mitigate this bias, the model adjusted for multiple markers of illness severity, including APACHE II score and Δ SOFA, which captures dynamic worsening of organ dysfunction at the time of antibiotic escalation. By incorporating these covariates within a Bayesian framework, the model estimates the independent association between antibiotic delay and mortality while accounting for severity-related confounding.

Model estimation was performed using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation with Gibbs sampling in R (version 4.2.1) interfaced with JAGS (version 4.3.0). Convergence of the chains was confirmed using trace plots, Monte Carlo standard errors, and the Brooks–Gelman–Rubin diagnostic. Model fit was assessed using the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC).

The results are presented as posterior means and 95% credible intervals (CrIs), representing the probability that the true effect lies within the specified range. Statistical inference was based on posterior probabilities rather than traditional p-values, providing a more intuitive interpretation of uncertainty and effect size.

Appropriateness of Antibiotics

A total of seven critical care experts independently assessed antibiotic appropriateness, and each prescription was reviewed by two experts. Antibiotic prescription was considered appropriate if both experts mentioned it as appropriate. It was considered inappropriate if both the experts mentioned it as inappropriate. If there was a discrepancy between the responses, it was categorized as indeterminate. The association between antibiotic appropriateness and patient outcomes (mortality) was evaluated using a GLMM.

The mean age was 50.65 (17.71) years, and 25.5% were above 65 years old (Table 1). Among them, 61.7% were males, and 38.33% were females. The ICU mortality was 25.91%. The majority of the patients (90.6%) received antibiotics within 3 hours and 42.82% within 1 hour of recognition of sepsis. The median delay in administration of antibiotics was 1 hour (0.5–1.67 hours). The baseline and clinical characteristics of patients, categorized by survivors and non-survivors, are provided in Table 1. Among the various comorbidities, hypertension and diabetes mellitus were the most common comorbid illnesses observed. A lower PaO2/FiO2 (PF) ratio at the time of antibiotic administration was associated with higher mortality (p = 0.026) (Table 1). Higher SOFA and APACHE II scores were significantly associated with increased mortality (p < 0.001). The association between information related to previous hospitalization and prior use of antibiotics with mortality (Table 1) was examined. The median length of ICU stay was 6 days (3–11). There was no difference in the length of ICU stay among survivors and non-survivors [5 (3–10) vs 6 (3–11), p-value: 0.561] (Table 1).

Univariate analysis for all potential risk factors is shown in Table 2. Variables that were found to be significant in the univariate analysis were age [posterior OR: 1.78 (1.01, 3.27)], delay in administration [posterior OR: 1.13 (1.11, 1.15)], presence of shock [posterior OR: 1.37 (1.20, 1.57)], PF ratio [posterior OR: 0.75 (0.59, 0.92)], hospital-acquired infection [posterior OR: 1.86 (1.04, 3.5)], Δ SOFA [posterior OR: 1.16 (1.05, 1.29)] and APACHE II score [posterior OR: 1.103 (1.067, 1.15)].

Bayesian GLMM incorporating all risk variables showed that the OR for participants with shock compared to those without shock, (posterior OR: 1.3, 95% CrI: 1.14, 1.48), had higher odds of mortality. On average, the odds of dying in the ICU for participants with shock were approximately 1.3 times higher than for participants without shock. Participants who received antibiotics for hospital-acquired infection (defined as the initiation of antibiotics after 48 hours of admission) also had higher odds for mortality (posterior OR: 1.79, 95% CrI: 1.01–3.5) (Table 2 and Fig. 1). At the time of antibiotic administration, a higher SOFA score than baseline increased the odds of mortality [posterior OR: 1.25 (1.12, 1.43)]. The APACHE II score also significantly increased the risk of mortality [posterior OR: 1.14 (1.09, 1.2)].

Appropriateness of Antibiotics

The appropriateness of the antibiotics was checked as described in the methodology. We did not observe a difference in the mortality based on the appropriateness of the antibiotics. Compared to inappropriate antibiotic use, appropriate administration was associated with an OR of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.37–1.21], while indeterminate administration showed an OR of 1.19 (95% CI: 0.69–2.08).

Discussion

The current study showed that in patients with suspected sepsis, each hour delay in administering a new antibiotic was associated with higher odds of ICU mortality (posterior OR: 1.129). Higher mortality was observed in patients with septic shock, with hospital-acquired infection, with a higher APACHE II score, and a change in SOFA score from the baseline.

The importance of early antibiotics was first reported in the largest database retrospective study by Kumar et al.8 Our observations are similar to Kumar et al.'s study. The retrospective studies and meta-analysis by Johnston et al. have demonstrated the importance of early antibiotics in sepsis or septic shock patients.8–10 In the prospective studies, antibiotic delay was not associated with increased mortality.14 In the preplanned analysis of the randomized controlled trial in a subgroup of septic shock patients, delay in antibiotic administration was not associated with mortality. However, if the antibiotic was started after the development of shock, mortality was higher.17

We observed a mortality of 25.91%. Kumar et al.'s study had a higher mortality of 56%, possibly due to the higher severity of illness with an average APACHE II score of 26 ± 8.6.8 In the current study, the median APACHE II score was 18 (13–25), with a higher APACHE II score in non-survivors, 23 (16.5–29). The Japanese study showed the APACHE II score ranged from 21 to 25, and the SOFA score was 8–9. However, the timing of antibiotics was not associated with the higher mortality.13 We observed significantly higher median SOFA score [10 (7–12)] (Table 1), and Δ SOFA was associated with the higher mortality (Table 2).

In comparison, our study has certain distinct characteristics. It was a prospective study including patients with sepsis and septic shock. The delay in antibiotic administration was studied among patients admitted to the ICU vs the emergency department. In the emergency department, it may not be possible to correctly identify the etiology of shock in a certain subset of patients. This can lead to inappropriate and unindicated use of antibiotics.

The study gives a pragmatic view about antibiotic administration in patients admitted to the ICU. The study also takes into account clinical and laboratory parameters like the presence of shock, oxygenation and creatinine, thereby evaluating the prior probability of the presence of sepsis. The decision of administration of antibiotics by the clinician is made by carefully evaluating priors and the likelihood of sepsis or septic shock in a particular patient. This is similar to the Bayesian approach. When the diagnosis of sepsis is certain, the delay in antibiotic administration is going to impact ICU mortality. This is demonstrated in our study.

There are certain limitations to our study. Although the diagnostic tools and clinical parameters used for confirming sepsis and following sepsis bundles will be similar in the various ICUs, there will be differences in practices across the institutions. The generalizability of the findings will be limited by the single-center design and the focus on ICU-based antibiotic escalation. We classified discharged against medical advice (DAMA) patients as alive and dead based on the clinical condition at the time of discharge. This approach was adopted, and DAMA patients with advanced illness, ongoing organ dysfunction, or a poor prognosis, and non-availability of reliable post-discharge outcomes were classified as dead. This may have resulted in overestimation of mortality, and DAMA should therefore be interpreted as a composite adverse outcome rather than confirmed death. We calculated the SOFA score at the time of antibiotic prescription to objectively confirm the presence of sepsis and to minimize the bias. This is also confirmed by Δ SOFA being a significant parameter associated with ICU mortality. However, the decision to initiate new antibiotics could be based on the treating physician's judgment and may not reflect the exact onset of sepsis but fulfills the criteria of organ dysfunction.

The appropriateness of antibiotics is difficult to determine. There are studies mentioning checking the appropriateness of antibiotics based on the in vitro culture report.11 The appropriateness based on the culture positivity may not be an accurate approach, considering low blood culture positivity rates around 18%. Reasons for negative culture reports include inadequate volume of blood, inoculation technique, or processing.17 We did not find any association of prior hospitalization on the appropriateness, probably due to the non-availability of data on prior antibiotics used in the majority of these patients. We did not find any association between the appropriateness of antibiotics and mortality. Delay in the administration was the only factor associated with the mortality.

This suggests early antibiotics are associated with reduced mortality in ICU patients admitted with sepsis. Timing of antibiotics is one of the factors in the treatment of sepsis, but there are several risk factors, like source control, which play an important role to ensure adequate antibiotic concentration at the site of infection. The reasons for the delay in administration can help us in introspecting the process of care.

In our analysis, the posterior estimates for the association between delay in administration of antibiotics and ICU mortality were consistent across univariate and multivariable Bayesian models. This consistency is attributable to the use of a highly informative prior for the delay coefficient, derived from high-quality evidence in the literature and specified with a narrow variance. Such a prior exerts substantial influence on the posterior distribution, effectively anchoring the estimate unless the observed data provide strong contradictory evidence. This behavior is an inherent feature of Bayesian inference when the prior is precise; the resulting posterior reflects a weighted combination where prior information dominates, particularly if the likelihood is of comparable or smaller precision.

Conclusion

Time to initiation of new antibiotics is an important factor associated with ICU mortality in sepsis patients. Septic shock, hospital-acquired infections, and higher severity of illness, as shown by APACHE II and Δ SOFA, were associated with higher ICU mortality. The findings of the study need to be validated in a larger cohort.
