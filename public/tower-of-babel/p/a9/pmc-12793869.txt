History of Presentation

The patient was a 53-year-old woman, who presented with a gradually developing shortness of breath that had been progressing over a period of 1 year and which became worse with climbing stairs. Her physical examination was unremarkable.

Past Medical History

The patient had a past medical history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and recurrent attacks of palpitations that were diagnosed as supraventricular tachycardia, and for which, she was started on metoprolol.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included myxoma, cardiac hemangioma, papillary fibroelastoma, and cardiac lymphoma.

Investigations

Basic laboratory and electrocardiography results were within acceptable parameters. Transthoracic echocardiography (ECHO) showed a large, homogeneous, pedunculated hyperechoic mass arising from the interatrial septum through a stalk and moving to and from the left atrial cavity, not causing mitral valve obstruction, and measuring 38 × 22 mm (Figures 1 and 2). The left ventricle was normal in size, and the estimated ejection fraction was 55% to 60%. No regional wall motion abnormalities were noted. The patient underwent coronary angiography, which showed no obstructive coronary artery disease. The left circumflex artery was found to give a big atrial branch, which shows fistulous malformations to the left atrium (Figures 3 and 4).

Management

The patient was initially diagnosed with a left atrial myxoma and was planned for surgical excision. She underwent median sternotomy with bicaval cannulation. Through a transseptal incision, a huge left atrial mass attached to the septum was found, which was excised successfully (Figure 4). The mass was sent to histopathology for diagnosis. Postoperatively, the histopathology results showed that the patient had a left atrial hemangioma (Figure 5).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient had a smooth postoperative course and was discharged home 6 days postoperatively. Follow-up ECHO after 3 months showed no regrowth of the mass, with a normal left ventricular function.

Discussion

Cardiac tumors are rare. The incidence of primary cardiac tumors found during autopsies is 0.02%, with 75% being benign.1 Among cardiac tumors, myxoma is the most common type.2 Vascular cardiac tumors, such as cardiac hemangiomas, are uncommon, with an incidence of 1% to 2% of all cardiac tumors, mostly originating from the right side of the heart and only 7% originating from the left atrium.1 The first case of cardiac hemangioma was reported in 1950 after mass removal by Hochberg and Robinson.3 Hemangiomas are usually asymptomatic. They are mostly discovered incidentally with routine examination or during autopsy, although some patients present with shortness of breath, chest pain, syncope, or heart failure.4 This case study presents a 53-year-old woman with a left atrial hemangioma who underwent surgical resection.

Unlike the gastrointestinal tract or cutaneous structures, hemangiomas rarely arise from the heart.

Both cardiac hemangiomas and myxomas originate from mesenchymal tissues. Hemangiomas originate from multipotent stem cells, primarily differentiating into endothelial cells. On the other hand, cardiac myxomas originate from multipotential mesenchymal cells and show multidirectional differentiation, expressing endothelial, epithelial, and myocardial markers, and have angioma-like thin-walled blood vessels that contain mucus.5 Cardiac hemangiomas can be divided histologically into 3 main types: cavernous, capillary, or mixed type.6 No specific electrocardiography finding can raise suspicion for cardiac hemangiomas.7 This patient presented with multiple attacks of supraventricular tachycardia, which were controlled with medical management.

A review article on multiple cases of atrial hemangiomas concluded that ECHO is the best diagnostic modality for assessment and surgical planning.7 In addition, cardiac magnetic resonance and computed tomography are valuable in determining the size and location of these tumors. Cardiac magnetic resonance typically shows a high signal intensity of the mass on T1- and T2-weighted images.8 In this case, ECHO was performed, and a hyperechoic mass originating from the left interatrial septum was found. As myxomas are the most common type of cardiac tumors, misdiagnosis can occur. Coronary angiography can aid the diagnosis by showing a unique feature of tumor blushing.8 In the present case, the patient's left circumflex artery was found to give a branch to the tumor. Surgical excision of hemangiomas is usually performed as soon as the mass is discovered, especially in symptomatic patients. Median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass is often necessary.6 This patient underwent surgical removal of the mass with no intraoperative complications. As cardiac hemangiomas have a low incidence, an accurate prognosis cannot be drawn. Multiple reports mention that the most common documented postoperative complication is inaccurate ligation of the hemangioma blood vessels.9 One study concluded that survival after surgical resection has a similar rate to that of a standard population, with a recommendation of annual ECHO follow-up for the first 4 years.10 In this case, the follow-up ECHO showed no mass growth with a normal heart function 3 months after the surgery.

Conclusions

Cardiac hemangioma is a rare primary cardiac tumor that occurs in the middle-aged and elderly population with a broad spectrum of presentation. Therefore, a high rate of suspicion is needed to investigate and manage this mass. ECHO is considered the best imaging modality for diagnosis, and surgical resection is the definitive management approach.
