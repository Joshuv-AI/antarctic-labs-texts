Automated experimentally validated antenna design framework using versatile parameterization scheme
Authors: Slawomir Koziel, Anna Pietrenko-Dabrowska, Stanislaw Szczepanski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractModern antenna design is an intrinsic undertaking. It relies heavily on expert knowledge and computational tools, especially electromagnetic (EM) simulation software. The most daunting phase is the development of a fundamental antenna topology that exhibits the assumed functionality (such as antenna’ ability to operate across a broad or multiple frequency bands) while fulfilling other conditions (e.g., sufficiently small size). The process typically takes weeks of interactive work and involves partly trial-and-error-based geometry modification and parametric studies. As a result, a limited number of possible configurations may be examined. Unsupervised topology generation constitutes an attractive alternative, although the existing methodologies are computationally expensive or intrusive (e.g., require dedicated fast solvers and/or adjoint sensitivities). This study introduces a database-aided automated antenna design technique and its experimental validation. Our approach employs a flexible parameterization incorporating adjustable elliptical patches and gaps. Comprehensive simulation data acquired for diverse arrangements of the building blocks and substrate sizes is looked up at the design stage, and the most promising architecture is tuned using the expedited gradient-based optimizer. As demonstrated, the presented approach enabled the specification-based development of high-performance antenna geometries, requiring fewer than two hundred EM simulations. For illustration, several broadband, ultrawideband, and multi-band antennas are designed, including structures optimized for minimum size. A distinctive feature of the procedure is the ability to generate wideband antennas (e.g., 5–6 GHz, 3.1–5.8 GHZ), ultra-wideband structures (3.1–10.6 GHz), and up to triple-band radiators (e.g., 2.45–5.3–7.5 GHz), or devices featuring specific dimensions (e.g., fixed width or length). Due to highly unconventional antenna geometries rendered by the proposed approach, experimental validation of the developed structures is instrumental to corroborate the relevance of the methodology. Toward this end, selected designs are prototyped, and their electrical and field characteristics are measured, showing good agreement with full-wave simulations. This underscores the suitability of the proposed technique for automated antenna development with no expert interaction required.Keywords: Antennas, Design automation, Experimental validation, Antenna measurement, Specification-based design, Design database, Versatile parameterization, Parameter tuning

Introduction

Antennas are essential for modern technologies, including wireless communications (5G, internet of things, etc.1–4), implantable devices, medical imaging, remote sensing, or wireless power transfer5–8. Their design is typically based on reusing already available baseline structures (patches, dipoles, monopoles) and incorporating alterations such as slots, metamaterial cells, substrate-integrated waveguides9–11, intended to realize required functionality (e.g., broadband operation, circular polarization). Initial geometries are subject to parametric studies12,13 or formal optimization14,15 to improve the key performance figures (bandwidth, impedance matching, axial ratio, gain, etc.). To ensure accuracy, antenna development is mainly realized using electromagnetic (EM) simulation tools16. Numerous numerical procedures are available targeting local tuning17–21, global search22–33, multi-criterial optimization34–38, and statistical design39–42. As EM-driven design entails considerable computational expenses39, diverse expedited methods were suggested, including surrogate-assisted techniques (using behavioral40–50 and physics-based models51–53), feature-based approaches54–57, response correction routines58,59, machine learning (ML)60–69, or variable resolution algorithms70–72. The work73 offers an overview of recent surrogate-assisted and ML techniques for antenna design.

As mentioned, implementing required functionalities and enhancing performance is typically done by modifying fundamental geometries (monopoles, patches). Eventually, this approach leads to structures resembling the baseline topologies74,75. Furthermore, the design process is laborious, restricting the exploration of possible alternative options. Topology optimization (TO) offers a conceptually distinct approach where the antenna geometry is subject to optimization. It can be realized by spatially discretizing the area assigned for the antenna, e.g., dividing it into square-shaped regions, which may be selectively filled with metal76–80. In specific situations, it is possible to discretize only selected antenna parts, e.g., radiator81. The allocation of the unit elements is determined using soft computing techniques82–85. The advantage of these methods is improved flexibility. The drawback is computational complexity: the design process becomes a large-scale combinatorial task. Another possibility is pixel antennas, where the design process aims to optimize connections between pre-defined unit cells86–90. Free-form TO is yet another methodology that allows the development of almost arbitrary geometries91–96. However, these methods typically rely on fast, dedicated EM solvers necessary to make the process computationally feasible97–101. Consequently, they cannot be integrated with commercial simulation environments. Also, due to relying on local (gradient-based) optimizers, the free-form TO outcome highly depends on the availability of quality initial designs.

The difficulties of techniques outlined above (limited versatility, high computational cost, reliance on reasonable initial designs, lack of integrability with commercial EM solvers) can be alleviated by procedures combining automated determination of antenna topology and low-cost dimension adjustment. In102, a specification-driven design methodology employing flexible geometry parameterization (rectangular-shaped patches and holes) has been proposed. The method enables almost unlimited topological variations while using a relatively small number of parameters. The two-stage design process involves computational intelligence algorithms for antenna architecture evolution and local optimizers for final tuning. Another unsupervised design technique103 utilizes similar optimization strategies but different parameterizations (elliptical-shaped patches and gaps). Both methods enable a rendition of unconventional antenna architectures and can be integrated with commercial EM simulation environments. Yet, due to the utilization of bio-inspired search algorithms, their computational efficiency is still relatively high.

This study suggests an alternative approach to unsupervised antenna design. Our methodology is a database-aided technique employing a flexible parameterization incorporating adjustable elliptical patches and gaps, similar to103. However, cost-intensive computational-intelligence-based antenna topology evolution is replaced by a database lookup. The latter encapsulates comprehensive simulation data acquired for diverse arrangements of the building blocks and substrate sizes. The most promising architecture undergoes local tuning realized using the expedited gradient-based optimizer. The initial cost of the proposed arrangement is high. However, the database can be reused as necessary. The lookup cost is negligible. These features have been demonstrated by designing several antennas with diverse characteristics (single-, dual-, triple-band, broadband), compact structures, and antennas with specific (constrained) dimensions, all using the same database. The only extra expenses associated with final tuning correspond to about 150 EM simulations (here, the underlying solver is CST Microwave Studio). Because the antenna topologies rendered by the proposed approach are highly unconventional, their experimental validation is imperative to corroborate the practical utility of the technique. This has been achieved developing a complete design framework that incorporates prototyping and physical measurements of electrical and field characteristics (radiation patterns) as its inherent part. The results obtained for selected designs show good agreement between the predictions of the respective computational models and the measured responses.

The main technical contributions of this research are (i) the development of the automated antenna design framework operating at a low cost of a few hundred EM analyses, (ii) proving the method’s capability to handle diverse scenarios through the design of several antennas of distinct characteristics using identical algorithmic setup, (iii) demonstrating the ability of the procedure to render unconventional antenna topologies also with constraints pertinent to antenna size and explicit miniaturization, (iv) development and execution of the complete design procedure involving automated antenna rendition, optimization, prototyping, and experimental validation. It should be reiterated that the entire design process is unsupervised and purely specification-driven. The framework can be integrated with commercial EM solvers. The latter is essential for its accessibility in academic and industrial contexts.

Database-driven unsupervised antenna design

The proposed unsupervised design procedure is detailed in this section. We start by outlining the parameterization employed, decision variables, and the computational model of the antenna in Section “Antenna parameterization and EM simulation model”. Data acquisition and database rendition are covered in Section “Antenna parameterization and EM simulation model”. Section “Local tuning” elaborates on local tuning of antenna parameters, whereas Section “Design framework” explains the operation of the complete framework.

Antenna parameterization and EM simulation model

Topology parameterization is a critical factor for any unsupervised design procedure. The main prerequisites are versatility and simplicity. On the one hand, parameterization must allow for rendering various distinct architectures. On the other hand, the number of decision variables should be relatively small so that the associated optimization problems are manageable, considering that the underlying computational model involves EM analysis. Also, designable parameters are preferred to be mainly continuous, which enables the utilization of gradient-based search procedures. Moreover, parameterization should be readily implementable in commercial simulation environments (here, CST Microwave Studio).

In this study, we adopt the parameterization suggested in103 for planar structures, which has been briefly recalled in Fig. 1. The antenna is rectangular (substrate width and length of W and L, respectively) with a partial ground plane (length Lg), a discrete port of a relocatable position, and front metallization assembles using NP elliptical patches and NG elliptical gaps. All components have adjustable sizes and locations, which enables a large variety of possible topologies, as illustrated in Fig. 1c. The overall number of decision variables, aggregated into a vector x, is n = 5 + 4(NP + NG) and depend on the number of applied patches and gaps. The discrete parameters (NP, NG) may be subject to optimization or fixed. Similarly, the substrate parameters W and L may be fixed for a specific application or optimized (e.g., to achieve minimum size). These features, and using mainly continuous variables, are considerable advantages of the discussed parameterization over pixel antennas and free-form topology optimization regarding flexibility and the computational complexity of the underlying design optimization task.

Planar antenna parameterization103: (a) essential components and their parameters, from left to right: substrate, ground plane, discrete port, one of NP elliptical patches, one of NG elliptical gaps; (b) assembling antenna geometry through Boolean transformations; (c) random topologies rendered with NP = 5 and NG = 3 to showcase variety of possible geometries (discrete ports shown using black dots).

The antenna’s computational model is implemented in CST Microwave Studio104. The CST template model encapsulates 10 patches and 6 gaps, that are disabled as necessary by assigning the unused components zero size. It is modified by incorporating the actual decision variable values. Antenna responses are then extracted from EM data obtained through a batch-model simulation. Database rendition

In this study, unsupervised antenna development is based on finding the most promising design (regarding the specific performance requirements) through a database lookup and further gradient-based refinement, as discussed in Section “Local tuning”. The database is constructed through the sequential design of experiments, evaluation of the computational model, and incorporation of the antenna responses into the dataset. Database acquisition is subject to trade-offs concerning the applicability range of the design frameworks. For example, we may consider the antenna size (W and L) as fixed or treat them as decision variables. Similarly, the antenna substrate may be fixed in terms of thickness h and relative permittivity εr; alternatively, h and εr may be considered variables. Increasing the number of parameters enhances the scope of applicability at the expense of a more significant number of data samples required to represent the design space adequately.

In this work, the computational model is evaluated using coarse-discretization EM analysis to reduce the database acquisition costs. This compromises the accuracy but makes the simulation considerably faster (by a multiplicative factor between two and three, design dependent). The accuracy loss will be accommodated at the local tuning stage, executed using the high-fidelity version of the EM model.

The solution process is broken down into a series of sub-problems where the subsequent approximations to x* are found using an auxiliary linear model of antenna’s responses. Antenna gradients are evaluated using finite differentiation (FD), which incurs n pluse one EM simulations in each iteration (recall that we have n = 5 + 4(NP + NG) decision variables). To lower the costs, parameters having minor effects on antenna responses are eliminated from the FD-based sensitivity updating process after the first iteration.

The rationale behind using the TR algorithm is that it enables a convenient handling of antenna characteristics. In particular, the procedure employs an intermediate model of antenna response (cf. (6)), which is established using the Jacobian matrix estimated through finite differentiation. Once constructed, the model is analytically smooth and easy to handle by the underlying search engine (cf. (5)). The same can be said about handling constraints, which are also computed using the linear model (6). In contrast, direct optimization of the EM-evaluated objective function (here, (1) or (3)) is much more troublesome because of certain level of numerical noise associated with EM analysis (e.g., due to adaptive meshing and residual energy during time-domain analysis), and the necessity of repetitive sensitivity estimation in each algorithm iteration.

Results

To demonstrate the ability of the presented unsupervised design framework, we develop several broadband and multi-band antennas. In some cases, additional constraints are imposed concerning the antenna size. The same database is used for all examples, and the algorithmic setup is identical regarding the parameterization complexity, control parameters of the tuning algorithm, etc. Because the antenna geometries generated by the proposed procedure are highly unconventional and do not conform to standard structures (monopoles, dipoles, etc.), experimental validation is essential to demonstrating the practical utility of the proposed approach, in particular, to verify that the predictions of the computational (EM) models are trustworthy.

To achieve that, prototyping and experimental validation of selected designs are carried out, and the comparison between measured antenna responses and those evaluated through full-wave simulation is analyzed.

It should also be emphasized that the proposed design framework is primarily deterministic. The only stochastic component, albeit formally outside the scope of the design procedure itself (cf. Fig. 5), is a generation of the database, normally composed of randomly assigned parameter vectors. Given the database, the design process itself is fully deterministic. In particular, both major steps (database lookup and fine-tuning) are deterministic. The database search is exhaustive, and, given design specifications, always produces the same results. Similarly, TR-based optimization always yields the same outcome when starting from the same initial design and using the same setup regarding its control parameters.

Discussion

The results gathered in Section “Results” demonstrate the exquisite performance of the proposed design framework. It has been shown capable of rendering a large variety of antenna structures satisfying diverse requirements such as single-, dual-, and triple-band operation and ultrawideband operation. It can also address more complex design scenarios, such as explicit size reduction under impedance matching constraints, or developing antennas of a pre-defined size, as long as the underlying database covers the respective parts of the decision variable space. For example, in Case 2, an ultrawideband antenna has been developed featuring around − 12 dB of maximum in-band |S11|, whereas in Case 9, a UWB antenna was designed for minimum size (featuring around − 10 dB of maximum in-band |S11|). The footprint of the antenna obtained for Case 9 is only 445 mm2 compared to 635 mm2 for Case 2. Similarly, the lower UWB antenna designed for best impedance matching exhibits a footprint of 615 mm2, whereas the antenna developed for minimum size has a footprint of only 380 mm2 (Cases 3 and 10). In the latter scenario, the bandwidth is limited to precisely comply with the requirements but slightly wider when no geometry constraints are present. In Case 11, a dual-band antenna featuring a pre-defined substrate length L = 15 mm was designed. In Case 12, the geometry conditions were set even stricter, to specific dimensions W = 30 mm and L = 16 mm. Yet, our framework could also render the structure fulfilling the assumed specs under this scenario.

A tremendous advantage of the presented methodology is the extremely low cost of antenna design, which is only about 150 EM simulations on average. Consequently, it only requires two to three hours to conclude the design process. As mentioned earlier, this efficiency level is possible because of exploring the database acquired beforehand. Data acquisition is associated with high costs. However, from the practical perspective, this is not a serious issue, as the sequential generation of random data samples can be realized in the background using computational resources available at any given moment for further use. Also, the database can be expanded as necessary to enhance the range of applicability of the framework. For example, for demonstration in this study, the database was limited to the FR-4 substrate of the thickness h = 1 mm. In principle, the substrate thickness and its relative permittivity may be treated as decision variables, which will be considered in future work. Another aspect is the sheer size of the database. While it is intuitively clear that adding the data samples should translate into improved reliability and extend the range of possible design specifications that can be attainable, the specific relationship between these factors is to be determined through additional studies.

Conclusion

This work introduced a novel approach to the high-efficacy un-supervised design of antenna structures. The critical components of our methodology are flexible and scalable parameterization of antenna geometry and a combination of database-driven rapid identification of promising topologies and local refinement of antenna dimensions using gradient-based algorithm. The parameterization includes relocatable patches and gaps of elliptical shape that may be adjusted and assembled into antenna architecture through Boolean transformations. Other building blocks (ground plane, discrete port) are also optimizable. The database encapsulates sequentially generated random designs corresponding to structures of diverse complexity and different substrate sizes and can be readily expanded by adding further designs using available computational resources. As database lookup is fast, the only added expenses are those associated with local tuning. The latter is expedited by variable screening and incorporating the sparse sensitivity updating strategy. The design procedure is fully guided by the user-defined specifications and requires no intervention from the experienced professional.

The proposed methodology was comprehensively showcased by designing twelve antennas of various characteristics, such as wide-band, narrowband, multi-band (both dual and triple), and compact structures rendered through explicit size reduction while observing impedance matching constraints imposed over the frequency ranges of interest. The ability to develop innovative antennas of specific footprint dimensions was also showcased. All devices were obtained using the same database containing 100,000 random samples, the identical parameterization complexity, and the local tuning algorithm setup. Selected designs were experimentally validated. Prototyping and physical measurements are imperative to demonstrate the design utility of the proposed methodology due to highly unconventional geometries of the generated antenna structures, which do not conform to traditional topologies (e.g., monopoles, patches, or dipoles). In this study, a complete design framework was developed, wherein manufacturing and experimental validation is an inherent part. The results corroborate the relevance of our approach with a good alignment between full-wave simulations and measurements obtained for all considered test cases.

The fundamental advantage of our framework is computational efficiency: the typical cost of rendering an antenna corresponds to just 150 EM simulations (the cost of database lookup can be neglected). Other benefits are versatility and expandability: the database can be readily enlarged by adding new samples, and the decision variable space may incorporate additional parameters such as substrate permittivity or its thickness. Although the initial cost of database acquisition is high, the data therein may be reused as necessary without incurring extra expenses. At the same time, generating innovative antenna structures takes just a couple of hours. Yet another benefit is the possibility of tailoring the framework to a specific class of antennas in terms of size, substrate, type of frequency characteristics, and so on. Future work will be focused on generalizations of the presented technique as outlined above, incorporating artificial intelligence tools to improve the exploration of available data and developing other classes of structures, such as circular polarization devices.
