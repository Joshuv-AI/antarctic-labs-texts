Prevalence of anaemia and associated factors among pregnant women living in Koloji camps of Fafan Zone of Somali Region, Ethiopia, 2024
Authors: Nimo Ahmed Ismael, Merga Dheressa, Wondimye Ashenafi Cheru, Mowlid Abdi Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnaemia is a major global public health problem affecting pregnant women, with a prevalence of about 40–50% in Sub-Saharan Africa and significant maternal and fetal consequences. Despite interventions, it remains highly prevalent in Ethiopia with regional variation. Internally displaced pregnant women are at higher risk due to food insecurity, poor diets, and limited health services. However, evidence on anaemia in Ethiopian IDP settings is limited.Therefore, this study assessed the prevalence and associated factors of anaemia among pregnant women in Koloji IDP camp, Somali Region, Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from February 1–29, 2024, among pregnant women residing in Koloji IDP camp. A total of 390 eligible pregnant women were approached, of whom 380 participated (response rate: 97.5%). Data were entered into Epi-Data version 3.1 and analyzed using STATA version 17. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with anaemia. Statistical significance was declared at p-value < 0.05.ResultsA total of 380 pregnant women participated in the study. The overall prevalence of anaemia was 42.11% (95% CI: 37.2–47.1). Of the anemic pregnant women, 32.5% had mild anaemia, 51.9% had moderate anaemia, and 15.6% had severe anaemia. Factors significantly associated with anaemia included maternal educational status, gestational age, history of blood loss, meal frequency, frequent consumption of coffee/tea immediately after meals, and lack of iron–folic acid supplementation.ConclusionAnaemia among pregnant women in Koloji IDP camp remains a severe public health concern. The findings highlight the need for strengthened maternal nutrition interventions, improved dietary counseling, and enhanced access to iron–folic acid supplementation services, particularly in humanitarian and displaced population settings.Trial registrationNot applicable. This study was not a clinical trial and therefore was not registered in a clinical trial registry.Keywords: Anaemia, Pregnant women, Internally displaced persons, Camps, Koloji, Somali Region, Ethiopia

Introduction

Anaemia remains a major global public health problem affecting low-, middle-, and high-income countries, with substantial consequences for health, cognitive development, work productivity, social wellbeing, and economic development. According to the World Health Organization, anaemia particularly affects young children, pregnant women, postpartum women, and women of reproductive age worldwide [22]. It occurs across all stages of the life course; however, the burden remains disproportionately high among pregnant women, women of reproductive age, and young children globally [22]. The World Health Organization defines anaemia during pregnancy as a hemoglobin concentration below 11.0 g/dL [21]. During pregnancy, anaemia is classified according to hemoglobin concentration as mild (10.0–10.9 g/dL), moderate (7.0–9.9 g/dL), and severe (< 7.0 g/dL) ([21]; McGuire 2015). Maternal anaemia remains a key concern because it is associated with reduced maternal health status, increased vulnerability to infection, and poor pregnancy outcomes [11, 14].

Globally, anaemia remains one of the most widespread public health problems, affecting an estimated 1.92 billion people worldwide, particularly women and children [22]. The burden is highest in low-income countries, particularly in Africa and South Asia [19]. A large systematic analysis reported that anaemia prevalence among women of reproductive age and pregnant women remains high in Central and West Africa and South Asia [19]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, anaemia during pregnancy remains widespread, and millions of pregnant women are affected annually (Susan T 2007). In East Africa, the prevalence of anaemia among pregnant women varies considerably, ranging from approximately 20% in Rwanda to above 30% in Uganda [5, 16].

Pregnancy substantially increases micronutrient requirements. Iron requirement increases two- to three-fold, while folate requirement increases markedly during pregnancy. WHO has highlighted that women in developing countries may spend a large proportion of their reproductive years pregnant, increasing their risk of anemia [12]. Although anaemia during pregnancy is multifactorial, iron deficiency is the leading contributor, and previous evidence suggests that a large proportion of anemia cases in pregnancy are attributable to iron deficiency. Several factors have been reported to be associated with anaemia in pregnancy, including maternal nutritional status, household wealth index, maternal education, maternal age, parity, place of residence, occupation, history of terminated pregnancy, iron intake during pregnancy, unimproved water sources, and marital status.

Anaemia during pregnancy is a major contributor to maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries ([11]; Nair et al. 2017). Globally, the prevalence of anaemia among pregnant women has been estimated to be 36.8%, with the highest prevalence in Africa (41.7%). In Sub-Saharan Africa, about 35.6% of pregnant women were reported to be anaemic [17]. The burden of anaemia also has substantial economic implications, contributing to loss of productivity and economic development [21]. Economic loss due to anaemia, particularly iron deficiency anaemia, has been estimated at approximately $2.32 per capita (0.6% of GDP), increasing to $16.78 per capita (4.05% of GDP) when cognitive losses are included. Anaemia was also responsible for a large burden of disability globally, contributing to 58.6 million years lived with disability in 2019, with the highest burden reported in Western Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Central Sub-Saharan Africa. In low- and middle-income countries, maternal anaemia is responsible for an estimated 12% of low birth weight, 19% of preterm births, and 18% of perinatal mortality [17].

In Ethiopia, anaemia remains a major public health problem among pregnant women, with considerable regional variation. Evidence from studies conducted in different parts of the country shows that the prevalence of anaemia among pregnant women ranges from 9.7% in North Shoa Zone to 56.8% in Eastern Ethiopia [6]. Nationally, the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) reported that 22% of women aged 15–49 years are anaemic; among these, 17% have mild anaemia, 5% moderate anaemia, and 1% severe anaemia. Anaemia prevalence was higher among pregnant women (22%) compared to breastfeeding women (19%) and non-breastfeeding women (15%). Despite improvements in antenatal care services and interventions such as iron supplementation, deworming, malaria prophylaxis, and distribution of insecticide-treated nets, anaemia in pregnancy remains persistent.

Internally displaced persons (IDPs) and refugee populations may be at particularly high risk of anaemia due to overlapping vulnerabilities. Displaced populations often experience food insecurity, limited dietary diversity, dependence on food assistance, and reduced access to health services. Overcrowded living conditions, inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene, and increased exposure to infectious diseases further increase the risk of malnutrition and micronutrient deficiencies [18]. In many camp settings, diets are dominated by non-perishable staple foods that may not provide adequate micronutrients. Although fortified staple foods are commonly provided through humanitarian food assistance programs, prolonged dependence on limited food rations may still increase the risk of micronutrient deficiencies among vulnerable populations [20]. Evidence also suggests that anaemia can remain a public health concern among displaced populations even where acute malnutrition does not reach emergency thresholds. However, empirical evidence on anaemia among pregnant women living in internally displaced persons (IDP) settings in Ethiopia remains very limited, despite the increasing number of displaced populations, particularly in regions such as Somali. Most existing studies in Ethiopia have focused on stable community or facility-based populations and have not adequately captured the unique and overlapping vulnerabilities faced by IDPs.

Unlike stable populations, pregnant women in displacement settings are exposed to intensified risks including severe food insecurity, limited dietary diversity, overcrowded and substandard living conditions, and poor access to essential maternal health services. In addition, increased exposure to infectious diseases and disrupted health service utilization may jointly influence both the prevalence and determinants of anaemia through interacting nutritional, infectious, and socioeconomic pathways.

Importantly, no sufficient evidence exists in Ethiopia examining how these combined determinants operate in IDP contexts to sustain the burden of anaemia despite ongoing nutritional and antenatal interventions. This represents a critical knowledge gap in understanding anemia in humanitarian and displacement settings. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of anaemia and identify associated factors among pregnant women living in the Koloji IDP camp in the Somali Region of Ethiopia, providing context-specific evidence to inform targeted, integrated, and effective public health interventions.

Methods and materials

Study area/setting and study period

The study was conducted in Koloji IDP camp, located in Fafan Zone of the Somali Regional State, eastern Ethiopia. Koloji is one of the largest conflict-induced internally displaced persons (IDP) camps in the region and is characterized by overcrowding and limited access to basic health and nutrition services. According to the International Organization for Migration (IOM) Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) Round 25 report, the camp hosted approximately 74,330 individuals living in Koloji-1 and Koloji-2 sites. The study was conducted from February 1 to 29, 2024.

Study design

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among pregnant women residing in Koloji IDP camp, Somali Regional State, eastern Ethiopia.

Population

Source population

The source population comprised all pregnant women living in Koloji IDP camp during the study period.

Study population

The study population included pregnant women selected from Koloji IDP camp using a systematic random sampling technique.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion

All pregnant women who had resided in Koloji IDP camp for at least six months during the study period were eligible for inclusion in the study.

Exclusion

Pregnant women who were seriously ill and unable to respond during the data collection period were excluded from the study.

Sampling procedure/technique

The study was conducted in two sub-camps, Koloji 1 and Koloji 2, within the Koloji Internally Displaced Persons (IDP) camp. To ensure adequate representation of pregnant women from the entire camp, both sub-camps were included in the study.

A sampling frame of pregnant women was prepared using registration records obtained from Health Extension Workers (HEWs) and Traditional Birth Attendants (TBAs) working in the camps. The lists were further verified and updated through household visits conducted by the data collectors to identify any unregistered pregnant women.

According to the updated records, there were 442 pregnant women in Koloji 1 and 4,153 pregnant women in Koloji 2, giving a total of 4,595 pregnant women in the study area. The total sample size of 390 participants was proportionally allocated to each sub-camp based on the number of pregnant women residing in each site. Accordingly, 37 participants were allocated to Koloji 1 and 353 participants to Koloji 2.

After proportional allocation, study participants were selected using a systematic random sampling technique in each sub-camp. The sampling interval (k) was calculated separately for each sub-camp by dividing the total number of pregnant women by the allocated sample size. The first participant was selected randomly using a lottery method from the first sampling interval, and subsequent participants were selected at regular intervals until the required sample size was achieved (Fig. 1).

Data collection methods

Data collection was conducted by teams of local enumerators in each setting through an interview in the local language of the pregnant women who are living in the refugee camp of Koloji.

Data collection instruments

A structured pretested interviewer administered questionnaire was used to obtain socio demographic information and present and past obstetric history in pregnant women. To obtain dietary habit, standard food frequency questionnaire adjusted for local food item was adapted and used to assess the usual intake of various food groups for the past one month with their respective consumption frequency. The questionnaire was developed in English and then translated into Somali language for simplicity then back-translated to English language for its consistency by two different language expert individuals who speak both English and Somali language fluently. Pretesting of the questionnaire was done on 5% of the sample size among pregnant women who will not be included in the study.

Data collectors

The data collectors were those who could speak Somali language and trained on data collection for an average of 5 days. The data was collected by 5 female and 3 male diploma midwifes and four BSc nursing holders as supervisors Interviews was conducted in the local language of the refugees.

Data collection procedures

Enumerators were trained for an average of 5 days. Teams was supervised during data collection by principal investigator. Data was collected through pretested and structured questionnaire by face-to face interviewing the pregnant women. The data collectors were explained the objective of the study to the pregnant women at the start of data collection. A sample of blood was also taken by pricking the fingertip after use of septic technique, then test strip was inserted into the device and the results were then recorded. Hemoglobin concentration was measured using the HemoCue Hb 301 system. Before field data collection, the device was checked and calibrated according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Quality control procedures included daily equipment inspection, regular cleaning of the optical chamber, use of standardized microcuvettes, and training of data collectors on proper blood sample collection and handling procedures. Pregnant women having Hb value below 11 g/dl were considered as anaemic. Pregnant women who had Hb value between 10 and 10.9 g/dl, 7–9.9 g/dl and < 7 g/dl were considered as mildly, moderately and severely anaemic, respectively.

Operational definitions and definitions of terms

Anaemia in pregnancy

It is defined as a hemoglobin concentration of less than 11.0 g/dL among pregnant women, irrespective of gestational age [21].

Public health importance of anaemia

It is considered a mild public health problem when the prevalence of anaemia is 5.0–19.9%, a moderate public health problem when the prevalence is 20.0–39.9%, and a severe public health problem when the prevalence is ≥ 40.0% [21].

Anaemia in pregnancy

Pregnant women with hemoglobin concentration < 11.0 g/dL were classified as anaemic [21].

Mild anaemia

Hemoglobin concentration of 10.0–10.9 g/dL [21].

Moderate anaemia

Hemoglobin concentration of 7.0–9.9 g/dL [21].

Severe anaemia

Hemoglobin concentration < 7.0 g/dL [21].

Eating habits

Different eating habits include eating animal foods, green leafy vegetables, taking fruit after meal, and drinking tea/coffee; this is measured by using food frequency questioner.

Gravidity

The number of times that a woman has been pregnant.

Primigravida

A woman who is pregnant for the first time.

Multigravida

A pregnant woman with one or more previous pregnancies.

Parity

The number of times that a woman has given birth to a fetus at a gestational age of 28 weeks or more, regardless of whether the child was born alive or was stillborn.

Folic acid

Water soluble vitamin B (B9) found mostly in leafy green vegetables like kale and spinach, orange juice, and enriched grains. Folic acid plays an important role in the production of red blood cells and helps fetal neural tube develop in the brain and spinal cord.

Singleton pregnancy

is a pregnancy in which the number of fetus inside the uterus is only one.

Data quality control

To assure data quality, 5% of estimated sample was pre-tested at outside of actual study sample before the data collection to see whether the questionnaires are simple and understandable. After every data collection the completeness and consistency of the questionnaire was checked. Data collectors training and daily supervision was made before and during the data collection period.

Methods of data analysis

EPI data version 4.6.6 was used to enter the data, and STATA version 17 was used for analysis. The percentages and frequencies of socio demographic characteristics and other pertinent variables in the study were described using descriptive analysis. Using both bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analysis, the associations of anemia among pregnant women were assessed. Both crude and adjusted odds ratios together with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals were computed to see the strength of association between the outcome and independent variables. All independent variables that were associated with the outcome variables (Anaemia) in bivariate analysis (with p value < 0.25) were included in the final multivariable logistic regression analysis. A p value of < 0.05 was considered to declare the result as statistically significant. The multi co linearity was checked with VIF and Hosmer–Lem shows test was performed for model fitness in the final model, and p value > 0.05 was considered a good fit. The result was presented in text, tables, charts and graphs based on the types of data.

The overall prevalence of anemia among the study participants was 42.11% (95% CI: 37.2%–47.1%). The mean hemoglobin (Hb) concentration of the participants was 10.75 ± 2.05 g/dL, with minimum and maximum values of 5.6 g/dL and 14.5 g/dL, respectively (Fig. 2).

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, variables including educational status of pregnant women, gestational age, history of blood loss, meal frequency per day, frequent consumption of coffee or tea immediately after meals, and iron and folic acid supplementation were significantly associated with anaemia at a p-value of < 0.05.

Accordingly, the odds of anaemia were nearly four times higher among illiterate pregnant women compared to literate pregnant women (AOR = 3.94, 95% CI: 2.10–7.37). Pregnant women in the third trimester were 4.55 times more likely to be anaemic compared to those in the first trimester (AOR = 4.55, 95% CI: 1.46–14.23).

The odds of anaemia were nearly eight times higher among pregnant women who had a history of blood loss compared to their counterparts (AOR = 7.84, 95% CI: 3.90–15.74). In this study, pregnant women who had a meal frequency of less than three times per day were two times more likely to be anaemic than those who had meal frequencies of greater than or equal to three times per day (AOR = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.16–3.56).

Similarly, the odds of anaemia were eight times higher among pregnant women who frequently consumed coffee or tea immediately after meals compared to those who frequently consumed milk after meals (AOR = 8.02, 95% CI: 3.75–17.16). Moreover, pregnant women who did not take iron and folic acid supplementation were nearly five times more likely to be anaemic compared to those who took iron and folic acid supplementation (AOR = 5.14, 95% CI: 2.78–9.50) (Table 6).

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of anaemia and associated factors among pregnant women living in the Koloji IDP camp in Somali Regional State, Eastern Ethiopia. The overall prevalence of anaemia was 42.11% (95% CI: 37.2–47.1%), which according to WHO classification indicates a severe public health problem (≥ 40%) [21]. This finding demonstrates that anaemia remains highly prevalent in humanitarian settings and continues to pose a major maternal health challenge.

In addition, the high exposure to infectious diseases in overcrowded and poorly sanitized camp environments may contribute to anaemia through chronic inflammation, parasitic infections, and impaired nutrient absorption. This supports the understanding that anaemia in displaced populations is driven by interacting nutritional, infectious, and health system determinants, rather than a single causal factor.

The predominance of moderate anemia (51.88%), followed by mild (32.5%) and severe anaemia (15.63%), further indicates a sustained and clinically significant burden in this population. Similar patterns have been reported in other humanitarian settings, where moderate anaemia is most common due to prolonged exposure to inadequate diets and limited healthcare access, while severe cases reflect cumulative nutritional and health deficits.

The prevalence observed in this study is comparable with findings from other fragile and resource-limited settings in Sub-Saharan Africa. Specifically, similar estimates have been reported among pregnant women in IDP camps in Mogadishu, Somalia (44.4%), Wolayita Sodo Town, Southern Ethiopia (39.94%) [8], and Madda Walabu University Goba Referral Hospital, Southeast Ethiopia (46.2%). These similarities suggest that anaemia remains highly prevalent in both humanitarian and low-resource settings where structural vulnerabilities persist.

However, the prevalence in the present study is higher than reports from Southeast Ethiopia (27.9%) [10], among South Sudanese refugees in Pugnido, Western Ethiopia (36.1%) [3], and among South Sudanese refugees in Uganda (36.3%). These differences may be explained by variations in contextual factors such as socioeconomic conditions, dietary diversity, cultural feeding practices, and access to antenatal care and nutrition-related interventions.

Importantly, the higher prevalence observed in the Koloji IDP camp may reflect more severe and sustained displacement-related vulnerabilities, including increased food insecurity, dependence on food assistance with limited micronutrient diversity, overcrowded living conditions, and reduced access to preventive and curative maternal health services. These factors likely interact to exacerbate both nutritional deficiencies and infection-related pathways of anaemia, thereby increasing its overall burden in this population.

Conversely, the prevalence in this study is lower than reports from Saharawi refugees in Algeria (76.5%), rural India (74.8%), Eastern Sudan (62.6%) [2], Niger Delta, Nigeria (66.7%), and the Gilgel Gibe Dam area in Ethiopia (53.4%) [7]. These differences may reflect variations in study settings, population characteristics, severity and duration of food insecurity, and coverage of nutrition and maternal health interventions. Methodological differences, such as sample size and gestational age distribution, may also contribute; for example, inclusion of women in later pregnancy stages may increase overall prevalence due to rising physiological iron demands across gestation.

In this study, maternal educational status was significantly associated with anaemia, where illiterate pregnant women had nearly fourfold higher odds of anaemia compared with literate women. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Northwestern Tigray, Addis Ababa [9], West Bengal, India, and Western Algeria. Education likely influences anaemia through multiple pathways, including improved knowledge of nutrition and antenatal care, better dietary practices, and increased utilization of health services. In contrast, low educational attainment is often associated with poorer socioeconomic status and reduced decision-making autonomy, which together increase vulnerability to inadequate dietary intake and iron deficiency.

Gestational age was also significantly associated with anemia, with women in the third trimester more than four times more likely to be anaemic compared to those in the first trimester. Similar findings have been reported in Mogadishu, Somalia, Wolayita Sodo [8], and Addis Ababa [6]. This pattern is biologically plausible, as iron requirements increase progressively during pregnancy due to expanded maternal blood volume, fetal growth, and placental development. Evidence shows that iron requirements rise from approximately 0.8 mg/day in early pregnancy to about 7.5 mg/day in late pregnancy [13], making inadequate intake and poor supplementation adherence more consequential in the third trimester.

A history of blood loss was another strong predictor of anaemia, with affected women nearly eight times more likely to be anaemic. This is consistent with findings from Adigrat (Berhe et al., 2019) and Eastern Ethiopia (Abdu et al., 2021). The association is explained by depletion of iron stores following blood loss, which increases physiological demand for erythropoiesis. Without adequate iron intake or supplementation, this imbalance can rapidly lead to reduced hemoglobin levels.

Low meal frequency was also significantly associated with anaemia, where women consuming fewer than three meals per day had about twice the odds of anaemia. This finding aligns with studies from Tigray, Mekelle, Hiwot Fana Hospital, and West Arsi [15]. Reduced meal frequency likely reflects inadequate overall nutrient intake and poor dietary diversity, which is particularly critical during pregnancy when energy and micronutrient demands are increased.

Frequent consumption of tea or coffee immediately after meals was significantly associated with anemia, consistent with findings from Somalia, Sudanese refugee settings in Ethiopia, West Arsi, and Egypt [1, 3, 15]. This may be explained by polyphenols (tannins) that inhibit non-heme iron absorption when consumed near mealtimes, which is particularly important in populations whose diets are largely plant-based and already low in bioavailable iron.

Finally, iron and folic acid (IFA) supplementation was strongly protective against anaemia. Women who did not take IFA supplements had nearly fivefold higher odds of anaemia compared with those who did. This is supported by studies from Northwestern Tigray and Sidama. IFA supplementation is essential during pregnancy because dietary intake alone is often insufficient to meet increased iron and folate requirements necessary for erythropoiesis and fetal development. However, in humanitarian settings such as IDP camps, adherence and consistent access may be limited, reducing program effectiveness.

Overall, the high prevalence of anaemia in this IDP setting reflects the combined effect of nutritional deficiency, physiological demands, and health service limitations. These findings underscore the need for strengthened and context-specific interventions, including improved antenatal care coverage, sustained IFA supplementation, nutrition education, and targeted strategies to improve dietary diversity and feeding practices in displaced populations.

Strength of the study

This study provides evidence on the prevalence and associated factors of anaemia among pregnant women in a highly vulnerable and understudied population living in an internally displaced persons (IDP) camp. Given the scarcity of data from humanitarian and displacement settings in Ethiopia, the findings contribute important context-specific evidence that may inform targeted maternal nutrition and health interventions in similar settings.

Limitation of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings.

First, due to resource constraints, important laboratory investigations such as malaria parasitemia testing, HIV screening, and stool examination for intestinal parasites were not conducted. Since malaria, HIV infection, and intestinal parasitic infestations are well-established contributors to anemia in pregnancy, the inability to assess these conditions limits the ability to fully explore the underlying causes of anemia. Therefore, the observed anaemia cannot be attributed exclusively to nutritional or dietary factors.

Second, the cross-sectional study design limits the ability to establish temporal or causal relationships between anaemia and its associated factors. As a result, the associations identified should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal.

Third, self-reported dietary and behavioral variables, including meal frequency and iron–folic acid (IFA) supplementation adherence, may be subject to social desirability bias. Participants may have over-reported positive health behaviors due to perceived expectations from data collectors.

Finally, although efforts were made to ensure data quality through training of data collectors and standardized procedures, recall bias may still have affected the accuracy of self-reported information.

Conclusion and recommendation

Conclusion

In this study, more than four in ten pregnant women were found to be anaemic. According to the World Health Organization classification, the magnitude of anaemia observed in this study represents a severe public health problem. Moderate anaemia was the most predominant type of anaemia among pregnant women. Educational status of pregnant women, gestational age, history of blood loss, meal frequency per day, frequent consumption of coffee or tea immediately after meals, and iron and folic acid supplementation were significantly associated with anaemia among pregnant women.

Recommendation

Based on the findings of the current study, the following recommendations are forwarded to the relevant stakeholders:
