Clinical Evaluation of Cardiac Failure in Post-Myocardial Infarction Patients
Authors: Visarg P. Patel, Sahil Kalsariya, Ujval R. Patel, Kaushik G. Khatrani, Punit Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:To assess the complications of myocardial infarction (MI) and evaluate the precautionary and preventive measures in patients developing cardiac failure post-MI.Materials and Methods:This 12-month prospective observational research at a tertiary care facility included 65 post-MI patients. A thorough clinical evaluation, Electrocardiogram (ECG), and two-dimensional echocardiogram (2D ECHO) were performed on patients. Study variables included functional cardiac anomalies, chamber enlargement, Regional wall motion abnormality (RWMA), New York heart association (NYHA) classification, and comorbidities.Results:In 59% of 2D ECHO patients, segmental dysfunction was the most frequent functional abnormality. The main chamber findings were left ventricular hypertrophy (32%) and left atrial enlargement (29%). Clinically, 78% had paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND)/orthopnea and 67% were NYHA Class III or IV. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (72%) and diabetes (60%).Conclusion:Segmental myocardial dysfunction, structural chamber anomalies, and particular ECG patterns substantially predict post-MI cardiac failure. This high-risk group needs early echocardiography, strong risk factor control, and timely heart failure treatment to decrease progression and enhance outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Despite advancements in interventional cardiology and medication, myocardial infarction (MI), or heart attack, is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally. Heart failure (HF) after MI severely reduces survivors’ quality of life and long-term outcomes.[1] Post-infarction heart failure is projected to increase with population aging and cardiovascular risk factors as diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.[2]

Even after reperfusion, significant myocardial injury, unfavorable ventricular remodeling, and chronic ischemia cause cardiac failure.[3] Many investigations have linked neurohormonal activation, left ventricular dysfunction, and myocardial fibrosis to HF after MI.[45] Improved prognosis requires early sequelae diagnosis and preventive and precautionary measures.

Beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors have been widely examined and recommended to reduce post-MI HF.[67] Cardiac rehabilitation, lifestyle modification, and patient education are becoming key nonpharmacological heart failure prevention techniques.[8]

Recent studies have shown that biomarkers including NT-proBNP, galectin-3, and high-sensitivity troponins can identify MI patients at high risk of HF.[9] Myocardial viability and scar burden may now be assessed more precisely with cardiac MRI, improving risk classification.[10]

This study examines MI consequences, particularly cardiac failure, and the efficacy of post-MI precautions.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

The present 12-month prospective observational study at an Indian tertiary care hospital assessed the incidence of cardiac failure after MI and the effectiveness of preventive strategies. A total of 65 patients diagnosed with acute MI and admitted for primary management were included in the study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee.

RESULTS

In post-MI cardiac failure patients, segmental dysfunction was most common on 2D ECHO, with left ventricular hypertrophy and left atrial enlargement being prevalent, and hypokinesia was the most frequent RWMA abnormality [Table 1]. Anterior-septal (AS) infarctions are the most common at 31%, followed by inferior wall (Inf) at 20%, extensive anterior infarction (Ext–A) at 17%, anterior infarction (AI) at 14%, and lateral (Lat), non-Q wave (NQ), and right ventricular (RV) infarctions at 6% each. Table 2 shows that 78% of study patients exhibited paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND) and orthopnea. 70% had high jugular venous pressure or edema, and 66% had cardiomegaly on examination or imaging. Seventy-two percentage of patients have hypertension (HTN), 60% have diabetes mellitus (DM), and 55% have obesity, thyroid disorders, or dyslipidemia, emphasizing the need for aggressive risk factor management in post-MI care.

DISCUSSION

After MI, cardiac failure is one of the most feared and prevalent consequences, causing extended morbidity and high mortality globally. Segmental dysfunction (SD) was found in 59% of 2D ECHO patients, highlighting the importance of localized myocardial injury in post-MI heart failure. Currently, left ventricular remodeling and localized wall motion anomalies are key factors in impaired systolic function after infarction.[11] Diastolic and concentric dysfunctions were also evident in 20% and 10%, confirming that myocardial damage impairs both phases of ventricular performance.

Left ventricular hypertrophy (32%) and isolated left atrial enlargement (29%) were the most prevalent chamber enlargement patterns. Studies imply that structural remodeling is a compensatory strategy that might cause hemodynamic problems if uncontrolled.[12] Less common but clinically substantial right atrial and ventricular hypertrophy indicated right-sided strain in certain patients. A powerful prognostic predictor in postinfarct patients, hypokinesia was the most common anomaly in RWMA (65%).[13]

The AS wall (31%) and inferior wall (20%) dominated electrocardiographic alterations in this investigation. Prior studies have demonstrated that anterior MI causes significant myocardial necrosis and pump failure, which explains their increased HF burden.[121314] PND, orthopnea, jugular venous pressure (JVP) elevation, and cardiomegaly were consistent with worldwide heart failure guidelines, with almost two-thirds of patients having at least two main symptoms.

In our group, 67% of patients were NYHA Class III or IV, indicating the considerable functional limitation and burden of HF. Hypertension (72%) and diabetes mellitus (60%) were prevalent comorbidities, supporting the significance of these illnesses in endothelial dysfunction, vascular stiffness, and unfavorable ventricular remodeling.[15] Their prevalence highlights the need for interdisciplinary post-MI therapy that prioritizes cardiac recovery and intensive systemic risk factor management.

CONCLUSION

This study confirms that damage to the heart, structural and functional alterations on imaging, and underlying comorbidities all contribute to HF after MI. Echocardiography, symptom classification, and customized therapy of risk factors like hypertension and diabetes can avoid HF progression in post-MI patients.
