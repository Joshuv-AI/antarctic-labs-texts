Whole genome dataset of bacterial strains isolated from urine analysed by Oxford Nanopore sequencing technologies in Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso
Authors: Pamane Djagbaré, Ezechiel Bionimian Tibiri, Wendyam Marie Christelle Nadembèga, Pakyendou Estel Name, Lassina Traoré, Emmanuel Sampo, Moussa Ouédraogo, Fidèle Tiendrebéogo, Jacques Simporé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA collection of 21 bacterial isolates recovered from urine specimens collected between September 2018 and February 2019 at Saint-Camille and SCHIPHRA hospitals in Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso, is presented. Isolates were identified using the API 20E system and tested for antibiotic susceptibility according to CASFM 2018 guidelines. Genomic DNA was extracted, barcoded with the SQK-RBK114.96 Rapid Barcoding Kit, and sequenced on an Oxford Nanopore MinION Mk1C using an R10.4.1 flowcell. Basecalling was performed with Dorado, and reads were quality filtered prior to de novo assembly with Flye, followed by polishing with Racon and Medaka. Assemblies were evaluated with QUAST, taxonomically assigned with Kraken2, and annotated with Bakta. The dataset comprises 21 barcode-specific raw read sets (1.5 million reads; 4.9 Gb), polished genome assemblies, per-isolate metadata including collection site, collection date, and antibiotic susceptibility measurements, and quality assessment outputs including read statistics, assembly metrics, and functional annotation completeness estimates. All data are available under NCBI BioProject PRJNA1307828. These data provide a useful resource for long-read bacterial genome assembly benchmarking, comparative genomics, and analyses integrating phenotypic and genomic antimicrobial resistance information.Keywords: Genome sequencing, Bioinformatics, Microbiology, Antimicrobial resistance, MinION

Background

Bacterial infections continue to represent a significant challenge to public health, particularly in settings where the capacity for microbiological surveillance and the characterisation of resistance mechanisms is constrained [[1], [2], [3]]. It is mentioned that unsuccessful treatment of bacterial infections associated with antibiotic resistance claims at least 700,000 every year globally and is projected to be associated with 10 million deaths per year by 2050 [4]⁠ In Burkina Faso, urinary tract and suppurative infections are prevalent and are frequently attributable to multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae or non-fermenting bacilli. [5]. The establishment of a local genomic resource documenting these pathogens was achieved through the sequencing of clinical isolates obtained from urine samples collected at Saint Camille Hospital and SCHIPHRA Hospital (Ouagadougou).

The generation of genomic data was accomplished through the utilisation of Oxford Nanopore MinION long-read sequencing, which was followed by a standard assembly workflow (Flye, Racon, Medaka) and functional annotation with Bakta. For each isolate, the dataset comprises raw reads, assembled genomes, quality control reports and key clinical metadata (sample type, healthcare facility, collection date and antibiotic susceptibility profile). The present data article delineates the structure of the collection and the experimental protocol, thus providing a reference framework for investigations into bacterial resistance in West Africa.

Data Description

This dataset includes raw FASTQ sequencing data and de novo assembled whole-genome sequences (contigs in FASTA format) for clinical bacterial isolates obtained from urine samples, as well as associated metadata (Table 1). These isolates were collected between September 2018 and February 2019 from Saint Camille and SCHIPHRA hospitals in Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso. They were then stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Both the raw reads and the corresponding assembled genomes have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession PRJNA1307828 (Table 1). This dataset provides valuable genomic resources for studying the genetic diversity, antimicrobial resistance and epidemiology of circulating clinical bacteria in Burkina Faso.

A total of 1.3 million Nanopore reads were generated across all barcodes, with read counts ranging from 11,061 to 170,995. The mean read length varied from 4141 bp to 8024 bp, with an average of around 6500 bp overall. Basecalling quality scores were consistent across samples, with mean Q-scores above 24 indicating reliable Nanopore sequencing performance (Table 2, Fig 2).

Genome assemblies produced one or a few large contigs per isolate, with total genome sizes ranging from ∼2.3 to ∼5.2 Mb, which is consistent with the expected size of bacterial genomes (Fig 1& 2). The longest contigs were up to 5.17 Mb in length, and assembly depths ranged from 12 × to 225 ×, ensuring sufficient coverage for accurate consensus generation. Read length N50 values ranged from 5014 bp to 13,588 bp, confirming the predominance of long reads that are well suited to high-quality assembly (Table 2, Fig 1).

Overall, the sequencing effort produced >4.3 billion bases, providing deep coverage (Fig 2) and robust assemblies that are suitable for downstream comparative genomic and phylogenetic analyses. These assemblies serve as reference-quality genomes for the clinical bacterial isolates sequenced in this study.

All sequencing reads and assemblies have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject PRJNA1307828. Collectively, these files furnish a consistent resource for the assessment of assembly pipelines, the examination of genome structure, and the correlation of genotypic characteristics to observed susceptibility profiles in clinically pertinent Escherichia coli, Bacillus subtilis, Enterobacter hormaechei, and associated pathogens from Burkina Faso and West Africa.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Sample collection, bacterial culture and bacterial isolation

The study was conducted on 21 bacterial strains collected between September 2018 and February 2019 in biomedical laboratories of Saint Camille and SCHIPHRA hospitals in Ouagadougou. The 21 strains included in this dataset were selected from an initial collection of isolates based on DNA quality and suitability for sequencing. These isolates correspond to non-duplicate bacteria isolates originating from urine cultures of outpatients undergoing routine diagnostic testing. Samples were obtained from two hospitals (Saint Camille and SCHIPHRA). The samples were inoculated on a chromogenic medium, Chromatic Detection from Liofilchem, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to promote bacterial growth. Strain identification was confirmed using the API 20E gallery (Biomérieux). An antibiogram was then performed according to the CASFM 2018 guidelines (Table 1). The susceptibility of the strains to antibiotics was determined using the disk diffusion method (Kirby-Bauer), allowing the measurement of inhibition diameters and establishing the resistance profile to commonly used antibiotics.

For E. coli, Bacillus, and Enterobacter, the tested antibiotics were:

β-lactams: Amoxicillin (AML, 30 µg), Amoxicillin + Clavulanic acid (AUG, 20µg/10 µg), and Imipenem (IMI, 10 µg), Third-generation cephalosporins (C3G): Ceftriaxone (CRO, 30 µg), Cefixime (CFM, 5 µg), Ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg), Sulfonamides: Trimethoprim + Sulfamethoxazole (SXT, 1.25µg/23.75 µg), Aminoglycosides: Amikacin (AK, 30 µg), Gentamicin (GN, 10 µg), Kanamycin (K, 30 µg), Tobramycin (TOB, 10 µg), Neomycin (N, 30 µg), and Netilmicin (NET, 10 µg), Other: Fosfomycin (200 µg).

DNA extraction and whole genome sequencing

Bacterial strains were isolated from urine specimens collected from outpatients. After collection, each clinical specimen was inoculated onto Luria-Bertani (LB) agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Single colonies were then grown in LB broth until they reached an optical density at 600 nm (OD_600) of 0.5, as measured by spectrophotometry.

Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed by spectrophotometry (NanoDrop™ 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and fluorometry (Qubit™ 4, Thermo Fisher Scientific) before library preparation.

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on an Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) platform using the SQK-RBK114.96 Rapid Barcoding Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK). Libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer's recommendations and loaded onto an R10.4.1 flow cell. Sequencing was performed until adequate coverage was achieved (typically >30 × per strain). Base calling of the raw signal (POD5 files) to nucleotide sequences (FASTQ) was performed using Dorado (v 0.9.1) (Oxford Nanopore Technologies).

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw reads were further processed to remove low-quality and contaminating sequences. This involved aligning the reads using Minimap2 (v 2.28) [7] ⁠using the recommended parameters for Nanopore long reads. De novo assembly was then performed using Flye (v 2.95) [8], which is designed to handle long and potentially error-prone reads.

Subsequent polishing steps were implemented to correct residual errors in the assemblies:

-Medaka (v 2.0.1) (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) was first used to remove base-level errors specific to nanopore reads.

Medaka (v 2.0.1) (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) was first used to remove base-level errors specific to nanopore reads.

-Racon (v 1.4.20) [3] was then used to further refine the consensus sequence through iterative alignment and polishing rounds.

Racon (v 1.4.20) [3] was then used to further refine the consensus sequence through iterative alignment and polishing rounds.

The quality and integrity of the final assemblies were assessed using QUAST (v 5.2.0) [6], which generated metrics such as total assembly size, number of contigs, and completeness estimates. Kraken2 (v2.0.8) was used as a rapid, initial taxonomic classifier to determine the species with the highest level of confidence [9], using updated bacterial databases to ensure accurate classification.

The assembled genomes of the 21 bacterial isolates were annotated in terms of both structure and function using Bakta v1.12.0 with the full v6.0 database. Bakta was chosen because it rapidly and comprehensively annotates bacterial genomes by detecting coding and non-coding genomic elements. Bakta predicted coding sequences (CDSs) and annotated major RNA features for each genome assembly, including tRNAs, rRNAs, ncRNAs, and regulatory non-coding RNA regions. The pipeline also screened for additional genomic features, such as CRISPR regions and putative replication- and mobility-related loci (including oriC and oriT), when present. Functional assignments were inferred using Bakta's curated reference database and built-in annotation workflow. The annotation outputs were subsequently used for genome description, comparative interpretation and circular genome visualization (Fig 3). All annotations were generated using Bakta v1.12.0 and database v6.0 (full), with the standard parameters recommended by the developers being followed.

All bioinformatic analyses were performed in the BurkinaBioinfo (BBi) platform [10], using default parameters recommended by the tool’s developers, unless otherwise specified.
