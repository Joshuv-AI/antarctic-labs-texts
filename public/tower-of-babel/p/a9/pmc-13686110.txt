NKX2‐1 Downstream Regulatory Structural Variants Explain a Substantial Proportion of Molecular Diagnoses in Patients With Benign Hereditary Chorea

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground NKX2‐1–related disorders (NKX2‐1‐RDs) classically present with a triad of neurological, endocrine, and pulmonary manifestations, including benign hereditary chorea. However, in a fraction of patients, NKX2‐1 coding variants are not detected, and variants outside the NKX2‐1 locus have been reported.ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to evaluate the clinical spectrum and frequency of regulatory variants in NKX2‐1‐RDs.MethodsEight families carrying structural variants near NKX2‐1 without disruption of the coding sequence were recruited, and clinical data were systematically collected. Regulatory activity was assessed using EpiMap data. Frequency and diagnostic yield were evaluated in a cohort of 26 individuals with molecularly confirmed NKX2‐1‐RD.ResultsEight families comprising 13 affected individuals were identified: five with downstream deletions and three with complex structural rearrangements. Neurological manifestations were universal, including chorea, myoclonus, and ataxia, while cognitive or behavioral abnormalities occurred in a subset. Individuals with deletions rarely presented extraneurological features, whereas all carriers of complex structural variants showed the complete triad. All deletions and breakpoints were located downstream of NKX2‐1. The core shared deleted region showed open chromatin and H3K27ac peaks in fetal brain, lung, and thyroid tissues, suggesting regulatory activity. Regulatory variants accounted for 27% of NKX2‐1‐RD diagnoses in our institutions. Exome sequencing combined with copy‐number variant analysis captured 92% of diagnoses when MBIP was evaluated as a target for downstream variants.ConclusionsDownstream regulatory variants are a substantial cause of NKX2‐1‐RDs. Diagnostic strategies should include this regulatory region and systematic structural variant detection, particularly when coding variants have been excluded. © 2026 The Author(s). The NKX2‐1 gene, previously known as thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF‐1), encodes a homeobox‐containing transcription factor (TF). During embryogenesis, it regulates morphogenesis of the ventral forebrain, particularly the basal ganglia and hypothalamus, as well as the differentiation of lung epithelial cells and thyroid follicular cell development. 1 , 2 , 3 In adult stages, it also regulates the expression of tissue‐specific genes, such as surfactant proteins in the lung and thyroid hormone genes, and contributes to neurological homeostasis. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Moreover, NKX2‐1 has been described as a classical lineage‐survival oncogene, acting as a context‐dependent tumor suppressor or oncogene. 9

Pathogenic variants in NKX2‐1 lead to a spectrum of disorders referred to as NKX2‐1‐related disorders (NKX2‐1‐RDs) (OMIM 610978). These rare conditions typically present with a triad of neurological, pulmonary, and thyroid manifestations, including benign hereditary chorea (BHC; OMIM 118700), hypothyroidism, respiratory distress, and interstitial lung disease. 10 However, only half of the NKX2‐1 patients present with involvement of all three organ systems, with the neurological system affected in nearly all cases. 11 , 12 , 13 Moreover, variability in disease onset, progression, and severity has been reported. 13 , 14 , 15 NKX2‐1‐RDs have also been associated with an increased risk of adult‐onset cancers, such as large‐cell lung carcinoma, papillary thyroid carcinoma, and glioblastoma multiforme. 16

From a genetic perspective, most individuals carry small coding variants, including frameshift, nonsense, splicing, and missense variants. 10 , 13 , 17 In addition, copy‐number variants (CNVs) represent an important disease mechanism and explain approximately 15% of the cases. 10 , 13 , 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 However, in some patients with a typical BHC phenotype, mutations in NKX2‐1 coding sequence fail to be detected, which historically has suggested alternative causative genes or mechanisms. 20 , 21 , 22 Since the first reports in 2012, deletions located downstream of NKX2‐1 without affecting the coding sequence have been increasingly reported in patients with BHC, including a highly conserved overlapping region that also comprises the MBIP gene. 13 , 19 , 23 , 24 , 26 , 27 Considering that no disease‐causing variants have been reported in MBIP, the existence of downstream regulatory elements influencing NKX2‐1 expression has been hypothesized. Furthermore, a recent report described a balanced rearrangement involving the same region, suggesting that additional classes of structural variants (SVs) may also perturb NKX2‐1 expression through regulatory mechanisms. 28 Although these regulatory variants have been described in several families, most are isolated observations, and therefore their overall relevance to the diagnosis of NKX2‐1‐RDs remains largely unknown.

This study aims to assess the diagnostic relevance of regulatory variants in NKX2‐1‐RDs. For this purpose, we applied a wide range of genetic approaches to detect CNVs and SVs in two cohorts from Spain and the Netherlands. Remarkably, 27% of all NKX2‐1 diagnoses involved regulatory variants located downstream of the coding region. We further present a detailed phenotypic characterization of 13 individuals from eight families carrying novel noncoding regulatory NKX2‐1 variants. These results underscore the importance of incorporating appropriate genetic approaches to prevent misdiagnoses when clinical suspicion is compelling.

Subjects and Methods

Study Cohort

The study cohort included families recruited from two centers: Radboud University Medical Center (Radboudumc) and Vall d'Hebron University Hospital (HUVH), using two different diagnostic approaches. At the Radboudumc, all exome sequencing (ES) and short‐read genome sequencing (sr‐GS) data from an initial cohort of 5,161 patients for whom a movement disorder panel had been requested were reanalyzed for CNVs or SVs within ±1 Mb of NKX2‐1. This strategy allowed for the identification of four families (families 1, 2, 4 and 5). At the HUVH, experts in pediatric movement disorders initially selected nine families with a phenotype of BHC who had tested negative for coding variants in NKX2‐1. A search for SVs was performed using ES data, array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH), karyotype, or optical genome mapping (OGM). This second strategy allowed for the identification of four additional families (families 3, 6, 7, and 8). Family 7 was first evaluated at Virgen del Rocio Hospital in Seville before referral to HUVH. Overall, we identified eight unrelated families carrying SVs located near NKX2‐1, without disrupting its coding sequence. In addition, families with confirmed pathogenic variants in the coding sequence of NKX2‐1 from both centers were anonymously included for comparative analysis.

This study was approved by the institutional review board in Nijmegen “Commissie Mensgebonden Onderzoek Regio Arnhem‐Nijmegen,” under number 2011/188, and in Vall d'Hebron University Hospital “Clinical Research Ethics Committee,” under number PR(AMI)466/2020. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their caregivers.

Genetic Testing Methods

SVs were identified using various genetic testing methods, including karyotyping, aCGH, OGM, and ES and sr‐GS. Specific details about the methodologies are described in the Supporting Information Methods.

In Silico Enhancer Analysis

To examine regulatory elements and enhancer activity at the downstream region of NKX2‐1, we evaluated epigenetic profiles using publicly available datasets from the EpiMap repository. 29 EpiMap integrates experimental data from large‐scale projects such as ENCODE and Roadmap Epigenomics, providing uniformly processed and imputed chromatin state annotations across multiple tissues and cell types. Samples were selected from tissues expressing NKX2‐1 (lung, thyroid, and embryonic brain) and from tissues not expected to express NKX2‐1 (heart, liver, and pancreas) as negative controls (Table S1). Samples with available experimental DNase sequencing (DNase‐seq) and H3K27ac data were prioritized. For embryonic brain, we selected samples <100 days postconception, when NKX2‐1 expression is expected to be the highest. In these samples, only experimental DNase‐seq data were available; therefore, imputed H3K27ac signals were used for enhancer assessment.

Estimation of Diagnostic Yield Across Genetic Testing Strategies

Using data from patients with variants affecting the coding sequence, as well as the downstream region of NKX2‐1 (with exclusion of family 7 because it was referred from an external center), the predicted diagnostic yields for different genetic testing strategies were assessed based on the observed variant types and their genomic location. Scenarios included karyotyping, aCGH, OGM, gene panels, ES, sr‐GS, and long‐read genome sequencing (lr‐GS) (assumptions are detailed in the Supporting Information Methods). First, for each technology, we determined whether a given variant type would be detectable, classifying outcomes as “yes,” “no,” or “case‐dependent.” Diagnostic yield for each strategy was then calculated as the proportion of cases in which the method would be expected to detect the causal variant in the cohort.

Results

Clinical Characterization of NKX2‐1‐Related Cohort

Data from eight families were collected, carrying either deletions (families 1–5) or complex balanced structural rearrangements (families 6–8) (Fig. 1A). Within these eight families, clinical data from 13 genetically confirmed patients were assessed, including eight females and five males, with a mean age of 22.7 years (range 6–53 years) at last evaluation.

Neurological manifestations were present in all individuals and represented the core phenotype (Tables 1 and S2). Disease onset occurred within the first 4 years of life (mean 1.2 years), typically presenting with motor delay (100%) and evolving into a mixed hyperkinetic movement disorder characterized predominantly by chorea (100%), followed by dystonia and ataxia (77%) and hypotonia (69.2%) (see Supporting Information Notes, Clinical Summaries). Myoclonus was documented in five individuals (38.5%), typically action‐induced and affecting the upper limbs. In several families (families 1–5), symptoms evolved dynamically during early childhood, progressing from hypotonia or mild ataxia to more recognizable choreic or dystonic manifestations. Although transient exacerbations triggered by stressors such as fever or hormonal changes were reported (families 3–5), the overall clinical course was generally stable or mildly improving over time, without evidence of neurodegeneration.

Cognitive outcomes were overall favorable, with normal cognition in most individuals (84.6%) and only mild developmental, learning, or behavioral difficulties observed in a subset of patients. Pharmacological response was variable and generally limited, although methylphenidate provided symptomatic benefit in two individuals with prominent chorea, whereas levodopa and/or tetrabenazine were generally ineffective. One patient underwent deep brain stimulation, showing partial clinical improvement in the Burke–Fahn‐Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) score.

Extraneurological manifestations were relatively mild in this cohort. Interestingly, they were predominantly reported in individuals carrying structural rearrangements, with the complete phenotypic triad appearing only in these patients (Table S2). Hypothyroidism requiring levothyroxine was the only thyroid alteration, documented in four individuals (31%). Pulmonary involvement was identified in five patients (38.5%), including neonatal respiratory distress, recurrent respiratory infections during childhood, chronic interstitial pneumopathy, with bronchospasm and dyspnea, and bilateral lung adenocarcinoma in one case.

Genetic Analysis of NKX2‐1‐Related Cohort

All eight identified variants within the cohort disrupted the downstream region of NKX2‐1, without altering the copy number or disrupting the coding sequence of NKX2‐1 (Table 2, Fig. 1B). Although we also assessed the upstream region, no variants were detected in this region in individuals with matching clinical features. All variants cosegregated with the phenotype and followed a de novo or dominant mode of inheritance (see the pedigree diagrams in Fig. 1A).

Most individuals underwent one or more genetic testing approaches before a definitive diagnosis was achieved (Table 2). Diagnoses initially made from ES (families 1–3) were achieved only after CNV analysis including MBIP as a target, with sr‐GS subsequently used to resolve the breakpoints. Furthermore, in two families (Families 4–5), the variant was first identified by sr‐GS but was retrospectively also detected in ES data applying the same CNV strategy. All variants detected using the different informative methods are illustrated in Figures S1–S8.

Families 1 to 5 carried variants resulting in loss of genetic material at overlapping loci, affecting regions ranging from 101 kb up to 894 kb. Four families carried simple deletions, whereas the variant in family 4 was more complex, comprising an inversion of ~20 kb flanked by two deletions totaling ~100 kb. Taken together, the affected regions converged on a minimal shared deletion region of 101 kb (hg38, chr14:36246270–36347313). When incorporating cases reported in the literature, this interval was further narrowed to a 33‐kb shared region (GRCh38, chr14:36262984–36295741) (Fig. 1B). 13 , 19 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 This region was located 221 kb downstream of NKX2‐1. To evaluate intolerance to copy‐number changes, we queried the gnomAD CNV and SV v4.1.0 databases and filtered them for variants ≥10 kb, but we did not find fully overlapping deletions (Fig. S9).

Families 6 to 8 carried structural rearrangements with breakpoints located downstream of NKX2‐1 but without genetic loss at this region. The variant in family 6 was a complex chromosomal rearrangement involving four different chromosomes (7, 13, 14, and 22) and seven breakpoints, whereas family 7 carried a balanced translocation between chromosomes 5 and 14. Other breakpoints in these families may contribute to the clinical phenotype. In family 8, a duplicated genomic segment of ~494 kb was inserted in inverted orientation downstream of NKX2‐1 (Table S3).

In Silico Study of NKX2‐1 Super‐Enhancer Region

A super‐enhancer region located around 250 kb downstream of NKX2‐1 was recently reported in lung adenocarcinoma samples (GRCh38, chr14:36207754–36272803) containing 10 enhancers with varying levels of activity. 9 All deletions directly affected the super‐enhancer, removing in all cases at least enhancers 9 and 10 (Fig. 2). Breakpoints of SVs were located between NKX2‐1 and the super‐enhancer region, thereby disrupting its interaction with the promoter. Using data from the EpiMap repository, we assessed possible enhancer activity in other tissues relevant for NKX2‐1 expression. 29 Besides detectable open chromatin signals in lung around the previously defined enhancers, relevant peaks were also observed in thyroid and embryonic brain tissues at highly conserved sites, some of which overlapped with the defined lung enhancers. H3K27ac signals colocalized with these peaks, supporting their role as active enhancers. In contrast, tissues selected as negative controls showed minimal accessibility and nearly absent H3K27ac signals, consistent with the lack of NKX2‐1 expression in this tissue. The profile was comparable across all examined samples, and a representative sample from each tissue is shown in Figure 2.

Variant Types and Predicted Diagnostic Yield in NKX2‐1‐RDs

To estimate the frequency of regulatory variants in NKX2‐1‐RDs, we identified families with confirmed coding variants at the two centers. Detailed clinical information is not shown for these families. Across both centers, 19 families were identified carrying 18 distinct variants affecting the NKX2‐1 coding sequence, including 16 families with SNVs (84%) and three with CNVs (16%) (Table S4). When including the seven families with downstream regulatory variants (family 7 excluded; see Subjects and Methods), these variants accounted for 26.9% (7/26) of cases in our cohort, with similar proportions between centers (Radboudumc: 4/16; HUVH: 3/10; P > 0.99).

To assess the potential applicability of different genetic testing approaches, we calculated the predicted diagnostic yield of each method for the variants identified in this cohort based on the analytical characteristics of each technique (Tables 3 and S4). This prediction indicated that both sr‐GS and lr‐GS would have identified all variants, although detection of some complex variants could be more challenging with sr‐GS. ES would have been informative in 92.2% of cases, provided that CNV analysis was included, with NKX2‐1 coding CNVs accounting for 11.5% and MBIP‐related CNVs for 19.2%, highlighting the added diagnostic value of including MBIP as a target. Gene panels would successfully detect variants only in the coding region, including both SNV/indels (61.5%) and CNVs affecting a large proportion of the NKX2‐1 coding region (7.7%). Regarding approaches designed to detect genomic alterations beyond SNVs and indels, OGM would have been effective for all CNVs and SVs (38.4%). aCGH would have identified CNVs that overlap with the probe design, including both the coding sequence and the downstream region (23%). Finally, karyotyping would have enabled detection of one variant (3.8%), and Multiplex Ligation‐dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA) would have detected two variants (7.7%).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that SVs affecting the downstream region of NKX2‐1, including deletions and structural rearrangements, represent a recurrent and common molecular mechanism underlying NKX2‐1‐RD. Despite their location outside of NKX2‐1, these variants produce a comparable phenotype with that observed in individuals with coding variants. Accordingly, diagnostic strategies focusing only on the coding region may miss a substantial fraction of pathogenic variants, underscoring the need for broader genetic testing strategies when NKX2‐1‐RDs are suspected.

In the combined cohort of 26 families, coding variants accounted for 73% of NKX2‐1 diagnoses, with the proportion of SNVs and CNVs consistent with previous reports. 10 , 15 , 17 , 19 In contrast, variants in the downstream regulatory region accounted for 27% of diagnoses, exceeding the 4% to 6% reported in previous cohorts. 13 , 19 However, these previous results may be underestimated, because the downstream region was not systematically captured or analyzed in earlier genetic screening approaches. All deletions reported here overlapped with previously reported deletions (Fig. 1B), defining a shared critical region that encompasses a recently described NKX2‐1 super‐enhancer in lung adenocarcinoma (Fig. 2). 9 , 13 , 19 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Our in silico analysis suggests that this regulatory region may also be active in additional tissues expressing NKX2‐1, ie, brain and thyroid. However, until experimental validation, the biological relevance of this regulatory region in these tissues remains uncertain, particularly for the embryonic brain, where only imputed H3K27ac signals were available. Structural rearrangements either disrupted the enhancer region directly or separated it from the NKX2‐1 promoter, supporting a disease mechanism mediated by altered enhancer‐promoter regulation and reduced NKX2‐1 expression. 9 , 30 NKX2‐1 encodes a TF, a family of genes in which enhancer‐disrupting variants have been found to be enriched, including reported examples in SHOX and GATA2. 31 , 32 , 33 The requirement for precise spatiotemporal regulation of these genes may explain this enrichment, because even modest expression changes may broadly disrupt downstream gene expression networks that have essential roles during development. 34 , 35

The clinical spectrum of NKX2‐1‐RD is highly heterogeneous in terms of age at onset, severity, and disease course. 13 , 14 , 36 Our cohort of individuals carrying regulatory variants is representative for this typical clinical variability, showing universal neurological involvement, while additional pulmonary manifestations and hypothyroidism were present in 38.5% and 31%, respectively. Because patient inclusion was driven by the presence of movement disorders, a certain bias could have been introduced, potentially leading to an underrepresentation of extraneurological features. Consequently, the conclusions derived from this cohort may not be fully generalizable to predominantly endocrine or pulmonary populations. Nevertheless, these findings are consistent with previous observations showing that a substantial proportion of individuals present with partial phenotypes. 13 , 19 , 27 Interestingly, most individuals (8/9) carrying deletions presented exclusively with neurological manifestations. This pattern is consistent with previously reported cases, in which downstream deletions were associated with less frequent pulmonary and thyroid involvement compared with coding variants (Table S2). In contrast, when looking specifically at our four patients with structural rearrangements, we found that 100% of them exhibited the clinical triad. To our knowledge, only one comparable case involving large structural rearrangements has been previously described in the literature. 28 This case presented with neurological and thyroid dysfunction, although meaningful comparison with our cohort remains difficult because of the limited number of reported cases. Such variants may additionally perturb topologically associating domain (TAD) boundaries, potentially leading to more pronounced alterations in NKX2‐1 expression. Phenotypic severity may also be influenced by disruption of additional genes, as illustrated by the complex translocation in family 6, which affected multiple genes that may contribute to the clinical presentation (Table S3).

In our cohort, all individuals exhibited a heterogeneous, mixed hyperkinetic movement disorder in which chorea was universal. 13 , 14 Symptoms typically began in infancy with motor delay, hypotonia, and ataxia, later evolving into more prominent choreiform and dystonic movements during childhood—a trajectory that supports the concept of ataxia as a prodromal feature and explains frequent early misdiagnosis as ataxic or dyskinetic cerebral palsy. 14 , 23 , 37 Symptom severity generally followed a stable or improving clinical course. 36 Although cognitive outcomes were generally favorable, specific learning difficulties and neuropsychiatric comorbidities such as attention‐deficit hyperactivity disorder and autism spectrum disorder were observed, aligning with emerging data on the role of NKX2‐1 in forebrain interneuron development and cognitive‐behavioral regulation. 14 , 38 Therapeutically, the movement disorder proved largely refractory, with improvement with methylphenidate in a small number of patients and partial benefit from deep brain stimulation in one severe dystonic case, supporting growing but still limited evidence for pallidal stimulation in NKX2‐1 deficiency. 13 , 14 , 17 Finally, malignancy risk remains a concern as illustrated by the occurrence of bilateral lung adenocarcinoma in one adult patient (8‐I:1), highlighting the need for long‐term surveillance. 17

Despite the growing knowledge about NKX2‐1‐RD, there is a lack of consensus in the diagnostic approach of this disorder. 15 First, the phenotypic heterogeneity makes clinical diagnosis difficult to standardize. 14 Second, the heterogeneity of genetic variant types, as shown here, hinders the selection of an optimal diagnostic algorithm, because each analytical technique presents intrinsic limitations that restrict its applicability to specific categories of variants. 27 , 39 Nonetheless, a recent European survey reported a high overall diagnostic success rate (>75%), using Sanger sequencing, gene panels, and ES commonly as first‐tier approaches, with MLPA and aCGH occasionally applied when sequencing yielded normal results. 15 In this study, we predicted that ES could recapitulate the diagnostic yield achieved by the five distinct methodologies mentioned earlier and, moreover, enable the detection of additional variants beyond those previously identified, reaching a diagnostic success rate of 92% (Table 3). However, this performance would be achievable only with careful attention to CNVs, including analysis of the MBIP region, which was responsible for 26% additional yield. In addition, advanced bioinformatic approaches leveraging ES off‐target reads and transposable elements should also be considered to detect additional variants. 39 , 40 , 41 It must be stated that, when ES is not available, incorporation of MBIP in targeted gene panels could be an alternative solution. Yet, SVs and deletions not including MBIP or other coding genes, such as the one reported in the study of Liao et al, 27 could still be missed by this approach. Emerging genomic technologies hold promises for improving the diagnostic yield by enabling the detection of defects in noncoding regions and improving detection of SVs. 27 , 39 Although we predict that sr‐GS could detect all SVs identified within our cohort, it may still miss certain disease‐associated variation because of inherent limitations of short‐read technologies, including variants in complex regions of the genome. 42 , 43 Consequently, lr‐GS should be considered as the gold standard approach for patients with clinical suspicion of NKX2‐1‐related disease, because it enables comprehensive detection of all classes of genetic variants. 42 , 44 However, given that lr‐GS is not yet widely accessible and that ES remains the current first‐line approach, our recommendation is to perform ES analyses with careful attention to CNV detection, including those affecting MBIP. In cases where ES yields negative results, OGM or sr‐GS should be considered to identify additional structural alterations not captured by ES. 28 , 45

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that downstream NKX2‐1 variants account for a significant proportion of NKX2‐1‐related diagnoses, likely through disruption of a super‐enhancer region. The overlap of all newly identified variants with previously reported NKX2‐1‐RD cases delineates a critical regulatory region for NKX2‐1, which may facilitate more accurate interpretation of variant pathogenicity. Based on these findings, we recommend genetic testing strategies that enable detection of SVs in the downstream region for all individuals with movement disorders and other clinical features consistent with NKX2‐1‐RDs, particularly when coding variants have been excluded.
