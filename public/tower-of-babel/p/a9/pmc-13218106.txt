Air pollution, urban environmental quality and health expenditures: evidence from industrial cities in Iran
Authors: Soroush Rahmatian, Vida Varahrami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRapid industrialisation and urbanisation can worsen air quality and increase the financial pressure on health systems. However, city-level evidence on how fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and urban environmental characteristics relate to healthcare expenditures remains limited in middle-income settings.MethodsWe conducted an ecological panel study of 10 industrial cities in Iran (2011–2024). We assembled city-year data on per-capita healthcare expenditures and key urban characteristics from municipal statistical yearbooks and linked them to annual PM2.5 estimates. We fitted log–log panel models with city random effects and year fixed effects using robust inference. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-city-out re-estimation and an alternative specification replacing income with the household-income Gini coefficient.ResultsPer-capita healthcare expenditures were positively associated with PM2.5 (elasticity=0.37; p=0.057), per-capita income (=0.18; p=0.020), population density (=0.28; p=0.010), and the share aged ≥60 years (=1.05; p=0.008). The association for green space was small and sensitive to specification (=0.05; p=0.034 in the baseline model and not statistically significant when lagged), while per-capita healthcare facilities were not statistically significant. In the inequality model, the Gini coefficient was positively associated with healthcare expenditures (p=0.002).ConclusionsHigher ambient PM2.5, urban density, population ageing and income inequality were associated with higher healthcare expenditures in this city-level panel analysis. These findings suggest that coordinated policies combining emission control, health-system strengthening (particularly preventive and primary care), and improved quality and equitable access to urban green infrastructure may help curb growth in healthcare spending in rapidly industrialising and urbanising settings.

Introduction

Air pollution and urban environmental quality are increasingly recognised as key determinants of population health and important drivers of healthcare expenditures. In many countries, medical spending has grown more rapidly than national income, placing pressure on public budgets and on households’ ability to finance care.1 Ambient air pollution, particularly fine particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter smaller than 2.5 micrometers (PM2.5), contributes substantially to this burden. Recent global assessments estimate that the welfare loss associated with premature mortality and morbidity attributable to ambient PM2.5, household air pollution and ozone represents a sizeable share of gross domestic product, with mortality costs constituting the largest component.25 These burdens are often concentrated in rapidly urbanising industrial regions where energy use, transport demand and population density are high.

Urban environmental characteristics can amplify or mitigate the health impacts of pollution. Features such as the availability and accessibility of green spaces, the configuration of urban vegetation, transport infrastructure and the spatial distribution of healthcare facilities shape everyday exposures and opportunities for health-promoting behaviours. A growing body of evidence suggests that well-designed urban green spaces and higher vegetation coverage can buffer exposure to heat and air pollutants, support physical activity and reduce stress, thereby improving health outcomes and potentially lowering medical spending.6 7 At the same time, unfavourable urban form, congested road networks, long commuting times and insufficient public transport can increase contact with traffic-related emissions and aggravate the health and economic burden of air pollution.

Epidemiological studies consistently document that both short- and long-term exposure to PM2.5 is associated with increases in all-cause and cause-specific mortality, especially from cardiovascular disease and lung cancer, with effects often more pronounced among older adults. These health impacts translate into higher utilisation of outpatient and inpatient services and increased pharmaceutical consumption over time, contributing to sustained growth in healthcare expenditures.1 The fiscal implications are particularly salient for health systems in low- and middle-income countries, where resources are limited and competing priorities are numerous.

Beyond objectively measured air-quality indicators and physical urban form, perceptions of environmental quality and neighbourhood conditions also matter. Cross-national evidence indicates that individuals’ satisfaction with their local environment does not always coincide with objective pollution levels, and that perceived environmental problems can influence health behaviours, stress and care-seeking independently of measured exposures.8 Socioeconomic conditions and income inequality further shape both environmental exposures and the capacity to cope with illness. Income gradients in healthcare use and spending, as well as unequal access to cleaner neighbourhoods and green spaces, may reinforce the health and financial consequences of pollution.1

Industrial cities in Iran embody many of these challenges. Rapid urbanisation, motorisation and industrial activity have produced complex patterns of ambient air pollution, while population ageing and uneven economic development create additional pressures on municipal health systems. However, in Iran, most quantitative evidence linking air pollution to health-related economic outcomes relies on national time-series data and/or broad pollution proxies (eg, CO₂/PM₁₀ or fuel-use indicators), rather than city-level PM₂.₅ exposure linked to municipal healthcare expenditures. Multi-city panel evidence that can track within-city changes over time is scarce, and existing studies rarely model air pollution jointly with urban-form and service indicators (eg, density, green space and healthcare capacity) and distributional factors such as income inequality. Consequently, the city-level fiscal correlates of PM₂.₅ in major industrial/rapidly urbanising Iranian cities remain insufficiently characterised.

The objective of this study is to examine the associations between air pollution, urban environmental quality and per-capita healthcare expenditures in selected industrial cities of Iran. Using panel data for ten major industrial cities over the period 2011–2024 (1390–1403), we relate per-capita healthcare expenditures to city-level PM2.5 concentrations, population density, per-capita green space, the per-capita number of healthcare facilities and the share of the population aged 60 years and older, while also considering the distribution of income. By integrating environmental, urban and socioeconomic dimensions within a unified empirical framework, this study aims to provide evidence that can inform urban planning and health policy in settings facing severe air-pollution challenges and rising medical costs. Accordingly, our inferences are intended to apply primarily to large, industrial/rapidly urbanising Iranian cities with similar pollution sources and urban-service structures; generalisation to non-industrial cities and rural areas should be made cautiously.

Literature review

Conceptual pathways and key modifiers

Air pollution, urban environmental quality and healthcare expenditures are tightly linked in both theory and empirical work. From an environmental-health perspective, exposure to ambient pollutants, especially PM2.5, acts through biological pathways of chronic stress and inflammation, increasing the incidence and severity of respiratory, cardiovascular and other non-communicable diseases and thereby raising the need for medical care.4 9 Within a health-production and health human-capital framework, health is the outcome of investments by households and the state combined with environmental and urban conditions; healthcare expenditures are a derived demand that reflects underlying health needs, preferences and institutional constraints. When pollution worsens population health, the resulting burden of disease and disability pushes up spending on outpatient visits, hospitalisations and pharmaceuticals, especially in vulnerable groups such as older adults.2 3 5

Building on the behavioural model of healthcare utilisation, the demand for healthcare services is typically viewed as a function of three sets of determinants: (1) need or health status, which can be influenced by air pollution and population ageing; (2) enabling factors such as income, insurance coverage and spatial access to services and (3) health-system supply, including the capacity and spatial distribution of healthcare infrastructure. A growing empirical literature shows that higher levels of air pollution are consistently associated with greater healthcare expenditures across a range of income levels and institutional contexts, even after controlling for per-capita income, age structure and other confounders.1013 These findings are consistent with the theoretical logic that pollution primarily raises expenditures by increasing medical need. Recent extensions of the health human-capital literature also distinguish between health-production efficiency and preferences for future health, highlighting that households may respond to pollution by simultaneously purchasing medical care and defensive goods such as masks and air purifiers; evidence from Chinese cities suggests that smog-avoidance investments and medical spending are both higher among individuals with stronger concern for future health and lower time preference.14

Urban form and environmental quality shape these relationships by influencing exposure pathways and health behaviours. At the city level, high population density, traffic congestion and vehicle-dependent transport systems can increase exposure to pollutants and strain healthcare capacity, whereas green infrastructure—such as urban vegetation and parks—can reduce localised heat and pollution and promote physical activity and stress reduction. Studies of urban environmental quality and health indicate that better access to and quality of green spaces are associated with improvements in self-rated health and reductions in hospitalisation costs and healthcare expenditures.6 15 Comparative assessments across cities in different income groups show that measurable evidence-based targets for air quality, transport and green infrastructure are often absent from planning documents and that some urban-development orientations are misaligned with public-health evidence, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.16 In parallel, work on urban heat islands underscores that high-density, poorly planned urbanisation can amplify the health impacts of air pollution, while ‘smart density’, walkability, green infrastructure and low-emission transport systems can reduce the combined burden of heat and pollution.17

From a demographic and fiscal perspective, population ageing and environmental governance further modify the pollution–expenditure link. Ageing societies face higher baseline needs for healthcare and greater vulnerability to pollution-related diseases, which implies that the elasticity of healthcare expenditures with respect to pollution shocks may be larger in older populations. At the same time, healthcare capacity can have a dual effect on spending: expanding supply may increase expenditures through demand inducement, or it may reduce expensive acute episodes by improving access to preventive and primary care; the net effect depends on institutional arrangements and the quality of governance.11 18 Economic assessments at regional and national levels suggest that the welfare losses from pollution are extremely high and that, in most cost–benefit analyses, the health and economic benefits of air-pollution control outweigh mitigation costs, with particulate-matter reductions as the main driver of gains.2 3 5 Recent work on low-carbon policy in China further shows that environmental regulation and health budgets are jointly determined: stricter low-carbon city pilot policies can reduce the relative share of health spending in the budget while increasing its absolute level, reflecting trade-offs and complementarities between environmental investment and health financing.19

Empirical evidence with emphasis on Iran

In Iran, empirical research on air pollution and health/economic outcomes can be summarised in three strands. First, valuation and damage-assessment studies quantify the welfare losses from polluted air and residents’ willingness to pay for cleaner air, including evidence from Tehran and other cities.2022 Hedonic evidence also suggests that air pollution is capitalised into housing prices in Tehran.23 24 Second, health-impact studies document pollution-attributable mortality and highlight the burden of PM2.5 in major Iranian cities.25 26 Third, time-series and panel analyses (Iran and broader regional/developing-country panels) generally find that air-pollution indicators/proxies are positively associated with health expenditures, alongside demographic and institutional factors.2734 Overall, while the Iranian evidence supports a positive pollution–health-cost nexus, much of it is national in scope, single-city or proxy-based—motivating the present multi-city panel focus on city-level PM2.5 together with urban-form and distributional indicators.

International evidence complements these findings and helps contextualise them across different settings. Using data from the East Asian Social Survey, Kamimura et al showed that perceived air, water and noise pollution are associated with both physical and mental health in China, Japan and South Korea, and that perceived environmental risks do not always mirror objective pollution rankings.8 Barwick et al used flexible distributed-lag models with instrumental variables to show that air pollution significantly increases healthcare spending in Chinese cities.35 Hao et al found that environmental pollution raises public health expenditures in Chinese provinces, while Sun and Wang documented positive direct and spillover effects of PM2.5 emissions on rural health spending.10 36 In Southeast Asia, Taghizadeh and Hesari reported that environmental quality and health expenditures jointly affect cancer prevalence.37 Time-series work for China indicates that air pollution and CO₂ emissions exert positive and asymmetric effects on household health expenditures, with bidirectional causality between health spending and pollution.12 Panel ARDL (Autoregressive Distributed Lag) analyses for Indian states and other developing countries suggest that the sensitivity of health spending to changes in pollution can exceed its sensitivity to income.13 38 At the microlevel, Li et al showed that higher air-pollution indices are associated with progressively higher hospital costs for respiratory patients in Beijing,39 while Mink used high-frequency French data and an instrumental-variable strategy to demonstrate that even pollution levels below current European standards impose substantial short-term financial burdens on health systems.40

Environmental and urban-planning literatures further emphasise the importance of urban design and regulation. Narrative reviews synthesise evidence on the broad range of pollutants, PM2.5, ozone, nitrogen and sulphur oxides and volatile organic compounds and their links with respiratory, cardiovascular, neurological and oncological outcomes, as well as the role of social factors and legal frameworks.9 Systematic reviews of air-pollution control strategies conclude that, in most cases, the economic benefits of mitigation exceed the costs, particularly through reductions in PM2.5 and associated mortality and morbidity, with both source-specific interventions and end-of-pipe technologies proving cost-beneficial.36 Emerging work on defensive behaviours shows that households investing in smog-avoidance measures continue to incur higher medical expenditures, suggesting that private protective investments do not simply substitute for healthcare spending and may capture additional welfare losses not visible in aggregate data.14 Urban-comparative analyses document gaps between health-oriented planning principles and actual transport and land-use policies, underscoring the need to embed health criteria into urban-development decisions.16

Despite this rich body of theoretical and empirical work, important gaps remain, particularly for low- and middle-income countries. Many studies rely on national aggregates or broad environmental proxies such as CO₂ emissions, which obscure intracountry heterogeneity in exposures and urban form.13 30 41 Few analyses jointly consider detailed air-pollution measures, urban environmental indicators—such as per-capita green space, health-facility density and population density—and demographic structure within a unified empirical framework.6 15 16 The role of income inequality and institutional quality in mediating the pollution–expenditure relationship also remains underexplored, even though existing work suggests that governance and income distribution affect both environmental performance and health-system financing.18 19 34 In the Iranian context, most empirical studies are either national in scope, focused on single cities or do not simultaneously incorporate air pollution, urban form and distributional indicators. The present study contributes to this literature by providing city-level panel evidence for ten industrial Iranian cities over 2011–2024, relating per-capita healthcare expenditures to PM2.5, population density, green space, healthcare capacity, ageing and income and income inequality within a consistent econometric framework.

Materials and methods

This study employs panel data for ten selected industrial cities in Iran—Tehran, Isfahan, Shiraz, Arak, Karaj, Tabriz, Mashhad, Kerman, Ahvaz and Bandar Abbas—over the period 1390–1403 (2011–2024). These cities are selected due to the concentration of industrial activities and the diversity of urban environmental conditions, which together provide variation to characterise city-level associations between air pollution, urban environmental quality and per-capita healthcare expenditures. HealthCost is an aggregate city-year measure of per-capita healthcare expenditure. Because the data are not available by service type/diagnosis/payer/age, we cannot decompose spending or identify which components drive the pollution–expenditure association. This selection is purposive rather than nationally representative. Therefore, the estimates should be interpreted as city-level associations within the target population of industrial cities and may not extrapolate to smaller, non-industrial or rural settings with different pollution mixtures, health-system capacity and socioeconomic structures. The panel-data framework, by combining cross-sectional (inter-city) and temporal (over years) dimensions, increases informational variance, allows control for unobserved city-specific heterogeneity and yields more efficient estimates and inference.42 This study used aggregated, publicly available city-level data; accordingly, coefficients should be interpreted as city-level associations and do not identify individual-level causal effects. The analytic dataset formed a balanced panel of 10 cities over 14 years with no missing values in the variables used in the main models. The dependent variable under study is per-capita healthcare expenditure (HealthCost), which, as in Equation (1), is specified as a function of per-capita income (Income), air pollution (Pollution), per-capita green space (GreenSpace), population density (PopulationDensity), per-capita number of healthcare facilities (Hospital) and the share of the population aged over 60 (Age60).

Data on per-capita healthcare expenditure, per-capita income, the area of urban green spaces, demographic variables and the number of healthcare facilities were extracted from the statistical yearbooks of each city published by the Statistical Centre. Per-capita green space (GreenSpace) was constructed as the reported area of urban green spaces divided by the city population; this is a quantitative indicator and does not capture quality, accessibility, microclimate functions or vegetation intensity. PM2.5 exposure data were obtained from the Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group (ACAG) at Washington University in St. Louis. Because both the outcome and the city-level covariates are available at an annual frequency, each city-year observation was linked to the corresponding annual PM2.5 estimate. Where locally reported air-quality data were available from domestic sources (eg, the Statistical Centre and air-quality monitoring systems), the PM2.5 series were cross-validated against the ACAG estimates. The air-pollution indicator used is fine PM2.5 smaller than 2.5 micrometers. We note that annual city-level PM2.5 averages represent an approximation of population exposure and cannot capture within-city spatial gradients in pollution. For the per-capita healthcare-facilities variable, the counts include all hospitals, primary healthcare centres and clinics and community health houses. To align more closely with the theoretical framework, the population aged over 60 is measured as the ratio of each city’s population aged over 60 to its total population. Variable definitions and data sources are summarised in online supplemental table S1.

Accordingly, as in Equation (2), the empirical specification is expressed in logarithmic form, which mitigates heteroskedasticity, accords with the theoretical intuition of health-demand and health-production functions and permits interpreting coefficients as elasticities describing city-level associations. Based on prior theory and evidence, we hypothesise that higher PM2.5, higher per-capita income, greater population density and a larger share of the population aged 60 and over will be positively associated with per-capita healthcare expenditures. We further hypothesise an inverse association for green space, although the per-capita measure may not capture quality or accessibility. The association for per-capita healthcare facilities is theoretically ambiguous and may be positive (through induced utilisation) or negative (through prevention and earlier, lower-cost care). As a robustness check and to account for the distribution of income, we re-estimated the model by replacing per capita income with the Gini coefficient of household income. The Gini index was used as a standard summary measure of income inequality (higher values indicating greater inequality). We also performed a leave-one-city-out sensitivity analysis, re-estimating the baseline model after sequentially excluding each of the ten cities.

The estimation results are broadly in line with theoretical expectations. Consistent with health-demand theory, higher per-capita income is positively associated with higher spending (elasticity=0.18); a 1% higher income level is associated with an average 0.18% higher per-capita healthcare expenditure. PM2.5 is also positively associated with expenditures (elasticity=0.37), and population density shows a positive association of about 0.28, consistent with congestion and contagion mechanisms. By contrast, the coefficient on per-capita green space is positive (elasticity=0.05), whereas theory would typically suggest a cost-reducing association; when green space was lagged one period, the estimates became statistically insignificant. This counterintuitive sign may reflect that per-capita green-space area is correlated with broader urban development and does not capture quality, accessibility, or the spatial distribution of green infrastructure; accordingly, we interpret this estimate cautiously. The coefficient on per-capita healthcare facilities is not statistically significant and its implied elasticity is therefore not interpretable. As expected, the share of the elderly population has a strong positive association with expenditures, with an elasticity close to 1.05. The estimated elasticities for income, PM2.5, and the proportion aged 60 and over are broadly consistent with findings from previous studies.

To assess the stability of these estimates, we performed a leave-one-city-out sensitivity analysis. In each case, one of the ten industrial cities was removed from the sample and the model re-estimated with the same structure (cross-sectional random effects and year fixed effects). Across all specifications, the signs of the key coefficients (air pollution, green space, population density, the share of adults aged 60+, and income) remained unchanged. Population density and the elderly share retained a positive and relatively stable association with per-capita healthcare expenditures throughout. The coefficient on air pollution was positive in every model and statistically significant at the 5% level in a substantial subset of estimations, particularly when Isfahan, Tabriz, Kerman or Bandar Abbas were excluded; omitting highly polluted cities such as Ahvaz and Mashhad reduced both the magnitude and significance of this effect. The coefficient on green space also remained positive in all models but its significance was more sensitive to sample composition and was in some cases only marginally significant at the 10% level. Overall, these checks suggest that the main pattern of relationships is not driven by any single city and can be regarded as empirically robust.

Table 5 reports a robustness specification in which per-capita income is replaced by the Gini coefficient of household income. In this model, the Gini index is positively and significantly associated with per-capita healthcare expenditures, indicating that higher income inequality is linked to higher spending. The coefficients on air pollution, population density and green space remain positive and of similar magnitude to those in the baseline model, although their statistical significance is reduced, whereas the effect of the share of the population aged over 60 remains robust. These results suggest that part of the variation in health expenditures across cities reflects underlying income inequality, without overturning the main environmental and demographic patterns identified in the baseline model.

Discussion and conclusion

Interpretation of findings

This study examined how air pollution and urban environmental characteristics are associated with per-capita healthcare expenditures in ten industrial cities of Iran over 2011–2024 (1390–1403). Using a panel EGLS model with cross-sectional random effects and year fixed effects, we found that per-capita income and population density are positively related to healthcare spending, consistent with health-demand and congestion mechanisms. PM₂.₅ concentrations also showed a positive, amplifying association with expenditures and were close to conventional thresholds of statistical significance. The share of the population aged 60 years and older had a strong positive elasticity, indicating that demographic ageing substantially intensifies fiscal pressures on urban health systems. By contrast, the coefficients on per-capita green space and healthcare facility capacity were not robustly significant. In an alternative specification, the Gini coefficient emerged as a strong positive correlate of healthcare expenditures, while the elasticities for PM₂.₅ and population density remained positive but less precisely estimated. Because HealthCost is aggregate, we cannot verify specific channels; future studies should test mechanisms using disaggregated utilisation/expenditure data (service/diagnosis/payer/age) when available.

These findings are broadly consistent with previous evidence from Iran and other settings. Using national time-series data, Raeissi et al reported that increases in CO₂ emissions are associated with higher public and private health expenditures in Iran, highlighting environmental degradation as a key driver of health spending.30 Our city-level panel based on PM₂.₅ concentrations and urban indicators for major industrial centres points to a similar positive relationship between air pollution and per-capita healthcare expenditures, suggesting that the fiscal burden of pollution is robust across different pollutants and empirical approaches. The city-level pattern also aligns with micro-level evidence from China showing that households investing in smog-avoidance measures continue to incur higher medical expenditures, implying that observed healthcare spending captures only part of the welfare loss from pollution. In addition, recent work on low-carbon city policies in China indicates that environmental governance and healthcare financing are closely linked, with cleaner urban development reshaping both disease burdens and the composition of public spending.

From an urban policy perspective, the positive association between PM₂.₅ and per-capita healthcare expenditures underscores the potential economic relevance of sustained PM₂.₅ reduction. If this association reflects morbidity and utilisation pathways documented in prior epidemiologic and quasi-experimental studies, lowering pollution may help moderate growth in healthcare spending, potentially through fewer outpatient visits and hospital admissions. Given the positive association with population density, density-management strategies should focus on decongesting services rather than simply lowering density, for example by strengthening neighbourhood-based primary care, improving the spatial distribution of health centres and using smart appointment systems to smooth queues. Demographic ageing emerges as a powerful cost driver, suggesting a need for targeted prevention and monitoring of chronic conditions in older adults, local rehabilitation and long-term care services, and financial protection mechanisms to moderate the growth of expenditures. The fragile and counterintuitive association between per-capita green space and healthcare costs suggests that municipal investments should prioritise the quality, spatial connectivity, shading and equitable access to green areas—especially in dense and polluted neighbourhoods—rather than focusing solely on aggregate area. The positive association between the Gini coefficient and healthcare expenditures underscores the role of income inequality: in more unequal settings, higher exposure and vulnerability among disadvantaged groups, combined with uneven access to formal care, may translate into higher aggregate spending and reinforce health disparities.

Taken together with prior evidence, our results suggest that the pollution–health expenditure relationship is consistent with common underlying mechanisms—such as morbidity, congestion and population ageing—observed across diverse urban and institutional contexts. Although local factors shape the intensity of these associations, their consistent direction across studies supports the interpretation that the Iranian evidence contributes to a broader understanding of how urban environmental quality is associated with health-system costs.

External validity and generalisability: Because our panel covers ten major industrial cities, the results are most directly informative for similar urban-industrial contexts where emissions are driven by industry, traffic and high density. While a positive pollution–health expenditure association is broadly consistent with prior evidence across countries and study designs, effect magnitudes may differ in non-industrial or rural areas due to structural differences in (1) the pollution mixture and dominant sources, (2) baseline health status and age structure, (3) insurance coverage and healthcare prices, (4) health-system supply and access (facility density and referral patterns) and (5) climate/topography that affects exposure dispersion. Future work using national city panels and larger multi-country LMIC samples could explicitly model heterogeneity (eg, random slopes/city clusters) and evaluate out-of-sample predictive performance.

Limitations, generalisability and future research

This study has several limitations. Our outcome is aggregate total healthcare expenditure; without component-level data (service/diagnosis/payer/age), we cannot identify which spending categories or clinical pathways explain the association with air pollution. First, the ecological design and reliance on city-level aggregates mean that coefficients should be interpreted as city-level associations and cannot be used to infer individual-level causal effects (ecological fallacy). Although the panel structure helps account for time-invariant city characteristics and common year shocks, unmeasured time-varying factors (eg, local policy changes, migration, or economic shocks) may still confound the observed associations. Second, our green-space indicator is purely quantitative and does not capture quality, microclimate, or accessibility; this may help explain the unstable and theoretically unexpected estimates. Future research should incorporate more spatially explicit and qualitative green-infrastructure measures (eg, NDVI/EVI, canopy cover and accessibility indices), consider inequality metrics for the distribution of green space and explore potential non-linearities and interactions with population density and pollution levels. Third, our PM2.5 exposure measure is based on annual city-level averages, which may be subject to exposure measurement error and spatial heterogeneity—especially in large cities with substantial within-city variation driven by topography, land use and traffic patterns. Classical measurement error in exposure would generally bias estimated elasticities toward zero (attenuation), potentially leading to conservative (ie, underestimated) pollution–expenditure associations. Fourth, potential simultaneity between healthcare expenditures and some determinants, such as income or healthcare capacity, may bias coefficients despite our panel structure. Finally, because the dataset does not include the spatial information needed to construct a defensible intercity weights matrix, we cannot explicitly estimate spatial econometric models; thus, cross-sectional dependence may partly reflect spillover processes rather than purely city-specific associations.

In conclusion, the findings suggest that integrated management of air quality and the urban physical and service structure can help curb the growth of healthcare costs in industrial cities. These conclusions are most applicable to industrial and highly urbanised settings, and further research using broader national city panels is needed to assess generalisability to non-industrial and rural contexts. A policy mix that combines emission control, transport and land-use planning, strengthened primary care and the intelligent design of urban green networks—alongside efforts to reduce income inequality—can support more health-oriented urban development and inform the economic appraisal of pollution-reduction projects by municipal and health-sector decision makers.
