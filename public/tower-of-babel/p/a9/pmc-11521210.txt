Inflammatory Myofibroblastic Tumor Masquerading as Congenital Pelvic-ureteric Junction Obstruction
Authors: Pallav Kumar, Mohammed Moinuddin Zameer, Vinay Chandrashekar, Sanjay Rao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) is a very rare tumor with still rare occurrence in the genitourinary system, presenting as a pelvic-ureteric junction (PUJ) obstruction (PUJO). We report a case of IMT at the PUJ mimicking congenital PUJO and review the relevant literature.

INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) is a rare benign tumor and is poorly understood. It is characterized by the proliferation of spindle cells mixed with inflammatory cell infiltrates. It is most commonly found in the lung but has rarely been found in extrapulmonary sites such as the uterus, larynx, stomach, liver, intestine, and genitourinary system.[1] The bladder is the most common site in the genitourinary tract. Only a few case reports on distal ureteral involvement have been reported.[1] Here, we report a case of a child with pelvic-ureteric junction (PUJ) IMT mimicking congenital PUJ obstruction (PUJO).

CASE REPORT

A 2-year-old girl presented with complaints of recurrent episodes of painless hematuria for 10 months. On per abdominal examination, the abdomen was soft and no mass was palpable. Renal function, hemogram, and urine analysis were normal. Ultrasonography of the renal system revealed left hydronephrosis with an anteroposterior diameter of the renal pelvis being 11 mm. A diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid scan demonstrated an obstructed drainage curve on the left side with a 30% split renal function. As the calyceal dilatation was inconspicuous for age, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the renal system was done, which showed dilatation of left renal calyces with abrupt cutoff at the level of PUJ and no drainage into the ureter, suggesting PUJO [Figure 1]. The child was posted left open pyeloplasty. Intraoperatively, a mass was visible at the PUJ (intrinsic), causing an almost complete obstruction [Figure 2]. A frozen section of the mass was reported as a proliferation of spindle cells in a collagenous background. The kidney was mobilized, renal vessels were slopped and mass was excised in toto with a rim of the pelvis (almost up to renal calyces), and Anderson–Hynes Pyeloplasty was done. The postoperative period was unremarkable. The biopsy report established the diagnosis of IMT with borderline malignancy and microscopic infiltration into the pelvis end of the resected sample. Immunohistochemistry was positive for vimentin and SMA, and negative for ALK 1. As further resection of the pelvis was not feasible, a decision was made to defer further surgery (which would have been nephrectomy). The child was started on chemotherapy, which included 16 weeks of weekly intravenous vinblastine (5 mg/m2), methotrexate (30 mg/m2), and oral celecoxib twice daily. The child will be evaluated for the response at 16 weeks. Once a complete response is achieved, the child will be started on injection vinblastin once every 3 weeks, methotrexate once a week, and celecoxib twice daily for a total of 45 weeks. Postsurgery CECT images were revisited, and a doubtful inconspicuous lesion was seen in the renal pelvis. The plan is to keep the child on close follow-up and in case of recurrence of the lesion, nephrectomy would be performed (have counseled the parents regarding the same). On a 3-month follow-up, the child is doing well and is asymptomatic. Ultrasound KUB shows no hydronephrosis or any lesion in the kidney.

DISCUSSION

IMT has been found in many different tissues. The lung is frequently involved. Lesions have been reported in the stomach, pancreas, liver, spinal cord meninges, retroperitoneum, appendix, and genitourinary system.[2] In the genitourinary system, the most common site is the bladder, and involvement of the ureter is very rare, most of which were either in the distal ureter or at the vesicoureteric junction.[1] In our case, the tumor was involving the PUJ, causing obstruction and it is one of the first reported cases in the pediatric population, to the best of our knowledge.[1234]

The tumor tends to occur in young patients with no gender preponderance.[1] The etiology is suspected to be a result of a localized inflammatory reaction in response to infection, surgery, or malignancy. In our patient, none of these risk factors were present. A high proportion of patients do not have any apparent risk factors.[1] The clinical manifestations of IMTs are nonspecific and vary in different sites. In children with genitourinary IMTs, the most common symptom was hematuria, followed by lower urinary tract symptoms, abdominal pain, and palpable mass.[4] On further evaluation, they have hydronephrosis or hydroureteronephrosis, depending on the level of involvement of the ureter. In our case, the child presented with painless hematuria and, on evaluation, was found to have hydronephrosis on the affected side with obstructed drainage and decreased renal function, which are classical of a patient with congenital PUJO.

The histology of the tumor is variable, but most of the tumors are predominantly composed of spindle-shaped cells with inflammatory cell infiltrates, mainly lymphocytes and plasma cells. ALK translocation positive is seen in 55% of IMT cases. Although these lesions are generally considered benign, they have the potential for malignant change and progressive organ damage.[4] Some of these lesions that have the potential for a more aggressive clinical course, malignant transformation, and recurrences have been reported with an overall mortality rate of up to 7%.[1] The main stay of treatment for IMTs are complete resection of the tumor. Chemotherapy is reserved for cases which are focally aggressive, invasive with positive margins, and surgically unresectable tumors. Our patient underwent surgical resection of the tumor with A-H pyeloplasty; on histopathology, it was revealed that the resected pelvic end showed microscopic infiltration by the tumor; hence, the child was started on adjuvant chemotherapy. The chemotherapy regimen for IMT is with vinblastine, celecoxib, and methotrexate.[5] Due to the extreme rarity of IMTs, data on chemotherapy in IMT are limited. A newer therapeutic option for IMT is crizotinib (ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitor), oral formulation is available to treat ALK-translocated IMT.[5]

As these tumors have malignant potential and carry the risk of recurrences, they should be kept under close follow-up.[4]

CONCLUSION

IMT of the PUJ is a very rare occurrence. It may mimic congenital PUJO. Treatment involves complete resection of the tumor and, in some selected cases, chemotherapy.
