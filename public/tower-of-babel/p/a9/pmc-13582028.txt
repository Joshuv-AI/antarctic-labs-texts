Efficacy and safety of transoral incisionless fundoplication in non-obese and obese adults: a population-based cohort study from the United States
Authors: Fouad Jaber, Saltenat Moghaddam Adames, Mohamed Jaber, Kinan Obiedat, Kalpesh Patel, Teminioluwa A. Ajayi, Roy Tomas DaVee, Fares W. Ayoub, Nirav Thosani, Wasseem Skef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransoral incisionless fundoplication (TIF) is relatively contraindicated in patients with a body mass index (BMI) > 35 kg/m2. However, the comparative efficacy of TIF in individuals with class I obesity (BMI 30–34.9) to non-obese patients remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of TIF in adults with class I obesity.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX US Research Network, including data from 69 healthcare organizations through December 2024. Adults (≥ 18 years) with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) who underwent TIF were identified using validated International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes. Patients were stratified by BMI (< 30 versus 30–35). Exclusion criteria in this study included the following: individuals with a BMI > 35, major esophageal motility disorders, or concomitant hiatal hernia repair. We performed propensity score matching (1:1) and controlled for age, sex, race, ethnicity, diaphragmatic hernia, and proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use. Primary outcomes included postoperative PPI recidivism (excluding the first 3 months), post-TIF esophagitis, and need for subsequent foregut surgery. Secondary outcomes included post-TIF dysphagia, gas bloat symptoms, and 30-day complications.ResultsAfter propensity matching, 112 non-obese and 112 class I obese patients were included with similar baseline characteristics. Median follow-up exceeded 1,000 days in both groups. Postoperative PPI recidivism, esophagitis, surgical interventions, dysphagia and complications were similar. Notably, bloating was significantly lower in the obese group at 1 year (aOR 0.420; 95% CI 0.18–0.95) and maximum follow-up (aOR 0.446; 95% CI 0.21–0.97).ConclusionsTIF appears safe and effective for GERD in adults with class I obesity with comparable outcomes to non-obese patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-026-13084-x.

Transoral incisionless fundoplication (TIF) is a minimally invasive endoscopic procedure for the treatment of patients with chronic symptomatic gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). Although limited to patients with axial hernia < 2 cm or hiatus diameters < 3 cm [1], several randomized clinical trials have demonstrated efficacy with low adverse event rates [2–4]. Furthermore, 10-year prospective observational data suggests that TIF has comparable proton pump inhibitor recidivism rates and improvement in GERD-specific severity scoring instruments to surgical fundoplication in appropriately selected patients [5].

In situations where the patient suffers from larger hiatal hernia and GERD, TIF remains a viable option if it is combined with cruroplasty. Current data on concomitant hiatal hernia repair with TIF (cTIF) demonstrates comparable short-term efficacy [6]. Furthermore, the combined procedure has been shown to have an excellent safety profile, thus permitting the management of refractory GERD with a low side effect profile.

Alternatively, surgical fundoplication, the minimally invasive Nissen fundoplication (NF) in particular, remains the “gold standard” anti-reflux intervention for symptomatic gastroesophageal reflux disease. NF is the preferred procedural treatment for non-obese patients (BMI < 30 kg/m2) with symptomatic GERD. However, the optimal approach for patients with obesity and GERD remains controversial due to conflicting results in the literature [7–10]. Given higher fundoplication failure rates with increasing BMI, the optimal surgical approach for class 1 obesity involves considering several factors including severity of GERD symptoms, obesity-related comorbidities, patient preference and potential for secondary benefits of weight loss and improvement in metabolic syndrome [11].

Similar to surgical fundoplication, obesity remains a limitation of TIF with guidelines advising that BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 as a relative contraindication for the TIF procedure despite a paucity of literature on this cohort of patients [12]. Several concerns include technical challenges, increased perioperative complications, decreased efficacy, and high risk of anatomic failure. Although class 2 obesity only represents 10% of patients with severe GERD symptoms according to the Nurses Health Study, 21% of patients with severe GERD have class 1 obesity (BMI 30–34.9 kg/m2) which represents a sizeable cohort [13]; therefore, it is imperative to understand how BMI affects the efficacy of TIF in this cohort.

In our study, we attempted to study the real-world effect of BMI on TIF outcomes using a recognized national database. Our hypothesis was that the efficacy and safety of TIF in adults with class I obesity would be similar to non-obese patients.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective cohort study uses the TriNetX US Research Network, a collaborative network database made up over 70 healthcare organizations across the United States. These organizations provide access to electronic medical records (EMRs), which include clinical data such as diagnoses, procedures, medications, lab results, and demographics. This database facilitates clinical research by enabling researchers to query data for cohort identification, trial feasibility, and outcomes research. This research network is designed to enhance research efficiency while maintaining patient privacy, with all data de-identified in compliance with HIPAA regulations. As a result, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required for this study.

Study design and population

Patient data were extracted from the TriNetX platform covering the period from database inception to November 2024. The study population included adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) who underwent transoral incisionless fundoplication (TIF). Patients were identified using validated International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) and procedure terminology (CPT) codes. Patients were stratified into two cohorts based on body mass index (BMI) at the time of TIF: non-obese (BMI < 30 kg/m2) and class I obesity (BMI 30–34.9 kg/m2). Individuals were excluded if they had a BMI > 35 kg/m2, a major esophageal motility disorder, underwent concomitant hiatal hernia repair with TIF, or had a history of previous TIF. Supplementary Table 1 summarizes the used ICD-10 and CPT codes.

TIF procedure overview

The procedure is performed under general anesthesia using a specialized device (EsophyX; Merit Medical, South Jordan, UT) either as a standalone procedure (if minimal hernia is present) or intraoperatively after hiatal hernia repair. The procedure involves advancing the device over a flexible endoscope into the stomach, where the fundus is retracted and wrapped around the distal esophagus. Serosa-to-serosa plication is performed using Polypropylene H-shaped fasteners to create a partial, omega-shaped fundoplication of approximately 270 to 300 degrees, with approximately 3 cm length of narrowing. This repairs the anti-reflux barrier via several mechanisms including flap valve reconstruction, restoring the angle of His, creating adequate length of narrowing between gastric and esophageal compartments and augmenting the high-pressure zone at the lower esophageal sphincter.

Matching and covariate balance

To minimize confounding, one-to-one propensity score matching was performed between the two BMI cohorts. Matching variables were selected a priori and included age, sex, race, ethnicity, presence of diaphragmatic hernia, and proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use. Covariate balance was assessed post-matching to ensure comparability between groups.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were (1) postoperative new PPI recidivism, defined as any PPI prescription filled after TIF (excluding the first 3 months to account for routine postoperative PPI use). (2) Incidence of post-TIF esophagitis, identified through follow-up diagnostic coding. (3) Requirement for any subsequent foregut surgery, including fundoplication or bariatric surgery. These outcomes were evaluated at 6 months, 1 year, 2 years post-TIF as well as maximum follow-up time after the procedure. Secondary outcomes included reported side effects such as dysphagia and gas bloat within 6 months, 1 year, and 2-year post-TIF, as well as complications occurring within 30 days of the procedure (Supplementary Table 1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the TriNetX Live platform (TriNetX LLC, Cambridge, MA), which offers a web-based interface for real-time data analysis. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were described using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Statistical comparisons between groups were conducted using adjusted odds ratios (aOR) or risk differences (RD), each reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses accounted for the matched design and controlled for baseline covariates to assess differences in outcomes between the non-obese and class I obesity cohorts.

To perform propensity score matching, the TriNetX system employed logistic regression to estimate propensity scores, followed by a greedy nearest-neighbor matching algorithm with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations, an approach validated for achieving optimal cohort balance. To mitigate selection bias, the platform also randomized the order of data rows prior to matching. After matching, secondary outcomes were evaluated and expressed as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics

Of the 350 patients who underwent TIF, 235 had a BMI < 30 kg/m2 and 115 had a BMI of 30–35 kg/m2. After propensity score matching based on balanced demographics and clinical characteristics, 112 patients remained in each group. The mean age at TIF was 55.1 years (± 15.0) in the BMI < 30 kg/m2 group and 54.0 years (± 12.9) in the BMI 30–35 kg/m2 group. In the BMI < 30 cohort, 65 (58.0%) were female, 83 (74.1%) were White, and 78 (69.6%) were not Hispanic or Latino. A total of 24 (21.4%) had a history of esophagitis, and 59 (52.7%) had a diaphragmatic hernia, although size was not reported. In the BMI 30–35 cohort, 64 (57.1%) were female, 83 (74.1%) were White, and 75 (67.0%) were not Hispanic or Latino. A total of 23 (20.5%) had a history of esophagitis, and 60 (53.6%) had a diaphragmatic hernia, although size was not reported (Table 1). Regarding the need for subsequent foregut surgery, there were no significant differences in the rate of bariatric surgery between BMI groups at 6 months, 1 year, or 2 years (all p = 1.00). At maximum follow-up, however, 2 patients in the BMI 30–35 group underwent bariatric surgery versus none in the BMI < 30 group (RD + 1.8%, 95% CI –0.7 to 4.2; p = 0.498). Similarly, the requirement for fundoplication was comparable between groups at 6 months (aOR: 1.00, 95% CI 0.40–2.51), 1 year (aOR: 2.57, 95% CI 0.49–13.53), 2 years (aOR: 1.21, 95% CI 0.36–4.09), and maximum follow-up (aOR: 1.25, 95% CI 0.52–2.98). (Table 2).

Side effects

The incidence of dysphagia was not significantly different between groups across all time points: at 6 months (aOR: 0.596, 95% CI 0.29–1.22), 1 year (aOR: 0.574, 95% CI 0.29–1.14), 2 years (aOR: 0.732, 95% CI 0.39–1.37), and maximum follow-up (aOR: 0.849, 95% CI 0.48–1.49). Similarly, bloating rates did not differ significantly at 6 months (aOR: 0.454, 95% CI 0.19–1.06) or 2 years (aOR: 0.514, 95% CI 0.23–1.16). However, there was a statistically significant reduction in bloating in the BMI 30–35 group at 1 year (aOR: 0.420, 95% CI 0.18–0.95; p = 0.034) and at maximum follow-up (aOR: 0.446, 95% CI 0.21–0.97; p = 0.038).

Complications

Discussion

We used a prospective, US-based database of numerous healthcare organizations to compare post-TIF procedure outcomes in patients with BMI < 30 kg/m2 and class I obesity. In our study, we found no significant differences in postoperative PPI recidivism or incidence of post-TIF esophagitis. Furthermore, we found no significant differences in subsequent foregut or bariatric surgery in both BMI groups. Finally, we found no differences in dysphagia or complications but a lower incidence of gas bloat in the BMI 30–35 cohort.

Our findings regarding BMI and TIF clinical efficacy are consistent with the existing literature on endoscopic fundoplication, although the data are limited. For example, in a prospective multi-center registry of 158 patients that underwent TIF that excluded patients with a BMI > 35 kg/m2, 117 (74%) had a baseline BMI of < 30 kg/m2 and 41 (26%) had a BMI 30–35 kg/m2. Univariate analysis showed no difference (unadjusted OR 1.1, 95% CI [0.5–2.2]) for successful resolution of classic GERD symptoms between both BMI groups [14]. In their study, they defined success as ≥50% reduction in GERD Health-Related Quality of Life (GERD-HRQL) score or normalization of the Reflux Symptom Index (RSI) (≤13) and were completely off PPIs. Similarly, in a prospective multi-center study of 85 patients that also excluded patients with a BMI < 35 kg/m2, univariate analysis again did not show any difference in clinical success across different BMI categories [15]. They defined clinical success similarly as improvement in at least one subjective end point (GERD-HRQL, RSI) and at least one objective metric (PPI use, acid exposure time off antacid medication, healing of esophagitis). We also found no difference in a larger population-based study between both cohorts in objective metrics such as PPI recidivism and esophagitis rates. However, it should be noted that the follow-up periods for all three studies were short to medium-term. Surgical literature suggests that BMI effects may amplify over time. For example, one study [16] of 201 patients with a maximum BMI of 34.9 that underwent laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication (LNF) included 43 (21.4%) with normal weight, 89 (44.2%) overweight and 69 (34.4%) with class I obesity. With a mean follow-up period of 16.5 years (range 11–22), the obese group had higher rates of esophagitis (31/69 vs. 5/43 normal weight, p < 0.0001; 31/69 vs. 19/89 overweight, p < 0.001) and reflux recurrence defined as DeMeester score ≥14.7 (19/69 vs. 1/43 normal weight, p < 0.0001; 19/69 vs. 7/89, p < 0.001). All wrap failures were observed between 3 and 11 years postoperatively; these data suggest that fundoplication failure may be associated with BMI but may not be detected in the early or intermediate postoperative period.

Given that our maximum follow-up period was ~ 1000 days, it is unsurprising that efficacy and redo surgery rates were similar although there was a numerically higher number of bariatric surgeries in the class I obesity cohort. One can presume that longer follow-up, as shown by Shietroma et al. [16], would show higher rates of TIF failure in the higher BMI group and possibly higher redo surgery rates. It should be noted that Roux-en-Y gastric bypass after failed anti-reflux surgery has been studied and found to be effective albeit with higher perioperative complication rates [17]. Typical reasons for revisional bariatric surgery after LNF are obesity with fundoplication failure or recurrent gastroesophageal reflux disease. In the setting of obesity, increased intragastric pressure can stretch the wrap and eventually lead to a disruption of the surgically altered anatomy [18]. One notable finding was a lower incidence of bloating in the higher BMI cohort although we did not have information on the breakdown of types of fundoplication and it was possible the class I obesity cohort had a lower number of 360o Nissen fundoplication.

These data suggest that TIF maintains a comparable safety and efficacy profile across non-obese and class I obese cohorts in the short to medium-term. These findings may inform clinicians to adequately counsel patients with class I obesity to avoid weight gain and refer to nutrition and weight loss specialists early to avoid revisional bariatric surgery. The use of a multi-institutional database provides a representation of endoscopic fundoplication across diverse geographic regions in the United States. By employing propensity score matching for variables such as age and PPI use [14], we sought to minimize confounding and produce comparisons reflective of routine clinical practice.

However, several inherent limitations must be considered. First, we did not compare our findings with surgical fundoplication in the same database which limits the ability to contextualize these results. However, our findings are supported by other studies evaluating the effect of obesity on laparoscopic anti-reflux surgery. For example, in their study of 505 patients that underwent anti-reflux surgery, Winslow et al. [9] similarly found no differences in clinical success or anatomic failure between obese (of which 64% fell within the class I obesity range) and non-obese groups. Furthermore, the absence of objective physiologic testing and validated GERD scoring instruments limits our ability to compare patient-specific outcomes between both groups; instead we relied on proxy endpoints such as PPI recidivism and esophagitis coding which is an inherent limitation of a database study. Also, our database lacked procedural data which restricts our ability to evaluate technical factors that may influence outcomes such as hiatal anatomy or procedure technique. For example, it is known that an ‘optimized’ TIF 2.0 valve (defined as a > 300 degree circumference and > 3 cm-long flap valve) is associated with superior pH response [15]. Additionally, the reliance on administrative coding introduces a risk of misclassification and underreporting of procedures or complications. TriNetX cohort-level data may be susceptible to confounding and biases without patient-level validation [19]. Finally, it is important to consider uncaptured data due to loss to follow-up or care at difference facilities not included in the database.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated comparable safety and short to medium-term clinical outcomes in patients with class I obesity and non-obese patients. These findings should be interpreted as reflecting intermediate follow-up, and long-term durability remains unknown and may diverge over time. TIF remains a viable endoscopic anti-reflux procedure in patients with BMI < 35 kg/m2 but it should not be utilized in patients with a BMI > 35 kg/m2 until further specific studies are conducted.
