Oral microbiota characteristics in children younger than 3 years with febrile seizures: a prospective observational study
Authors: Nan Shen, Xiaoxue Shan, Lu Zheng, Jibing Qiao, Yazhou Jiang, Rang Wu, Tao Ning, Song Liu, Chang Dong, Jingjing Yan, Tiantian Lu, Suyue Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the oral microbiota of children younger than 3 years with febrile seizures (FS). Oral swab samples were collected from 48 children with FS and 47 healthy controls, and the V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was sequenced. The Shannon index did not differ significantly between groups, whereas the ACE index was significantly lower in the FS group. Although beta-diversity analysis based on weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances showed no significant between-group differences, partial least squares discriminant analysis provided an exploratory supervised visualization showing apparent separation between the FS and control groups. Among the key genera, Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Granulicatella were significantly enriched in the FS group, whereas Neisseria, Haemophilus, Porphyromonas, and Capnocytophaga were significantly depleted. Predicted functional analysis showed increased relative abundances of pathways related to translation, replication and repair, nucleotide metabolism, cell growth and death, and bacterial infectious diseases, whereas pathways related to energy metabolism, cell motility, signaling molecules and interaction, and transport and catabolism were reduced. These findings suggest that FS is associated with selective ecological reconfiguration of the early-life oral microbiota, mainly reflected by altered proportions of key resident genera.Keywords: Febrile seizures, Oral microbiota, 16S rRNA sequencing, Children, Key genera, Microbial ecology

Introduction

Febrile seizures (FS) are the most common type of seizure in early childhood, with a particularly high susceptibility during the first three years of life [1]. FS are typically triggered by febrile illnesses and usually resolve spontaneously; however, recurrent episodes may increase the risk of subsequent epilepsy or neurodevelopmental impairment [2–4]. Although previous studies have implicated genetic susceptibility, ion channel dysfunction, and inflammatory responses in the pathogenesis of FS, the precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood [5–7].

In recent years, the concept of the “microbiota–brain axis” has provided a novel perspective for elucidating the mechanisms underlying FS [8–10]. As one of the earliest sites of microbial colonization and an important reservoir for respiratory pathogens, the oral cavity is increasingly recognized to influence physiological and pathological processes in distant organs, including the brain, through multiple pathways, such as mucosal immune regulation, microbial translocation, and the dissemination of systemic inflammatory mediators [11, 12]. Accordingly, the oral microbiota may represent a potential intermediary linking upstream infectious stimuli to downstream abnormal neuronal excitability.

The first three years of life constitute a critical window for oral microbial colonization and succession, characterized by ecological immaturity, dynamic community remodeling, and limited microbial stability [13–15]. Based on this rationale, this study used high-throughput sequencing to profile the oral microbiota of children younger than 3 years with FS, focusing on microbial community structure, diversity, relative abundance, and key genera. These findings may provide preliminary microbiological evidence and a reference basis for further exploring the potential association between the oral microbiome and FS.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted at the Affiliated Suqian Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University from June 2024 to May 2025. Children diagnosed with FS during the study period were enrolled in the FS group, whereas healthy children who underwent routine health examinations at the same hospital during the same period were prospectively recruited as the control group.

The inclusion criteria for the FS group were as follows: (1) diagnosis of FS according to the Experts consensus of diagnosis, treatment and management of febrile seizures (2017 Practical version) formulated by the Subspecialty Group of Neurology, the Society of Pediatrics, Chinese Medical Association [16]; (2) clinical classification as simple FS; and (3) age younger than 3 years. The exclusion criteria were: (1) congenital genetic disorders, metabolic diseases, or clinically evident oral diseases; (2) concomitant bacteremia or other confirmed systemic bacterial infections; and (3) use of antibiotics or probiotics within 1 month before enrollment [17–19]. The same exclusion criteria were applied to the control group.

This study was designed as an exploratory prospective observational study. Because reliable prior estimates of effect sizes for oral microbiota profiles in children younger than 3 years with FS were unavailable, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. Eligible participants were consecutively enrolled during the study period, and the final sample size was determined by enrollment feasibility and sequencing quality control. A post hoc statistical sensitivity estimate was performed based on the final analytic sample size.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Suqian Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Approval No. 2024022). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants before enrollment.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Oral swab samples were collected in the hospital by trained personnel according to a standardized aseptic protocol [20]. In the FS group, oral swab samples were collected within 1 h after seizure cessation during the acute febrile illness. For the control group, samples were collected during routine health examinations. Before sampling, parents or legal guardians were asked to ensure that the children had not eaten or drunk for at least 30 min. After hand hygiene and routine disinfection, a sterile swab was inserted into the oral cavity and gently rubbed against the inner buccal mucosa with repeated vertical strokes and rotation for approximately 15 s. When necessary, a sterile tongue depressor was used to facilitate exposure. A second swab was collected from the contralateral buccal mucosa using the same procedure. The paired swabs from each participant were immediately transferred into a single sterile cryogenic tube and stored at -80 °C until subsequent processing.

Following completion of sample collection, genomic DNA was extracted using the BeaverBeads™ Saliva DNA Kit (Beaver Biomedical, Suzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA purity, concentration, and integrity were assessed by NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometry, Qubit 3.0 fluorometry, and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. Only samples with an A260/280 ratio of 1.8–2.0, a DNA concentration ≥ 100 ng/µL, and a clear predominant genomic DNA band on agarose gel electrophoresis were considered eligible for sequencing.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing

The V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 515 F (5′- GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), which were synthesized by BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China) [21].

For each sample, 50 ng of quality-controlled genomic DNA was used as the template for PCR amplification with the primer pair described above. PCR amplification was performed using 2× Phanta Max Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The resulting amplicons were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), eluted in elution buffer, and used for library preparation. The size distribution and concentration of the prepared libraries were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Quality-controlled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), with the sequencing strategy determined according to the insert size.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw sequencing reads were processed using a standardized quality-control pipeline. Primer and adapter sequences were first removed using Cutadapt v3.0. Low-quality reads were subsequently filtered using a 25-bp sliding-window strategy; reads were truncated at the position where the mean Phred quality score within the window fell below 20, and reads shorter than 50 bp after truncation were discarded. Reads containing ambiguous bases were removed, and low-complexity sequences were further excluded.

Paired-end reads were merged into full-length tags using FLASH v1.2.11, with a minimum overlap length of 10 bp and a maximum mismatch ratio of 0.2 within the overlapping region. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% sequence similarity using USEARCH v7.0.1090, and chimeric sequences were detected and removed using UCHIME v4.2.40 [22, 23]. Representative OTU sequences were taxonomically annotated using the RDP Classifier v2.14 with a confidence threshold of 0.6. Unclassified and non-bacterial OTUs were removed before downstream analyses.

Alpha diversity was analyzed in QIIME2 using the Shannon and ACE indices to evaluate within-sample microbial diversity and richness, respectively [24]. Beta diversity was assessed based on weighted and unweighted UniFrac distance matrices to quantify between-sample differences in microbial community composition [24]. In addition, partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed using the mixOmics package in R v3.2.1 to provide an exploratory supervised visualization of sample distribution patterns, with no cross-validation or independent overfitting assessment [24].

Based on OTU taxonomic annotation, the relative abundances of microbial taxa were calculated at the phylum, class, order, family, and genus levels. The top 10 genera by overall relative abundance were defined as key genera. Differentially enriched taxa between the FS and control groups were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), with the logarithmic linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score threshold set at 2.0 and no additional multiple-comparison correction, including false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment, applied in this analysis; discriminative taxa were further visualized using cladograms [25]. Putative microbial functional profiles were inferred using PICRUSt2 and annotated against Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways [26, 27].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R software v3.2.1. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. For predicted KEGG functional analysis, pathways were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction, and adjusted P < 0.05 was considered significant. For other analyses, two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

During the study period, 106 children were prospectively screened for eligibility. Eleven were excluded for the following reasons: complex FS (n = 2), age older than 3 years (n = 2), clinically evident oropharyngeal vesicular lesions (n = 3), and antibiotic or probiotic use within 1 month before enrollment (n = 4; FS group, n = 3; control group, n = 1). Finally, 95 children were included in the final analysis, including 48 in the FS group and 47 in the control group. The final analytic sample size provided 80% power at a two-sided α level of 0.05 to detect a moderate between-group difference of approximately Cohen’s d = 0.58. All participants were aged 6–36 months. The clinical diagnoses in the FS group were upper respiratory tract infection (n = 21, 43.8%), bronchitis or pneumonia (n = 9, 18.8%), influenza (n = 5, 10.4%), roseola infantum (n = 4, 8.3%), gastroenteritis (n = 2, 4.2%), urinary tract infection (n = 2, 4.2%), Kawasaki disease (n = 1, 2.1%), and fever without a clear focus (n = 4, 8.3%). As shown in Table 1, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in sex, age, body weight, number of erupted primary teeth, gestational age, mode of delivery, or early-life feeding pattern.

Diversity analysis

A total of 1,223 non-redundant OTUs were identified across the two groups. Of these, 823 OTUs were detected in the FS group and 893 in the control group, with 493 OTUs shared by both groups. In addition, 330 OTUs were unique to the FS group and 400 were unique to the control group (Fig. 1).

Distribution of oral microbial OTUs between the FS and control groups

Alpha diversity was assessed using the Shannon and ACE indices. The Shannon index was 2.68 (2.09, 3.08) in the FS group and 2.73 (2.25, 3.06) in the control group, with no statistically significant difference between groups (P = 0.479). The ACE index was lower in the FS group than in the control group, with values of 100.5 (65.0, 121.5) and 122.0 (100.0, 137.5), respectively, and the difference was statistically significant (P = 0.005) (Fig. 2).

Alpha diversity of the oral microbiota in the FS and control groups. A Shannon index; B ACE index

Beta diversity analysis showed that the unweighted UniFrac value was 0.37 (0.28, 0.45) in the FS group and 0.35 (0.29, 0.41) in the control group, with no significant between-group difference (P = 0.058). Similarly, the weighted UniFrac value did not differ significantly between the FS and control groups, with values of 0.23 (0.16, 0.30) and 0.23 (0.17, 0.30), respectively (P = 0.816) (Fig. 3).

PLS-DA was performed to visualize sample distribution patterns. The score plot showed an apparent separation between the FS and control groups in the supervised ordination space (Fig. 4).

Oral microbial community composition and relative abundance

At the phylum level, the FS and control groups showed broadly similar oral microbial profiles, mainly consisting of Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Actinomycetota, and Fusobacteriota. Bacillota was the predominant phylum in both groups, accounting for 59.57% in the FS group and 47.59% in the control group. The relative abundances of Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, and Fusobacteriota were 16.32%, 13.45%, 5.83%, and 3.89% in the FS group, and 15.58%, 25.62%, 5.88%, and 3.96% in the control group, respectively (Fig. 5A).

Relative abundance profiles of the oral microbiota in the FS and control groups. A At the phylum level; B At the genus level

At the genus level, the relative abundance profiles of the dominant genera were further analyzed. In the FS group, the top ten genera were Streptococcus (31.00%), Veillonella (24.03%), Prevotella (10.27%), Neisseria (8.78%), Haemophilus (3.00%), Rothia (2.71%), Leptotrichia (1.79%), Granulicatella (1.79%), Schaalia (1.58%), and Porphyromonas (1.20%). In contrast, the control group showed a different ranking of dominant genera, with Veillonella (23.92%), Streptococcus (20.21%), Neisseria (13.37%), Haemophilus (8.57%), Prevotella (5.30%), Porphyromonas (3.09%), Rothia (2.87%), Capnocytophaga (2.27%), Leptotrichia (2.16%), and Gemella (1.28%) comprising the top ten genera (Fig. 5B).

LEfSe analysis of differential oral microbial taxa

LEfSe analysis identified multiple taxa with significant differences between the FS and control groups. A total of 78 differentially abundant taxa were detected, including 4 phyla, 9 classes, 13 orders, 17 families, and 35 genera. Among them, 33 taxa were enriched in the FS group, including 2 phyla, 5 classes, 5 orders, 8 families, and 13 genera; 45 taxa were enriched in the control group, including 2 phyla, 4 classes, 8 orders, 9 families, and 22 genera (Fig. 6A).

Differential oral microbial taxa identified by LEfSe between the FS and control groups. A LDA score plot; B LEfSe cladogram

The LEfSe cladogram showed that the discriminative taxa were distributed across multiple phylogenetic levels. FS-enriched taxa were mainly concentrated in the Bacillota lineage, particularly along the Bacilli–Lactobacillales–Streptococcaceae branch. In contrast, control-enriched taxa were primarily distributed within Pseudomonadota- and Campylobacterota-related lineages, including several order- or family-level branches such as Neisseriales/Neisseriaceae, Pasteurellales/Pasteurellaceae, Flavobacteriales/Flavobacteriaceae, Porphyromonadaceae, and Campylobacterales/Campylobacteraceae (Fig. 6B).

Changes in key genera

The top 10 genera ranked by relative abundance were defined as key genera and compared between the FS and control groups. Among these genera, seven showed statistically significant between-group differences. Streptococcus (P = 0.012), Prevotella (P = 0.015), and Granulicatella (P = 0.001) were significantly more abundant in the FS group than in the control group. In contrast, Neisseria (P = 0.040), Haemophilus (P < 0.001), Porphyromonas (P = 0.004), and Capnocytophaga (P = 0.018) were significantly less abundant in the FS group. No statistically significant differences were observed in Veillonella, Rothia, or Leptotrichia between the two groups (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 7).

Predicted functional difference analysis of the oral microbiota

Predicted functional difference analysis identified 31 functional categories in the oral microbiota. Compared with the control group, the FS group showed higher predicted relative abundances of translation, cell growth and death, replication and repair, nucleotide metabolism, infectious diseases: bacterial, and metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides. In contrast, energy metabolism, folding, sorting and degradation, cell motility, immune diseases, endocrine system, signaling molecules and interaction, and transport and catabolism were lower in the FS group. No significant differences were observed in the remaining functional categories after FDR correction (Fig. 8).

Discussion

This study investigated the oral microbial characteristics of children younger than 3 years with FS. The FS group showed a significantly lower ACE index, whereas the Shannon index was comparable between groups. Since the ACE index mainly reflects community richness and is sensitive to less abundant taxa, while the Shannon index reflects community diversity, this pattern suggests reduced oral microbial richness without a broad disruption of community diversity [28, 29]. This interpretation is supported by the beta-diversity results: weighted UniFrac did not differ between groups, indicating that abundance-weighted community structure was largely preserved, whereas unweighted UniFrac showed a borderline trend (P = 0.058), consistent with changes in the presence or absence of less abundant lineages. The apparent separation observed in the PLS-DA plot was therefore interpreted as an exploratory supervised visualization rather than evidence of statistically significant between-group beta-diversity differences.

At the phylum level, both groups shared a typical pediatric oral microbial framework, mainly composed of Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Actinomycetota, and Fusobacteriota. Although the overall phylum-level profiles were broadly similar between groups, the FS group showed a higher relative abundance of Bacillota and a lower relative abundance of Pseudomonadota. LEfSe analysis further supported the presence of lineage-level microbial shifts. FS-enriched taxa were mainly concentrated within the Bacillota lineage, particularly along the Bacilli–Lactobacillales–Streptococcaceae branch, whereas control-enriched taxa were primarily distributed within Pseudomonadota- and Campylobacterota-related lineages. These results suggest that the microbial differences between the two groups were structured along broader phylogenetic lineages rather than being restricted to isolated taxa .

The genus-level findings provide the clearest biological signal. Streptococcus was the most abundant genus in the FS group and was significantly enriched compared with controls. This observation is broadly consistent with pediatric epilepsy gut microbiome studies reporting increased Streptococcus abundance, while emerging FS-related studies also suggest gut microbial dysbiosis in children with FS [10, 30, 31]. Although the oral cavity and gut represent different ecological niches and should not be directly equated, oral microbes can continuously seed the gastrointestinal tract, suggesting that the enrichment of Streptococcus observed in the oral cavity may be related to broader host–microbiota interactions rather than representing an isolated oral phenomenon [32–35]. FS is closely associated with fever-related inflammatory responses, and previous cytokine studies have reported higher cerebrospinal fluid IL-1β and serum IL-6 levels in children with FS [36]. In this context, streptococcal enrichment may reflect fever-related changes in the oral microbial environment or the inflammatory background accompanying FS. However, because inflammatory mediators were not directly measured in the present study, this interpretation should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a causal link between Streptococcus and seizure susceptibility.

The enrichment of Prevotella and Granulicatella in the FS group may also be consistent with an inflammation-responsive shift in the oral microbial ecosystem. Prevotella is a core anaerobic genus of the oral microbiome and has been linked in previous studies to mucosal immune activation, including epithelial production of IL-6, IL-8, and CCL20, as well as potential promotion of Th17-type responses and neutrophil recruitment [37, 38]. Granulicatella, a nutritionally variant streptococcus commonly found in the oral and upper respiratory tract microbiota, may also exhibit opportunistic pathogenic potential under specific host conditions [39–41]. However, the enrichment of these genera should not be interpreted as direct evidence of pathogenic infection or causal involvement in FS, because their biological effects are highly dependent on species, strain, niche, and host context [42, 43]. In the present study, the concurrent increase in Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Granulicatella may reflect a shift toward a more inflammation-responsive oral microbial configuration, rather than colonization by exogenous pathogens. This pattern may be relevant to the acute febrile and inflammatory context in which FS occurs.

Conversely, the decreased abundance of Neisseria, Haemophilus, Porphyromonas, and Capnocytophaga in the FS group indicates that FS-associated dysbiosis also involved the reduced representation of several resident oral taxa. Neisseria and Haemophilus are common members of the healthy pediatric oral microbiome, and Neisseria is among the genera associated with oral nitrate reduction, a pathway linked to nitric oxide bioavailability and oral ecological homeostasis [44–46]. Their reduction may therefore imply a weakening of specific commensal ecological functions within the oral microbiota. By contrast, Porphyromonas and Capnocytophaga, although also part of the normal pediatric oral community, accounted for relatively low proportions in both groups and should be interpreted more cautiously [47–50]. Rather than representing isolated genus-level changes, the concurrent depletion of these taxa, together with the enrichment of Streptococcus and Prevotella and the lower ACE index, supports an FS-associated ecological reconfiguration characterized by selective redistribution of resident oral taxa despite largely preserved overall community diversity.

The predicted functional analysis further suggested that FS-associated taxonomic shifts were accompanied by changes in the functional potential of the oral microbiota. Compared with controls, the FS group showed higher predicted abundances of pathways related to translation, replication and repair, nucleotide metabolism, cell growth and death, and bacterial infectious diseases, whereas pathways associated with energy metabolism, cell motility, signaling molecules and interaction, and transport and catabolism were reduced. These findings suggest that the oral microbiota in children with FS may differ not only in taxonomic composition but also in predicted community-level functional profiles. However, because these results were inferred from 16 S rRNA sequencing data, they should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require validation by metagenomic, transcriptomic, or metabolomic approaches [51–54].

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; it remains unclear whether oral microbial alterations precede FS, arise as a consequence of fever, or reflect host susceptibility. Second, the control group consisted of healthy children rather than febrile children without seizures, and the FS group included heterogeneous fever-triggering clinical diagnoses; thus, the observed microbial differences should be interpreted as FS-associated features in the context of acute febrile illness, rather than changes exclusively attributable to seizures themselves. Third, 16 S rRNA sequencing provides limited taxonomic resolution and cannot reliably distinguish species- or strain-level differences, which is particularly important for genera such as Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Haemophilus.

In conclusion, children under 3 years of age with FS exhibited an altered oral microbial profile characterized by reduced microbial richness, selective enrichment of Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Granulicatella, and decreased abundance of Neisseria, Haemophilus, Porphyromonas, and Capnocytophaga. These findings suggest that FS is associated with a selective ecological reconfiguration of the early-life oral microbiota, rather than a broad disruption of overall microbial diversity.
