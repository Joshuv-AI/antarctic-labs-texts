High-rate phase association with travel time neural fields
Authors: Cheng Shi, Giulio Poggiali, Chris Marone, Maarten V. de Hoop, Ivan Dokmanić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Earthquake science and seismology rely on phase association: grouping seismic arrivals recorded across multiple stations into the earthquakes that generated them. Modern deep-learning detectors now routinely identify many more small events than before, providing information about fault dynamics on increasingly fine spatiotemporal scales. But the resulting high-rate arrival sequences are increasingly difficult to associate, especially when local wave speeds are heterogeneous or poorly known. Here we introduce HARPA, a phase-association framework that represents observed and predicted arrival sequences as probability distributions and compares them using an optimal-transport metric. HARPA jointly estimates earthquake locations, origin times and a low-dimensional representation of the wave-speed field using travel-time neural fields, neural networks that map coordinates to travel times. On standard low-rate datasets with simple wave speed, HARPA performs comparably to state-of-the-art associators. At high rates and with laterally heterogeneous or unknown wave speed, HARPA outperforms existing methods. Our results show that adaptive travel-time modeling becomes important when seismicity is dense and suggest a route toward joint association and passive-source tomography from unassociated arrivals.

Introduction

Seismic signals recorded by dense networks are a primary way to study earthquakes and the Earth’s interior. In standard processing workflows, algorithms first identify wave arrivals in each seismogram, including their arrival times (picks) and wave types (phases). The next step, phase association, groups picks recorded at different stations into the earthquakes that generated them.

Recent breakthroughs in deep-learning-based seismic processing enable reliable detection of small-magnitude events1–3, with ongoing developments promising to reduce detection thresholds even further4. This has the potential to reveal finer detail about fault-zone elastic properties and earthquake dynamics at increasingly fine spatiotemporal scales. At the same time, realizing this potential is increasingly challenging for phase association. Current techniques are designed for lower-rate, larger-magnitude events that occur within homogeneous crust or regions with simple variations in seismic wave speed. In accord with the Gutenberg–Richter law, as magnitude detection thresholds decrease the number of events increases 10-fold, and the corresponding high arrival rates pose unique combinatorial challenges for phase association5. Following standard seismological usage, we use the terms arrivals, picks, and phases interchangeably when the distinction is not essential; similarly, we use earthquake, source, and event interchangeably.

Association errors caused by overly simplistic wave-speed models are minimal when earthquake rates are low but become critical at high event rates. Although existing algorithms can incorporate more complex wave-speed parameterizations for travel-time computation7, the true model is rarely known with sufficient accuracy. To address these issues, we propose HARPA—a framework for high-rate seismic phase association that combines learned travel-time modeling, optimal transport, and stochastic optimization. During inference, HARPA jointly estimates earthquake locations, origin times, and a low-dimensional representation of the wave-speed field by matching predicted and observed arrival-time distributions. Once these continuous parameters are estimated, assigning individual picks to events reduces to a simple linear assignment problem. This avoids repeated event-by-event combinatorial search during inversion.

Unknown wave speed is handled through a learned low-dimensional prior. We train an autoencoder on plausible wave speed models and couple its latent representation to a coordinate-based neural field8–10 that predicts travel times from source–receiver coordinates and latent codes. Following terminology common in the inverse-problems literature11, we refer to the decoder as a generative model, but our goal is not to generate arbitrary synthetic datasets or to learn a full probability distribution over latent codes. Rather, the decoder provides a structured family of plausible wave-speed models over which HARPA optimizes during association. Arrival data at each station are represented as arrival-time distributions, and association is posed as optimal-transport matching between observed and predicted distributions. The overall workflow is summarized in Fig. 2 and detailed in the “Methods”. As we show in “Results” and in the Supplementary Information (SI), selecting the best wave speed from this learned family makes the association highly robust, even when the true wave speed differs from those seen during training. Because HARPA estimates event parameters together with a low-dimensional representation of wave speed from overlapping arrivals, the resulting inference problem is non-convex. We regularize the search by restricting wave speed to a learned low-dimensional family and use stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD) during inference; full optimization details are given in the “Methods”.Fig. 2Schematic of the HARPA workflow.a shows the inference stage. Over the last decade, there has been increasing interest in the application of machine learning in seismology, for example, in seismic denoising12, phase picking1–3,13, travel-time computation14,15, and wave speed “inversion”16. Machine learning approaches to phase association use either probabilistic7,17,18 or graph models19,20. Some algorithms use fast phase grid search or grid division approaches21,22. However, when arrivals are highly scrambled, these approaches become either exceedingly slow or inaccurate. Some of the methods adopt a homogeneous or a depth-only dependent wave speed model18, while others incorporate wave speed information indirectly through supervised learning. In the latter case, the association with a specific wave speed can be learned by fitting the association labels of synthetic events whose arrivals are generated based on the same (approximate) wave speed model17,20. Since supervised approaches do not explicitly leverage wave physics, they demand large quantities of data for training. Implicit representations have been recently used for association with more complex but fixed wave speed models7,14.

But the fundamental challenge is that the wave speed model is not known, certainly not at the resolution required to effectively process data from high-rate seismicity. Furthermore, the event-by-event association paradigm common to all existing methods becomes ineffective at higher seismicity rates even when the wave speed is fixed and simple, e.g. volcano monitoring. We show that rethinking the association problem in terms of probability distributions of arrivals enables a more effective, implicit utilization of wave propagation models from the outset. We developed HARPA using a travel time neural field, which is a neural network that takes coordinates as input and produces a continuous function as output. In this approach, the network learns a continuous 4D field of travel times rather than precomputed travel-time tables, grid search, or explicit seismic wave speed models.

There are also recent studies that directly locate events from continuous waveforms, thereby bypassing the phase association problem23–25. These methods, based on so-called migration in wave speed models—which are assumed known—identify events by searching for peaks in stacked back-projections. Although this avoids dealing with disordered arrivals, it is complicated by waveform inaccuracies and heterogeneous magnitudes, particularly when arrivals become dense. As a result, these algorithms are applied to settings with tens of events per day. Our work rather aims at operating with tens of events per minute (epm).

If earthquake source locations, occurrence times, and wave speeds were all known, the phase association problem could be formulated as a linear sum assignment problem (LSAP), which is effectively solved by the Hungarian algorithm26. If only the occurrence times are unknown, the problem is related to a quadratic assignment problem (QAP)27,28, which is much harder to solve (it belongs to the class of NP-hard problems). While QAPs can be solved in certain cases using heuristics and meta-heuristics29,30, there has been growing interest in applying machine learning to hard combinatorial problems31–33. However, in seismology the situation is often even more complex: neither the wave speed nor the spatiotemporal earthquake source locations are known, although sometimes a rather coarse wave speed model is available. These unknown degrees of freedom result in a mixed-integer programming problem which is harder than LSAP or QAP and challenging to solve even with a small number of earthquakes and stations34–36.

The joint localization–wave speed recovery problem has a unique solution under certain conditions37, but there are no reliable algorithms that solve it provably and efficiently. There exist algorithms that attempt joint full waveform (or sometimes travel time) inversion and wave speed model refinement but with temporally well-separated sources, thus implicitly avoiding the association problem (but not the ill-posedness)38–40. In the context of micro-seismicity, simple parametric models41 and Bayesian priors42 were used for regularization. In our case, the prior implicit in the generative model ensures a stable reconstruction. We note that our strategy of converting combinatorial data into measures—in order to avoid combinatorial optimization—is reminiscent of earlier work on unassigned Euclidean distance data43,44 with applications in genomics and room acoustics.

In this work, we show that phase association can be made robust at high arrival rates—even when the wave-speed model is unknown or uncertain—by combining distributional arrival-time matching with learned wave-speed adaptation. Indeed, across synthetic, semi-synthetic, and real seismic settings, HARPA accurately associates arrivals at event rates where the performance of existing methods deteriorates, while remaining robust to unknown wave-speed models.

Field data, low-to-moderate rate regime and fixed wave speed model

We first apply HARPA to data from two major earthquakes: the Mw 8.2 Iquique Chile earthquake of 1 April 2014, which was preceded by a Mw 7.6 foreshock on 16 March 2014; and the Mw 7.1 Ridgecrest earthquake of 5 July 2019, which was preceded by Mw 6.4 and Mw 5.4 events on 4 July 2019. For the Iquique earthquake, we use data for a 1-month period starting on 15 March 2014 (Fig. 3). For the Ridgecrest sequence, we use data for a 7 h period starting at 17:00 on 4 July 2019 (Fig. SI14 in SI). In the Chile dataset, the arrival phases (both P and S) are identified from continuous waveforms using PhaseNet3, following the same settings as in GaMMA18. In this experiment, the typical event rate is about 0.3 epm. Our experiments show that in this low-to-moderate rate regime, where a simple, fixed wave speed is a good model, HARPA matches the performance of SOTA algorithms GaMMA18 and PyOcto22. We obtain similar results on the Ridgecrest dataset with the picks obtained by PhaseNet; here the typical rate is about 3 epm (with a maximum of 7 during that week). HARPA again matches the SOTA performance of GaMMA and PyOcto.

We now move to the challenging regime of high-rate arrivals and unknown wave speed models. One seemingly straightforward way to test phase associators in this case would be to lower the detection magnitude threshold in PhaseNet. The problem with this strategy is that it may include a large number of false picks because PhaseNet is not adapted to the low signal-to-noise ratio arrivals of the smaller events that would dominate in this case according to the Gutenberg–Richter (GR) law. When sufficiently dense, the new picks resulting from a lower detection magnitude threshold in PhaseNet can masquerade as false events. These events would pass all filtering criteria and are geometrically consistent with wave speed geodesics and thus cannot be eliminated solely at the association stage. Thus, while the detection magnitude threshold in PhaseNet is complex, we nonetheless include such an experiment in the SI while noting the important caveats. We note that although there is no ground truth for the picks in this scenario, the number of events detected reflects the algorithm’s ability to find geometrically correct associations between picks and sources. In this regime, HARPA detects more events than GaMMA and PyOcto in SI Fig. SI14. For transparency, we also include a magnitude test that indeed shows that the detected “events” do not have the expected GR magnitude distribution.

We emphasize that the comparisons in Figs. 3 and SI14 in the SI are still for a fixed, homogeneous wave speed model, which is a reasonable approximation in Ridgecrest and a setting that GaMMA and PyOcto were optimized for. We used the same wave speed for all algorithms: 7.0 km/s for the P wave and 4.0 km/s for the S wave in Chile, and 6 km/s for the P wave and 3.53 km/s for the S wave in Ridgecrest. GaMMA and PyOcto support a depth-only dependent wave speed model, but it has a minimal effect on their performance. This is understandable in Ridgecrest, where events happen at shallow depths, but it is also true in Chile, as the depth-only dependent model for P-wave velocities, ranging from 5.8 km/s at sea level to 8.6 km/s at 300 km below sea level, does not identify more events than the constant wave speed model in this regime22.

In order to corroborate these findings, we next design a robust set of experiments with processed field data where we have access to ground truth associations (cf. Fig. 1), which is not the case when we lower the detection threshold in Fig. SI14. We first remain in the fixed wave speed setting and then move to complex, variable backgrounds. These experiments demonstrate HARPA’s advantage in high event-rate settings of complex seismic wave speed.

High-rate experiments with densified field data

Recall that the typical rates in Chile and Ridgecrest data are 0.3 epm (cf. Fig. 1(a)) and 3 epm (cf. Fig. 1(b)). Although in Ridgecrest arrivals begin to mildly overlap at 3 epm, they remain distinguishable even by naked eye. The primary association challenge in this regime is thus to identify false and missing picks. No phase association algorithms were applied at rates higher than those reported in the 2019 Ridgecrest data in GaMMA and Neuma publications7,18. (We focus on rate but note that factors like event depth and wave speed also modulate the problem difficulty).

Indeed, we are not aware of existing curated benchmarks at higher rates, but both the Gutenberg–Richter law and augmented catalogs using template matching techniques (e.g., ref. 47) suggest that a wealth of small-magnitude events exist that are not included in common seismic catalogs. This is due not only to the technical difficulty of detecting such low-SNR events, but also to the density of the recording network and the quality-control requirements in the downstream seismic location pipeline. Nevertheless, this landscape is rapidly evolving. We anticipate that phase association and location will soon benefit from higher detection rates enabled by the latest AI-driven seismic detection tools, such as SeisLM4 or48, and seismic denoising methods12,49, coupled with an increase in the spatial density of instrumentation.

With this in mind, we designed another test by manually creating high-rate seismograms using data from the 2019 Ridgecrest sequence. To do this we extracted a time window in which ground truth events and their associated picks are available in an existing catalog, at a detection threshold sufficiently high that they can be considered ground truth (Fig. 1). We then advanced or delayed occurrence times of events by consistently adjusting arrival times between stations. This adjustment leaves the wave travel paths intact, resulting in highly-realistic high-rate data. The same strategy can also be applied to seismograms; we show this in the SI.

As seen in Fig. 1(c), when the rate increases to 30 epm, arrivals mix significantly and association is markedly more complex. All algorithms perform similarly at rates below 3 epm. However, as the rate increases and the association problem becomes more challenging, only HARPA remains effective. We emphasize that unlike grid-search algorithms which become impractically (exponentially) slow in high-rate regimes with a large number of arrivals, the runtime of HARPA, with the travel time neural field that casts phase arrivals as a probability distribution, is minimally affected by event frequency; a detailed comparison is given in the SI.

Unknown wave speed model: experiments with processed field data, synthetic data, and low- to high-rate regimes

In low-rate settings with simple wave speed models (Fig. 1(a) or the second row in Fig. 5), the accuracy of the wave speed model has a limited impact on association performance. But in the high-rate regime (Fig. 1(c)), a more accurate wave speed model becomes essential. We designed three experiments to show this: one with re-processed field data from Ridgecrest where we programmatically introduce a wave speed discrepancy, while keeping the source geometry and relative arrival data consistent with real data, one with synthetic inhomogeneous wave speed models (with noiseless P-arrivals), and one with a random-field wave speed model which also shows that HARPA effectively does non-linear tomography. For this simple wave speed heterogeneity, it is mathematically simple to adjust arrival times while keeping the source geometry and all other problem parameters intact.

Ridgecrest with across-fault wave speed contrast

In Fig. 4, we present an experiment based on the 2019 Ridgecrest sequence that highlights the complexity of seismic phase association in areas of complex seismic wave speed. We divided the crust into two areas with different P-wave velocities and studied seismic wave speed contrasts ranging from 6/6 km/s to 3/9 km/s. Note that these are not standard one-dimensional wave speed models. The data cannot be fit with a depth-only wave speed model, such as those used in typical phase-association methods. In fact, HARPA introduces the idea of adaptive wave speed modeling. That is, HARPA treats the wave-speed (or, equivalently, travel-time) field itself as an unknown to be inferred from the data, rather than a fixed input. The results of Fig. 4 show that HARPA adapts well to such complex structures.

We tested three variants of HARPA: 0D (a fixed, constant 6 km/s wave speed that is equivalent to the baseline models), IND (adaptive in-distribution, in which the model is trained on a wave speed model that is the same as that for testing), and OOD (adaptive, trained on out-of-distribution models with Gaussian perturbations, very different from the simple halfspace contrast used for testing). By out-of-distribution, we mean outside the training distribution used to fit our wave speed parameterization. It effectively means outside the range of the wave speed model. The Baseline algorithms use the same constant wave speed assumptions as HARPA OD—that is, no depth dependence of wave speed. We simulate events at 3 epm and evaluate the results under a stricter timing tolerance of 1 s. As shown in the figure, HARPA consistently outperforms all baselines when the wave speed contrast increases. This confirms that adaptive wave speed modeling is crucial even in lower-rate regimes, while constant or low-dimensional approximations rapidly degrade under heterogeneous conditions. The IND model unsurprisingly performs best, but the OOD model still performs strongly at high contrasts. We emphasize again that the OOD model was deliberately trained on wave speeds very different from the test model in order to underscore HARPA’s robustness to wave speed model mismatch. In practice, the generative training set should be designed to cover a wide range of plausible wave speeds that includes the expected test scenarios.

Ellipsoidal perturbations

Figure 5 shows an experiment with synthetic wave speed models created by augmenting a vertical gradient wave speed (the first panel) with smooth ellipsoidal perturbations. The top row shows four wave speed models from the training distribution. We compare HARPA’s performance with GaMMA and PyOcto for both constant (OD) and 1D models. Importantly, as above, we also run an out-of-distribution experiment (see SI) where we compare an instance of HARPA trained on Gaussian random fields that are qualitatively very different from the wave speeds we test on.

Comparing the results at different rates clearly shows that in the high-rate regime, neither a constant nor a 1D approximation used in existing algorithms suffices for effective association. This highlights the need for better, adaptive wave speed modeling. We emphasize that the out-of-distribution model still significantly outperforms a constant wave speed instance of HARPA (and all baseline algorithms) in high-rate regimes, which further highlights the importance of wave speed adaptivity. High-resolution differentiable parametrizations of wave speed models such as those employed by Neuma7 (and also here) facilitate implementation, but the key advantage of HARPA is that it handles unknown wave speed models. The fact that at higher rates all instances of HARPA significantly outperform all baselines points to the importance of combining wave speed adaptivity with probabilistic modeling and stochastic optimization.

Toward seismic tomography with unassociated arrivals

The experiment in Fig. 6 shows that HARPA can also serve as an alternative approach to seismic tomography from unknown sources, directly from unassociated phase picks. The ability to reconstruct complex wave speed models from unknown sources is a challenging problem in seismic tomography37 and existing approaches typically require well-separated sources and/or accurate initial source locations. To further underline this point, we present a preliminary result on the 2014 Chile dataset. As argued above, the arrival rates in that dataset are low enough that existing methods can effectively associate the picks even with a simple, constant wave speed model. However, when we nonetheless let HARPA optimize the wave speed model, we recover a high-speed anomaly at depths >200 km, as shown in Fig. 7. Comparing this to the known literature suggests that HARPA recovers part of the subducting slab of the oceanic plate50; the anomaly appears consistently over multiple runs of the model. Discussion

Existing seismic phase-association algorithms work well in the low-rate regime, where arrivals from different earthquakes are sufficiently separated in time and a simple wave speed model is adequate18. In that setting, the main practical difficulty is filtering spurious or missing picks. Our goal here was not to claim a universal advantage in that already well-served regime, but to address a different one: dense seismicity in which arrivals overlap strongly and the travel-time model itself becomes limiting.

The results show that two ingredients matter in this regime. First, it is useful to represent the arrival times at each station as distributions rather than to enforce event-by-event matching throughout the inversion. This converts a difficult combinatorial search into a continuous inverse problem for source parameters followed by a final linear assignment step. Second, it is useful to adapt the travel-time model during association, rather than treating wave speed as a fixed input. The Ridgecrest experiments and the synthetic heterogeneous models both show that this adaptive treatment becomes important once wave-speed complexity and event rate are high enough.

More abstractly, this is a mixed discrete–continuous inverse problem. If source locations, origin times, and wave-speed model were all known, association would reduce to a linear sum assignment problem; if only the occurrence times were unknown, it would be closer to a quadratic assignment problem; with both source parameters and wave speed unknown, the problem becomes a mixed-integer nonlinear inverse problem26–28,34,36. Results on wave-speed recovery from unknown sources under suitable conditions show that uniqueness is possible in principle37, but practical recovery from finite, noisy observations remains difficult. HARPA does not resolve that theory in full generality. Rather, it makes the joint problem tractable by lifting arrivals to distributions, restricting wave speed to a learned low-dimensional family, and postponing the final discrete assignment until after the continuous inversion. In this sense, it is also related in spirit to inverse problems with unassigned data, where one works with distributions or unlabeled observations rather than explicit correspondences43,44.

This perspective also clarifies that HARPA is not only an associator but also a framework for joint source and background inference from unassociated arrivals. This is why the preliminary Chile result is relevant. Although the Chile sequence is not a setting in which existing associators fail, it provides a real-data illustration that the same inversion can recover large-scale structural information, suggesting a route from phase association toward passive-source tomography37,50. More broadly, the approximate locations and wave-speed estimates produced by HARPA could be used as warm starts for iterative travel-time or waveform inversion, and perhaps eventually as the basis of a passive-source tomographic workflow when the sources are not known in advance.

At the same time, there are clear limitations. First, the highest-rate real-data regime is presently constrained by the quality of the phase picks themselves. When picks become highly dense, waveform-level information or denser instrumentation may still be needed to distinguish real small events from geometrically consistent false associations. Second, the tomography result is deliberately presented as a proof of principle rather than a finished structural interpretation. More targeted validation against regional wave speed models and independent catalogs will be needed in future work.

With these caveats, the present results establish a practical and conceptual point: when seismicity is dense and wave-speed structure is complex or uncertain, association and wave speed modeling should not be treated as separate problems. HARPA provides one way to couple them while remaining computationally tractable. It performs competitively on standard low-rate catalogs and offers a clear advantage once overlap and structural complexity increase.

Generative modeling of wave speeds by neural fields

Solving (8) requires repeated evaluations of travel times Tc(s; r) across many candidate wave-speed models c. Obtaining these travel times via direct numerical solves at each iteration becomes prohibitively expensive. This is compounded by the fact that a direct joint (source and wave speed) inversion over unconstrained, high-dimensional wave speed fields is an ill-posed and computationally challenging problem. In HARPA, we leverage the fact that physically plausible wave-speed models, and hence their induced travel times, admit a low-dimensional latent structure. We therefore (i) parameterize wave speeds with an autoencoder-based generative model and (ii) represent travel times with a continuous, coordinate-based neural field8–10.

Training travel-time neural fields

The main computational bottleneck in solving (10) is the evaluation (and differentiation) of traveling time T(s, z; r). Grid- or mesh-based numerical solvers are inconvenient for continuous optimization over source locations s, and they are costly to differentiate with respect to both s and the latent code z.

In HARPA, after obtaining a low-dimensional latent space of complex wave speed models in the first step, we train a coordinate-based neural field8,9 to approximate travel times. Instead of training a single neural field for a single wave speed, we train for many wave speeds at once, and use the latent code z as additional input coordinates in addition to the spatial coordinates. Ground-truth travel times for training are computed using the fast marching method as implemented in scikit-fmm52. We use a three-layer deep network Tθ (with parameters θ) equipped with periodic activations8 so that both travel times and their derivatives with respect to s and z can be efficiently evaluated at continuous coordinates. The learned wave-speed representation shows strong robustness across event distributions, station geometries, and plausible velocity ranges. Models trained on P-wave velocities can be efficiently fine-tuned for S-wave prediction or adapted to new regions, avoiding the cost of training from scratch. A practical workflow can start from a pre-trained travel-time model and fine-tune it using synthetic events or a modest local catalog. The framework requires only basic station metadata, consistent picking, and coarse wave-speed bounds, and remains modular so that individual components can be upgraded independently. Future extensions may further decouple spatial and wave speed representations to improve pretraining and transferability without altering the core framework.
