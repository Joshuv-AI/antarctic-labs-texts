Cannabidiol in Anorexia Nervosa: A Double‐Blind Randomized Placebo Controlled Pilot Study to Test Safety, Pharmacokinetics, and Symptom Change
Authors: Neha Sahota, David J Grelotti, Tyler Nguyen, Walter H Kaye, Skylar Swindle, Raymond Suhandynata, Spencer Seely, Megan E Shott, Guido K W Frank
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAnorexia nervosa (AN) is a severe psychiatric disorder marked by an intense fear of gaining weight and persistent body dissatisfaction, both during periods of underweight and after weight restoration. The endocannabinoid system may offer therapeutic benefits, particularly in reducing anxiety. This randomized controlled trial of cannabidiol (CBD) investigated safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and symptomatic improvement in AN.MethodIn a double‐blind design, women with AN or Atypical AN were randomized to receive CBD (n = 16) or placebo (n = 16) over 21 days. The dose was up‐titrated weekly from 1.25 mg/kg twice daily to a maximum of 6.25 mg/kg twice daily, while assessing CBD and metabolite levels, liver function, and severity of eating disorder, depression, and anxiety symptoms.ResultsAge at baseline was similar between the CBD and placebo group (22.9 ± 2.8 years vs. 22.5 ± 3.5 years), as was body mass index (BMI, kg/m 2 , 20.1 ± 2.5 vs. 19.4 ± 1.8). CBD demonstrated the expected pharmacokinetics with limited and nonserious adverse events. Repeated measures MANCOVA indicated a small but significant group‐by‐time interaction for BMI increase in favor of CBD (F = 3.039, p = 0.046, partial η 2 = 0.252). Effect sizes for improvements in shape concern and perception of lack of control over eating were also large (partial η 2 > 0.14), favoring CBD but nonsignificant.DiscussionThis study suggests that CBD is well tolerated in individuals with AN. Furthermore, indication of better weight recovery and improvement of eating disorder specific symptoms in the CBD group suggest treatment effects of CBD in AN. The study was, however, constrained by its small sample size and limited duration and requires replication in a larger sample.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04878627Keywords: anorexia nervosa, cannabidiol, cannabis, CBD, psychopharmacology, treatment, weight gain

Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a severe psychiatric illness associated with food restriction and emaciation, an intense fear of gaining weight or becoming fat, and a disturbance in how one's body weight or shape is experienced (American Psychiatric Association 2022). AN is associated with a high disease burden, high treatment costs (Khalsa et al. 2017; Hay et al. 2017), and excessive mortality rates (Golden 2003; Arcelus et al. 2011; Sullivan 1995). Efforts to find effective pharmacological treatments for AN have achieved limited success thus far, with olanzapine showing a small weight increase but poor adherence and no impact on psychopathology (Attia et al. 2019). Additionally, current recommendations support that the role of pharmacology in eating disorders is largely to treat comorbid conditions (Himmerich et al. 2023). However, new pharmacological targets have shown some promise in psychiatric disorders and could also hold promise to improve treatment outcome in AN (Kirchberg et al. 2024; Rodriguez and Zorumski 2024).

One such target is the endocannabinoid system, which is comprised of the cannabinoid 1 and 2 receptors (CB1R and CB2R, respectively), endogenous ligands such as anandamide (N‐arachidonoyl‐ethanolamide [AEA]) and 2‐arachidonoyl glycerol (2‐AG), and enzymes for ligand biosynthesis and inactivation (Kirkland et al. 2022). Endogenous cannabinoids (endocannabinoids) are involved in the regulation of appetite, food intake, and energy balance (Pagotto et al. 2006). Exogenous cannabinoids derived from marijuana or hemp or made synthetically may also have activity at cannabinoid receptors or other receptor systems, and a few are approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for specific clinical conditions. For example, dronabinol, a synthetic delta‐9‐tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), has been approved for AIDS‐related weight loss and chemotherapy‐associated nausea and vomiting (Frank 2020). Additionally, an oral solution (Epidiolex) containing purified cannabidiol (CBD) derived from the cannabis plant is FDA‐approved for the treatment of seizures associated with Dravet and Lennox–Gastaut syndrome and tuberous sclerosis complex (Lattanzi et al. 2019).

CBD is a particularly interesting cannabinoid. Unlike THC, which is a partial agonist of cannabinoid receptors, CBD is believed to be a CB1R antagonist, facilitating endocannabinoid signaling by inhibiting the cellular uptake and enzymatic hydrolysis of endocannabinoids. It also binds to serotonin 1A (5HT1A) receptors, a mechanism that might explain its anxiolytic properties. CBD also modulates striatal mitochondrial lipid metabolism (Rong et al. 2017; Bisogno et al. 2001; Gomes, Casarotto, et al. 2011; Ferland et al. 2025; Franzen et al. 2023). There is some in vitro evidence suggesting CBD is associated with the induction of brown cell genes in adipocytes, which suggests that it may have distinct metabolic effects (Parray and Yun 2016). Furthermore, the effects of CBD appear to be dose dependent, with research showing that it follows an inverse U‐shaped dose–response curve (Zuardi et al. 2017).

Several studies suggested that the endocannabinoid system could be relevant to the pathophysiology of AN. Rodent models simulating AN using the activity‐based anorexia (ABA) model have demonstrated a decrease in 2‐AG and subsequent upregulation of the CB1R receptor, suggesting that endocannabinoid regulation may adapt to food restriction in AN (Collu et al. 2019). Validation of this theory in humans was provided when brain imaging studies revealed an increase in CB1R availability in individuals with AN, as well as elevated levels of CB1R mRNA (Gerard et al. 2011). In rodents, the availability of CB1R receptor binding was inversely associated with the fat cell‐derived hormone leptin and BMI, which may explain a potential mechanistic link between body weight regulation and the endocannabinoid system (Thanos et al. 2008). Clinical studies in AN found elevated blood levels of the endogenous ligand AEA, elevated levels of CB1R mRNA, and the aforementioned increase in CB1R availability using functional brain imaging (Gerard et al. 2011). Furthermore, a polymorphism of the fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) gene, an enzyme necessary for the breakdown of AEA, has been reported in a Japanese population with AN (Ando et al. 2014). However, other studies in AN have linked genetic variations in cannabinoid system gene allele frequency to associated comorbid psychiatric conditions, but not to the eating disorder diagnosis itself (Gonzalez et al. 2021).

Some research connected cannabinoids and the endocannabinoid system with specific behaviors that are relevant to AN. Animal studies found that CBD decreased fear expression while enhancing fear extinction, and disrupted fear memory reconsolidation (Jurkus et al. 2016; Franzen et al. 2022). Those results could have important therapeutic implications for AN, given its association with persistent fear‐based avoidance of food and body‐related stimuli, and conditioned fear response to high‐calorie food stimuli predicting long‐term body weight (Christian et al. 2025; Spix and Jansen 2025; Weider et al. 2024; Gorrell et al. 2024; Simonazzi et al. 2023). Furthermore, CBR1 stimulation has been show to enhance reversal learning, which could improve cognitive flexibility in AN (Hill et al. 2005; Tchanturia et al. 2004). For CBD to be effective, it may need to be applied over more extended periods of time, though. Studies in humans showed that a single application of CBD did not affect anxiety in a social anxiety paradigm; at the same time, CBD significantly reduced social anxiety symptoms versus placebo in a study that applied CBD over 4 weeks (Leen‐Feldner et al. 2025; Masataka 2019). Mechanistically, those effects appear to be exerted involving striatal mitochondrial lipid metabolism and hippocampal serotonin receptors (Ferland et al. 2025; Franzen et al. 2023). In summary, there is growing evidence that CBD could be an effective therapeutic agent to treat conditioned fear and anxiety and thus could be effective in reducing conditioned food and body‐related fears in AN.

Previous attempts at pharmacological manipulation of the endocannabinoid system as a treatment for AN using THC or its synthetic formulation, dronabinol, agonists at the CB1 receptor, have achieved varying levels of success (Rogers and Pacanowski 2023). A randomized controlled trial using dronabinol in a small sample of patients with AN revealed a small but significant weight gain over 4 weeks, without any concomitant improvement in ED psychopathology (Andries et al. 2014). Another double‐blind small study that focused on delta‐9‐THC in AN found no significant improvements and the compound elicited dysphoric reactions in three study participants (Gross et al. 1983). The addictive and anxiogenic effects of THC may be a cause of concern for use in the AN population that is already documented to have higher baseline anxiety levels (Sharpe et al. 2020). To date, there are no clinical trials that examined the effect of CBD in individuals with AN.

The primary aim of this placebo‐controlled, randomized, double‐blind, pilot study was to establish the safety and tolerability of CBD for adults with AN. We predicted that CBD would be well‐tolerated and not associated with weight loss or other signs of relapse. Our secondary aim was to examine the pharmacokinetics of CBD in adults with AN. Finally, we wanted to test the effects of CBD on symptomatology in AN. We hypothesized that CBD, relative to placebo, would decrease body and eating‐related anxiety levels and be associated with increased weight.

Method

Study Participants

Study participants were women, aged 18–40 and were recruited from the UC San Diego Health Eating Disorders Center for Treatment and Research or the community, study data collection started on April 15, 2022, and ended on December 31, 2024. To be eligible, participants must have met at the time of study DSM‐5 criteria for restricting type AN or OSFED Atypical AN and have had a duration of illness ≥ 6 months. Participants with AN had to be underweight at the time of study entry (BMI < 18.5), and individuals with Atypical AN had to be within normal BMI range but had to have had a weight loss of at least 5% as part of their disorder (Forney et al. 2017), and have been medically stable as assessed by a comprehensive medical evaluation, including laboratory assessment and EKG. Participants in IOP or PHP programs were permitted to continue participating in the program during the trial. Exclusion criteria were psychotic or other mental illness requiring inpatient hospitalization, current dependence on drugs or alcohol or use of THC or CBD, physical conditions (e.g., diabetes mellitus, pregnancy) known to influence eating or weight or liver disease which could have affected pharmacokinetics of the study drug, changes in medication in the prior month, expression of acute suicidality, and previous hypersensitivity to CBD (Epidiolex) or any of its constituents.

Written informed consent was obtained before participation, and the University of California San Diego Institutional Review Board approved all study procedures. This study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (identifier NCT04878627) prior to participant enrollment.

Screening Visit and Baseline Assessments

Participants who met eligibility criteria based on a phone screen were invited to interview with the principal investigator and provide informed consent. Medical stability to participate was determined at the screening visit by a brief physical examination, including a weight measurement and laboratory tests, including EKG, vital signs, complete blood count with differential, plasma electrolytes, hepatic function panel, thyroid function, a urine toxicology screening, a urinalysis, and female sex participants completed a pregnancy test.

All participants were assessed at the baseline visit with the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐5 Disorders by a doctoral‐level interviewer to confirm eligibility (First et al. 2015), and completed the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE‐Q) (Sysko et al. 2005), State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) (Spielberger et al. 1983), Temperament‐Character Inventory (TCI) (Cloninger 1994), Eating Disorder Inventory (EDI3) (Clausen et al. 2011), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI‐II) (Beck et al. 1996), Visual Analogue Scales (VAS) (Flint et al. 2000), and the Food Craving Questionnaire state and trait (FCQ‐S, FCQ‐T) (Moreno et al. 2008).

Study Design and Medication Administration

This was a placebo‐controlled, randomized, double‐blind study that was conducted over 2 years. Participants were randomized (https://www.randomizer.org) to receive CBD (n = 16) or placebo (n = 16) over the course of 21 days. The study was supported by the UCSD Center for Medicinal Cannabis Research (NCT04878627, clinicaltrials.gov). Figure 1 shows a flow diagram of the study procedures.

Study procedure flow chart.

At the end of the baseline visit, the research staff distributed the study drug or placebo solution for the 1st week. Staff explained that participants should begin their first dose the following day, in the morning with breakfast. Past research has shown that the consumption of CBD‐based products with food increases their absorption (Saals et al. 2025). Thus, participants were asked to take the daily morning dose with breakfast or an AM snack and the daily evening dose with dinner or a PM snack. Study participants were advised not to drive or operate machinery until they gained sufficient experience on the study medication to gauge whether it adversely affected their ability to drive or operate machinery. Study participants were also educated about the possibility of suicidal thoughts occurring after starting the study medication. Participants were advised to inform the treatment team and seek emergency treatment immediately if suicidal thoughts and behavior emerged during treatment.

In this study, we used Epidiolex, a plant derived highly purified pharmaceutical formulation of CBD extract developed and provided by GW/Jazz Pharmaceuticals, and currently the only FDA‐approved CBD formulation. The formulation is a 100 mg CBD/mL solution, which allows for easy dose titration and administration even to young children. The placebo compound, also provided by GW/Jazz Pharmaceuticals, was matched in color and taste to the active CBD and was administered in the same volume as the CBD, so that participants and investigators could not detect differences between the two treatments.

Every 7 days, participants were assessed for vital signs, liver function, mood, anxiety, and adverse events, and received an increased dose of the medication. On each study visit, study participants completed the STAI, EDE‐Q, BDI‐II, and FCQS. Additionally, the PI completed the UKU‐Side Effect Scale (Lingjaerde et al. 1987), and the Treatment Acceptability/Adherence Scale (TAAS) (Milosevic et al. 2015) with each patient.

Participants receiving CBD were initially given 1.25 mg/kg twice a day for Week 1, 3.75 mg/kg twice a day for Week 2, and 6.25 mg/kg twice a day for Week 3. Dosing was based on animal studies and studies in human populations, which determined that a range of 7.5 mg/kg daily to 12.5 mg/kg daily was well tolerated by individuals with anxiety (Crippa et al. 2011; Martin‐Santos et al. 2012; Zuardi and Karniol 1983). Participants receiving placebo were given isovolumetric amounts of the placebo solution. A blood draw was also conducted at the end of each study week to assess levels of CBD, 7‐hydroxy CBD (6‐OH‐CBD), 7‐carboxy CBD (7‐COOH‐CBD), and AEA. A multiplexed liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) assay was used to quantify cannabinol (CBN), CBD, THC, 11‐hydroxy‐THC (11‐OH‐THC), 11‐carboxy‐THC (THC‐COOH), cannabigerol (CBG), tetrahydrocannabivarin (THC‐V), 7‐hydroxy‐CBD (7‐OH‐CBD), 7‐carboxy‐CBD (7‐COOH‐CBD), and N‐arachidonoylethanolamine (AEA) in plasma based on methods described previously (Hubbard et al. 2020). Isotope dilution used deuterium labeled internal standards (IS). After the addition of the IS, protein precipitation was performed in 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. After centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred to a Waters Oasis HLB 30 mg solid phase extraction plate and washed using 25% methanol in water. Samples were eluted using 90% acetonitrile and 10% methanol followed by evaporation under a stream of nitrogen. All analysis was performed using a Waters ACQUITY I‐class UPLC System coupled to a Waters Xevo TQS‐Micro mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was facilitated with a 2.1 × 50 mm ACQUITY 1.7 μm UPLC BEH C18 column with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 VanGuard Precolumn, 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 5 mm maintained at 40°C. The linear gradient began at 50% mobile phase B and ended at 90% of mobile phase B across 4 min at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min (Mobile phase A: 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in water, Mobile phase B: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). Calibrators and quality controls were prepared in Mass Spect Gold plasma (Golden West BioSolutions), and method validation was conducted in accordance with CLSI C62‐A (Lynch 2016).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS Version 28.1). Descriptive statistics (e.g., mean, S.D., frequencies) were used to summarize variables at all time points. In addition, the assessments were used to evaluate the clinical effect of CBD on (1) anxiety, (2) cognitive AN‐related symptoms, and (3) restrictive eating behavior. Data were normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk test), and parametric tests were used. Chi‐square tests were used for categorical variables, and independent t‐tests for continuous behavioral variables at baseline between groups. Changes in STAI, BDI‐II, VAS, FCQS, EDE‐Q Global Score, and meal consumption across baseline, Days 7, 14, and 21 between those who received CBD and those who received a placebo were assessed using a repeated measures MANCOVA that included comorbidity and medication as factors in the model. All other physiological, behavioral, and psychological outcomes were considered secondary or exploratory.

Results

Demographic Group Characteristics

A total of 32 participants completed the study, with 16 in the placebo group and 16 in the CBD treatment group. One person in each the CBD and placebo group was discontinued from the study at visit 2, due to nonspecific rashes that had occurred in the week before the assessment and were resolving at the time of notice. Study participants were removed to avoid any risk and were not included in the analysis. The mean age of study participants was 22.7 years, and the sample was entirely cisgender female. The results indicated no significant baseline differences between the experimental and placebo groups with respect to age, BMI, years of education, EDI‐3 Drive for Thinness, EDI‐3 Body Dissatisfaction, STAI‐State and Trait Anxiety levels, EDE‐Q Global, Restraint, EDE‐Q Eating Concern, comorbidity, or medication use measures. Additionally, there were no statistically significant differences in baseline BMI between the AN and AAN participants in both the CBD (p = 0.43) and placebo groups (p = 0.23). Pharmacokinetic Data

The urine toxicology screen was negative at baseline. Concentrations of CBD and metabolites (6‐OH‐CBD, 7‐COOH‐CBD, and AEA) were measured at 7, 14, and 21 days in the CBD and placebo group via venous blood samples. Plasma levels of CBD and its metabolites increased each week, reflecting the increased dose. Mean concentrations over time are shown in Figure 2. Bodyweight‐based dosing ranged from 101.3 to 172.8 mg total daily dose at baseline, 312.8 to 514.5 mg at the first, and 515.0 to 861.3 mg at the second dose increase. At assessment visit one, the mean plasma concentration of CBD was 18.5 ± 6.2 ng/mL, reaching 125.5 ± 33.1 ng/mL at assessment visit three. The mean concentration of 6‐OH‐CBD increased from 10.0 ± 1.2 to 61.8 ± 13.6 ng/mL across the same time points. Similarly, the average 7‐COOH‐CBD concentration rose from 762.3 ± 104.0 after the 1st week to 2572.8 ± 225.9 ng/mL by the last study visit. As expected, there was no detectable level of CBD, OH‐CBD, and COOH‐CBD in the placebo group. Levels of the naturally occurring AEA were similar in both the CBD and placebo group (p = 0.14) throughout the course of the study.

A small but significant group by time difference with a large effect size was observed for BMI (p = 0.046, partial η 2 = 0.252). In the CBD group, mean BMI was 20.1 ± 0.7 at baseline, 20.4 ± 0.7 at assessment visit 1, 20.4 ± 0.7 at visit 2, and 20.5 ± 0.7 at visit 3. By contrast, in the placebo group, the mean BMI was 19.3 ± 0.7 at baseline, 19.3 ± 0.7 at visit 1, 19.4 ± 0.7 at visit 2, and 19.4 ± 0.7 at visit 3. The difference in the BMI of the CBD group at assessment visits 1, 2, and 3 when compared to baseline BMI was considered significant (p = 0.002, p = 0.004, and p = 0.001, respectively). Wilk's Lambda for the CBD group and BMI over time was 0.247, F = 7.167, p = 0.015, partial η 2 = 0.754, power 0.842. In contrast, for the placebo group, Wilk's Lambda for BMI over time was 0.637, F = 1.328, p = 0.340, partial η 2 = 0.363, power 0.223.

In addition to BMI, we analyzed body weight by group and over time using MANCOVA. In the CBD group, mean weight (kg, ±SE) was 53.4 ± 1.9 at baseline, 54.1 ± 1.8 at assessment visit 1, 54.2 ± 1.8 at visit 2, and 54.5 ± 1.9 at visit 3. By contrast, in the placebo group, the mean weight (kg, ±SE) was 52.5 ± 1.9 at baseline, 52.6 ± 1.8 at visit 1, 52.8 ± 1.8 at visit 2, and 52.9 ± 1.9 at visit 3. The difference in the BMI of the CBD group at assessment visits 1, 2, and 3 when compared to baseline BMI was considered significant (p = 0.002, p = 0.005, and p = 0.001, respectively). Wilk's Lambda for the CBD group and BMI over time was 0.222, F = 8.167, p = 0.011. In contrast, for the placebo group, Wilk's Lambda for weight (kg, ±SE) over time was 0.663, F = 1.184, p = 0.382. Individually, three participants in the CBD group lost weight from baseline (−0.3, −0.4, and −0.7 kg), and five individuals in the placebo group (−0.1, −0.5, −0.9, −1.7, −1.9 kg), but due to low sample size we refrained from further statistical analyses.

Other outcome variables did not show significant group‐by‐time differences. However, large effect sizes (partial η 2 > 0.140) for group by time contrasts were observed for FCQ‐S Lack of Control (p = 0.063, partial η 2 = 0.233), EDE‐Q Shape Concern (p = 0.132, partial η 2 = 0.185), and STAI State (p = 0.146, partial η 2 = 0.178).

There were no significant correlations between weekly CBD, CBD metabolite, or AEA levels and behavior outcome measures. The comparison of individuals with AN versus Atypical AN within study groups did not provide significant results, but the study was not powered to provide those data.

Acceptability and Side Effects

Generally, CBD was well tolerated. The UKU scale indicated that throughout the course of the study, one participant (6% of all participants taking CBD) reported abdominal symptoms, which were mild in nature. The rashes that occurred in one participant in each group were nonspecific and already resolving at the time of the visit and likely unrelated to the study intervention. However, out of an abundance of caution, participants were excluded from further participation. Acceptability for the treatment, assessed through the TAAS, revealed that over the three visits, the mean TAAS score for the CBD group was 60.90 (±5.20), and the placebo group had a mean TAAS score of 62.47 (±4.49), which was not significantly different from the CBD group.

Discussion

The results of this randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, pilot study suggest that CBD is well tolerated in the AN and Atypical AN population. The CBD group showed a dose‐dependent increase in CBD and its metabolites, supporting normal bioavailability. Additionally, CBD was associated with a small but significant increase in BMI over time compared to placebo.

The primary aim of this study was to establish the safety and tolerability of CBD for adults with AN. Our initial exploratory hypothesis was that CBD would be well‐tolerated in individuals with AN and would not be associated with weight loss or other signs of relapse. Overall, the study validated this hypothesis. Participants were able to tolerate CBD for the duration of the study without any symptoms suggesting increased food restriction or concerns of eating, shape, or weight. Furthermore, individuals with CBD did not show statistically significant deviations in treatment acceptability or adherence compared to placebo, supporting the idea that CBD can be both a safe and tolerable intervention that can be studied further as a possible treatment.

Our secondary aim was to examine the pharmacokinetics of CBD in adults with AN. We expected that in the CBD group, metabolite levels would increase proportionally each week, reflecting the increased dosage. The weekly CBD and metabolite levels support this hypothesis. While the overall repeated measures ANOVA was not significant, AEA showed a decrease at Week 2 in the CBD group. CBD has been found to increase AEA levels over time. Still, some research showed that acute or early response to CBD application can be associated with a decrease in AEA plasma levels, which would be consistent with the results of this study (Sempio et al. 2022). Whether this slight initial decrease had a meaningful clinical effect is unclear. CBD typically increases AEA levels by blocking FAAH, and it is possible that this effect could not be appreciated at the lower dose and short study duration (Leweke et al. 2012). It is further possible that any therapeutic effect of CBD in this setting was not related to increased AEA levels and subsequent stimulation of the CBR‐1 receptor (Scherma et al. 2019). Whether the effects of CBD may be more congruent with animal research, where its effects were primarily mediated by the 5HT1A receptor, or via its CBR‐1 receptor antagonism, requires further study (Gomes, Resstel, and Guimaraes 2011).

Finally, we wanted to test the effects of CBD on symptomatology in AN. We hypothesized that CBD, relative to placebo, would decrease anxiety levels and increase weight. Albeit small, CBD was associated with a significant increase in BMI over time compared to the placebo group. The BMI result was supported by an additional analysis of changes in body weight, not adjusted for height. Here, the CBD group also showed a significant increase, while the placebo group did not. This is an important result from this study, suggesting that CBD can improve the hallmark problem of AN, low weight. Whether this is due to reduced fear around food or due to metabolic effects requires further study. While much is still unknown about CBD's mechanistic effects in human health and disease, one recent study, for instance, indicated that CBD reduced conditioned salient fear via ventral striatal mitochondrial lipid metabolism (Ferland et al. 2025).

The primary behavioral outcome measures related to AN symptoms and anxiety did not show any statistically significant changes compared to placebo. However, effect sizes (partial η 2 > 0.14) favoring CBD were large for measures of shape concern (EDE‐Q) and lack of control over eating (FCQ‐S). While not statistically significant, these behavioral data represent important exploratory findings, especially in the context of the prior literature suggesting that CBD may help reduce levels of conditioned fear and anxiety. The decrease in metrics related to fears around eating (lack of control) and body image (shape concern) may be associated with this mechanism and is a finding that warrants further research.

Our study patients in both the CBD and placebo groups demonstrated an initial decrease in measures for depression (BDI), food craving (as measured by the FCQ), and overall eating disorder pathophysiology (as measured by the EDE‐Q) that was most notable between the baseline and first study visit. This decrease was not statistically significant between the two groups. The initial downward trend in some of the self‐reported measures at the outset suggests the presence of a psychological expectancy effect. It is important to acknowledge that this may have had an impact on some of the observed results.

Interpretation of these results should be done within the context of the study's limitations. The sample size was limited in this study, which means overall power and generalizability are also not as robust. All study participants were cis‐gender female and whether the results would hold for a male study sample would require testing. Ninety percent of the community sample across both groups were not in regular psychotherapy and therefore this factor was not systematically assessed. Additionally, this was a relatively short‐term study that took place over the course of 3 weeks. Comparable studies assessing the anxiolytic effects of CBD range anywhere from 4 to 15 weeks (Dahlgren et al. 2022). While this pilot study indicates some improvement associated with CBD, a longer‐term study should be conducted. The presence of a large effect size does suggest that there is some effect of CBD on AN‐related symptoms. Still, it is possible that, given the chronic time course of AN and the ingrained nature of the habits that sustain the disease, much more time would be needed to appreciate a clinically distinguishable effect. A repeated measures MANCOVA was selected as the statistical approach because the study design was straightforward, there were no missing data, the data were normally distributed, and sphericity assumptions were met. However, more recent approaches to analyzing repeated‐measures intervention data, such as linear mixed‐effects models, are increasingly recommended (Muhammad 2023). In an additional analysis, we examined BMI change from baseline to the end of the study across groups using a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) linear mixed‐effects model in SPSS, applying the same parameters used in the original analysis. In this group by time analysis, the Type III fixed effect results significantly distinguished the study groups, with the CBD group demonstrating an increase in BMI (F = 2.643, p = 0.021), thereby supporting the findings of the original analysis. The dosage of CBD (which at maximum was 6.25 mg/kg BID) is considered low to moderate. While lower doses are associated with more anxiolytic effects than higher doses, the maximal dose to which the anxiolytic effect is still seen in this population is unclear (Tayo et al. 2020). However, current pharmacokinetic data do not suggest that there is any unique risk to individuals with AN involving CBD use at high doses. Thus, studies with different dose ranges would provide more clarity on CBD's effects in AN. The study included both underweight individuals with AN and weight‐normalized individuals with Atypical AN. There were no significant differences between those subgroups, and larger samples will need to be recruited and studied to provide clarity whether CBD is, for instance, better suited for a particular AN subtype. The small number of participants in high level of care (PHP) per group precluded the use of repeated‐measures MANCOVA to examine potential effects of treatment setting between groups. However, an exploratory analysis restricted to participants from IOP and community referrals, which were well matched between groups, using paired t‐tests with Bonferroni correction, revealed greater symptom improvement in the CBD group compared to Placebo. Specifically, reductions were observed in EDE‐Q Global score (CBD –0.9, d = 1.3, p = 0.006 vs. Placebo −0.6, d = 0.7, p > 0.05), EDE‐Q Restraint (CBD –1.0, d = 1.2, p = 0.018 vs. Placebo −0.6, d = 0.4, p > 0.05), and EDE‐Q Shape Concern (CBD –0.9, d = 1.1, p = 0.03 vs. Placebo −0.5, d = 0.7, p > 0.05). BMI increased slightly in the CBD group (+0.06, d = −0.2) and decreased in the Placebo group (−0.1, d = 0.04), though neither change reached statistical significance. Overall, findings from this larger subgroup not receiving high levels of care support a beneficial effect of CBD on eating disorder psychopathology and is in support of the overall study conclusions.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a favorable safety profile for CBD in women with AN and Atypical AN, with the capacity to support weight recovery and an indication of improving eating disorder psychopathology. The findings in this study offer an important step for identifying the strengths and limitations in the use of CBD for treating AN. The study demonstrates the need for further research into the treatment before definitive clinical recommendations can be made. Future research into CBD, focusing on a larger sample size and longer course, may be necessary to make definitive conclusions about the mechanism through which CBD impacts AN pathophysiology as well as the maximal dose at which effects are still seen. Additionally, combining CBD with other treatment strategies such as a specific dietary alteration may be necessary to appreciate its full therapeutic capacity.
