An immunoassay for the quantification of phosphorylated α-synuclein at serine 129 in human cerebrospinal fluid
Authors: Achani M Silva, Elizabeth S Hickford, Paul Cutler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe link between alpha Synuclein (α-Syn) phosphorylation and Parkinson's disease pathogenesis has not been fully elucidated, in part due to analytical methods with finite specificity and sensitivity, resulting in conflicting data on pathophysiological levels of the protein.One factor hindering the assessment of the role of pSer129 α-Syn is the lack of a fit for purpose assay. Antibodies were assessed for quantification of pSer129 α-Syn, resulting in a sensitive and specific assay suitable for use in Parkinson's disease and control CSF, with no significant difference found between the two populations. Total α-Syn was measured using a commercial kit, demonstrating a positive correlation between total and pSer129 α-Syn.This adds to available methods for pSer129 α-Syn in support of α-synucleinopathy research.

Introduction

α-Synuclein (α-Syn) is a small, 14-kDa, highly soluble and disordered, intracellular protein encoded by the SNCA gene [1]. α-Syn is predominantly expressed in the central nervous system (CNS), in neurons of different brain regions [2,3]. It has however also been located in non-CNS cell types and organ systems such as in muscle, liver, heart, lungs, kidney, hematopoietic cells of the bone marrow and within circulating blood cells [4–6].

Since its discovery, and subsequent validation as a genetic target associated with Parkinson's disease (PD), the study of the biological role of α-Syn and its pathological implications has been the subject of increasing attention. Although its putative function is as a pre-synaptic neuronal protein, which binds lipids and regulates the release of synaptic vesicles, its physiological functions remain a matter of debate and are yet to be fully elucidated [1,7,8].

The search for a complete understanding of its function is driven by the fact that α-Syn has been implicated in numerous neurodegenerative disorders known as synucleinopathies, which are characterized by the progressive loss of neuronal populations in the brain [9]. Primary α-synucleinopathies include PD, dementia with Lewy bodies (DLBs) and multiple system atrophy (MSA) [10,11].

PD is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disease after AD and several studies have suggested that the misfolding of α-Syn results in the formation of aggregated α-Syn into neuronal inclusions named Lewy bodies (LBs) [12,13], which are the pathogenic hallmark of these diseases. While the abnormal aggregation of α-Syn plays an important role in PD pathogenesis, the specific pathogenic α-Syn species remains undefined [14]. Furthermore, while α-Syn has emerged as a viable therapeutic target, the molecular and cellular determinants involved in the initiation or propagation of α-Syn aggregation and toxicity, as well as the relationship between these processes and disease progression remain undefined.

Several post-translational modifications (PTMs) have emerged as critical determinants of the physiological and pathological functions of α-Syn, since they can influence the aggregation and/or cytotoxicity [15]. Alongside genetic mutations, an increasing amount of evidence has implicated the involvement of PTMs in PD. These modifications include, but are not limited to, nitration, ubiquitination, truncation and phosphorylation. In fact, phosphorylated forms have been found in LBs from patients with PD, highlighting the potential link between PTMs and PD pathology [16].

A major phosphorylation site of α-Syn within LBs, which occurs at serine 129 (pSer129), has emerged as a potentially defining characteristic of PD and related synucleinopathies, and so has become a focus of investigation as a potential biomarker for early detection of these diseases [17,18].

While the importance of phosphorylated α-Syn as a biomarker for PD has been postulated, the demand for good quality assays to measure these phosphorylated forms remain high. There are various commercial ligand binding assay (LBA) kits available for the measurement of total α-Syn in biofluids such as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), serum and plasma (e.g. alphaLISA, Perkin Elmer), including some for high sensitivity platforms (e.g. Singulex Erenna, Merck and Simoa, Quanterix), allowing the measurement of very low levels of the protein. One of the most widely used LBA kits, is an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) on the Mesoscale Discovery (MSD) platform, which is thought to detect multiple forms of α-Syn (i.e. ‘total α-Syn’). Several groups have published α-Syn levels in CSF at a range of concentrations using this assay kit [19–21]. Despite the existence of a good quality method to quantify total α-Syn, the options to measure phosphorylated forms are less well established.

Although there are numerous commercially available antibodies against phosphorylated α-Syn, there are no well characterized commercial kits to measure pSer129 α-Syn. Other groups have reported development of LBAs for quantification of this form of α-Syn, although it is acknowledged that the commercially available antibodies used have various limitations, including finite specificity, which is commonly seen in antibodies to phospho proteins [22–24]. The lack of standardized assays and well-characterized antibodies has resulted in varying reports in the literature on the levels of phosphorylated α-Syn in disease and its relative involvement in pathology compared with total α-Syn. For that reason, a good quality and well characterized assay, which is highly sensitive and specific for phosphorylated α-Syn has become essential in the field of α-synucleinopathy research. Furthermore, considering that levels of pSer129 α-Syn are lower in biofluid samples, sensitivity remains a major challenge in measuring the analyte. Reports from prior studies showed that they were unable to reliably measure the analyte in human biofluids samples due to sensitivity limitations of the assays [21,24], while Majbour et al. was able to detect pSer129 α-Syn concentrations of 207 pg/ml to 296 pg/ml in CSF of PD patients [20]. In comparison, the total α-Syn assays are better established both commercially and internally through in-house generated antibodies and sensitivity is less of a limitation, as shown by Majbour et al. and Wang et al. where 1300 and 380 pg/ml concentrations were detected in CSF of PD patients, respectively [20,21]. An additional consideration is the relative quantitative nature of LBA approaches for biomarker assays where the endogenous analyte differs from the recombinant calibrator material, limiting the possibility to directly compare analyte concentrations reported from different methods or between total and phosphorylated forms of α-Syn. Another concern is that although some commercially available human pSer129 α-Syn kits have been developed (e.g. Human Phosphorylatad Alpha Synuclein [PSNCA] ELISA kit, MyBioSouce, CA, USA), they may not have the sensitivity required, be tested in the relevant matrix or species and the vendor may not have assessed (or provided information on) specificity. Therefore, specificity is a key challenge to overcome for the detection of pSer129 α-Syn.

Here, we report the development of an alternative ECLIA method for human pSer129 α-Syn in CSF, based on the well characterized total α-Syn MSD assay, in which the detection antibody was replaced with a commercially available monoclonal antibody (MJF-R13 8-8, Abcam) against pSer129 α-Syn. A fit-for-purpose biomarker assay validation was undertaken, based on the context of use (COU) of specifically measuring pSer129 α-Syn relative to total α-Syn in CSF samples from PD patients and NDCs [25,26].

Methods

Context of use

The objective was to develop an assay capable of quantifying pSer129 α-Syn in human CSF. Total α-Syn was measured in the same samples using the MSD α-Syn U-Plex kit. These assays were used to compare relative levels of pSer129 α-Syn to total α-Syn in CSF samples from PD patients and from controls with no known neurodegenerative diseases or diagnosis of dementia, which will be referred to throughout this article as ‘non-dementia control's (NDCs). The data were to be used to assess any potential differences in expression for the purposes of internal decision-making, to support the design of biomarker strategies for the development of novel therapeutics for α-synucleinopathies.

Fit-for-purpose assay validations were performed in accordance with our internal biomarker assay validation strategy [26], based on the context of use of the assay.

Total α-Syn assay

Total human α-Syn in CSF was measured using the MSD U-Plex kit (K151WKK, MesoScale Discovery, MD, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, an MSD Gold small spot streptavidin-coated 96-well plate was coated with biotinylated anti-human alpha synuclein capture antibody for 1 h at room temperature (RT) with shaking at 200 rpm before washing three-times with 300 μl of phosphate buffered saline (Gibco DPBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) + 0.05 % (v/v) Tween-20 (VWR, PA, USA) per well. These conditions slightly differ from the manufacturer's recommendations in the kit instructions. The recombinant human α-Syn calibrator provided with the kit was prepared as recommended and CSF samples were diluted in MSD Diluent 49 provided by the vendor. Samples were thawed on ice and diluted in sample diluent (ice-cold Diluent 49 containing 1 % [v/v] protease and phosphatase inhibitors [Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA]) immediately before loading onto the assay plates. Diluted calibrators and samples were added 25 μl/well in duplicate, followed by 25 μl/well of the Sulfo-TAG labeled anti-human α-Syn detection antibody. Assay plate was then incubated for 2 h at RT with shaking at 200 rpm (Stuart Scientific SO3 Orbital Shaker, Stuart Scientific). The plate was washed for a second time and 150 μl of 1X MSD Read Buffer T was added to each well before reading in an MSD S600 Sector Imager (MesoScale Discovery).

Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by spiking recombinant α-Syn (MSD kit calibrator material) into artificial CSF (aCSF) (150 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 1.4 mM CaCl2.2H20, 0.8 mM MgCl2.6H20, 0.8 mM Na2HPO4-7H2O, 0.2 mM NaH2PO4-H2O) prepared in-house with 0.1 % (v/v) human serum (BioIVT, NY, USA) at 50 ng/ml (High QC), 2 ng/ml (Mid QC) and 200 pg/ml (Low QC). These were stored at -80°C in single-use aliquots of 50 μl, which were diluted 1 in 10 in sample diluent on the day of the assay, resulting in final assay concentrations of 5 ng/ml, 0.2 ng/ml and 20 pg/ml (representing the upper, mid and lower portions of the standard curve) before adding to each assay plate.

A 4-parameter logistic (4PL) curve fit, with 1/Y2 weighting was applied to the standard curve and the sample and QC data back-calculated using Watson LIMS or GraphPad prism.

pSer129 α-Syn assay development

Detection antibody selection

To develop an assay for the specific detection of pSer129 α-Syn, we tested four commercially available anti-pSer129 antibodies (Table 1) as potential replacements for the detection antibody in the total α-Syn MSD kit described above. The four anti-pSer129 antibodies were labeled with MSD Sulfo-TAG NHS-ester (R91AO, MesoScale Discovery), following the manufacturer's instructions and stored at 4°C, then were tested as detection antibodies at 1 μg/ml across a wide concentration range of recombinant human pSer129 α-Syn (The protein was expressed in E. coli, purified and the purified fraction was then phosphorylated in vitro by polo-like kinase 3 [PLK3], custom made by Promise Proteomics, Grenoble, France). For the purposes of antibody screening, we selected three concentrations representing high, mid and low concentrations of pSer129 a-Syn across the approximate range that we wanted to achieve to give an assay with sufficient sensitivity to measure pSer129 a-Syn in biological samples. The recombinant pSer129 α-Syn concentrations tested were 10 ng/ml, 160 pg/ml and 9 pg/ml, diluted in MSD Diluent 49 (R50AM, MesoScale Discovery). These concentrations were not used in further experiments or in the final assay format and were adequately prepared for the purposes of this particular screening experiment. A Diluent 49 blank control was also included.

Final format of pSer129 α-Syn assay

MJF-R13 (8-8) antibody labeled with MSD Sulfo-TAG was diluted to 1 μg/ml and used to replace the detection antibody in the MSD U-Plex kit, as described above. The diluted antibody was added to the plate (25 μl/well) with calibrators, QCs and samples (25 μl/well) before incubating overnight at 4°C with shaking at 200 rpm (Stuart Scientific SO3 Orbital Shaker, Stuart Scientific). Human recombinant pSer129 α-Syn was used to generate the standard curve by diluting in trebling dilutions in sample diluent (as described for the total α-Syn assay), to a final assay range of 10 ng/ml to 2 pg/ml.

Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by spiking recombinant human pSer129 α-Syn into aCSF with 0.1 % (v/v) human serum (BioIVT) at 6 ng/ml (High QC), 0.6 ng/ml (Mid QC) and 60 pg/ml (Low QC). These were stored at -80°C in single-use aliquots of 50 μl and diluted 1 in 2 in sample diluent, resulting in final assay concentrations of 3 ng/ml, 0.3 ng/ml and 30 pg/ml, respectively, before adding to each assay plate.

All other steps were performed as described for the MSD U-Plex kit.

Standards, samples and QCs were analysed in duplicate. Standard curve fitting (4 parameter logistic fit) with 1/Y2 weighting was applied and the sample and QC data back-calculated using Watson LIMS and GraphPad prism.

Fit for purpose validation of pSer129 method

Precision

To assess inter-assay precision, QC samples prepared from recombinant human pSer129 α-Syn in aCSF with 0.1 % (v/v) human serum were run in technical duplicate in six assays across 3 days by two different operators.

A single assay was performed with six sets of QCs to assess intra-assay precision.

Parallelism

The aim of the parallelism experiment was to assess the relative accuracy of the method and to ensure that the recombinant calibrator was appropriate for the relative quantification of the endogenous protein.

Parallelism was assessed by twofold serial dilutions of PD (n = 4) and NDC CSF (n = 4) from neat to 1 in 8 in MSD Diluent 49 + 1 % (v/v) phosphatase and protease inhibitors. All dilutions and the undiluted CSF samples were analysed in technical duplicate. The dilution factor-adjusted concentrations were plotted against dilution factor to visually assess the impact of sample dilution on the quantitation of pSer129 α-Syn.

Alpha-synuclein depletion for specificity assessment

Immunodepletion of α-Syn from PD CSF (n = 3) and NDC CSF (n = 1) was performed by incubating samples, spiked with 50 pg/ml recombinant pSer129 α-Syn, at 4°C for 2 h with a final concentration of 50 μg/ml anti-α-Syn mouse IgG antibody 4B12 (GTX21904, GeneTex, Taiwan) and 675 μg of magnetic Protein G beads (10003D, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) that had been washed and re-suspended in MSD Diluent 49 containing 2 % (v/v) protease/phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The beads with sequestered IgG + α-Syn were pelleted using a magnet (DynaMag-2 magnetic rack, ThermoFisher) and the flow-through was removed to a clean tube before being tested in the pSer129 α-Syn ECLIA method. Control samples were processed and analysed in parallel using a mouse IgG1 isotype control antibody (UCB reagent) with the beads.

Samples

Human ante-mortem CSF samples from PD patients and NDCs were obtained from PrecisionMed (CA, USA) and post-mortem samples from Netherlands Brain Biobank (NBB, Amsterdam, NL), mixture of both male and female genders. Sample size of n = 32 for PD patients and n = 31 for NDCs were used in the study due to limited availability of material. No formal power analysis was performed and therefore these data are considered exploratory. These were thawed and divided into single-use aliquots in Protein LoBind Eppendorf tubes (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) for storage at -80°C. Samples were subjected to one freeze-thaw event for aliquoting purposes prior to analysis.

NBB CSF samples have been obtained using procedures, information and consent forms of the NBB which have been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the VU Medical Centre on April 30, 2009, under the reference 2000/148. PrecisionMed CSF samples were collected under the supervision of WRIB protocol number 20081324 and study number 1125900. These were then acquired to be stored under UK HTA licence number 12504. All human CSF products were then stored, used and disposed of in accordance with UCB Biopharma's internal procedures and UK Human Tissue Authority Act guidelines, Code E.

Results

Development of pSer129 α-Syn MSD assay

An ECLIA assay on the MSD platform was developed for measuring pSer129 α-Syn levels in human CSF by adapting the commercially available α-Syn MSD U-Plex kit (which was used in parallel for measurement of total α-Syn). First, four anti-pSer129 α-Syn antibodies were assessed as detection reagents to replace the kit detection antibody to specifically detect recombinant α-Syn phosphorylated in vitro at Serine 129 by PLK3 (Table 1). The biotinylated anti-human α-Syn capture antibody provided in the MSD U-Plex kit was used as the capture reagent throughout. The four detection antibodies were initially screened at two dilutions (1 in 500 and 1 in 1000) against 100 ng/ml of recombinant pSer129 α-Syn. All antibodies at both dilutions gave negligible detection of the diluent blank control (i.e., 0 ng/ml α-Syn), while antibody MJF-R13 (8-8) gave the highest signal to noise ratio (S:N) in response to 100 ng/ml α-Syn, with no difference between the two antibody dilutions tested in a single experiment. MJF-R13 (8-8) was confirmed as the detection antibody that gave the highest S:N in a second experiment where all four antibodies were tested at a 1 in 1000 dilution against a wide range of recombinant pSer129 α-Syn concentrations, from 9 pg/ml to 10 ng/ml, to give an initial indication of potential sensitivity that could be achieved by each of the reagents (Figure 1A). MJF-R13 (8-8) was the only one of the four reagents tested that achieved a S:N >1 at 9 pg/ml pSer129 α-Syn, suggesting that high assay sensitivity may be achieved using this detection antibody (Table 2).

Additionally, the suitability of MJF-R13 (8-8) detection antibody for the specific detection of pSer129 α-Syn compared with total α-Syn was tested by comparing recombinant proteins of both the phosphorylated and non-phosphorylated (MSD U-Plex kit calibrator) forms across an approximately equivalent concentration range. A curve was produced using pSer129 α-Syn diluted from 10 ng/ml to 1.5 pg/ml using the MJF-R13 (8-8) detection antibody, with no signal above background generated using the MSD U-Plex kit standard, showing specificity of the assay for the phosphorylated form of the protein (Figure 1B). Furthermore, there was no difference observed in the curves when pSer129 α-Syn and the MSD U-Plex kit standard were run in the MSD U-Plex kit assay, using the detection antibody supplied with the kit (Figure 1C). For that reason, we considered that the MSD U-Plex kit gave a measure of ‘total’ α-Syn as it could detect both forms of the recombinant proteins tested. That is, the U-Plex total α-Syn assay could detect the phosphorylated standard in an equivalent way to the non-phosphorylated form.

Due to the potential sensitivity demonstrated and the specificity for recombinant pSer129 α-Syn over the non-phosphorylated form of α-Syn, MJF-R13 (8-8) was selected as the detection antibody to take forward for further development and optimisation of a pSer129 α-Syn ECLIA method.

To assess the impact of plate incubation time in the assay, recombinant pSer129 α-Syn was serial diluted from 10 ng/ml to 14 pg/ml. With a 16 h incubation performed overnight at 4°C the mean ECL signals increased at all pSer129 α-Syn concentrations, compared with 2 h at RT (Figure 2). Furthermore, S:N increased by between 1.3- and 1.8-fold in 6 out of 7 pSer129 α-Syn concentrations with the overnight incubation, suggesting that additional sensitivity may be achieved using this longer incubation time (Table 3).

Parallelism

Although MJF-R13-8-8 had been shown to specifically detect recombinant pSer129 α-Syn, it was important to test whether it could detect the endogenous form of the protein, and if so, if it diluted in parallel with the standard curve. The latter point would demonstrate whether the calibrator material used in the standard curve was suitable for relative quantitation of the endogenous protein, which may differ from the recombinant protein due to biological heterogeneity (e.g. truncations, post-translational modifications). Parallelism was tested with ante-mortem PD CSF samples from neat to 1 in 8, diluted in Diluent 49 + protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After an initial 1 in 2 dilution, subsequent dilutions resulted in comparable concentration results (with CVs ≤24 %) (Figure 3A). The lower concentrations observed in neat samples indicated that a dilution of 1 in 2 was required to dilute out matrix interference in PD CSF in this assay and so was used as the minimum required dilution (MRD) for sample analysis.

Parallelism was also assessed for the total α-Syn assay with post-mortem CSF samples, from 1 in 10 to 1 in 100, and CVs between dilutions ranged from 2.1 % to 14.8 % for each sample (Figure 3B). A 1 in 10 dilution was used as the MRD for sample analysis in the total α-Syn assay.

Specificity

The positive results from the parallelism experiments suggested that the assays were specific for α-Syn, although the parallelism alone does not prove specificity and so additional evidence supporting specificity of the assays was sought. Specificity was assessed by conducting an immuno-depletion experiment in which ante-mortem PD and NDC CSF was incubated with anti-α-Syn antibody 4B12, to bind total α-Syn, which was subsequently captured onto protein G beads. Aliquots of the same samples were also incubated in the same way with an IgG1 isotype control. The flow-through from the pelleted beads was then run in the pSer129 α-Syn and total α-Syn assays to determine whether immuno-depletion of α-Syn knocked down the α-Syn concentrations detected in the assays.

The CSF used in this experiment was found to contain levels of pSer129 α-Syn below the assay range, although a decrease in signal with 4B12 compared with isotype control was observed in every sample (PD CSF, n = 6; NDC CSF, n = 4) (Figure 4A). For this reason, four further CSF samples (PD, n = 3; NDC, n = 1) were spiked with 50 pg/ml recombinant pSer129 α-Syn and run in the pSer129 α-Syn assay, to allow the α-Syn depletion to be quantified (Figure 4B). Mean depletion of α-Syn using antibody 4B12, compared with isotype control antibody, was 64.4 % in PD CSF (n = 3) and 64.5 % in the NDC CSF sample (Figure 4C).

Total α-Syn was measurable in the CSF samples used and so α-Syn depletion with antibody 4B12 was quantifiable without spiking recombinant material (Figure 4D). 82.6 % depletion was achieved in PD CSF samples (n = 6), compared with 83.3 % in NDC CSF (n = 4).

The assumption that antibody 4B12 and the conditions used resulted in full capture of both endogenous and spiked recombinant α-Syn make it difficult to conclude that the assays were fully specific for α-Syn, although the extent of the knock down suggests that at least 64 % (for the pSer129 α-Syn assay) and at least 82 % (for the total α-Syn MSD U-Plex kit) of the signal can be attributed to α-Syn. It was interesting to observe consistent data between CSF samples from different individuals, with similar extent of α-Syn knock down. These findings support that data shown from the parallelism experiments (Figure 3A & B), which suggests that the assays are specific for α-Syn and also support the specificity of the pSer129 α-Syn assay for the phosphorylated form of the protein demonstrated using recombinant material during detection antibody selection (Figure 1).

Fit-for-purpose validation

Fit-for-purpose validation of the assays was performed in accordance with our internal biomarker assay validation strategy utilized within the organisation based on the COU [26]. Key parameters that were assessed for the pSer129 α-Syn assay were: precision and relative accuracy; parallelism; and specificity. Despite total α-Syn being measured with a commercially available kit, with well characterized parameters documented in the kit insert we also assessed parallelism and specificity as the assessments provided by the vendor were not adequate for our purposes.

Intra- and inter- assay precision and relative accuracy of the pSer129 α-Syn assay were assessed using QC samples (recombinant pSer129 α-Syn spiked into artificial CSF) over six assay runs. Due to limitations in sample volumes and concentrations it was not possible to use endogenous QCs using human CSF in this instance. Different levels of QC samples were included as described in Table 4, at the estimated levels of 3000 pg/ml, 300 pg/ml and 30 pg/ml. The inter-assay CVs were between 5 % and 10 % while the intra-assay CVs for the six sets of CSF tested were between 3 % and 6 % (Table 4).

Determination of Total & pSer-129 α-Syn levels in biological samples

As a next step the levels of total and pSer129 α-Syn in both PD and NDC CSF samples were measured. The average concentration of total α-Syn in PD CSF was found to be 491.8 ± 65.64 pg/ml (Mean ± SEM), while in NDCs the mean concentration was 426.1 ± 48.22 pg/ml, with no significant difference observed between PD and NDC (p = 0.179, Figure 5A). These results are consistent with some literature which has also shown no changes in total α-Syn levels between these populations [27]. Although there was a slight increase observed in PD compared with NDC (12.81 ± 1.37 pg/ml compared with 11.44 ± 0.71 pg/ml, respectively), this was not significant (p = 0.059, Figure 5B).

Due to the relative quantitative nature of the assays limiting the possibility of directly comparing concentrations obtained from two different assays, the ratio of pSer129 to total α-Syn in CSF between PD and NDC individuals was assessed and showed no significant differences (p = 0.5308, Figure 5C). The results do not support the hypothesis of a higher ratio of phosphorylated α-Syn to total αSyn in PD compared with NDC in CSF, which was not expected based on the study published by Majbour et al. 2016. However, a wider spread of data was observed in PD group with some patients showing a much higher ratio.

Correlation analysis of total and pSer129 α-Syn in CSF in NDC and PD cohorts was investigated. A significant positive correlation was observed between total and pSer129 α-Syn concentrations in both NDC (r = 0.41, p = 0.021, Figure 5D) and PD (r = 0.75, p < 0.0001, Figure 5E) groups. The data indicate a greater correlation of total and pser129 α-Syn in PD patients compared with NDC in CSF. However, an inverse correlation observed by some other papers in PD CSF was not observed.

Discussion

The accumulation and aggregation of misfolded α-Syn is considered to be the cause of PD of which LBs are the characteristic histopathological hallmark [12,13]. In the last few years, an increasing number of studies reported that α-Syn within LBs is subject to several PTMs suggesting that these modifications may play a crucial role in the regulation of aggregated α-Syn and the resulting toxicity in vivo [28]. Phosphorylation of α-Syn at serine 129 is a key PTM that has been identified to be associated with the misfolding process [29], and is known to increase to 90 % of total α-Syn in LBs [17,18]. However, attempts in understanding the pathogenic relevance of this PTM have been challenging due to the limited availability of commercial assays capable of detecting and quantifying phosphorylated α-Syn in relevant biological samples [22–24]. Studying the role of phosphorylation on the pathogenesis of α-synucleinopathies and determining the validity of phosphorylated forms of α-Syn as potential biomarkers or as viable therapeutic targets could be transformational in the quest for treatments of these devastating neurological disorders.

Biomarkers are increasingly used in the pharmaceutical industry to predict the efficacy of novel pharmacological interventions. There is currently a high unmet need in PD for biomarkers that may act as a surrogate of disease progression in studies of potential disease-modifying therapies. Biomarkers also play an important role for diagnosis and staging in new biologically defined PD. The ability to quantify disease-relevant, post-translationally modified forms of α-Syn, with high specificity and sensitivity, has not yet been adequately addressed for clinical and biomarker studies [30]. To contribute to efforts to respond to this need, we have herein described the development of a novel assay specific for the detection of pSer129 α-Syn.

Due to the molecular heterogeneity of endogenous biological molecules and the lack of regulatory guidance specific to biomarker assay validation for the purposes of drug discovery and development, biomarker assays must be carefully designed and subjected to a fit-for-purpose validation based on the context of use (COU) of the data, i.e. how the data will be specifically used to interpret a particular study [25,26]. We required an assay for the specific quantitation of pSer129 α-Syn, with the purpose of assessing the change in relative levels in CSF compared with total α-Syn in PD patients and NDCs. Our assay, based on the MSD U-PLEX Human α-Synuclein Kit, was developed by substituting the kit detection antibody with a pSer129 α-Syn-specific reagent, to detect and quantify pSer129 α-Syn at low pg/ml concentrations in human CSF. The approach that we have taken for the development of this pSer129 α-Syn assay involves using an existing commercially available immunoassay kit for total α-Syn but modifying the assay by substituting the detection antibody to specifically detect the pSer129 form. Of the various detection antibodies assessed (Table 1), specificity and high sensitivity were achieved when using the anti-α-Syn monoclonal rabbit antibody MJF-R13-8-8, a reagent that has previously been reported for the detection of phosphorylated α-Syn in other published methods [24,31].

As antibodies to phosphorylated proteins can be notoriously non-specific, key parameters to be assessed were specificity and/or cross-reactivity. This is particularly important for antibodies against phosphorylated proteins, and several anti-phospho α-Syn antibodies have been shown to have certain limitations in their specificity [22]. Numerous studies have primarily focussed on the cross-reactivity of the pSer129 antibodies toward other proteins [31–34], but results have been contradictory. Lashuel et al. conducted a thorough review of the α-Syn literature and revisited the specificity to phosphorylated proteins by assessing several commonly used commercially available antibodies across various methods. The study confirmed that the presence of additional PTMs in the C-terminal region close to the serine129 residue interferes with the capacity of some pSer129 antibodies to detect pSer129 α-Syn [22]. Of the antibodies evaluated, the antibody used as the detection reagent in our assay, MJF-R13-8-8, demonstrated high specificity for pSer129 in Lashuel's work [22]. MJF-R13-8-8 has also been successfully used in assays previously described for pS129 α-Syn and proven to be human and α-Syn specific [24,31]. Without relevant positive and negative biological sample controls, specificity of biomarker LBAs can be very difficult to prove [25,26]. To address this, we took data from different assessments to build confidence in the specificity of the assay.

Firstly, the α-Syn-specific capture tool reagent contained in the MSD kit conferred specificity of the assay to α-Syn. The alternative approach of capturing with an anti-phospho α-Syn reagent was not possible in this instance as we wanted to keep the capture antibody and overall method as similar as possible to the MSD kit, for measuring total α-Syn in the same samples. As the kit capture antibody was provided biotinylated it could not be labeled for use at the detection antibody, based on the working principle of the MSD platform. Additionally, capturing the phospho protein and detecting with a total α-Syn reagent would be expected to yield lower sensitivity compared with the opposite antibody pair orientation as any non-specificity of the capture antibody would theoretically decrease the specific binding of α-Syn to the assay plate.

Secondly, we demonstrated the specific detection of recombinant pSer129 α-Syn compared with the non-phosphorylated protein. However, as experiments using recombinant protein can be of limited relevance due to differences from the endogenous biomarker with its inherent biological heterogeneity (including PTMs), we also looked to the parallelism experiment. Although parallelism of the endogenous protein with the standard curve does not confirm specificity, a lack of parallelism would have indicated an element of non-specificity [35,36].

We observed parallelism in both diseased and control CSF (Figure 3A), and while these data do not in themselves confirm specificity of the assay to pSer129 α-Syn, the information helps to build confidence in the method. Assessing the effects of dilution on the quantitation of endogenous analyte in matrix in this way also allowed an assessment of selectivity (i.e. the impact of matrix interference on the assay) and for a minimum required sample dilution (MRD, i.e. the dilution required to dilute out any matrix interference) to be established, as well as endogenous levels of pSer α-Syn in healthy and diseased populations to be assessed in a single experiment simultaneously [35]. Absence of parallelism would have indicated a lack of specificity and/or selectivity in the assay.

An experiment was also performed whereby we immunodepleted α-Syn from human PD and NDC CSF samples using a different antibody to those used in the assay and ran the depleted samples in the assay to show that the α-Syn signal was reduced. While depletion is never absolute, we did observe significant depletion of total alpha synuclein without the proportional drop in the phosphorylated signal. This further built confidence that the signal we are seeing is specific to the phosphorylated form in biological matrix (CSF).

A multitude of different techniques and platforms have been used in the past for the quantification of α-Syn with varying degree of success toward a goal of finding a biomarker for PD and other α-synucleinopathies. However, when comparing the pSer129 ECLIA method described here with other published methods for measuring pSer129 α-Syn in biological fluids, it is important to note the conflicting results from different groups. Majbour et al. developed a sandwich ELISA and reported concentrations of pSer129 α-Syn of 200 to 300 pg/ml in CSF of PD patients compared with 180 to 275 pg/ml in healthy controls [20]. Likewise, Wang et al. found higher levels of pSer129 α-Syn (50 to 80 pg/ml) in the CSF of patients with PD or MSA using a bead-based Luminex assay compared with healthy controls [21]. Another study by Majbour et al. demonstrated a significant longitudinal decrease of pSer129 α-Syn in CSF of PD patients (from 220 to 180 pg/ml at follow-up) [19]. Dutta et al. recently developed a pSer α-Syn ECLIA method with an lower limit of detection (LLOD) of 6 pg/ml, but levels measured in human CSF were found to be highly variable in PD using this method and the small sample numbers did not allow for statistically relevant comparisons [23]. A recent study by Cariulo et al. was unable to detect pSer129 α-Syn in CSF despite using the MJF-R13-8-8 detection antibody on an ultra-sensitive Singulex Erenna immunoassay with an LLOD of 0.15 pg/ml [24]. In contrast, our data using the same detection antibody on the MSD system was able to detect pSer129 α-Syn in CSF, although no significant differences were found between PD and NDC's (averaging 12.81 ± 1.37 pg/ml compared with 11.44 ± 0.71 pg/ml, respectively). The inability to measure endogenous pSer129 α-Syn in the CSF samples used for our immunodepletion experiments, which were obtained from a different source to those used for the comparisons between PD and NDC CSF, indicates that factors such as sample collection and processing, analyte long-term stability and storage conditions may be critical to the concentrations reported and so may account for some of the differences in literature data, alongside the relative quantitative nature inherent to many of these methods.

The capture antibody we used that was provided by the α-Syn MSD kit, combined with the highly specific phospho antibody MJF-R13-8-8 could have increased our ability to detect pSer129 α-Syn in the CSF compared with Cariulo and co-workers. Additional reasons may be due to the differences between plate-based ECLIA and bead-based Singulex assay technologies, workflows and pre-analytical sample processing, as well as stability of the endogenous molecule. The difficulty and inconsistency of measuring this phospho α-Syn species in CSF has been further highlighted by Lashuel et al. using IP-MS/MS where pSer129 α-Syn was also not detected [22]. What is clear from all these findings is that a well characterized, fit-for-purpose and a highly specific assay is hard to establish yet could be of huge benefit in providing consistency in the field of α-synucleinopathy research.

The concentrations of total α-Syn in CSF have been reported to be reduced in PD patients compared with healthy controls in several studies [19,27,37–40]. Our data contradicts these findings where no significant differences in total α-Syn were detected. The reason for this discrepancy might be explained by the sample size used in our study as large numbers of PD patients and healthy controls may need to be analysed to detect significant changes in total α-Syn [38]. Furthermore, the differences observed in previous studies were small [37,39,40]. It is interesting to note that not all the studies published reached the same conclusion and our data adds to various reports in the literature where total α-Syn CSF levels did not distinguish PD patients from controls [41–43]. The comparability to previous research is hindered by the different assays used for the detection of total α-Syn and differences in the demographic data of patients and controls. Another consideration is the inter-individual variability observed by us and others, which may mask within-subject longitudinal changes in α-Syn correlating with disease progression.

When assessing the ratio of pSer129 to total α-Syn, our observations were not as expected based on previously reported data as we found no evidence to support the hypothesis of a higher ratio of phospho α-Syn to total in PD compared with NDC CSF [19,21,44].

The most interesting and unexpected observation in our study was the significant positive correlation between pSer129 and total α-Syn concentrations in CSF of PD patients, which is contrary to previous studies that have indicated an inverse correlation, suggesting a disease-relevant association between both markers [20,21]. These discrepancies are likely due to numerous factors including the differences in the assay platforms, collection and handling of the CSF samples, heterogeneity of the patients included in the studies and requirement of a larger cohort of PD patients to see a marked effect. Further work with other samples will be needed to verify (or not) the correlation we observed.

Conclusion

In summary, by screening commercially available antibodies, we have been able to successfully modify an existing α-Syn method to allow for the specific quantification of α-Syn phosphorylated at Ser129 in human CSF. Previous studies have led to conflicting results, in part due to the lack of a highly specific and sensitive method. The assay developed here has potentially wide-ranging applicability in the field of PD (and other α-synucleinopathies), with differing contexts of use. Therefore, when used by others it is important that a fit-for-purpose validation is performed taking into account the specific use of the data for any particular study. Although challenging to prove, evidence was gathered in support of specificity of the method, which was key to the assurance that pSer129 α-Syn was being measured. Work published by others has described increased levels of pSer129 α-Syn and decreased concentrations of total α-Syn in the CSF of patients with PD, yet we observed no marked differences between total and pSer129 α-Syn between PD and healthy controls in CSF. This highlights that further investigation into pSer129 α-Syn as a potential biomarker of PD and other synucleinopathies may be warranted and some consensus on methods used within the scientific community for measurement of this analyte may be justified. Although the potential use of our assay was verified in a cohort study, validating the utilization of our assay in large-scale, longitudinal and well-controlled studies with regards to long-term stability and processing of samples and including patients with other neurological diseases would be beneficial.
