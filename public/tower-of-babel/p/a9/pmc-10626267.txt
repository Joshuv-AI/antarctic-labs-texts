A robust pipeline for high-content, high-throughput immunophenotyping reveals age- and genetics-dependent changes in blood leukocytes
Authors: Thomas Liechti, Sofie Van Gassen, Margaret Beddall, Reid Ballard, Yaser Iftikhar, Renguang Du, Thiagarajan Venkataraman, David Novak, Massimo Mangino, Stephen Perfetto, H Benjamin Larman, Tim Spector, Yvan Saeys, Mario Roederer
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryHigh-dimensional flow cytometry is the gold standard to study the human immune system in large cohorts. However, large sample sizes increase inter-experimental variation because of technical and experimental inaccuracies introduced by batch variability. Our high-throughput sample processing pipeline in combination with 28-color flow cytometry focuses on increased throughput (192 samples/experiment) and high reproducibility. We implemented quality control checkpoints to reduce technical and experimental variation. Finally, we integrated FlowSOM clustering to facilitate automated data analysis and demonstrate the reproducibility of our pipeline in a study with 3,357 samples. We reveal age-associated immune dynamics in 2,300 individuals, signified by decreasing T and B cell subsets with age. In addition, by combining genetic analyses, our approach revealed unique immune signatures associated with a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) that abrogates CD45 isoform splicing. In summary, we provide a versatile and reliable high-throughput, flow cytometry-based pipeline for immune discovery and exploration in large cohorts.Keywords: immunophenotyping studies, high-dimensional flow cytometry, large cohorts, human immunology, flow cytometry, immune homeostasis, immune regulation, immune aging

Introduction

Recent advances in high-dimensional flow cytometry enable the analysis of >30 parameters per cell in millions of cells from thousands of specimens and thereby provide insights into human immune responses at unprecedented depth. The high-throughput capabilities of flow cytometry are particularly useful for large immunophenotyping studies. However, large sample numbers necessitate multiple experiments, which leads to non-biological, inter-assay data variation. Thus, rigorous sample processing and data analysis pipelines are necessary to achieve high measurement precision and minimal non-biological data variation.

Several sources of errors occur in flow cytometry-based studies. Suboptimal and inconsistent sample processing reduces cell viability and alters cellular phenotypes, which results in non-biological artifacts. Thus, optimized and standardized procedures are required to ensure consistent sample handling. Furthermore, variable instrument performance leads to inconsistent data between instruments. Rigorous instrument setup and performance tracking are essential for consistent signal and data generation.1,2,3

We developed a sample processing and data analysis pipeline suitable for large human immunophenotyping studies. Our optimization strategies and sample processing pipeline can be easily adapted to accommodate distinct study requirements, such as unique staining protocols or specimens (e.g., tissue samples).

Furthermore, we implemented strategies to minimize non-biological variation as well as quality control (QC) measures to monitor experiment progress. We increased sample throughput to 192 per experiment to accelerate analysis as well as to reduce the number of experiments and therefore minimize inter-assay variation. Using our pipeline in a study with 3,357 samples across 19 experiments conducted over 6 months, we achieved high reproducibility and measurement precision that enabled automated and/or reproducible manual gating.

Unsupervised data analysis algorithms facilitate the automated identification of relevant immune features from complex multidimensional datasets.4,5 However, these algorithms can be computationally demanding and inadequate for large datasets. We implemented a rigorous data pre-processing and analysis pipeline suitable for large and complex flow cytometry datasets. The analysis pipeline includes removal of irregular raw data using FlowAI,6 batch correction with CytoNorm,7 unsupervised clustering with FlowSOM,8 and data visualization with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).9

Here, we demonstrate the value of our sample handling and data analysis pipeline by revealing longitudinal intra-individual and age-dependent immune dynamics. Previous studies describe low intra-individual variation of the immune composition over months.10,11,12 We extend these studies and demonstrate that both immune composition and the capacity of T cell effector functions remain stable for up to 10 years. Thus, our results highlight the remarkable regulation of immune homeostasis in healthy individuals over a decade. However, aging may cause impairment in the immune system,13 signified by changes in the cellular composition14 and reduced functional capacity of immune cells.13,15 Ultimately, the extent of inflammation during aging is a predictor of longevity,16 which suggests that age-dependent immune alterations affect health in the elderly. Thus, a better understanding of age-related immune dynamics is critical to better define factors that influence the risk of mortality.16

Overall, we provide a highly optimized and versatile pipeline for sample processing and unsupervised data analysis in combination with high-dimensional flow cytometry. We demonstrate the usability of our pipeline by identifying age-dependent changes in the immune composition in 2,300 individuals. Furthermore, our approach revealed a rare immune signature in ∼2% of individuals that was associated with a mutation that prevents splicing of CD45RA into CD45RO during T cell differentiation. These individuals are characterized by high frequencies of CD45RA-expressing, non-naive T cells. Thus, our results are important for immunophenotyping studies relying on CD45 isoform expression for identifying subsets of leukocytes. In conclusion, our pipeline ensures high throughput, high reproducibility, and low non-biological data variation and serves as a blueprint for large flow cytometry-based immunophenotyping studies with thousands of samples.

Results

Cohorts and staining panels

Our study aims to investigate genetic, environmental, and demographic factors that contribute to the regulation of immune homeostasis. Such studies require large sample sizes to ensure statistical robustness. Thus, we included a large number of samples from two cohorts: namely, the TwinsUK (n = 2,454) and Vaccine Research Center (VRC) cohort (n = 903). Twin cohorts are a powerful tool to investigate heritability17; however, the TwinsUK cohort is relatively homogeneous with regard to gender and race. In contrast, the VRC cohort is more diverse in terms of demographics (Table S1; STAR Methods). Peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples from both cohorts were stored in liquid nitrogen for several years prior to analysis (TwinsUK, days mean ± 1 SD = 1,600 ± 857; VRC, 3,948 ± 1,391). We used our high-throughput sample processing and data analysis pipeline in combination with high-dimensional flow cytometry to characterize the peripheral immune system as well as T cell effector functions upon in vitro stimulation. The flow cytometry staining panels used in this study are described elsewhere.18,19,20 Samples were processed in 19 independent, week-long experiments carried out over 6 months. We defined two different classes of immune traits: (1) abundance of immune subsets expressed as percent of parent cell population or in relation to other cell populations and (2) the expression levels of functional receptors as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). Robustness of these two experimental traits was the primary value we optimized.

Experimental procedure

Large immunophenotyping studies carried out over multiple experiments are prone to experimental errors and increased data variability. However, increasing sample throughput reduces inter-assay batch variability but substantially increases the risk and impact of procedural and experimental errors. We aimed to test 192 samples per experiment, which translates to approximately $42,000 of reagent costs (average cost of ∼$2.5/reagent/sample) for three 28-color panels. Therefore, a sample processing pipeline necessitates prevention of loss of samples as well as failure of expensive experiments. To this end, we implemented thorough QC procedures to ensure low inter-experimental data variation and avoid experimental errors. The experimental workflow is visualized in Figures 1A and 1B. The STAR Methods and step-by-step protocols in Methods S1 contain detailed information about method optimization, experimental procedures, and protocols.

Traditionally, cryovials are partially thawed in a 37°C water bath for 5–10 min, followed by a washing step with pre-warmed cell culture medium. In contrast, the Thawsome protocol requires a 5-min centrifugation step to pellet cells, followed by washing with pre-warmed cell culture medium. Our optimized thawing protocol substantially accelerates and standardizes the thawing procedure and enabled us to thaw 192 samples in roughly 2.5 h with two lab personnel.

Briefly, we reserved the first day for experiment planning and preparation. During this preparation phase, we pre-mixed staining reagents. This enabled us to assess the completeness of staining reagent cocktails (referred to as reagent QC) prior to sample processing (Figures 1A and S1). Furthermore, we pre-stained compensation controls to correct for spectral overlap of fluorochromes (referred to as spectral compensation).22 Pre-mixing and storage at 4°C did not affect staining reagent cocktails (Figure S2) or pre-stained compensation controls (Figure S3).

The next day, we thawed cryopreserved PBMCs using the “Thawsome” device.21 This device enabled us to thaw 192 samples in less than 3 h with high viability and yield (Figures 1C and 1D). Cells were then split across three staining panels. Two panels aimed to assess the peripheral composition of the immune system. These two panels focused on B cells and myeloid cells19 or natural killer (NK) cells and innate and conventional T cells20 and are referred to as the BDC and TNK panels, respectively. The third panel enabled the assessment of T cell functions by intracellular staining (ICS) of cytokines and activation markers (referred to as the ICS panel).18 These molecules are optimally detectable intracellularly after 6 h of in vitro stimulation using phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) and ionomycin (Figure S4A). Because of the time-consuming staining protocol, we included an overnight resting step for the ICS panel.23 Furthermore, resting of PBMCs can results in the loss of apoptotic cells, which is often preferred for detection of rare cytokine-expressing T cells. Additionally, apoptotic cells can cause unspecific binding of staining reagent and thus negatively impact assay precision.24,25,26 A resting step was not feasible for the two surface panels because of substantial shifts in the immune cell composition, which were particularly evident within monocytes and NK cells (Figures S4B–S4D). Data acquisition occurred on the third and fourth day for the surface panels and T cell effector panel, respectively. We started data pre-processing on days 4 and 5. A more detailed step-by-step protocol is included in Methods S1.

Data pre-processing and QC

In flow cytometry analysis, several pre-processing steps are required to eliminate data artifacts.5 For instance, measurement errors can arise because of instrument pressure irregularities or partial clogging leading to fluorescence signal shifts that adversely affect data quality. FlowAI efficiently and automatically detects and removes such data irregularities.6 Our FlowAI analysis showed >10% irregular events in only 2.5% and 5% of the samples from the ICS panel and BDC and TNK panels, respectively (Figures S5A and S5B). We removed irregular events prior to downstream data pre-processing and analysis; none of the samples had to be entirely discarded.

Subsequently, we performed spectral compensation using single-stained controls (STAR Methods; Methods S1). Reagent-capturing beads are commonly used for compensation because of their bright and uniform signal. However, we used a mix of cell- and bead-based single-stained controls because spectral characteristics of fluorochromes can differ between these two staining matrices, and under certain circumstances, the latter is more accurate (Figure S3). After spectral compensation, we “cleaned” the data by removing unwanted events, such as dead cells and cell debris, which lead to staining artifacts.27 Most samples showed viability above 75% for the surface panels (TNK and BDC) (Figures 2 and S5C). In contrast, some samples from the ICS panel had dramatically lower viability (<25%, n = 768), which was not evident with the surface panels (Figure 2). These low-quality samples showed distinct immune composition within all three panels, suggesting that the sample viability and quality are compromised but not detectable with viability dye immediately after thawing (Figures 2C and S5D). Consequently, we removed data from all three panels for samples with viability lower than 25% in the ICS panel because the low quality of some samples may lead to compromised data quality and flawed data interpretation (Figures 2 and S5D). After data-pre-processing, we continued with data analysis using manual gating or unsupervised data analysis to define immune traits.

Data consistency and inter- and intra-individual data variation

We included several QC steps to facilitate the elimination of experimental and technical errors and quantify biological and non-biological data variation. Rigorous instrument characterization, standardization, and performance tracking procedures ensure consistent performance over time and between instruments.1,2,28 Beyond these procedures, we used beads with defined amounts of fluorochromes (BD FC beads, BD Biosciences) to quantify technical variation (i.e., variability of instrument performance) across experiments. Although these beads are not available for each fluorochrome, they enabled us to assess at least one channel from each laser except the UV laser (355 nm) (Figure 3A). We observed minimal technical variation in both instruments used for our study (instrument 1: coefficient of variation [%CV] range 2%–12.9%; instrument 2: %CV range 2.46%–7.5%) (Figure 3A). However, one outlier (run 5) on instrument 1 resulted in increased %CV values (Figure 3A). Daily instrument QC is the primary procedure to ensure consistent instrument performance,1,2 and we used daily instrument QC as a measure to decide whether to proceed with data acquisition. The BD FC beads were solely used to quantify variation in instrument performance retrospectively but were not used to inform any decision regarding data acquisition or exclusion. Instrument QC passed on the day of sample acquisition for the fifth experiment. Therefore, we continued with sample acquisition and included the data in our analysis because the source of error of the FC bead variation was likely not related to instrument performance. In addition, no substantial deviation of the data from the fifth experiment occurred, as shown later with the QC sample data and the unsupervised data analysis. Thus, we decided that the FC bead outlier must have derived from an operational error specific to these beads and not due to increased technical variation or instrument error.

Optimal instrument performance ensures comparable signal intensities and will result in coherent compensation and spill-over spreading values across experiments. Indeed, the variance of compensation values and spill-over spreading error (SSE) across all 19 experiments was low for most fluorochrome combinations (Figures S6A–S6C). The SSE reflects the inherent measurement error caused by (among other things) photon counting statistics, which is often the major source of signal background.29 Thus, consistent SSE and signal intensities increase measurement precision and ensure that the same boundaries in manual gating can be applied across experiments.

While daily instrument QC1,2 and BD FC beads are suitable tools to assess instrument performance, other sources of error during experimental procedures may occur. In order to quantify the sum of all technical and experimental errors and determine measurement precision, we included a sample of PBMCs from the same donor and batch of cells in each experiment (referred to as QC sample) (Figure 3B).

High precision is critical to accurately measure and enumerate immune traits by flow cytometry. The precision of cell frequency measurements is dependent on the number of cells acquired and is, at a minimum, Poisson counting error.30 We assessed the variation of immune cell frequencies (n = 1,016) from the QC sample across the 19 experiments as a function of cell counts. Most immune subsets aligned along the Poisson distribution (Figure 3B). Thus, we achieved data variation close to the smallest possible error because of Poisson counting error (Figure 3B). This was also reflected when we compared the variation of 108 main immune cell subsets between the 19 QC samples and all 2,300 unique samples (VRC = 791, TwinsUK = 1,509) (Figure 3C), suggesting that we achieve high precision to enumerate immune cell populations.

Many functional markers, such as PD-1, are expressed at low levels, and their measurement may be more affected by signal variation compared with well-resolved lineage markers. In addition, the analysis of expression levels (MFI) is often more sensitive to small signal variation compared to enumeration of immune cell lineages. However, we obtained comparable MFI values from the QC samples, as shown for PD-1 (%CV = 2.327%) on CD8+ T cells (Figure 4A). MFI signals were consistent across all 3,357 samples, as outlined for B cell markers (Figure S6D). In general, %CV values derived from MFI measurements on immune cell populations were lower across the 19 QC samples compared with variability across all 2,300 individuals, demonstrating the high reproducibility and precision achieved with our pipeline even for expression quantification (Figure 4B). Similarly, variation of cytokines and activation markers in CD4+, CD8+, and γδ T cells upon in vitro stimulation showed lower variation within the QC sample compared with all 2,300 individuals in frequency and MFI measurements (Figure S7).

Longitudinal assessment of intra-individual immune changes

Previous studies suggest low intra-individual variation of the immune composition within 1 year.10,11,12 We analyzed longitudinal samples from 158 individuals enrolled in the TwinsUK cohort with up to 10 years between time points (Figure 5A). This analysis enabled us to determine intra-individual immune variation over a much longer period than previously described. We compared the inter-individual with the longitudinal intra-individual immune variation to assess the temporal stability of the immune system. Indeed, the temporal immune variation within an individual is smaller compared with inter-individual variation (Figure 5B). We used the change ratio based on the earliest time point per individual to assess the magnitude of change over time. Our analysis suggests that the frequency of most immune traits and the expression level of various receptors were stable for a period of at least 10 years (Figures 5C, 5D, S8A, and S8B).

The magnitude of T cell responses is determined by several factors, such as duration and affinity of T cell receptor (TCR)-induced stimulation.31 However, it remains unknown whether the inherent intra-individual magnitude by which T cells can respond to activation varies over time. To this end, we stimulated PBMCs with PMA and ionomycin, which mimic T cell activation downstream of the TCR.32 Therefore, our data reflect the maximal responsiveness of the T cell pool per individual irrespective of the TCR repertoire and antigen affinity. In our analysis, T cell effector functions in response to in vitro PMA/ionomycin stimulation showed remarkable longitudinal stability over several years (Figures S8C and S8D). Our data confirm and extend previous results suggesting that the immune composition and T cell functionality are tightly regulated and stable over time. In addition, our analysis demonstrates that the high measurement precision achieved with our pipeline enables the precise assessment of temporal changes in the immune system.

Implementing unsupervised data exploration with FlowSOM clustering for large datasets

We extended our pipeline with unsupervised clustering and data exploration to define immune cell subsets beyond manual analysis (Figure S9A). Unsupervised data analysis tools assess the entire high-dimensional space and are therefore considered less biased in defining immune cell subsets compared with manual gating.4,33,34 Consequently, these algorithms can potentially lead to unprecedented insights into immune dynamics in human diseases. However, several pitfalls can arise if these tools are not adequately applied or if the data input is not of sufficient quality, as described previously.5 Despite these caveats, these analysis tools, if applied correctly, enable automated and rapid analysis of flow cytometry data and accelerate exploratory discovery.

We applied the clustering algorithm FlowSOM to cells expressing HLA-DR and/or CD14 from the BDC panel and all viable cells from the TNK panel (Figure S9A).8 Some cytokines (e.g., interleukin-13 [IL-13]) are marginally expressed despite broad T cell stimulation. Current clustering algorithms are not suitable for the ICS data because of insufficient sensitivity in detecting these rare populations. The computational demand of current clustering algorithms also renders them ineffective for large datasets. Thus, we trained the FlowSOM model based on the samples from the first two experiments (n = 118) and mapped all additional samples on the existing model (STAR Methods). This approach substantially expedited the clustering of all 2,587 samples. We defined 40 FlowSOM clusters (metaclusters [MCs]) per panel for each sample (2,300 individuals, 2,587 samples including longitudinal specimens) based on subset-identifying markers (Figures S9B–S9E). Notably, we obtained high reproducibility of cluster frequencies (Figure S10). Dimensionality reduction algorithms are sensitive to inter-assay differences and thus useful to detect batch effects.7 Our initial dimensionality reduction analysis of the cluster composition in all samples (n = 2,587) revealed minor batch variation with the BDC but not the TNK data (Figure S11). The batch effect derived from instrument service between experiments 4 and 5 (STAR Methods; discussion). These minute differences in sensitivity affected the unsupervised analysis (Figure S11). We used CytoNorm7 and treated experiments 1–4 and 5–19 as batch 1 and 2, respectively, which was sufficient to correct the batch effect (STAR Methods; Figures S9A and S11).

Next, we investigated how common traits such as age, gender and cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection regulate the immune composition. To this end, we performed UMAP analysis based on the 80 FlowSOM cluster abundances in 2,300 individuals (2,587 samples). We identified age and CMV serostatus but not gender as drivers of the UMAP (Figures 6A–6E). TwinsUK participants were significantly older (p < 2.2e−16, Wilcoxon) and thus showed a distinct distribution in the UMAP (Figures 6A–6C). Notably, the 19 QC samples appeared in a small group supporting the high reproducibility of clustering across experiments (Figure 6A), while no experiment-dependent distribution of samples occurred (Figure 6F).

We further investigated age-dependent changes in the abundance of FlowSOM clusters. TNK clusters declining with age included naive T cells (MC12 and MC17), mucosal-associated invariant T cells (MAIT) cells (MC5), and Vγ9+Vδ2+ T cells (MC14), while HLA-DR+ activated transitional memory CD4+ T cells (MC7) increased (Figures 6G and 6H).

Within the BDC data, CD14+CD16− classic monocytes (MC21) increased and immunoglobulin G (IgG+) memory B cells (MC26 and MC27), IgD+IgMdimCD27+ marginal zone (MZ) B cells (MC16) and CD11c+ memory B cells (MC29) declined with age (Figure 6H). MC29 contained a mix of IgG+ memory and MZ B cells.

The UMAP analysis revealed unique immune signatures (immunotypes) shared between individuals (Figure 7A). We focused on a small group of individuals with a distinct immunotype signified by dramatically increased levels of stem cell-like CD4-or CD8-expressing CCR7+/dimCD27+CD28+CD45RA+CD95+ T cells (MC6, MC13, and MC32) and reduced abundance of CD45RA−CD95+ memory T cells (MC1, MC8, and MC16) (Figures 7B and 7C). CD45RA-to-CD45RO transition occurs during T cell maturation from naive to memory T cells.35 A rare mutation in exon A of the CD45-encoding PTPRC gene from G to C at position 77 (SNP rs17612648) affects splicing from CD45RA to CD45RO and is associated with increased risk for autoimmune diseases and multiple sclerosis.36,37 Indeed, we observed stronger correlation between monozygotic (Spearman rank correlation; CD4+ stem-cell-like memory T [TSCM] cells, R = 0.9; CD8+ TSCM cells, R = 0.76) than dizygotic (Spearman rank correlation; CD4+ TSCM cells, R = 0.6; CD8+ TSCM cells, R = 0.51) twin siblings regarding the abundance of CD4+ and CD8+ TSCM cells and FlowSOM clusters with similar phenotypic characteristics (Figures 7D and 7E). This differential correlation between monozygotic and dizygotic twins suggests a strong genetic regulation of these cell subsets.38 We hypothesized that the unique immunotype with increased levels of CD45RA+ TSCM cells is, in fact, a splicing defect of memory T cells caused by the SNP rs17612648. To this end, we tested the SNP rs17612648 from these individuals from whom we had genomic data available. Indeed, individuals with the unique immunotype showed primarily a guanosine, while the other individuals carried a cytosine, in the SNP rs17612648 (Figure 7F). Notably, some individuals were misclassified by the phenotype, emphasizing that, despite impaired splicing of CD45, these individuals showed an immune signature that was more similar to individuals with normal CD45 splicing activity (Figure 7G).

Discussion

Reproducibility in large flow cytometry-based studies is challenging because non-biological data variation increases with the number of experiments and study sites.39,40 Thus, large studies require rigorous strategies to monitor and mitigate sources of data variation. We optimized our high-throughput sample processing pipeline for high-dimensional flow cytometry to ensure optimal staining resolution and minimal technical and experimental error. As part of the optimization, we increased the throughput to 192 samples per experiment to reduce the number of experiments and inter-assay variation. In addition, the extensive preparation on the first day prevented long procedures and minimized the risk of experimental errors. This included the testing of staining reagent cocktails prior to sample processing, which minimizes erroneous staining and prevents the waste of staining reagents and precious specimens. Therefore, this strategy should be considered for every immunophenotyping study under circumstances where the staining reagent cocktails are stable at 4°C for a period of at least 1–3 days.

We implemented various QC measures to track and minimize non-biological data variation. For example, we included a PBMC sample from the same donor and batch of cell preparation in each experiment. These QC samples enable the assessment of technical and experimental variation across experiments. The QC sample may have a unique immune cell composition, as seen in Figures 3C and S7. It is not necessary that the QC sample falls into the normal range of immune traits. However, it is critical that the QC sample shows reliable signal for each measured parameter and enables the definition of most, if not all, the important immune cell subsets in order to accurately assess the technical and experimental variation. Additionally, the QC sample is useful in many ways, such as correction for spectral overlap across experiments and for batch correction with CytoNorm.7 Thus, the inclusion of such a QC sample in every experiment of a large study should be considered. In contrast, BD FC beads enable the quantification of technical, instrument-derived variation. Thus, a combination of both QC measures (BD FC beads and QC sample) facilitates the distinction of different sources of non-biological variation and supports troubleshooting of instrument- and sample processing-derived issues.

Flow cytometers are standardized based on target fluorescence values,1,2 which ensures a consistent fluorescence signal over time. However, these procedures do not assess background noise variability. One of our instruments underwent maintenance between the fourth and fifth experiment, resulting in batch effects despite adjustment of the detectors to match pre-maintenance target values (Figure S11). The batch effect was based on optimized background noise after maintenance, which resulted in lower spill-over spreading. Thus, slight changes in instrument performance can affect the data even when instrument standardization protocols are implemented. We used CytoNorm to correct for the batch effect. CytoNorm applies FlowSOM clustering to the QC samples to estimate and normalize the non-biological, inter-assay data variation.7 We treated experiments acquired pre and post instrument maintenance as two datasets (STAR Methods) to correct for the batch effect because we identified its source, which was sufficient to eliminate the inter-assay data variation (Figures S9A and S11). Collectively, data normalization may be necessary to avoid batch-related data variation. CytoNorm takes the QC sample into account to estimate the correction needed and is therefore particularly efficient in correcting inter-assay data variation. Nevertheless, data normalization poses the risk of correcting true biological variation and should only be applied if necessary.5

Dead cells lead to staining artifacts and are excluded from flow cytometry analysis.41 Low viability suggests diminished sample quality resulting from suboptimal cell isolation or cryopreservation procedures. In our study, we observed a better distinction between low- and high-quality samples based on viability after resting and stimulation with PMA/ionomycin (Figures 2 and S5). In contrast, the viability of the same samples did not substantially differ when stained and acquired immediately after thawing. Nevertheless, immune composition was affected in low-quality samples from all three staining panels (Figures 2 and S5D). Thus, poor sample quality affects immune subsets and phenotypes even if no difference in viability is detectable. Notably, we noticed increased precision in genome-wide association studies in combination with our immunophenotypes after low-quality samples were removed, suggesting that low-quality samples increase non-biological variability (data not shown). Therefore, undetected samples with poor cell quality affect data precision. Our data highlight the need for standardized and optimized protocols for sample collection, cell isolation, and cryopreservation to achieve high sample quality and to avoid data artifacts. Furthermore, our data demonstrate that enumerating dead cells by using a viability dye alone may not be sufficient to identify samples with low quality when samples are stained and fixed immediately after thawing.

Our optimized sample processing and staining procedures resulted in low non-biological variation and high precision (Figures 3 and 4A). The high precision enabled us to use the same cutoffs (“manual gating”) to define immune cell populations and receptor expression across all experiments. Furthermore, high precision facilitates data analysis with unsupervised algorithms and prevents analysis artifacts due to irregular data. Precision of cell frequencies is based on event counts and, at a minimum is the Poisson counting error.42 For most immune traits we achieved precision that aligned closely with the Poisson counting error rate (Figure 3B). However, we did not achieve sufficient event counts for some traits because of their rarity, which resulted in a CV above the often-applied acceptance criterion of 30%.42 This cutoff is often not feasible in research because of the rarity of some immune cells of interest. Nevertheless, we utilized the 30% threshold in our analysis as a reference to avoid ambiguous conclusions based on highly variable traits.

Finally, we combined our pipeline with unsupervised data analysis and visualization (Figure S9A). We adapted the conventional FlowSOM analysis procedure because clustering algorithms are computationally demanding and not feasible for large datasets. Our strategy used samples from the first two experiments to train the model, and then all additional samples were mapped onto the existing model. This approach has the advantage that additional data can be mapped onto the existing model without the need to re-train the model, which enables real-time analysis. However, the training dataset needs to be sufficiently large to capture the entire cellular heterogeneity. Otherwise, new immune cell subsets from subsequent experiments will be mapped onto the existing model and not detected. Our unsupervised clustering analysis enabled fast and versatile investigation of immunological mechanisms, as shown in Figures 6 and 7. Most noticeably, our approach led to the identification of individuals with a unique immune composition characterized by high levels of stem-cell like CD4 and CD8 T cells. In combination with genetic analysis, we uncovered a unique and rare genetic variant associated with diminished splicing of CD45RA to CD45RO, a process that occurs during the maturation of T cells from the naive to the memory state.35 Expression of CD45RA and CD45RO is regulated by splicing of exons from the PTPRC gene encoding for CD45. A small number of individuals (2.3%) carry a variant (SNP rs17612648) in exon A of the CD45-encoding PTPRC gene at position 77 from G to C, which prevents splicing from CD45RA to CD45RO.36 The cluster, with unusually high frequency of TSCM cells, was highly enriched for individuals carrying a different nucleotide polymorphism at rs17612648 resulting in the G77C mutation. Thus, our analysis enables the connection between immune traits and genetic variants by combining genomics and high-dimensional phenotyping.

The immune system is remarkably stable over time.10,11,12 Here, we assessed intra-individual immune variation with up to 10 years between measured time points. We observed virtually no change in the abundance of most immune subsets over 10 years (Figures 5 and S8). This is in line with the fact that the human immune system is tightly regulated during homeostasis38 and returns to baseline after perturbations caused by infections and immune activation.43,44 The magnitude of T cell responses depends on multiple factors, such as TCR affinity and antigen load.31 Here, we measured the functional capacity of T cells irrespective of TCR repertoire using PMA/ionomycin in vitro stimulation.32 Our results demonstrate that the intra-individual functional capacity of T cells does not substantially vary over 10 years in adults. Overall, an individual maintains the quality of their T cell response over time.

Nevertheless, the immune system gradually becomes dysfunctional during aging, which affects immune responses against pathogens and the efficacy of vaccines.13 We observed declining frequencies of naive T cells (Figures 6G and 6H; TNK: MC12 and MC17) with increased age. This decline likely occurs because of reduced thymic output and peripheral homeostasis. Consequently, the reduced pool of naive T cells and TCR diversity may affect the ability to defend against pathogens.14 Similarly, our analysis confirmed previous results that suggest a decline of MAIT cells and Vγ9+Vδ2+ T cells.45,46 Innate T cells, such as MAIT cells and Vγ9+Vδ2+ T cells, respond rapidly to microbial pathogens and represent an early defense against bacterial infections.47 Thus, elderly individuals may lack an early defense line provided by unconventional T cells and may therefore be more vulnerable to infections. Memory B cell subsets declined during aging (Figures 6G and 6H; BDC: MC16, MC26, MC27, and MC29).48 In contrast, Blanco et al.49 showed that plasma IgG levels did not decline in the elderly.48 This suggests that the output of long-lived plasma cells in the bone marrow, which are the main source of serum antibody, is only minimally affected by aging, while the levels of circulating memory B cells are reduced. Therefore, the generation of memory B cells might be diminished in the elderly, which suggests increased vulnerability to recurring infections. Notably, we did not correct for CMV infection status because CMV serostatus was only available for a subset of samples in our analysis, and thus we cannot rule out CMV-dependent immune alterations in older individuals.46,50 Our analysis in Figure 6 shows that some CMV-seropositive individuals have a distinct immune composition and confirms the profound CMV-induced immune changes in some individuals.46,50 Nevertheless, our unsupervised analysis enabled us to rapidly delineate age-dependent changes in the immune composition and serves as a proof of principle for rapid discovery of immune signatures in large datasets.

Overall, our high-throughput sample processing pipeline in combination with high-dimensional flow cytometry provides a useful tool to gain precise measurements even in multiexperiment studies by eliminating or reducing potential technical or experimental errors. We provide a versatile toolbox that facilitates large immunophenotyping studies and can easily be adapted to the unique requirements of each study. Furthermore, this blueprint provides useful strategies for optimization of immunophenotyping pipelines and QC strategies necessary to ensure high reproducibility.

Limitations of the study

Data variation can derive from other sources, such as PBMC cryopreservation, as shown in Figures 2, S5C, and S5D. Our pipeline will not mitigate any data variation derived from different PBMC isolation and cryopreservation protocols implemented in different cohorts. Thus, this needs to be considered during data and statistical analysis. Furthermore, study-specific requirements (e.g., type of immune cell stimulation, specific requirements for unique staining reagents) necessitate adaptation of the pipeline, and the optimization steps described here should be repeated to ensure optimal results. Last, unsupervised data analysis strategies are often staining panel-specific (i.e., based on marker and fluorochrome selection) and the implementation of new or adapted panels requires re-optimization of the algorithm parameters in our unsupervised data analysis pipeline.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cryopreserved PBMC samples were available from healthy individuals as part of the TwinsUK (n = 2109) and VRC (n = 829) cohort. Historically, the TwinsUK cohort was established to study the genetics of autoimmune diseases in women due to their increased risk for autoimmunity.54 The VRC cohort was included as a validation cohort and is more diverse in terms of demographics such as gender and ethnicity. Demographics for both cohorts are summarized in Table S1. All study participants provided informed consent. This study was approved by the IRB of NIAID (NIH) and London-Westminster NHS Research Ethics Committee. We had access to longitudinal samples from 158 individuals from the TwinsUK cohort with 2–4 samples per individual to study intra-individual temporal immune stability (Figure 5A). The QC sample was part of the VRC cohort. The VRC cohort included longitudinal samples (n = 80) from 24 individuals enrolled in a Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) vaccine trial (VRC-317, NCT03049488).51 Samples from these individuals were collected pre-vaccination (n = 24) and 4 (n = 24), 16 (n = 16) and 44 (n = 16) weeks after vaccination with an experimental RSV vaccine.51 VRC-317 samples were only included whenever the full dataset was analyzed (Figures 2, S5 and S6D) and for initial FlowSOM computation. In total, we acquired 3357 samples per panel. Duplicated and VRC-317 samples were removed and only the first available timepoint from individuals with longitudinal samples were included for most analyses. As described below low-quality samples were removed based on viability of ICS samples. The final dataset contained data from 2300 individuals (TwinsUK, n = 1509; VRC, n = 791). The influence of age and gender on immune composition is described in more detail in Figure 6.

Method details

Staining reagent cocktails

A more detailed step-by-step protocol is included in the supplementary material (Methods S1). Staining panels used in this study are described in OMIP-051, -058 and -06018,19,20 and in Methods S1. For OMIP-051, Brilliant Blue 515 (BB515) labeled tetramer with Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV)-derived pre-fusion trimer (DS-Cav1) was included instead of CD73. DS-Cav1 tetramer production and staining was performed according to Crank MC et al.51 Staining reagents except Live/Dead UV Blue viability dye were mixed into Brilliant stain buffer plus (BD Biosciences, final dilution 1:5) and volume was adjusted with staining buffer which consisted of RPMI w/o Phenol Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 4% heat-inactivated Newborn Calf Serum (R&D Systems). For the intracellular antibody mix from the T cell effector staining (ICS) panel, staining reagents were diluted into double distilled H2O (ddH2O) containing Brilliant stain buffer plus (final dilution 1:5) and Wash/Perm buffer 10x from the eBioscience Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific, final dilution 1:10). Volume was adjusted using ddH2O. Live/Dead UV Blue viability dye was prepared fresh as described below. CD107a Ax700 was added directly to the T cell stimulation as described and was not a component of the antibody staining mix.55 For CD107a Ax700, we mixed the necessary quantity for the entire study and performed a buffer exchange with R10 as described20 since the sodium azide in the original storage buffer impairs the cytokine response likely due to cytotoxicity (Methods S1). Samples were stained in 50μL and an overage of 10% and 5% of antibody cocktail was prepared for each experiment with less and more than 100 samples, respectively. Antibody mixes were stored at 4C protected from light until the day of staining when mixes were spun at 10,000g for 5 min to remove dye aggregates. Cells were stained in 50μL reagent staining mix per sample in 96-well, V-bottom plates (Corning). All staining reagents with vendor information are listed in Methods S1 and in the key source table. Optimal reagent concentration was estimated using 8-dilution titration and full panels were tested prior to study.18,19,20 We ordered or prepared sufficient quantities of the same production lot from each staining reagent in order to mitigate production lot dependent variations. In general, we opted for 15–20% overage of reagent quantities from what we estimated for the entire study. If not possible, we mixed all lots prior to reagent titration. In case of CD3 APC-H7 from ICS panel, reagent quantity was not sufficient and had to be replaced by a new lot after 13 experiments. In addition, DS-Cav1 tetramer was only stable for two months and several batches were used for the study. In both cases, we titrated the old and new lot in parallel on the same PBMCs and matched concentration to ensure comparable signal intensity between lots.

Quality control of staining reagent cocktail

For staining reagent cocktail QC on the first day, we thawed cryopreserved PBMCs in pre-warmed R10 consisting of RPMI without glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10% heat-inactivated Fetal Bovine Serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Penicillin/Streptomycin/Glutamine 100x (Thermo Fisher Scientific, final dilution 1:100) supplemented with Benzonase (EMD Millipore, final concentration 0.5U/ml). Benzonase was added to prevent cell clumping during thawing due to released DNA from dead cells. Cells were washed once with 1mL staining buffer before staining with 25μL antibody mix for 30 min. Subsequently, PBMCs were washed twice with 1mL staining buffer and fixed using 0.5% Paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS without Ca2+ and Mg2+ (Thermo Fisher Scientific) prior to acquisition on a BD FACSymphony A5 cytometer (BD Biosciences).

For the assessment of T cell effector panel staining cocktails, we created a large batch of PBMCs stimulated in bulk for 6 h with 5 ng/ml Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Millipore Sigma) and 500 ng/ml Ionomycin (Millipore Sigma) in the presence of protein transport inhibitors Monensin (6.66 μL/mL, BD Biosciences) and Brefeldin A (10 μL/mL, BD Biosciences). Cells were subsequently stained with Live/Dead UV Blue viability dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted 1:780 in PBS for 20 min and fixed and permeabilized using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD Biosciences) according to manufacturer’s recommendation. Afterward, we cryopreserved aliquots containing 10 million cells in 1mL freezing media consisting of R10 cell culture media containing 10% DMSO (Thermo Fisher Scientific). At the day of reagent cocktail QC, we thawed cells in 10mL BD Perm/Wash buffer (BD Biosciences) followed by a permeabilization step for 20 min in 1mL BD Perm/Wash buffer. Afterward, cells were stained with 25μL of both the surface and intracellular antibody mix combined for 30 min and washed twice with 1mL BD Perm/Wash buffer prior to acquisition. Our procedure enabled us to generate a large batch of stimulated T cells for staining reagent QC without the necessity to prepare stimulated T cells for every experiment. The procedure outlined above resulted in sufficient expression of almost all markers and enabled us to assess the presence or absence of staining reagents. However, the signal intensity for some markers (IL-8, IL-13, and Ki-67) was weak or absent compared to the original staining procedure.

We developed analysis templates for each panel in FlowJo 9.9.6 to assess the staining reagent cocktails. These FlowJo templates included at least one gate for every marker/staining reagent as outlined for the BDC panel in Figure S1. We used the same compensation matrix for this staining reagent QC step across all experiments because I) we aimed for a qualitative answer (i.e., is the staining reagent present or not?) and II) due to our rigorous instrument standardization compensation values did not change dramatically over time. Thus, for this QC step we accepted some imprecision derived from suboptimal compensation. Of note, for the actual study samples we measured single-stained compensation controls and calculated spectral compensation for each experiment separately. Once the staining reagent cocktails passed this QC step, we started the sample processing the next day as described below.

Compensation controls

Antibody-capturing beads are often used for compensation controls in order to obtain very bright and uniform signal. We stained compensation beads for most staining reagents with 1μL staining reagent in 100μL staining buffer for 30 min in V-bottom 96-well plates. Subsequently, we washed the compensation controls twice with staining buffer (200μL per well) and stored the controls in 0.5% PFA (100μL per well) at 4C until acquisition. Concentration of staining reagents were adjusted as needed, in order to prevent off-scale signals and to ensure that the fluorescence signal was brighter than the signal from the samples. In most cases, compensation beads resulted in precise calculation of compensation values. However, excitation characteristics of fluorochromes can differ when measured on beads or cells for reasons not fully understood. For compensation controls we therefore used antigen-capturing beads for all three panels and buffy coat-derived PBMCs for the two surface panels in each experiment (Figure S3). We did not include cell-based compensation controls for the T cell effector function panel since these controls would require PBMC stimulation and intracellular staining, which is not practicable in a high-throughput setting. In addition, many cytokines are expressed at low levels or in only few cells which would not result in accurate compensation controls. Of note, compensation issues often intensify with higher signal intensity and are not as pronounced in the lower range of signal intensity which makes irregular compensation negligible for these markers. PBMCs from buffy coat were used for cell-based compensation controls and were stained as described in Methods S1.

Thawing

We stored cryopreserved PBMCs in cryoboxes in liquid nitrogen tanks and ensured that the order of samples in these boxes matched the processing order of each experiment. Each box contained 48 vials due to our iterative thawing approach described below and in the step-by-step protocol in Methods S1. This ensured that only a fraction of samples was on dry ice and outside of the freezer during the thawing process. All additional samples from the same experiment were temporarily stored at −70C during the thawing process (∼3 h). In order to avoid batch effects, we randomized samples from both cohorts equally across experiments. Furthermore, we ensured that all samples from the same individual (longitudinal samples) and samples from siblings were analyzed within the same run. For the TwinsUK samples, we aimed to have monozygotic and dizygotic twin pairs equally distributed across experiments.

We started the first experiment with 48 samples and gradually increased the number of samples by 48 samples per experiment up to 192 samples in order to test the throughput capabilities of our pipeline. 192 samples were the maximum due to instrumentation, but the throughput of our pipeline can potentially be further increased.

At the day of sample processing, the thawing process was performed by two lab personnel and samples were thawed in batches of 16 cryovials per cycle (Figure 1). Potentially, more samples can be thawed per iteration but 16 cryovials per iteration worked best in order to synchronize the work of both lab personnel. 16 cryovials were thawed simultaneously into 14mL conical collection tubes containing 9mL of pre-warmed R10 supplemented with Benzonase (EMD Millipore, final concentration 0.5U/ml) using the Thawsome device.21 Collection tubes were prepared on the first day and slowly warmed up from 4C to 37C with a temperature rate-controlled incubator to ensure optimal temperature at the second day when we started sample processing. Initial centrifugation with the Thawsome was done at 700g for 5 min and all subsequent centrifugation steps were performed at 863g for 5 min. After the first iteration one person switched to washing cells with 5mL R10 medium, resuspending cells in 200μL R10 and distributing 50μL of cell suspension per well to 96-well V-bottom plates containing 200μL/well R10 for the two surface staining panels and 48-well flat-bottom plate (Corning) for overnight resting in the case of the ICS panel. Cells in the 48-well plate were rested in 500μL R10 at 37C overnight while staining of surface panels was performed immediately after thawing.

Staining of surface panels

Our PBMC samples were cryopreserved at a cell number of 3x106 to 107 cells per vial. We used one-fourth of these cells for each of the panels which results in a theoretical range of 7.5x105 - 2.5x106 cells per staining which is common in flow cytometry. Cells in 96-well V-bottom plates were washed once with 250μL PBS per well and stained in 100μL freshly prepared Live/Dead UV Blue viability dye diluted in PBS for 20 min at room temperature. For the BDC panel, Fc receptor blocking reagent was added at a dilution of 1:20 to the viability dye (BD Biosciences). The Fc blocking reagent is based on protein and can potentially affect the performance of the viability dye. We previously confirmed that combining viability dye and Fc blocking reagent immediately before staining only marginally affects the viability dye resolution.19 Subsequently, cells were spun down, and the supernatant was discarded. Cells were stained in 50μL staining reagent cocktails per well for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were washed twice with 250μL staining buffer per well and fixed overnight in 250μL 0.5% PFA (Tousimis) in PBS at 4C prior to acquisition with a FACSymphony A5 cytometer (BD Biosciences). We tested prior to the study that overnight fixation did not affect fluorescence signal compared to the routinely performed 30 min fixation with 0.5% PFA (data not shown).

T cell stimulation and intracellular staining

After overnight resting, cells were transferred from the 48-well to a 96-well V-bottom plate using an adjustable spacer manual pipette. Cells were washed once with 250μL/well R10 and resuspended in 100μL/well R10 containing 5 ng/ml Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate, 500 ng/ml Ionomycin, protein transport inhibitors Monensin (6.66 μL/mL) and Brefeldin A (10 μL/mL) and Alexa Fluor 700 labeled CD107a. Cells were incubated for 6 h at 37C in 5% CO2 incubator. We tested stimulation duration with PMA/Ionomycin to obtain optimal cytokine responses and upregulation of activation and co-stimulatory receptors (Figure S4A). Some cytokines and receptors continuously increased over time such as IFNγ and CD137. However, we opted for 6 h of stimulation since some of the rare cytokines such as IL-13 and IL-17A declined after 6 h and we put higher priority to the detection of rare cytokines at the cost of more abundant markers (Figure S4A). Subsequently, cells were washed once with 250μL PBS and stained with 100μL Live/Dead UV Blue viability dye diluted in PBS for 20 min. Next, cells were stained with the surface staining reagent cocktail (50μL/well) for 30 min at room temperature. Fixation and Permeabilization were performed using the eBioscience FoxP3/Transcription factor buffer set according to manufacturer’s recommendation. To this end, cells were incubated in 200μL Fix/Perm solution for 45 min at 4C and washed twice with 200μL Wash/Perm solution. Afterward, cells were stained with the 50μL intracellular staining reagent cocktail for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, cells were washed twice with 200μL Wash/Perm solution and once with 200μL staining buffer. Cells were stored overnight at 4C in 250μL staining buffer prior to data acquisition.

Data acquisition and pre-processing

We performed vigorous instrument optimization, calibration and tracking to ensure optimal and consistent performance. Detailed protocols exist based on hard-dyed beads.1,2 More recently, procedures with light emitting diode (LED) pulser have been implemented.28 Both protocols are based on the same principle where the optimal separation between background noise and fluorescent signal is determined for each detector and transformed into a target value with fluorescent beads. The target value is used to calibrate the instrument daily. Before sample acquisition, we performed daily QC to ensure consistent instrument performance.

Cells were resuspended with a multichannel pipette and acquired using a BD FACSymphony A5 cytometer (BD Biosciences) with a configuration described elsewhere.20 Forward scatter parameter was set based on lymphocyte population at 100K to ensure similar forward and side scatter patterns across experiments. This facilitated the definition of lymphocytes during data analysis across experiments. Samples were acquired with a high-throughput sampler system. We split samples from one plate to the first six columns of two plates and added three washing columns. The first wash column in the seventh column of the 96-well plate contained sodium 0.1M hydroxide followed by two columns with ddH2O. Rigorous washing is necessary to ensure uncompromised data acquisition and to prevent clogging of the sample line. While our instrument characterization and performance QC strategy ensures comparable data across multiple instruments, we measured all samples from one panel on the same instrument in order to avoid any instrument-to-instrument variability. Due to technical issues on the 19th experiment for the T cell effector function panel (ICS panel), we had to switch to the second instrument. Nevertheless, we did not observe substantially different deviations compared to the previous experiments based on the QC sample.

After data acquisition, we used an R-implemented (R version 4.0.0) version of FlowAI (v1.18.5) to cleanup data from potential irregular events due to pressure instabilities or other issues.6 We generated a new set of data files with an additional parameter introduced by FlowAI which enabled the discrimination of low- and high-quality events. If a sample had less than 10% low-quality events, we removed these events without further inspecting the file. For the few samples with more than 10% low-quality events, we manually inspected the sample in FlowJo 9.9.6 and excluded all irregular events according to the FlowAI output (Figures S5A and S5B). We included data from all samples after removal of low-quality events.

After FlowAI, we continued with the correction for spectral overlap in FlowJo 9.9.6. In the first experiment, we iteratively assessed compensation issues and whether cell-based compensation controls can solve these issues (Figure S3). For subsequent experiments we used cell-based controls for these fluorochromes to calculate spectral compensation. The remaining fluorochrome pairs which could not be improved by cell-based compensation controls were corrected manually. We used the QC sample to evaluate differences in compensation across experiments. With this approach we obtained reproducible and comparable compensation (Figures S6A–S6C). After correction of spectral overlap, we created new data files using FlowJo 9.9.6 containing only viable cells. For the T cell effector function panel (ICS) we exported viable CD3+ T cells as the in vitro stimulation resulted in increased cell death and debris. Exclusion of irrelevant events such as debris, other non-cellular events and dead cells minimizes computational demand per sample.

Data analysis – Manual gating

We transformed fluorescence signals using biexponential transformation with parameter-specific values and manually defined immune traits with biaxial gating using FlowJo 10.7. Immune traits can be divided in two main categories, namely frequency and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). We defined a total of 4669 immune traits of which 1035 were frequency (BDC = 71, TNK = 213, ICS = 751) and 3634 MFI (BDC = 441, TNK = 2437, ICS = 756) traits, respectively. We used samples from the first experiment to generate template FlowJo workspaces and additional samples from each experiment were loaded separately to these templates due to exhaustion of computational resources. We created movies consisting of the gating tree of each sample across all experiments to assess the accuracy of the gating. The same gates in all three panels were applied to all samples except for the RSV probe and Granzyme B (GzmB). 12 out of 3357 samples showed unusually high background with the RSV probe for which we adjusted gates. The GzmB staining reagent results in donor-specific background noise as described20 which required donor-specific gating of Granzyme B-expressing cells. Therefore, GzmB traits were not included in any of the analyses. As discussed in the results and discussion section, quality of biological specimen can be affected by isolation and cryopreservation methods which results in artificially distinct immune phenotypes (Figures 2, S5C and S5D). We identified low-quality samples using a viability cutoff of 25% from the ICS panel and excluded these samples from all three staining panels for statistical analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

We used mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) instead of median fluorescence intensity to investigate expression levels. While median is more often used in flow cytometry to determine expression levels and is more robust to outliers compared to mean it is less sensitive to minute changes in expression or large changes in only a small fraction of cells. Thus, we computed mean instead of median fluorescence signal values to accommodate for the fact that certain receptors may be expressed in only small fractions of immune populations.

MFI values within the range of autofluorescence do not reflect precise measurements. Thus, we aimed to filter samples with robust expression for comparison with the QC sample (inter-assay variation). To this end, for analysis in Figures 4B and S7B, background values were subtracted from MFI values. Background MFI values were based on the average 84th percentile fluorescence intensity of unstained and compensated PBMCs across all 19 experiments. Subsequently, samples from immune traits with corrected values below or equal to 1 were removed from the analysis.

We excluded immune traits and samples with more than 80% missing values for viability analysis shown in Figure S5D to avoid small sample sizes per trait for comparison between low- and high-quality samples. This analysis excluded 66 samples and 349 immune traits with 4320 remaining immune traits and 3208 samples. All 66 excluded samples derived from the TwinsUK and 65 showed low viability (<25% in ICS panel).

Genetic data was available from 1328 individuals (AFR = 202, EUR = 540, TwinsUK = 586). From the TwinsUK twins only one sibling was used to avoid bias derived from family structure. Genetic data was obtained as described.38 Serology against the human virome was measured in 764 individuals from the VRC cohort with PhiP-Seq as described56 and CMV serostatus was calculated based on the AVARDA algorithm.57

To assess intra-individual longitudinal changes, we determined the change ratio for each individual. Change ratio is defined as frequency at time x subtracted by frequency from the first timepoint divided by frequency from first timepoint. In addition, %CV from longitudinal samples is defined as the average of all intra-individual %CV between timepoints per immune trait.

Unsupervised data analysis

We used the compensated raw data containing only viable cells from all 2587 and 2589 samples from the TNK and BDC panel, respectively, after excluding low quality samples based on viability (ICS panel viability <25%). Two samples from the TNK panel had low cell counts and were excluded from the analysis. The same two samples were excluded from the BDC data for immunotype analysis shown in Figures 6 and 7. We performed data preprocessing for the automated pipeline, including transformation of the fcs (flow cytometry standard) files. The transformation was determined by the estimateLogicle function of the flowCore package (v2.6.0) on the first sample (“1.fcs”) of the panel and applied to all samples. For the BDC panel, a fixed gate to select only CD14 and/or HLA-DR positive cells was included.

From these preprocessed files, samples of Run 1 and 2 were included in the training dataset. An aggregate was created containing up to 50,000 cells per file, which was then used as input for the FlowSOM training, using the FlowSOM R package (v2.0.0). We used a 12x12 grid (resulting in 144 clusters), 40 metaclusters, and only the lineage markers for training. Lineage markers were defined as markers which were used in the manual gating to delineate immune cell subsets. We limited the analysis to these markers in order to maintain some degree of comparability with the manual analysis. All other FlowSOM parameters were kept as default. Once the model was trained, all samples from all runs were mapped onto the model. We extracted metacluster percentages and median fluorescence intensities as outcome.

Samples stained with TNK and BDC panel were acquired on separate instruments. The instrument used to measure samples stained with the BDC panel was serviced by the manufacturer between the fourth and fifth experiment which caused increased sensitivity (i.e., reduced spill-over spreading error). As mentioned above, the instruments were standardized across the entire study to meet the same signal target value. Thus, while the signal intensity did not change, we obtained improved detector sensitivity (i.e., background noise) which was sufficient to affect the FlowSOM analysis resulting in distinct immune clusters. Notably, manual analysis was less affected due to panel development strategies implemented to mitigate spill-over spreading issues. However, unsupervised analysis algorithms consider the full high-dimensional space and are more likely to be affected by such issues. Thus, we created a normalized version of the BDC raw data files. We used the CytoNorm R package (v0.0.7),7 which was trained with 2 batches, one consisting of the QC samples from run 1–4 and the other consisting of the QC samples from run 5–19. For the normalization, an internal FlowSOM model with 1,000,000 cells, 144 clusters and 10 metaclusters was used. To estimate the splines, 49 quantiles were used, going from 2 percent to 98 percent in steps of 2 percent, plus two fixed identity points on −1 and 4 (which fall on the outer boundaries of the expected range after transformation). Batch 1 was set as the goal distribution and all samples of the BDC panel were normalized using this model (as batch 1 was the goal distribution, applying the normalization on run 1–4 applied the identity function and did not change any values, only values from files from batch 2 (run 5–19) were adapted). These normalized files were then mapped onto the original FlowSOM model. An overview of the unsupervised analysis pipeline is depicted in Figure S9A.

Using the 40 metacluster percentages from the TNK panel and the 40 metacluster percentages from the normalized BDC panel, a two-dimensional projection of the donors was created using the umap function from the uwot R package (v0.1.11), with column-scaled input values and the n_neighbors parameter set to 10.

For UMAP and related analysis shown in Figures 6 and 7 we included only one sample per individual and excluded the VRC-317 samples. This resulted in the inclusion of samples from 2300 individuals. The exact number of samples used per group are described in detail in the figure captions for Figures 6 and 7. For comparison of these features with age, Spearman correlation was computed using only pairwise complete observations. For comparison between the two immunophenotypes, the subimmunotype phenotype was manually selected based on the UMAP coordinates and comparison was done using a Wilcoxon test. All p values were corrected using Bonferroni multiple testing correction if not otherwise stated.
