Deciphering the interplay between oxidative stress and inflammation in polycystic ovary syndrome: outcome of a case-control study
Authors: Khurshid Ahmad Padder, Nusrat Jahan, Mohmad Aadil Yousuf, Idrees Ayoub Shah, Gulzar Ahmad Bhat, Rabia Khurshid, Mahrukh Hameed Zargar, Mohd Ashraf Ganie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextLow-grade inflammation (LGI) and oxidative stress (OS) are key features in women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), but the interplay between these factors and genetic makeup in PCOS pathogenesis remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study investigated OS indicators, sub-inflammation, and their correlations in women with hyperandrogenic and insulin-resistant PCOS, examining the relationship with WNT3 gene expression.MethodologyA case-control study included 270 age-matched women (135 with PCOS and 135 healthy controls). Participants underwent detailed medical and clinical examinations, followed by biochemical, hormonal, inflammation, and OS and WNT3 gene expression assessments.ResultsSerum levels of inflammatory markers TNFα (24.98 ± 3.07vs.20.07 ± 6.83), IL6 (4.08 ± 0.90vs.3.09 ± 0.90), and IL1β (12.03 ± 2.81vs.8.61 ± 4.02) were significantly higher in women with PCOS compared to controls (p < 0.001). Total oxidant stress (TOS) was significantly elevated in PCOS cases (6.16 ± 0.46vs.1.82 ± 0.71), whereas antioxidant enzymes Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) and catalase were higher in controls (p < 0.001). Waist circumference positively correlated with IL1β, LDL, and triglycerides, and negatively with HOMA, QUICKI, HDL, and TNFα (p < 0.05). TNFα positively correlated with IL1β (r = 0.26, p < 0.0001). TOS correlated positively with HOMA, TNFα, IL1β, and LDL, and negatively with SOD, catalase, and testosterone (p < 0.0001). Catalase positively correlated with QUICKI and SOD, and negatively with HOMA and TOS (p < 0.0001). WNT3 gene mRNA expression was undetectable in peripheral blood samples from both PCOS cases and controls.ConclusionThe study underscores the significant role of OS and sub-inflammation in PCOS pathophysiology. However, WNT3 gene expression in peripheral blood was undetectable; suggesting further research with different tissues or more sensitive methods is needed.Keywords: Polycystic ovary syndrome, Oxidative stress, Inflammation, SOD, Catalase

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common and complex endocrine condition that affects 8–20% of reproductive-age women [1, 2]. Although the primary hormonal abnormalities associated with PCOS are insulin resistance (IR) and hyperandrogenism, the long-term metabolic effects of PCOS are also attributed to the effects of OS and chronic low-grade inflammation (LGI) [3–7]. LGI is considered one of the main factors associated with the pathogenesis of PCOS [8]. Additionally, OS, i.e. the imbalance between free radical production and antioxidant defenses, might contribute to PCOS and its metabolic manifestations [9, 10]. Lipid peroxidation induced by OS alters the protein and DNA promoting the risk of many diseases such as cancer and cardiovascular diseases due to effects on redox-sensitive signaling pathways and gene expression [11]. Many studies have shown that OS plays an important role in reproductive system physiology [12–14] and suggested multiple biomarkers for measuring total oxidant status and antioxidant levels in PCOS patients [15]. The total amount of oxidant or antioxidant defenses present in serum could be evaluated by measuring the total oxidant status (TOS) or the levels of antioxidant enzymes [9, 16], respectively.

To date, mechanisms relating to OS, inflammation, and PCOS-associated metabolic abnormalities such as hyperandrogenism and IR have not been addressed clearly. With very few notable exceptions [17, 18], these studies have been carried out with smaller sample sizes, different criteria employed, variation in cutoffs used, and multiple ethnicities. Several PCOS-associated characteristics like androgen excess levels, abdominal adiposity, IR, and obesity may contribute to the development of local and systemic OS and inflammation [19] which may reciprocally worsen these metabolic abnormalities. Additionally, specific inflammatory pathways have been identified as contributing to the dysregulated immune response seen in PCOS, further underscoring the role of inflammation in the syndrome’s metabolic, systemic and reproductive manifestations [20, 21].

The WNT pathway, as a conserved regulator, plays a key role in tissue homeostasis, which is disrupted in PCOS patients [22, 23]. The regulation of the WNT signaling by steroid hormones is widely recognized to affect endometrial proliferation [24], embryo implantation [25], placenta development [26], endometrial decidualization [27], and folliculogenesis in the adult ovary [28]. Previous literature had demonstrated that significant differences were observed in the expression levels of genes involved in inflammation, lipid metabolism and WNT signaling in the adipose tissues of non-obese PCOS patients vs. BMI-matched controls [23, 29, 30]. The regulatory role of these genes and pathways affects adipogenesis and/or IR contributing to the metabolic abnormalities of PCOS [31]. Genetic evidence of PCOS also comes from the presence of its components, such as androgenic T-2 diabetes mellitus, in the first-degree family of PCOS patients [32]. The expression of WNT ligands, such as WNT3, WNT7a and WNT8b, which activate the canonical pathway during endometrial development, has been reported in several studies [33]. These ligands are glycoproteins that play a similar function in endometrial re-formation during the menstrual cycle. WNT3 gene consists of a single N-terminal peptide and two N-linked glycosylation sites [34] and is most similar to WNT3A (84.2% total amino-acid identity) among other human WNTs [35]. Literature reported that the onset and progression of OS are also closely linked to WNT3A/β-catenin signaling [36]. WNT3 was significantly up-regulated in various human disorders [34] through activation of the WNT/β-catenin-TCF signaling pathway [37]. Additionally, WNT3 and WNT8b’s involvement in the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle is demonstrated by their up-regulation in the proliferative phase of the endometrium [33]. It has been shown that WNT3 gene expression down-regulation regulates follicle development, ovulation, and luteinization [38]. Given this evidence and the paucity of knowledge, particularly regarding WNT3 gene expression among women with PCOS, we aimed to investigate the interplay between inflammation, OS and the role of WNT3 gene among women with PCOS.

Methodology

Study population

Women between the ages of 18–40 years reporting to the PCOS/infertility clinic SKIMS, Srinagar, Jammu, and Kashmir, India, presenting with irregular cycles, unwanted male pattern hair growth, acne, difficulty in losing weight, or with primary infertility and other PCOS-related symptoms were recruited in the study. Participants with hyperandrogenism due to causes other than PCOS, the presence of any genetic or congenital disorders, a history of medication use such as steroids or contraceptive pills/injections, substance abuse, androgenic hormonal therapy for infertility, pregnancy, or being in the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, as well as smokers, were excluded from the study. The diagnosis of PCOS was done by Rotterdam, 2003 criteria. Apparently healthy control women without any chronic conditions, acute illnesses, or significant medical complaints at the time of assessment, were recruited from various educational institutions from the Kashmir region of UT of Jammu and Kashmir, India.

Clinical assessment

Participants underwent a detailed clinical history and physical examination which included measurement of parameters like body weight, height, and waist circumference by using standard calibrated instruments (SECA 213, Hamburg Germany), and blood pressure (Omron HEM7120). A mean of three readings was taken as the final value for these parameters. Hirsutism was quantified by using a modified Ferriman-Gallwey (mFG) score (more than 8 or above out of a total of 36 from nine body areas taken as significant). All subjects were further evaluated for biochemical, hormonal, inflammatory, and OS parameters.

Biochemical and hormonal assessment

The oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was performed after an overnight fast of 10–12 h. A total of 5mL of venous blood was drawn in a fasting state between day 2 and day 7 of either spontaneous or progesterone-induced (in amenorrhea patients) menstrual cycle. Biochemical parameters like plasma glucose, insulin, cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoproteins, high-density lipoproteins, alkaline phosphatase, calcium, and phosphorous were estimated on a fully automated DiaSys 200, chemistry analyzer (DiaSys Diagnostics India Pvt Ltd.) using standard commercially available kits while the aliquots for hormones and other required investigations were stored at − 80 °C until the assay. Hormonal assays include (serum insulin levels total T4, total testosterone, 17-OHP, cortisol, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), prolactin, luteinizing hormone (LH), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), prolactin, cortisol) were conducted by chemiluminescent immunosorbent assay on Cobas e411 (Roche Diagnostics). The ultrasonographic evaluation was done by a single investigator by a 3-MHz trans-abdominal transducer (LogiqP6; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) in the follicular phase.

Inflammatory and oxidative stress marker assessment

The levels of serum inflammatory markers (TNF-α, IL6, and IL1-β) were assayed by sandwich ELISA, using commercially available kits, and according to manufacturer protocol (Diaclone, France). Briefly, 96-well plates pre-coated with antibodies against TNF-α (Cat. No. 950.090.096), IL6 (Cat. No. 950.030.096), and IL1-β (Cat. No. 850.006.096) were incubated with patient sera individually for each marker. The sample, control, and standard diluent (zero) were run in duplicates. Next, diluted secondary biotinylated antibodies of TNF-α, IL6, and IL1-β were added to all wells, followed by covering the plate and incubating at room temperature (18–25 °C) for 3 h. The wells were then washed by aspirating the liquid, adding 0.3 ml of wash buffer, and repeating this process twice more. Subsequently, 100 µl of Streptavidin-HRP was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 30 min at room temperature, followed by another wash. Finally, 100 µl of TMB substrate was added, and the plate was incubated in the dark for 10–15 min before adding the stop reagent (100 µl of H₂SO₄) to each well. Absorbance values for each well were read on a spectrophotometer immediately after adding the stop reagent, using 450 nm as the primary wavelength and 620 nm (or between 610 and 650 nm) as the reference wavelength.

The OS among PCOS cases and healthy controls was assessed by measuring the serum TOS using a standardized calorimetric method as previously described [16] and the levels of antioxidant enzymes Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) (Batch no.H4706-1l032 A) and Catalase (Batch no. H0643I032 A) were assayed by ELISA, using commercially available kits and according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd.). Briefly, in a 96-well plate, each well was filled with 100 µl of standard or sample, the plate was sealed and incubated statically at 37 °C for 90 min, followed by two washes. Next, 100 µl of biotin-labeled antibody solution was added, and the plate was sealed and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C, then washed three times with 1-minute immersion each. In the third step, 100 µl of SABC solution was added, and the plate was sealed and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by five washes with 1-minute immersion. Then, 90 µl of TMB substrate solution was added, the plate was sealed, and incubated for 10–20 min at 37 °C with visual control. Finally, 50 µl of stop solution was added, and absorbance was read immediately at 450 nm.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and WNT3 gene expression analysis

Total RNA extraction was performed using GenElute™ Universal Total RNA Purification Kit by Sigma Alrdich (St. Louis, USA) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. cDNA synthesis was performed using the ReadyScript™ cDNA Synthesis Mix by Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, USA) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Expression levels of WNT3 gene were analyzed using gene-specific primers and the SYBR® Green PCR Kit (Qiagen, Germany) and their qPCR reaction mix, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Gene-specific primers were designed to flank exon–exon junctions using the Primer3 tool. We used GAPDH as the normalizing gene for the analysis. The primer sequences used were WNT3: Forward: 5’-CTCGGCGCCTCTTCTAATGG-3’; Revere: 5’- AGCCCAGAGATGTGTACTGC-3’; GAPDH: Forward: 5’-CCATGGGGAAGGTGAAGGTC-3’, Revere: 5’- TGGAATTTGCCATGGGTGGA-3’. All the samples were run in duplicates. A comparative 2−ΔΔCT method using GAPDH as a reference gene was used to analyze the data to quantify WNT3 mRNA level [39].

PCOS sub-phenotypes

The recruited PCOS subjects were subdivided into three groups as; (I) PCOS with hyperandrogenism (HA+ PCOS), (II) PCOS with hyperandrogenism and IR (HAIR+ PCOS), and (III) PCOS without hyperandrogenism and IR (HAIR− PCOS). Hyperandrogenism (HA) was assessed either clinically or biochemically. Clinical HA was defined as the presence of hirsutism, with mFG score > 8, and subjects with serum total testosterone > 0.88ng/ml [40] were considered as biochemically hyperandrogenic. PCOS subjects with HOMA-IR > 2.5 [41] were classified as insulin resistant (IR).

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed in the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (SPSS v26 Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive analyses for clinical, biochemical, hormonal, inflammatory, and OS parameters were performed. Results were expressed as mean ± SD. Student t-test and ANOVA were used to compare continuous variables between the groups. Also, Bivariate Pearson Correlation analysis was used to determine the correlation. Logistic Regression analysis was done using R- software (version 4.4.3). P-values were used to compare the results, P-values < 0.05 were considered as significant.

Results

A total of 270 age-matched subjects were enrolled in this case-control study which included 135 PCOS-affected women and 135 apparently healthy controls.

Comparison between women with PCOS and healthy controls

The evaluation between women with PCOS and controls in terms of clinical, biochemical, hormonal, inflammatory, and OS parameters revealed significant differences as depicted in Table 1. The mean age (years) of PCOS cases and healthy controls was 23.23 ± 4.56 vs. 23.20 ± 4.45 respectively. However, the age of menarche was slightly lower in the PCOS group (12.85 ± 1.20) compared to controls (13.02 ± 0.92) (p < 0.001). Unlike controls, PCOS cases reported fewer menstrual cycles/year (11.92 ± 0.52 vs. 6.79 ± 4.39; p < 0.05) and significantly higher mFG scores (4.33 ± 2.01vs.10.84 ± 3.39; p < 0.05). Mean BMI (25.08 ± 4.18 vs. 22.55 ± 3.18) and waist circumference (cm) (89.73 ± 12.71 vs. 82.11 ± 9.62), the total serum LH (10.06 ± 4.41 vs. 6.99 ± 3.15), testosterone (0.64 ± 0.34 vs. 0.30 ± 0.06), 17(OH) progesterone (1.03 ± 0.82 vs. 0.46 ± 0.35), morning Cortisol (13.28 ± 4.63 vs. 7.32 ± 5.39) levels, fasting glucose (88.8 ± 9.86 vs. 83.85 ± 7.71) and fasting insulin (11.64 ± 5.77 vs. 7.36 ± 2.83) levels in PCOS subjects were substantially elevated (p < 0.00). Additionally, HOMA-IR values were more in cases (2.28 ± 1.44) than in controls (1.25 ± 0.74) and conversely QUICKI values were more in controls (0.37 ± 0.03) than in cases (0.35 ± 0.04) (p < 0.001). Serum total cholesterol (mg/dl) levels (166.45 ± 49.97 vs. 122.07 ± 36.98; p < 0.001), triglycerides (mg/dl) (136.54 ± 68.91 vs. 122.71 ± 44.35; p < 0.050), and HDL (mg/d) (56.43 ± 27.01 vs. 50.79 ± 14.62; p < 0.001) were higher in the PCOS group compared to controls.

We observed significantly elevated levels of the studied inflammatory markers; TNF-α (24.98 ± 3.07 vs. 20.07 ± 6.83), IL6 (4.08 ± 0.90 vs. 3.09 ± 0.90), and IL1-β (12.03 ± 2.81 vs. 8.61 ± 4.02) among women with PCOS than controls (P < 0.001). Bivariate Pearson correlation analysis between anthropometric, biochemical, hormonal, inflammatory and oxidative stress markers among women with PCOS

Bivariate Pearson’s correlation analysis among recruited PCOS subjects revealed a strong positive correlation between BMI with waist circumference (r = 0.61, p < 0.05) and HOMA-IR (r = 0.39, p < 0.05) and a weak positive association with triglyceride (r = 0.26, p < 0.05) and cholesterol (r = 0.11, p < 0.05) respectively. Conversely, BMI was negatively correlated with QUICKI (r=. -0.30, p < 0.05) and IL-6 (r= -0.17, p < 0.05). Additionally, we observed waist circumference had positive association with IL-1β (r = 0.30, p < 0.05), LDL (r = 0.26, p < 0.05) and triglyceride (r = 0.15, p < 0.05) & negative association with HOMA (r= -0.32, p < 0.05), QUICKI (r= -0.22, p < 0.05), HDL (r= -0.21, p < 0.05) and TNFα (r= -0.11, p < 0.05), depicting associations statistically significant. The correlation analysis showed that TNF-α exhibited a strong positive correlation with IL-1β (r = 0.26, P < 0.0001). The TOS presented a strong positive association with HOMA-IR (r = 0.22, p < 0.0001), TNFα (r = 0.22, p < 0.0001), IL1β (r = 0.24), p < 0.0001), LDL (r = 0.25, p < 0.0001) and strong negative association with SOD (r= -0.34, p < 0.0001) a catalase (r= -027, p < 0.0001) and testosterone r= -0.22, p < 0.0001). The correlation analysis also demonstrated that antioxidant enzyme catalase correlated positively with QUICKI (r = 0.34, p < 0.0001) and SOD (r = 0.24, p < 0.0001) and negatively correlated with HOMA-IR (r=-0.43, p < 0.0001) and TOS (r= -0.27, p < 0.0001). The catalase enzyme also showed a substantial negative but not statistically significant correlation with TNF-α (r= -0.155, p > 0.05) and IL-6 (r= -0.170, p > 0.05), as indicated in Fig. 1.

Logistic regression analysis for predicting PCOS cases based on anthropometric, hormonal, inflammatory and OS markers

The logistic regression model (Table 4) summary reveals the relationship between the predictors and the pseudo-label outcome. Notably, BMI (p = 0.02), waist circumference (p = 0.02), serum total testosterone (p = 0.01), IL-6 (p = 0.01), serum HDL (p = 0.02), HOMA_IR (p = 0.02), and QUICKI (p = 0.03), TOS (p = 0.03), SOD (p = 0.01) and catalase (p = 0.02) are significant predictors at the 0.05 level, suggesting they influence the likelihood of the outcome. TNF-α, serum total cholesterol, serum triglyceride and serum LDL show p-values greater than 0.05, indicating no significant association with the pseudo-label in this model. The intercept term is negative (-6.30) but not statistically significant. The model deviance (116.30) suggests a moderate fit, with an AIC of 144.3, which can be compared with other models for model selection. Overall, the significant predictors, BMI, waist circumference, serum total testosterone, IL-6, serum HDL, HOMA_IR, QUICKI, TOS, SOD and catalase may warrant further exploration.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that compared to controls, serum inflammatory markers (TNF-α, IL6 and IL1-β) levels were significantly elevated along with poor antioxidant status among women with PCOS. In the present study the observation of higher BMI and consequently higher pro-inflammatory cytokines among women with PCOS than the control group indicating obesity associated in PCOS is linked to low-grade chronic inflammation which is also reported in existing literature [43, 44]. We also found that OS and sub-inflammation among women with PCOS was independent of WNT3 gene expression.

Hyperandrogenism is a hallmark characteristic of PCOS [45]. Severe metabolic changes including IR, inflammation and OS have been linked to the hyperandrogenic phenotype in earlier published research [46, 47], whereas metabolic problems are less common in those with normal-androgenic PCOS [48]. In our study, the TOS (µmol H2O2/L) levels in C phenotype (HA + PCOM) were reduced to that of the other phenotype groups which is in consistence with the study reported that hyperandrogenic PCOS phenotypes have elevated MDA, TOS and OSI levels [15, 49, 50]. According to these findings, PCOS patients presented comparatively decreased antioxidant enzyme activities contributing to their elevated oxidative stress. Our investigation demonstrated that the mean catalase and SOD levels across PCOS phenotypes A, B, C, and D were progressively increasing. All four phenotypes presented statistically significant intra-phenotypic differences indicating that lower serum antioxidant activity of SOD is by the additive effect of hyperandrogenism, oligo-anovulation and PCOM. Also, this decrease in SOD and catalase levels in this study may be connected to increase utilization of both antioxidant enzymes to scavenge produced ROS brought about by both hyperglycemia and excess free fatty acids. In PCOS, hyperandrogenism may result from impaired intrinsic steroidogenesis or elevated LH levels brought on by abnormal hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis regulation, which is also influenced by insulin in ovarian theca cells [51]. Accordingly, our study reported that serum LH and 17(OH) progesterone levels were elevated in hyperandrogenic PCOS. Adrenal hyperandrogenism has also been linked to increased peripheral cortisol metabolism [52]. Our study also revealed similar results as HA PCOS and HAIR PCOS groups presented increased serum cortisol levels. In comparison to PCOS without HAIR, our study revealed elevated levels of fasting serum insulin in PCOS HA and PCOS HAIR groups. Bergh et al., reported that insulin controls the production of androgens in cultured human theca cells, which function as substrate for the synthesis of E2 in granulosa cells [53]. Our study also reported statistically significant higher values of QUICKI in HA PCOS than HAIR and without HAIR PCOS.

The HA and advanced glycation end products (AGEs) in women with PCOS induce oxidative stress and inflammation [54]. A published study reported that overexpression of TNF-α, a proinflammatory cytokine, led to the development of IR in PCOS [55] and hyperandrogenism by lowering the tyrosine kinase activity of the Insulin Receptor gene (INSR) [56, 57], our study reported similar results with statistically significant elevated levels of TNF-α in PCOS HAIR group when compared with PCOS without HAIR. Rey-Rolden et al., could not find any significant correlation between IL-6 levels and hyperandrogenism among PCOS participants [58], in contradiction, our study revealed that the serum levels of IL-6 in HAIR- subjects were significantly lower than in HAIR+ and HA+ PCOS groups. Proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β has several functions crucial for ovulation, fertilization and implantation among reproductive-age women in PCOS implicating its role in the pathophysiology of PCOS [59]. Accordingly, our study reported that HA+ & HAIR+ had equally high levels of IL-1β, with the exception of the HAIR-PCOS group, which showed comparatively lower levels. This conclusion recommends that the presence or absence of HA and IR or both HAIR may influence the expression of IL-1β, a pivotal pro-inflammatory cytokine implicated in reproductive processes and metabolic dysregulation.

OS has been linked to the development of hyperinsulinemia, hyperandrogenism, PCOM and oligo anovulation in PCOS as well as the promotion of inflammation and IR [60, 61]. On comparing OS between the IR and HA PCOS groups, the TOS level was higher in the HAIR+ PCOS group; HAIR- had higher serum levels of SOD which was found in earlier published reports [62]. A higher risk of diabetes has been linked to hereditary catalase deficiencies [63]. In the study by Volkan Turan et al., IRPCOS subjects had lower levels of the antioxidant enzymes SOD and CAT compared to non-IRPCOS patients [64]. Accordingly, our study reported that in comparison with HAIR- & HA+ PCOS groups, the HAIR+ PCOS group had reduced serum catalase levels. Our research indicates that factors such as hyperinsulinemia, hyperandrogenism, dyslipidemia and IR actively contribute to the decline in antioxidant levels seen in the HAIR+ PCOS group with rising oxidative stress. Consequently, the decreased SOD and catalase activity indicates that these PCOS-affected subjects have increased cellular damage. Furthermore, our findings also align with recent studies highlighting the role of oxidative stress in PCOS. For instance, Ünlü et al. (2023) evaluated serum telomerase activity in normal-weight young girls with PCOS and reported significant correlations with metabolic parameters, suggesting that oxidative stress and related metabolic disruptions might play crucial roles in PCOS pathophysiology and could contribute to cellular aging processes. This insight supports our observation of elevated oxidative stress markers and further underscores the necessity of understanding OS-related biomarkers in the PCOS inflammatory profile [65].

The regulation of ovarian steroid synthesis, follicular maturation, and the processes of ovulation, fertilization, and implantation parameters all compromised in women with PCOS—are just a few of the key roles that TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β play in reproductive physiology [66, 67]. Our study reported a positive association between TNF-α and IL-1β in PCOS participants indicating TNF-alpha and IL-1β can act synergistically, meaning that the presence of one cytokine can enhance the effects of the other [68]. They may amplify the inflammatory response by promoting the production of other inflammatory mediators and activating immune cells [69]. In addition, there was a significant inverse relationship between HOMA-IR and QUICKI as found in a published study [70]. Strong positive associations were identified between TOS and HOMA-IR, TNFα, IL-1β, and LDL, indicating that increased OS may coincide with higher IR and inflammation levels. Conversely, SOD exhibited negative association with TOS suggesting its potential role in mitigating OS and inflammatory responses among the studied subjects. Additionally, our study highlighted catalase exhibited positive correlations with QUICKI, indicating enhanced insulin sensitivity, and negative correlations with HOMA-IR, suggesting that higher catalase levels may be linked to lower IR. OS disrupts insulin signaling via activation of kinases like JNK and IKK, phosphorylating IRS proteins and hindering insulin signaling [71]. Catalase’s capacity to mitigate oxidative stress preserves insulin signaling pathway integrity, promoting overall insulin sensitivity. This suggests a protective role of catalase against oxidative stress-induced IR. Proinflammatory cytokine, TNF-α induces OS directly by promoting the generation of ROS through activation of NADPH oxidase and other ROS-generating enzymes, leading to oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA [72]. TNF-α increases the lipid peroxidation of mitochondrial membranes [71] IL-6 promotes IR therefore exacerbating and further inducing oxidative stress [73–75]. Additionally, there was a strong negative association between the catalase enzyme and IL-6 and TNF-α, which suggests that catalase, with its antioxidant properties, may play a role in suppressing inflammation by reducing the production of IL-6 and TNF-α, via MAPK (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase) and JAK-STAT (Janus Kinase-Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription) pathways [76]. By dampening these signaling cascades, catalase can attenuate the production of IL-6 and TNF-α. When considered collectively and in the perspective of other published works [77, 78], these findings indicate that the interaction between IR and hyperandrogenism promotes inflammation, which in turn causes OS by lowering antioxidant defenses in PCOS-affected women.

In this study, WNT3 gene expression in peripheral blood was undetectable, contrasting with findings by Buil et al., who reported endogenous WNT3 expression in the human endometrium, with levels independent of hormonal fluctuations [79]. Similarly, Tulac et al. observed a significant up-regulation of WNT3 (4.7-fold, P < 0.05) in proliferative endometrium [80], suggesting that WNT3 expression may be highly tissue-specific, potentially limited to endometrial or other reproductive tissues, and not consistently expressed in peripheral blood. This discrepancy underscores the role of WNT3 in tissue-specific physiological processes mainly in PCOS that may not extend to peripheral blood circulation.

Our findings revealed that women with HAIR+ PCOS sub-phenotype had elevated levels of insulin, HOMA-IR, TNF-α, IL-1β, TOS and a reduction in QUICKI, antioxidant enzymes catalase and SOD. Together, these results strengthen the idea that PCOS is a chronic low-grade inflammatory and pro-oxidant condition and that inflammation and OS stress mediators play a role in causing and exacerbating hyperandrogenic, IR and dyslipidemia tendencies.

In conclusion, our study underscores the key roles of inflammation and OS in PCOS pathophysiology. Women with PCOS exhibited elevated serum pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) and diminished antioxidant defences, including lower catalase and SOD activity. WNT3 gene expression in peripheral blood was not implicated in the observed OS or inflammation, suggesting a tissue-specific expression pattern. Hyperandrogenism appears to amplify OS and inflammatory pathways, exacerbating IR and dyslipidaemia. Elevated OS markers like TOS and inverse correlations between antioxidant enzyme activity and inflammatory cytokines highlight the interplay driving disease progression. WNT3’s regulatory roles in reproductive tissues, such as the endometrium, further emphasize the need for tissue-specific investigations into its function in PCOS. Therapeutic strategies targeting OS and inflammation, through antioxidants, anti-inflammatory therapies, or lifestyle modifications, could mitigate metabolic and reproductive complications, particularly in hyperandrogenic sub-phenotypes.

Future research directions include WNT3 expression in ovarian, adipose, and endometrial tissues using advanced techniques like RNA sequencing and single-cell transcriptomics. Longitudinal studies are needed to track OS and inflammation across PCOS phenotypes, while biomarker discovery may enable early diagnosis and personalized treatment strategies to improve outcomes and quality of life in PCOS.

Limitations of the study

The limitations of this study include the limited sample size of the HAIR-PCOS subgroup and the recruitment of just one patient with IR PCOS but no hyperandrogenism. Therefore, a considerable sample size in IR but no hyperandrogenism PCOS group would be required to precisely assess their characteristics.
