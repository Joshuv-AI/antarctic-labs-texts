NeuroRecovery Clinics: A Survey to Understand the Current Landscape and Opinions of Post-NeuroICU Clinics
Authors: Matthew N. Jaffa, Julia M. Carlson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:Survival following severe acute neurologic injury (SANI) is increasing. The complexities of caring for these patients are vast and gaps have been highlighted in post-acute care follow-up. While the development of post-ICU follow-up clinics have been increasing in popularity there is limited literature describing the landscape of post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics. We sought to describe the current landscape and identify benefits and barriers to clinic development in the United States.DESIGN:We developed a 19-question cross-sectional survey study.SETTING AND SUBJECTS:The survey was disseminated to clinicians working in neurocritical care units throughout the United States and open for completion from August 2023 to December 2023. Responses were characterized by descriptive statistics.INTERVENTIONS:None.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:Two hundred eighteen unique individuals responded to our survey. Post-neuroICU and/or NeuroRecovery clinics were uncommon and operational at only 69 of 215 respondents’ institutions (32.1%). Forty-two percent reported an interest in engaging with a post-neuroICU clinic and an additional 39% showed interest but had identified other obligations preventing participation. Among the identified potential benefits of a clinic for survivors of SANI mitigating gaps in care, identifying differences between predicted and actual outcome, and reassessment of communication/prognosis ranked highest.CONCLUSIONS:Few post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics exist in the United States but interest in participating in this aspect of care is common within the neurocritical care community. The identification of gaps in care, obstacles to continued recovery, and potential to adjudicate differences between actual and predicted outcomes ranked among the most important potential benefits for extending the current neurocritical care paradigm to the clinic setting.

KEY POINTS

Question: As post-ICU clinics grow among medical specialties what does the current landscape of neurocritical care clinicians practicing in the full spectrum of their patients’ care look like?

Findings: This national survey study of neurocritical care clinicians identified only a limited number of active post-neuroICU clinics. There is strong interested in participating in the post-ICU/NeuroRecovery clinic among the neurocritical care community to follow-up patient outcomes and identify gaps in care.

Meaning: Neurointensivists are interested in participating in the longitudinal care of their patients but greater understanding of the barriers to active engagement in this care paradigm is needed.

Post-intensive care clinics have gained significant traction both in the United States and internationally in recent years (1). This rise reflects a growing awareness of the long-term sequelae associated with ICU survivorship. These health impacts, collectively termed post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) and PICS-family, encompass a range of cognitive impairments, psychiatric symptoms, and physical disabilities (2). Post-ICU clinics aim to address these complex challenges by improving care coordination within an ever more fragmented healthcare system (3).

Patients discharged from ICUs frequently experience diverse recovery trajectories and complex healthcare needs that are not easily addressed by the current primary care paradigm (4). Many of these complexities are intricately linked to the ICU experience itself, rather than the primary illness that necessitated admission. Evidence from the medical ICU population highlights significant gaps in post-discharge care (5). For example, one retrospective study revealed that only 11% of sepsis survivors received all four recommended elements of post-sepsis care—medication optimization, impairment screening, monitoring for deterioration, and treatment alignment—within 90 days of discharge (6). Notably, addressing at least two of these elements is associated with reduced readmission rates and mortality, underscoring the critical role of specialized ICU follow-up clinics (5, 6).

Despite the potential benefits for post-ICU clinics they have not targeted the specific needs of survivors of severe acute neurologic injury (SANI) and post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics remain underrepresented. Only four such clinics are publicized, with an additional six known through professional networks. These post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics are generally staffed by clinicians that practice principally in the neurocritical care unit and are familiar with both brain injury and critical care enabling them to bridge a specific gap not well filled by other neurologic subspecialities or general post-ICU clinics. While survivors of SANI may experience similar physical, cognitive, and psychologic challenges to those of critical illness identification and assessment is complicated by the underlying SANI itself (7).

There is increasing awareness and interest in these specific clinics, with several recent descriptions of different models (8–10). The scarcity of these clinics raises important questions about their prevalence and the barriers to their establishment and continued development. To address this gap, we designed a survey study to explore the current landscape of post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics across the United States. This study aims to identify existing clinics, understand the motivations behind their development and sustainability, and examine why some neurocritical care professionals have not adopted ICU-specific follow-up models. We also seek to assess the neurocritical care community’s interest in expanding this type of post-acute care. The findings of this survey will enhance future research to examine the effect of these clinics on neurocritical care survivor outcomes.

METHODS

Study Design

This study was designed as a cross-sectional survey targeting clinicians who manage patients with SANI in the neurocritical care setting.

Instrument

We developed a 19-question survey delivered via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) application hosted at Hartford Healthcare (Supplemental Appendix 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B573) (11, 12). The survey instrument was created using an iterative development design (13). An initial creation and review of the question set was developed by the authors (M.N.J., J.M.C.) to capture the demographics of the participants (including healthcare profession, years of practice, type of practice, and whether or not there was access to a post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinic where they practice) as well as their individual opinions regarding purpose and benefits or barriers to clinic development utilizing both Likert scale and rank order questions. Subsequent iterations of the survey culled questions based on completion and review by four experts in post-neuroICU recovery. For the purposes of the study, we defined a post-neuroICU clinic as a follow-up clinic that evaluates and manages patients treated for stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, traumatic brain injury, seizure follow-up, rehabilitation, or cardiac arrest, etc where the recruitment and/or referral is generated for patients who were cared for in a neuroICU setting and/or provided by neurocritical care staff members.

Dissemination of Survey

The survey was initially distributed via quick response (QR) code at the Neurocritical Care Society annual meeting in August 2023. Subsequently, an email invitation for survey distribution and completion was sent to every identified neurocritical care unit and medical director in the United States utilizing a web search by state. The survey remained open for completion from August 2023 to December 2023.

Statistical Analysis

This was a cross-sectional study design, with results reported as percentages. Participants had the opportunity to skip questions, which resulted in variability in the total number of answers per question. The denominator varied according to each question. Missing data were excluded when calculating the percentage. Descriptive statistics of the data were analyzed utilizing REDCap native statistical analysis (11, 12).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

To ensure proper reporting of results from an electronic survey, guidelines set by the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) were followed (14). The survey study entitled “NeuroRecovery: Understanding the Landscape of Post-NeuroICU Clinics” was approved by the Hartford Hospital Institutional Review Board (No. HHC-2023-0148) on August 3, 2023, and electronic consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures of the study adhered to the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

RESULTS

Demographics

We identified 149 neurocritical care programs in the continental United States and were able to find contact information for 121. From these initial inquiries, a total of 244 respondents started the survey and 218 unique individuals completed the survey. Multiple respondents completing the survey from an individual program was encouraged. Table 1 presents the demographics of respondents and make-up of their healthcare institutions. The majority of participants 132 of 218 (60.6%) described themselves as attending physicians, 15 (6.9%) as neurocritical care fellows, and 47 (21.6%) as advanced practice providers. The overwhelming majority of respondents practice in an academic or tertiary care hospital (93.1%). Nearly one-third of participants (32.1%) noted the existence of an active post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinic operating at their institution, representing ten unique sites.

Opinions From Respondents Without Post-NeuroICU Clinics

A large majority of respondents do not have access to an active post-neuroICU clinic 146 (67.9%). However, among this cohort of respondents there was strong interest in clinic participation with 42% stating “yes, I would love to…” participate and an additional 39% acknowledge they might be interested in participating but have other ongoing responsibilities (Fig. 1A). Nearly two-thirds of participants (25/38) with a desire to participate in clinic report a lack of resources (i.e., financial, personnel, space, time, etc) as barriers to opening a clinic.

Opinions From Respondents With Post-NeuroICU Clinics

Even among respondents who identified practicing within an institution that had an operational post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinic just more than one-third participated in the clinic themselves (n = 24). Twenty of these respondents (47.6%) reported that while they were interested in evaluating patients in the clinic at their institution they had other obligations that prevented them from doing so (Fig. 1B).

Table 2 displays the characteristics of clinics as described by those with active clinics. Frequency of clinician involvement in clinic ranges from once per month (30% of respondents) to once per week (29.2% of respondents). The overwhelming majority of clinics at institutions currently considered operational are staffed by “all” or “some” of the clinicians in each neurocritical care group (83.4%). Two-thirds of clinics are reported to be multidisciplinary in structure (66.7%) with the most common collaborators being identified as therapy services (physical, occupational, speech), pharmacy, psychology, and social work (Table 2). Patients are identified for recruitment to the clinic by the clinician or ICU team members for 20 of 24 respondents as well as through referral from other service lines for 12 of 24 respondents. Multiple barriers to patient follow-up were identified; the most common being a lack of awareness of the clinic’s goals or purpose in the context of the patient’s ongoing care (29%), persistent disability (21%), insurance coverage (12.5%), and too many appointments with physicians (12.5%).

The primary purpose of the clinic was identified as an opportunity to reflect on critical illness and ask questions about the acute hospitalization (37.5%), reengage clinical, rehabilitation, or social services (33.3%), and reassess communication of prognosis (20.8%) with no respondents identifying research outcomes or medication reconciliation as the primary goal of their clinic (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

Through this nationwide survey of the neurocritical care community, we have mapped the current landscape of post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics in the United States. While few in number, 67% of respondents did not have an active clinic at their institution, there is a healthy interest in engaging with a post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinic expressed by close to half of respondents. Post-neuroICU clinic as a means to identify challenges during acute critical illness and ongoing barriers to recovery were reported by all as the greatest potential benefits for both medical teams and survivors.

As anticipated based on our a priori search, post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics are not common in the United States. However, contrary to the traditional belief that most clinicians entering the field of neurocritical care do not have a desire to practice in an outpatient environment, we found a significant proportion of those surveyed expressing interest in participating in these clinics (15). In fact, less than 20% of respondents to our study reported that caring for patients in the post-ICU setting would not be considered a component of their practice. It is unclear if this unanticipated interest in post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics represents a previously unrecognized desire by neurocritical care clinicians to extend our current care model, a recognition of the benefits to both survivors and the medical team met by continuing the therapeutic relationship beyond the doors of the ICU, or a reflection of the growing literature promoting and supporting this care paradigm (1, 3, 8–10, 15–20).

Chief among identified barriers to participation in clinic among neurocritical clinicians were financial, personnel, clinic space, and time resources. This has been recognized in other surveys of clinicians from multispecialty critical care groups for post-ICU care (16). The Critical and Acute Illness Recovery Organization (CAIRO) post-ICU clinic collaborative found that most clinics rely on discretionary support from both clinicians and institutions operating post-ICU recovery clinics (1, 17). Financial and human support for post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics has also been highly reliant on volunteerism and the recognition by institutions that this is “the right care” for our patients as we advocate to start and build individual programs. While, anecdotally, it is believed by those that practice in this space that hospital readmissions, excess testing, discontinuation of unneeded mediations, etc, are reduced, leading to fewer healthcare dollars spent, these benefits have yet to be adjudicated in the literature (21). The complexity and heterogeneity of this patient populations’ recovery trajectory will require further deliberation in order to identify the most useful outcomes for monitoring.

While the overarching focus for developing post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics has identified bidirectional benefits for survivors and the clinical critical care team the primary purpose of individual clinics varies widely (8). Clinics have been advertised as traumatic brain injury clinics, post-acute symptomatic seizure clinics (22), transitional care clinics, among other disease/treatment-specific cohorts (e.g., post-cardiac arrest, intracerebral hemorrhage, spinal cord injury). However, among clinicians following survivors of SANI in our survey the primary focus of clinics is varied and heterogeneous with some focusing on one particular disease population, research/outcomes, time-limited trials of restorative care for survivors with uncertain prognosis, among several others. Approximately one-third of respondents in this study identified the primary objective of their clinic as an opportunity to reflect on critical illness and ask questions about the acute hospitalization or to reengage clinical, rehabilitation, or social services, while one-fifth use clinic to reassess the communication of prognosis.

Appropriate patient selection for clinic is crucial. As most post-neuroICU clinics are only able to accommodate a small number of follow ups among the cohort of patients that are cared for in the neurocritical care unit each month determining criteria for clinic referral and a means to direct referrals is paramount (9). We, the authors, have developed basic criteria by which survivors of SANI are evaluated to determine suitability for clinic follow-up that has evolved over time and as our clinics have developed their own identities. Others have begun to define baseline survivor’s characteristics that support appropriateness for evaluation in the post-neuroICU clinic (20, 23). Despite best efforts clinic “no-show” rates have been documented as falling between 11% and 15% (9, 10). Similar to other reports in the literature, we found that the greatest barriers to active clinic participation by survivors were related to persistent or worsening disability, insurance, and transportation (9, 10, 23). We also found that an additional barrier to survivor participation in clinic was related to a lack of understanding the goals of a post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinic and the abundance of clinic visits patients were already attending. This suggests a need for significant education on the opportunities and potential benefits that these clinics offer even prior to survivors being discharged from the hospital. Future efforts to further clarify the needs of survivors of SANI and their surrogates at different time points following injury are necessary to balance potential benefits against the burden accumulated with additional visits and duplicated care.

Several pertinent limitations merit discussion. Principally, our survey was distributed to points of contact at each institution with a neurocritical care unit identified through internet search precluding direct notification to all those practicing in the neurocritical care environment as well as the distributing of unique links to prevent multiple responses. In addition, while the survey was open to all who work in neurocritical care, we received a limited number of completed surveys from nurses, therapy services, and pharmacists, which may have biased the priorities and potential benefits identified as important to the clinic. Importantly, the voice of the survivors of SANI were not included in the results of this survey but would serve as a marker against which to assess the potential benefits of such a care paradigm. Since most respondents were affiliated with academic hospitals and not stratified by geographic region, the generalizability of our findings among the broader community may be limited. Our study did not specifically ask respondents if another generalized or specialty focused post-ICU clinic was also operating at their institution preventing a deeper understanding of factors that might optimize post-neuroICU clinic development such as resource and experience sharing. Finally, some bias may be introduced by multiple respondents originating for the same hospital system and those who responded to the survey may have been more predisposed to be supportive of this type of care model.

Through this survey the NeuroRecovery Clinic Consortium was formed with the explicit mission to improve post-neurocritical care recovery through innovative treatment, research, education, and collaboration. The consortium meets regularly to provide support and guidance for developing programs, identify opportunities to harmonize data being collected at individual institutions, discuss areas for research collaboration, and additional means by which to optimize care for survivors of SANI.

CONCLUSIONS

Here, we define the current landscape of post-neuroICU/NeuroRecovery clinics in the United States. While limited in overall numbers, there is a healthy appetite among the neurocritical care community to participate in the post-acute care of survivors of SANI. Future efforts to define the role and benefits of neurocritical care clinicians in this care paradigm are necessary and will be accomplished through the collaborative work of a newly developed NeuroRecovery Clinic Consortium.
