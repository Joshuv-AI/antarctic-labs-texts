The efficacy of scalp cooling in patients receiving chemotherapy for primary gynecologic cancers: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Shuk Tak Kwok, Lesley Suk Kwan Lau, Tat On Chan, Mandy Man Yee Chu, Siew Fei Ngu, Karen Kar Loen Chan, Hextan Yuen Sheung Ngan, Polly Pui Yee Ho, Lina Wu, Damaris Suk Mei Hung, Heidi Ka Ying Lo, Christina Sze Man Wong, Ka Yu Tse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScalp cooling can prevent chemotherapy-induced alopecia in breast cancer patients. But data in gynecologic patients were scarce. The CHARM study was a single-center randomized study. Women planning for 3-weekly carboplatin and paclitaxel were randomized to either scalp cooling or usual clinical practice in 1:1 ratio. The primary endpoint was the efficacy in hair preservation after the completion of chemotherapy as assessed by the clinicians. Secondary endpoints included quality of life, the incidence of psychological distress, and safety. 142 women were invited to join the study but 56 refused. Finally, 44 women were randomized to the intervention group and 42 to the control group. The successful rate in preserving hair, defined as Dean score ≤ 2, upon completion of chemotherapy was significantly higher in the intervention group (29.6%, 95% confidence interval (CI) 12.4–46.9%) than the control group (0%, 95% CI 0–0%) (p = 0.002). Compared to the control group, the intervention group had significantly better global health status, physical functioning, and role functioning during the chemotherapy, lower incidence of psychological distress after cycle 2 of chemotherapy (27.8% vs. 62.2%, p = 0.003), and lower median PHQ-ADS scores after cycle 2 (5.5 vs. 11.0, p = 0.045) and 2–3 weeks post-chemotherapy (5.0 vs. 9.0, p = 0.033). However, the intervention group had a 30% drop-out rate despite no major adverse events. Scalp cooling could reduce the incidence of CIA and potentially alleviate psychological distress and improve the QOL in gynecologic cancer patients. Further effort is needed to improve the acceptance and compliance rates in Asian population. Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced alopecia (CIA) is a distressing adverse event (AE) in women receiving chemotherapy because it directly affects their self-image1. About 17% of women with gynecologic cancers regarded alopecia as the most traumatic AE, and up to 14% might refuse curative therapies that are associated with alopecia2. CIA typically starts within 2–4 weeks after the initiation of chemotherapy, and it will advance to complete alopecia in 2–3 months3. In gynecologic cancers, several chemotherapy drugs can cause CIA. For instance, CIA occurred in 80–100% of cases with paclitaxel or docetaxel, 60–100% with topotecan or etoposide, and over 60% with cisplatin, carboplatin, or ifosfamide2,4. Using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), our previous study showed that about 25% of patients receiving chemotherapy had poor self-image and depression due to CIA5. Therefore, the physical, psychological, and social impact of CIA cannot be underestimated.

Scalp compression, topical calcitriol, topical minoxidil, alpha-tocopherol and herbal remedies have been studied for CIA prevention4. However, these approaches have shown inconsistent efficacy and have not been widely adopted in clinical practice. Other agents like topical vasoconstrictors, cyclin-dependent kinase 2, epidermal growth factor, fibroblast growth factors, photobiomodulation therapy, and platelet-rich plasma injection, are still under evaluation6. Scalp cooling is the most well-established method. It impedes the onset of alopecia by lowering the scalp temperature, inducing vasoconstriction, and reducing the blood flow rate by 20–40% at the scalp. This reduces the delivery of cytotoxic drugs to the hair follicles and lowers the basal metabolic activities of the keratinocytes, thereby rendering the follicles less vulnerable to chemotherapy-induced damage2. A recent study utilizing Folliscope 5.0 (Lead M, Seoul, Korea) demonstrated that scalp cooling improved the quality of regrowth hair by increasing hair thickness and density7. Scalp cooling systems were approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration to prevent CIA in solid tumors in 2017/188. The overall efficacy was 40–60%9,10. It was cost-effective as the purchase of wigs could be postponed11. One study showed that 56.3% of women without scalp cooling felt less attractive after the diagnosis or treatment of breast cancer, while only 27.3% with scalp cooling experienced similar feelings (p = 0.02)12. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network recommends using scalp cooling devices to mitigate CIA in ovarian cancer patients13. However, most scalp cooling studies focused on breast cancer, with limited data for Asian populations and gynecologic cancer patients who often receive carboplatin and paclitaxel. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy on hair preservation, quality of life (QOL), safety and acceptability of scalp cooling in primary gynecologic cancer women receiving platinum-based chemotherapy.

Methods

Study design

The CHARM study was a single-center, randomized controlled study conducted in a university-affiliated gynecologic oncology unit. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (UWB 19-514). The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki, the Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation, and the local regulatory requirements. Written informed consent was obtained from all women enrolled in this study. This study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04168242 (19/11/2019).

Patients

Women aged ≥ 18 years with newly diagnosed gynecologic cancers planning to receive at least four cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy were eligible. The target chemotherapy was carboplatin (AUC 5-6) and paclitaxel (175 mg/m2). Patients using neoadjuvant chemotherapy were also included for analysis and they received adjuvant chemotherapy within 3 weeks after interval cytoreductive surgery. Dose modification and treatment delay were allowed if there was a clinical indication. Women whose Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance score was ≥ 2, those who had pre-existing alopecia of Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 grade ≥ 1, who had received brain irradiation, and pregnant women, were excluded. Other exclusion criteria included those with active unresolved thyroid disorders, uncontrolled diabetes, liver or renal derangement, severe untreated anemia, migraine, scalp or brain metastasis, cold sensitivity, cold agglutinin disease, cryoglobulinemia, cryofibrinogenemia, or psychiatric disorders.

Randomization and scalp cooling procedure

Eligible women subjects were randomly assigned to either the scalp cooling (intervention) group or the usual clinical practice (control) group in a 1:1 ratio. Randomization was performed prior to any procedures, and permuted block design was used.

All women in the intervention group had scalp cooling using the Paxman® Orbis II system (Paxman Coolers Limited, Huddersfield, United Kingdom). The device was approved to be used in this study by the Medical Device Control Office, Department of Health, Hong Kong SAR Government (listing number: 180330). Women had to moisten their hair before applying the size-specific cool caps. Cooling began 30 min before chemotherapy, continued during infusion, and extended 90 min after treatment. The cap temperature was maintained at around 18–20 °C as per manufacturer recommendation. Women were monitored regularly by the nurses and/or doctors during and after cooling. The control group received chemotherapy without scalp cooling as usual.

All participants were followed up two weeks after each course of chemotherapy, and then 3 and 6 months after chemotherapy. At each visit, clinicians trained in using the Dean score assessed the patients’ alopecia at the beginning the consultation, without reviewing their group assignment. All AEs were recorded in the medical documents and case report forms.

Study endpoints and assessment

The primary endpoint was the efficacy in hair preservation 2–3 weeks after the completion of chemotherapy, where successful hair preservation was defined as having score ≤ 2 as assessed by the investigators using the Dean’s Alopecia Scale (scores 0–4 represent hair loss of 0, < 25%, 25–50%, 50–75%, and > 75%, respectively)14.

The secondary endpoints included (i) the quality of life (QOL) assessed by the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire–Core 30 (EORTC-QLQ-C30)15, QLQ-OV28 for ovarian cancer patients, QLQ-CX24 for cervical cancer patients, and QLQ-EN24 for endometrial cancer patients; (ii) the incidence of psychological distress as assessed by the Patient Health Questionnaire Anxiety and Depression Scale (PHQ-ADS)16; (iii) incidence of treatment-related AEs graded by the CTCAE version 5.0. The PHQ-ADS is a 16-item composite measure of depression and anxiety based on the Patient Health Questionnaire 9-item depression scale (PHQ-9)17 and 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7)18. Scores 0–9, 10–19, 20–29, and 30–48, indicate minimal, mild, moderate, and severe levels of psychological distress, respectively. PHQ-ADS, using either the original 16-item scale or the modified 15-time scale, has been used to evaluate psychological distress in patients with breast cancer19 and other conditions like chronic musculoskeletal pain, dialysis, human immunodeficiency virus infection, dizziness and arthritis20. All self-administered questionnaires were conducted before all procedures at the first cycle of chemotherapy, 2–3 weeks after the 2nd, 4th, and 6th or the last cycles, and 3 months and 6 months after the completion of chemotherapy. The completeness of the questions was checked by the research assistants. As scalp cooling was not commonly performed in our locality and scalp coldness might induce physical illness according to traditional Chinese culture, the enrollment rate of the current study and the compliance rate to scalp cooling were the exploratory endpoints.

Women showing signs of depression during the study period, and those having persistently high depressive symptom scores after chemotherapy, would be referred to the Department of Clinical Psychology and/or Department of Psychiatry for further management.

Statistical analysis

At the time of the design of this study, there was no relevant data on scalp cooling in gynecologic cancer patients using 3-weekly carboplatin and paclitaxel. Therefore, assuming the successful rates of reducing CIA in the intervention group was 25% and in the control group was 0% after four cycles of carboplatin and paclitaxel, it was estimated that a total of 64 subjects (32 per group) could achieve 90% power to detect a difference of 25% between the two groups, based on the one-sided Z-test with pooled variance and the significance level set at 0.05. 20 more patients were recruited for the potential 30% drop-out rate from either chemotherapy or the cool cap system, and the final total sample size was 84.

The analysis of this study was performed in the intention-to-treat population comprising women who had received scalp cooling for at least one cycle of chemotherapy. The efficacy of hair preservation would also be reported in the per-protocol population, i.e., those who complied with all study procedures and received scalp cooling in every cycle of chemotherapy. Categorical variables like the incidence of psychological distress, CIA, and other AEs were tabulated in frequency and percentage. These were compared by Chi-square test or Fisher exact test where appropriate. Continuous variables like PHQ-ADS, PHQ-9, GAD-7, QOL scores were expressed by descriptive methods, such as median (interquartile range, IQR) or mean (standard deviation, SD). These were compared by t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Mann-Whitney U test and Friedman’s test were used to compare continuous variables across different groups and within the same group, respectively. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 28 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Il, USA).

Results

Patients

142 women met the inclusion criteria and were invited to join the study between November 2019 and November 2022 (Fig. 1). 56 women (39.4%) declined to participate, and the most common reason was worries about coldness (S_Table 1). 44 were randomized to the intervention group and 42 women to the control group. Subsequently, 40 women in the intervention group and 39 in the control group received at least one cycle of the assigned treatment, and their data were included in the intention-to-treat analysis. Hair preservation

One patient refused pre-cooling during cycle 1 of chemotherapy due to feeling cold despite supportive measures like blankets and hot water, and she was excluded from the ITT group starting from cycle 1. In the intervention group, two patients shaved their heads after the first cycle of chemotherapy, and two more did so after the second cycle as a result of alopecia. 11 patients in the control arm shaved their hair, 10 after cycle 2 and 1 after cycle 5. All patients who shaved their hair regardless of their group assignment were categorised as failure of hair preservation12. After cycle 2 of chemotherapy, higher proportion of the intervention group attained hair preservation than the control group (Table 2). This effect lasted till the completion of chemotherapy, where significantly more women in the intervention group (29.6%, 95% confidence interval (CI) 12.4–46.9%) had preserved hair successfully compared to none in the control group (0%, 95% CI 0–0%) (p = 0.002).

Quality of life

The EQ-5D index is based on five domains, i.e., mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. A 5-digit string generated is converted to an index with reference to a value set specific to Hong Kong, where 1 refers to full health and 0 refers to a state as bad as being dead21. The EQ-5D index of the intervention group was higher than the control group throughout the treatment period, especially at cycle 2, 2–3 weeks and 3 months after chemotherapy (Table 3).

In general, the intervention group had a better global health status than the control group, though the difference was only significant after cycle 4 (Table 3). Psychological distress

22 women (55.0%) in the intervention group and 23 (59.0%) in the control group had mild to severe psychological distress, i.e., PHQ-ADS score ≥ 10, before the start of chemotherapy (p = 0.721) (Table 4). The incidence of psychological distress during chemotherapy was around 30% in the intervention group, in comparison to 40–60% in the control group, and the difference was significant after cycle 2 (27.8% vs. 62.2%, p = 0.003). Significant differences in the median PHQ-ADS scores between the intervention and control groups were also observed after the 2nd cycle of chemotherapy (5.5 vs. 11.0, p = 0.045) and 2–3 weeks after completion of chemotherapy (5.0 vs. 9.0, p = 0.033) (Fig. 3). Compared to the baseline, Friedman’s test showed that the PHQ-ADS scores deteriorated in both groups. However, the overall trend was only significant in the control group (Z score 12.841, p = 0.025) but not the intervention group (Z score 7.885, p = 0.163) (S_Table 3). The detailed changes of the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores at different time points from baseline were illustrated in S_Table 4. Significantly fewer women in the intervention group had suicidal ideations than the control group at 3 months after completion of chemotherapy (8.0 vs. 20.0%, p = 0.02). The proportions of women required for referral to psychologists and/or psychiatrists were similar in the two groups during the entire study period.

Adverse events

All patients were included in the evaluation of AEs. More women in the intervention group had AEs than the control but the difference was not significant (80.0% vs. 64.1%, p = 0.115), and most were limited to grades 1–2 that were resolved with conservative management (S_Table 5). In particular, significantly more women in the intervention group experienced coldness than the control group (20.0% vs. 0%, p = 0.005). No thermal injury, burns, or device incident was reported in this study. One 55-year-old woman developed a rare hair tangling condition after scalp cooling at cycle 2 of chemotherapy despite adopting the recommended haircare (S_Figure 1). Her hair clumped together, not being resolved by combing or oil application, ultimately requiring a haircut. Her hair returned to normal about 10 months after completion of the chemotherapy.

Discussion

This study showed that scalp cooling reduced CIA in up to 30% women after chemotherapy, and this protective effect was seen as early as after the 2nd cycle of chemotherapy. The efficacy in our cohort and few others22,23 was lower than others reported in the literature7,9,24. Such differences could be due to multiple factors. First, the types of malignancies and the chemotherapy used were different. Most studies were conducted on breast cancer7,9,24, and there were scarce data on gynecologic cancers and the 3-weekly carboplatin and paclitaxel regimen. A study by Fehr et al. showed that 8 of 11 women with breast, ovarian or endometrial cancer (72.7%) receiving 6 cycles of 3-weekly carboplatin and paclitaxel had grades 0–1 CIA (0–25% hair loss) after using DigniCap®, another scalp cooling system which is based on thermoelectric cooling technology25. But that study was limited by its small sample size. Lee et al. evaluated the effectiveness of scalp cooling in a retrospective study involving 83 patients26. However, only 10 of these patients had gynecologic cancers, and the chemotherapy regimen did not include the standard 3-weekly carboplatin and paclitaxel. Additionally, some participants had pre-existing alopecia at enrolment, making the results difficult to interpret. Another study by Oki et al. containing 9 evaluable gynecologic cancer showed that the efficacy of scalp cooling in hair preservation was 33.3%27. In the only prospective study on gynecologic cancers, DigniCap® failed to prevent CIA in women undergoing 3–4 cycles of 3-weekly carboplatin and paclitaxel, compared to 87.5% receiving the dose-dense regimen23. However, this was a pilot study with only 8 women using the 3-weekly regimen and 20 using the dose-dense regimen. One website estimated that the efficacy of scalp cooling was 34% after 3-weekly carboplatin and paclitaxel (https://coldcap.com/scalp-cooling-outcomes-calculator/). However, the way of estimation was not specified.

Second, most studies were performed on Caucasians and the efficacy of scalp cooling in Asians was unclear. The HOPE study showed that 26.7% of Asian women had no alopecia after scalp cooling compared to 0% in the control group after 4 cycles of cyclophosphamide with either doxorubicin or docetaxel in breast cancer patients22. No patients in that study used carboplatin and paclitaxel. One simulation trial suggested that parameters like the overall head size, hair follicle depth, hair/air layer thickness, and the hair/air layer thermal conductivity could affect the performance of cooling devices28. The removal of air gap between the cap and the scalp could lower the hair follicle temperature and enhance the performance of scalp cooling. As Asian women usually have a smaller body habitus than Caucasians, the cool caps, which the sizes are tailormade for Caucasians, might not fit some small Asian women as observed by our nurses.

Third, there were different assessment tools for CIA, such as the CTCAE, Dean score, and World Health Organization (WHO) assessment. Yet, the study endpoints and the definition of CIA varied in different studies, making it difficult to compare across different studies29. For example, Fehr et al. utilized the WHO scoring system and the study time point was after 6 cycles of chemotherapy25. One randomized control study in breast cancer defined successful hair preservation as a Dean score of 0–2 at 4 weeks after chemotherapy12. The HOPE study defined this as no alopecia at the end of chemotherapy22.

Another factor that might affect the reported effective rate was the acceptance and compliance to scalp cooling. 39.4% of our patients refused to join the study despite counselling by experienced research officers and investigators with written information sheets. Furthermore, our drop-out rate was 30%, in comparison to 2.8–25% reported in the literature8,12,25,27. Compliance to scalp cooling might affect the effectiveness of scalp cooling. Although this study was conducted during the COVID pandemic, none of the patients who refused to join the study or dropped out was because of COVID. Instead, the main reason was related to coldness. One systemic review illustrated that fear of coldness was a significant obstacle to the acceptance of scalp cooling30. The most common AEs of scalp cooling included cold feeling, headache, and neck pain24. Grades 1–2 cold thermal injury were also reported in four women31. In our population, the fear of coldness could be aggravated by some traditional beliefs. Coldness equates ‘wind’ in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), and wind is regarded as an exogenous pathogen that disrupts the body defense and functioning32. Head coldness, also known as ‘head wind’ or ‘tou feng’33, was commonly associated with general weakness and diminished immune function. Such belief might aggravate their fear of coldness. The efficacy of CIA prevention was up to 38.9% in the per-protocol population in our study. Several ways may improve the acceptance and compliance rates of scalp cooling in Chinese women, such as promoting patients’ awareness of scalp cooling, establishing a platform for patients to share experiences, education and care by accredited TCM practitioners on ‘head wind’, and making smaller cool caps for those with smaller head circumferences to reduce the scalp-cap air gap.

CIA is often overlooked by clinicians because it is reversible and not life-threatening30. However, its psychological and social repercussions could be profound and cannot be underestimated . A Korean study found that breast cancer women with high distress from CIA had poorer body image, higher depression levels, and lower emotional, role and social functioning, compared to those with less distress from CIA34. For these women, CIA symbolized a new loss of femininity, as both breasts and hair were signs of femininity35. Limited research had examined the psychological burden and patients’ reported outcomes (PROs) of CIA in gynecologic malignancies23,36,37. These malignancies often require treatments leading to the loss of female reproductive organs, which is important symbol of femininity for women. In Asian socio-cultural setting where physical appearance is tied to personal and social identity, women are expected to maintain their appearance for their social and familial roles. CIA can undermine these expectations, leading to a perceived failure in fulfilling their roles as daughters, wives, or mothers, thus ending in psychological distress.

The scalp cooling group in our study had significantly lower incidence of mild to severe psychological distress than the control group only after cycle 2 of chemotherapy (Table 2). Although the difference was not significant after cycle 4 and 2–3 weeks after completion of chemotherapy, the incidence was in fact 10–15% lower in the intervention group than the control group, which might be explained by the small sample size. Besides, we demonstrated that the actual median PHQ-ADS scores were significantly lower in the intervention group after cycle 2 and 2–3 week after completion of chemotherapy (Fig. 3), and the trend of worsening of PHQ-ADS scores was only significant in the control group but not the intervention group (S_Table 3). This highlighted that the psychological distress in the control group could not be neglected.

The stereotypical appearance of CIA stigmatizes women as cancer patients, leading to social withdrawal and worsening their QOL. Consequently, some women shave their hair early during chemotherapy to avoid facing continuous hair loss and problems with hair care. To conceal CIA, many women resort to wigs or head coverings which can be costly and inconvenient35. Our study demonstrated a better QOL in the scalp cooling group. This might be due to reduced incidence of CIA that potentially facilitated them to resume their normal social life after treatment. Furthermore, in TCM, hair symbolizes health and ‘Qi’, where ‘Qi’ represents vital energy and is a fundamental life element38,39. Qi depletion was historically associated with alopecia and cancers40,41. It was hypothesized that hair could be preserved by invigorating Qi through acupuncture42. Hair preservation could be regarded as a signal of retaining sufficient Qi, energy, and resources for vital organs, which might explain the perception of improved physical capability and physical functioning in women who could preserve their hair.

To our knowledge, our study was the first randomized trial on scalp cooling in gynecologic cancers in Asian population10. This was also the largest cohort evaluating its value on psychological distress in women using 3-weekly carboplatin and paclitaxel. Another strength of our study was the comprehensive and longitudinal evaluation of the psychosocial impact and QOL of CIA based on various questionnaires. Nevertheless, our study was limited by the relatively high drop-out rate, exceeding the reported drop-out rate and our estimation by almost 10%. A larger sample size might have improved the likelihood of detecting the statistical significance of some of the findings. The sample size in the per-protocol population was only 19 in the intervention group, which might also affect the assessment of the efficacy of the scalp cooling. Yet, our study provides new information on the barriers to the acceptance of scalp cooling, which can facilitate the design of future studies. In addition, photographs were not taken for Dean score evaluation by a fully blinded investigator, because patients were generally reluctant to consent to photo-taking based on our experience. Although clinicians were unaware of patient group assignments and were instructed to assess the Dean score at the start of each consultation, there might still be a remote possibility that patients inadvertently disclosed their group allocation during the assessment. Lastly, we did not measure our patients’ head circumferences, and hence the correlation with the efficacy of scalp cooling and compliance could not be evaluated.

Conclusion

Scalp cooling can reduce CIA with tolerable AEs. It can potentially improve the QOL of women during and after chemotherapy. However, approximately 40% of eligible women declined to participate in the study, and up to 30% of the patients using cool caps did not adhere to the treatment regimen. This poor acceptance and compliance may be attributed to some traditional misbeliefs that associated coldness with ‘head wind’ and poor health status. Further effort is required to overcome the obstacles in the acceptance of scalp cooling. A larger-scale study is needed to confirm the results of this study.
