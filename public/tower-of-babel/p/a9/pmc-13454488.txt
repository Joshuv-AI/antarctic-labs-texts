System dynamics modeling of livestock and poultry manure supply chains in the water-energy-food-carbon-waste nexus
Authors: Changjiang Yu, Xiaojing Jia, Shandong Mou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe utilization of livestock manure is a crucial area for reducing pollution and carbon emissions. However, current research focuses mainly on the impact of individual actors, overlooking a system view. This study uses system dynamics to develop a regional supply chain model for manure resource utilization, examining its overall effects through the water-energy-food-carbon emissions-waste Nexus. The main findings are as follows. (1) Within the Business-as-usual baseline scenario supply chain, comprising small- and medium-sized livestock and poultry farmers (SMS-LPFs), third-party companies (TPCs), and regional crop farmers, water, energy, food, and waste quantities all increase significantly over the simulation period. However, carbon emissions first decrease and then rise, underscoring the need for sustainable supply chain management. (2) Investing in water-saving facilities for manure treatment by SMS-LPFs can significantly reduce water use, energy consumption, carbon emissions, and waste. However, excessive investment may reduce livestock numbers and grain yield growth, whereas modest investment can improve the profits of SMS-LPFs. (3) There is no direct proportional relationship between manure treatment water-saving rates and the supply chain’s carbon reduction. Although higher initial water savings enhance carbon reduction, further increases in water savings may eventually hinder carbon mitigation. (4) Improving the precision of government supervision reduces manure leakage and environmental pollution but can paradoxically lead to increased carbon emissions in the supply chain. These insights highlight the importance of adopting a system dynamics approach and integrated management strategies to promote sustainable manure resource use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46423-y.Keywords: Manure management, System dynamics, Sustainable supply chainSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

China accounts for 65% of global pork production1,2. While large-scale livestock farming supplies meat to people, it also uses vast amounts of water3 and energy4. Persistent environmental pollution issues come from poor manure management5. Animal husbandry account for approximately 15% of total global carbon emissions that refer to the quantity of CO₂-equivalent released into the atmosphere, and livestock and poultry excreta are the primary sources of carbon emissions in the animal husbandry sector6. To meet the rising demands of a growing population, meat and grain production need to increase by about 70% by 20507, requiring expansion of both livestock and crop systems. On the one hand, much of the resources used for livestock feed are wasted by humans8, and competition between food and feed reduces the efficiency of current food systems, raising environmental and resource costs9. On the other side, processed livestock manure provides organic fertilizers and biogas slurry that can significantly improve crop yield and quality10. By combining livestock and crop production into a closed-loop system, we can leverage synergistic effects to support both large-scale growth and environmental health11. The relationship between crop cultivation and livestock rearing in China has evolved from a traditional integrated model to a current state of separation. This shift has triggered a series of agroecological challenges. On the planting side, the prolonged and intensive use of chemical fertilizers has degraded soil quality, while on the livestock side, inadequate manure management has caused widespread non-point source pollution. A key measure to advance agricultural sustainability is the reconstruction of the crop–livestock nexus12,13, which requires leveraging diverse platforms and organizational mechanisms to bridge the existing disconnect between planting and livestock production12.

The utilization of livestock manure is crucial for establishing a closed-loop livestock-crop system. However, the relentless pursuit of economies of scale by large-scale livestock enterprises has led to the separation of livestock and crop production systems, leaving insufficient nearby land to absorb the manure produced. The disconnect between waste producers and potential users of recycled resources requires intermediary platforms to bridge the gaps in circular utilization14. Third-party companies (TPCs), acting as platform organizations, enable centralized collection and treatment of manure from small- and medium-sized livestock and poultry farmers (SMS-LPFs), and then market organic fertilizers and biogas slurry to crop producers. This facilitates effective supply-demand matching between manure generators and end-users15–17. Sustainable management of livestock manure necessitates systemic improvements in both production efficiency and environmental performance18. Building on this, the present study defines the livestock manure supply chain led by TPCs as a relational network consisting of SMS-LPFs, TPCs, and regional crop producers within a specific area (see Fig. 1). The operation of this supply chain decouples manure absorption from constraints on local land resources, thereby enabling the long-distance and regional-scale integration of livestock and crop production.

Regionally integrated manure resource utilization supply chain led by TPC.

Optimizing system efficiency by managing interconnected resource flows significantly advances sustainable development19. Research focusing on socially and environmentally responsible value chains is an emerging area within operations management20. In livestock manure supply chains led by TPCs, multiple resource streams—including water, energy, food, carbon, and waste—are involved. However, few studies address how to improve overall resource utilization within these supply chains. The Nexus approach is particularly suited for exploring interactions, interdependencies, and synergies among resources, with agricultural waste resource recovery being a key area of Nexus research21. Nexus offers a comprehensive framework for analyzing resource efficiency and management strategies in integrated livestock-crop systems, highlighting the natural interconnections among resources during their development and use22–24. This approach generally combines related resources within a unified systems model25. System dynamics, a method well-suited for analyzing complex, interconnected systems, is especially appropriate for Nexus modeling26. In this study, we use the water-energy-food-carbon emissions-waste Nexus framework and apply system dynamics to examine how changes in agricultural policy and management strategies affect resource utilization efficiency in regional livestock manure supply chains. Accordingly, we identify management strategies that create synergistic and systemic benefits, fostering the sustainable development of integrated livestock-crop systems.

Literature review

Management of manure from SMS-LPFs

The primary responsibility for managing manure from large-scale livestock operations lies with the enterprises themselves. Past research has mainly examined government actions, such as increasing penalties or offering subsidies27,28, and implementing environmental taxes and fees29. Environmental risks linked to livestock manure and public concerns have influenced government decisions30. However, these interventions often experience delays and do not produce quick economic or social gains, while also adding to the government’s fiscal load31. To encourage the use of livestock manure as a resource, developing centralized treatment systems is crucial17. Centralized manure processing can effectively reduce pollution, and involving specialized organizations, such as TPCs, in manure management and resource recovery should be promoted, possibly through targeted subsidies5. The third-party governance model is increasingly seen as a key trend in China’s efforts to control environmental pollution32,33. Managing manure involves many stakeholders, and conflicting incentives can often occur in multi-actor collaborations34. Game theory studies show that large livestock enterprises aim to cut manure treatment costs, TPCs want to maximize profits, and local governments focus on protecting the environment35,36. There is also a need to develop efficient systems for the centralized collection and distribution of manure and other agricultural waste, along with better methods and industries for effective manure use15. Jia et al found that involving TPCs in carbon trading did not reduce manure transfer fees but did help shift livestock manure management from government-led to market-oriented governance37. China has launched large-scale policies to upgrade equipment, including livestock manure collection and treatment facilities. If TPCs invest in and upgrade these facilities for SMS-LPFs, it will further support the move toward market-based livestock manure management38.

These studies provide a solid foundation for manure management and resource utilization research, demonstrating a transition from government-driven to market-oriented approaches. However, conflicting motivations among stakeholders in the manure treatment process reduce the efficiency of resource utilization. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate strategies to enhance the efficiency of livestock manure resource utilization from a supply chain perspective39.

Water-energy-food-carbon emissions -waste nexus

The water-energy-food (WEF) Nexus has become a popular analytical framework across various fields. Recently, waste has been included in the WEF Nexus40. Garcia and You assert that waste management should be a key part of the WEF Nexus19. Improving the water-energy-food-waste (WEFW) Nexus can enhance economic and environmental sustainability41. Preuss et al using a life cycle assessment approach, studied technological options for managing livestock manure from the WEFW Nexus perspective, suggesting the use of manure for organic fertilizers, biogas, pyrolysis, and biochar42. Future studies should focus on designing and improving waste supply chains within the WEFW Nexus19, including analyzing how the WEFW supply chain affects regional development43. Additionally, carbon considerations are increasingly being added to the Nexus framework23,44. Manure is a major source of greenhouse gas emissions in large-scale livestock operations29,45, but integrated crop-livestock systems can greatly reduce these emissions46. Therefore, using the Nexus framework to explore sustainable development strategies for integrated livestock-crop systems is very appropriate47,48.

In summary, the WEF nexus has been widely adopted as an analytical framework in agricultural resource management (see Table 1), providing a solid foundation for the present study. However, existing research in this field has rarely incorporated the WEFCW nexus as a unified analytical construct. Given the urgent environmental and climate challenges of our time, expanding the traditional WEF nexus is both necessary and timely; thus, this study is the first to employ the WEFCW nexus in its entirety. Moreover, prior studies in agricultural resource management have typically examined single-scenario, one-directional changes in water, energy, or food, neglecting a systems perspective and overlooking the emergent properties intrinsic to the nexus. In the regionalized livestock manure resource utilization supply chain proposed herein, water, energy, food, waste, and carbon circulate dynamically, generating synergistic interactions that hold substantial implications for regional development and the promotion of a circular economy. This work represents the first study to investigate livestock manure management through the lens of a regionalized supply chain within the WEFCW nexus framework.

Research methodology and model construction

Regional supply chain for the resource utilization of livestock manure

Within a network comprising SMS-LPFs, TPCs, and regional crop producers, the TPC serves as a critical link between upstream SMS-LPFs and downstream crop producers, fostering the development of an integrated, regionally based ecological circular agriculture model. In this framework, manure generated by numerous local SMS-LPFs is processed and utilized by regional crop producers, closing nutrient loops and enhancing sustainability. Guided by the TPC, the livestock manure supply chain advances resource recovery through coordinated regional operations and strategic supply chain collaboration (see Fig. 2). This TPC-led regional livestock manure supply chain encompasses multiple interrelated subsystems—water, energy, food, carbon, and waste—and operates within a dynamic context shaped by external social, economic, and environmental factors.

Construction of the system dynamics model

System dynamics uses computer-based modeling to perform system simulation analyses, with its earliest uses rooted in operations management55. For complex problems in sustainable development—such as stakeholder behavior, public preferences, and technical processes—mathematical optimization models often fall short, whereas system dynamics is highly effective56. These models are versatile across many scenarios, providing strong solutions for operations management57, and are especially suitable for challenges in agriculture and natural resource management58. Based on the basic inflow tree modeling method of system dynamics59–61, this study develops simple inflow tree models, T1(t) to T9(t) (see Table 2), for the regional livestock manure resource utilization supply chain (LMRUSC).

Case study

The research team conducted field investigations in Jiujiang and Xinyu in Jiangxi Province, Dongying in Shandong Province, Chengde in Hebei Province, and Fangshan District in Beijing, along with their respective TPCs. This study selects Jiangxi ZH Environmental Protection Group Co., Ltd., headquartered in Xinyu, Jiangxi Province, as a representative TPC case, with simulation data drawn from the company and its Luofang Town location. Functioning as a pivotal intermediary in the livestock manure resource utilization supply chain, the company’s operational model has been formally recognized by China’s National Development and Reform Commission and included in the List of Reform Measures and Best Practices for the Promotion of the National Ecological Civilization Pilot Zones. Model validation

In this research, variables are assigned equations in additive, multiplicative, product-difference, and table-function forms; detailed formulations for all variables are provided in the Supplementary Equations file. To validate the model, system dynamics testing was conducted, primarily through extreme condition tests and behavioral sensitivity analyses.

Extreme condition testing

The model must undergo extreme condition testing, which essentially ensures that each equation remains valid even when specific parameters are set to extreme values. Accordingly, we assign key system parameters to their limits, simulate the model, and observe system behavior to assess its alignment with real-world expectations. Specifically, the number of pigs raised is abruptly set to zero, as manure generation is directly related to livestock numbers and represents the upstream segment of the manure resource utilization supply chain. The simulation results are shown in (Fig. 12). When the pig inventory of a single farm is set to zero, none of the stock variables in the system dynamics model produce any simulated data. This outcome shows that the model successfully passes the extreme condition test.

Extreme condition testing.

Behavioral sensitivity testing

Because of interactions among various feedback loops, system dynamics models of complex systems often show low sensitivity to most parameters. Therefore, referencing real-world systems is necessary to evaluate the effects of parameter changes. When setting the manure treatment water-saving rate variable, this study noted that SMS-LPFs rarely invest in water-saving facilities during manure collection and treatment, mainly due to cost concerns. Consequently, the relationship between investment and water-saving rate was based on Wang et al64, and a behavioral sensitivity analysis was conducted. The simulation results are presented in (Fig. 13). According to these results, when SMS-LPFs do not invest in water-saving facilities for manure collection and treatment, the water consumption of the supply chain follows the trend shown by Curve 1. When a 100,000 RMB investment is made in such facilities, the water consumption follows Curve 2, demonstrating a significant—and nonlinear—reduction in clean water usage for manure management. These findings confirm that the model passes the behavioral sensitivity analysis.

Scenario analysis

This research designs three scenario analyses (see Table 4): Scenario 1 – Business-as-usual baseline scenario, representing current supply chain strategies; Scenario 2 – Optimization through investment in water-saving manure treatment facilities by SMS-LPFs; and Scenario 3 – Optimization through improved regulatory precision by TLG. Scenario 1 (Business-as-usual) examines the performance of water, energy, food, waste, and carbon emissions within the supply chain over the simulation period, assuming its main participants maintain existing management and market strategies. Scenario 2 investigates the impacts on water, energy, food, waste, carbon emissions, and the economic benefits of supply chain participants when SMS-LPFs adopt water-saving facilities for manure treatment. Scenario 3 explores the effects on water, energy, food, waste, and carbon emissions when TPCs share manure treatment data from livestock enterprises with local governments, enabling the latter to apply artificial intelligence and big data analytics to estimate illegal manure discharge volumes. This improvement in regulatory precision is then assessed for its influence on supply chain dynamics. Finally, based on the comparative results of these three simulation scenarios, appropriate management strategies are proposed for regionalized livestock manure resource utilization supply chains to achieve systematic and sustainable development.

Scenario 1 – Business-as-usual baseline scenario

In the livestock manure resource utilization supply chain, using current data as the baseline scenario, simulation results show that over 60 months, total water consumption within the supply chain will reach 16,128.6 tons (see Fig. 14), energy consumption will total 1 million kWh (see Fig. 15), the average monthly pig inventory per farm will be 928 heads (see Fig. 16), grain yield will increase by 954 tons (see Fig. 17), carbon emissions equivalent will be 43.26 tons (see Fig. 18), and total waste treated will be 16,395 tons (see Fig. 19). The monthly profit of TPCs is 1.75 million RMB (see Fig. 20), while the monthly profit for SMS-LPFs is 183,300 RMB (see Fig. 21). Water consumption, energy use, grain yield, and waste treatment all show a consistent upward trend, mainly due to the gradual increase in livestock numbers at individual farms, leading to higher manure production at the source. As TPCs process more manure, the production of organic fertilizer and biogas also increases.

However, the carbon emission simulation curve initially rises, then declines, and later resumes an upward trend. This pattern shows that in the early stages of building the manure resource utilization supply chain, benefits of the carbon mitigation that denotes strategies and actions to reduce or offset carbon emissions are limited. As the cooperation rate between SMS-LPFs and TPCs increases, the supply chain becomes a net-negative GHG emissions within the modeled boundary. However, as livestock numbers continue to grow, carbon emissions from manure transportation, organic fertilizer production, and fertilizer transport eventually exceed the reductions, leading to a rise in total carbon emissions.

These findings indicate that, within the livestock manure resource utilization supply chain, gradually establishing cooperative relationships between SMS-LPFs and TPCs can effectively lower carbon emissions and support further increases in both grain production and livestock numbers. Therefore, additional regulatory and policy adjustments are needed to improve the supply chain’s sustainability.

Scenario 2 – Optimization through investment in water-saving manure treatment facilities by SMS-LPFs

King and Lenox used statistical methods to analyze the direction and significance of the relationship between different pollution reduction strategies and profitability65. Their study showed that waste prevention provides economic benefits, but no evidence was found to indicate that companies profit from reducing pollution through other methods. Therefore, this section examines the effects of SMS-LPF investments in water-saving facilities on the manure resource utilization supply chain. The parameter settings are provided in Table 5. It is assumed that SMS-LPFs invest 250,000 RMB, 500,000 RMB, 750,000 RMB, and 1,000,000 RMB (In the figure, labels 1–5 correspond to the respective investment amounts for this measure), respectively, in water-saving facilities for manure treatment, with a depreciation period of 60 months. The investment range was derived from interviews with case enterprises. This range reflects typical engineering cost components, including water-saving cleaning systems, drinking water-saving facilities, solid-liquid separation equipment, storage and conveyance upgrades, as well as related civil works and installation. Given regional differences in farm scale, labor costs, and infrastructure requirements, this range should be interpreted as a plausible investment interval for regions with conditions similar to those of the case study area.

When SMS-LPFs invest in water-saving facilities for manure treatment, Fig. 22 shows that water consumption in the manure resource utilization supply chain decreases significantly with increased investment, exhibiting diminishing returns; the difference in water-saving effectiveness between 750,000 RMB and 1,000,000 RMB investments is relatively small. As illustrated in Fig. 23, energy use also drops sharply as the total waste processed decreases (see Fig. 24), thereby reducing the energy required for TPC transportation. At the same time, grain yield declines (see Fig. 25), because reduced water use in manure management results in less manure collected by TPCs. This increases the total solids (TS) concentration in manure, reducing the amount of crop straw needed for blending, and ultimately lowering both organic fertilizer and biogas slurry production by TPCs. As a result, TPC profits also decrease (see Fig. 26). Importantly, SMSLPF’s investment in water-saving facilities helps offset early-stage losses for TPCs. Furthermore, as investment levels increase, profit fluctuations become more noticeable since higher TS concentration in manure leads to larger organic fertilizer inventories.

Figures 27 and 28 show that investing in water-saving facilities reduces SMS-LPF profits, and lower income slows livestock growth. However, Curves 1 and 2 in Fig. 28 indicate that during the first five months of supply chain operation, when SMS-LPFs invest 250,000 RMB, their profits are actually higher than in the no-investment scenario. This unexpected result occurs because small investments in water-saving facilities, when implemented in collaboration with TPCs, lower the manure treatment fees that SMS-LPFs are required to pay, thereby improving their profitability in the early stages of operation. A deeper underlying reason is that, in the initial phase of the simulation, SMS-LPFs maintain relatively small herd sizes, resulting in low overall profitability. At this stage, manure treatment fees and penalties imposed by local government environmental oversight account for a relatively large share of their total operating costs. Consequently, modest investments in water-saving facilities can significantly reduce both manure treatment expenses and regulatory penalty costs, thereby boosting profits in the early period. As livestock operations expand and herd sizes grow, the resulting economies of scale substantially increase profitability. In this later stage, the proportion of manure treatment fees and environmental regulatory penalties in total costs becomes comparatively small, and SMS-LPFs are more inclined to allocate resources toward further herd expansion rather than environmental investments. Therefore, environmental regulatory and penalty measures for pollution control should be designed as dynamic, scale-linked mechanisms rather than static, fixed rules. As shown in Fig. 29, investing in water-saving facilities for manure management significantly cuts carbon emissions in the manure resource utilization supply chain. Specifically, by the end of the simulation, carbon emissions under Scenario 1 remain positive, while all water-saving investment scenarios show negative carbon emissions in which yield a negative carbon balance within the defined system boundary, indicating that water-saving measures can reduce, and even reverse, the supply chain’s carbon emissions—turning it into a negative-carbon supply chain. Moreover, in the first eight months, investing more in water-saving facilities results in more significant carbon mitigation effects. However, after 12 months, the scenario with a 500,000 RMB investment (Curve 3) achieves the most significant overall carbon reduction. This indicates that the supply chain’s carbon mitigation effect is not directly proportional to the level of investment in water-saving facilities. Excessive investment can impair the ability of TPCs within the supply chain to generate renewable energy. At this stage, the causal feedback loop linking water-saving investments, manure total solids (TS) concentration, technological pathway choice, and carbon outcomes operates as follows: water-saving investments increase the TS concentration of manure, which induces third-party companies to reduce biogas production and shift toward greater organic fertilizer production. Under these conditions, carbon emissions within the supply chain increase rather than decrease.Therefore, policies for livestock manure resource utilization should not focus solely on the SMS-LPFs, but rather prioritize comprehensive supply chain management. In summary, from the perspective of carbon mitigation, increasing investment in water-saving facilities does not always produce the optimal outcome for the livestock manure resource utilization supply chain.

Scenario 3 – Optimization through improved regulatory precision by TLG

In the context of cooperation between SMS-LPFs and TPCs, current research consistently advocates strengthening government oversight. However, in real-world situations, factors such as avian influenza can keep farms closed for long periods, making it hard for authorities to access them and verify manure leakage and discharge, thereby creating enforcement challenges. Therefore, this section explores how enhancing the accuracy of government oversight can influence the livestock manure resource utilization supply chain. Assuming the penalty amount set by the government stays the same, regulatory precision can be improved through sharing manure collection data with TPCs. Given the strong correlation between farm scale and manure output—and with farm scale already recorded in government registries—significant discrepancies between expected and actual manure treatment volumes can serve as a clear indicator of illegal discharge by livestock enterprises. For example, the Hunan Provincial Department of Ecology and Environment of China issued a notice in March 2026 entitled Proposal to Livestock and Poultry Farms (Households) Across the Province, which explicitly requires TPCs to establish manure collection records and ensure full traceability throughout the process of manure resource utilization.These methods help local governments better analyze and detect manure leakage at farms, thus improving regulatory accuracy. The regulatory precision settings are provided in Table 6. In the simulation figures, the label baseline scenario denotes a regulatory precision of 0.5, while enhancing the regulatory precision indicates a regulatory precision of 0.8. As shown in Figure 30, greater regulatory precision leads to lower water consumption across the supply chain, and this impact becomes increasingly significant over the simulation period. This occurs because as regulatory precision improves, the chances of detecting and penalizing manure leakage rise, which reduces the profits of SMS-LPFs (see Fig. 31) and consequently decreases the number of pigs they keep (see Fig. 32), ultimately resulting in lower water consumption throughout the supply chain. Conventional wisdom may suggest that greater precision in manure regulation—alongside reductions in livestock numbers and water consumption—would naturally lead to lower energy use, decreased waste treatment volumes, and reduced carbon emissions. However, when analyzed from the perspective of the livestock manure resource utilization supply chain, the results reveal a more complex reality. As shown in Fig. 33, enhanced regulatory precision actually increases the total volume of waste processed within the supply chain. This indicates that stricter government oversight increases cooperation among SMS-LPFs and TPCs, thereby boosting waste treatment throughput. While higher regulatory precision reduces the profits of SMS-LPFs (Fig. 31), it simultaneously increases the profitability of TPCs (Fig. 34). Furthermore, as Fig. 35 illustrates, supply chain carbon emissions rise rather than fall. This is primarily due to increased energy consumption (Fig. 36) and to shifts in the technological pathways adopted by TPCs. The strategy directly amplifies the volume of waste transported and processed within the supply chain, which in turn substantially raises energy usage. Moreover, it indirectly reduces water usage by livestock enterprises, thereby increasing the TS concentration in manure and prompting TPCs to consume more energy to produce more organic fertilizer rather than biogas energy; however, the increase in organic fertilizer enhances grain yields (Fig. 37). It should be noted that the current carbon accounting boundary focuses primarily on emissions generated within the regional manure supply chain. Under this boundary, more precise supervision reduces manure leakage and environmental pollution, increases the proportion of manure entering formal collection and treatment pathways. This, in turn, expands transport and processing activities and therefore increases carbon emissions within the supply chain. However, this result does not imply that stricter supervision is environmentally undesirable. Rather, it highlights a trade-off between pollution control and operational carbon emissions within the defined system boundary. If broader accounting boundaries were adopted, the carbon implications could differ.

Conclusions

Theoretical contributions

First, this study reconceptualizes livestock manure utilization as a regional circular supply chain problem rather than merely an environmental treatment issue. By explicitly modeling the interactions among SMS-LPFs, TPCs, and crop farmers, the study shifts the focus from isolated actor-level decisions to coordinated, multi-actor supply chain dynamics. In this sense, TPCs are not treated simply as service providers, but as platform organizations that connect upstream manure generation, midstream treatment and conversion, and downstream agricultural utilization. This perspective extends the literature on sustainable and circular supply chains by showing how residual-resource flows, decentralized actors, and platform-based coordination jointly shape regional operational performance over time.

Second, the study shows that extending the nexus from WEF or WEFW to WEFCW generates new theoretical insight beyond an incremental addition of variables. In previous nexus-based system dynamics studies, carbon emissions and waste are often treated separately, or one is incorporated while the other remains external to the system boundary. In contrast, this study endogenizes both dimensions simultaneously. This is theoretically important because waste in the manure supply chain is not only a disposal burden but also a recoverable resource flow, while carbon emissions are not merely an output indicator but a dynamic consequence that feeds back into treatment choices, investment effects, and policy outcomes. Their simultaneous inclusion reveals additional feedback loops and trade-offs among manure leakage, treatment intensity, water-saving investment, agricultural absorption, and carbon mitigation. As a result, policies that appear effective under a narrower WEF or WEFW framework may produce unintended consequences once waste accumulation and carbon responses are jointly considered. The finding that stronger supervision can reduce leakage and pollution while increasing carbon emissions, and that higher water-saving rates do not monotonically improve carbon reduction, directly illustrates the added explanatory power of the WEFCW framework.

Third, this study advances operations and supply chain management theory by demonstrating the value of a supply-chain-centered system dynamics approach for analyzing circular resource systems. Unlike static evaluations that focus on immediate environmental or economic outcomes, the proposed model captures endogenous delays, nonlinear feedbacks, and intertemporal trade-offs among investment, treatment capacity, resource recovery, emissions, and profitability. The nine Fundamental in-tree models provide a structured way to represent how operational decisions propagate through the regional manure supply chain and affect both economic performance and nexus outcomes. In doing so, the study contributes to operations and supply chain management research by explaining how dynamic coordination, capacity adjustment, and policy intervention influence the long-term sustainability of circular supply chains under coupled resource and environmental constraints.

Practical implications

This study compares the need for investing in water-saving facilities at SMS-LPFs and examines how improved government regulatory precision affects the entire manure resource utilization supply chain. The findings indicate: (1) Investing in water-saving manure treatment facilities at farms is essential, but more investment is not always better; (2) Enhancing government regulatory precision can decrease manure leakage from farms but may also increase carbon emissions; (3) The carbon-mitigation potential of the manure resource utilization supply chain should be given due attention, with carbon-reduction accounting undertaken at the earliest opportunity and participation in carbon trading actively pursued. In doing so, carbon-trading revenues can become an additional profit source for the supply chain, thereby strengthening the incentive for SMS-LPFs to make moderate investments in water-saving facilities.

Policy recommendations

From a short-term strategic perspective, TLGs should actively promote collaboration between SMS-LPFs and TPCs. They should also encourage regional crop growers to increase their awareness of the benefits of organic fertilizer application and to purchase organic fertilizers produced by TPCs actively. From a long-term strategic perspective, TLGs should adopt a systems-thinking approach to advance the resource utilization of livestock waste, shifting the focus from merely penalizing SMS-LPFs for manure mismanagement to strengthening the entire supply chain. A comprehensive set of policies could be introduced to facilitate the upgrading of manure treatment and water-saving facilities in SMS-LPFs and to support the integration of carbon-trading mechanisms into the supply chain. These measures would significantly accelerate the achievement of regional pollution reduction and carbon mitigation goals, while contributing to selected targets within Sustainable Development Goals 12 and 13. The specific recommendations are as follows:

For TLG. (1) Shift the focus beyond farm-level manure management to a broader supply chain perspective, and formulate policies tailored to the manure resource utilization supply chain (Fig. 2). (2) Guide and facilitate the accounting of carbon-emission reductions across the manure resource utilization supply chain, thereby encouraging SMS-LPFs and TPCs to participate in carbon trading (Fig. 18). (3) Accurately assess the regional demand for organic fertilizer in crop production and incentivize farmers to purchase organic fertilizer supplied by TPCs.

For SMS-LPFs. (1) Make modest, early-stage investments in water-saving facilities. While this entails additional costs, it can enhance profitability by reducing manure treatment fees and mitigating environmental penalty payments (Fig. 29). (2) Establish a “raw material supply–carbon-reduction dividend” partnership model with TPCs to indirectly obtain carbon-trading revenues, thereby offsetting the cost of water-saving investments. This reduces water consumption while lowering manure treatment expenses.

For TPCs. (1) Actively collaborate with SMS-LPFs to develop dynamic manure-treatment pricing mechanisms and optimize technological process routes—both of which can be simulated using the presented system dynamics model—to improve operational efficiency. (2) Share manure-processing data with government agencies to enhance regulatory precision and increase cooperation rates among SMS-LPFs (Fig. 34 and Fig. 35). This can be supported by artificial intelligence platforms that conduct regular, dynamic analyses of manure collection volumes and synchronize the results with relevant government departments. (3) Proactively assume social responsibility within the supply chain by participating in carbon trading at an early stage and adopting win-win strategies with SMS-LPFs, thereby strengthening their market position.

For other regions worldwide. The model developed and the conclusions drawn in this study are based on the context of China, and thus their generalizability cannot be fully assured. Nevertheless, certain strategies may be transferable. First, the concept of a manure resource utilization supply chain proposed herein is particularly suited to regions with a high concentration of SMS-LPFs, and its effectiveness is further enhanced when TPCs are available to undertake centralized manure treatment; the development of such TPCs is therefore recommended. Second, in regions lacking both carbon trading markets and TPCs, local governments could organize livestock enterprises to jointly apply for international Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) carbon trading programs. This approach would increase the revenues of livestock enterprises while contributing to regional carbon reduction targets.Third, regional authorities should emphasize the integration of livestock farming and crop cultivation, thereby promoting the resource-oriented utilization of manure within a defined area. Livestock enterprises could establish long-term partnerships with crop producers, facilitating both manure disposal and crop yield enhancement. Fourth, TLGs may introduce a manure treatment ledger system to document treatment activities in detail, thereby improving regulatory precision.

Limitations and future research directions

Limitations of this research. The feedback mechanisms governing livestock and poultry manure supply chain management are inherently complex; the manure treatment costs incurred by SMS-LPFs may vary substantially depending on the specific treatment methods adopted. This study did not differentiate between such variations. Additionally, the nutrient composition of manure-derived organic fertilizer can fluctuate between batches, potentially affecting crop yields—an effect that was not quantitatively assessed in this work. Moreover, the present model does not incorporate a dedicated crop-farmer subsystem. Future research could address these gaps by integrating crop farmers into the supply chain framework to enable a more holistic analysis and by expanding methodological approaches—such as participatory modelling in collaboration with stakeholders—to enhance the model’s richness and robustness. Further investigations might also explore pricing strategies for waste acquisition within the supply chain, alongside the strategic design and implementation of delayed-storage pools for biogas slurry, thereby strengthening and extending the management framework for livestock manure and agricultural waste.
