pTJK, a rare Mammaliicoccus lentus phage with broad-host-range, antibiofilm, and synergistic interactions with antimicrobials against resistant Staphylococcaceae
Authors: Faizan Ahmad, Pedro Rocha Garcia, Vitor Emanuel Lanes Viana, Sandy de Almada Estanislau, Nohman Rasheed, Rafael Reis de Rezende, Eduardo Luís Menezes de Almeida, Poliane Alfenas Zerbini, Marisa Alves Nogueira Diaz, Monalessa Fábia Pereira, Marcia Giambiagi-deMarval, Ciro César Rossi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance in Staphylococcaceae poses a serious threat to human and animal health, driving the search for alternative strategies such as bacteriophage therapy. Here, we describe the isolation and characterization of pTJK, one of the first bacteriophages reported to infect Mammaliicoccus lentus, recovered together with its host strain TJK24 from sewage-contaminated stream water. pTJK is a novel lytic phage with a broad host range, capable of infecting multiple Mammaliicoccus and Staphylococcus species. It significantly reduced biofilm formation and disrupted preformed biofilms, including in non-host strains, and displayed synergistic activity with gentamicin and erythromycin. The phage remained viable under moderate temperature and pH variations, UV exposure, and chloroform treatment. Genome analysis revealed a 135.8 kb double-stranded DNA genome with 231 ORFs, including multiple endolysins and depolymerases potentially involved in antibiofilm activity, and no detected resistance or host virulence genes. Comparative genomics placed pTJK within the genus Sciuriunavirus, representing a novel yet-to-be-defined species. These findings highlight the rarity, genomic distinctiveness, and biotechnological potential of pTJK, reinforcing the relevance of Staphylococcaceae phages as promising tools to combat antimicrobial resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00203-026-04786-w.

Introduction

The Staphylococcaceae family encompasses over 100 recognized species (Parte et al. 2020), with Staphylococcus standing out as the most prominent genus due to its widespread presence in human, animal, and environmental microbiota. Several Staphylococcus species are well-known for their ability to cause a broad range of infections in humans, from mild skin conditions to severe, life-threatening diseases (Bartlett et al. 2022; Rossi et al. 2024).

Closely related to this genus is Mammaliicoccus, which was only recently reclassified based on phylogenomic analyses—its species were formerly included within Staphylococcus (Madhaiyan et al. 2020). This genus includes species that are component of the commensal flora in a variety of animals, and it can be isolated from humans, animals, food products, and the environment (Xu et al. 2024, 2025; Padmavathi et al. 2025). Although often regarded as less pathogenic, some Mammaliicoccus species, such as M. lentus and M. sciuri, have occasionally been isolated from clinically relevant human and animal infections (Mazal and Sieger 2010; Matos et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2024). Importantly, these bacteria have also been implicated in the horizontal transfer of antimicrobial resistance genes to Staphylococcus spp., contributing to the spread of resistance within a One Health context that links human, animal, and environmental health (Carvalho et al. 2024).

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among Staphylococcaceae species, particularly within the genus Staphylococcus, represents a significant and growing threat to public health. The emergence and global spread of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) have been especially concerning, as MRSA is associated with both healthcare- and community-acquired infections that are increasingly difficult to treat (Turner et al. 2019). In recognition of this threat, the World Health Organization (WHO) has classified MRSA as a high-priority pathogen for research and development of new strategies to combat antimicrobial resistance (WHO 2024).

One promising alternative to conventional antibiotics is phage therapy, which involves the use of bacteriophages—viruses that specifically infect and lyse bacterial cells—as therapeutic agents. Originally explored in the early 20th century, phage therapy has regained scientific interest in recent decades due to the escalating crisis of antimicrobial resistance. Bacteriophages offer several advantages over traditional antimicrobials: they are highly specific to their bacterial targets, minimizing disruption to the host microbiota; they can self-amplify at the site of infection as long as susceptible bacteria are present; and they often remain effective against multidrug-resistant strains (Yao et al. 2025).

In this context, the present study aimed to isolate an environmental bacteriophage and comprehensively characterize its potential against multidrug-resistant Staphylococcaceae strains. The phage was assessed for host range, adsorption kinetics, stability under different temperature, pH, UV exposure, and chloroform conditions, and antibiofilm activity against forming and preformed biofilms. In addition, whole-genome sequencing and comparative genomic analyses were performed to determine its taxonomic placement, genomic features, and safety profile. Synergistic interactions with conventional antibiotics were also evaluated. Through this integrated approach, we sought to evaluate its therapeutic relevance and contribute to the growing field of alternative strategies for controlling resistant staphylococcal infections.

Materials and methods

Bacterial host

The bacterial strain used as the original host for phage isolation was obtained from a water sample collected from a sewage-contaminated stream in the Tijuca neighborhood, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil (coordinates: 22°55’28.2"S, 43°13’51.3"W). The sample was collected in a sterile 50 mL Falcon tube and kept refrigerated until processing. At the laboratory, the sample was seeded onto mannitol salt agar (Becton Dickinson, USA) using a sterile swab and incubated at 37 °C for up to 48 h. Colonies resembling Staphylococcaceae—characterized by circular, convex, opaque morphology with yellowish to white pigmentation—were transferred to brain heart infusion (BHI) agar (Becton Dickinson) and subjected to Gram staining and catalase testing. Gram-positive and catalase-positive isolates were then submitted for species-level identification by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF/MS), as described by Tomazi et al. (2014). MALDI-TOF/MS was performed in biological and experimental triplicate, using the FlexControl software and the MALDI Biotyper Compass 4.1 platform, with the MBT Compass Library 2022 as the reference database. Only identification scores greater than 2.3 were considered reliable for species-level classification. The identified isolate was then preserved in liquid BHI medium supplemented with 30% glycerol at − 80 °C. Prior to each assay, the strain was activated by incubation in BHI broth at 37 °C for 24 h to ensure active growth.

Antibiogram

The isolated strain was tested for antimicrobial susceptibility using the disk diffusion method, following the guidelines provided by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST, available at www.eucast.org), using commercial antibiotic discs (Sensidiscs, Brazil) with the following agents: amoxicillin (10 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), nitrofurantoin (300 µg), norfloxacin (10 µg), oxacillin (1 µg), streptomycin (10 µg), tetracycline (30 µg), and vancomycin (30 µg).

Bacteriophage isolation

Phage isolation was performed as previously described (Viana et al. 2025). Briefly, a bacteriophage was isolated from the same water sample from which the bacterial host strain had previously been obtained. The sample was first centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter to remove bacterial cells and debris. An aliquot of the filtered sample was then mixed in equal volume with an exponentially growing culture of the host strain and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow phage propagation. After incubation, the culture was centrifuged and filtered once more, and the resulting filtrate was subjected to serial tenfold dilutions. These dilutions were tested for the presence of lytic bacteriophages using the double-layer agar method, to allow visualization of plaque-forming units (PFUs) as clear lysis zones. To ensure phage purification, propagation was repeated three additional times from isolated plaques. Phage stocks were then prepared following the protocol described by Ahmad et al. (2025b), using SM buffer (0.1 M NaCl, 0.2 M MgSO4·7 H₂O, 1 M Tris-HCl pH 7.5, and 0.1% gelatin). The purified phage suspensions in SM buffer were supplemented with 3% chloroform to prevent potential bacterial contamination and stored at 4 °C until use in subsequent experiments.

Transmission electronic microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to visualize the morphology of the phage particles, as described previously (Ahmad et al. 2025b). A 5 µL drop of a high-titer phage suspension was carefully placed onto 300-mesh copper grids previously coated with a formvar-carbon support film. After adsorption, the sample was negatively stained with 5% uranyl acetate for 30 s. Excess stain was gently removed using filter paper. The grids were examined under an FEI Morgagni 268 transmission electron microscope (FEI Europe, Netherlands) operating at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. Images were captured with a MegaView G2 digital camera system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Phage adsorption assay

Phage adsorption kinetics were evaluated by monitoring the decrease in free phage particles over time following contact with host cells, adapting from Kropinski (2009). Briefly, an equal volume of phage solution was mixed with an exponentially growing culture of the bacterial host (MOI = 0.01) in microcentrifuge tubes and incubated at 37 °C to allow phage adsorption. At 5-minute intervals, up to a total incubation time of 30 min, individual tubes were removed and centrifuged at 10,000 × g to pellet the bacterial cells. The supernatant, containing non-adsorbed phages, was serially diluted and quantified using the double-layer agar assay to determine the concentration of free phages. As a control, a phage suspension that had not been exposed to host cells was subjected to the same procedure.

Phage host spectrum

To assess the ability of the isolated bacteriophage to infect bacterial strains beyond its original host, a spot test was performed. In this assay, 5 µL drops of the phage stock were deposited onto bacterial lawns prepared using the double-layer agar method. The assay was conducted against 38 multidrug-resistant Staphylococcaceae isolates, most of which were positive for the methicillin resistance gene mecA, and represented 13 different species (Supplementary Table S1). After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, only bacterial strains exhibiting clear plaques at the site of phage application were considered susceptible and classified as permissive hosts.

Phage viability in diverse environmental conditions

To evaluate the environmental stability of the isolated bacteriophage, its viability was tested under a variety of physical and chemical stress conditions. Four independent treatments were performed: (i) temperature stability was assessed by incubating aliquots of the phage at temperatures ranging from − 20 °C to 60 °C. Samples were collected at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 24 h to monitor the thermal impact on phage viability over time; (ii) pH tolerance was evaluated by diluting the phage (1:5) in SM buffer adjusted to pH values between 1 and 13 using HCl or NaOH. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to simulate acid and alkaline stress; (iii) UV-C radiation resistance was tested by exposing viral suspensions to a germicidal UV-C lamp (Osram, 25 W, 254 nm, Austria) at room temperature (approximately 25 °C). The total energy received ranged from 0 to 4.5 kJ/m², with aliquots collected at defined time points over a 30-minute period. UV intensity was monitored using a calibrated radiometer (Radialert 100, Instrutherm, Brazil); (iv) chemical stability was analyzed by incubating the phage with chloroform at final concentrations of 3% and 10% (v/v) at 37 °C for 24 h. Following each treatment, the remaining phage particles were quantified by the standard double-layer plaque assay. A non-treated phage suspension served as a positive control in all experiments.

Phage impact on pre-formed biofilms and biofilm formation

The antibiofilm activity of phage pTJK was assessed against its original host strain Mammaliicoccus lentus TJK24 as well as against other Staphylococcaceae strains listed in Supplementary Table S1, following standardized procedures previously described by Ahmad et al. (2025b). Two experimental approaches were employed to evaluate phage impact on both biofilm formation and pre-formed biofilms. To assess the effect of the phage on biofilm formation, bacterial cultures in exponential growth phase were co-incubated with the viral solution at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1.0 in 96-well polystyrene microplates containing BHI medium supplemented with 1% glucose. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the planktonic cells were carefully removed, and the adhered biomass was stained with crystal violet, according to the crystal violet method described by Stepanovic et al. (2007). Biofilms formed in the absence of phage served as negative controls. To evaluate the phage’s ability to disrupt pre-formed biofilms, bacterial cultures were first allowed to adhere and form mature biofilms by incubation in BHI + 1% glucose for 48 h at 37 °C in 96-well plates. After this period, the supernatant was discarded, and the wells were gently washed before being treated with phage solution at 10⁸ PFU/mL for 6 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the wells were washed again, and residual biofilms were quantified by crystal violet staining. Wells treated with SM buffer alone served as controls for the pre-formed biofilm disruption assay. All experiments were conducted in biological and technical triplicates.

Synergism between phage and antibiotics

The synergistic interaction between the isolated bacteriophage and antibiotics was evaluated using the checkerboard method (Nikolic et al. 2022). Serial tenfold dilutions of the phage suspension (from 1010 to 104 PFU/mL) were combined with two-fold serial dilutions of the antibiotics gentamicin and erythromycin (Merck, Germany), and tested against the original host strain, as well as the multidrug-resistant strains Staphylococcus haemolyticus B82iii and Mammaliicoccus sciuri B126iii (Supplementary Table S1). After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, bacterial growth was assessed, and the fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index was calculated using the formula: FIC = (MIC of the antibiotic in combination / MIC of the antibiotic alone) + (MIC of the phage in combination / MIC of the phage alone). FIC values were interpreted as follows: synergy (FIC ≤ 0.5), indifference (0.5 < FIC ≤ 4), or antagonism (FIC > 4).

To complement the analysis, the impact of phage-antibiotic combinations on biofilm formation was also investigated. Subinhibitory concentrations of phage and antibiotics, applied both separately and in combination, were incubated with bacterial suspensions in 96-well polystyrene microplates in BHI supplemented with 1% glucose. After 24 h at 37 °C, biofilm biomass was quantified by crystal violet staining as previously described.

DNA purification and sequencing

Phage genomic DNA was extracted following the protocol described by Viana et al. (2025). Briefly, a 50 mL aliquot of phage stock was precipitated with 10% polyethylene glycol (PEG) 8000 (Merck) at 4 °C for 24 h and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min. The pellet was resuspended in nuclease-free water and treated with 2U of DNase RQ1 and 1U of RNase (Promega, USA) for 1.5 h at 37 °C to eliminate contaminating nucleic acids, followed by digestion with proteinase K in the presence of SDS 10% and 0.5 M EDTA. After incubation at 65 °C, proteins were removed using sequential extractions with phenol and chloroform. The DNA was precipitated with ethanol, washed, air-dried, and resuspended in water. Purified DNA was used for library preparation with the Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-LSK109, Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on a MinION device using a FLO-MIN106 R9.4.1 flow cell for approximately 24 h.

Genome assembly and annotation

Raw sequencing reads were basecalled and demultiplexed using Guppy v6.5.7 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) in high-accuracy mode to ensured rigorous quality control, including adapter trimming and barcode recognition. Read quality metrics were evaluated using PycoQC v2.5.2 (Leger and Leonardi 2019). De novo genome assembly was carried out with SPAdes v4.0 (Prjibelski et al. 2020), and initial annotation was performed using Phold (Bouras et al. 2025). For further functional characterization, virulence genes were screened using VFDB (Liu et al. 2022a), antimicrobial resistance genes were identified via CARD (Alcock et al. 2023), and insertion sequences were searched with TnCentral (Ross et al. 2021). Bacterial defense systems were investigated using PADLOC (Payne et al. 2022) and DefenseFinder, while anti-defense elements were predicted using AcrFinder (Tesson et al. 2022). A genomic map of the phage was generated using the Proksee web server (Grant et al. 2023).

Viral classification and phylogeny

The lifestyle (lytic or temperate) of the isolated phage was inferred using PhaBOX (Shang et al. 2023). Homologous genomes were retrieved from the GenBank database via BLASTn, and those exhibiting more than 15% genome coverage were selected for comparative analyses. These genomes were aligned with MAUVE (Darling et al. 2004) and also used to construct a full-proteome phylogenetic tree with VIPTree (Nishimura et al. 2017). The taxonomic placement of the isolated phage was further assessed using VIPTree, to generate a genome-based phylogenetic trees by comparing the input genome to all viral genomes available in its internal database. The core genome of similar phages was calculated with Roary 3.13.0 (Page et al. 2015). Additionally, VIRIDIC (Moraru et al. 2020) was employed to calculate intergenomic similarities between the isolated phage and the selected reference genomes. Based on these results, the phage was classified at the genus and species levels using the thresholds of ≥ 70% nucleotide identity for genus and ≥ 95% for species, as recommended by Turner et al. (2021).

Statistical analysis

All experiments requiring statistical support were performed in biological and technical triplicates. Values obtained at different time points during the adsorption assays and under various environmental conditions were compared using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Data from biofilm treatment experiments were compared to their respective controls using the Student’s t-test. In all analyses, differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

The host strain

From the sewage-contaminated stream sample, we isolated a bacterial strain identified as Mammaliicoccus lentus, named TJK24 after the neighborhood where it was collected (Tijuca) and the year of isolation (2024). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed that TJK24 was resistant to gentamicin, erythromycin and oxacillin.

Phage pTJK is a tailed generalist bacteriophage

A bacteriophage capable of infecting M. lentus TJK24 was isolated from the same sewage-contaminated stream sample (Fig. 1A) and was named pTJK, after “phage Tijuca.” Phage pTJK exhibits a morphology typical of the class Caudoviricetes, with an icosahedral capsid approximately 85 nm in diameter and a long tail measuring about 175 nm (Fig. 1B). Adsorption assays revealed that 93.64 ± 1.21% of phage particles were adsorbed to host cells after 10 min of incubation, reaching 98.36 ± 0.55% by 30 min (Fig. 1C).

In addition to infecting its original host M. lentus TJK24, phage pTJK was also capable of lysing cultures of M. sciuri (strains GLS3 and B126iii), Staphylococcus epidermidis (B2), Staphylococcus haemolyticus (B82iii), Staphylococcus saprophyticus (B200i), and Staphylococcus xylosus (LGN3 and B72iii).

Phage pTJK withstands diverse environmental conditions

Phage pTJK demonstrated high stability under a range of adverse environmental conditions. When incubated at temperatures between 37 °C and 42 °C, viral titers remained comparable to the initial control levels up to 24 h. However, exposure to 50 °C for 4 h led to a nearly two-log reduction in viability (approximately 96.7% decrease). More extreme temperatures, such as − 20 °C and 60 °C, completely abolished phage viability within the first hour of treatment (Fig. 1D). The phage also maintained viability across a broad pH range. Titers remained similar to those of the control (pH 7.0, corresponding to the SM buffer stock) under most conditions, with complete inactivation observed only at extreme pH values of 1 and 13 (Fig. 1E). Upon exposure to UV-C light, pTJK titers remained statistically unchanged for up to 3 min (0.45 kJ/m²). Although a gradual decline was observed thereafter, high titers (~ 10⁶ PFU/mL) were still detected after 10 min (1.5 kJ/m²) of irradiation (Fig. 1F). Complete inactivation occurred after 15 min of exposure. Additionally, phage viability was not affected by chloroform concentrations up to 10% under the conditions tested (Fig. 1G).

Phage pTJK affects biofilms of host and non-host strains and acts synergistically with antibiotics

Biofilm formation by most of the tested Staphylococcaceae strains was negatively affected by the presence of phage pTJK, with reductions ranging from 6.6% to complete inhibition (100%) when compared to the respective controls (Fig. 2A). Similarly, pTJK-containing solutions were able to disrupt preformed biofilms, although to a lesser extent, with reductions ranging from 0.8% to 69.4%. Notably, the biofilm-reducing effect was also observed in strains that were not susceptible to lysis by pTJK, suggesting additional mechanisms of interference.

The antibiofilm effect of pTJK was enhanced when combined with antibiotics, as evaluated in three different strains. In the presence of gentamicin, a synergistic interaction was observed for strains B126iii (FIC = 0.499) and TJK24 (FIC = 0.063), while an indifferent effect was observed for strain B82iii (FIC = 1.0). In contrast, synergy with erythromycin was detected for strains B82iii (FIC = 0.499) and TJK24 (FIC = 0.250). For B126iii, no bacterial growth was observed at any of the erythromycin concentrations tested, preventing FIC calculation. As shown in Fig. 2B, in cases where synergism was observed, the combination of phage and antibiotic also impaired biofilm formation at subinhibitory concentrations of both agents.

Phage pTJK encodes diverse lytic enzymes and no virulence or resistance genes

Viral DNA sequencing enabled complete genome assembly, yielding 32,958 reads with an average length of 1.3 kb and an approximate coverage of 297.5×. Phage pTJK possesses a linear double-stranded DNA genome of 135,775 bp, deposited in GenBank under the accession number PQ453512. Based on predictions from PhaBOX, pTJK was classified as a lytic phage. A total of 231 open reading frames (ORFs) were identified (Supplementary Table S2), of which 36.6% (n = 84) could be assigned putative functions. These were categorized into three major functional groups: (i) genes involved in nucleic acid metabolism, primarily associated with DNA binding and processing, replication, transcription, and gene expression regulation; (ii) genes encoding structural and morphogenesis-related proteins essential for viral assembly; and (iii) genes associated with host cell lysis. This last group included proteins potentially involved in biofilm disruption, such as a holin, depolymerases (including a transglycosylase and a glycerophosphoryl diester phosphodiesterase [GDPD]), and multiple endolysins (Fig. 3A).

No genes related to antimicrobial resistance, bacterial virulence, insertion sequences or transposases were detected in the pTJK genome. However, a DNA methyltransferase was identified, suggesting a viral strategy to circumvent bacterial restriction systems.

pTJK belongs to the Herelleviridae family and clusters with globally distributed Sciuriunarivus phages

Phage pTJK was assigned to the family Herelleviridae based on phylogenomic analysis using VipTree (Fig. 3B), a family that includes most known phages infecting Staphylococcaceae. Despite this classification, few known phages showed high genomic similarity to pTJK, as only a limited number exhibited > 15% genome coverage in BLASTn comparisons (Figs. 4A, B). Among the publicly available genomes, coverage ranged from 16% to 95%, with nucleotide identities between 77.92% and 98.12%. Only eight known phages displayed coverage above 85% (Supplementary Table S3).

Genome alignment of these homologous phages showed that the largest conserved syntenic blocks correspond to regions of the pTJK genome enriched in genes with assigned functions. These include the region spanning approximately 0–30 kb, which predominantly contains genes involved in nucleic acid metabolism, and the region between ~ 70 and 140 kb, mainly associated with virion morphogenesis and lysis (Fig. 4A, as detailed in Fig. 3A). Despite undergoing rearrangements, these blocks are consistently present across all analyzed phages. The closest known relative was phage MSShimiriz1, isolated from sewage in Switzerland and infecting M. sciuri, which shared 95% coverage and 97.73% nucleotide identity with pTJK. A phylogenetic tree constructed from the most similar viral genomes showed that pTJK clusters with phages belonging to the genus Sciuriunavirus (Fig. 4B). These phages have been primarily isolated from wastewater samples collected in various regions, including Egypt, Jerusalem, Pakistan, Poland and Switzerland, between 2016 and 2024. Most infect Mammaliicoccus species, although some target Staphylococcus spp. as well. The remaining genomes with intermediate coverage levels (between 15% and 85%) were affiliated with phages from the genus Kayvirus, highlighting the genomic distinctiveness of pTJK within the Herelleviridae family.

Considering only the phage genomes within the genus Sciurinavirus, the nine phages in this group comprise a pangenome of 639 genes, of which 64 belong to the core genome (present in > 99% of the phages), 413 to the shell genome (present in 15–95% of the genomes), and the remaining 162 to the cloud genome (present in < 15%) (Fig. 4C). Notably, most genes with assigned functions are concentrated within the core genome, consistently with the alignment obtained with MAUVE (Supplementary Table S4).

VIRIDIC analysis revealed an intergenomic similarity of 91.6% between pTJK and MSShimiriz1 (Fig. 5), indicating that both belong to the same yet-to-be-defined species. Among the other Sciuriunavirus phages that cluster with pTJK, vB_SscM-1 and vB_SscM-2 are classified within the species Staphylococcus virus SscM1, while the remaining phages in the group also represent undefined species within the genus.

Discussion

Mammaliicoccus lentus (formerly Staphylococcus lentus) is an understudied member of the Staphylococcaceae family but is increasingly linked to environmental dissemination and antimicrobial resistance (Martins-Silva et al. 2023; Ahmad et al. 2025a). Although less clinically relevant than other Staphylococcus species, its wide distribution suggests a potential role as a resistance reservoir. Genomic evidence of extensive horizontal gene transfer between Mammaliicoccus and Staphylococcus further highlights the evolutionary and epidemiological relevance of this genus (Carvalho et al. 2024). This intergenus exchange of genetic material adds complexity to the evolutionary dynamics of the family and poses additional challenges for antimicrobial resistance surveillance and control strategies.

To date, only one bacteriophage has been reported with M. lentus as its original host, the jumbo phage vB_StaM_SA1. Isolated from sewage in China, this phage (~ 260 kb) infects multiple Staphylococcus species, including S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and S. haemolyticus (Zhang et al. 2022). Similarly, phage pTJK exhibits a broad host range, infecting both Mammaliicoccus and Staphylococcus strains, reflecting the close evolutionary relationship between these genera and highlighting its potential relevance in shared environments where horizontal gene exchange occurs.

Assessing phage stability under diverse environmental conditions is critical for future biotechnological applications, as it directly affects storage, transport, and efficacy (Preadeep et al. 2022). Temperature, pH, UV exposure, and chloroform tolerance provide complementary insights into phage robustness under conditions relevant to environmental exposure, therapeutic use, and long-term storage.

In this context, pTJK exhibited a robust stability profile, maintaining activity across moderate temperature and pH ranges, tolerating short UV exposure, and remaining viable after chloroform treatment. These characteristics support its potential for environmental and clinical applications. Future strategies to improve long-term stability may include the use of stabilizing agents such as sugars, proteins, or polyols, as well as lyophilization, cold storage, and encapsulation approaches to protect phage particles under adverse conditions (Jończyk-Matysiak et al. 2019).

Beyond bacterial killing, antibiofilm activity is particularly relevant for Staphylococcus spp., which readily form complex biofilms composed of polysaccharides, proteins, and nucleic acids that protect cells from environmental stress and host defenses and facilitate horizontal gene transfer (Michaelis and Grohmann 2023; Rossi et al. 2024). The ability of pTJK to reduce biofilm biomass even in strains that are not lytically susceptible suggests the involvement of lysis-independent mechanisms. While productive infection and bacterial lysis contribute to biofilm disruption in susceptible hosts (Liu et al. 2022b), the observed effects in non-host strains are likely mediated by phage-encoded enzymes, including endolysins, depolymerases, and nucleases, that can be released into the extracellular environment. These enzymes may degrade structural components of the biofilm matrix, such as polysaccharides, proteins, and extracellular DNA, thereby weakening biofilm integrity independently of phage replication. This dual mode of action broadens the potential applicability of pTJK as an antibiofilm agent.

Although antimicrobial resistance is well established, bacteria can also evolve resistance to phages through diverse defense mechanisms, including receptor modification, restriction–modification systems, CRISPR-Cas immunity, and abortive infection (Labrie et al. 2010; Bondy-Denomy et al. 2016; Bernheim and Sorek 2020). Despite this, phage resistance rarely undermines the long-term potential of phage therapy, as it is often associated with fitness costs and counterbalanced by the vast diversity and rapid evolution of phages, including the acquisition of anti-defense systems that enable evasion of bacterial immunity (Örmälä and Jalasvuori 2013; Hampton et al. 2020).

Combining antimicrobial agents, particularly in phage–antibiotic synergistic approaches, can reduce the emergence of resistance by requiring bacteria to overcome multiple killing mechanisms simultaneously (Segall et al. 2019). In our assays, pTJK showed synergistic interactions with gentamicin or erythromycin, enhancing bacterial killing and impairing biofilm formation even at subinhibitory concentrations. These findings support the potential of phage–antibiotic combinations as effective strategies to improve treatment outcomes and limit resistance spread.

In addition to encoding enzymes with antimicrobial and antibiofilm potential, the genome of phage pTJK supports its safety profile, as no resistance or virulence genes were detected. The absence of known anti-defense systems may limit its ability to overcome host immunity, reinforcing the rationale for high-titer application and synergistic use with other antimicrobials. Nonetheless, the diversity of phage anti-defense mechanisms remains incompletely characterized, and unannotated systems may still be present (Bondy-Denomy et al. 2013).

Although pTJK is one of the few phages reported to infect M. lentus, phylogenetic analyses of the limited group of phages with > 85% genome coverage reveal that closely related viruses have also been isolated from other Staphylococcaceae hosts, consistent with pTJK’s ability to infect multiple species. Nevertheless, the fact that only a single phage from the same yet-to-be-defined species (MSShimriz1) has been sequenced to date underscores how little is currently known about this lineage. This knowledge gap highlights the need for further exploration of Staphylococcaceae phages, whose biotechnological potential to counteract antimicrobial resistance is becoming increasingly evident (Stepanović et al. 2007).

Conclusions

The characterization of pTJK adds to the still limited knowledge of M. lentus phages and reinforces their potential as valuable tools to address antimicrobial resistance in Staphylococcaceae. Its broad host range, antibiofilm properties, and genomic safety profile align with desirable traits for future therapeutic and environmental applications. Future studies will focus on in vivo validation and on exploring synergistic phage–antibiotic combinations as strategies to enhance efficacy and limit the emergence of phage resistance. The scarcity of closely related isolates in current databases highlights the vast unexplored diversity of phages infecting this bacterial family. Continued efforts to discover, characterize, and apply such viruses will be essential to harness their biotechnological potential within a One Health framework.
