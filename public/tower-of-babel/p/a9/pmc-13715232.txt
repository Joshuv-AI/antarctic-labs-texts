Background

Oral and maxillofacial space infections (OMSI) are common conditions in the field of oral medicine and are characterized by acute onset and rapid progression [1]. OMSI can lead to patient death if they are not controlled within a short period. Recent epidemiological data show an annual incidence of OMSI of 9.8 per 100,000 population, with odontogenic infections being the primary cause of OMSI [2]. Approximately 20% of hospitalized patients develop severe complications, including mediastinal infection, sepsis, and airway obstruction. The overall mortality rate ranges from 0.5% to 1.5% [3]. In recent years, expert consensus has provided substantial support for the standardized implementation of relevant diagnoses and treatments [4]. Meanwhile, clinical guidance on the timing of pharmacotherapy has significantly addressed the gap in standardized medication use in clinical practice [5]. However, there is limited guidance on the timing of decision-making during treatment, which hinders physicians from making prompt decisions and may occasionally result in delayed management of the condition.

Surgical intervention (incision and drainage) constitute crucial steps in the management of OMSI [6]. Timely and effective incisions and drainage can significantly improve patient outcomes. Currently, the timing and criteria for surgical intervention in OMSI are primarily guided by clinicians’ assessment of diagnostic markers—creating a risk of delayed decision-making. Such delays in surgical intervention can, in some cases, be life-threatening, presenting a critical challenge that imposes significant demands on timely clinical decision-making [7, 8]. Beyond surgical intervention, the decision to initiate ICU care is equally critical for patients with OMSI [9]. Severe OMSI often lead to poor outcomes or death, typically resulting from airway obstruction or sepsis [1]. Timely assessment of the need for ICU intervention at the time of initial triage and evaluation is therefore essential [10]. Currently, decisions regarding this remain largely experience-based, with no rapid, evidence-based tool available to guide clinical judgment [11].

Prognostic models have become indispensable tools in modern clinical practice [12], providing a data-driven foundation for critical clinical decisions. In an ideal framework, an effective prognostic model exhibits three core characteristics: the ability to deliver real-time risk stratification, the capacity to reliably distinguish distinct clinical trajectories, and compatibility for seamless integration into existing clinical workflows [13]. This demand for robust prognostic tools is particularly urgent in emergency clinical management, where rapid, precise interventions are pivotal to preventing adverse outcomes [14].

While several studies have explored prognostic models for OMSI, most remain in their infancy [15, 16]. Current predictive tools predominantly focus on a single outcome: hospital length of stay [17, 18]. Although hospital length of stay is a relevant metric for healthcare resource utilization, it serves as a downstream, often indirect surrogate for initial disease severity and the timeliness of interventions—limiting its utility for guiding early, actionable clinical decisions.

Our study aims to further assess the clinical utility of prognostic models. Our objectives were to: (1) evaluate the performance of multiple machine learning algorithms (Logistic regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost); (2) assess model performance across three key clinical outcomes (prolonged hospital stay, surgical intervention, and ICU admission); and (3) identify the most effective prognostic models. Ultimately, this work aims to enable early intervention for high-risk patients, reduce complication rates, and improve overall clinical outcomes for patients with OMSI.

Methods

Participant inclusion and data collection

From January 2015 to December 2024, we retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 432 patients admitted to the Department of oral and maxillofacial surgery for the management of maxillofacial infections. The inclusion criteria were as follows: adherence to the OMSI diagnostic criteria (local tissue inflammation characterized by erythema, oedema, hyperthermia and tenderness); compromised functions such as difficulties opening the mouth, swallowing, or breathing; the presence of purulent material confirmed by puncture or computed tomography (CT); and laboratory examinations indicating an underlying infection process. The exclusion criterion for this study was the absence of clinical data, recurrent cases and length of hospital stay less than 24 h (Fig. 1). The following data were extracted from medical records: (1) Basic characteristics: age, gender, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), days before hospitalization, the extent of mouth opening, the space of infected spaces, and the source of infection. (2) Vital sign indicators: body temperature, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and respiration. (3) Complete blood count parameters: white blood cell count, absolute neutrophil count, absolute lymphocyte count, and absolute monocyte count. (4) Serum biochemical parameters: C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin, potassium, sodium, chlorine, calcium, uric acid, blood urea nitrogen, procalcitonin (PCT), fibrinogen, and blood glucose.

Patient Screening Flowchart

We assessed all patients using a 5-category frailty assessment tool, the detailed protocol of which has been described in our previous study [19]. The scores for the five tasks were then summed.

To prevent data leakage and ensure temporal validity, all predictors were restricted to variables available at the time of hospital admission (T0), prior to the clinical decisions under investigation. Specifically, for ICU admission prediction, predictors were limited to admission data collected before ICU triage. For surgical intervention prediction, predictors did not include any intraoperative or postoperative findings. A detailed summary of the timing of feature availability is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Xuchang Central Hospital (Approval No. 2023-10-002), and was conducted in full compliance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was formally waived by the ethics committee, due to the retrospective design of the study, the full anonymization of all patient data, and the absence of any risk to patient privacy, rights, or clinical care.

This method was applied to all continuous clinical indicators, including demographic parameters (age, height, weight, BMI), laboratory biomarkers (white blood cell count, neutrophils, lymphocytes, CRP, PCT, albumin, electrolytes, etc.), vital signs (body temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiration), and clinical assessment scores (frailty scores, mouth opening degree). Categorical variables (gender, source of infection, space, ICU treatment, incision, etc.) were not included in outlier detection. The detailed list of specific outliers is presented in Table S2.

Outlier verification protocol

All identified outliers were rigorously verified against the original electronic medical records to eliminate data entry errors, in accordance with best practices for clinical data quality control. All outlier values were extracted and matched to corresponding patient identifiers.

Two independent researchers cross-checked the raw data against original medical records, laboratory reports, and vital sign recordings. All outliers were confirmed to be clinically valid abnormal values reflecting true pathophysiological status, with no data entry or transcription errors identified. Since all outliers represented genuine clinical abnormalities, no outlier exclusion or modification was performed.

Model development

This study adhered to the TRIPOD-AI guidelines to ensure methodological transparency and completeness of reporting. The TRIPOD-AI checklist is provided in Supplementary Appendix S1.

Model development was conducted for three binary clinical outcomes: prolonged hospital stay (> 10 days) [15, 17], ICU admission, and surgical intervention. After importing and standardizing the raw data, continuous and categorical predictors were defined, and cases with missing values were excluded. A fixed random seed was used to split the data into training (70%) and test (30%) sets. Three models were developed: logistic regression (LR) using bidirectional selection based on Akaike’s Information Criterion; random forest (RF) with 10-fold cross-validation and tuning of the mtry parameter; and XGBoost with 10-fold cross-validation and early stopping to determine the optimal number of boosting rounds. Model performance was evaluated on the test set using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, with 95% confidence intervals calculated via bootstrap and the Wilson method. Calibration was assessed using the Brier score and Hosmer‑Lemeshow test, and calibration curves were generated. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the XGBoost and Random Forest models across a range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities from 0.1 to 0.5, with the treat-all and treat-none strategies set as reference controls. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1), with results and visualizations exported for reproducibility. (Fig. 2)

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables (age, height, weight, BMI, CRP, white blood cell count, absolute neutrophil count, absolute lymphocyte count, absolute monocyte count, albumin, potassium, sodium, chlorine, calcium, uric acid, blood urea nitrogen, PCT, fibrinogen, blood glucose, body temperature, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and respiration, days before hospitalization) are reported as the means and standard deviations. Categorical variables (mouth opening degree, gender, source of infection, space of infection, and frailty scores) are reported as percentages and frequencies. For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test was first performed to assess normality. Data that did not follow a normal distribution were analysed using the Mann-Whitney U test. For normally distributed data, homogeneity of variance was further examined via the Welch test. Unpaired t-test was applied for data with homogeneous variance, while Welch’s t-test was used for data with heterogeneous variance, whereas Fisher’s exact test was applied for categorical variables. P values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Three risk factors associated with clinical outcomes were investigated, including ICU admission, surgical intervention, and prolonged hospital stay (> 10 days). Univariate logistic regression analyses were first performed to assess the association between each candidate predictor and the respective clinical outcome. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in these univariate analyses or those deemed clinically relevant were further included in a multivariate logistic regression model to adjust for potential confounding factors.

We employed random forest and XGBoost models, utilizing recursive feature elimination and feature importance selection to identify the most relevant features for predicting different clinical outcomes. The model was trained on 70% of the dataset (n = 302) and validated on an independent test set (n = 130). The performance of the different model was evaluated using the following metrics: AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Calibration performance was assessed using the Brier score and Hosmer–Lemeshow (H–L) test. The statistical data were visualized through the software programs Prism GraphPad (version 8.0, GraphPad Software, Inc., USA), R (version 4.5.1) and SPSS V.22 (IBM).

Results

From January 2015 to December 2024, a total of 432 patients underwent inpatient treatment. Among these patients, a total of 236 patients had a hospitalization duration exceeding 10 days. 88 patients required admission to the ICU for intensive monitoring and treatment. Additionally, 271 patients underwent surgical intervention.

Table 1 summarizes the comparisons between two groups of patients stratified by length of hospital stay: >10 days vs. ≤10 days. No significant differences were observed in baseline demographic characteristics between the two groups, including gender, frailty score, body weight, and BMI. The mean age was slightly higher in the long group, but this difference did not reach statistical significance. Regarding infection-related characteristics, the two groups differed significantly in infection source distribution, with glandular infection being more prevalent in the short group. Infection severity and degree of trismus also exhibited significant intergroup differences: the long group had notably higher proportions of moderate-to-severe infection and severe trismus. Laboratory findings revealed that the long group exhibited a more prominent inflammatory response. Compared with the short group, this group had significantly higher levels of white blood cell count, absolute neutrophil count, CRP, and procalcitonin. Additionally, the long group had poorer nutritional status, as evidenced by a significantly lower serum albumin level. In terms of vital signs, the long group had a longer duration of pre-admission symptoms and a higher mean body temperature at admission.

Comparison by ICU treatment

In terms of baseline characteristics, no significant differences were noted in gender, age, height, or frailty score between the two groups. However, patients in the ICU group had significantly higher body weight and BMI compared with the non-ICU group. Extremely significant differences were observed between the two groups in infection severity and degree of trismus. In the ICU group, the proportion of patients with severe infection was substantially higher than that in the non-ICU group. Correspondingly, patients in the ICU group presented with more severe trismus: 60% of ICU patients had severe trismus, whereas this figure was only 14% in the non-ICU group. Laboratory findings indicated that the ICU group exhibited a more robust systemic inflammatory response and poorer nutritional status. Compared with the non-ICU group, the ICU group had significantly elevated levels of white blood cell count, absolute neutrophil count, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin, while serum albumin level was significantly lower. Additionally, the ICU group had significantly higher levels of fibrinogen, blood glucose, and blood urea nitrogen, whereas serum sodium, serum chloride, and serum calcium levels were significantly lower. Regarding vital signs, patients in the ICU group had a higher body temperature, a faster heart rate, and an increased respiratory rate.

Table 3 summarizes the comparisons between patients who underwent surgical intervention and those who did not, with significant differences observed between the two groups in infection characteristics, inflammatory markers, nutritional status, and other relevant aspects. There was a significant difference in the distribution of infection sources between the two groups, with a higher proportion of odontogenic infections in the surgery group. Meanwhile, the infection severity in the surgery group was significantly greater: the proportion of severe infections in this group was notably higher than that in the non-surgery group. A significant difference was also noted in the degree of trismus: the surgery group had a higher proportion of patients with severe trismus, whereas the non-surgery group had a higher proportion of patients with normal mouth opening. Laboratory tests indicated that patients who underwent surgical intervention exhibited a more intense systemic inflammatory response. Compared with the non-surgery group, the surgery group had significantly higher levels of white blood cell count, absolute neutrophil count, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin. Additionally, the surgery group had poorer nutritional status, as reflected by a significantly lower serum albumin level and a lower serum calcium level. In terms of vital signs, patients in the surgery group had a slightly higher body temperature and a significantly longer duration of pre-admission symptoms. Notably, there were no significant differences between the two groups in gender, age, BMI, frailty score, or most electrolyte indices.

Comparison by surgical intervention

Table 4 summarizes the predictive performance of three models for three clinical outcomes. First, regarding the prediction of hospital stay > 10 days: the Logistic regression model demonstrated the optimal predictive performance., the highest accuracyand sensitivity, while the XGBoost model had the highest specificity. Second, for the prediction of ICU admission: the XGboost and Random forest models exhibited excellent predictive performance. The XGBoost model performed the best. Although the Random Forest model also had a high sensitivity, its specificity was relatively lower. Notably, the logistic regression model for ICU admission showed significant miscalibration, indicating that its predicted probabilities deviated substantially from the actual observed event rates. This miscalibration limits its clinical utility for this specific outcome. Third, in terms of predicting surgical treatment: the Logistic regression model showed the best overall performance and the highest sensitivity, though its specificity was relatively lower. The Random Forest and XGBoost models had higher specificities. The final feature set selected for the model is summarized in Table S4. The results of the sensitivity analysis are detailed in Table S5.

In both the random forest and XGBoost models, infection severity, degree of trismus, and inflammatory markers were the leading predictors for all three outcomes. For ICU admission prediction, CRP and PCT were the most influential features. DCA analysis demonstrated that for the clinical endpoint of ICU admission, both models maintained a positive net benefit across the entire range of tested thresholds, significantly outperforming the two extreme reference strategies at all evaluated time points.

For prolonged hospital stay (> 10 days), all models exhibited acceptable calibration, with the XGBoost model achieving the lowest Brier score and a non-significant H–L test. For ICU admission, XGBoost demonstrated superior calibration with the lowest Brier score and an excellent H–L P value, whereas the logistic model showed significant miscalibration. For surgical treatment, the logistic model revealed significant miscalibration, while both random forest and XGBoost models showed favorable calibration (Table S6).

A small-sample preliminary external validation was performed in this study. The results preliminarily suggested that the Random Forest model had certain generalization performance. The XGBoost model showed acceptable performance for the outcomes of length of hospital stay > 10 days and ICU admission. However, due to the extremely limited sample size, these results cannot provide robust evidence for the generalizability of the models (Table S7).

Discussion

For patients with OMSI, the length of hospital stay serves as a robust metric for assessing both disease severity and the efficacy of therapeutic interventions. In the present study, prolonged hospital stay (defined as > 10 days) was significantly associated with three critical factors: a more severe degree of local infection, heightened systemic inflammatory responses, and compromised nutritional status.

Our analysis revealed that among patients with long length of hospital stay, the proportion diagnosed with severe space infection was significantly higher, with a concurrent marked increase in the proportion of patients with severe trismus. Notably, Longchamp G and colleagues validated using a multivariate regression model that multi-space infection serves as a robust predictor of prolonged stay—this finding aligns with our observations [16]. However, our study employed classification metrics to define cumulative infected spaces, a methodological choice that enhances model construction and stability.

In contrast, Wang J’s study evaluated cumulative space count using a quantitative approach but reported no significant correlation between this metric and prolonged hospital stay [17]. By comparison, Aditya NK [20]and co-workers identified cumulative infected space count as a significant predictive factor. This discrepancy suggests that the quantitative indicator of cumulative space count may lack stability, whereas categorical grouping of cumulative infected spaces offers greater analytical utility. Additionally, Aditya NK’s team observed that trismus acts as an effective predictor, though their classification system for trismus severity lacked granularity [20].

Notably, patients in the long length of hospital stay group exhibited significantly elevated levels of white blood cell count, neutrophil count, CRP, and PCT. This observation suggests that prolonged hospital stay is closely associated with an excessive or dysregulated systemic inflammatory response to infection—findings corroborated by studies conducted by Longchamp G [16], Aditya NK [20], and Wang Y [3], among others. Furthermore, serum albumin levels were significantly lower in the prolonged hospitalization group: a result that not only indicates nutritional depletion secondary to the acute inflammatory response, but also likely reflects inadequate baseline nutritional reserves. Both of these factors—nutritional depletion and insufficient baseline reserves—can directly impair immune function and tissue repair capacity, as supported by Huang Y’s research [21].

Additionally, patients with prolonged hospitalization demonstrated lower serum potassium and calcium levels. These perturbations may reflect infection-induced stress-related metabolic disturbances, which could further compromise the patients’ physiological reserves and impede their recovery potential. Patients in the prolonged hospitalization group exhibited a longer duration of pre-admission symptoms and a higher body temperature at the time of admission. This observation suggests that delayed medical consultation may drive infection progression. This progression worsens initial disease severity, complicates subsequent treatment, and ultimately leads to prolonged hospital stays. Consistent with Wang J’s work emphasizing the marked influence of pre-hospitalization interventions on patient prognosis, our findings highlight the critical need to prioritize pre-hospitalization management [17]. This may require more research to further evaluate.

Determining the need for ICU admission represents a critical clinical decision, with direct implications for patient care experience and healthcare resource utilization. In the present study, patients admitted to the ICU had a significantly higher proportion of severe space infections compared with non-ICU patients, accompanied by more severe trismus. This observation emphasizes that the specific anatomical location of the infection elevates the risk of life-threatening complications. These complications include airway compression and systemic sepsis. This elevated risk therefore necessitates more advanced life support interventions and continuous physiological monitoring. Consistent with our observations, Velhonoja J and colleagues demonstrated that pharyngeal space involvement is a key determinant of ICU admission in such patients [18]. James J further emphasized that treatment response also influences the decision to initiate ICU care, highlighting the critical role of early, proactive surgical drainage and airway assessment in managing this patient population [22].

Furthermore, patients admitted to the ICU exhibited a markedly exaggerated systemic inflammatory response. Notably, we observed orders-of-magnitude elevations in white blood cell count, neutrophil count, CRP, and most markedly PCT. These findings are strongly suggestive of severe systemic bacterial infection or sepsis, providing a clear clinical rationale for the implementation of advanced life support interventions. Wang B’s [23] research supports this, confirming that PCT serves as a robust marker of OMSI severity and that elevated PCT levels signify the necessity of ICU admission. Additionally, a prospective study by Velur A [24] validated the utility of PCT as a biomarker for monitoring treatment response in OMSI.

Patients in the ICU also presented with pronounced disturbances in internal homeostasis and metabolic stress. The higher prevalence of hypoalbuminemia, hyponatremia, hypocalcemia, and hyperglycemia in this group is indicative of potential nutritional depletion, capillary leak syndrome, and metabolic dysregulation. Sun Y [25] identified a significant reduction in serum albumin as an independent predictor of ICU admission, a conclusion further corroborated by Huang Y’s work [21]. These findings align with the results of our study.

Alhudaithi and colleagues demonstrated that surgical intervention constitute a key component of OMSI management [26], with timely intervention effectively improving patient prognosis. Our findings support this conclusion and further identify that infection involvement of key anatomical locations serves as a more precise predictor of surgical need.

Notably, no significant differences were observed in key baseline characteristics—including age and frailty scores—between patients who underwent surgical intervention and those who did not receive this intervention. This observation demonstrates that surgical decision-making for OMSI is not primarily determined by patient age or overall clinical status. Instead, it depends far more on the disease-specific features of the infection, most notably its local invasiveness and the magnitude of the induced systemic inflammatory response. Even in patients with relative contraindications, surgical intervention remains clinically necessary [1]. In our constructed models, frailty score were excluded from the final feature set. This suggests that although frailty is widely recognized as a predictor of adverse outcomes in surgical and critically ill populations [27, 28], its predictive value may be attenuated in the context of acute OMSI. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more diverse frailty score are needed to clarify the interaction between baseline functional status and acute infection severity.

Developing and validating predictive models to support rapid clinical decision-making and prognostic assessment for patients with OMSI holds substantial clinical value [29]. Wang J and colleagues developed a Random Forest model to predict prolonged hospital stay, achieving an AUC of 0.712 in the validation set [17]. This performance is comparable to that of our model; however, our model incorporates more clinically relevant predictors and is based on a larger sample size, resulting in greater stability in performance. Furthermore, we performed a parallel comparative analysis using two modelling approaches: logistic regression and XGBoost. Both models achieved comparable performance in predicting prolonged hospital stay, with logistic regression presenting a slight advantage in overall predictive performance. This observation suggests that the factors influencing hospital stay duration may have relatively linear relationships, which are readily captured by traditional parametric models [30].

For the prediction of ICU admission, we constructed models using the same three algorithms, and all exhibited excellent discriminative ability. But the logistic regression model had significant miscalibration. Therefore, we strongly recommend against the standalone clinical use of the logistic regression model for predicting ICU admission risk. The XGBoost model performed optimally, achieving high accuracy and sensitivity when considering overall performance. This robust model performance delivers meaningful clinical utility. Specifically, it allows for highly accurate identification of patients at risk of critical illness and in need of advanced life support. This precision directly facilitates rapid triage and optimal allocation of scarce intensive care resources, both at emergency presentation and in the early stages of hospitalization. Notably, the Random Forest and XGBoost models demonstrated extremely high sensitivity, meaning they could identify nearly all patients who ultimately required ICU care. This attribute is critical for a screening tool, as its primary goal is to “rule out” low-risk patients while ensuring no high-risk patients are missed [31].

A clinically relevant performance trade-off was identified in the prediction of surgical intervention. The logistic regression model was characterized by high sensitivity and low specificity. Specifically, it successfully identified most patients who required surgical treatment, but incorrectly classified a large proportion of non-surgical patients as needing intervention [32]. In contrast, the Random Forest and XGBoost models exhibited the opposite profile—high specificity but low sensitivity. While these models excelled at identifying patients who did not require surgery, this came at the cost of potentially missing nearly half of patients who actually needed surgical treatment [33]. This finding underscores the importance of aligning model selection with clinical priorities: whether to prioritize capturing all patients in need of surgery, or to minimize unnecessary surgical evaluations [34].

It is important to emphasize that these predictive models are designed to support, rather than replace, clinical judgment. Their role is to augment clinicians’ decision-making by providing data-driven risk estimates, particularly in time-critical and high-risk clinical scenarios. We herein propose potential strategies for integrating such models into the routine clinical care of patients with OMSI [35]. When embedded into hospital internal information systems, the ICU admission prediction model can serve as an automated screening tool at initial patient presentation. The model delivers direct, actionable clinical utility for patients identified as high risk for ICU admission. Specifically, it can be configured to automatically trigger a formal consultation referral to the critical care team. This automated workflow facilitates early specialist involvement, allowing for proactive assessment and intervention for impending airway compromise or incipient sepsis, before overt clinical deterioration occurs. In parallel, the surgical intervention prediction model can help ensure timely treatment for patients requiring surgical drainage, which carries particular value in emergency settings. When incorporated into daily clinical operating systems, these models can deliver real-time alerts to medical personnel regarding critical findings that require close attention, thereby promoting more standardized and consistent patient care.

However, this study has inherent limitations. Although we adjusted for several confounding variables, unmeasured confounders (e.g., chronic kidney disease status) may still have influenced the results. The single-center sample size limits both statistical power and generalizability of the findings. First, clinical practice patterns for OMSI vary substantially across institutions and regions. There are marked differences in ICU admission criteria, the timing of surgical surgical intervention, antimicrobial stewardship protocols, and standards of perioperative supportive care between tertiary and primary hospitals, as well as across different geographical regions. These discrepancies may lead to performance degradation of our model when applied to external cohorts. Second, our study population is predominantly composed of patients with odontogenic infection as the primary aetiology, which may not be fully representative of populations with distinct aetiological spectra or comorbidity profiles. Most importantly, the lack of prospective multi-centre external validation represents the key limitation of this study. While our model exhibits excellent performance in the internal held-out test set, internal validation cannot fully confirm its stability and generalisability in real-world multi-centre clinical settings. Although we performed a preliminary small-sample external validation, the sample size is far from sufficient to evaluate the stability and generalizability of the models in real-world clinical settings. The performance of the models may degrade significantly when applied to populations with different clinical practice patterns or etiological spectra. Future work should validate these models in large-scale prospective multi-centre cohorts to improve their external validity.

Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully developed, validated, and compared predictive models for key clinical outcomes in patients with OMSI. These artificial intelligence driven predictive tools are based on real world clinical data. This model for predicting the risk of ICU treatment lays a foundation for personalized and proactive clinical management of patients with OMSI in the future. They further hold promise for ultimately optimizing the diagnostic and therapeutic workflow and improving outcomes for patients with this common yet potentially life-threatening condition.
