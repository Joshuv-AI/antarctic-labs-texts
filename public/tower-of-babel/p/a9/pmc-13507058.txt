Physics-aware generative fusion via Complex-Flow Mamba enables robust 4D radar-vision perception
Authors: Xuguang Yang, Junjun Guo, Lyu Zhe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
4D imaging millimeter-wave radar is quickly redrawing the map for autonomous perception. However, a significant limitation persists: conventional deep learning models often fall into a 'point cloud fallacy.' They treat radar echoes as simple discrete points, which washes out the complex-domain phase coherence. This renders systems highly vulnerable to interrupted sampling repeater jamming (ISRJ) and degrades performance on sparse targets at long ranges. We propose Complex-Flow Mamba, a framework that dives below the feature level to work directly at the signal-physics layer. We started by building a continuous Implicit 4D Complex Field. By using polar decoupling and Von Mises distribution modeling, we brought back the phase gradients that traditional discretization just throws away. To mitigate complex ISRJ interference, we developed a Complex Latent Diffusion Module (CLDM). Unlike standard filters, this generative prior performs microscopic phase correction to decouple coherent artifacts at their source. On the architecture side, we used Mamba-2’s Structured State Space Duality (SSD) theory to create a bi-directional state-modulation backbone with O(N) linear complexity. Here, visual semantics act as a dynamic filter for radar, while radar dynamics serve as a physical gatekeeper for the vision stream, creating a deep entanglement between heterogeneous flows. We also added a generative detection head based on Consistency Models and a physics-consistent self-supervised loss to fix the “hollowing” effect in far-field detection. Tests on the View-of-Delft (VoD) and K-Radar datasets show that Complex-Flow Mamba hits SOTA levels in mean Average Precision (mAP) and NuScenes Detection Score (NDS). It also maintains a 0.88 jamming suppression gain under extreme electronic pressure.

Introduction

Robust perception is the backbone for autonomous driving as it tries to cross the "long-tail" gap1. As 77 GHz millimeter-wave radar moves from traditional 3D sensing to high-resolution 4D imaging, we’ve gained the ability to see through rain or fog while measuring distance, azimuth, elevation, and instantaneous Doppler. This hardware dividend means radar is no longer merely an auxiliary sensor to LiDAR; it has become a core sensor capable of independent 3D topological reconstruction. Despite these advancements, a notable deficiency persists in the current literature. Even though 4D imaging radar provides incredibly rich raw data, most deep learning models are still stuck in a "point cloud fallacy2."

This bias actually stems from how we’ve historically borrowed LiDAR processing techniques. Foundations like PointNet3, VoxelNet4, and the industry-standard PointPillars5 were built to map unstructured point sets into structured feature spaces. Later, SECOND6 speed things up with sparse convolutions, and works like RCFusion7 tried to bring this logic into the 4D radar domain. However, these methods process radar echoes primarily as unstructured geometric coordinates8. We will find that this habit discards the most vital physical clue: complex-domain phase coherence9. In adversarial conditions, such as during Interrupted Sampling Repeater Jamming (ISRJ)10, magnitude information alone is almost useless for distinguishing a real echo from a coherent fake. Earlier attempts such as M2-Fusion11 addressed point cloud divergence via Gaussian pre-processing; nevertheless, operating strictly within the real-number domain fundamentally limits the ability to capture the signal’s core physical properties.

Furthermore, multimodal fusion strategies frequently encounter a critical trade-off between computational efficiency and perception accuracy. Unified BEV representations, from LSS12 to the deformable attention in BEVFormer13, have become the standard way to merge data. BEVFusion14 even optimized pooling to handle multi-modal aggregation efficiently. But when we face the massive token sequences from 4D imaging radar, Transformer-based SOTA models—like TransFusion15 and DeepInteraction16—run into the quadratic O(N2) complexity wall. Even in newer setups like ZFusion17 and UniBEVFusion18, developers often downsample the radar data just to keep the system running in real-time. Phase info is crucial, yet it gets tossed out as noise during these pooling and sampling steps. On top of that, the “geometric hollowing” effect of 4D radar makes traditional center-point regression heads fail at long ranges. Conventional deterministic neural networks often exhibit suboptimal performance under such physical uncertainty19.

To address these hurdles, we are introducing Complex-Flow Mamba, as shown in Fig. 1. We suggest moving away from fragmented point sets and instead reconstructing 4D radar observations as a continuous, physics-aware implicit complex field. To strip away coherent interference at the source, we moved past discriminative denoising and built a complex latent diffusion model (CLDM). This uses a generative prior to find the coherent signal within the latent space. For the architecture, we took inspiration from the linear scaling of PointMamba20 and used the Structured State Space Duality (SSD) theory of Mamba-221 to build a bi-directional continuous-flow backbone. Such a configuration lets visual semantics act as a "step-size regulator" for radar, while radar dynamics serve as a physical gatekeeper for the vision stream. Related works

Bird’s-eye view (BEV) and occupancy space modeling

Aligning data from heterogeneous sensors within a single, unified space is a classic hurdle in perception research. Early vision-only BEV methods mostly relied on geometric transforms24 or depth guesses25. For example, LSS12 introduced a way to predict depth distributions to lift 2D features into 3D, while BEVDet26 showed that extracting features directly in the BEV view makes 3D detection much more effective. To avoid the heavy math of explicit depth, frameworks like OFT27 and BEVFormer13 used 3D spatial grids for view mapping, employing deformable cross-attention to pull in semantic data.

A noticeable trend recently is the move from flat BEV maps to volumetric 3D Occupancy modeling. Bounding boxes are often too simple to describe complex shapes, but occupancy fields offer a far more detailed look at the world’s geometry. Even so, most current occupancy models still have a blind spot when it comes to radar. As UniBEVFusion18 pointed out, many systems treat 4D radar as just a "sparse LiDAR with height." This simplification throws away the Doppler info and makes the system fragile when cameras fail in heavy rain or at night.

4D imaging radar

By adding a vertical dimension, 4D imaging radar has effectively closed the gap with LiDAR. In the world of point cloud processing, we usually see two camps: point-based28 and voxel-based29. Methods like PointNet13 and Point-RCNN30 work on raw points to keep every bit of geometry, though they eat up a lot of power. On the other hand, VoxelNet4 and PointPillars5 use spatial quantization to stay fast, and SECOND6’s sparse convolutions have become the industry standard for 4D data. RCFusion7 even used a “Radar PillarNet” to prove that 4D data can work well in a unified BEV setup.

One fact that often gets ignored, however, is that any discretization—be it voxels or pillars—is essentially a lossy way to sample the original signal. 4D radar provides dense points, but it also brings in multi-path clutter and active interference. Some researchers tried Gaussian pre-processing in M2-Fusion11 or “Split” modules in ZFusion17 to balance speed and accuracy, but these stay strictly in the real-number domain. To really solve things like ISRJ interference, we have to look at the complex-valued nature of the signal. Keeping phase consistency is still one of the toughest challenges in deep learning for radar.

Multi-modal fusion strategies

The way different sensors talk to each other defines how tough a system is. Early "decision-level" fusion was basically just a late-stage handshake, which missed out on the strengths of each sensor. Later, "data-level" fusion like PointPainting31 tried to “decorate” 3D points with 2D semantic scores. This sounded good on paper, but the sparsity of radar meant that most of the rich visual data just went to waste.

To fix this, feature-level interaction has taken center stage. DeepInteraction16 used a two-way feedback loop to close the gap between modalities, while 4D-Net32 used radar features to tell the camera where to look. We are now seeing fusion move from “static stacking” to "dynamic entanglement." CVFusion33, for instance, uses a cross-view strategy to find more targets. But there is a catch: most of these rely on Transformers. When we are dealing with high-res 4D radar tokens, the O(N2) complexity of standard attention is just too slow. It ends up lagging the system and washing out the fine motion details we need.

State space models (SSM) and generative perception

There is a constant tug-of-war between speed and quality. State space models, especially Mamba34, are starting to change how we think about 3D vision. PointMamba20 proved that we can process irregular points with linear scaling, offering a way out of the Transformer bottleneck. At the same time, generative perception is having a moment. DiffusionDet35 showed that treating detection as a denoising task helps the model generalize better, and Consistency Models36 have finally made this approach fast enough for real-time use.

Our work sits right at the intersection of these shifts. Instead of treating radar and images as separate boxes to be checked, we want a system that can “heal” its own physical data. By using a complex-diffusion prior to find the original signal and Mamba-221 to entangle the two streams, we aren’t just making a better BEV fuser.

Methodology

Problem formulation

In developing reliable 3D perception systems, a frequent misconception in current deep learning research is the reliance on pre-processed, discrete point clouds for feature extraction. While computationally convenient, this habit treats radar as little more than a low-resolution LiDAR24,25. Doing so ignores the most vital “physics dividends” radar offers: complex-valued phase information and fine-grained Doppler dynamics. In contested environments where active coherent interference is present, relying on signal magnitude alone makes it nearly impossible to distinguish a real echo from a deceptive jammer.

We feel that high-performance 4D radar perception shouldn’t stay on the surface of geometry. Instead, it must start with the underlying physical mechanisms of the signal. Rather than building our architecture on fragmented point sets, we model the raw 4D tensor and reconstruct it as a continuous, physics-aware implicit field.

4D radar signal model with non-linear coherent interference

Our study uses a 4D imaging radar system as the primary experimental platform. This system obtains high-precision time–frequency data by transmitting Linear Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave (LFMCW)37 signals. In which, the rect(.) function constrains the pulse envelope in the time domain, while Tp represents the pulse width, and fc & μ denote the carrier frequency and modulation slope.

In actual autonomous driving, the received signal is often heavily distorted by ISRJ. A jammer generates deceptive coherent targets by non-uniformly slicing and retransmitting the intercepted radar pulses. We model this interference as a non-linear modulation between a sampling operator p(t) and the original waveform. It is important to note the practical physics underlying the sampling operator p(t). Mathematically, p(t) is modeled using an ideal rectangular function to represent the logical envelope of the ISRJ chopping mechanism. However, in practical Digital Radio Frequency Memory (DRFM) hardware, generating a perfect rectangular pulse is physically unfeasible due to finite switching speeds, filter group delays, and amplifier bandwidth limitations. Real-world jammers produce non-ideal pulses (e.g., trapezoidal edges with Gibbs ringing). These hardware imperfections inevitably induce transient phase distortions at the slicing boundaries. Rather than invalidating our model, these practical non-idealities actually reinforce our core motivation: the unnatural phase transients caused by sluggish real-world hardware switches make the jamming signal even less coherent. By modeling the ideal rectangular chop, we are essentially tackling the worst-case theoretical boundary of interference. If our system can purify this perfect mathematical discontinuity, it is inherently equipped to handle the messier, more distorted phase jumps produced by practical DRFM hardware.

We will find that the raw time-domain signal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{Y}}(t_{r} ,t_{a} )$$\end{document} cannot be directly mapped to perception semantics. It must pass through a Digital Signal Processing (DSP) pipeline to establish a mapping with 4D physical space. We first perform pulse compression (Range FFT) along the fast-time tr to resolve the range dimension R. Next, a Doppler FFT is executed along the slow-time ta to capture the radial motion dimension V. Finally, we use Digital Beamforming (DBF)38 based on the antenna array manifold to resolve the elevation H and azimuth W. This transforms the signal into a 4D complex tensor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{Y}\ominus } \in {\mathbb{C}}^{H \times W \times R \times V}$$\end{document}. This linear transformation preserves the complex coherence and maps the mixed signal into an orthogonal physical feature space.

Consider the received signal as a collection of physical variants of the original waveform. For a true target, the phase evolution is dictated by continuous displacement, resulting in a stable phase gradient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla \phi_{true}$$\end{document}. Even though the interference might look identical to a target in the magnitude spectrum, its phase spectrum inevitably introduces unnatural jumps, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi_{jam}$$\end{document}. This microscopic phase opposition—smooth gradients versus shattered jumps—is exactly why we advocate for complex-valued implicit fields. We want to find the coherent essence of the signal hidden within the chopped sequences.

Implicit 4D complex field representation

The shift from discrete voxels to a continuous Implicit 4D Complex Field is motivated by the inherent limitations of discretization in radar perception. Discrete representations (e.g., voxels or pillars) suffer from quantization errors and energy aliasing, which degrade the precision of phase-dependent Doppler measurements. In contrast, our implicit representation treats radar observations as a continuous function, allowing for high-precision coordinate transformation between spherical radar geometry and Cartesian BEV space. Furthermore, this implicit formulation establishes a fully differentiable physical manifold, which is essential for applying our physics-informed constraints (Doppler-geometric consistency) and enabling the generative diffusion head to perform precise topological refinement of sparse far-field targets.

Generative complex diffusion prior

Latent space compression and physics-aware encoding

The implicit field feature frad obtained from Eq. (5) is treated as the input to the hierarchical complex residual autoencoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{E}}$$\end{document}, which compresses frad into the latent variable z0 for the subsequent diffusion process.

We need to shrink the massive 4D complex tensor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{Y}} \in {\mathbb{C}}^{H \times W \times R \times V}$$\end{document} into a tight latent space without losing the physics. To do this, we built a hierarchical complex residual autoencoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{E}}$$\end{document} using four sets of Decoupled Complex Blocks (DCB).

Applying single-stage convolutions directly to a 4D tensor typically exacerbates the 'curse of dimensionality.' To get around this, we use a spatio-Doppler decoupled mapping. Bi-directional Mamba-2 fusion backbone

For the past few years, Transformer-based Cross-Attention has dominated multi-modal fusion. But as we shift toward 4D imaging radar, this approach hits a serious scalability wall. The 4D tensors (H,W,R,V) generate a massive volume of sampling tokens when queried through the implicit field. The O(N2) complexity of standard attention not only triggers memory overflows but also struggles to maintain motion consistency across long sequences. We address this by turning to the Mamba-221 architecture. By designing a Bi-Directional Continuous-Flow Fusion Backbone, we pull the complexity down to a linear O(N).

Probabilistic occupancy detection head

The output of our bi-directional Mamba-2 is a hybrid feature flow where physical certainty and semantic clarity are deeply intertwined. Translating these dynamically rich tokens into precise 3D bounding boxes presents a significant computational challenge. Traditional heads, for instance CenterPoint40 or standard anchor-based regressors, usually bank on the assumption that feature responses cluster at a target’s geometric center. 4D radar perception often breaks this rule due to multi-path effects and "glint." Radar energy often strays from the physical center, sometimes creating a "hollowed-out" distribution within the target itself.

This inherent randomness means that deterministic regression, trying to nail down a single set of coordinates in one go, often leads to jittery results. To address this, we diverge from conventional deterministic regression methodologies. Instead, we treat the detection task as a probabilistic generation process, evolving from a noise distribution into a stable geometric structure. This is what we call our Diffusion-Refined Occupancy Head.

Rather than guessing coordinates, we model a target’s 3D state as a probability field. Physics-informed self-supervised learning

Developing high-performance fusion models is frequently constrained when relying exclusively on manually annotated 3D bounding boxes. In the real world, "long-tail" scenarios are everywhere. Labels are expensive, and they rarely cover the messy physical degradation we see in extreme weather. To push the boundaries of Complex-Flow Mamba, we came up with a self-supervised strategy. The idea here is to let the network exploit the “information asymmetry” between radar and vision, using the laws of physics to correct itself without needing a human in the loop.

Since radar waves can “see through” noise or occlusions that blind a camera, we can use that penetration to fill in missing visual gaps. Doppler-geometric consistency constraints

We didn’t stop at semantics; we also brought in hard kinematic rules. There’s a strict cosine projection between the radial velocity vr measured by radar and the actual 3D motion vector v of an object. This isn’t a learned statistic—it’s an objective physical fact that doesn’t need a single label.

For every object the head predicts, we take the mean velocity vector μv from Eq. (23) and project it onto the radar’s line-of-sight. We then define the Doppler Consistency Loss:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{dop} = \sum\limits_{i} {1_{fg}^{(i)} \cdot \left\| {\left( {{{\boldsymbol{\upmu}}}_{v}^{(i)} \cdot \frac{{{\mathbf{q}}_{i} }}{{\left\| {{\mathbf{q}}_{i} } \right\|}}} \right) - \overline{v}_{radar} ({\mathbf{q}}_{i} )} \right\|}^{2}$$\end{document}

In this equation,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{q}}_{i} /\left\| {{\mathbf{q}}_{i} } \right\|$$\end{document} is just the unit direction from the radar to the object, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{v}_{radar}$$\end{document} is the local Doppler baseline we pull from the implicit field. One detail that’s easy to miss is the ego-motion compensation. We pre-process the radar data to remove the car’s own movement, making sure the constraints work in a proper inertial frame. We also use an indicator function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1_{fg}^{(i)}$$\end{document} to act as a gate. It keeps the background clutter from introducing random noise into our velocity gradients.

It is noteworthy that while our Doppler-geometric consistency constraint (Eq. 25) significantly improves velocity precision, it inherently relies on non-zero radial velocity. In tangential motion scenarios where Doppler responses are minimal, the system’s reliance on Doppler-based supervision is adaptively gated by the radar hidden state. In such edge cases, the detection head pivots to rely on the non-Doppler features (reflectivity and spatial occupancy) preserved within the Implicit 4D Complex Field, complemented by the vision-based semantic occupancy features. The heteroscedastic uncertainty branch (Eq. 23) further ensures that velocity estimations with low Doppler SNR are treated with high uncertainty, preventing physical bias in the perception output.

Joint optimization and training objective

To tie all these physical threads together, we use an end-to-end joint loss function to train the whole stack:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{total} = \lambda_{1} ({\mathcal{L}}_{cls} + {\mathcal{L}}_{reg} ) + \lambda_{2} {\mathcal{L}}_{score} + \lambda_{3} {\mathcal{L}}_{CD} + \gamma_{1} {\mathcal{L}}_{rec} + \gamma_{2} {\mathcal{L}}_{dop}$$\end{document}

We tuned the weights λ and γ to balance the primary detection task with signal cleanup and self-supervision.

The optimization objective is designed to strike a balance between physical signal recovery and semantic detection. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{score}$$\end{document} (Eq. 11) acts as a generative prior that forces the latent space to conform to the coherent phase manifold of real targets, effectively acting as an intelligent non-linear filter. The Doppler Consistency Loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{dop}$$\end{document} (Eq. 25) serves as a kinematic regulator; it constrains the predicted velocity vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu_{v}$$\end{document} within the physical subspace defined by the radar’s line-of-sight velocity, preventing the model from converging to kinematically implausible solutions that frequently arise in multi-path interference scenarios.

Computational complexity and efficiency analysis

Backbone

Most radar-vision fusion setups, especially those relying on Transformers, eventually get trapped in the quadratic maze of O(L2) complexity. Processing the massive token sequences generated by 4D imaging radar renders this quadratic scaling computationally prohibitive. As the sequence length L grows, Transformer VRAM usage doesn’t just increase—it spikes. For edge hardware on a vehicle, this is usually where the system crashes.

Our Bi-directional Mamba-2 backbone takes a different path, rooted in Structured State Space Duality (SSD). By using matrix block decomposition and recursive evolution, the core operators follow a strictly linear scaling law:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Complexity_{Backbone} \approx O(L \cdot d \cdot E)$$\end{document}

In this equation, d represents the state dimension and E is the expansion factor. We will find that no matter how much we densify the sampling points in the implicit field, the backbone stays at O(L) linear complexity.

For a sequence length L, the standard cross-attention mechanism requires \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(L^{2} \cdot D)$$\end{document} operations, which leads to quadratic growth in both computation and VRAM. In our Mamba-2 backbone, the state-space duality reduces this to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(L \cdot d \cdot E)$$\end{document}, where d is the state dimension and E is the expansion factor. Since our implicit field effectively compresses the 4D input into a manageable number of tokens (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L \ll H \cdot W \cdot R \cdot V$$\end{document}), we effectively decouple the inference latency from the raw sensor resolution. This linear dependency is the key factor enabling our real-time deployment on embedded hardware, where VRAM constraints typically render transformer-based fusion strategies inapplicable.

Generative modules

Diffusion models are often the “latency anchor” of an architecture because they usually need so many iterations. We cleared this hurdle with a two-part reduction strategy.

The first move is what we call Latent Space Parsimony. Specifically, we don’t run the diffusion process in the raw, ultra-high-dimensional H × W × R × V space. Instead, our CLDM works entirely inside the compressed latent space z0. By using the decoupled complex convolutions we defined earlier, we shrink the footprint of the diffusion operator to less than 1/64 of the original signal space. This effectively removes the heavy lifting from the generative path.

The second move is Self-consistency Mapping (NFE = 1). This distillation technique serves as the key technical enabler for achieving real-time inference. By distilling the denoising mapper with Consistency Models, we collapse the usual multi-step ODE solving into a single, deterministic function pass.

Experiment

Datasets

We evaluate our framework using two complementary open-source datasets: View-of-Delft (VoD)41 and K-Radar42.

The View-of-Delft (VoD) dataset serves as our primary benchmark for urban 3D detection. It contains 8,691 synchronized frames of 4D imaging radar, camera images, and 64-line LiDAR data. What makes VoD particularly valuable is its focus on Vulnerable Road Users (VRUs), such as pedestrians and cyclists. With approximately 130,000 3D bounding box annotations—including 26,000 for cyclists—it provides a challenging environment for verifying our "diffusion-refined occupancy head." Since these small targets are often represented by just a few tokens in 4D radar point clouds, they are perfect for testing the model’s ability to recover geometric topology at a distance.

While VoD handles semantic accuracy, the K-Radar dataset provides the raw data needed for our complex-domain signal processing. It consists of roughly 35,000 frames captured across varied weather conditions like rain, snow, and fog. Unlike datasets that only offer discrete point clouds, K-Radar retains the full 4D radar power tensor. This allows us to work directly with the H × W × R × V structure and verify the Mamba-2 backbone’s capacity for long-sequence temporal association.

Real-world datasets rarely include adversarial electronic interference, so we introduced a coherent interference simulation to fill this gap. Rather than adding simple Gaussian noise, we used the defined physical equations to inject ISRJ into every raw radar frame. We varied the sampling duty cycle from 10 to 50% and set the Jamming-to-Signal (J/S) ratio between 5 and 25dB. By layering these "ghost target arrays" over the urban scenes of VoD and the adverse weather of K-Radar, we built a robustness benchmark of over 150,000 samples.

It is important to clarify that our framework operates on the 4D Radar Tensors (4DRT) provided by these datasets. Unlike traditional point clouds which discard complex-domain information, the 4DRT format preserves the complex reflectivity and phase coherence derived from the radar’s antenna array signal processing. We treat these tensors as our primary input, which contain sufficient phase and motion information to support the complex-valued operations in our CLDM and the implicit field reconstruction, thereby ensuring the physical validity of our phase-aware generative approach.

Baselines

Our first set of comparisons includes BEVFusion14 and CRN43. BEVFusion is a gold standard for unified BEV representation, while CRN uses deformable attention to fix spatial misalignments between radar and camera. Comparing our model to these allows us to see if the linear O(L) scaling of Mamba-2 actually outperforms traditional pooling and attention mechanisms when handling 4D radar’s massive token streams. In addition, we also include DeepInteraction16 and the recent UniBEVFusion18. UniBEVFusion’s rejection of the "radar as sparse LiDAR" idea aligns with our own motives. Its RDL module for depth correction serves as a baseline to see if our complex latent diffusion—which fixes the signal at its root—is more robust against ISRJ than point-cloud-level corrections. Finally, we include PointMamba20 to show that our "Hilbert interleaved serialization" is better at protecting 4D radar’s spatiotemporal coherence than generic geometric ordering strategies.

To ensure a fair evaluation of robustness, we subject all baseline models to the identical ISRJ interference benchmark. The jamming signals generated via Eq. (2) are injected directly into the raw radar tensors, ensuring that all models, including ours and the SOTA baselines, process the same signal-level noise and ghost-target artifacts. The performance metrics reported in Table 2 reflect the inherent resilience of each architecture when dealing with these standardized coherent interference patterns.

Evaluation metrics

Basic perception accuracy

We use the mean Average Precision (mAP) to measure the spatial overlap between predicted boxes and ground truth. However, since 4D radar offers so much more than just location, we also adopt the NuScenes Detection Score (NDS)44. NDS is a weighted combination of mAP and several attribute error terms:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$NDS = \frac{1}{10}\left[ {5 \cdot mAP + \sum\limits_{{attr \in {\mathcal{A}}}} {(1 - \min ({\mathrm{Error}}_{attr} ))} } \right]$$\end{document}

The set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{A}}$$\end{document} covers errors in translation (ATE), scale (ASE), orientation (AOE), velocity (AVE), and attributes (AAE). Velocity Error (AVE) is particularly important here, as it reflects how well the model inherits the raw Doppler tensor.

We use Mean Translation Error (ATE) and Mean Velocity Error (AVE) to check our velocity point estimates. ATE tracks the Euclidean distance deviation of the target center q, while AVE focuses on the 3D velocity vector μv:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AVE = \frac{1}{{N_{\det } }}\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{{N_{\det } }} {\left\| {{\mathbf{v}}_{gt}^{(i)} - {{\boldsymbol{\upmu}}}_{v}^{(i)} } \right\|_{2} }$$\end{document}

Since Complex-Flow Mamba outputs an anisotropic covariance matrix Σv, a lower AVE proves that the network has learned the physical difference between radial and tangential Doppler observations.

Recall-at-distance

To address the “echo sparsity” of 4D radar at long ranges, we use a stratified recall analysis. We divide targets into five 20-m intervals: 0-20 m, 20-40 m, 40-60 m, 60-80 m, and 80-100 m. Recall is calculated for each:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Recall = \left. {\frac{TP}{{TP + FN}}} \right|_{d \in Range}$$\end{document}

Jamming Suppression Gain (JSG)

This original physical metric measures how well our complex diffusion model cleans up interference. We define JSG to quantify performance resilience under ISRJ:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$JSG = \frac{{mAP_{jammed} }}{{mAP_{clean} }}$$\end{document}

A physically consistent system should have a JSG close to 1. We also track the False Alarm Rate (FAR) to see if the latent space diffusion correctly identifies and removes the coherent ghost targets defined in Eq. (2).

Implementation details

The software stack is built on PyTorch 2.5.1 and CUDA 12.6. We turned to the Triton compiler for custom Mamba-2 kernel fusion. Our hardware setup is split: training and heavy evaluations happen on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 with its 24 GB of VRAM and 1 TB/s bandwidth, while we test the edge latency on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin (64 GB).

Handling the spherical-to-Cartesian mapping in real-time can be a drain on the CPU, so we used pre-computed lookup tables (LUTs) to skip the heavy trigonometric calculations during inference. For the Complex VAE, we locked the latent channel count C at 128 to stay 1-to-1 with our implicit field’s dimension F. The decoupled complex convolution block (DCB) uses a 3 × 3 × 3 convolution kernel in the spatial dimension, while the Doppler branch employs a 1D convolution kernel of length 7. The Mamba-2 backbone uses dstate = 16 and an expansion factor E = 2, with the structured state space duality (SSD) matrix compression turned on for every layer. To keep the Hilbert curve scanning stable, we quantized the grid to 64 × 64 × 32 using a recursion order of n = 6.

Our generative module uses a Variance Preserving (VP) SDE with T = 1000 steps and a linear schedule for β(t) ranging from 0.1 to 20. We use Consistency Model (CM) distillation with 200 initial queries and M = 32 dynamic sampling points in fϕ. By collapsing the sampling path into a single deterministic map (NFE = 1), the system gets the generative benefits of diffusion without the typical wait times.

We optimized the whole stack using AdamW with a learning rate of 2e − 4 and cosine annealing over 60 epochs. A heuristic warm-up strategy helped stabilize the weights. For the first 10 epochs, we set the signal enhancement loss λ2 to 5.0 to help the model learn the physical manifold but kept the physical constraint γ2 at zero. Once the features settled, we ramped γ2 up to 2.0. This prevents the Doppler projection from causing early training jitters or diverging gradients. We also ensured 30% of each batch was injected with ISRJ to simulate the kind of intermittent interference seen in contested electronic environments.

Conclusion

Our work with Complex-Flow Mamba tackles the long-standing issues of signal coherence loss and compute redundancy in 4D imaging radar. The fundamental objective of this work was not merely to construct higher-order non-linear mappings. It turns out that radar’s real potential is only unlocked when we can reclaim the phase essence lost to interference and use state-space models to entangle modalities without the typical O(N2) cost.

Each part of the framework, from the implicit field mapping to the phase-preserving diffusion, demonstrated its value in the final results. The generative detection head’s capability to reconstruct topological geometry in sparse regions, combined with the self-calibration from our Doppler constraints, offers a fresh perspective on the "long-tail" problems of autonomous driving. By decoupling accuracy from the quadratic compute cost, we’ve brought heavy-duty generative algorithms into the realm of real-time onboard standards.

Perception is likely to move from being purely data-driven to being driven by a mix of data and physics. While we made some headway in interference suppression and far-field reconstruction, there is still plenty of room to explore long-range temporal coherence across frames. We believe the complex-domain generative paradigm started by Complex-Flow Mamba will be a useful foundation for the next generation of reliable, all-weather driving systems.
