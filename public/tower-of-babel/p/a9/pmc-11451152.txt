Brain abscess caused by Nocardia farcinica in a person living with HIV
Authors: Lele Yu, Jun Yan, Zhongdong Zhang, Feng Li, Rongrong Zheng, Jinchuan Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Nocardia farcinica is the most pathogenic Nocardia, which is easy to disseminate. It can be caused by trauma, and even lead to severe lung or central nervous system infection. This report covers a case of Nocardia brain abscess in an HIV patient, who underwent resection of the brain abscess, followed by anti-infective therapy with sulfamethoxazole and meropenem, and eventually made a good recovery. The mortality rate of Nocardia farcinica brain abscess has been attributed to the severity of the underlying disease, the difficulty in identifying the pathogen, and its inherent resistance to antibiotics, leading to inappropriate or late initiation of treatment. Medication should follow the principle of sufficient dosage and sufficient course of treatment.

Background

Nocavdia is a genus of aerobic Actinomycetes, the agents of these infections are aerobic filamentous Gram-positive, weakly acid-fast bacteria that live as soil saprophytes [1]. Edmond Nocard first isolated Nocardia in 1888 [2], so far, about 80 species have been isolated and identified, 33% of which are pathogenic to humans. The most common and most reported pathogens in humans are Nocardia asteroids, Nocardia brasiliensis, Nocardia farcinica, and Nocardia caviae. Among them, Nocardia farcinica is one of the most pathogenic Nocardia, which is easy to disseminate [3]. It can be caused by trauma, and even lead to severe lung or central nervous system infection [3]. In 1/3 of hospitalized patients, the infection commonly manifests as pulmonary disease, it is estimated that 9% of Nocardia infections lead to a brain abscess, with N. farcinica being the most frequent species [4].

Case presentation

A 53-year-old man was admitted to the hospital, presenting with a 4-day history of weakness of the left limb. The patient was able to walk with support to a local hospital that morning. However, in the afternoon, weakness in his left limb worsened and he was unable to walk. Physical examination yielded the following findings: a temperature of 36.2 °C, pulse rate of 88 beats/min, respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min, and blood pressure of 1121/77 mmHg. Clear consciousness, physical examination cooperation; Bilateral pupils were equal and round, with a diameter of 3.0 mm. Sensitive light reflection; There was no ptosis of the eyelids, and the eye movement was slightly limited. Blurred vision, left hemianopsia; The forehead lines are symmetrical, showing no deviation of the mouth Angle of the teeth, and the tongue is centered; Positive neck resistance (3 transverse fingers), GCS:15 points.

Laboratory examinations revealed anti-HIV antibody was positive, and the patient’s CD4 T-cell count was 2 cells/ml. HIV RNA level was 7.05 × 104 IU/ml. The cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was colorless cleat, with significantly elevated protein levels (1015 mg/L), cell count (nucleated cells: 2 × 104/L), glucose (2.7 mmol/L), and chloride (124 mmol/L) levels. CSF pressure is 140mmH2O.

Chest CT (Fig. 1) scan showed a little inflammation in the lower lobe of the left lung, and a few inflammatory fiber lesions in the lower lobes of both lungs. Head CT (Fig. 2)showed large patches of low-density shadow in the right parietooccipital lobe, within which there were patches of soft tissue density shadow, adjacent to the brain parenchyma, and the right ventricle was compressed, and the midline structure slightly deviated to the left. Contrast-enhanced MR (Fig. 3) Showed a lesion in the right parietooccipital lobe with perifocal edema, and brain abscess was the first consideration. The patient received empirical treatment with meropenem and vancomycin.

Based on the MR Findings, the neurosurgeon performed brain abscess resection. Pyogenic bacteria were Gram stain positive, acid-fast stain weakly positive, and identified as Nocardia farcinica positive by mass spectrometry. Antiviral therapy with BIC/FTC/TAF was initiated. The antibiotic regimen was changed to meropenem and sulfamethoxazole for 3 weeks, and the reexamination of the head CT(Fig. 4) was better than before. the patient’s left limb muscle strength recovered to grade 4 to discharge from the hospital, physical examination showed improved eye movement, clear vision, and no neck resistance, followed by amoxicillin and clavulanate potassium and sulfamethoxazole for 12 months.

Discussion

Nocardia farcinica is an intracellular microorganism that grows in macrophages and host tissues. They have catalase and superoxide dismutase, giving them the ability to inactivate reactive oxygen species produced by neutrophils. In addition, the cell wall of Nocardia can prevent activation of macrophages by interferon-γ, preventing phagocytosis and fusion of phagolysosomes [5, 6]. Nocardia is often missed. One of the main reasons is that Nocardia grows slowly, and very small colonies will grow after 2–3 days of culture. Therefore, more attention should be paid to the smear detection. If Gram stain is positive and rod or branched filaments with different lengths are found in the smear microscopy, weak acid staining should be added and the culture time should be prolonged appropriately to help the detection of Nocardia.

Clinically, immunosuppressed and immunocompetent patients such as people with autoimmune diseases, malignant tumors, AIDS, organ transplantation, and chemotherapy are susceptible to Nocardia infection [7]. Central nervous system nocardiosis has diverse clinical manifestations such as headache, seizures, neurological abnormalities, and mental status changes. Other symptoms include fever, weight loss, and night sweats.

The mortality rate of Nocardia farcinica brain abscess is high. The mortality rate of patients with normal immune function is as high as 20%, while the mortality rate of patients with normal immune function is as high as 55%. These high rates have been attributed to the severity of the underlying disease, the difficulty in identifying the pathogen, and its inherent resistance to antibiotics, leading to inappropriate or late initiation of treatment [8, 9].

There is no difference in imaging features between brain abscesses caused by Nocardia farcinica and other common pathogens, and there is no specificity in imaging manifestations. Therefore, laboratory detection of Nocardia farcinica is still an important basis for diagnosis.

There is still a lack of guidelines for the management and treatment of Nocardia infection. Despite the limited literature, surgical excision is considered necessary in most cases [10]. Hall et al. [11] surgical aspirations alone were considered appropriate as the initial management of brain abscess. Others have proposed that craniotomy and excision of the entire abscess and wall are more effective than aspiration and drainage [8]. It has been suggested in the literature that abscesses > 25 mm in diameter and that fail to shrink after use of antibiotic therapy should be aspirated to confirm the diagnosis regardless of the immune status of the patient [8].

The treatment of Nocardia infection is mostly individualized according to the patient’s condition and the results of the in vitro bacterial susceptibility test. Medication should follow the principle of sufficient dosage and sufficient course of treatment. The antimicrobial susceptibility profile was highly variable between the species, but in general, amikacin, linezolid, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole demonstrated good in vitro activity against most species [12]. Penetration of sulfonamides and trimethoprim into the CSF at high doses is considered sufficient to treat CNS infections with susceptible bacteria. A study by Brown-Elliott et al. [13], involving 522 clinical isolates, showed that only 2% of the isolates exhibited in vitro resistance to TMP-SMX. In a retrospective analysis of 84 patients with Nocardia infection in the central nervous system, Anagnostou et al. [14] found that the mortality and recurrence rates were lower in the group treated with compound sulfamethoxazole than in the group not treated, and the treatment course of more than 6 months also significantly reduced the mortality and recurrence rates. Based on these results, and its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier, TMP-SMX is considered the mainstay of treatment.

For immunocompromised patients, patients with pulmonary infection, or patients with disseminated infection, a two-drug combination or three-drug combination is often necessary, usually requiring at least 12 months or even longer course of antimicrobial therapy, meanwhile, disease recurrence should be vigilant.

In conclusion, Nocardia farcinica is a rare opportunistic infection pathogen. Due to the lack of specificity in clinical manifestations and imaging examinations, the diagnosis of patients with Nocardia farcinica infection is easy to be delayed, with poor prognosis and high mortality. Early diagnosis and treatment are important. The principle of medication should follow the guidelines of combined use, sufficient dose and sufficient course of treatment.

Unfortunately, susceptibility results were lacking in our case, so we choosed meropenem, SMZ-TMP, and amoxicillin clavulanate potassium based on the literature and our experience.
