Investigating the synergistic effects of internal curing and pozzolanic activity in mortar utilizing clay-burnt brick fines
Authors: Tarek Uddin Mohammed, Jamil Ahmed Joy, Chowdhury Zubayer Bin Zahid, Redwan Ul Islam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the combined effect of internal curing and pozzolanic activity in mortar through the incorporation of clay-burnt brick fines (CBBF) as a partial substitute for sand. Simultaneously, the study explores the degree of pozzolanic reactivity by replacing a part of cement with finer clay-burnt brick fines (CBBP, particle size <75 μm). These materials are sourced from waste materials generated during brick-chip production. The 50 mm cubic mortar specimens were prepared by substituting a portion of fine aggregate with pre-wetted CBBF up to 20 % v/v and replacing cement with CBBP up to 27 % v/v. Two separate sand-to-cement ratios (S/C) of 1.2 and 1.6 were used in the mortar mixes. Samples underwent mechanical testing and were analyzed using ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) and accelerated carbonation tests. At a S/C of 1.6, increasing CBBF content in the mortar led to higher compressive strength. Conversely, for S/C = 1.2, the optimal replacement level was 4 % v/v of CBBF, beyond which compressive strength gradually decreased due to the porous nature of CBBF particles. Moreover, an 8 % CBBF replacement demonstrated maximum resistance to carbonation. SEM analysis revealed a denser matrix, indicating the effectiveness of CBBF in enhancing mechanical and durability properties.

Introduction

In recent decades, numerous countries have emerged from economic stagnation, investing substantially in infrastructure development to foster robust and sustained economic growth. Consequently, the demand for concrete materials has surged significantly. This escalation, however, raises environmental concerns, especially given that the cement industry alone is responsible for approximately 8 % of global carbon dioxide emissions [1]. Addressing the environmental sustainability of this development becomes imperative considering these increasing demands. Consequently, there is a pressing need within the scientific community to develop more sustainable and environmentally friendly concrete and cementitious materials through the incorporation of various methods and materials [2].

The utilization of high-strength concrete is on the rise worldwide, driven by the construction of high-rise buildings, long-span bridges, and other infrastructure projects. However, the increased use of cement with a relatively low water-to-cement ratio (W/C), leads to desiccation of cement paste as well as significant autogenous shrinkage. To overcome these concerns, internal curing (IC) agents in concrete are used.

Several studies on the influence of IC on concrete were conducted, considering different materials, such as lightweight aggregate [[3], [4], [5]], recycled concrete aggregate [6], recycled brick coarse aggregate [7], brick coarse aggregate [8], polymer-based absorbent [9], etc. Various investigations have been carried out with lightweight aggregate (LWA) and other IC agents considering the compressive strength of internally cured concrete. The studies concluded that, up to a certain portion of the pre-wetted LWA replacement, the compressive strength of the concrete enhanced and afterward it began to erode [10,11]. Similar results have been reported in some literature for ultrafine ingredients as an internal healing agent [12,13]. It is also stated that the water released from pre-wetted LWA enhanced the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) between the aggregate and cement paste, resulting in fewer pores and improved microstructure in high-performance concrete (HPC) [14]. However, the effect of IC by LWA is mostly influenced by the quantity and size of the particles, along with their distribution in concrete [15]. On the other hand, Vázquez-Rodríguez et al. [16] also investigated the effects of an alternative internal curing technique in HPC using mineral aggregate and observed that the mechanical properties of HPC improved by replacing 20 % of sand with an IC agent. Whereas three distinct waste ceramic aggregates were utilized by Xu et al. [17] as internal curing materials for HPC. Based on the experimental outcomes, it was concluded that it reduced autogenous shrinkage, and improved compressive strength while replacing 10 % of recycled ceramic aggregates. Furthermore, superabsorbent polymers were also evaluated as an IC agent [18], where it was found to enhance the workability of the concrete in the presence of water from the IC agent without altering the water-to-cement (W/C) ratio. However, very few studies have been conducted to understand the possibility of IC by clay-brunt brick (as CBBF and CBBP) in the form of fine aggregates as well as mineral binders. Although Ge et al. [19] explored the use of recycled fine clay brick aggregates as an IC agent, however, the use of waste clay-brick fines and its effect on concrete or mortar of different strengths have not been investigated.

Similarly, several studies have been conducted replacing cement using slag [[20], [21], [22], [23]], fly ash [[24], [25], [26], [27]], silica fume [28,29], glass powder [30], quartz powder [31], and many other materials. For example, Bahedh & Jaafar [24] showed that cement could be replaced to over 10 % by fly ash to get high compressive strength under autoclaving and water curing conditions, whereas Young et al. [25] created cement mortars by substituting 25 % of the cement with more than fifteen different types of fly ashes, resulting in improved compressive strength with age due to their pozzolanic reactivity. Yet, more investigations are essential to be conducted using CBBP as a replacement for cement. Sinkhonde et al. [32] assessed the effect of burned clay-brick powder on rubberized concrete and found that the addition of 5 % burned clay-brick powder as a cement substitute improved the compressive and split tensile strength. Several pieces of literature [[33], [34], [35], [36]] also recognized the pozzolanic activity of clay-bricks fines. Moreover, the early-age hydration behavior of waste clay brick incorporated blended cement was also observed by Zhao et al. [37]. Within emerging technologies such as 3D printed concrete, utilization of both ground granulated blast-furnace slag and clay brick powder resulted in a reduction in chloride ions migration in mortars [38]. However, the influence of various proportions of brick powder in mortar as a cement substitute as well as the combined effect of internal curing and pozzolanic activity of CBBP had not been thoroughly studied.

In numerous countries, the scarcity of natural stone resources has led to a heavy reliance on alternative materials in the concrete-making process. In certain regions, crushed clay-burnt red building blocks, commonly referred to as clay bricks, serve as the sole substitute for traditional stone chips in concrete production [39]. During the process of brick crushing, approximately 5–10 % of the bricks are reduced to powder, typically screened out using 4.75 mm sieves. Unfortunately, this byproduct is often disregarded and indiscriminately disposed of, contributing to long-term soil pollution. However, clay-burnt brick fines possess water-absorbing properties and exhibit pozzolanic activity, making them suitable for use as an internal curing agent for concrete and as a partial substitute for cement.

Recognizing the potential of clay burnt brick fines as a valuable construction by-product, there is a pressing need for research to explore their effectiveness in high-strength concrete applications and their ability to replace a portion of cement. This research initiative aims to leverage this underutilized material as an alternative to conventional internal curing agents and as a sustainable substitute for cement. By doing so, the goal is to promote sustainable practices and environmentally friendly applications for these materials in the construction industry.

With the above context, a comprehensive investigation has been carried out to identify the impact of the CBBF and CBBP as internal curing agents in mortar utilizing both destructive and nondestructive testing. Recognizing the direct correlation between mortar and its corresponding concrete, this investigation on mortar is expected to yield insights into the behavior of concrete [40]. Eventually, this study also explored the potential of using clay burnt brick fines (as CBBF and CBBP) in terms of the combined effect of internal curing (IC) and pozzolanic activity.

As demonstrated in Table 3, sand was substituted with clay-burnt brick fine aggregate (CBBF) up to a volume fraction of 20 % (serial no. 1 to 8). Prior to incorporation into the mortar, the CBBF underwent full immersion in water for a duration of 24 h. This pre-wetting process was performed to guarantee the maximal absorption of water by the CBBF. Moreover, water content during mixing was adjusted according to the specifications outlined in the mix design presented in Table 3. To understand the effect of CBBF on high-strength mortar, the sand-to-cement ratios (S/C) were set at 1.2 and 1.6. CBBF-4-1.6 indicates the sample made with 4 % replacement of sand by pre-wetted CBBF and S/C = 1.6. The water-to-cement ratio (W/C) was kept low, which is 0.3, to understand the effect of internal curing. Some of the samples were also made without the use of CBBF (serial no. 9 to 14 of Table 3), but the amount of water that will be absorbed by CBBF was added additionally and the equivalent amount of sand volume was deducted from the mix to control the mix volume to 1 cubic meter. For instance, CBBF-4W-1.6 indicates that the case is made without CBBF but by adding the water absorbed by 4 % sand-replaced CBBF. Following the mixture proportions listed in Table 3, 50 mm cube specimens were made as per ASTM C109 [37] and cured in a moist cabinet with 95 % relative humidity and 23 °C temperature.

Testing procedure

The mortar cube specimens were tested for compressive strength following the ASTM C109 [45] at the ages of 7, 14, 28, and 56 days. CBBP was tested for strength activity index (SAI) as per ASTM C311 [46]. Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) through the mortar specimens was also evaluated as per ASTM C597 [47] at the age of 56 days. A direct transmission procedure was followed for measuring UPV, and to maintain a smooth contract between the sample and the transducer, Castrol Pyroplex Blue grease was used. For each time of testing, 3 cube samples were tested per case, and the mean value is presented in this study.

Some samples were stored in an accelerated carbonation test chamber at a relative humidity of 70 %, CO2 concentration of 3 % [48], and a temperature of 40 °C [49]. Samples made with sand replacement with pre-wetted CBBF were exposed to the carbonation chamber for 34 days and the samples prepared by replacing cement were kept in the accelerated carbonation chamber for 15 days. After exposure in the carbonation chamber, mortar cubes were fractured in the middle, and carbonation depth was measured after spraying 1 % phenolphthalein solution.

To explore the microstructure of mortar, at the age of 56 days, the samples were investigated by TESCAN VEGA3 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). To avoid the accumulation of charges during SEM investigation, the freshly fractured mortar samples were coated with gold. Photographs were taken to understand the microstructure of the mortar samples made with and without CBBF. Experimental results and discussion

Effect of CBBF and CBBP on compressive strength of mortar

Fig. 4(a) and Fig. 4(b) show the compressive strength of the mortar samples made by replacing a part of fine aggregate by CBBF for S/C = 1.6 and S/C = 1.2, respectively.Fig. 4Compressive strength of mortar of (a) S/C = 1.6 and (b) S/C = 1.2.Fig. 4

Compressive strength of mortar of (a) S/C = 1.6 and (b) S/C = 1.2.

As shown in Fig. 4(a), for S/C = 1.6, increasing the volume of CBBF in the mortar increased the compressive strength of the mortar. Compared to the controlled sample (without CBBF), at the 56-day mark, the compressive strength exhibited increments of 8.13 %, 11.96 %, and 13.58 % with sand replacements of 4 %, 8 %, and 12 % by CBBF, respectively. For the specimen prepared with S/C = 1.2, the compressive strength increased by 3.05 % when 4 % v/v of CBBF was used as a pre-wetted internal curing agent, compared to the control sample. However, beyond this optimal replacement level, the compressive strength experienced a subsequent reduction. Specifically, the strength decreased by 5.56 % and 7.53 % for the replacement of 12 % and 20 % of sand with CBBF, respectively. This observed trend is elucidated in the subsequent section.

For both cases (S/C = 1.2 and S/C = 1.6), it is observed that samples made with CBBF exhibited higher compressive strength at the age of 7 days compared to the control specimens made without CBBF. The strength gain percentages of mortar specimens over time are shown in Fig. 5. It is found that at 7 days, strength gains of the mortar specimens prepared by incorporating CBBF were higher compared to the respective control samples (CBBF-0-1.6 and CBBF-0-1.2). The strength gain from 7 to 14 days and 14 to 28 days was found relatively lower for the specimens made with CBBF compared to respective control samples. The higher early strength of mortar with CBBF (at 7 days) can be explained due to the internal curing, as water absorption of CBBF is much higher than the sand (Table 1). This can be implied that pre-wetted CBBF released additional moisture into the mortar mixture during the curing phase without changing the W/C, which accelerated the hydration process and early strength development of the mixture [[50], [51], [52]]. These statements can be supported by the following observations.Fig. 5Strength gain percentage of mortar specimen with time, (a) S/C = 1.6 and (b) S/C = 1.2.Fig. 5

Strength gain percentage of mortar specimen with time, (a) S/C = 1.6 and (b) S/C = 1.2.

The 7-day compressive strength of mortar samples prepared without CBBF, but with additional water that would have been absorbed by the CBBF in the corresponding mix, is shown in Fig. 6. The added water into the mortar samples insignificantly influences the 7-day compressive strength compared to the respective control samples (CBBF-0-1.6 and CBBF-0-1.2). However, samples prepared by incorporating CBBF showed significant strength gain compared to their respective counterparts. For instance, mortar sample prepared by incorporating pre-wetted CBBF gained 20.14 % (CBBF-4-1.6), 22.31 % (CBBF-8-1.6), and 21.15 % (CBBF-12-1.6) more compressive strength compared to their counterparts for the cases of S/C = 1.6. And, for the cases of S/C = 1.2, it was 27.64 % (CBBF-4-1.2), 26.83 % (CBBF-12-1.2), and 23.88 % (CBBF-20-1.2).Fig. 6Compressive strength at the age of 7 days of the mortar prepared with CBBF and adjusting water, (a) S/C = 1.6 and (b) S/C = 1.2.Fig. 6

Compressive strength at the age of 7 days of the mortar prepared with CBBF and adjusting water, (a) S/C = 1.6 and (b) S/C = 1.2.

The above observations are clearly indicating the effectiveness of CBBF as an internal curing agent. The results also indicate that CBBF is more effective as an internal curing agent for cases with lower cement content (S/C = 1.6) irrespective of their ages, and at the early age (7 days) for the cases made with higher cement content (S/C = 1.2).

Again it can be seen from Fig. 5, from 28 to 56 days, the percentage of strength gain began to increase for the specimens made with pre-wetted CBBF, which is expected due to the pozzolanic activity of CBBF [53]. However, the pozzolanic reactivity also depends on the particle size as it decreases when the particle size increases [54]. The observation of this pozzolanic activity was more prominent for the samples prepared with higher cement content (S/C = 1.2) or specimens with higher compressive strength. For instance, specimens prepared without CBBF gained 0.5 % of compressive strength at the age of 56 days from 28 days, whereas specimens prepared with 20 % CBBF gained 6.1 % more strength compared to the strength at 28 days. The increase in strength at a later age can be explained due to the pozzolanic activity of CBBF. Further explanations of this observation are provided in the following paragraph.

From Table 2, it is seen that CBBP contains considerable amounts of Silicon Dioxide (SiO2), Aluminum Oxide (Al2O3), and Calcium Oxide (CaO), which are essential constituents for the development of calcium aluminosilicate hydrate (C-A-S-H) gel during the pozzolanic reaction [55]. To justify the statement, the strength activity index (SAI) of CBBP was determined as per ASTM C311 [46] by following eq. (1).(1)SAI=AveragecompressivestrengthofcubeswithCBBPAveragecompressivestrengthofcontrolmixcubes×100

SAI of 28 days and 56 days of CBBP was found 81.6 % and 92.6 %, respectively. Whereas the minimum required SAI for fly ash is 75 % as per ASTM C618 [56]. These results suggest a significant pozzolanic activity of CBBP.

For further investigating the pozzolanic properties of CBBP, some specimens were made by replacing cement with CBBP (8.5 %, 17 %, and 27 % v/v). Some samples were also made by substituting cement with slag and fly ash (27 % replacement of cement by volume). A case was also made entirely with CEM Type II/A-M as per BDS EN 197: 2010. The compressive strengths of these specimens are presented in Fig. 7.Fig. 7Compressive strength of mortar for replacement of cement by CBBP, fly ash, and slag.Fig. 7

Compressive strength of mortar for replacement of cement by CBBP, fly ash, and slag.

In samples incorporating CBBP, the 7-day compressive strength diminished as the CBBP content in the binder increased. However, at later ages, the compressive strength exhibited an upward trend. Notably, among the mortar specimens made with Type II A-M, OPC + fly ash, and OPC + slag, those composed of OPC + slag showed higher compressive strength. This strength was nearly equivalent to samples containing OPC + CBBP (17 % replacement by volume). Moreover, even the specimen made with OPC + CBBP (27 % replacement by volume) performed better in compressive strength compared to the specimen made with OPC + fly ash (27 % replacement by volume), which strongly indicates the pozzolanic properties of CBBP.

From the aforementioned outcomes, it can be concluded that CBBF can be utilized as an effective internal curing agent. The porous and softer CBBF particles may reduce the strength of high-strength mortar during early ages, however, the reduction in strength can be offset by gaining strength due to internal curing as well as pozzolanic activity in later ages. Based on the results, extended research on the utilization of CBBF for making high-strength concrete is continuing separately.

Effect of CBBP on UPV

The UPV of the mortar samples at 56 days is shown in Fig. 8. As illustrated in Fig. 8(a), for S/C = 1.6, UPV is increased with the increase of the amount of CBBF. For S/C of 1.2, the UPV exhibited an increase up to a 4 % replacement of sand with pre-wetted CBBF. However, beyond this optimal replacement level, UPV gradually declined with the increasing proportion of CBBF, as illustrated in Fig. 8(b). Similar results are also observed in compressive strength data. This observation can be explained by the resultant benefits from the negative influence of the inclusion of porous CBBF in the mix, the positive benefits of internal curing, and the positive benefits of the pozzolanic activity of CBBF. The vector of benefit become positive for all cases of S/C = 1.6, i.e. lower cement content.Fig. 8UPV of mortar sample of (a) S/C = 1.6, (b) S/C = 1.2, and (c) the sample containing CBBP as cement substitute.Fig. 8

UPV of mortar sample of (a) S/C = 1.6, (b) S/C = 1.2, and (c) the sample containing CBBP as cement substitute.

Additionally, Fig. 8(c) illustrates the impact of UPV on the mortar samples prepared by replacing cement with CBBP. The figure also shows that UPV for mortar samples prepared entirely of CEM Type II A-M and the cement containing fly ash, and slag. With the replacement of cement by CBBP, the UPV values of mortar dropped. The UPV of samples comprised of CBBP was found to be lesser than the samples prepared with CEM Type II A-M and cement replaced by slag, however higher than samples made with cement substituted with fly ash.

The findings suggest that CBBP exhibits pozzolanic activity, which is more efficient than fly ash. The results also can be aligned with the results of compressive strength [57] as explained earlier.

However, such substitution with CBBP could significantly influence the environmental footprint of the cement industry [60].

Conclusions

Based on the findings of this investigation, the following insights are made:•CBBF has demonstrated its efficiency as an internal curing agent by releasing absorbed water in a manner that accelerates the hydration process, thereby enhancing early-age compressive strength.•The evident increase in compressive strength at later ages, coupled with the SAI value and chemical composition analysis, strongly indicates the pozzolanic behavior of CBBF.•An 8 % CBBF replacement exhibited maximum resistance to carbonation in the mortar specimen, suggesting an optimal percentage for the formation of the most compact microstructure facilitated by CBBF.•Denser microstructure was observed around the CBBF due to the combined effect of internal curing and pozzolanic activity.•The dense microstructure around the CBBF acts as a barrier to the penetration of CO2 into the mortar and enhances its durability.•In comparison to other pozzolans and considering the SAI, CBBP can be a viable and sustainable alternative clay-based mineral admixture to partially replace cement.
