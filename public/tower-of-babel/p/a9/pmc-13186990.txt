Deception in orbital games: simulation and dissimulation with a maneuverable decoy
Authors: Hongyu Han, Zhaohui Dang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates deception in orbital games, focusing on how maneuverable decoys can manipulate players’ perceptions, forcing them to make probabilistic decisions. This paper distinguishes between physical-level concealment (stealth and camouflage) and cognitive-level deception (simulation and dissimulation), modeling it as a balance of expectation shaping, mixed strategies, and information entropy manipulation. The study proposes a game-theoretic framework that integrates perfect rationality, common knowledge, and mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium, enabling the pursuer and evader to reason over probabilistic outcomes under incomplete information. A two-phase model is constructed: (1) a single-step game where agents can only maneuver once, and (2) a multi-step game involving Boyd-cycles, belief updates, and active target switching. The framework is extended to accommodate imperfect decoys via a realism coefficient, and it analyzes how distinguishability affects the Nash equilibrium. Results from a geostationary orbit indicate that when the evader faces an overwhelming pursuer (maneuverability 6:1), introducing a decoy can increase the evader’s survival probability from almost zero to 30.8%. This study offers a foundational perspective on orbital deception beyond evasive scenarios, including feints and scenarios in which both teams employ decoys. Findings emphasize the tactical value of cognitive deception in space security and point to future directions in robust, belief-aware game design under uncertainty.

Introduction

Orbital deception represents a typical kind of orbital game. Traditionally, “Deception” is defined as actions meant to mislead an opponent by manipulating, distorting, or falsifying evidence to cause the opponent to respond in a way that harms their own interests. In orbital games, Zhao1 has provided a preliminary definition of concepts such as “orbital lurking” and “orbital camouflage”, characterizing them as “the deployment of one or more satellites on one or several latent trajectories, concealing their true intentions, and forming a preparatory or operational posture to non-cooperative targets.” Szymanski2 offers a more detailed account of orbital feints and orbital deceptions, defining the “demonstration” in orbital deception as “a show of force in an area where a decision is not sought that is made to deceive an adversary...to confuse him about your ultimate objectives.” and “exploitation” as “removal of units from areas under enemy control by stealth, deception, surprise, or clandestine means”. Among the well-known strategies for improving space asset survivability3,4, deception is a complex but essential element. Systematically modeling and analyzing deception not only clarifies the mechanisms underlying optimal mixed strategies but also enhances intelligent decision-making under limited resources and asymmetric information, offering both theoretical insights and practical applications for building intelligent multi-agent game systems.

A substantial body of research has been devoted to orbital games, particularly the Orbital Pursuit-Evasion Games (OPEGs), in which a pursuer attempts to capture an evader, and the evader seeks to avoid being captured. “Capture” may refer to rendezvous, interception, etc1. As early as 1976, Anderson5 modeled the OPEG between finite-thrust spacecraft using differential game theory, framing it as a classical zero-sum game. Subsequent researchers have examined OPEG strategies under various assumptions: Venigalla6 and Han7 analyzed one-step optimal interception and anti-interception strategies in different initial status; Ma8 tries to solve OPEG using reachable domain and optimization method; Xie9 and Xu10 employed a finite action set with game-tree search to investigate optimal decisions under varying initial conditions; Zhou11, Liu12, and Jiao13 proposed strategies for proximity operations and observation of semi-cooperative space targets. Emerging methodologies have also been applied to OPEG. Zhao used the Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG) framework to solve OPEG with14 and without15 Boyd cycles (Observation, Orientation, Decision, Action, OODA), analyzing win probabilities across different maneuverability ratios and initial states using large-scale datasets. Zhao further extended this to two-vs-one pursuit-evasion, exploring multi-agent coordination strategies.16

Research in orbital games is not limited to OPEG. After modeling spacecraft relative motion patterns17, Sun proposed intent recognition frameworks for non-cooperative targets using both BiGAT18 and deep neural networks19, and extended the problem to incomplete time series data20. Jing advanced these efforts by incorporating large language models to handle non-cooperative targets’ unstructured intent information21. Han investigated orbital blockades across different orbital regimes–including geosynchronous orbit22, the Earth–Moon Lagrange point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_1$$\end{document}23, and low lunar orbit24–by analyzing blockers’ mission capable rates and effectiveness. Li25 and Zhao26 examined orbital target-attacker-defender (TED) games using differential game theory and AI-based approaches, respectively. Han27 extended the temporal scope of orbital games from short-term engagements (a few orbital periods) to long-duration scenarios (spanning several weeks), explicitly incorporating the long-term impact of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_2$$\end{document} perturbation into the game dynamics. Qin28 proposed a space-to-ground infrared “camouflage strategy” (a satellite surface design) that is compatible with radiative thermal management, achieving high absorptivity across multiple spectral bands while simultaneously reducing surface temperatures to minimize detectability. Yang29 and Li30 employed optimization methods and differential game theory, respectively, to address pulsed and continuous orbital camouflage. In their studies, “orbital camouflage” was interpreted as maintaining geometric occlusion between the agent and observer, thereby disrupting line-of-sight assessment and complicating adversarial inference.

While notable progress has been made in OPEG–including incorporating sensing delays and multi-agent cooperation–the concept of deception, a critical cognitive element in adversarial engagements, remains largely unaddressed in the existing orbital game research. On the other hand, certain research in game theory models deception through incomplete information and hidden types, such as concealed identities, manipulable signals, and opponents’ belief formation and updates. For example, Huang31 models robots’ deception as a dynamic game, where each robot’s private information is represented as a type, validates the framework in deceptive pursuit-evasion, and introduces metrics such as deceivability and the price of deception to measure the benefits and costs of deception. This research offers valuable insights into the strategic structure of deception and counter-deception. Building on this foundation, this paper expands the paradigm specifically to orbital games. Figure 1 illustrates a typical orbital deception scenario: a pursuer attempts to capture an evader, while the evader releases a decoy and diverges; the task of the true evader is exploitation (EX, to hide the truth), while the task of the decoy is demonstration (DEM, to show the false), increasing the pursuer’s capture cost and difficulty2. The scenario exemplifies a form of cognitive deception, in which the evader seeks to induce a misjudgment of the pursuer, specifically, to convince the pursuer that the decoy is the true evader. This is achieved through a deliberate orchestration of expectations, mixed strategies, and obfuscation, so that the pursuer cannot unambiguously determine its target’s identity in a single round of the game.

Simulation and dissimulation operate on the cognitive layer. These two terms naturally assume that the opponent has already detected at least two individuals; the very distinction between “true” and “false” presupposes a cognitive community in which multiple narratives, objections, comparisons, and interpretations coexist. As in Fig. 1, the pursuer is aware of the kinematical states of both targets, yet remains uncertain about their identities. Throughout the game, the evader intentionally conceals the behavioral biases, aiming to maximize the opponent’s uncertainty and thereby minimize their opponent’s predictive capacity and inverse reasoning. This interplay is designed to maintain a mutually optimal stance under the Nash equilibrium framework, where no player can unilaterally deviate without reducing their own payoff.

This study will construct a comprehensive scenario framework for orbital deception, formulate a payoff function that explicitly accounts for decoys and uncertain targets, and analyze the behavior of agents with varying levels of intelligence. Both single-step and multi-step game algorithms are developed to evaluate and compare outcomes with and without decoys, thereby demonstrating the effectiveness of decoy-based tactics and the proposed algorithmic approach. In addition, this study offers a preliminary discussion on the implications of decoy credibility. It explores extended tactical scenarios, aiming to provide a conceptual and technical foundation for further research on orbital games.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section “Models and methods” of this paper introduces the dynamical models adopted and provides a more detailed interpretation of orbital deception. It then establishes the game-theoretic framework for both single- and multi-stage settings, outlining the strategies and reasoning for both the pursuer and the evader. Section “Numerical experiments and results” presents numerical evaluations under typical initial conditions. Numerical experiments are conducted for both single- and multi-step scenarios to validate the proposed strategies, analyze their underlying logic, and compare them with traditional OPEGs that do not use decoys. Section “Discussion” discusses the direction for improving the method introduced, the implications of a less-than-perfect decoy on game outcomes, and explores several potential extensions to more complex orbital deception scenarios. Finally, section “Conclusion” summarizes the key contributions and findings of this study.

Modeling the deception problem

Assuming that both players possess a sufficiently high level of intelligence (later sections will prove that players with limited intelligence represent a special case of this more general setting). That is, both players possess perfect rationality and common knowledge33. Any attempt to employ “tactical cleverness”, such as momentary deviations from optimal strategy or exploiting presumed informational asymmetries, becomes ineffective, as a sufficiently intelligent opponent will anticipate such behavior and neutralize it through probabilistic counter-strategies. Under this premise, any strategy based on a deterministic choice in a single-stage encounter is inherently exploitable. The only robust approach is to adopt a mixed-strategy equilibrium, which can be perturbed only by introducing new information.

Figure 1 illustrates a relatively simple scenario: the evader deploys a decoy that, from the pursuer’s perspective, is indistinguishable from the true evader (more complex variations will be addressed in section “Discussion”). The pursuer only learns whether the captured object was the real evader or a decoy after a capture. The only difference between the game rules of deception games and traditional multi-evader OPEG is in the termination condition: the game ends immediately upon the successful capture of the true evader, regardless of whether the decoy has been captured. Without this constraint, the deception process degenerates into a conventional one-versus-many OPEG, losing its cognitive and informational asymmetry.

From the perspective of the evader to minimizing the pursuer’s expected interception cost, it would choose the probability p to maximize the expected cost incurred by the pursuer, while still forcing the pursuer into adopting a mixed strategy, that is, ensuring the pursuer perceives no expected utility difference between its pure strategies. Multi-step orbital deception game

Extend the single-step deception game to a multi-step formulation (e.g., when the pursuer requires multiple steps to intercept the first target). In this multi-step setting, the pursuer may adopt a one-shot sampling with commitment approach, selecting a target based solely on the first observation and maintaining that choice throughout the pursuit. While this strategy appears logical, it overlooks the critical dynamics unique to multi-step games: the evader can apply additional maneuvers over the course of the game, dynamically altering the perceived probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p^*$$\end{document}. The pursuer’s target selection, therefore, should not rely solely on the initial snapshot. Instead, it must be informed by repeated observations, calculating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p^*$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q^*$$\end{document} at each time step, followed by iterative decision-making.

It is reasonable to speculate that the weaker the maneuvering capabilities of both players, the longer the game time scale and the more obvious the influence of orbital dynamics, the more likely this point is to deviate [0.5 0.5]. Consider an extreme case in which both players have very limited maneuver capability. This can be viewed as requiring prohibitively long transfer times to reach a target, and it is also representative of engagements conducted in lower orbits (e.g., LEO), where feasible \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta V$$\end{document} budgets yield only marginal changes in orbital altitude (the vertical axis in Fig. 7). In this one-dimensional problem, when the decoy and the true target lie on the same side of the pursuer along the phase coordinate, the decoy and the associated mixed-strategy randomization become largely ineffective: the game reverts to the pure-strategy regime captured by Eq. (14), where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p\rightarrow 0$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p\rightarrow 1$$\end{document}. Consequently, as orbital altitude decreases–or, more generally, as both players’ maneuverabilities scale down proportionally while the kill radius remains unchanged–the advantage shifts systematically toward the pursuer.

Although, in this case, the pursuer’s belief-updating process slightly increases the evader’s expected outcome, it is difficult to definitively determine which team benefits more from target switching in practice. Sometimes re-sampling causes the pursuer to shift from capturing the wrong target to the correct one, thereby reducing the final cost (as shown in the difference between Fig. 11d and e); Sometimes, re-sampling leads to an even greater disadvantage (as shown in Fig. 11f)—this not only alters the expectations of the problem but also changes the variance of the problem, an aspect not considered in this article. From another perspective, resampling can bring the problem closer to reality when the observed information can be continually updated, especially in real-world scenarios, an issue discussed in section “Discussion”.

From the outcome perspective, this case demonstrates an encouraging phenomenon: even when facing an adversary with overwhelming maneuvering superiority (6:1), an evader equipped with decoys has a non-negligible 30.8% chance of escape. This contrasts sharply with previous research15, which has shown that when the maneuverability ratio exceeds 2:1, the evader has virtually no chance of escape. Moreover, this study assumes that the pursuer has already established an interception trajectory before the game begins (that is more favourable for the pursuer), whereas previous research15 uses a random start. The key difference lies in the presence of the decoy, which effectively transforms the problem into a one-vs-two engagement, enabling the evader to spread risk through various methods.

Discussion

Distinguishable decoys

The previous sections assumed that the decoy and the true evader are indistinguishable. However, in reality, distinctions often exist between decoys and true targets, whether in electromagnetic, infrared, or dynamic characteristics. The discernibility in appearance or behavior will affect the structure of the game equilibrium. Suppose this decoy realism can be described by a parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta \in [0,1]$$\end{document}: the decoy is mistaken as a true evader with probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document}, and identified correctly as a fake evader with probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1-\delta$$\end{document}, while the true evader is always identified correctly.

An intelligent pursuer, possibly through open-source intelligence or other pre-game analyses, may know the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document} before the game begins, although it still cannot determine which target is true. Unfortunately, the multi-step, dynamically changing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document} proposed above is very hard to apply directly to the algorithm in this article. Due to the computational characteristics of the optimization pipeline used in this paper, each level of optimization requires repeated statistical evaluation of the opponent’s expected outcome. In the multi-step numerical experiment, these expectations are approximated via shooting-sampling (for thousands of cycles each sample point) rather than closed-form expressions, which can lead to substantial runtime. As a result, the current simulation is necessarily limited in scale, which reduces the extent to which we can (i) explore deeper nested models of higher-order reasoning, (ii) systematically tune scenario-dependent coefficients (e.g., the expert-selected weights in Eqs. 20–22) across large sets of trials, or (iii) conduct exhaustive sensitivity analyses. Accordingly, the present work emphasizes demonstrating convergence and correctness on representative cases, while broader coverage of operating regimes is left for future study.

This runtime overhead arises mainly because the core optimizers adopted in this paper (BFGS and PSO)34 treat the expected-value evaluation as an external black box and therefore require many fresh rollouts during the search process, without explicitly amortizing trajectory-level information across evaluations or across repeated games. As additional decision layers are nested, the number of required rollouts can increase rapidly, making comprehensive multi-step equilibrium exploration computationally demanding. A natural direction to alleviate this limitation is to incorporate learning-based methods that leverage memory across repeated interactions—i.e., outcome-driven approaches that can amortize experience across many simulated games and implicitly tune the coefficients in Eqs. (20–22) (rather than relying solely on expert-specified weights). Examples include modern reinforcement learning algorithms such as SAC and recurrent-policy architectures (e.g., LSTM-based models) that capture temporal dependencies and reuse past experience. The authors pledge to publish a follow-up study soon that will explore the pursuer’s strategies when confronting distinguishable decoys, particularly those involving observation and discrimination, as well as potential information-waiting strategies. This follow-up will investigate learning-based techniques for deriving more adaptive pursuer policies under such settings.

Conclusion

This paper presents a systematic modeling and analysis of orbital games involving deception, with particular emphasis on the role of decoys in the orbital game. By extending beyond classical orbital game frameworks, this paper introduced and validated a multi-agent, multi-round decision-making model incorporating Boyd-cycle, belief updating, and limited maneuverability. It demonstrates that deception can greatly improve the survival probability of the evader (from almost 0% to 30.8%), even when facing a pursuer with overwhelming maneuverability (6:1).

Firstly, this paper constructs the framework of orbital deception games, analyzes the strategies adopted by pursuers of different intelligences when facing decoys, and presents the Nash equilibrium strategies of both teams under the assumption of single-step games. The research emphasizes that, unlike the traditional OPEG scenario, where there is a unique optimal result in a given situation, orbital deception would yield different optimal solutions, for both teams adopt mixed strategies governed by probabilities. Therefore, this paper points out that when optimizing the game process involving deception, designers should not only consider the result of a single game but also take into account the expectations of many game results, whether obtained through probability calculation or statistical averaging. For multi-step deception games, the pursuer’s control actions are established based on finite acceleration capabilities, single-step state predictions, and dynamically select targets and update beliefs based on limited observations. At the same time, the evader’s strategy is parameterized and optimized using particle swarm optimization. The expected cost function incorporates both time and fuel usage, and the probabilistic nature of the game is explicitly considered.

Numerical experiments were performed under both single-step and multi-step deception scenarios to validate the effectiveness of the proposed model and strategy. The single-step game scenario demonstrates that the appearance of the decoy can vastly increase the pursuit cost (from 5.1 m/s, J = 23, to an average of 23.5 m/s, J = 44, and possibly up to 39.5 m/s, J = 66). It proves the Nash equilibrium of the game from both the analytical theory and experimental results: The optimal possibility values of the evader team letting a specific target be the true evader, the pursuer team believing a specific target is the true evader, are located at the saddle point. Any sudden change in beliefs and possibilities by either team will not affect the outcome of the game. In multi-step scenarios, where the pursuer adapts its strategies across observation cycles, the addition of belief updates and multiple decision points provides richer pursuit dynamics. The simulations show that with proper deception strategies, an evader facing a pursuer with overwhelming mobility superiority (6:1) can still achieve a non-negligible (30.8%) probability of escape. Analysis highlights that deception can greatly reshape the game outcome. While previous studies suggested that an evader under overwhelming pursuit rarely survives, this study shows that the introduction of decoys fundamentally alters this situation.

Lastly, the deception game model is extended to account for imperfect decoys, where agents may partially distinguish true targets from fakes based on a “decoy authenticity” parameter, with updated expected values and modified Nash equilibrium strategies, allowing for Bayesian belief updating in dynamic engagements. Beyond defensive evasion, the paper explored deception in offensive and symmetric contexts, including decoy-supported feint attacks and games where both teams deploy decoys, forming a richer and more intricate game landscape. These scenarios suggest broader applications of deception in orbital games.
