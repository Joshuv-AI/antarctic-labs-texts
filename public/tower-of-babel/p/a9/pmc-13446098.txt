Deep learning decodes multi-horizon dynamics and probabilistic risks of harmful algal blooms
Authors: Ke Yu, Bo Nie, Fengming Zhang, Huan Zhang, Qi Zhou, Renke Wei, Shen Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Harmful algal blooms are expanding globally across freshwater ecosystems due to climate change and accelerating eutrophication, posing escalating threats to public health and economic stability. Robust early warning requires predictive frameworks that are accurate at hourly resolution, uncertainty-aware, and mechanistically interpretable. Existing process-based models are limited by sparse parameterization, while statistical and deep-learning approaches typically operate at daily resolution, produce only point predictions, and rely on threshold-based classifications or post-hoc explanations that fail to capture high-frequency temporal dynamics or quantify predictive uncertainty, thereby hindering proactive intervention and masking hidden ecological risks. Here we present BloomNet, an intrinsically interpretable deep neural network architecture configured for multi-horizon quantile forecasting, resolves these limitations by integrating future environmental covariates with historical observations from a hyper-eutrophic lake. Evaluated on a four-year hourly record, BloomNet achieves exceptional predictive stability across 24-, 48-, and 72-h horizons (R2 up to 0.78, 24-h MAPE = 25.7%), outperforming long short-term memory, Transformer, and temporal convolutional network baselines while circumventing iterative error accumulation. The network's dual-path variable-selection and attention mechanisms decode shifting ecological drivers directly across horizons, revealing a transition from short-term thermal regulation (water temperature weight up to 0.640) to medium-term nutrient governance (total phosphorus weight up to 0.689), while isolating distinct multimodal attention spikes 24h prior to bloom onset. Real-time interpretability further reveals that driver importance and temporal dependencies shift dynamically between bloom-onset and non-bloom states. These probabilistic quantiles establish a graduated, risk-oriented warning protocol that transforms reactive water resource administration into precision ecotechnology.

Introduction

Harmful algal blooms (HABs) have increased in frequency, duration, and geographical distribution across freshwater systems worldwide, largely driven by climate change, eutrophication, and increasing anthropogenic pressures [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Post-event mitigation is often ineffective and may even lead to secondary pollution [5,6]. The significant ecological, economic, and public health costs of HABs [[7], [8], [9]] have made the development of reliable early warning systems critical for water resource management. However, forecasting HABs remains difficult due to their rapid expansion under specific conditions [[10], [11], [12]] and the complex influence of multiple environmental factors, such as temperature, nutrient loading, agricultural runoff, and wastewater discharge [[13], [14], [15]]. Indeed, these characteristics challenge existing models and underscore the need for tools that are accurate, uncertainty-aware, and interpretable.

Process-based hydrodynamic–ecological models encode nutrient cycling, physical transport, and algal growth [[16], [17], [18]], although they are hampered by sparse parameterization and structural uncertainty. In contrast, classical statistical models (e.g., multiple linear regression, autoregressive integrated moving average) [19,20] rely on linearity and stationarity assumptions, which are rarely satisfied in rapidly changing aquatic systems. Recently developed machine learning and deep learning architectures, including support vector machines [21,22], convolutional networks [23,24], recurrent networks [[25], [26], [27]], and transformers [11,28], have improved short-horizon algal dynamic forecasts, but three key gaps persist.

First, current forecasting models typically operate at daily or weekly resolutions [21,23,27], which are insufficient for capturing the rapid, hourly-scale dynamics of algal blooms. In hourly forecasting, achieving the same lead time as daily forecasts requires more iterations (e.g., a 72-h forecast requires 72 steps at hourly resolution but only 3 steps at daily resolution), which can lead to error accumulation and reduced accuracy over longer horizons. Furthermore, many models underutilize available environmental covariates [11,26,27], and none incorporate known future values, thereby reducing the accuracy of multi-horizon forecasts. Given the strong temporal dependencies and fluctuations in environmental variables, incorporating such values would improve responsiveness, especially for long-term forecasts.

Second, algal bloom warnings are generally formulated as threshold-based classifications [20,[27], [28], [29]], in which a bloom is declared only when the predicted chlorophyll a concentration or algal density exceeds a fixed threshold. BAs HAB events are relatively rare, models can achieve seemingly high overall accuracy simply by correctly predicting the predominant non-bloom conditions, rather than truly detecting the onset of HABs. This raises concerns about the reliability of such apparently highly accuracy for genuine early warning capabilities. Moreover, most such models provide only point predictions and fail to quantify predictive uncertainty, limiting their use in real-time early warning systems.

Third, interpretability remains limited. Most models [30] rely on post hoc explanation tools such as local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) [31] or Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) [32], which assess feature importance outside the forecasting pipeline and often ignore temporal dependencies. Consequently, these tools identify which variables matter but fail to clarify when or how they drive bloom development. While transformers [28,33,34] offer promising intrinsic interpretability via attention mechanisms, existing applications focus on ranking the average variable influence without resolving dynamic attribution over time or across algal bloom states.

To address these gaps, we developed BloomNet, an interpretable deep learning model that advances beyond the limits of existing approaches in several key ways. Unlike models that focus solely on historical observations [[25], [26], [27]], BloomNet explicitly integrates future environmental drivers with high-frequency historical observations. In addition, its dual intrinsic interpretability mechanisms, variable selection for driver importance, and temporal attention for critical time periods provide both globally and temporally localized insights. Moreover, BloomNet's quantile regression strategy generates probabilistic multi-step forecasts, thereby enabling scenario-based assessments of algal bloom risk under uncertainty. Hence, BloomNet is a flexible, uncertainty-aware forecasting tool that is suited to the dynamic nature of HABs, and its use supports the development and implementation of transparent and ecologically grounded early warning strategies in rapidly changing aquatic environments.

Methods

Study area and data processing

Datong Lake is the largest inland aquaculture lake in Hunan Province, China, covering approximately 120 square kilometers with a mean depth of 2.5 m. The lake supports local fisheries and agriculture but is increasingly threatened by eutrophication and recurring HABs driven by nutrient inputs from agricultural runoff, aquaculture, and domestic wastewater.

To develop and test BloomNet for algal bloom early warning and driver attribution, we compiled an automatic-monitoring dataset from Datong Lake spanning January 1, 2019, to October 28, 2022 (Fig. 1; Supplementary Text S1). The dataset includes 11 key parameters relevant to algal dynamics: temperature (TEM), pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), electrical conductivity (Con), turbidity (Tur), permanganate index (IMn), ammonium nitrogen (AN), total phosphorus (TP), total nitrogen (TN), chlorophyll a concentration (Chl a), and algal density. Time feature engineering for algal dynamics prediction

BloomNet was formulated as a multi-horizon forecasting model for algal density. It predicts algal density at multiple future horizons using three groups of inputs: the historical values of the target itself; a set of past covariates, available only from previous observations; and a set of known future covariates, whose values are known or can be reliably estimated over the forecast horizon. Because meteorological data are unavailable, we used water temperature as a reliable proxy. Its high thermal inertia and strong correlation with the atmospheric conditions make it a predictable driver. Accordingly, algal density was used as the prediction target, water temperature was treated as a known future covariate, and all remaining variables were treated as past covariates (Fig. 2a). Formally, at each time step t, the target (algal density) is represented as the scalar yt∈R, the past covariates as the vector xt∈Rnp, and the known future covariates as the vector zt∈Rnf, where np and nf are the numbers of past and known future covariates, respectively.Fig. 2Temporal structure and model architecture of BloomNet. a, Construction of model inputs and outputs, including past covariates, known future covariates, and target values. BloomNet performs multi-horizon quantile forecasting and produces probabilistic predictions, including the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. The 10th percentile provides a conservative, low-risk estimate, indicating a 10% probability that the actual algal density will fall below this value. The 50th percentile forecast (median forecast) represents the most likely or typical state of the water body. The 90th percentile prediction captures the upper bound of predicted outcomes, suggesting a 10% chance that algal density may exceed this threshold and thereby serving as an indicator of potential high-risk or extreme events. Quantile forecasting does not assume that errors or target variables follow a normal distribution, making it suitable for the complex, asymmetric, or heavy-tailed distributions commonly seen in ecological and environmental data.

For each quantile q, the multi-horizon forecast yˆq,t+1:t+o can be defined using the following function:(1)yˆq,t+1:t+o=fq(l,yt−l+1:t,xt−l+1:t,zt−l+1:t+o)where fq is the forecasting model for quantile q, l is look-back window length, and o is the forecast horizon; yt−l+1:t denotes the historical target values, xt−l+1:t the past covariates, and zt−l+1:t+o the known future covariates. Here, zt−l+1:t+o denotes covariates whose values are known in advance over both the look-back window and the forecast horizon, and we call them known future covariates because their future values from t+1 to t+o are available at prediction time, unlike the past covariates. We use information from the target and all the covariates (Fig. 2a) within the look-back window, together with the known future covariates from time t+1 to t+o, to predict yˆq,t+1:t+o. The forecast over the horizon is the vector:(2)yˆq,t+1:t+o=[yˆq,t+1,…,yˆq,t+o]

Structure of BloomNet

We developed BloomNet as an interpretable deep neural network framework for multi-horizon quantile forecasting that separates historical observations from known future inputs (Fig. 2b). Given historical target values, past covariates, and known future covariates, BloomNet first organizes the raw time-series data into historical and future sequences. A dual-path variable-selection network assigns time-varying importance weights to input features, enabling the model to capture context-dependent driver relevance. The weighted sequences are then processed using a long short-term memory (LSTM) encoder–decoder, which compresses the historical information and incorporates the known future covariates into a unified temporal representation. This representation is then refined by gate–add–norm modules, masked multi-head attention and gated residual transformations, enabling the model to identify critical historical periods under causal constraints while stabilizing nonlinear feature extraction. Finally, dense and quantile-forecast layers generate probabilistic predictions across multiple percentiles, enabling uncertainty-aware forecasting. The variable-selection network, masked multi-head attention layer, and quantile-forecast layer are described below, with full architectural details provided in Supplementary Text S3.

Variable selection network

BloomNet features a dual-path architecture that separately processes historical and future inputs via learnable, time-varying variable selection. At each time t, the input for variable a is denoted ξt,a, and the inputs for all n variables are concatenated into a single vector ξt. This vector is processed by a gated residual network (GRN; Supplementary Text S3 and Fig. S2) to capture inter-variable dependencies and produce selection scores, which are normalized by a softmax into importance weights vt:(3)vt=Softmax(GRN(ξt))where n is the number of variables. Each element vt,a represents the importance weight of variable a at time t; the variable-selection weight sum to 1, and higher weights indicate that the corresponding variables have stronger impacts on the model's forecast. Next, each input variable a is independently processed through its own GRN at each time step:(4)ξ˜t,a=GRNa(ξt,a)where ξ˜t,a is the processed feature vector for variable a. The transformed features are then combined using the importance weights:(5)ξ˜t=∑a=1nvt,aξ˜t,a

The historical (Vhist∈R(np+nf)×l) and future (Vfut∈Rnf×o) selection-weight matrices are obtained from the two paths:(6)Vhist=[vt−l+1,hist,…,vt,hist],Vfut=[vt+1,fut,…,vt+o,fut]

These weights are later averaged across time steps and samples to identify overall driver importance or analyzed at specific time points to understand event-specific mechanisms.

Masked multi-head attention layer

BloomNet uses a masked multi-head attention layer to capture long-term dependencies in time-series data, focusing on the crucial historical patterns that influence algal dynamics (Block 1, Fig. 2b). In this block, past covariates are processed through causal attention, whereas known future covariates are passed through a parallel pathway and integrated without exposing the model to unavailable future observations. This design preserves the data's temporal integrity, ensuring the model's predictions rely solely on the information available at each time point while allowing direct integration of the known-in-advance future variables.

The stabilized temporal embeddings serve as input to this block, and the multi-head design runs H parallel attention computations (heads), each specializing in different temporal patterns. BloomNet employs this masked multi-head attention layer [34] to identify the historical time steps most relevant to the forecast: for each head h, query, key, and value projections Qh, Kh, and Vh are computed from the stabilized temporal embeddings; scaled dot-product scores are masked causally to prevent information leakage; the masked scores are normalized by a row-wise softmax; and the multi-head outputs are concatenated and linearly combined. A head-averaged global attention map Aglobal is used for interpretation, and an L1 sparsity constraint encourages the model to focus selectively on the most relevant time steps. The full derivation, including the causal mask, the row-wise softmax, and the sparsity constraint, is given in Supplementary Text S3.5.

Quantile forecast layer

Rather than producing single-point predictions, BloomNet generates multiple quantiles, such as the 10th, 50th, and 90th quantiles, to characterize the full predictive distribution. This is achieved by applying a linear transformation to the final decoder states. For each future time step t+τ and each quantile q, the q-th quantile of the algal density at time t+τ is predicted as:(7)yˆq,t+τ=Wq,t+τO4,t+τ+bq,t+τwhere Wq,t+τ∈R1×ddense is the weight vector for quantile q at horizon t+τ, O4,t+τ is the dense-layer output (Supplementary Text S3.9), and bq,t+τ is the corresponding bias. Stacking the predictions over all Nq quantiles and all o forecast steps gives the complete output matrix:(8)Y=[yˆq,t+τ]q=1,…,Nq;τ=1,…,o∈RNq×owhere each row corresponds to one quantile and each column to one forecast step.

We also designed a quantile loss M to characterize the complete probability distribution of the predictions rather than just the point estimates [35,36]:(9)M=1Nso∑i=1Ns∑j=1o∑q=1NqLQ,i,j,qwhere LQ,i,j,q denotes the quantile loss for the q-th quantile at the i-th sample and j-th time step; Ns is the number of samples, o is the number of steps to forecast each time, and Nq is the number of quantiles, set to 3 in this study (i.e., the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles; Supplementary Text S4). Note that the loss is summed rather than averaged over the Nq quantiles. More specifically, the loss for each quantile is calculated using the following formula:(10)LQ,i,j,q=max(ρq(y−yˆq),(ρq−1)(y−yˆq))where yˆq is the predicted value for the q-th quantile, y is the true target, and ρq denotes the level of the q-th quantile. For example, ρ1=0.1 corresponds to the 10th percentile.

Model training and optimization

The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test subsets, comprising 80%, 10%, and 10% of the data, respectively. All variables were min–max scaled using parameters fitted only to the training data to prevent data leakage. BloomNet targets multi-step-ahead forecasting of algal density at 24-, 48-, and 72-h horizons by minimizing quantile loss. The Adam optimizer [37] was leveraged to minimize the loss function (Supplementary Text S5). The model's optimal state with the minimum mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) value during training was saved as a checkpoint, which was later loaded and invoked during model testing and prediction to ensure consistent model performance.

Hyperparameters were optimized with Optuna using Bayesian optimization [38]. Instead of exhaustively testing every possible combination of hyperparameters via a grid search, Bayesian optimization constructs a probabilistic surrogate model of the objective function to efficiently navigate the hyperparameter space. The objective function to be minimized in the present study was the quantile loss M (Supplementary Text S4), as derived from model backtesting (Supplementary Text S6), where l is the look-back window for training and Θ represents other model-specific parameters.

A Gaussian process surrogate model was used to approximate the objective function, predict performance across different hyperparameter sets, and quantify prediction uncertainty. An acquisition function guided the selection of the next hyperparameter set to evaluate by balancing exploration of uncertain regions with exploitation of promising configurations. This function helped determine which points in the hyperparameter space should be sampled next to most efficiently identify the optimum. After each evaluation, the surrogate model was updated with new information, thereby progressively improving its approximation accuracy. The optimization process (as detailed in Supplementary Text S6) can be expressed as follows:(11)(λ∗,Θ∗)=argminλM(λ)where λ∗ and Θ∗ represent the optimal hyperparameter values. The resulting optimized hyperparameter configuration was adopted for all subsequent BloomNet experiments (Supplementary Table S3).

Model evaluation and baseline models

We used widely used metrics, including MAPE, MSE, MAE, root mean squared error (RMSE), and coefficient of determination (R2), to evaluate model performance. These metrics were applied to the test set to simulate real-world forecasts, with lower values indicating greater accuracy for the error metrics and higher values indicating a better fit for the R2. We employed a direct multi-step forecasting approach with a sliding-window evaluation, in which models predict 24-, 48-, and 72-h horizons simultaneously. Details of metric calculation and aggregation are provided in Supplementary Text S7.

To assess BloomNet's performance, we applied three widely established deep learning architectures as baseline models: LSTM, transformer, and temporal convolutional network (TCN). These baselines were chosen for two reasons. First, they offer complementary time-series inductive biases that span the canonical recurrent, attention-based, and convolutional paradigms. LSTM is a recurrent neural network designed to capture long-range dependencies while mitigating vanishing gradients [39]. The transformer uses self-attention [34] mechanisms to process sequential data and capture long-range dependencies without recurrence. TCN relies on dilated causal convolutions with residual connections to represent multi-scale temporal patterns [40]. Second, recent studies report improved short-horizon algal dynamics forecasts with these architectures [11,[24], [25], [26],28].

A key distinction between BloomNet and the baseline models is that BloomNet can incorporate both past and known future covariates, whereas the latter uses only past covariates. For all models, algal density served as the target variable. To ensure a fair comparison, all physicochemical parameters and Chl a were configured as past covariates in the baseline models. All models underwent identical training and evaluation protocols, including use of the same observational data split, normalization, early stopping criteria, and hyperparameter optimization (Supplementary Text S8 and Table S3). The model performance was assessed using the metrics described above (MAPE, MSE, MAE, RMSE, and R2).

Results

Overall predictive performance of BloomNet

We prioritized the relative error (MAPE) for algal density evaluation, complemented by absolute metrics (MAE, MSE, and RMSE), given that both over- and underestimation present ecological risks. Relative metrics better represent the significance of predictions across orders of magnitude. For instance, an error of 50 cells L−1 represents a 50% relative deviation when the observed value is 100 cells L−1, but a much smaller relative deviation when algal density is high.

Across the 24-, 48-, and 72-h forecast horizons, BloomNet showed a strong balance between short-term accuracy and longer-horizon robustness compared with LSTM, transformer, and TCN baselines (Fig. 3a). BloomNet achieved the lowest errors for the 24-h prediction (MAPE = 25.7%, MAE = 0.048, MSE = 0.006, RMSE = 0.077). For the 48-h prediction, BloomNet (MAPE = 31.8%) slightly trailed LSTM (MAPE = 31.6%) but outperformed the transformer (MAPE = 32.5%) and TCN (MAPE = 38.4%). In forecasting the algal density over the next 72 h, BloomNet maintained the lowest errors (MAE = 0.070, MAPE = 34.6%, MSE = 0.011, RMSE = 0.105). Prediction errors increased with forecast horizon for all models, reflecting growing uncertainty. However, BloomNet showed the slowest increase in relative error, indicating more stable and robust accuracy. Step-wise R2 analysis corroborated this pattern: first-step R2 values of 0.78, 0.72, and 0.76 for the 24-, 48-, and 72-h forecasts, respectively, indicating that BloomNet explains over 70% of the algal density variance at short lead times, while full-window averages of 0.68, 0.45, and 0.47, respectively, reflect the expected decline as errors accumulate over extended horizons (Supplementary Table S5). The full-window R2 aggregates performance across all lead times, including the most challenging far-horizon steps, thereby representing a more stringent evaluation than the single-step assessments. Despite this, BloomNet's multi-step performance remains highly competitive against even recent algal-bloom warning models specifically designed for single-step or short-term prediction (Supplementary Table S6).

Scenario-based quantile forecasting and uncertainty assessment

BloomNet incorporates quantile forecasting to support tiered, risk-informed decision-making for aquatic ecosystem management. The 50th percentile forecast reflects typical conditions and serves as a reference for routine monitoring. The 90th percentile captures upper-bound scenarios, flagging potential escalation of algal blooms even when median values remain below the bloom threshold. Increasing 90th percentile values signal an elevated risk, warranting preemptive action. The 10th percentile provides a conservative lower bound, with elevated values suggesting a persistent bloom risk even under optimistic assumptions.

We evaluated quantile forecasting performance by analyzing the initial and final steps of each multi-step prediction (e.g., the 1st and 24th hour in a 24-h forecast). In the first step, the 50th percentile closely tracks the observed dynamics across forecast horizons, with most values falling within the 10th–90th percentiles (Supplementary Fig. S3). Final-step predictions provide a stringent test of predictive capability. For the 24-h horizon, the median predictions aligned well with the observations. However, the accuracy decreases at the 48- and 72-h horizons, particularly during the peak bloom period in July–August 2022. Despite this decline, the 90th-percentile predictions still captured the general trends in bloom escalation and decline.

The identified errors may stem from inadequately modeled environmental disturbances or from insufficient extreme-event samples in the training data. Prediction accuracy naturally decreases with longer lead times due to error accumulation in multi-step forecasting. Although BloomNet partially reduced lag effects, delayed responses occurred near the turning points, especially at longer horizons. For instance, in early August 2022, the prediction lagged behind the actual data (Fig. 3b, red dot). Such lags are common in machine-learning- and deep-learning-based time-series forecasting [41,42] and underscore the need for improved modeling of shifts in dynamic patterns during ecological transitions. Currently, BloomNet incorporates only a limited set of known future covariates, suggesting that including additional forecast variables, such as solar radiation, wind speed and precipitation, would enhance the model's responsiveness to sudden bloom onsets or collapses.

Average environmental drivers’ importance and temporal patterns

The dominant drivers (what) and their temporal memory (when) together explain why predictions vary with lead time and risk level (Fig. 4). This interpretation can support water-quality management by identifying variables that require priority monitoring and the time windows over which they most strongly inform forecasts. As quantified by BloomNet's variable-selection weights, the dominant predictors shift with the forecast horizon (Fig. 4a). For the 24-h forecast, water temperature carries the highest weight across quantiles (0.359–0.640), directly regulating algal metabolism and short-term growth [43,44]. Chl a ranks second, with weights increasing from 0.100 at the 10th quantile to 0.333 at the 90th quantile. Chl a [33,45] reflects photosynthetic efficiency and algal activity, while algal density represents cumulative biomass, lagging behind the Chl a [46,47]. While historical target values typically impact predictions to a significant extent [11], past algal density had a low weight in the multi-step forecasts, likely because past Chl a data adequately capture algal density trends, reducing BloomNet's reliance on historical algal density data.

At the 48-h horizon, slower-changing drivers become more important. TP is the most influential driver across all percentiles (0.316–0.689), indicating that medium-term forecasts increasingly rely on nutrient availability, particularly in high-risk scenarios. pH becomes the second-most important variable (0.043–0.298). This significance stems from the pH value's direct impact on algal physiology and metabolism over medium time scales, given that algal growth is generally favored within a pH range of 6.5–9.0 [48,49]. For the 72-h prediction, DO dominates as a predictor (0.234–0.520) by serving as a critical proximal indicator of pre-bloom photosynthetic activity. Surges in the DO saturation signal the incubation phase before visible biomass accumulation. BloomNet leverages this metabolic signature to anticipate the onset of blooms three days in advance [50,51]. IMn shows increasing importance (0.108–0.380), highlighting how long-term forecasts depend on composite indicators of organic contamination [52,53].

The attention mechanism reveals temporal influence patterns (Fig. 4b), with the highest weights assigned to the most recent inputs across all horizons. Full attention distributions are shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. For the 24-h forecasts in particular, the attention distribution shows a steep increase in the final hours leading up to the prediction time. Higher-quantile forecasts, particularly the 50th and 90th percentiles, show more pronounced attention spikes near the prediction point than the 10th percentile, indicating that extreme-event predictions primarily focus on recent fluctuations, whereas baseline conditions draw more evenly from historical data.

Discussion

Threshold-based classification versus multi-parameter identification of algal bloom

To investigate whether algal dynamic forecasting can be effectively used to provide an early warning of HABs, we compared two algal bloom classification schemes. The criteria used serve solely as post hoc classification tools applied to the model outputs, independent of model training. Moreover, their comparison was intended to evaluate BloomNet's early warning performance from complementary ecological perspectives. Criterion 1 uses a fixed algal density threshold (108 cells L−1) [54] to label observations as algal blooms, whereas Criterion 2 is an expert-derived ecological benchmark that integrates multiple water quality parameters and observation records. If a serious algal bloom occurs, it is labeled as 1; otherwise, it is labeled as 0. Comparing Criterion 1 with Criterion 2 reveals structural limitations in threshold-based approaches (Supplementary Fig. S5). Criterion 1 misses many blooms identified by Criterion 2 (recall = 0.28–0.34) and shows minimal alignment (precision = 0.09–0.11), despite an overall accuracy of 0.90. These discrepancies arise because in situ optical measurements are vulnerable to environmental interference from water-depth variations, surface turbulence, and non-algal particulates that affect scattering and absorption properties [28,29]. Moreover, spectral responses can vary significantly among phytoplankton species, even at equivalent cell concentrations, due to differences in pigment composition and cellular structure [55]. Microcystis aeruginosa, for example, absorbs strongly near 620 nm via phycocyanin, whereas diatom-dominated assemblages exhibit peak absorption near 490 nm via fucoxanthin, making a single density threshold insufficient to capture bloom severity across species. Fixed thresholds therefore both under-detect biologically relevant blooms and over-report non-threatening fluctuations. While BloomNet performs well under Criterion 1, the limited agreement with Criterion 2 reflects this structural mismatch, suggesting that early warning systems should shift toward event-centered prediction frameworks that incorporate multiple parameters.

To further assess robustness, we stratified the test set predictions by month and by bloom condition (Supplementary Table S7-S8). BloomNet shows broadly comparable performance across months, with the all-step average MAPE ranging from 18.09% to 41.06% across horizons. Similar patterns were observed for the first-step predictions. In the bloom-stratified analysis, non-bloom periods were generally easier to forecast than bloom periods, with all-step average MAPE values of 23.37–26.08% for the non-bloom samples versus 23.37–41.43% for the bloom samples across horizons and criteria. The transition periods exhibited the largest errors in most cases, reaching 35.19%, 45.63%, and 43.60% at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, respectively, under Criterion 1. These results suggest that BloomNet captures seasonal variability reasonably well, although abrupt bloom transitions remain more difficult to predict.

These findings have two broader implications for HAB forecasting. First, overall accuracy is an insufficient benchmark for early warning systems; instead, metrics that directly quantify event-level detection performance warrant greater emphasis under the class-imbalanced conditions typical of bloom datasets. Second, the structural mismatch between threshold- and expert-based classification underscores the need to move toward multi-parameter, event-centered frameworks.

BloomNet's interpretability when compared with traditional explainable AI approaches

BloomNet's intrinsic interpretability mechanisms extend beyond those of both traditional post hoc methods (SHAP, LIME) and standard transformer implementations. While post hoc approaches separate explanation from prediction, typical attention mechanisms only rank the average variable influence, BloomNet integrates interpretability directly into forecasting, thereby enabling real-time variable attribution, explicit temporal attention patterns, and differentiated interpretation of bloom or non-bloom conditions.

Temporal attention patterns also differed between bloom and non-bloom conditions. For the 72-h forecast, attention scores were smoother during non-bloom periods but became multimodal during bloom events, suggesting that BloomNet drew on multiple historical intervals when predicting bloom development (Fig. 6). Similar patterns were observed for 24-h forecasts (Supplementary Fig. S6). Averaged attention-score differences between bloom and non-bloom time points, defined according to Criterion 2, further supported these event-level contrasts (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Taken together, this event-level interpretability confirms that BloomNet has learned plausible drivers of algal blooms and can inform the development of novel mechanistic algal bloom warning criteria. Variable-selection weights combine the effects of temperature, nutrients, and other factors, while fluctuations in attention scores may signal environmental instability and capture the abrupt changes that occur during blooms.

Operationalizing BloomNet for precision ecotechnology

BloomNet facilitates a transition from rigid, threshold-based monitoring to a hierarchical, risk-oriented intervention framework. By leveraging the probabilistic quantiles, we propose a four-tier warning protocol: Level 0, no warning, when the 90th percentile falls below the algal bloom threshold; Level 1, low warning, when the 90th percentile exceeds the threshold but the 50th remains below it; Level 2, medium warning, when the 50th percentile exceeds the threshold but the 10th percentile remains below it; and Level 3, high warning, when the 10th percentile exceeds the threshold. These quantile-based predictions support scenario planning by quantifying uncertainties. For example, increasing divergence between the 10th and 90th percentiles signals greater forecast uncertainty, which should prompt managers to prepare multiple contingency plans. Conversely, narrow prediction intervals with high median values suggest high-confidence bloom forecasts that require immediate action. This statistical approach aligns with modern risk management practices in water resource administration, where probabilistic forecasts enable cost–benefit optimization of intervention strategies. For instance, a Level 1 warning may prompt low-cost preparatory measures such as increased monitoring frequency, whereas a Level 3 warning justifies immediate and resource-intensive interventions such as algaecide application or water intake suspension.

Beyond risk-level classification, BloomNet's forecast horizons map onto distinct management action windows. For instance, the 24-h forecast supports emergency response, such as issuing public advisories and suspending water intake, with its relatively low error remaining compatible with threshold-based decision triggering. The 48-h forecast provides sufficient lead time to mobilize personnel and resources, while the 72-h forecast, despite greater uncertainty, remains informative for lower-cost preparatory actions, where the consequences of false alarms are modest relative to those of missed detections. This cost asymmetry supports the use of upper-quantile predictions for operational decisions.

BloomNet's interpretability identifies the dominant input variables and temporal patterns driving bloom dynamics, enabling targeted responses. In the case of Datong Lake, managers should prioritize approaches based on the dominant variables, such as enhancing monitoring networks when temperature drives short-term predictions, addressing potential nutrient sources when nutrients dominate medium-term predictions, and focusing on circulation patterns for longer forecasts. Temporal attention patterns can serve to further refine intervention timing by highlighting critical environmental transition periods. The multimodal attention distributions prior to bloom events serve as early warning indicators, revealing optimal intervention windows. When attention spikes appear 48 h before a predicted bloom, alongside significant changes in variable-selection weight, managers can implement preventive measures, thereby transforming reactive management into proactive intervention and reducing the harmful impacts of algal blooms.

Limitations and future research directions

Despite its potential for risk-based decision-making and built-in interpretability, BloomNet has several limitations. First, the ecological interpretations and driver importance values utilized in this study are site-specific and so cannot be directly generalized to other lakes. BloomNet may be transferable as a modeling framework, although its parameters and feature relations would need to be retrained and revalidated using local data from the target system. Future studies should validate this transferability across lakes using different climatic and trophic characteristics. A comparison of BloomNet with existing HAB forecasting frameworks across five key dimensions is provided in Supplementary Table S10. Second, the observed water temperature served as a proof of concept for known future covariates. The ablation study confirmed that incorporating the future context mitigates error accumulation (Supplementary Table S4), validating the potential for broader meteorological integration. Third, while the variable selection and attention modules used in this study enhanced the interpretability, they do not establish causality. Future studies should combine our approach with domain expertise or causal models to validate the identified drivers.

Additional limitations arise from the monitoring design and data structure. This study relied on single-station observations and did not explicitly model the spatial heterogeneity of algal blooms. Single-point monitoring may not fully capture the distribution and/or propagation of the bloom across a water body. Future studies could incorporate multi-station networks, spatial modeling methods (e.g., graph neural networks or spatiotemporal convolutions), and satellite remote sensing data to jointly characterize bloom dynamics across both temporal and spatial dimensions. High-frequency automated monitoring may introduce measurement errors and noise; therefore, regular sensor calibration and periodic model re-training are recommended to maintain the forecast reliability. Finally, the uneven distribution of bloom events in the training data also limited the model's ability to recognize rare bloom patterns, suggesting that future research should address this imbalance to improve the model's robustness. The discrepancy between threshold-based and expert-based bloom definitions further indicates that future early-warning systems should move beyond single-value alarms towards multi-parameter, event-centered criteria to improve bloom detection.

Conclusion

This study shows that BloomNet can support risk-based HAB forecasting by combining multi-horizon prediction, uncertainty quantification, and model interpretability. By incorporating both historical observations and known future covariates, BloomNet achieves high accuracy and robust performance for 24- to 72-h forecast horizons. The generation of probabilistic predictions with quantified uncertainty would enable water resource managers to implement graduated response strategies based on confidence levels rather than binary alerts.

BloomNet's intrinsic interpretability reveals that environmental drivers differ significantly between bloom and non-bloom conditions, with temperature and nutrient variables being more important during pre-bloom periods. The temporal attention mechanism further identifies critical forecasting windows 24 h before bloom events. This transparency validates the model's reliability by aligning with established ecological principles and builds stakeholder trust by making the “black box” decision process accessible to non-technical users.

BloomNet's modular design enables scalable adaptation to diverse data and ecological contexts, thereby paving the way for integration with remote sensing products and the expansion of meteorological forecasts. Such flexibility supports broader operational deployment across varying freshwater ecosystems, potentially transforming how communities monitor water quality and respond to increasingly frequent ecological threats. By providing transparent, uncertainty-aware HAB forecasting, BloomNet demonstrates the potential of deep learning to support practical environmental management in freshwater ecosystems.
