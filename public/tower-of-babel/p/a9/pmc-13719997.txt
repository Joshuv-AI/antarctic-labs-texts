Digital health interventions for HIV-related sexual risk behaviors: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Jing Ji, Yun Zhang, Xingliang Zhang, Jiaye Liu, Junfang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHIV-related sexual risk behaviors (e.g., condomless anal intercourse, inconsistent condom use, multiple sexual partners) remain a major driver of HIV infection globally. Digital health interventions (DHIs), ranging from SMS and mHealth to AI-driven programs, offer promising strategies to modify these behaviors. We systematically reviewed the comparative effectiveness of DHIs targeting HIV-related sexual risk behaviors. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in five databases, including the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus, with the search date up to March 4, 2026. Based on a network meta-analysis of 35 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 33,183 participants, SMS or phone call interventions were associated with the largest reductions in unprotected anal intercourse prevalence (OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.04–0.79) and inconsistent condom use frequency (MD −3.49, 95% CI −5.17 to −1.81). eHealth was associated with a significant reduction in the number of sexual partners (MD −0.28, 95% CI −0.52 to −0.03). Subgroup analyses indicated that follow-up duration significantly modified intervention effects for inconsistent condom use frequency and the number of sexual partners, with mHealth showing the most favorable relative ranking over longer periods ( ≥ 6 months). These findings suggest the potential of specific digital modalities, particularly SMS or phone call and eHealth, to complement comprehensive HIV prevention frameworks. However, the majority of network comparisons were supported by low or very low certainty evidence, indicating that future high-quality trials may alter these rankings.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Sexual transmission remains the predominant route of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection worldwide, accounting for over 70% of all new infections1. This predominance is largely attributable to the persistently high prevalence of HIV-related sexual risk behaviors (SRBs), including engaging in condomless sexual intercourse and having multiple or concurrent sexual partnerships2. These persistent behavioral patterns continue to undermine global efforts to end AIDS as a public health threat, highlighting the need for effective behavioral strategies. Although evidence has shown that pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and viral suppression (U = U) could effectively reduce the risk of HIV transmission associated with condomless sex, behavioral interventions remain critical for populations with limited access to or suboptimal adherence to these biomedical strategies3. Furthermore, unlike biomedical approaches that directly target the virus, reducing HIV-related SRBs confronts complex challenges. The core of this difficulty lies in the deeply personal, culturally sensitive, and often stigmatized nature of sexuality. This challenge is particularly evident in traditional face-to-face counseling or public health consultations, where individuals may feel judged, uncomfortable, or concerned about confidentiality breaches, leading them to withhold information or avoid health services4.

Digital health interventions (DHIs) have emerged as a promising strategy to precisely target HIV-related SRBs. These approaches leverage digital, mobile, and wireless technologies such as eHealth, mHealth, artificial intelligence (AI), short message service (SMS) or phone calls, and multimodal platforms to improve health outcomes5,6. Unlike conventional face-to-face counseling, DHIs provide a discreet and highly accessible platform that effectively mitigates the stigma and privacy concerns often associated with seeking sexual health services7. Digital tools could reduce HIV-related SRBs by enhancing motivation, facilitating goal-setting and self-monitoring (e.g., condom use), and delivering personalized behavioral feedback8. The burgeoning field of digital health has produced a multitude of intervention studies targeting HIV-related SRBs. A growing body of literature has explored various DHIs to promote condom use9, increase HIV testing frequency10,11, and reduce multiple sexual partnerships12. Collectively, these studies suggest that targeted digital strategies and personalized feedback can effectively facilitate uptake of preventive behaviors among diverse populations13.

Despite these promising findings, current evidence syntheses have several critical limitations that restrict their applicability to precise public-health decision-making. Firstly, most conventional meta-analyses have only performed pairwise comparisons (e.g., digital intervention vs. control), treating “digital health” as a homogeneous category14,15. This approach overlooks the substantial heterogeneity among interventions, which range from simple SMS reminders to complex AI-driven multi-modal platforms. Consequently, it remains unclear which specific type of digital intervention is most effective for which behavioral outcome. Secondly, with the rapid evolution of digital technologies, newer and more advanced interventions continue to emerge16. Therefore, a comprehensive synthesis that can compare these diverse modalities within a unified framework is needed.

To address these gaps in the existing literature, we conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA)17. The primary objective of this study was to systematically identify, evaluate, and synthesize evidence from RCTs examining the efficacy of various DHIs in reducing HIV-related SRBs. Our aim was not only to determine whether these interventions are effective overall, but also to identify which specific intervention is most effective in promoting safer sexual practices. By comparing different DHI patterns, this study aims to provide comparative evidence to inform the selection and design of future digital behavioral interventions.

Results

A total of 915 records were identified through database searches. After screening and eligibility assessment, 35 RCTs published between November 2008 and March 2025 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the NMA (Fig. 1).

Study selection and characteristics

Figure 2 shows the network structure of the included interventions. Network plots for different outcomes are provided in Supplementary Information 4. In total, 33,183 participants were enrolled. The mean participant age across studies was 28.79 years. The predominant study populations were MSM (37%, n = 13) and youths (29%, n = 10), with North America being the most common study region (48%, n = 17). DHIs modalities included SMS or phone call (n = 12), mHealth (n = 13), eHealth (n = 8), AI-driven (n = 1) and combined (n = 3) interventions. Detailed study characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Information 3. Of the 35 included RCTs, 23 (66%) studies reported the effects of digital interventions on reducing the prevalence of HIV-related SRBs. Specifically, five examined the prevalence of unprotected anal intercourse (UAI), 18 studies evaluated the prevalence of inconsistent condom use (ICU). In addition, 14 (40%) studies reported the impact of DHIs on cumulative exposure to HIV-related SRBs, including five on the frequency of condomless anal intercourse (CAI), seven studies on the frequency of ICU, and four on the number of sexual partners (NSP). Furthermore, two studies simultaneously assessed both the prevalence (UAI and ICU) and cumulative exposure (NSP) outcomes. Network diagrams for each outcome are provided in Supplementary Information 3.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias evaluations indicated generally acceptable conduct for randomization (18 studies rated low risk), deviations from intended interventions (17 low), missing outcome data (30 low), outcome measurement (22 low), and selection of the reported result (31 low). After integrating domain judgements, 10 studies were rated overall as low risk of bias, 14 as some concerns, and 11 as high risk. Given that 25 of 35 studies were judged as high risk or with some concerns, network estimates should be interpreted with caution. Full domain-level details are provided in Supplementary Information 5.

Efficacy of DHIs on the prevalence of HIV-related SRBs

Five studies (14%) assessed the effect of DHIs on the prevalence of UAI18–22. Compared with the active non-digital control, all DHIs including SMS or phone call (OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.04–0.79)19, eHealth (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.17–0.99)18, mHealth (OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.33–0.60)20,21, and AI (OR 0.28, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.60)22 based intervention were associated with a reduction in UAI prevalence. When compared with passive control, SMS or phone call (OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.11–1.77)19 and AI-based intervention (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.39–1.20)22 resulted in a non-significant reduction in UAI prevalence (Fig. 3). The results of pairwise comparisons are detailed in Supplementary Information 6 (Table S10 and Fig S7). Based on the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) rankings, SMS or phone call interventions showed the highest relative ranking (SUCRA 85.6%); the probability of being the best treatment (PrBest 66.7%) and mean rank (best average rank 1.7) analyses were consistent with this pattern (Fig. 5, Panel A). Full details are provided in Supplementary Information 7 (Table S15).

Eighteen studies (51%) reported data on the effects of DHIs on the prevalence of ICU23–40. Compared with either passive control or active non-digital control groups, none of the digital interventions showed a statistically significant reduction in ICU prevalence (Fig. 3). The results of pairwise comparisons are detailed in Supplementary Information 6 (Table S11 and Fig S8). These rankings should be interpreted with caution, as they indicate relative performance trends rather than conclusive evidence of efficacy. Efficacy of DHIs on the frequency of HIV-related SRBs

Five studies (14%) evaluated the effect of DHIs on the frequency of CAI41–45. Compared with passive control or active non-digital control, none of the digital interventions demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in CAI frequency (Fig. 4). The results of pairwise comparisons are detailed in Supplementary Information 6 (Table S12 and Fig. S9). According to SUCRA results, SMS or phone call interventions showed the highest relative ranking (SUCRA 68.6%), followed by mHealth (SUCRA 57.0%) (Fig. 5, Panel C). Seven studies (20%) assessed the effect of DHIs on ICU frequency46–52. Compared with active non-digital control, both SMS or phone call (MD −3.49, 95% CI −5.17 to −1.81)46,47 and mHealth (MD −1.49, 95% CI −2.83 to −0.15)48,50–52 interventions were associated with significant reductions in ICU frequency (Fig. 4). Detailed pairwise comparisons results are provided in Supplementary Information 6 (Table S13 and Fig. S10). In terms of comparative ranking, all analyses consistently identified SMS or phone call as the highest-ranked intervention in reducing ICU frequency (Fig. 5, Panel D). Full details are available in Supplementary Information 7 (Table S18).

Four studies (11%) reported the effect of two types of DHIs on the NSP18,37,42,50. Compared with active non-digital control, eHealth interventions were associated with a significant reduction in the NSP (MD −0.28, 95% CI −0.52 to −0.03)18,37 (Fig. 4). The results of pairwise comparisons are detailed in Supplementary Information 6 (Table S14 and Fig. S11). In terms of comparative ranking, eHealth showed the highest relative ranking for reducing the NSP across the available analyses (Fig. 5, Panel E). Full details are available in Supplementary Information 7 (Table S19). Given that several outcome networks were informed by a limited number of trials (five for UAI prevalence, five for CAI frequency, four for NSP, and a single trial for the AI-driven node), the SUCRA and PrBest values reported throughout should be interpreted as relative probability estimates rather than as definitive evidence of clinical superiority.

Subgroup and meta-regression analyses

The test for subgroup differences by follow-up duration ( < 6 months vs ≥6 months) was significant for ICU frequency (Coeff: −2.887, 95% CI −4.105 to −1.668, p < 0.001) and the NSP (Coeff: −0.887, 95% CI −1.201 to −0.573, p < 0.001), but not for UAI prevalence (p = 0.631), ICU prevalence (p = 0.490), or CAI frequency (p = 0.287). For the two outcomes with a significant subgroup difference, mHealth showed the highest relative ranking in the long-term ( ≥ 6 months) subgroup (ICU frequency, SUCRA 78.2%; NSP, SUCRA 91.4%). Stratification by duration also markedly reduced the heterogeneity observed for ICU frequency (e.g., I²=71.4% for mHealth vs passive control) in the main analysis, indicating improved model consistency within subgroups. Full subgroup results are provided in Supplementary Information 8.

Based on the network meta-regression analyses, country was a significant moderator for ICU prevalence (Coeff: −0.216, p = 0.005), CAI frequency (Coeff: −0.375, p < 0.001), and the NSP (Coeff: −0.290, p < 0.001). Average age showed no significant associations with any outcomes. Control group category significantly moderated UAI prevalence (Coeff: −0.687, p = 0.006), but was not a significant moderator for the other outcomes. HIV status at baseline significantly moderated CAI frequency (Coeff: −0.625, p = 0.004) and the NSP (Coeff: −0.534, p = 0.010). Full meta-regression results are provided in Supplementary Information 9.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis based on the reclassified intervention framework revealed that the findings were consistent across most outcomes, supporting the robustness and reliability of the study’s conclusions. Notably, for CAI frequency, new significant findings emerged for eHealth (MD −2.02, 95% CI −2.63 to −1.41) versus passive control, and eHealth versus active non-digital control (MD –2.39, 95% CI −3.05 to −1.73). For ICU frequency, the reclassified framework showed additional statistically significant effects for SMS or phone call (MD −2.06, 95% CI −2.46 to −1.66), eHealth (MD −0.23, 95% CI −0.24 to −0.22), mHealth (MD -1.22, 95% CI –1.43 to −1.01) and combined (MD −1.89, 95% CI −2.29 to −1.49) compared with passive control. Full sensitivity results are available in Supplementary Information 10.

Inconsistency and heterogeneity

No significant global inconsistency was found across all outcomes (p > 0.05). Heterogeneity was generally low or non-significant in most direct comparisons. Notably, substantial heterogeneity was found in CAI frequency between SMS or phone call interventions and passive control (I² = 98.7%, p < 0.001), while moderate but non-significant heterogeneity was observed in ICU frequency for mHealth versus passive or active non-digital controls (I² = 71.4%, p = 0.06; I² = 53.6%, p = 0.14). Many comparisons could not be assessed for heterogeneity due to including only single studies. These findings should be interpreted with caution, and further investigation via subgroup analysis or meta-regression is recommended to explore sources of heterogeneity. Full details are available in Supplementary Information 11.

Publication bias and certainty of evidence

Funnel plots and Egger’s test did not indicate significant asymmetry for most outcomes. However, these analyses were heavily underpowered due to the limited number of trials within specific outcome networks. The full details are in the Supplementary Information 12. According to the CINeMA assessment, comparisons between combined interventions and passive control for the prevalence of ICU were rated as having moderate-certainty evidence. Similarly, for ICU frequency, the comparisons of active non-digital control versus combined interventions, active non-digital control versus passive control, mHealth versus passive control, combined interventions versus passive control, and combined interventions versus mHealth were supported by moderate-certainty evidence; for the NSP, the comparisons of active non-digital control versus eHealth and of mHealth versus eHealth were similarly supported. In contrast, all other comparisons of controls with the remaining interventions across the evaluated outcomes were assessed as having low to very low certainty. The full details are in the Supplementary Information 13.

Discussion

This network meta-analysis synthesizing evidence from 35 randomized controlled trials provides a comprehensive assessment of the comparative efficacy of DHIs in reducing HIV-related SRBs. Overall, digital interventions demonstrated measurable behavioral benefits across multiple modalities. SMS or phone call interventions showed the highest relative ranking for reducing the prevalence of UAI and the frequency of ICU. eHealth interventions showed the most favorable ranking for reducing the NSP. These findings highlight the potential of accessible, scalable, and low-cost digital strategies to complement biomedical HIV prevention efforts and strengthen behavioral components of comprehensive HIV care.

Our findings may be interpreted alongside a recent network meta-analysis by Zhu et a.l53. Whereas that study focused on youth and adopted a broader definition of safer sexual behaviors, our review focused specifically on HIV-related behavioral outcomes; thus, the two analyses provide complementary perspectives. The larger number of youth-focused studies included by Zhu et al. likely reflects their broader eligibility criteria, including outcomes beyond directly measured behavioral risk indicators. Notably, both analyses suggest that communication-based interventions, such as telecommunication or text messaging based approaches, may represent promising strategies for promoting safer sexual behaviors. Furthermore, our results complement the conclusions of Berendes et al.54. They reported that text messages increased STI/HIV testing rates but had little effect on overall condom usage (SMD 0.02, 95% CI -0.09 to 0.14). This discrepancy likely stems from their broad focus on general STI prevention and universal condom use, which encompasses diverse populations. Similarly, a previous review focusing exclusively on eHealth interventions for MSM8 found only low-certainty evidence for mid-term sexual risk behavior reduction (d = −0.12, 95% CI −0.19 to –0.05). While that review treated “eHealth” as a homogeneous category, our network meta-analysis reveals that the effectiveness of digital interventions is not uniform, but is critically dependent on aligning the specific intervention with the targeted behavioral outcome.

The relative effectiveness of communication-based interventions may be partly explained by their low engagement barriers and capacity to provide just-in-time support19,46,47. Unlike modalities such as mobile apps or online platforms that typically require proactive login, SMS and phone calls are push-based interventions that deliver information directly to participants’ daily devices55. These might function as Ecological Momentary Interventions56, potentially providing immediate prompts during high-risk windows to enhance engagement and adherence. Furthermore, as most of the SMS or phone call interventions included in the present review were interactive, this human-centered interpersonal engagement may foster critical factors, such as trust and partnership, thereby potentially improving behavioral adherence more effectively than fully automated platforms57. Conversely, eHealth interventions showed the most favorable ranking for reducing the NSP, suggesting this complex behavioral change requires deeper cognitive processing41. eHealth platforms support deeper processing of normative beliefs, risk perception, and relationship skills through structured educational modules, making them better suited for sustained behavioral changes such as partner selection58.

Furthermore, across the majority of evaluated outcomes, our findings indicate that DHIs demonstrated greater relative effectiveness when compared with passive controls than with active non-digital controls. This pattern suggests that structured traditional non-digital approaches, including face-to-face behavioral counseling and in-person educational sessions, remain effective strategies for promoting behavior change59. Beyond technological access, such interventions may derive their effectiveness from interpersonal support, therapeutic alliance, and social accountability, which can serve as important drivers of adherence and behavior change60. In settings with low digital literacy or limited infrastructure, well-designed non-digital behavioral interventions therefore still achieve meaningful effects even without digital delivery.

The network meta-regression results indicate that participant country significantly moderated intervention effects of most outcomes. Differences in stigma58, access to services, sexual education norms, and sociolegal environments could explain why the same intervention yielded divergent effects in different settings61,62. Although SMS or phone call interventions showed the highest relative ranking overall, follow-up duration significantly modified effects for ICU frequency and the NSP. Similarly, HIV infection status at baseline significantly influenced reductions in CAI frequency and the NSP, potentially reflecting differences in risk perception and prevention motivation between HIV-positive and HIV-negative individuals63.

For the two outcomes in which follow-up duration was a significant effect modifier (ICU frequency and the NSP), mHealth showed the highest relative ranking in longer-term ( ≥ 6 months) interventions, consistent with habit formation theory, whereby mobile platforms provide the sustained support needed to maintain new behaviors64. By contrast, the short-term ranking of the AI node for UAI prevalence was not supported by a significant test of subgroup differences and, being based on a single trial, is hypothesis-generating only. The test was likewise non-significant for ICU prevalence, so the relatively high long-term ranking of passive control should not be over-interpreted.

This study has several notable strengths, primarily grounded in its rigorous methodological design. The review adhered to established guidelines for systematic reviews and network meta-analyses, ensuring methodological transparency and reproducibility. The risk of bias of individual trials was critically appraised using RoB 2.0 tool, and the overall certainty of evidence was evaluated using the CINeMA framework, together providing a structured and transparent assessment of evidence quality. Moreover, heterogeneity and robustness were systematically explored through predefined subgroup analyses based on intervention duration, as well as sensitivity and meta-regression analyses, which further strengthen the reliability of the pooled results. In addition, the study applied a comprehensive classification framework for both DHIs and sexual risk behaviors, enabling nuanced comparisons across heterogeneous intervention modalities. Finally, the inclusion of both short-term and long-term behavioral outcomes allowed for a more integrated understanding of the temporal dynamics of intervention effectiveness.

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations. First, methodological constraints, including varying risks of bias among included trials and heterogeneity in behavioral measurements (e.g., differing recall periods and partner typologies), may limit the precision and comparability of our pooled estimates. Second, the operationalization of ICU was not uniform across the included RCTs. Trials varied both in how condom use was measured (the occurrence of condomless intercourse versus inconsistent condom use) and across calendar time, as the studies span nearly two decades (2008–2025), during which PrEP and U = U transformed the clinical meaning of condomless sex. Because we extracted ICU as originally reported without retrospective reclassification, a given level of condomless behavior may carry different infection risk across trials and eras, posing a potential threat to the transitivity assumption. Findings should therefore be interpreted in the context of this measurement and temporal heterogeneity, and future network meta-analyses may benefit from stratifying on measurement type or trial era, or conducting era-specific meta-regression when sufficient evidence accumulates. Third, due to the limited availability of mechanistic evidence in the current literature and the absence of data on clinical and behavioral pathways (e.g., real-time usage metrics, response rates, and psychological mediators), our interpretations regarding the practical advantages and potential mechanisms of specific digital interventions remain speculative. In addition, the available evidence did not allow us to account for heterogeneity in user engagement, such as who responded to the intervention, the frequency of interactions, interruptions in participation, and differential access or use. These factors may meaningfully influence intervention effectiveness and should be more systematically examined in future research. Finally, the network geometry for several outcomes was sparse, with some nodes informed by very few trials and the AI-driven node informed by only a single RCT. Under these conditions, SUCRA and related ranking metrics are statistically unstable and sensitive to the random variation of individual studies, limiting the certainty of the comparative rankings. Findings concerning the AI-driven modality, in particular, should be regarded as hypothesis-generating, pending more dedicated trials.

Future empirical research should integrate real-time metrics and process evaluations to clarify exactly how digital interventions drive sexual risk behavioral change. Furthermore, scaling research to low- and middle-income countries and broader high-risk populations is essential for achieving global external validity. To overcome long-term intervention fatigue, future studies could explore innovative intervention formats, such as utilizing AI to provide dynamic and real-time support. Finally, researchers should explore synergistic strategies, such as combining the immediacy of text message reminders with the deep cognitive engagement of eHealth platforms, to optimize sustained HIV risk reduction outcomes.

Overall, this network meta-analysis synthesizes evidence from randomized controlled trials and demonstrates that DHIs can reduce HIV-related SRBs, with communication-based strategies (such as SMS or phone call reminders) and web-based eHealth programs showing the greatest promise. Ultimately, optimizing HIV prevention requires shifting from uniform interventions to strategically matching digital models with specific behavioral targets.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA-NMA reporting guidelines65 and the methodological recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Comparing Multiple Interventions Methods Group)66. The study protocol was developed following PRISMA standards and prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251122414). There were no amendments to the study protocol after registration in PROSPERO. A completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in the Supplementary Information 1.

Eligible studies were RCTs that evaluated the effects of DHIs aimed at reducing HIV-related SRBs, compared with standard care or placebo controls. Studies were excluded if they: (1) used digital technologies solely as tools for data collection or communication (e.g., social media used only for questionnaire distribution); (2) compared two or more digital interventions without a non-digital control arm; (3) employed a single-arm design; (4) focused exclusively on knowledge, attitudes, or perceptions toward HIV-related SRBs without measurable behavioral outcomes. There were no restrictions on participants’ age, gender, sexual orientation, HIV status, drug use history, or other demographic characteristics.

We systematically searched the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus for English-language studies published and indexed up to March 25th, 2025. The last surveillance was conducted on March 4th, 2026, and identified no eligible RCTs. The search strategy used both free-text terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), a standardized vocabulary developed by the National Library of Medicine for indexing biomedical topics. MeSH maps synonyms to controlled concepts, thereby improving term standardization and enhancing both the recall and precision of the search66. The complete search strategy for each database is provided in Supplementary Information 2. Additionally, we performed a manual search of the reference lists of eligible systematic reviews and included trials to identify further relevant studies.

After the database search, all retrieved references were imported into EndNote X9 for management, and duplicate records were removed prior to screening. Two reviewers (JJ and ZY) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all identified studies using standardized eligibility forms. Potentially relevant articles were retrieved in full text for further assessment. Both reviewers then independently assessed the full texts, and all inclusion decisions were cross-verified. Any discrepancies during either the abstract screening or full-text assessment stage were resolved through discussion; unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third investigator (XJF). This multi-stage screening process ensured consistent application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria and resulted in the final set of studies for data extraction and analysis.

Data analysis

Two reviewers (JJ and ZY) independently extracted data using a standardized electronic form, collecting study characteristics, participant demographics, intervention details, and outcomes. For continuous variables, data were extracted as mean and standard deviation, while event counts were recorded for dichotomous variables. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or a third reviewer (XJF). Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2.067 tool across domains including randomization, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement and selection of the reported result. Ratings of low, high, or unclear risk were assigned, with final assessments adjudicated by the third reviewer.

The primary outcome was HIV-related SRBs, including both prevalence and cumulative exposure measures. The prevalence of HIV-related SRBs reflects the proportion of individuals engaging in these behaviors during the intervention period. Two indicators were evaluated: (1) prevalence of UAI, referring to the proportion of individuals reporting CAI during the specified period68; and (2) prevalence of ICU, defined as the proportion of individuals reporting condomless or inconsistent condom use during anal intercourse over the study period69. The operationalization of this outcome varied across the included trials in two respects. First, some trials reported any occurrence of condomless intercourse, whereas others reported inconsistent (rather than complete) condom use. Second, because the trials span 2008 to 2025, a period during which PrEP (approved in 2012)70 and U = U (consensus reached in 2016)71 reshaped HIV prevention, the clinical risk attached to a given level of condomless behavior also evolved over time. To preserve comparability, we extracted ICU as originally reported by each trial, without post-hoc reclassification. Cumulative exposure to HIV-related SRBs measures the frequency or intensity of those behaviors. Three indicators were analyzed: (1) CAI frequency, representing the mean number of CAI acts during the study period; (2) ICU frequency, representing the mean number of penetrative intercourse acts without correct condom use during the study period; and (3) the NSP, defined as the average number of sexual partners, including regular, casual, and commercial partners, reported during the study period72. DHIs were categorized based on the WHO Global Strategy 2020–20256 into five groups: (1) SMS or phone call interventions, which use mobile communication technology such as one-way or interactive text messaging, voice messaging, booster phone calls, or telephone counseling; (2) eHealth interventions, which require internet connectivity and multi-device access, including social networking platforms, online programs, and web-based education packages and games; (3) mHealth interventions, which utilize mobile devices such as smartphones or tablets, including mobile applications (APP), mobile-based video conferencing, and wearable technologies; (4) AI-driven interventions, which are powered by artificial intelligence, including chatbots and virtual reality-based health games; and (5) combined interventions, which integrate two or more digital modalities, such as APP + SMS, eHealth + phone call, or other multi-component programs. Ambiguous definitions were classified by the dominant technology. Comparators included both passive controls (standard care or wait-list) and active non-digital controls. Active non-digital controls involved structured face-to-face behavioral counseling, in-person educational sessions, or the provision of non-digital printed materials. These interventions were designed to control for participant engagement and to deliver behavior change strategies without utilizing digital modalities.

Prior to synthesis, we assessed the transitivity assumption by qualitatively comparing the included trials across key clinical and methodological characteristics that could plausibly act as effect modifiers. Specifically, we examined the distribution of the target population (e.g., MSM, youth, or people living with HIV, which served as a proxy for baseline risk profile), mean participant age, study country, and intervention duration, as summarized in Supplementary Information 3. This assessment indicated that the included trials shared the common eligibility framework of evaluating DHIs against non-digital controls in populations at risk of HIV, but that clinical heterogeneity was present across target populations (predominantly MSM and youth) and geographical settings (predominantly North America). We considered the transitivity assumption to be reasonable but not fully verifiable, and we therefore complemented this assessment with subgroup analyses and network meta-regression to explore residual heterogeneity.

A frequentist random-effects NMA was conducted to compare the relative effectiveness of different DHIs across all eligible studies73. The evidence network topology was visualized using a network plot, in which node size reflected the total sample size per intervention and line thickness indicates the number of studies providing direct comparisons. Separate analyses were conducted for outcomes stratified by SRBs prevalence and SRBs cumulative exposure. Direct and indirect evidence were integrated within a coherent network framework to obtain pooled effect estimates.

Global consistency was assessed via the design-by-treatment interaction model and local inconsistency through node-splitting74, while between-study heterogeneity was quantified using I²75. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were estimated for dichotomous outcomes and mean differences (MDs) for continuous outcomes. To rank the comparative effectiveness of interventions, we calculated three complementary ranking metrics: the SUCRA, the PrBest, and the mean rank. Higher SUCRA and PrBest values indicated greater relative effectiveness. Subgroup analyses stratified results by follow-up duration ( < 6 months vs. ≥6 months). The 6-month threshold is based on the Transtheoretical Model of Behavioural Change, in which the maintenance stage is generally defined as sustained behavior change for at least 6 months, providing a theoretically meaningful distinction between short-term and sustained intervention effects76. Network meta-regression was conducted with country, average age, control group category and HIV status at baseline. Sensitivity analyses tested the robustness of findings by reclassifying ambiguous interventions. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test.

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the CINeMA platform77. To evaluate imprecision, Minimum Clinically Important Differences (MCID) were defined as a risk difference of 10% or an OR of 1.5 for dichotomous outcomes77, and 0.5 times the baseline standard deviation for continuous outcomes78. Disagreements regarding certainty assessments were resolved through discussion.

Data availability

Data from individual randomised clinical trials were extracted from publicly available articles for the specific purpose of conducting this network meta-analysis. The datasets generated and analyzed during the current study are not publicly available due to the proprietary nature of the compiled data or the copyright restrictions of the original primary studies, but are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Requests for access to the raw data of the individual trials should be directed to the responsible parties corresponding to the respective studies.

Code availability

The custom code used for this study is available from the corresponding author (xujf2019@zju.edu.cn) upon reasonable request. The analysis was performed using Stata version 17.0. The code includes specific parameters and variables used for generating the network geometry, SUCRA rankings, and consistency assessments.
