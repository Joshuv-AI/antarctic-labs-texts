Epigenetic Modulation of the ACE System Underlies the Slow Aerobic Muscle Phenotype and Metabolic Exercise Response
Authors: Flück Martin, Benedikt Gasser, Giraud Marie‐Noëlle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEpigenetic regulation of the angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE)—particularly CpG methylation within its promoter—may modulate gene expression and skeletal muscle adaptation to endurance exercise, complementing the known effects of the ACE I/D polymorphism (rs1799752). We hypothesized that ACE promoter methylation correlates with markers of aerobic metabolism and interacts with genotype and enzyme activity to explain interindividual variability in muscle oxidative capacity. 114 samples from healthy, physically diverse white Caucasian men (age: 29.5 ± 8.1 years; weight: 76.9 ± 14.5 kg) were analyzed for aerobic capacity, metabolic profiles and cellular composition of m. vastus lateralis. Molecular endpoints of the ACE system included ACE genotype, promoter methylation (23 CpG sites), mRNA expression, enzyme activity, angiotensin 2 levels, and markers of mitochondrial, glycolytic, and lipid metabolism. ACE promoter methylation in muscle was compared to the methylation in capillary blood from a second study group of 24 VO2max‐ and genotype‐matched male white Caucasian subjects. Statistical analyses comprised Pearson correlations, regression modeling (α = 0.05), network analysis, and ANOVA. Participants exhibited a wide range in VO₂max (2.1–5.4 L·min−1) and ACE promoter methylation (0.37 ± 0.18). ACE promoter methylation in muscle was higher than in blood, doubling with heart rate‐elevating physical activity > 6 h weekly and endurance exercise (p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.090 and η 2 = 0.059), and was lowest in inactive, aerobically unfit ACE I‐allele carriers. Exercise induced significant metabolic shifts, including a rise in blood angiotensin 2 from 18.1 to 45.6 pg/mL. ACE promoter methylation was inversely correlated with ACE activity (r = −0.406), angiotensin 2 (r = −0.447), and post‐exercise ACE mRNA expression (r = −0.745), and there was an influence of the ACE I/D gene polymorphism in interaction with the state of aerobically fitness (p = 0.046, η 2 = 0.297). Regression models incorporating ACE regulatory parameters significantly explained variance in slow‐twitch fiber percentage and cross‐sectional area, mitochondrial volume density, and key lipid/glucose metabolites (mean r = 0.493; power > 0.8). Seven metabolites, including acetyl‐CoA, adenosine monophosphate, and phosphocreatine, displayed opposing associations with ACE genotype and methylation, indicating divergent regulatory pathways. Notably, low promoter methylation exerted pronounced effects in ACE‐II homozygotes. ACE promoter methylation, integrated with other regulatory indexes of the ACE system, contributes to the slow aerobic muscle phenotype and its metabolic response to endurance exercise. Low ACE‐promoter methylation may override I‐allele–driven transcriptional silencing, offering a novel marker of individual training adaptation.Keywords: ACE, aerobic metabolism, angiotensin 2, endurance exercise, epigenetics, genotype, methylation, skeletal muscle

Introduction

DNA methylation, the covalent addition of methyl groups to cytosine residues—primarily forming 5‐methylcytosine (5mC) in CpG dinucleotides—is a key epigenetic mechanism regulating gene expression [1]. In promoter regions, methylation can modulate transcription by altering local DNA conformation and restricting transcription factor binding, thereby shaping gene‐specific expression programs. Typically, hypermethylation of repressive elements and hypomethylation of activating elements promote gene expression during development and differentiation [1, 2, 3]. This mode of epigenetic regulation remains dynamically responsive to environmental and metabolic cues, including nutritional status [4, 5, 6], as DNA methyltransferase activity is tightly coupled to cellular energy flux and high‐energy phosphate metabolism [7, 8, 9]. Because methylation signatures differ substantially across tissues, interpreting epigenetic regulation requires measurements in the biologically relevant compartment; peripheral blood, although frequently assessed, provides only a partial and often indirect reflection of regulatory states in metabolically active tissues such as skeletal muscle.

In skeletal muscle, CpG‐rich promoter regions orchestrate fiber‐type‐specific gene expression [10]. Hypermethylation is associated with reduced expression of genes supporting glycolytic and anaerobic metabolism (e.g., SERCA1, PFKM, ALDOA, UCP3) in slow‐twitch oxidative fibers [11, 12]. Endurance training, characterized by sustained moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity conduced repeatedly for a prolonged periods [13], induces promoter‐specific methylation remodeling, including demethylation of slow fiber genes (e.g., MYH7, MYL3) and increased methylation of transcriptional regulators such as FOXO3 and PGC‐1α, reflecting the tissue's transcriptional plasticity under metabolic stress [10, 14, 15, 16, 17]. How habitual physical activity shapes the methylation landscape of genes exerting higher‐order control over metabolic pathways remains incompletely understood [16]. Direct assessment of human skeletal muscle is therefore essential: minimally invasive biopsies enable precise characterization of tissue‐specific epigenetic regulation [18], providing insights that cannot be reliably inferred from peripheral blood methylation profiles, which capture systemic influences but only weakly mirror muscle‐intrinsic regulatory dynamics [18, 19].

Angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE), a zinc metallopeptidase of the renin‐angiotensin system (RAS), contributes to skeletal muscle metabolism, cardiovascular and neuroendocrine regulation [20, 21]. Via angiotensin 2 signaling, ACE influences glucose uptake, substrate transport, and adipogenesis [20, 22, 23]. ACE inhibition alters muscle‐to‐fat ratios and modulates mitochondrial and lipid adaptations to training [20, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31], yet the local mechanisms linking ACE activity to muscle metabolism remain unclear [25, 32, 33, 34, 35].

The ACE I/D polymorphism is associated with interindividual variability in endurance and power phenotypes [36], with the I‐allele linked to reduced ACE transcript levels and serum activity [37, 38]. This supports a model in which the I‐allele exerts a transcriptional silencing effect, potentially attenuating exercise‐induced mitochondrial and metabolic adaptations [31, 38, 39, 40, 41]. Our prior findings suggest that aerobic fitness training may override this genetic repression, as ACE transcript levels in exercised muscle differ between I‐allele carriers and non‐carriers [37, 38].

We hypothesized that ACE promoter methylation interacts with the ACE I/D genotype to regulate ACE expression and activity, thereby influencing metabolic traits associated with the slow oxidative phenotype. To test this, we quantified ACE promoter methylation and ACE I/D genotype in relation to physical activity level, aerobic fitness, and metabolic responses in vastus lateralis muscle under standardized fasting and exhaustive one‐legged cycling conditions [35, 39]. In addition, ACE promoter methylation was assessed in whole blood from aerobic fitness and ACE genotype matched subjects to provide a systemic reference for comparison with muscle specific methylation patterns. ACE promoter methylation was determined by bisulfite sequencing [42, 43], complemented by ACE I/D genotyping, muscle metabolic profiling, and detailed endurance phenotype characterization [44, 45].

Materials and Methods

Experimental Design

Samples from a first group of 114 healthy, physically able young to middle‐aged white male Caucasian volunteers from the Greater Manchester Area (United Kingdom) and the Canton of Berne (Switzerland) were analyzed. Aspects of their cellular composition of knee extensor muscle and exercise performance had in part been reported earlier [31, 38, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49]. Subjects were categorized by their weekly volume of moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity (i.e., heart‐rate–elevating exercise) as recorded based on responses to two validated lifestyle questionnaires or otherwise reported training behavior based on memory over the past year [50, 51]. Subject did not smoke and did not demonstrate contraindications to exercise vigorously. Fitness and ACE related (epi‐)genetic data on capillary blood were retrieved anonymously from a second study group of 24 male healthy and physically fit white Caucasian subjects that were part of a larger cohort of subjects, as described elsewhere [52].

Subjects of group 1 reported rested to the laboratory to collect a baseline sample from m. vastus lateralis of the typically non‐dominant leg and determine their basic anthropometry as well as aerobic capacity during a two‐legged protocol of cycle ergometry into exhaustion. In a next visit, after a period of rest and an overnight fast, a subset of 44 subjects conducted a fatiguing bout of one‐legged exercise at a power‐matched intensity. During this visit, further biopsy samples were collected from m. vastus lateralis of the exercised dominant leg, 8 h after exercise. At baseline and immediately after exercise, blood was drawn from the cubital vein to assess angiotensin 2 and metabolic substrates in blood serum.

Muscle samples were subsequently subjected to the analysis of ACE promoter methylation, the ACE I/D gene polymorphism, selected metabolic compounds and cellular composition in terms of fiber types and capillaries and selected gene transcripts ACE, subunit 1 of cytochrome C oxidase subunit 4 (i.e., COX4I1) and glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (i.e., GAPDH) as proxies for mitochondrial and glycolytic metabolism in vastus lateralis muscle, respectively [49, 53]. Aerobic capacity was determined with cycle ergometry during a ramp protocol into exhaustion.

Anthropometry

Body mass, height and age of each subject were recorded with a balance and height assessed with a stadiometer. Fat free mass was determined during a whole‐body scan in supine position with a dual energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA) using a Lunar Prodigy Advance instrument (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, Wiconson, USA).

Exercise Test for Aerobic Capacity

Participant of study group 1 completed two incremental ergospirometric tests to the limit of exercise tolerance with two and one leg. Tests were separated by at least 24 h. The protocol deployed an electrically braked cycle ergometer (Jaeger ergo‐line 2000, Germany) which was specifically adapted to meet the body dimensions of each participant.

Subjects first rested in a seating position for 5 min to determine systolic and diastolic blood pressure at rest with an automated Blood Pressure Monitor (Riester, ri‐championN, Jungingen, Germany). The exercise started with a four‐minute warm‐up period at 40 W. Subsequently, the external work rate was increased by a further 5 W every 10 s until volitional exhaustion, at which the maximal performance (Pmax) was recorded. A pedal frequency of 80 rpm was maintained throughout the exercise period. Pulmonary gas exchange was measured breath‐by‐breath (Cosmed K4b2, Italy), and heart rate was monitored throughout with a suitable device (Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland). Immediately following termination of the test, the participants were encouraged to perform an active recovery by cycling at a very low external workload. Maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) was determined from 30 s averages of recorded oxygen uptake based on established criteria [54]. Respiration exchange ratio was calculated from the ratio between 15‐s averages of recorded volumes for exhausted CO2 and inhaled O2.

Aerobic capacity in the second group was assessed using a cardiopulmonary exercise test consisting of uphill treadmill running while wearing a 12.6 kg vest, performed until volitional exhaustion. Pulmonary gas exchange was measured with a calibrated breath‐by‐breath spirometry system (MetaMax 3B‐R2, Cortex Biophysics, Leipzig, Germany), as described in detail elsewhere [52]. Prior to exercise, buccal swabs were collected in a nucleic‐acid–preserving solution (DNA/RNA Shield Collection Tube w/Swab, R1109, Zymo Research, Lucerna‐Chem AG, Lucerne, Switzerland) for ACE I/D genotyping. Approximately 200 μL of capillary blood was spotted onto FTA membranes (QIAcard FTA Classic, Qiagen, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland), dried, and stored in sealed plastic bags at 4°C–8°C for subsequent analysis of ACE promoter methylation.

Metabolic Challenge of Exercise

Subjects of group 1 arrived after an overnight fast and 2 days of reduced physical activity. Under anesthesia, a resting biopsy sample was collected from the vastus lateralis muscle of the non‐dominant leg. Capillary blood was repeatedly collected as 30 μL aliquots from a finger using a sterile blood lancet (Safety Lancet; HemoCue; Ängelholm, Sweden) into a heparinized capillary plastic tube (Polymer Technology Systems; Indianapolis, IN, USA) to analyze metabolic substrates in serum. Subjects then conducted a one‐legged exercise test with the dominant leg on a stationary cycle ergometer (Ergometrics Ergoline 800, Jaeger, Bitz, Germany) at a set cadence of 80 rpm. They performed a warm‐up at 15% of the predicted two‐legged Pmax, followed by 25 min of exercise at 30% of the two‐legged Pmax, with intensity ramped up in 10 W increments per minute until exhaustion. A 3‐min cool‐down phase at 15% of the calculated two‐legged Pmax was allowed at the end, during which capillary blood samples were again collected. VO2, VCO2, and ventilation were monitored, and VO2max and the maximal RER were determined. 0.5 and 8 h after the metabolic challenge of exercise, a further biopsy was collected.

Biopsy Collection

Biopsy samples (30–100 mg tissue) were collected by an experienced physician under local anesthesia from the maximal muscle point of thickness of vastus lateralis muscle essentially as described [38, 47, 55]. In brief, the overlying skin was shaved and sterilized (Videne Antiseptic Solution, Ecolab, Saint Paul, MN, USA). A sterile drape from a wound care pack (Premier, Shermond Bunzel Retail & Health Care Supplies Limited, Enfiled, Middlesex, UK) ensured sterile conditions. 1 mL of 2% Lidocaine was injected subcutaneously and within 5 min, a 5 mm incision was made with a scalpel, and a muscle sample was collected using a biopsy needle (TSK Acecut 11G, Emergo Europe, The Hague, the Netherlands) or a Bergstroem needle [38, 55]. Firm pressure was applied to the biopsy site until the bleeding stopped, and the wound was subsequently closed with Steri‐Strips (3 M Health Care, Germany) and dressed (Mepore Ultra, Molnlycke Healthcare, Sweden). Subjects were discharged with a pressure bandage for the first 4 h after the biopsy sample to reduce further bleeding. The collected sample was immediately processed by a split in three parts, one of which being processed for biochemical or microscopic analysis, essentially as described [44, 49]. For the light microscopic sample, one biopsy portion was mounted under a binocular on cork with Tissue‐Tek O.C.T. TM Compound (Weckert Labortechnik, Kitzingen, Germany) in an orientation so that the fiber bundle stands perpendicular to the plane of the cork, and frozen in liquid nitrogen cooled isopentane. The cork was trimmed and the sample stored airtight in a 2 mL screw cap Nunc cryotube (Sigma‐Aldrich, Buchs, Switzerland) until further processing. For the electron microscopic analysis, one biopsy portion was fixed in & glutaraldehyde until being processed. For biochemical analysis, samples were rapidly frozen while shaking the sample and stored airtight in a 2 mL tube (Nunc, M, Buchs Switzerland) until further processing.

Methylation of the ACE Gene Promoter

Cytosine methylation in the ACE promoter region (positions −456 to −255) was quantified by sequencing of PCR amplified genomic DNA after bisulfite‐conversion. The procedure was based on a previously described protocol [43] and optimized for both muscle tissue and capillary blood samples. For muscle samples, pooled 20‐μm cryosections from vastus lateralis biopsies (≈2 mm3 of tissue) were homogenized in radio‐immunoprecipitation buffer using a hand‐held Polytron homogenizer (Kinematica, Lucerne, Switzerland), followed by freeze–thaw cycles and centrifugation (5 min at 5000 g , 4°C) to enrich nuclear material in the pellet fraction [56]. Subsequently, the biological material with contained DNA was resuspended in 20 μL of sterile, nuclease free H2O (Ultrapure, DNase/RNase Free, Invitrogen). For dried capillary blood samples, DNA was extracted in phosphate‐buffered saline and isolated using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Cat. No. 69504, Qiagen, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland), essentially as described previously [57]. All DNA isolates were stored at −20°C for a maximum of 3 weeks prior to analysis.

On occasion, the DNA content was verified based on nanodrop‐based spectroscopy and the preparation was subjected to a bisulfite conversion with the EZ DNA Methylation‐Lightning kit (Zymo Research, LucernaChem, Luzern, Switzerland), following the generic protocol. This included a denaturation for 12 min at 98°C followed by a reaction in Lightning solution for 60 min at 54°C, a desulphonation step, and finally a column‐based purification in a microcentrifuge at room temperature. Half of the final purified material in 10 μL was subjected to a specific PCR reaction to amplify the unmethylated promoter region of the ACE gene with specific oligonucleotides corresponding to (forward, 5′‐TTA TGG TTT GGT GAA GAA GT‐3′; reverse, 5′‐AAA AAA ACC TCC TCT CTT TAA A‐3′) using KAPA SYBR Fast Universal Master mix (Ref# KK4601, Merck, Buchs, Switzerland) based on a specific thermal protocol (denaturation: 95C for 2 min; amplification: 55 cycles at 95°C for 15 s then 55°C for 15 s, extension: 72°C 30 s).

Figure 1 provides an example of the detection of cytosine methylation in CpG dinucleotides within the ACE promoter as isolated from vastus lateralis muscle. Reaction products were subjected to Sanger sequencing on a SEQ‐ABI02 DNA analyzer (Applied Biosystems) by a commercial provider (Microsynth, Balgach, Switzerland). Chromatograms were first inspected for consistency and visualized using the CutePeaks software (version 0.2.3, https://appimage.github.io/CutePeaks/; data assessed 26.11.2023). The degree of dinucleotide methylation was estimated for a region of the ACE promoter that had been characterized to reflect the main cis‐regulatory region [58]. The methylation at each of the 25 assessed CpG dinucleotide positions was determined based on the ratio of protected (due to methylation) and unprotected (i.e., converted) cytosine nucleotides and averaged over all assessed CpG dinucleotides to reveal the ACE promoter methylation. Towards this end we made use of a semi‐automatic algorithm (i.e., Macro) in Ms‐Excel (Microsoft Excel für Microsoft 365 MSO Version 2501 Build 16.0.18429.20132, 64 Bit) on csv‐files for sequence reads that were exported from the chromatograms. Recuring sequence anomalies at specific nucleotide positions and results were validated in instances in separate digital and analogue assessment of the respective signal intensities in the chromatograms. The coefficient of variation for repeated measurements of ACE promoter methylation was 12.3%.

Metabolic Compounds

Polar metabolites and non‐polar lipids in muscle biopsies were analyzed with MS, essentially as described [59]. For the analysis of polar compounds, 5 mg of muscle sample was extracted in a corresponding volume of cold methanol:methyl‐tert‐butyl ether:H20 = 360:1200:348, and the polar was recovered, dried down and reconstituted in 100 μL of 50 mM ammonium acetate in acetonitrile: water 9:1 (v/v). 1 μL of sample was separated across a BEH‐Amide capillary column (200 μm × 150 mm, 1.7 μm particle size; Waters, Baden‐Dättwil, Switzerland) with a gradient of ammonium acetate in acetonitrile and water using nanoAquity UPLC (ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography; Waters, Baden‐Dättwil, Switzerland). Fractions were automatically injected into the coupled Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole‐Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Reinach, Switzerland) via a nano‐electrospray ionization source with an ion fragmentation over a range of 80–1200 m/z. Mass spectroscopy (MS) data were acquired using a negative polarization at a resolution of 70 000 (MS) and 25 000 (MSMS).

Non‐polar lipids were characterized in extracts being prepared from ~15 mg of muscle sample with the help of a ball mill into cold chloroform: methanol = 1:1. The non‐polar phase was flash‐frozen, and samples were directly infused into a Thermo LTQ Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) that was operated in negative electrospray ionization mode. The MS data were acquired with settings for independent fragmentation of all ions and stepped collision energy.

Datasets were processed with Progenesis QI (Nonlinear Dynamics, A Waters Company), followed by peak picking on an aggregated ion intensity map to detect and quantify the compound ions of interest based on the consensus retention times of observed mass‐to‐charge ratio (m/z) signals for the major adduct ions in all the samples. The detected compound ions were further annotated through database searches, including the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, www.hmdb.ca), to match observed fragmentation spectra with theoretical fragmentation spectra within a tolerance of 50 m Daltons. Lipid compounds were identified by comparison to the lipidmaps Database (lipidmaps.org) for a match in the neutral mass and hydrogen adducts, with a tolerance of 70 m Daltons. Determined total electron counts for the respectively identified compounds were normalized to the average of signals of all measures for the respective compound for subsequent quantitative analyses. The adenine nucleotide energy charge was calculated as described from the signals for ATP, ADP and AMP as described [60]. Quality controls were run on individual and mixed samples to determine technical accuracy based on 20 selected compounds (amino acids, nucleotides, and metabolic intermediates) in mixed samples using Quan Browser (Xcalibur, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 61 further abundant ions using Progenesis QI software (Nonlinear Dynamics). The coefficients of variation for biological and technical replicas demonstrated values near or below 20%.

In addition, glycogen concentration in muscle biopsies was quantified in a volume of 1 mm3 tissue per total muscle protein using the Assay Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), as described [61].

Cellular Composition

The capillary‐to‐fiber ratio, the mean cross‐sectional area (MCSA) and percentage of slow (type I) and fast (type II) muscle fibers were determined in 15 μm‐thick cryosections from pre‐exercise biopsies on microscopic recordings with an Axioskop 2 system (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and subsequent quantification using the ImageJ software (version 1.6.0_33; http://imagej.nih.gov/ij), based on immunological or histochemical methods and morphometric methodology as described [44, 45, 61]. At least 24 representative fibers were analyzed per subject. In addition, the composition of muscle fibers in terms of the volume density of myofibrils, mitochondria and intramyocellular lipids was quantified using morphometric methodology on electron micrographs from frozen and/or glutaraldehyde‐fixed bioptic material as described and published in parts in other contexts [45, 47].

Substrates in Blood Serum

The concentrations of glucose, triacylglycerol (TAG), total cholesterol (T Chol), low‐density lipoprotein and high‐density lipoprotein (LDL and HDL), and total ketone (mainly bHb), were monitored in thirty microliters of capillary blood using a portable whole blood test system (CardioCheck ST Analyzer, Polymer Technology Systems; Indianapolis, IN, USA) as described [38].

Genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from bioptic material (group 1) and mucosa (group 2) as described [52, 61]. DNA was subjected to specific polymerase chain reactions with defined reagents on a magnetic induction cycler (Mic Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reactions (PCR) System, Bio Molecular Systems; Labgene, Châtel‐St‐Denis, Switzerland). The specific conditions included the use of 200 nM sense and antisense oligonucleotides in combination with a SensiFAST HRM Mix (Meridian Biosciences; BIO32005, Labgene, Châtel‐St‐Denis, Switzerland) and temperature protocols to detect the presence (I) or deletion (D) of the 287‐bp long Alu‐repeat sequence in intron 16 of the ACE gene on chromosome 17 as outlined [52, 62]. Genotypes were identified based on high‐resolution melting curves against genotype references as established in previous PCR experiments based on Sanger sequencing of the amplicons by a commercial provider (Microsynth, Balgach, Switzerland).

ACE and COX4 Transcript Expression

The transcript levels of COX4I1, ACE, and GAPDH mRNA were quantified as described previously [63]. In brief, total RNA was isolated from 5 mm3 of biopsy sample using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland) and 600 ng was reverse‐transcribed using the Omniscript RT Kit with random hexamers (Qiagen, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland). cDNAs were amplified from 6 ng of cDNA and quantified via polymerase chain reactions. The primers used were as follows: For COX4I1 (forward, 5′‐GCC ATG TTC TTC ATC GGT TTC‐3′; reverse, 5′‐GGC CGT ACA CAT AGT GCT TCT G‐3′), for ACE (forward, 5′‐TCA CTA CGG GGC CCA GCA CA‐3′; reverse, 5′‐TGC GCC CAC ATG TTC CCC AG‐3′) and for GADPH (forward primer: 5′‐GGA GCG AGA CCC CAC TAA CA‐3′; reverse, 5′‐GCC TTC TCC ATG GTG GTG AA‐3′). Reactions were run at a primer concentration of 100 nM using KAPA SYBR Fast Universal Master mix (Ref# KK4601, Merck, Buchs, Switzerland) and run on a StepOne Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Fisher Scientific, Reinach, Switzerland). The thermal protocol consisted of 40 cycles of denaturation for 15 s at 95°C, annealing, and extension at 60°C. The specificity of amplification was assessed based on melting curves versus a reference that was constituted from a dilution of a PCR sample, for which specificity was confirmed by commercial microsequencing (Microsynth, Balgach, Switzerland). Relative transcript amounts were calculated using the comparative CT method, taking the amplification efficiency for each template into account using the comparative method for the threshold cycle for target amplification, as described [63]. For each sample, transcript signals (in fluorescent counts) were related to the estimated input amount of total RNA in microgram and instances expressed per GAPDH transcript levels.

ACE Activity

The enzymatic activity of ACE per muscle protein was determined with a fluorescence‐based assay based on the conversion of Abz‐Gly‐p‐nitro‐Phe‐Pro‐OH and expressed as conversion per microgram of muscle protein and relative to the concentration of the fluorogenic substrate Abz‐Gly‐OH (Bachem AG, Bubendorf, Switzerland) in muscle homogenates as described [38, 64].

Angiotensin 2 Concentration

The octapeptide angiotensin was determined on venous blood being collected before and immediately after one‐legged exercise under ice‐collected conditions in the presence of relevant inhibitors with a commercial ELISA system as described [38].

Scaling of the Data

To enable integrative analyses of muscle composition, selected morphometric parameters were scaled using molecular proxies and pooled across datasets. Missing values for mitochondrial volume density were imputed based on a validated relationship between transcript levels of the mitochondrial marker COX4I1 mRNA (expressed as counts per μg total RNA) and morphometric estimates of total mitochondrial volume density (expressed as % per muscle fiber) in resting biopsy samples of m. vastus lateralis. Similarly, the volume density of intramyocellular lipid (VV(li,f)) was estimated by integrating morphometric data with molecular concentrations of five representative triglyceride compounds (PE 38:5, FA 14:4; O3, PI 35:3, ACer 56:1; O2, PS 40:5) as detected by mass spectroscopy. This scaling approach was established in previous studies involving measurements in biopsy samples of m. vastus lateralis muscle at rest [38, 49, 53, 65]. Muscle fiber cross‐sectional area (MCSA) was derived by combining light microscopic measurements with electron microscopic estimates. To account for tissue shrinkage induced by glutaraldehyde fixation, a correction factor of 27% was applied, consistent with established protocols for ultrastructural preservation and dimensional scaling [66].

Statistics

Statistical analyses were conducted using the JASP package (version 0.18.3; https://jasp‐stats.org/download/; 11.01.2024). Pearson's moment correlations were carried out to test linear relationships between the 8 aspects of ACE gene activity (i.e., model terms, Figure S1) versus the 102 assessed metabolic parameters as these were affiliated with 9 ontologies of general metabolic/energetic functioning (i.e., energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, ketone metabolism, substrate handling, muscle metabolism, contraction, systemic metabolism, signaling) and specific metabolic functioning (glycolysis, mitochondrial metabolism, respiration, energy metabolism, contraction, contraction per respiration, systemic metabolism, turnover, signaling). The numeric coding of the ACE promoter methylation and ACE genotype (rs1799752) for correlation analysis were according to the anticipated inhibitory influence on the expression/activity on the ACE transcript and enzyme; whereby a score of zero and one applied for a promoter methylation below or above the group average, respectively, and a score of one for each ACE I‐allele. The code for the combined effects of genotype and epigenotype was calculated by the sum of the score for promoter methylation and ACE I/D genotype. For a linear relationship to be deemed relevant the respective p values had to meet a threshold of < 0.05, categorized based on the following criteria: very strong > 0.8; strong > 0.6; moderate > 0.4; weak > 0.2. In general, weak correlations are not reported in the main manuscript. The connectivity of linear relationships was assessed for hierarchic organization and displayed using the Cytoscape software (version 3.10.1) [67]. Linear regression analysis was carried out to estimate the in‐sample model‐fit of a dependent parameter based on the 8 covariates (model terms of ACE gene activity regulation, i.e., ACE promoter methylation, ACE genotype and ACE enzyme activity, and angiotensin 2 levels). Data were anonymized and missing data were assigned as NAN for the statistical evaluation. Data are presented as Mean ± standard deviation (SD), under provision of minima and maxima. Single or interaction effects of a genotype, physical activity, or aerobic fitness (based on a VO2peak cutoff value of 50 mL O2 min−1 kg−1) [41, 54], on the assessed factors were assessed with ANOVA with respective post hoc tests of Tukey or Fisher. Effects of exercise on mRNA expression and metabolic parameters were evaluated using repeated‐measures ANOVAs. Interaction effects between genotype and promoter methylation were examined by categorizing ACE promoter methylation into two groups—higher or lower CpG methylation relative to the sample mean. Comparisons of ACE promoter methylation between capillary blood and vastus lateralis muscle were performed using a two‐factor ANOVA with the factors “tissue type” and “CpG dinucleotide number”. This analysis included 24 subjects from the second group who were matched for VO2max and ACE I/D genotype, allowing a maximum deviation of 7% in VO2max (Table S1).

Results

ACE Promoter Methylation

In order to assess methylation of promoter of the ACE gene we adapted a bisulfate sequencing‐based PCR procedure. The amplicon for the ACE promoter was detected as a broad peak, sometimes with two maxima, indicating a mix of DNA variants existed where cytosines are converted to thymidines during the bisulfate reaction. The ACE promoter exhibited a heterogeneous CpG‐methylation profile differentiating the degree of cytosine methylation for CpG site 9 from that of sites 12, 15, and 21 (Figure 2A); and summing to a mean degree of CpG dinucleotide methylation of the ACE promoter of 0.284 ± 0.088.

Association of ACE Promoter Methylation With Aerobic Physical Activity and Fitness

Variability in the mean degree of ACE promoter methylation in vastus lateralis muscle was associated with the weekly volume of moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity (p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.090). Methylation levels were approximately twice as high in subjects performing more than 6 h of heart‐rate–elevating exercise per week over the past year compared with those performing less (Figure 2B).

Consistently, ACE promoter methylation in vastus lateralis muscle was associated with habitual endurance exercise (p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.059), being 0.220 points higher in the endurance exercising subjects, and showed a trend towards an interaction with the ACE I/D genotype (p = 0.067, η 2 = 0.010). The lowest methylation levels were observed in ACE I‐allele carriers who did not engage in endurance exercise (Figure 2C). Accordingly, ACE promoter methylation was higher in aerobically fit subjects compared with unfit subjects (0.451 ± 0.139 vs. 0.195 ± 0.139; p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.297), and this effect interacted with the ACE I/D genotype (p = 0.046, η 2 = 0.297). ACE‐DD genotypes exhibited the highest degree of ACE promoter methylation overall (p = 0.009), an effect that was further accentuated among aerobically unfit individuals (Figure 2D). The ACE I/D genotype per se did not affect ACE promotor methylation (p = 0.083, η 2 = 0.033).

ACE promoter methylation in vastus lateralis muscle differed from that observed in peripheral blood in a second group of subjects matched for aerobic fitness and ACE I/D genotype (Table S1, Figure S2; p = 0.047, η 2 = 0.004). This difference reflected a higher overall proportion of ACE promoter methylation in vastus lateralis muscle compared with capillary blood (0.325 vs. 0.270). Tissue dependence further interacted with CpG site number (p = 0.010, η 2 = 0.057): muscle showed greater cytosine methylation at CpG sites 6, 9, and 24, whereas CpG site 21 was more highly methylated in capillary blood.

Discussion

This study provides novel evidence that ACE promoter methylation contributes to the regulation of skeletal muscle metabolism and aerobic phenotype, independent of and in interaction with the ACE I/D genotype. Among eight modeled aspects of ACE gene regulation—including promoter methylation, genotype, enzyme activity, and angiotensin 2 levels—ACE promoter methylation emerged as the most consistent predictor of metabolic traits associated with slow‐twitch oxidative muscle fibers.

To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to characterize CpG methylation of the ACE promoter in human skeletal muscle. Previous work on (epi‐)genetic regulation in contractile muscle tissue has primarily examined global DNA methylation responses to exercise, microRNA expression or the associations between ACE genotype and ACE activity [10, 16, 38, 68]. These approaches have provided valuable insights but offer limited resolution regarding promoter‐specific regulatory mechanisms. Our novel findings on the interplay between genetic and epigenetic aspects of ACE gene regulation in m. vastus lateralis suggest that ACE may contribute not only to vascular tone regulation but also to metabolic processes within skeletal muscle. This interpretation aligns with prior evidence linking ACE activity to systemic lipid and glucose metabolism [69, 70, 71] and broader metabolic phenotypes [22, 25, 32, 72]. Within this framework, we observed that physical activity level—particularly endurance exercise—is associated with variation in ACE promoter methylation. This relationship appears to interact with ACE I/D genotype and may help explain interindividual differences in slow‐twitch contractile and metabolic muscle fiber characteristics.

Linear correlations between ACE promoter methylation and metabolic parameters—including mitochondrial density, fiber composition, and lipid metabolites in knee extensor muscle (Figure S1)—support its role as a biomarker of the oxidative muscle phenotype. Notably, ACE promoter methylation was inversely associated with ACE activity, angiotensin 2 levels, and post‐exercise ACE mRNA expression, and there was an influence of the ACE I/D gene polymorphism in interaction with the state of aerobic fitness (Figures 2B and 4). These findings suggest that ACE promoter methylation may override genetic suppression of ACE transcription in homozygous I‐allele carriers.

Regression models revealed that ACE promoter methylation and angiotensin 2 levels in blood together explained substantial variability in aerobic performance and muscle composition (e.g., VO2max, area percentage and MCSA of slow type muscle fibers, mitochondrial volume density, as well as compounds associated with glucose (i.e., glycogen and the rate‐limiting metabolite for glucose uptake in muscle fibers, G6P [73]) and lipid metabolism (i.e., IMCL, ACer 56:1; O2; FA 14:4; O3)). These findings are consistent with previous findings in White Caucasian men [31, 38, 74].

These findings align with prior reports linking the ACE I/D genotype with conditionable metabolic traits of muscle tissue being favorable for endurance exercise in White Caucasian men [27, 28, 31, 38, 74] and suggest that methylation status may refine genotype‐based predictions. This connects to findings on the correlation of ACE promoter methylation (and ACE I/D genotype) with body mass already at birth [75, 76] and role of ACE in whole body metabolism via activity‐dependent glucose and lipid metabolism in muscle tissue [25, 31, 61, 72, 77].

In this respect, the further observed associations between ACE promoter methylation and 22 lipid species (Figure 6A)—including glycerolipids DG 35:3, which decreased post exercise, the phosphoinositol PI 35:3, and the phosphoethanolamine PE 32:0, which also correlated with blood serum angiotensin 2—highlight a novel association of ACE with lipid‐related signaling in muscle tissue [78, 79], in addition to lipid storage and β‐oxidation in skeletal muscle. Meanwhile, the lack of correlation with fat mass and associations with fat‐free mass suggest that the measured ACE methylation in vastus lateralis muscle is reflective of metabolic processes in intramuscular compartments rather than extramuscular aspects of angiotensin 2 regulated metabolism such as lipolysis in adipocytes [72]. In this respect, the relationships of total cholesterol and HDL with angiotensin 2 concentrations in blood serum and post exercise with the ACE (epi‐)genotype, highlight a regulatory role for angiotensin 2 in hepatic cholesterol release that is of interest. They suggest a potential involvement of lipase‐mediated lipid uptake into muscle cells, a process closely tied to capillary function [80].

Importantly, reciprocal relationships between ACE genotype and ACE promoter methylation were observed for several key metabolites (AMP, Ac‐CoA, G3P, PCr), indicating divergent regulatory mechanisms. In individuals with the ACE‐II genotype and low promoter methylation, metabolic responses to exercise were markedly altered, suggesting that methylation may, in some contexts, override genetic constraints on ACE expression and downstream metabolic regulation. Given that the affected metabolites are themselves tightly linked to nutritional and energetic status [81], these patterns raise the possibility of a metabolic feedback loop influencing ACE promoter methylation. Together, the findings point to a mechanistic interface between epigenetic regulation and energy metabolism, potentially mediated by nucleotide charge and S‐adenosylmethionine availability [8].

The ACE‐I/D– and methylation‐dependent regulation of aforementioned metabolites combined with the observed correlation between their concentration and the ACE transcript level suggests that ACE transcription is directly affected by the energetic status. In particular, AMP and PCr—core components of the phosphagen system—may contribute indirectly by modulating kinases that phosphorylate transcription factors in response to changes in energy charge [82]. This interpretation aligns with the linear relationships identified between ACE mRNA levels in vastus lateralis muscle before and after exhaustive one‐legged exercise and the methylation status of CpG dinucleotides located between positions −456 and −255 of the ACE promoter. To date, specific transcriptional mechanisms acting on these cis‐regulatory regions (i.e., CpGs 1–13 and 25) have not been defined. However, several trans‐acting factors warrant consideration, including stimulatory protein‐1 (Sp1) – for which binding to GC‐rich regions in the ACE promoter has been predicted for a region closer to the transcription initiation site [83], as well as AP‐2, KLF4, and CTCF [84]. SP1 has been implicated in shifting transcriptional programs towards energy conservation, in a manner reminiscent of AMP‐activated protein kinase–driven responses [82, 85, 86]. However, it remains unclear whether SP1 activity in skeletal muscle is modulated by the cellular energy state during exhaustive endurance exercise [81], or whether such regulation specifically affects ACE transcription [85].

The associations identified here between parameters of ACE gene regulation and variation in slow‐twitch fiber percentage, mitochondrial volume density, and key lipid and glucose metabolites support the view that ACE promoter methylation contributes to the regulation of still poorly understood metabolic pathways in skeletal muscle and its surrounding metabolic supply network [16]. In this context, it is notable that ACE promoter methylation and genotype also correlate with the capillary fiber ratio and capillary density of vastus lateralis muscle (Figure 6). These structural associations align with the fact that ACE mRNA and protein in human skeletal muscle are largely localized to endothelial cells lining capillaries [87], which play a central role in angiotensin 1‐to‐2 conversion [88, 89].

We observed that post‐exercise angiotensin 2 concentrations in blood were associated with capillary density (Figure 6A), consistent with prior work implicating angiotensin‐2–mediated capillary growth and findings from ACE‐inhibition studies suggesting angiotensin‐2–dependent angiogenic signaling and perfusion during exercise [69, 71, 90, 91]. These results point to post‐transcriptional regulation of ACE expression and activity in endothelial cells as a potential contributor to vascular reactivity and muscle metabolic function. This interpretation further suggests that ACE‐related vascular processes may interact with metabolic pathways reflected in muscle fiber composition and metabolite profiles. Consequently, there is a clear need to examine how endurance‐type physical activity shapes ACE promoter methylation across biological compartments, including skeletal muscle in addition to the classical cardiovascular compartment, where endothelial ACE‐producing cells remain difficult to access [71, 87]. In this context, it is notable that ACE promoter methylation differed between skeletal muscle and capillary blood in a matched cohort (Figure S2), indicating tissue‐specific CpG methylation patterns. Importantly, blood‐based measurements still yielded biologically interpretable signals.

With respect to the connection between the ACE system and the conditioning of metabolic and contractile traits in muscle tissue, it is notable that individuals who performed more than 6 h per week of moderate‐to‐vigorous activity and regularly conducted endurance exercise showed much higher ACE promoter methylation in the vastus lateralis, which interacted with the ACE I/D genotype. The identified threshold of weekly activity influencing ACE promoter methylation is close to the recommendation for moderate‐intensity cardiorespiratory exercise of ≥ 150 min per week (in sessions lasting more than 30 min) to improve and maintain metabolic fitness and health [13]. Together with the established association of the ACE system with systemic metabolic regulation [20, 21], as well as the observation of a pronounced influence of ACE promoter methylation on the muscle concentration of key metabolites post‐exercise in ACE‐II genotypes (Figure 8), these findings point to the suitability of ACE promoter methylation as a conceptual framework for personalized recommendations regarding metabolically effective exercise dose and modality. Inasmuch as ACE‐DD genotypes in skeletal muscle appear considerably less sensitive to methylation changes than I‐allele carriers, this pattern aligns with the widely accepted view of superior trainability of endurance in ACE‐I allele carriers [36, 38, 39].

Longitudinal research designs will be essential to more fully address how ACE‐I/D–associated individual differences influence the physical‐activity‐mediated regulation of ACE gene activity and related metabolic traits. Such work should include early and late time points across periods of increased or decreased activity of varying intensity. A key question is whether changes in ACE promoter methylation precede or accompany shifts in the slow‐oxidative muscle phenotype, and whether methylation at specific CpG sites—such as sites 11–13 and 23 (Figure 5C)—modulates these adaptations through interactions with trans‐acting factors [92, 93].

The potential reversibility of ACE promoter methylation also warrants investigation. Exercise‐responsive DNA methylation in human skeletal muscle is known to be dynamic and modifiable over time [14, 68, 94], raising the possibility that ACE promoter methylation may exhibit similar temporal plasticity.

Limitations of our investigations include the cross‐sectional design and use of tissue homogenates, which preclude cell‐type‐specific resolution. Nonetheless, the reproducibility of methylation measurements (coefficient of variation ~12% across replicates from ~2 mm3 muscle samples) and their robust associations with metabolic traits support the biological relevance of ACE promoter methylation in skeletal muscle.

We also acknowledge that the supplementary analysis of ACE promoter methylation in capillary blood in comparison to an independent second cohort was likewise cross‐sectional and derived from whole‐blood DNA rather than isolated leukocyte subpopulations. The dried‐blood‐spot approach, while validated for whole‐blood methylation profiling, does not permit cell‐fraction–resolved interpretation. Even so, the observed CpG‐specific differences between muscle and whole blood provide biologically meaningful indications of tissue specificity and highlight promising directions for future work on cell‐type–resolved ACE regulation.

We also note that the absence of plasma ACE activity measurements constrains the interpretation of systemic versus tissue‐specific ACE gene regulation. In this regard, the observed inversion of the relationship between ACE activity in vastus lateralis muscle and circulating angiotensin 2 concentrations—from a positive association at baseline to a negative association post‐exercise (r = 0.572 vs. –0.466)—may suggest contributions from ACE sources other than skeletal muscle [71, 89]. Future studies with dedicated sampling and analytical capacity will be required to address this question fully. Taken together, the current findings remain consistent with our hypothesis that ACE promoter methylation contributes to ACE gene regulation in skeletal muscle. They further suggest—through a mechanism whose cellular origin remains to be identified and for which we are not aware of existing literature—that this regulation is reflected in systemic angiotensin 2 responses.

In conclusion, ACE promoter methylation appears to modulate its transcriptional and enzymatic responses in skeletal muscle to exercise, correlates with oxidative muscle traits, and interacts with genotype and aerobic fitness to influence muscle's lipid and carbohydrate metabolism. Given that genetically modulated ACE activity affects muscle activity‐dependent metabolic processes in skeletal muscle through influences on capillary perfusion [69, 71] and training adaptations [27, 28], our findings position ACE promoter methylation as a candidate biomarker for personalized training adaptation and metabolic profiling in health and performance contexts, including those related to energy‐expenditure–based weight control.
