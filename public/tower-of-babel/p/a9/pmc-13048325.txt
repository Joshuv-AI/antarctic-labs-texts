Revisiting Intranasal Salmon Calcitonin: Historical Osteoporosis Evidence and a Potential Role in Acute Orthopaedic Pain Management
Authors: Areeb Ahmad, Kassem Ghayyad, Elisabeth Davis, Daniel Goltz, G. Russell Huffman, Olamide Oshikoya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
»Intranasal salmon calcitonin (IN-CAL) is a well-tolerated, noninvasive antiresorptive agent with reproducible pharmacodynamic activity, despite lower systemic bioavailability than parenteral formulations.»In preosteoporotic postmenopausal women, IN-CAL provides small, spine-limited preservation of bone mass, with minimal hip or forearm benefit; treatment response depends more on baseline bone turnover than on dose escalation.»In established postmenopausal osteoporosis, IN-CAL produces early biochemical suppression (1-3 months) and small but consistent lumbar spine bone mineral density (BMD) gains with sustained therapy (≥6-12 months).»IN-CAL is less effective than bisphosphonates and modern anabolic agents for increasing BMD and reducing fracture risk; accordingly, its use is best reserved for patients who cannot take first-line therapies due to contraindications, intolerance, or personal preference.»IN-CAL provides short-term pain relief for vertebral fractures in osteoporosis and offers a similarly time-limited analgesic benefit in acute orthopaedic settings, with pain reduction often within days and biochemical or skeletal effects evolving over subsequent weeks.

Intranasal salmon calcitonin (IN-CAL) is a peptide antiresorptive agent historically used in the management of postmenopausal osteoporosis, where early randomized trials demonstrated lumbar spine-specific increases in bone mineral density (BMD) with minimal peripheral effects1-3. In the Prevent Recurrence of Osteoporotic Fractures trial, vertebral fracture reduction was observed at 200 international units (IU) daily but lacked a consistent dose-response relationship4,5.

Subsequent head-to-head comparisons showed smaller BMD gains and less consistent fracture protection with IN-CAL than with later-generation antiresorptive therapies6,7. Accordingly, contemporary guidelines no longer recommend IN-CAL as first-line treatment for chronic osteoporosis8,9, and concerns regarding a potential malignancy signal further limited long-term use10,11.

Despite its diminished role in chronic osteoporosis management, IN-CAL has consistently demonstrated clinically meaningful analgesic effects in patients with acute osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures12,13. Pain reduction often occurs within days and may precede measurable skeletal changes14,15. Similar short-term analgesic benefits have been reported in selected acute fracture and perioperative orthopaedic settings16,17, suggesting that pain modulation represents one of the more consistent clinical effects observed with IN-CAL.

A landmark review by Plosker and McTavish summarized the pharmacologic and clinical evidence for IN-CAL through the mid-1990s18. The present review builds upon this foundation by contextualizing historical data and synthesizing subsequent evidence across 3 domains: (1) pharmacology and mechanisms; (2) established and preventive osteoporosis; and (3) acute orthopaedic applications, with particular emphasis on analgesia and time-dependent therapeutic effects.

Pharmacology, Mechanisms, and Adverse Effects

Salmon calcitonin is a synthetic peptide analog of endogenous calcitonin with higher receptor affinity, longer biologic half-life, and greater antiresorptive potency than human calcitonin19-21. Intranasal delivery was developed to improve tolerability and adherence compared with injectable formulations, though systemic bioavailability remains lower than parenteral administration18,19. Despite reduced absorption, IN-CAL demonstrates reproducible pharmacodynamic activity at clinically feasible doses22-24.

Antiresorptive Effects

IN-CAL binds high-affinity G protein-coupled calcitonin receptors on osteoclasts, activating intracellular cAMP signaling pathways that disrupt osteoclast cytoskeletal function and suppress resorptive activity25-27. With repeated exposure, IN-CAL reduces osteoclast recruitment and activity, narrowing the remodeling space and preserving trabecular microarchitecture28,29.

Although direct anabolic effects on osteoblasts have been demonstrated in vitro with calcitonin, including increased proliferation and alkaline phosphatase activity at pharmacologic doses30-33, these effects have not been consistently reproduced in vivo. This discrepancy likely reflects the indirect, osteoclast-mediated nature of calcitonin signaling in vivo, where tightly coupled remodeling dynamics and physiologic exposure conditions differ substantially from isolated, high-dose in vitro systems. Histomorphometry instead demonstrates increased trabecular bone volume and reduced resorptive surface, consistent with a net positive remodeling balance driven primarily by antiresorptive mechanisms34-36.

Clinically, biochemical suppression occurs within weeks, whereas measurable BMD changes require sustained therapy14,18,37. Across studies, response correlates more strongly with baseline turnover than dose escalation, consistent with a threshold effect near 200 IU/d38-41.

Analgesic Effects

N-CAL demonstrates analgesic effects in vertebral fracture-related pain and selected musculoskeletal conditions, often preceding measurable skeletal changes18,42,43. Proposed mechanisms include central modulation of monoaminergic and serotonergic pathways, enhancement of endogenous β-endorphin signaling, and suppression of prostaglandin-mediated nociception12,44. Although increases in circulating β-endorphin have been reported in some studies, others have failed to replicate this finding, suggesting a multifactorial mechanism24,45,46.

Intranasal administration bypasses hepatic first-pass metabolism and may permit partial direct central nervous system delivery via olfactory and trigeminal pathways47-49. Clinically, analgesic benefit has been consistently observed in both postmenopausal osteoporosis and acute orthopaedic injury settings, supporting a time-dependent framework in which pain relief may occur within days to weeks, whereas skeletal effects evolve over weeks to months18,43,50.

Adverse Effects

IN-CAL is generally well tolerated. The most common adverse effects include nasal irritation, rhinitis, epistaxis, flushing, nausea, and mild gastrointestinal symptoms18, which are typically self-limited and rarely require discontinuation. Antisalmon calcitonin antibodies may develop with prolonged use, but available evidence suggests a minimal clinical impact18.

Of greater clinical relevance is the reported association between long-term IN-CAL use and malignancy. Overman et al. reviewed the U.S. Food and Drug Administration committee data and noted that although more malignancies were reported in IN-CAL arms across multiple trials, most studies were not designed to adjudicate cancer outcomes and had poor-quality methods for cancer assessment, limiting causal interpretation10. Sun et al., in a population-based nested case-control study from Taiwan, reported mixed findings, with an observed association between IN-CAL use and liver cancer but a reduced incidence of breast cancer, further complicating interpretation and suggesting the possibility of residual confounding or chance findings11.

In addition, a meta-analysis by Wells et al. evaluating oral calcitonin concluded that although a weak statistical association cannot be entirely excluded, the overall evidence does not support a causal relationship51. Collectively, the malignancy signal associated with calcitonin is small, methodologically limited, and unlikely to represent a direct carcinogenic effect.

IN-CAL vs. Placebo or Supplementation-Only Controls

Early investigations consistently demonstrated that IN-CAL, when combined with calcium (±vitamin D) supplementation, attenuates bone loss more effectively than supplementation alone in postmenopausal women without established osteoporosis. Across multiple randomized trials conducted before 1996, IN-CAL 50 to 200 IU/d preserved or modestly increased lumbar spine BMD over 1 to 2 years, whereas calcium alone often failed to prevent bone loss, with the greatest benefit seen in early postmenopause and in women with higher baseline bone turnover1,52,53,57,58. IN-CAL's protective effect was largely confined to the lumbar spine, with minimal benefit observed at the hip or forearm57,58. Biochemical suppression of resorption markers occurred early but was not consistently sustained beyond 6 to 8 weeks14,37. Notably, bone formation markers generally remained unchanged, reinforcing IN-CAL's mechanism as primarily antiresorptive rather than anabolic14,37,52,58.

Several early 1990s trials also compared IN-CAL with placebo nasal sprays as controls. Across 4 randomized studies conducted before 1996, daily 200 IU IN-CAL attenuated lumbar spine bone loss over 2 to 3 years compared with progressive loss in placebo-treated groups, although the absolute between-group differences were small54,55,59,60. Similarly, the antiresorptive effect was most pronounced in women with higher baseline bone turnover, and daily dosing outperformed intermittent regimens of IN-CAL.

In 1996, Arnala et al. evaluated IN-CAL in perimenopausal women and found no differences in BMD or turnover markers after 3 years of 100 IU/d, likely because these women still had enough endogenous estrogen to keep bone turnover low, leaving little room for an antiresorptive effect56. By contrast, Rizzoli et al. showed that 200 IU/d IN-CAL produced a small but statistically significant lumbar spine BMD increase over 2 years in postmenopausal women with osteopenia (+1.0% vs. −0.4%, p < 0.001), with no benefit at the hip61. Collectively, trials of IN-CAL in postmenopausal preosteoporotic women demonstrate a spine-specific antiresorptive effect with greater efficacy in women with higher baseline bone turnover.

Dose-Response Studies

Early dose-ranging studies aimed to determine the minimum effective dose of IN-CAL required to suppress bone resorption. Perrone et al. evaluated 100 IU/d administered either continuously or in cyclic regimens over 1 year in women within 6 years of menopause38. Lumbar spine BMD was maintained in treated groups compared with a decline in the calcium-only control group, and bone turnover markers decreased early, without evidence that cyclic administration conferred additional benefit over continuous dosing. Later in 1994, a trial by Overgaard et al. confirmed this pattern in a 2-year trial of recently postmenopausal women39. The 200 IU and 400 IU regimens prevented lumbar spine bone loss, whereas both the 100 IU and placebo groups experienced significant declines. However, the skeletal response was essentially identical between 200 IU and 400 IU, reinforcing that IN-CAL's efficacy plateaus once a threshold dose is reached, with no observed benefits at peripheral sites.

Subsequent studies shifted focus toward biochemical specificity and baseline metabolic activity. Schlemmer et al. reported that both 100 IU and 200 IU/d significantly suppressed bone resorption, but the degree of suppression was similar between all doses40. Ongphiphadhanakul et al. further demonstrated that DPD suppression after 1 month of IN-CAL (50, 100, or 200 IU/d) was unrelated to dose but strongly correlated with pretreatment bone turnover levels41. Notably, the antiresorptive response diminished by 3 months, consistent with emerging evidence of tachyphylaxis with prolonged use. Finally, Campusano et al. tested ultra-low-frequency dosing (50 or 100 IU thrice weekly) and found no significant suppression of resorption markers over 90 days62. This finding likely reflects IN-CAL's short plasma half-life of approximately 40 to 90 minutes18, reinforcing the threshold nature of daily or near-daily administration for efficacy.

Taken together, dose-response studies indicate that IN-CAL's antiresorptive effect is threshold-dependent rather than dose-proportional, with therapeutic benefit plateauing at approximately 200 IU/d. Suppression of biochemical markers of bone resorption is typically observed within the first 4 to 8 weeks of therapy40,41, whereas statistically significant changes in lumbar spine BMD are generally reported after 12 to 24 months of sustained treatment39,55,61. Across trials, baseline bone turnover consistently emerges as a stronger determinant of response than dose escalation41.

IN-CAL vs. Supplementation Alone

Early trials in women with established postmenopausal osteoporosis consistently showed that IN-CAL plus calcium was superior to calcium alone. Two pre-1996 randomized studies demonstrated that 100 to 200 IU/d attenuated bone loss at the lumbar spine and distal radius over 12 to 24 months, whereas calcium monotherapy led to continued demineralization66,67. Agnusdei et al. reported increased distal radius bone mineral content with IN-CAL (p < 0.01) vs. a decline with calcium alone (p < 0.05), along with reduced resorption markers66. Abellán Pérez et al. similarly reported that IN-CAL (100 IU/d administered in 14-day on/14-day off cycles) plus calcium supplementation significantly improved back pain and reduced new vertebral fractures over 1 year compared with calcium alone (both p < 0.001)67. These studies established IN-CAL's early skeletal and symptomatic advantages over calcium supplementation alone in high-risk postmenopausal women.

Post-1996 trials, incorporating modern imaging and biochemical markers, confirmed and extended these findings. Daily or cyclic 200 IU of IN-CAL dosing improved lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD, enhanced tibial cortical parameters, suppressed bone turnover, and reduced back pain compared with calcium ± vitamin D68,69. Shorter trials demonstrated early biochemical effects within months, consistent suppression of serum and urinary resorption markers and initial femoral neck BMD gains, as well as rapid improvements in vertebral fracture-related pain and disability, though these brief studies were not positioned to detect structural changes15,64.

IN-CAL vs. Placebo Nasal Spray

Early placebo-controlled trials provided foundational evidence for the clinical efficacy of IN-CAL in established postmenopausal osteoporosis. Across 4 studies, IN-CAL (100-200 IU/d) significantly reduced biochemical markers of bone resorption and preserved bone mass, particularly at the forearm and lumbar spine, compared with placebo or no treatment2,3,12,63. Overgaard demonstrated lumbar spine BMD preservation over 2 years with 200 IU/d IN-CAL, while placebo patients showed progressive loss2. Though small in scale, The trial of Pun and Chan suggested that IN-CAL also provided analgesic benefits in patients with acute vertebral collapse, reducing pain scores and analgesic use12. Thamsborg et al. and Nielsen et al. highlighted consistent reductions in bone turnover, with significant gains in distal forearm BMD3,63.

Several post-1996 studies reinforced and expanded upon earlier placebo-controlled trials. Lyritis et al. demonstrated in a 2-year placebo-controlled trial of 116 women with vertebral fractures that 200 IU/d IN-CAL significantly increased lumbar spine BMD, while placebo patients continued to lose bone, with parallel reductions in back pain and analgesic use13. Kapetanos et al. further reported that in a 12-month placebo-controlled trial of 46 women with vertebral fractures and high bone turnover, 200 IU/d IN-CAL produced significant BMD gains at the lumbar spine (+6.2%), Ward triangle (+13.8%), and trochanter (+17.7%), whereas placebo patients experienced progressive losses, particularly at the hip70.

In 2005, Colpan et al. randomized 40 postmenopausal women to IN-CAL 200 IU/d or placebo for 6 months, with all participants receiving calcium and vitamin D71. Although the short duration limited BMD assessment, IN-CAL produced marked biochemical suppression at 6 months, while no meaningful placebo changes occurred (p < 0.05). These reductions were already evident by 3 months, illustrating that IN-CAL's biochemical effects emerge early and precede slower structural changes in BMD.

Dose-Response Studies

Early trials evaluating dosing in established postmenopausal osteoporosis consistently showed that 100 to 200 IU/d of IN-CAL preserved lumbar spine BMD and suppressed bone resorption markers, with no additional benefit at 400 IU/d5,65,72-74. Overgaard et al. confirmed this threshold effect: Both 200 IU and 400 IU/d attenuated spinal bone loss over 2 years, whereas 100 IU/d did not, and the 200 IU and 400 IU groups showed no meaningful differences, indicating no linear dose-response at higher doses5. Similar patterns were also reported by Thamsborg et al. and Gennari et al.72,74

Tekeoğlu et al. further explored dosing frequency by comparing daily, 15-day per month, and 10-day per month administration of IN-CAL 200 IU in 60 women with established osteoporosis75. All regimens produced comparable 6-month improvements in lumbar spine BMD, bone turnover markers, and back pain, suggesting that once a sufficient exposure threshold is reached, increasing dosing frequency does not proportionally enhance skeletal response.

Collectively, trials in established postmenopausal osteoporosis demonstrate that IN-CAL produces consistent lumbar spine preservation and early suppression of bone resorption markers, with associated improvements in vertebral fracture-related pain in select placebo-controlled studies2,13,70,71. Supplementation-controlled trials similarly show superiority over calcium alone in both skeletal and symptomatic outcomes66,67. Peripheral responses are more variable but appear more evident in higher-turnover or more advanced disease63,70. Across dose-ranging investigations, skeletal effects are threshold-dependent rather than dose-proportional, plateauing near 200 IU/d5,72,74,75. Biochemical suppression typically occurs within 1 to 3 months, whereas clinically meaningful BMD gains generally require sustained therapy of at least 6 to 12 months40,41. Across trials, baseline bone turnover consistently emerges as a stronger determinant of response than dose escalation41.

IN-CAL vs. Other Pharmacologic Modalities

In addition to comparisons with bisphosphonates, limited data exist evaluating IN-CAL against other antiresorptive and newer agents (Table III). The earliest head-to-head randomized trials were conducted by Adami et al. (n = 286; n = 286), which demonstrated substantially greater and dose-responsive BMD gains with alendronate compared with IN-CAL6,77. Over 24 months, alendronate increased lumbar spine BMD by +5.2% (10 mg/d) to +7.3% (20 mg/d), while IN-CAL showed no improvement (−0.8%) and did not differ from placebo. At the hip, alendronate improved femoral neck and trochanteric BMD (+1.2% to +7.2%), whereas IN-CAL declined at the femoral neck (−1.8%) and showed no trochanteric benefit.

This hierarchy was reinforced by Downs et al. (n = 299), where alendronate outperformed IN-CAL across all lumbar spine and hip sites at 12 months (5.16% vs. 1.18% lumbar spine gain; p < 0.001) and produced markedly stronger biochemical suppression (43% vs. 9% reduction in bone-specific alkaline phosphatase; 62% vs. 11% in urinary N-telopeptide)7. However, Dursun et al. (n = 151) reported that both alendronate + calcium and IN-CAL + calcium improved BMD, pain, and quality of life similarly over 1 year, with no difference in vertebral fracture rates78. Accordingly, IN-CAL may be considered in patients with contraindications to bisphosphonates, including renal impairment, hypocalcemia/vitamin D deficiency, esophageal pathology, or intolerance to GI or renal adverse effects8,9.

Real-world comparative data further support bisphosphonate superiority. In a large observational cohort study of 43,135 older adults receiving oral bisphosphonates, nasal calcitonin, or raloxifene, Cadarette et al. found that IN-CAL recipients experienced significantly more nonvertebral fractures compared with alendronate (hazard ratio [HR] 1.40, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.20-1.63)76. No significant differences were observed between risedronate or raloxifene and alendronate overall, although raloxifene was associated with higher fracture risk in patients with prior fracture history (HR 1.78, 95% CI 1.20-2.63).

Less commonly, IN-CAL has been compared with other pharmacologic approaches such as nandrolone decanoate. Flicker et al. (n = 123) reported that while IN-CAL improved lumbar spine BMD, it was associated with proximal femur loss. Nandrolone preserved both axial and appendicular sites but resulted in high rates of androgenic and systemic adverse effects, leading to frequent discontinuation79. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Câmara et al. (n = 293) reexamined randomized trials of nandrolone decanoate in postmenopausal osteoporosis and found moderate-certainty evidence for fracture reduction and improvements in muscle mass, along with modest increases in BMD80. However, these benefits were accompanied by increased virilizing adverse events, and the overall evidence base was limited by small sample sizes and older trial methodology. Notably, nandrolone remains off-label for osteoporosis in several regions and is not incorporated into most contemporary treatment guidelines.

While no direct head-to-head randomized trials have compared IN-CAL with denosumab, raloxifene, or anabolic therapies, the Endocrine Society notes that calcitonin's vertebral fracture efficacy appears similar to raloxifene in established osteoporosis but is less robust than bisphosphonates81. Current guidelines recommend IN-CAL only when patients cannot tolerate or are not appropriate candidates for first-line osteoporosis therapies8.

Collectively, the comparative literature demonstrates that IN-CAL is less effective than bisphosphonates and modern anabolic agents for increasing BMD and reducing fracture risk and may be associated with higher nonvertebral fracture rates compared with alendronate in real-world settings. Limited evidence suggests similar vertebral fracture efficacy to raloxifene, though direct comparative trials are lacking. Overall, IN-CAL provides consistent lumbar spine benefit and favorable tolerability but is best positioned as a secondary or alternative therapy for patients who cannot receive more potent antiresorptive or anabolic agents, with additional value in pain reduction and patient-reported outcomes.

IN-CAL in Acute Orthopaedic Injury and Surgical Recovery

While IN-CAL has historically been used in metabolic bone disease, several small studies have evaluated its short-term use in acute fracture settings, primarily for pain control and modulation of bone turnover (Table IV). The earliest investigations centered on its ability to preserve bone mass during periods of immobilization. Petersen et al. conducted a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in patients with surgically fixed ankle fractures, initiating daily IN-CAL (200 IU) within 7 days of injury and continuing treatment for 12 weeks82. Although bone mineral content loss in the injured limb at 3 months was numerically lower with IN-CAL (2.1% vs. 14% in placebo), this difference did not reach statistical significance. By contrast, a significant intergroup difference was observed in the contralateral limb as early as 6 weeks, suggesting a systemic antiresorptive effect during immobilization.

Huusko et al. later evaluated IN-CAL in a large randomized, double-blind trial of elderly patients with acute hip fractures, initiating treatment on the first postoperative day and continuing for 3 months84. Surgical management was determined by fracture type and included hemiarthroplasty or total hip replacement for displaced femoral neck fractures and internal fixation (IF) for selected fracture patterns. While overall pain, BMD, and functional outcomes were similar between groups, a preplanned subgroup analysis demonstrated significantly higher fracture union rates among patients treated with IF (84% with IN-CAL vs. 63% with placebo at 3 months; p = 0.029). This finding suggested that short-term postoperative IN-CAL may enhance early fracture healing in select hip fracture populations.

Peichl et al. extended these findings to postmenopausal women undergoing cementless total hip arthroplasty for hip fractures83. IN-CAL (200 IU daily), initiated approximately 10 to 14 days postoperatively and continued for 12 months, was associated with significant reductions in bone turnover markers and attenuation of bone loss compared with calcium and vitamin D alone. Over 1 year, patients receiving IN-CAL demonstrated stabilization of forearm BMD, whereas controls experienced continued decline. Notably, no refractures occurred in the IN-CAL group compared with 5 refractures due to aseptic loosening in controls (p = 0.04), and heterotopic ossification was markedly less frequent in the IN-CAL group (6 vs. 20 patients). Similarly, Karachalios et al. evaluated elderly women with pertrochanteric fractures randomized to IN-CAL (200 IU daily) or placebo initiated on admission and continued for 3 months85. IN-CAL treatment resulted in early suppression of bone resorption markers alongside higher bone formation markers by day 15. At 1 year, BMD at the contralateral femoral neck increased by 3.6% in the IN-CAL group compared with a 9.2% decline in controls. At 4-year follow-up, new fractures occurred in 4.6% of IN-CAL-treated patients vs. 24% of controls, most commonly involving the contralateral hip. Overall, these studies suggest that early postoperative IN-CAL use in hip fracture and arthroplasty patients may favorably modulate bone turnover, preserve skeletal integrity at the contralateral hip, and reduce the risk of subsequent fragility fractures during recovery.

IN-CAL's postfracture benefits were later investigated in upper extremity trauma. Karponis et al. initiated IN-CAL within 3 days of injury in women with conservatively managed distal radius fractures and continued therapy for 3 months16. Patients receiving IN-CAL experienced significantly greater early pain reduction, with VAS scores decreasing by 50% at 10 days compared with 16.7% in the placebo group (p = 0.028), while no differences were observed at later time points. These findings are consistent with a short-term, time-limited analgesic effect during the acute postfracture period.

Beyond bone trauma, IN-CAL has also been evaluated in periarticular soft-tissue conditions. In a double-blind randomized trial, Rouhani et al. studied patients with shoulder adhesive capsulitis treated with intranasal IN-CAL (200 IU daily) for 6 weeks in conjunction with physiotherapy and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs86. Compared with placebo, the IN-CAL group demonstrated significantly greater reductions in pain (mean VAS 2.7 vs. 4.8; p < 0.05), superior improvements in shoulder range of motion across all planes, and markedly better functional outcomes (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand, Shoulder Pain and Disability Index, and Health Assessment Questionnaire scores; all p < 0.001). In addition, fewer patients in the IN-CAL group required subsequent intra-articular corticosteroid injection (32% vs. 72%; p = 0.004), supporting a potential analgesic role in periarticular recovery.

More recently, Roy et al. evaluated IN-CAL as an adjunct following open reduction and IF of mandibular fractures in a randomized controlled trial87. IN-CAL (200 IU daily for 30 days) was associated with significantly higher postoperative serum osteocalcin levels (67.8 vs. 57.7 ng/dL; p = 0.029) and more rapid pain resolution, with all treated patients reporting complete pain relief by postoperative day 5 compared with day 21 in controls (p < 0.01). Radiographic healing at 12 weeks favored the IN-CAL group, with 28.6% demonstrating complete fracture fusion vs. none in the control cohort, although this difference did not reach statistical significance.

Mandalia et al. extended these observations to nondisplaced acromial and scapular spine stress fractures following reverse total shoulder arthroplasty17. In this retrospective case series, 10 patients were treated with intranasal IN-CAL (200 IU daily for 6 weeks) in conjunction with sling immobilization. Fractures included acromial fractures (n = 9) and 1 scapular spine fracture, spanning Levy type I (n = 4), type II (n = 5), and type III (n = 1) patterns. Following treatment, patients demonstrated mean reductions of 5.8 points in VAS pain scores, gains of 46° in active forward elevation, and a 43.6-point increase in ASES scores (all p < 0.01). Importantly, all fractures progressed clinically without delayed union, reoperation, or treatment-related adverse events; however, the absence of a control group limits conclusions regarding treatment efficacy.

Taken together, available studies suggest that IN-CAL may provide short-term analgesic benefit in acute fracture and perioperative settings, with biochemical suppression of bone turnover occurring within weeks. Evidence for effects on fracture healing, contralateral bone preservation, or long-term structural outcomes remains limited and is derived largely from small or heterogeneous studies. Accordingly, IN-CAL's role in acute orthopaedic care appears most consistent as a short-term adjunct for pain control rather than as a definitive strategy for enhancing fracture healing or altering long-term skeletal outcomes.
