Salivary mitofusin levels in periodontal disease: a cross-sectional case-control study
Authors: Ömer F Okumuş, Nezahat Kurt, Alpdoğan Kantarcı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitofusin (Mfn) is a mitochondrial fusion protein. It has two isoforms (Mfn1 and 2) and is critical in intracellular energy production, calcium transfer, and phospholipid metabolism. Based on previous evidence linking mitochondrial function with the pathophysiology of periodontal inflammation, we hypothesized that salivary Mfn1 and Mfn2 levels may differ across periodontal health, gingivitis, and periodontitis, and that these levels correlate with markers of inflammatory and oxidative stress. Although previous studies have suggested that Mfn2 plays a role in inflammatory and oxidative pathways, clinical evidence regarding its role in periodontal disease is lacking. The null hypothesis of this study was that salivary Mfn levels are not significantly altered with periodontal conditions and are not correlated with inflammation or oxidative stress markers in saliva.MethodsA total of 81 participants were included in this cross-sectional case-control study, with 27 individuals per group. Periodontally healthy patients, patients with gingivitis, and patients with stage 3 or stage 4 grade C periodontitis were recruited for the study. Mfn-1 and Mfn-2 levels were measured. To identify their correlation with inflammatory mechanisms, interleukin-1β levels, total oxidative status (TOS), and total antioxidant status (TAS) were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and colorimetric measurements. The oxidative stress index (OSİ) was calculated.ResultsSalivary Mfn1 levels were significantly higher in patients with periodontitis compared to healthy controls (36.54 ± 13.15 ng/mL, 24.67 ± 8.69 ng/mL, p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of Mfn2 levels (p > 0.05). While significant differences were found between Group P and Group H in TAS and OSI values (p < 0.05), TOS values ​​did not show any significant difference between the groups (p > 0.05). A positive correlation was found between Mfn1 and Mfn2 and IL-1β levels (p < 0.05). Mfn1 and Mfn2 did not have any significant correlation with TAS, TOS, or OSI values (p > 0.05).ConclusionsElevated Mfn1 levels in the saliva of patients with periodontitis suggested that mitochondrial function was disrupted in severe periodontitis. Background

The mitochondrial functions, which are essential for cellular energy production and homeostasis, are closely linked to the inflammatory processes of periodontitis and gingivitis. Mitochondria undergo dynamic morphological changes during important cellular events, including inflammation [1]. Mitochondrial fusion proteins (MFPs), mitofusin-1 (Mfn1), and mitofusin-2 (Mfn2), located in the outer mitochondrial membrane, determine mitochondrial morphology [1]. These proteins also form mitochondria-endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane contact (MERC) sites [1, 2]. MERC sites play a crucial role in essential cellular activities, including the production of mitochondrial ATP, the transfer of calcium between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria, and the regulation of phospholipid metabolism [3].

MFPs could play a role in the development of periodontal diseases [2, 4, 5]. For instance, a recent clinical study found that the levels of Mfn1 and Mfn2 were different in the gingival crevicular fluid of people with periodontal disease compared to those who are healthy [2]. Since macrophage response in periodontal disease may be one of the mechanisms linking MFPs and periodontal inflammation, and Mfn2 has been reported to be associated with the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, which regulates the release of IL-1β, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, from macrophages [6, 7], changes in Mfn2 levels could impair macrophage function. This, in turn, may lead to increased IL-1β release and exacerbation of inflammation [7]. Another factor associated with mitochondrial dysfunction that could play a critical role in the development of periodontal disease is oxidative stress (OS) [8, 9]. Disruption of the balance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant systems leads to OS in periodontal disease [10]. Since the primary source of ROS is mitochondria [9], an impaired mitochondrial function and an increase in ROS production may exacerbate inflammation [9]. However, the role of Mfn2 in OS remains controversial. Some studies have reported that Mfn2 deficiency increases ROS production [11], while others, conversely, report that Mfn2 deficiency decreases ROS production [12]. Furthermore, there are conflicting findings regarding the effects of Mfn2 deficiency on MERC regions, which are critical for inflammation and its relationship with OS [13, 14]. Indeed, oxidative damage-induced apoptosis in periodontal ligament fibroblasts through a mitochondria-dependent pathway was associated with dysregulated expression of Mfn1 and Mfn2 [4].

Based on these reports, we hypothesized that Mfn expression increases progressively with disease severity, from health to gingivitis and then to periodontitis, in parallel with inflammatory and oxidative processes. To test this hypothesis, we measured salivary Mfn1 and Mfn2, IL-1β, total antioxidant status (TAS), total oxidative status (TOS), and oxidative stress index (OSI) in patients with periodontitis and gingivitis.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional case-control study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Erzincan University (March 21, 2024; approval number: 2024-04/04). The study was also registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration number: NCT06510608). The participants were divided into three groups: periodontally healthy individuals (n = 27), patients with gingivitis (n = 27), and patients with stage 3 and 4 grade C periodontitis (n = 27). The participants were selected from patients who presented to the Department of Periodontology at the Erzincan University Faculty of Dentistry for periodontal treatment between April 2024 and August 2024. The study was conducted in full compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in 2013). All participants provided both verbal and written informed consent.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (Ⅰ) antibiotic use in the last six months, (Ⅱ) receiving periodontal treatment within the last six months, (Ⅲ) possession of fewer than 20 teeth (excluding third molars), (Ⅳ) presence of any systemic diseases and medication use, and (Ⅴ) being a current smoker or having quit within the last five years. Demographic data (age and gender) of all participants were recorded.

Periodontal examination, classification, and group allocation

The intraoral and radiographic examinations were performed in a single session by the same calibrated periodontist (Ö.F.O.). Intraexaminer variability was detected > 98%. The plaque index (PI) [15], gingival index (GI) [16], probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), bleeding on probing (BOP) [17] were assessed by examining six sites per tooth (mesiobuccal, midbuccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, midlingual, and distolingual) using a Williams periodontal probe (Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA). The percentage of BOP was calculated by dividing the number of bleeding sites by the total number of sites examined for each participant. BOP was determined based on the ratio of bleeding sites to the total examined sites during probing.

In patients with periodontitis, the percentage of bone loss was calculated in panoramic radiographs relative to the total root length of the tooth exhibiting the most severe bone loss. Measurements were made from the cementoenamel junction to the top of the alveolar crest and the most apical point of the root apex using a program that allows millimetric measurements on panoramic images (Akgun PACS Viewer 4.0, Akgun A.Ş., Ankara, Turkey).

Based on the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions [18, 19], the participants were divided into three groups: periodontally healthy (Group H, n = 27, PD ≤ 3 mm, %BOP < 10, and no CAL or radiographic bone loss), gingivitis (Group G, n = 27, PD ≤ 3 mm, %BOP > 30, and no CAL or radiographic bone loss), and stage 3 and 4, grade C periodontitis (Group P, n = 27, PD ≥ 6 mm, CAL ≥ 5 mm, %BOP ≥ 30, tooth loss due to periodontitis, radiographic alveolar bone loss extending to the middle or apical third of the root, exaggerated destruction relative to biofilm/calculus levels, and bone loss-to-age ratio > 1). Restricting the periodontitis group to patients with stage 3 or 4, grade C disease was designed to minimize biologic variability and increase consistency of molecular data among individuals with well-defined advanced disease.

Collection of saliva samples

Saliva samples were collected following the method described by Navazesh [20]. The participants were invited to return the day after the periodontal examination for sample collection. They were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking, or chewing gum for at least two hours prior to their visit and to avoid oral hygiene practices, such as toothbrushing or mouthwash use. All samples were collected between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. The participants were first asked to rinse their mouths with tap water for 2 min and then spit it out. After 10 min, they were instructed to accumulate saliva in 15 mL polypropylene tubes for 5 min. The saliva samples were placed on ice, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until the day of analysis. On the analysis day, the samples were thawed on ice and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were used for analysis. After saliva samples were collected, appropriate periodontal treatments were administered to individuals with periodontal disease. In the gingivitis group, only supragingival plaque control and calculus removal were performed. The treatment of periodontitis patients was planned and conducted in accordance with the stepwise periodontal therapy approach outlined in the clinical guidelines published by the European Federation of Periodontology in 2020 [21]. Saliva samples from all participants were collected prior to any periodontal intervention.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis

All laboratory analyses were performed at the Medical Biochemistry Laboratory, Faculty of Medicine, Erzincan Binali Yıldırım University, by the biochemistry specialist (N.K.). Concentrations of Mfn1, Mfn2, and IL-1β in saliva samples were spectrophotometrically measured using the ELISA method. The following commercial kits were employed: Human Mfn1 ELISA Kit (Catalog no: 201-12-5332, Sunred), Human Mfn2 ELISA KIT (Catalog no: 201-12-5331, Sunred), and Human IL-1β ELISA Kit (Catalog no: 201-12-0144 Sunred). For each measurement, standards and samples (All sample volume: 50 µL) were pipetted into microplate wells pre-coated with specific antibodies for the desired parameter. The Mfn1, Mfn2, or IL-1β in the samples bound to the immobilized antibodies. Subsequently, enzyme-conjugated secondary antibodies were added to interact with the primary antibodies. Finally, a substrate for the enzyme on the secondary antibody was added, producing a color change proportional to the concentration of Mfn1, Mfn2, or IL-1β. The reaction was stopped, and the color intensity was measured at 450 nm using a multimicroplate reader spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific/Multiskan). All samples were standardized per milliliter of saliva.

The sensitivity values of the ELISA kits used were reported as 0.372 ng/mL for Mfn1, 0.271 ng/mL for Mfn2, and 15.013 pg/mL for IL-1β, respectively. Additionally, the intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) for all three kits was reported to be less than 10%, and the inter-assay CV was reported as 12%.

All kits used were validated for saliva as well as other biological fluids, according to the manufacturers’ specifications.

TAS and TOS analysis and OSI calculation

TAS and TOS were evaluated spectrophotometrically using commercial colorimetric kits: Total Antioxidant Status (Rel Assay Diagnostic) and Total Oxidant Status (Rel Assay Diagnostic), respectively. TAS measurement was based on the stabilization of a characteristic blue-green color of the 2,2’-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) radical cation by antioxidants, with the color change measured at 660 nm (sample volume: 18 µL, as specified in the kit protocol). A vitamin E analogue, Trolox Equivalent (1 mmol/L), provided within the kit by the manufacturer, was used as the standard, and all values were calculated based on the concentration and absorbance of this standard solution. For quality control (QC), QC Level-1 (0.5 mmol/L) and Level-2 (2.0 mmol/L) calibrators included in the kit were used. For the TAS kit, the inter-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was 2.8%, while the intra-assay CV was 3.3%. The principle of TOS measurement relied on the oxidation of the iron ion-o-dianisidine complex by oxidants to ferric ions, which formed a colored complex with xylene orange in an acidic medium. The color intensity, measurable at 530 nm, correlated with the total oxidant molecules in the sample (sample volume: 45 µL, as specified in the kit protocol). For the TOS standard, a hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) solution at a concentration of 10 µmol/L, provided within the kit, was used to calculate all values. For quality control, QC Level-1 (5 µmol/L) and Level-2 (20 µmol/L) calibrators included in the kit were utilized. The inter-assay coefficient of variation (CV) for the TOS kit was 3.2%, while the intra-assay CV was 3.9%. OSI was calculated as the ratio of TOS to TAS using the following formula and expressed in arbitrary units (AU):

OSI (AU) = (TOS (nmol H2O2 Eq/L)/TAS (nmol Trolox Eq/L)) x 100.

This ratio reflected the OS level in the tissue, providing critical insights into the extent of oxidative damage and the intensity of cellular responses.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software (α = 0.05, power = 0.80) assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.40). This estimate was based on a previously published clinical study measuring salivary IL-1β levels in patients with periodontal disease [22]. Although no previous salivary data were available for Mfn1 or Mfn2, the reference biomarker selected (IL-1β) was both used in our study and biologically relevant to our objectives as it is an inflammation-associated protein. Using One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), we determined that 22 participants per group would be sufficient, resulting in a total of 66 participants. To increase the power of the study, 27 participants were included in each group.

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 22.0 (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation values for normally distributed variables and median (minimum-maximum) values for non-normally distributed variables. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Group comparisons of demographic data were conducted using one-way ANOVA for age and Pearson’s chi-square test for gender. Differences in group means were assessed using one-way analysis of variance. The differences in median values were evaluated with the Kruskal-Wallis test, and post hoc pairwise comparisons related to these tests were performed using the Tukey test and the Dunn-Bonferroni test, respectively. Relationships between continuous variables were analyzed using Pearson correlation for normally distributed data and Spearman correlation for non-normally distributed data. Group distributions were visualized with bar charts and box plots, as appropriate. Additionally, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to examine the effect of periodontal status on salivary levels of Mfn1, Mfn2, IL-1β, TAS, TOS, and OSI, while controlling for age, gender, and plaque index as covariates. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Multivariable analysis (ANCOVA)

To adjust for potential confounding factors, ANCOVA was conducted with age, gender, and plaque index as covariates. After adjustment, Mfn1 levels remained significantly different between groups (p = 0.016), with a moderate effect size (partial η² = 0.103). Similarly, IL-1β levels showed a significant group effect (p = 0.027, partial η² = 0.092).

No statistically significant group differences were observed for Mfn2, TAS, TOS, or OSI after adjusting for covariates (p > 0.05). These results support the robustness of the observed differences in Mfn1 and IL-1β, independent of demographic and clinical variability. Overall, the main conclusions of the study were not materially altered after multivariable adjustment.

Discussion

In this study, the levels of Mfn1 and Mfn2, along with IL-1β associated with inflammation and TAS, TOS, and OSI parameters related to OS, were evaluated in the saliva of healthy individuals and patients with gingivitis and periodontitis. The concentration of Mfn1 was statistically significantly higher in patients with periodontitis compared to periodontally healthy participants. Mfn1 levels correlated with all clinical periodontal parameters. Importantly, these group differences in Mfn1 levels remained statistically significant after adjusting for age, gender, and plaque index using ANCOVA, supporting the robustness of the association. These findings suggested a potential role of Mfn1 in the pathogenesis of periodontal disease, although causality cannot be inferred due to the cross-sectional design.

Previous studies have reported that the overexpression of Mfn1 led to mitochondrial hyperfusion, which negatively affected mitochondrial function and mobility [23]. Consistent with our results, a prior clinical investigation found that total Mfn1 levels in the gingival crevicular fluid of patients with periodontitis were significantly higher than in healthy individuals; however, no significant difference in Mfn2 levels was detected [2]. In this study, although Mfn1 levels were higher in patients with periodontitis compared to healthy individuals, no significant difference was observed in the gingivitis group compared to the other groups. On the other hand, Mfn2 levels did not show a significant difference between any of the groups. These findings regarding MFPs under the conditions examined in the cross-sectional design may imply that Mfn1 levels exhibit a stepwise increase associated with surpassing a threshold of inflammation rather than a linear progression with periodontal inflammation, and that Mfn1 may play a more prominent role than Mfn2 in periodontal inflammation. In an in vitro study by Aral et al., it was reported that the expression of Mfn1 and Mfn2 genes in human gingival fibroblasts was significantly dysregulated following exposure to periodontal pathogens [5]. It has been shown that Mfn1 and Mfn2 gene expression may respond differently depending on the type of periodontal pathogen (P. gingivalis or F. nucleatum) and the dose of exposure [5]. In this context, the upregulation of Mfn1 in advanced stages of periodontal inflammation, while Mfn2 levels remain unchanged, may suggest a more complex regulatory mechanism shaped by individual variability in the proportion and dosage of pathogens in the dental plaque flora.

Several previous in vitro studies have addressed the relationship between Mfn2 and OS [11, 12, 14]. Mfn2 is considered a critical protein in situations where macrophages require high amounts of ROS [12]. Some studies have related the Mfn2-OS link to the structure and function of MERC domains [9, 11, 24]. The disruption of calcium transfer between mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) due to disruptions in MERC regions increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production caused by the dysfunction of both organelles [11]. Mfn2 deficiency can increase ROS production [11], impair mitophagy, and result in increased ROS production related to dysfunctional mitochondria, which may lead to OS [25]. MFPs play a role in the formation of MERC regions, but recent studies have reported that Mfn2 deficiency results in uncontrolled excessive contact in MERC regions, which may result in increased ROS production [11, 14]. In another study, the absence of Mfn2 reduced ROS production by macrophages [12]. There is limited data examining the relationship between the antioxidant system and MFPs, and one study reported that catalase and superoxide dismutase levels were not affected by Mfn2 deficiency [12]. Our results did not reveal any significant correlation between Mfn1 and Mfn2 proteins and OS parameters (TAS, TOS, OSI). This finding suggests that, under clinical conditions, the regulation of MFPs may not directly parallel oxidative stress markers, possibly due to more complex underlying mechanisms or the inherent limitations of global OS indices such as TAS and TOS.

TAS levels of healthy individuals were significantly higher than those of patients with gingivitis and periodontitis. A previous study reported that TAS levels were lower in patients with periodontitis than in healthy individuals [26]. Baltacıoğlu et al. reported that TOS and OSI levels were higher in patients with periodontitis than in healthy individuals [8]. Our results are consistent with those of a previous study reporting no significant difference in TOS and OSI levels between groups [27]. Zhang et al. also reported similar non-significant differences in salivary TOS levels between periodontitis patients and healthy controls [27]. Furthermore, despite our stringent exclusion criteria, the literature has shown that TOS values are highly sensitive to uncontrolled variables such as obesity and diet [28, 29]. It is also known that inflammation-induced ROS production precedes tissue destruction, and a large portion of the resulting ROS is rapidly neutralized either through reactions with target proteins or by endogenous antioxidant systems [30, 31]. Therefore, as Zhang et al. have suggested, more specific markers such as lipid peroxidation products or DNA damage markers may serve as more sensitive indicators of OS in the periodontal environment [27]. Therefore, to better understand the role of MFPs in periodontal inflammation, further clinical studies are needed to evaluate the relationship of these proteins with OS, using different OS markers.

OS and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β are closely linked in the pathogenesis of periodontal disease [32]. While IL-1β levels are elevated in periodontal disease and contribute to OS, OS, in turn, aggravates inflammation, causing tissue damage and creating a vicious cycle [33]. In addition to the relationship between MFPs and OS, investigating the potential relationships between MFPs and inflammatory cytokine (IL-1β) production provided a different perspective on the relationship between these proteins and periodontal disease. In this study, both Mfn1 and Mfn2 levels were positively correlated with IL-1β levels, suggesting that mitochondrial dynamics determined by MFPs may affect inflammatory cytokine production. Although Mfn2 levels did not differ significantly between groups representing different levels of periodontal inflammation, its positive correlation with IL-1β suggests that Mfn2 may be more closely related to individual inflammatory burden rather than to differences observed at the group level. Disruptions in MERC regions formed by MFPs activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting the conversion of pro-IL-1β to its active form [34]. In macrophages lacking Mfn2, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, were lower, whereas the absence of Mfn1 had no such impact [12]. Our findings aligned with previous studies that associated MFPs with elevated IL-1β levels in periodontal disease and highlighted the critical role of mitochondrial integrity in modulating periodontal inflammation.

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design and lack of treatment intervention limited the evaluation of the analyzed parameters as biomarkers. The cross-sectional design of this study limits causal inference regarding the observed associations between Mfn levels and periodontal inflammation. Additionally, the periodontitis group consisted solely of stage 3 and stage 4 grade C patients, preventing assessments of other periodontitis variations. Sample size was based on IL-1β values from previous studies as salivary Mfn1 and Mfn2 data are missing in the literature. Another limitation of this study is the evaluation of a single biological fluid (saliva) and the modest sample size. The convenience sampling approach may have introduced a selection bias that should be considered when interpreting the results. Although multivariable adjustment was performed for age, gender, and plaque index, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be completely excluded. Finally, as biomarker concentrations were expressed per milliliter rather than normalized to total protein, potential variability due to differences in salivary protein composition cannot be entirely excluded.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study suggested that Mfn1 may play a role in the pathogenesis of periodontal diseases, especially periodontitis, through its association with inflammatory cytokine (IL-1β) levels and clinical periodontal parameters, although causality cannot be established. Mfn1 levels were higher in periodontitis patients. Further interventional or longitudinal research is needed to understand the relationship between MFPs and OS in periodontal disease pathogenesis.
