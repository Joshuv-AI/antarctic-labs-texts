Chromothripsis: an emerging crossroad from aberrant mitosis to therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Umer Ejaz, Zhen Dou, Phil Y Yao, Zhikai Wang, Xing Liu, Xuebiao Yao, Zhiyuan Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChromothripsis, a type of complex chromosomal rearrangement originally known as chromoanagenesis, has been a subject of extensive investigation due to its potential role in various diseases, particularly cancer. Chromothripsis involves the rapid acquisition of tens to hundreds of structural rearrangements within a short period, leading to complex alterations in one or a few chromosomes. This phenomenon is triggered by chromosome mis-segregation during mitosis. Errors in accurate chromosome segregation lead to formation of aberrant structural entities such as micronuclei or chromatin bridges. The association between chromothripsis and cancer has attracted significant interest, with potential implications for tumorigenesis and disease prognosis. This review aims to explore the intricate mechanisms and consequences of chromothripsis, with a specific focus on its association with mitotic perturbations. Herein, we discuss a comprehensive analysis of crucial molecular entities and pathways, exploring the intricate roles of the CIP2A–TOPBP1 complex, micronuclei formation, chromatin bridge processing, DNA damage repair, and mitotic checkpoints. Moreover, the review will highlight recent advancements in identifying potential therapeutic targets and the underlying molecular mechanisms associated with chromothripsis, paving the way for future therapeutic interventions in various diseases.Keywords: genomic stability, DNA damage repair, chromothripsis, synthetic lethality, cell division

Introduction

Cell division is the process by which one cell propagates its genetic materials into two genetically identical daughter cells through segregation of chromosomes (Cleveland et al., 2003; Liu et al., 2020). Chromosomes are thread-like structures comprised of proteins and DNA organized in a compact manner to allow the accurate and equal distribution of genetic materials to daughter cells during mitosis (Liu et al., 2020). Although a century of study might leave the impression that most of the important mechanisms of chromosome segregation have already been discovered, many fundamental questions remain. For example, how do daughter cells decondense chromosomes to resemble the interphase chromosome territory and inter-chromosome interactions of the parent cell and what happens if a chromosome structure is ruptured during cell division (Figure 1A).

Orchestration of accurate chromosome segregation and plasticity during the cell division cycle is essential for healthy proliferation in the eukaryotic kingdom, in particular metazoans, as cellular communications are key to organ physiology and nearly 300 million epithelial cell divisions occur every second in the adult human gastrointestinal tract (Yao and Smolka, 2019; Sender and Milo, 2021). Centromeres, chromatins, and telomeres are compartments of all eukaryotic chromosomes (Liu et al., 2020). Despite the presence of highly sophisticated cell cycle checkpoints, chromosomal mis-segregation events can occur in both healthy and cancer cells (Worrall et al., 2018; Bolhaqueiro et al., 2019). The ratio of chromosomal mis-segregation events may increase more than 100-fold in cancer cells with unstable chromosomes (Bakhoum et al., 2009a, b; Thompson and Compton, 2011; Bolhaqueiro et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2023). These events, although infrequent, can lead to a condition known as aneuploidy, where cells gain or lose chromosomes during somatic cell division. Aneuploidy has been associated with various diseases, including cancer, making it a critical area of research to understand the mechanisms that safeguard genomic stability during cell division (Maiato and Silva, 2023). Two challenging questions in cell biology are: how are chromosomal dynamics and compartmentalization regulated during cell division and what happens with chromosome structure when mitosis goes wrong.

Complex chromosomal rearrangements (CCRs) have been an intriguing subject of study since the early days of cytogenetic techniques. The term ‘complex chromosomal translocations’ was initially coined to describe cases involving more than one reciprocal exchange of segments between two chromosomes, resulting in multiple derivative chromosomes (Nuzzo et al., 1968). In 1970, Fredga and Hall (1970) identified a complex chromosomal translocation involving three or possibly four chromosomes, which they referred to as a ‘complex translocation’ and ‘complex rearrangement’. Over time, the definition of complex chromosomal translocations, now known as ‘CCRs’, has evolved to encompass cases where more than one reciprocal exchange of segments occurs between two chromosomes, resulting in multiple derivative chromosomes (Pai et al., 1980; Koltsova et al., 2019).

Chromothripsis, a term derived from the Greek word thripsis for ‘shattering’, has emerged as a remarkable and enigmatic phenomenon in the field of genomics (Stephens et al., 2011). This process involves the rapid acquisition of tens to hundreds of structural rearrangements within a short period, leading to complex alterations in one or a few chromosomes (Korbel and Campbell, 2013; Figure 1B). The resulting derivative chromosome bears little resemblance to its original configuration, and its distinct mutation signature has been identified in various cancers, particularly bone, blood, and brain cancers (Parker et al., 2014; George et al., 2015; Notta et al., 2016; Fraser et al., 2017; Scarpa et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2023). The discovery of chromothripsis rekindled interest in the biology of micronuclei, a product of aberrant cell division (Stephens et al., 2011). Evidence from various studies suggests that micronuclear chromosomes result from acquired DNA damage and exhibit delayed replication kinetics compared to the main nucleus, characteristics also seen in cancer-associated chromothripsis (Terradas et al., 2009; Okamoto et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2011). Despite these intriguing findings, the experimental creation of a functional, micronucleus-derived chromothriptic chromosome capable of stable transmission remains an ongoing challenge (Ly and Cleveland, 2017b). Interestingly, a recent case report had successfully applied chromothriptic strategy to attenuate warts, hypogammaglobulinemia, immunodeficiency, and myelokathexis (WHIM) syndrome caused by mutations in the gene for the chemokine receptor CXCR4 (McDermott et al., 2015a). This case highlights the potential therapeutic application of chromothripsis for interrogation of diseases such as WHIM syndrome (McDermott et al., 2015a) and glioma (Wu et al., 2022b).

In this review, we begin by outlining the key features of chromothripsis and chromosome abnormality during cell division. We then address why and how chromothripsis happens during cell division. We next discuss how chromothripsis orchestrates chromosome instability and extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) formation during cell division. Finally, we discuss future directions, applying three-dimensional (3D) model systems to unravel the dynamics of chromothripsis and chromothripsis-based interrogation for developing novel cancer therapeutics.

Genomic instability and chromothripsis

Definition and hallmark of chromothripsis

Chromothripsis is characterized by the shattering of one or several chromosomes into small fragments, followed by random reassembly, which often results in numerous breakpoints and multiple deletions without duplications (Stephens et al., 2011). Apart from chromothripsis, two other types of CCRs have been described over the last few years: chromoanasynthesis and chromoplexy, all collectively referred to as ‘chromoanagenesis’ (from the Greek ‘anagenesis’—‘rebirth’), which indicates structural chromosome reorganization (Holland and Cleveland, 2012). Chromoanagenesis denotes structural chromosome reorganization and includes chromothripsis, chromoanasynthesis (involving altered DNA copy numbers), and chromoplexy (chains of translocations involving multiple chromosomes with frequent deletions at breakpoints) (Liu et al., 2011; Stephens et al., 2011; Rausch et al., 2012; Baca et al., 2013; Krupina et al., 2023). It is essential to differentiate between these three CCR types based on various characteristics, such as the number and distance between breakpoints, copy number states, and the order and orientation of segments at joining points (Poot, 2018; Koltsova et al., 2019). The mechanisms underlying the occurrence of chromothripsis are believed to differ from those of other chromoanagenesis phenomena, and the genomic regions involved in the rearrangement may play a crucial role in determining its phenotypic effects, such as chromosome pulverization in micronuclei, reversible apoptosis, and dicentric chromosome breaks into fragments (Koltsova et al., 2019).

The most comprehensive definition of chromothripsis has been suggested by Ly and Cleveland (2017b): ‘chromothripsis is a catastrophic genomic event characterized by the shattering and random reassembly of one or a few chromosomes, resulting in complex rearrangements within a short timeframe within a few cell cycles’. The causes and mechanisms of chromothripsis have been the subject of an ongoing investigation. Several factors have been proposed as potential triggers, including telomere damage, exposure to ionizing radiation, and viral infections. Emerging evidence suggests that aberrant epigenetic regulation may also play a role in promoting chromothripsis.

Several new techniques have been developed to explore chromothripsis and associated genetic alterations (Krupina et al., 2023; Figure 2). The shattered chromosome(s) in chromothripsis undergoes random religation, resulting in the production of numerous DNA segments and the formation of massively rearranged derivative chromosome(s) (Forment et al., 2012; Rosswog et al., 2021). These chromothriptic rearrangements can lead to multiple carcinogenic insults, including the loss of tumor suppressors, amplification of oncogenes, and the formation of oncogenic gene fusions (Kloosterman et al., 2011; Stephens et al., 2011; Molenaar et al., 2012; Teles Alves et al., 2013). Understanding the mechanistic consequences of chromosome segregation errors in chromothripsis has been aided by the development of a selective induction method for mis-segregation of the human Y chromosome (Ly et al., 2017). This approach has provided valuable insights into the role of chromosome segregation errors in driving chromothripsis and its potential implications in disease development (Ly and Cleveland, 2017a). In fact, a mis-segregation of a single chromosome into a micronucleus can initiate a diverse array of genomic rearrangements (Ly et al., 2019).

Exploring mechanisms of action underlying chromothripsis through diverse genetic approaches. This diagram illustrates an array of investigative methodologies employed in the examination of chromothripsis. Various techniques, including whole exome sequencing (WES), single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis, next-generation sequencing (NGS), fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH), and comparative genome hybridization (CGH), have played instrumental roles in deciphering the intricate aspects of chromothripsis. Detection of chromothripsis ideally necessitates the integration of complementary techniques. A benchmark approach involves the combination of paired-end whole genome sequencing (WGS) with SNP microarrays, spectral karyotyping, and multi-color FISH. The precision of sequence resolution and spatial resolution varies among complementary techniques applied for chromothripsis detection. A comprehensive exploration of these methodologies, including their limitations and advancements, is available in a recent review by Krupina et al. (2023), providing valuable insights.

Cause of chromothripsis

Factors contributing to chromothripsis susceptibility have been a subject of scientific investigations, aiming to understand the underlying mechanisms of this complex genomic event. Recent studies revealed that specific chromosomes are more frequently involved in this phenomenon. Notably, chromosomes 17, 8, 12, 11, and 6 have shown a higher incidence of chromothripsis in tumor cells, with chromosome 17p13.1 containing the crucial TP53 gene, which regulates cell cycle checkpoints, DNA damage responses (DDRs), and apoptosis control (Cai et al., 2014). Losses of TP53 were observed in a significant proportion of chromothripsis-like cases, emphasizing its pivotal role in cellular responses to DNA damage and its potential link to chromothripsis, cell cycle arrest, or apoptosis (Cai et al., 2014). Further investigation of the unique characteristics of chromosomes involved in chromothripsis, along with deciphering the role of TP53 and other critical genes in this process, could provide valuable insights into potential therapeutic targets for chromothripsis-associated diseases. On the other hand, germline chromothripsis appears to affect different chromosomes more frequently, with chromosome 5 being the most common, followed by chromosomes 7, 3, and 9 (Nazaryan-Petersen and Tommerup, 2016). Chromosomes that are larger in size and positioned peripherally have been proposed to have a higher likelihood of mis-segregation into micronuclei, potentially initiating chromothripsis. Nuclear envelope rupture occurs more frequently in micronuclei containing larger chromosomes with lower gene density. Future genomic analyses may reveal whether chromothriptic breakpoints tend to preferentially occur in gene-poor regions of larger chromosomes (Krupina et al., 2023). The variation in chromosome involvement between somatic and germline chromothripsis may be indicative of distinct mechanisms of origin or selective pressures in different cellular contexts. This may be because chromothripsis can manifest in different cell types depending on their cycling state. In actively cycling somatic cells, impaired DNA double-strand break (DSB) responses combined with loss of TP53-dependent cell cycle checkpoint control can lead to chromothripsis.

Cells constantly face the risk of DNA damage stemming from both internal and external factors (Cannan and Pederson, 2016) and promptly respond to such challenges to ensure genome stability (Harper and Elledge, 2007; Ciccia and Elledge, 2010). This response leads to DDR checkpoints that enforce cell cycle arrest, facilitate DNA repair, and initiate apoptosis (Jackson and Bartek, 2009; Petsalaki and Zachos, 2020). Although DDR checkpoints are primarily active during interphase, they permit chromosome segregation when damaged cells enter mitosis (Finn et al., 2012; Sekelsky, 2017). DNA breaks in mitosis activate sustained DNA damage signaling and the alternative end-joining repair protein DNA polymerase θ (Clay et al., 2021). Interestingly, this orchestrated signaling not only facilitates the segregation of broken chromosomes but also serves as a protective measure, mitigating the formation of micronuclei.

Among the various types of DNA damage, DSBs are the most hazardous due to their potential to cause chromosomal translocations or structural alterations linked to tumorigenesis (Polo and Jackson, 2011). Consequently, actively cycling cells are compelled to mount a response against DSBs to avert genome instability. The improper segregation of chromosomes harboring DSBs during mitosis leads to the formation of micronuclei (Clay et al., 2021). DSBs are prominent sources of genome instability, necessitating cells to employ a spectrum of repair mechanisms encompassing HR, non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), and microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) (Groelly et al., 2023). HR entails orchestrated DNA end resection and harnesses an intact copy of the affected locus for precise, error-free repair. Nonetheless, HR utilization of the homologous chromosome instead of the sister chromatid can give rise to amplifications, deletions of repetitive sequences, and loss of heterozygosity. NHEJ, by directly fusing DSB termini with minimal or no processing, carries the potential to induce point mutations and minor deletions. The reliance of MMEJ on short microhomology stretches for annealing and ligation renders it markedly error-prone, leading to the emergence of small insertions/deletions (indels) or proximal amplifications (Audibert and Soutoglou, 2023). In fact, it was postulated that NHEJ serves as the predominant DNA repair mechanism responsible for rejoining chromosome fragments originating from micronuclei (Ly et al., 2017).

In addition, recent findings suggest that chromothripsis might function not solely as a resultant outcome, but also as a cause of disruptions within DNA repair pathways (Ratnaparkhe et al., 2018). Given the common correlation between chromothripsis, defective DDR, and micronuclei generation, it is plausible that chromothripsis could manifest in cells affected by defects in HR-mediated DNA repair pathways (Nazaryan-Petersen et al., 2020). In some instances, chromothripsis may be facilitated by homologous repeats, in particular short or long interspersed nuclear elements. These repetitive elements harbor potential L1 endonuclease cleavage sites, leading to DSBs. Subsequently, these DSBs may undergo DNA repair through the HR machinery within the affected genomic regions (Nazaryan-Petersen et al., 2016).

Umbreit et al. (2020) recently revealed the potential subclonal heterogeneity in chromothripsis patterns, highlighting that such heterogeneity could be the result of a cascade of mutational events occurring within a relatively short period. The initiation of this cascade involves the formation of chromosome bridges, resulting from end-to-end fusions of chromosomes, which can occur due to chromosome breakage or telomere crisis (McClintock, 1941; Van Steensel et al., 1998). Early studies have suggested possible mechanistic links between breakage–fusion–bridge (BFB) cycles and chromothripsis (Garsed et al., 2014; Li et al., 2014; Mardin et al., 2015). Interestingly, Umbreit et al. (2020) identified bridge extensions and cytoplasmic actomyosin contractility as critical requirements for bridge breakage. Loss of telomere protection can trigger BFB, leading the DNA repair machinery to recognize unprotected chromosome ends as DNA DSBs. This recognition prompts the ligation of chromosome ends, resulting in fused chromosomes with two active centromeres (‘dicentric chromosomes’). During anaphase, these dicentric chromosomes can be simultaneously pulled to opposite poles, forming a bridge. BFB may recur in subsequent cell divisions, as the resolving of the bridge produces chromosomes without telomeres (Maciejowski and de Lange, 2017; Barra and Fachinetti, 2018). The process can generate micronuclei on both sides of the bridge, with the nuclear envelope assembled around DNA in bridges representing a telomere crisis-induced phenomenon (Umbreit et al., 2020).

Micronuclei formation marks the commencement of chromothripsis, a phenomenon initiated during a single-cell division by a cascade of DNA damage events (Crasta et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2015). Notably, a significant portion of this damage occurs during interphase when the nuclear envelope of the micronuclei ruptures, exposing enclosed chromosomes to the cytoplasm (Hatch et al., 2013). In the context of ruptured micronuclei, the accumulated RNA–DNA hybrids are subsequently edited by ADAR enzymes, generating deoxyinosine. Furthermore, the conversion of deoxyinosine into abasic sites is facilitated by the base excision repair glycosylase, N-methyl-purine DNA glycosylase (Lee et al., 2009; Krokan and Bjørås, 2013). APE1, the base excision repair endonuclease, then cleaves these abasic sites, creating single-strand DNA nicks that may be converted to DNA DSBs during replication or when closely spaced nicks occur on opposite strands (Krokan and Bjørås, 2013). These intricate findings elucidate a mechanistic pathway for the fragmentation of micronuclear chromosomes, a crucial step in chromothripsis. Importantly, the proposed mechanism suggests that the eukaryotic cytoplasm selectively damages chromosomes with preexisting defects rather than affecting normal chromosomes (Tang et al., 2022).

Consequences of chromothripsis

The cellular consequences of chromothripsis have become a focal point of investigation, aiming to unravel the intricate processes that followed this catastrophic genomic event. Once the DNA repair system comes into play, the shattered DNA segments are randomly reassembled, leading to the formation of complex rearrangement sequences or circularized ecDNA structures (Rosswog et al., 2021; Shoshani et al., 2021). The evolution of ecDNAs may involve multiple rounds of chromothripsis and the incorporation of damaged DNA, and recent studies have proposed that ecDNA could initially arise through BFB cycles before undergoing chromothripsis (L'Abbate et al., 2014; Shoshani et al., 2021). Lee et al. (2023) postulated that oncogene neighborhoods undergo translocation in G1, resulting in the creation of a dicentric chromosome. Subsequent replication of the dicentric chromosome and segregation of dicentric sister chromosomes during mitosis lead to the formation of a chromosome bridge. This bridge undergoes breakage, with the resulting fragments often circularizing into ecDNAs. This intricate process, observed in breast cancer, betters our understanding of ecDNA dynamics, connecting the phenomena of translocation-bridge amplification with the broader context of ecDNA evolution (Lee et al., 2023). Furthermore, the coexistence of various amplicons within the same specimen suggests that ecDNAs may undergo a multistep process, transforming into multifragment structures over time (Chen et al., 2023). In the context of micronucleation, it is noteworthy that newly generated structural variants tend to preferentially distribute into one of the two daughter cells (Zhang et al., 2015).

While numerous investigations have emphasized the role of chromothripsis in driving various diseases, a compelling case study stands out, underscoring the potential therapeutic implications of chromothripsis. This case revolves around the remarkable spontaneous remission of a WHIM syndrome patient, WHIM-09, a 63-year-old white female, and the first reported case of WHIM syndrome. WHIM syndrome, characterized by autosomal dominant combined immunodeficiency resulting from a CXCR4 gain-of-function mutation, exhibited astonishing improvement (Krill et al., 1964; Zuelzer, 1964; McDermott et al., 2015a). In this unique instance, WHIM-09, having undergone splenectomy as a child and becoming CXCR4 haploinsufficient as an adult, experienced chromothripsis in hematopoietic stem cells and the myeloid lineage, but not in the lymphoid lineage. This event resulted in the deletion of 35 megabases of DNA and the random rearrangement of the remaining segments, forming a derivative chromosome lacking the entire mutant copy of CXCR4, along with 163 other genes (McDermott et al., 2015a). So far, WHIM-09 remains the only known individual to have had a beneficial effect from chromothripsis (McDermott et al., 2015b). Understanding the cellular consequences of chromothripsis and its underlying mechanisms is pivotal in unraveling the complexities of genomic instability and its implications in cancer development and treatment resistance such as glioma (Wu et al., 2022a).

Chromosome dynamics and chromothripsis

Chromosome segregation defects and chromothripsis

Error-free mitosis depends on accurate chromosome attachment to spindle microtubules, congression of those chromosomes, segregation in anaphase, and assembly of a spindle midzone upon mitotic exit (Akram et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2020). The orchestrated interplay of microtubule forces and microtubule-associated motors facilitates the precise movement of mitotic chromosomes, enabling their alignment during metaphase and the subsequent physical separation of sister chromatids during anaphase (Foley and Kapoor, 2013; Zhou et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2019; Sedzro et al., 2022). This ensures that identical sets of chromosomes are accurately segregated to each spindle pole, contributing to the formation of the genomes of the two daughter cells. Studies of the Cancer Genome Atlas show that gastric tumorigenesis is a result of perturbation of chromosome stability during cell division (Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network, 2014). In fact, chromosome mis-segregation, a common occurrence in cancer cells, can lead to numerical and structural chromosomal alterations, resulting in increased phenotypic diversity that serves as a substrate for natural selection (Bakhoum and Cantley, 2018). Notably, defects in chromosome segregation can result in the formation of abnormal structures such as micronuclei or chromatin bridges, which are frequently observed in various human neoplasms (Fenech et al., 2021; Krupina et al., 2021). These micronuclei and chromatin bridges have been identified as triggers for chromothripsis (Zhang et al., 2015; Ly et al., 2017; Shoshani et al., 2021).

One of the primary causes of mitotic errors is the failure to properly attach the spindle apparatus to the kinetochores (Ding et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2020). Bub1B, which encodes a mitotic spindle checkpoint kinase, BubR1, and its mutants are implicated in gastric tumorigenesis (Cahill et al., 1998). Huang et al. (2019) solved the crystal structure of the kinase domain of Drosophila melanogaster BubR1 and revealed that BubR1 is a bona fide kinase that phosphorylates CENP-E, causing a switch from a laterally attached microtubule motor to a plus-end microtubule tip tracker. Since BubR1 mutations are implicated in chromosome stability and tumorigenesis in the gastrointestinal tracts (Cahill et al., 1998), it would be of interest to evaluate whether disease-associated mutations also drive chromothripsis.

Interplay between mitotic defects and chromothripsis

Chromothripsis has been linked to faulty cell division processes, particularly the formation of micronuclei containing isolated chromosomes (Zhang et al., 2015; Figure 3). A notable cause of chromothripsis is chromosome mis-segregation during mitosis, where individual chromosomes fail to properly attach to the mitotic spindle, leading to their delayed segregation and eventual incorporation into micronuclei instead of the main nucleus of daughter cells (Knouse and Amon, 2015). Recent mechanistic insights have shed light on the complex evolution of nuclear membrane rupture-associated DNA damage and the formation of chromothripsis. For instance, chromatin trapped in bridges during mitotic errors can lead to micronucleation in the next cell cycle, resulting in increased fragmentation events and a higher frequency of rearrangements (Hoffelder et al., 2004; Umbreit et al., 2020; Shoshani et al., 2021), likely due to defective centromere assembly (Wang et al., 2014; Maciejowski et al., 2015; Fouquerel et al., 2019; Shoshani et al., 2021). Defective centromere assembly in aberrant micronuclei has been implicated in the mis-segregation of chromosomes into micronuclei or chromatin bridges, contributing to the perpetuation of chromosomal abnormalities (Soto et al., 2018; He et al., 2019; Kneissig et al., 2019).

Chromosome trapping in micronuclei due to mitotic errors and impact of CIP2A–TOPBP1 complex on chromosome fragment inheritance. During mitotic cell division, incomplete congression and kinetochore–microtubule attachment of chromosomes lead to their entrapment within micronuclei post-anaphase. Mis-segregated chromosomes are enveloped by a nuclear membrane, forming micronuclei. Chromothripsis occurs in micronuclei. In the next cycle, in the presence of CIP2A and TOPBP1 proteins, aberrant chromosome fragments are cohesively linked, promoting their inheritance and subsequent rearrangement. Conversely, absence of the CIP2A–TOPBP1 complex results in random dispersion and segregation of chromosome fragments, causing genetic loss and impacting cell viability. The outstanding interests focus on how mitotic chromosome segregation errors elicit the initiation of chromothripsis and what happens on the fate of the daughter cell harboring chromothripetic fragments.

To maintain genomic stability, it is imperative that each chromosome possesses a singular centromere. The formation of dicentric chromosomes, characterized by the presence of two active centromeres, can occur through a variety of mechanisms. This includes the fusion of non-homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids, often initiated by telomere dysfunction, leading to the exposure of chromosome ends or the ligation of broken sister chromatids following DNA replication. Alternatively, the epigenetic activation of a new centromere, known as a neocentromere, may result in the generation of a dicentric chromosome, especially if the original centromere remains functional (Mazzagatti et al., 2023). Dicentric chromosomes play a crucial role in the generation of ecDNA. However, the exact mechanisms underlying ecDNA formation remain a subject of ongoing investigation (Shoshani et al., 2021; Krupina et al., 2023), with proposed models such as BFB cycles, chromothripsis, the translocation-excision-deletion-amplification model, and the episome model (Yi et al., 2022). The identification of ecDNA in pre-cancerous tissue implies its potential involvement in the early stages of malignant transformation (Wang, 2023).

The spatial characterization of chromothripsis rearrangements on specific chromosomal regions suggests that pulverization may occur during mitosis, particularly in spermatogonia, which undergoes numerous mitotic divisions before entering meiotic prophase. However, examples involving multiple chromosomes suggest that chromothripsis may also occur during interphase when chromosomes are relaxed throughout the nucleus. This raises questions about the spatial organization of chromosomes within nuclear territories and the movement of chromatin within the nucleus, both potentially linked to DSB repair processes (Bickmore and Van Steensel, 2013; Dion and Gasser, 2013). It is now of interest to discover how mitotic errors elicit chromothripsis using proximity-ligation proteomics.

Mitosis, checkpoints, and chromothripsis

Mitotic checkpoint machinery

Cell cycle checkpoints are essential feedback control mechanisms that regulate the cell cycle progression, ensuring that critical events are completed before cells progress to the next stage. These checkpoints provide an opportunity for error correction, preventing potentially harmful errors that could compromise cell viability or contribute to cell transformation (Hartwell and Weinert, 1989). Relieving the dependency on checkpoints enables cells to progress through the cell cycle despite the presence of potentially harmful errors, which can ultimately compromise cell viability or facilitate cell transformation. In both mitosis and meiosis, chromosome segregation errors are prevented through the action of spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC), which actively monitors the attachment status of kinetochores to spindle microtubules (Du et al., 2008; Dou et al., 2019; McAinsh and Kops, 2023).

Although SAC is effective in preventing most chromosome attachment errors, it is not sensitive to sense the merotelic attachments, where a single kinetochore attaches to microtubules oriented to both spindle poles (Dou et al., 2015). Thus, merotelic attachment can escape the SAC and error correction machinery. These enduring chromosome attachment errors that still satisfy SAC pose an intriguing question with important clinical implications regarding how cells deal with such errors, potentially leading to mis-segregation events (O'Connell et al., 2008; Uchida et al., 2021; Maiato and Silva, 2023; Figure 4). Therefore, understanding the cellular responses to these errors could shed light on spatiotemporal dynamics of chromothripsis and may provide insights into progression of chromosome instability related diseases.

The SAC machinery for quality control of cell division. Various kinetochore–microtubule binding configurations occur in mitosis, including monotelic attachment where one kinetochore is unattached while the other attaches to a single spindle pole microtubule, syntelic attachment where both kinetochores attach to microtubules from the same spindle pole, and amphitelic attachment where proper microtubule numbers attach to each sister kinetochore from both spindle poles. Defects can activate SAC, halting APC activity through MCC formation, whereas accurate amphitelic attachment triggers APC activity for chromosome segregation. It is postulated that an inactivation of spindle checkpoint promotes chromothripsis and outstanding interests focus on the mechanisms of action underlying initiation and the spatiotemporal dynamics of chromothripsis.

SAC exerts its regulatory control over the anaphase promoting complex/cyclosome functions via the formation of mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC) (Sudakin et al., 2001; Figure 4). This MCC-mediated inhibition of the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) encompasses multifaceted mechanisms serve as the molecular embodiment of the ‘wait anaphase’ signal (Chao et al., 2012; Han et al., 2013). Dysfunctions in SAC have been implicated in chromosomal instability observed in cancer cells (Cahill et al., 1998; Dou et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2019). Errors in chromosome segregation can arise through various routes, some of which may escape detection by the mitotic checkpoint. These aberrant attachments can lead to chromosome misalignment in metaphase or result in lagging chromosomes during anaphase. Additionally, mis-segregated chromosomes can be damaged during cytokinesis or be encapsulated into micronuclei, which are spatially isolated from the main nucleus during interphase. Recent sequencing studies have revealed that complex structural rearrangements similar to chromothripsis can occur in micronucleated chromosomes, providing new insights into the molecular basis of chromosomal instability in cancer (Tan et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2015). Their studies revealed that the SAC machinery is a robust system to safeguard genome integrity, and defects in its functioning lead to lethal imbalances in the genome, severely impacting cellular fitness and hindering cell propagation, even in cancer. Moreover, mitotic errors not only result in numerical chromosomal abnormalities, mainly through lagging chromosomes, but they can also give rise to structural abnormalities through anaphase bridges (Pampalona et al., 2016; Uchida et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023). However, it remains to be established how the perturbation of SAC elicits the biochemical process leading to chromothripsis.

Mitotic tethering of shattered chromosomes

Chromosome mis-segregation in cancer cells can result in both numerical and structural alterations, leading to increased phenotypic diversity and providing a basis for natural selection (Bakhoum and Cantley, 2018). Abnormalities in chromosome segregation often manifest as micronuclei or chromatin bridges, which are commonly observed in various human neoplasms. These structures trigger chromothripsis, wherein a chromosome undergoes shattering, yielding numerous acentric fragments (Kato and Sandberg, 1968; Stephens et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2015; Ly et al., 2017; Bakhoum and Cantley, 2018). Subsequently, the shattered pieces are reassembled and ligated in a random order, resulting in a highly rearranged chromosome(s). The precise mechanism underlying the rejoining of shattered chromosomes to generate a chromothriptic chromosome remains elusive (Ly and Cleveland, 2017b). Nevertheless, after micronucleation, the newly generated structural variants tend to be preferentially present in one of the daughter cells (Zhang et al., 2015; Figure 3).

To address the challenge of segregating these fragmented chromosomes, a plausible solution would involve physically linking them to their corresponding centromere-bearing fragments during cell division. Indeed, evidence suggesting the existence of such tethers has been proposed over the years (Warecki and Sullivan, 2020). Expanding on this, when DNA DSBs are induced in mitosis, DNA topoisomerase 2-binding protein 1 (TOPBP1), a key factor for DNA replication and DDR, is recruited to DNA damage sites marked by phosphorylated H2AX (γH2AX), a process dependent on the presence of mediator of DNA damage checkpoint protein 1 (MDC1). Disruption of this recruitment, as observed through MDC1 depletion, results in persisting DNA damage and an increased formation of micronuclei containing acentric chromatin fragments (Leimbacher et al., 2019). Cancerous inhibitor of PP2A (CIP2A) was originally discovered as an oncogenic inhibitor of the tumor-suppressor protein phosphatase PP2A and plays multiple roles in DDR, G2/M checkpoint and chromothripsis. Notably, CIP2A–TOPBP1 foci, observed at sites of DNA damage, increase in number upon induction of DSBs by ionizing radiation and can manifest as filamentous structures. Furthermore, these ionizing radiation-induced CIP2A–TOPBP1 foci and filaments co-localize with γH2AX and MDC1 (Leimbacher et al., 2019; Adam et al., 2021).

Laine et al. (2021) revealed that CIP2A was dispensable for normal mammary gland development and for unperturbed mitosis, but selectively essential for mitotic progression of DNA-damaged cells. They further characterized a direct interaction between CIP2A and TOPBP1. Interestingly, CIP2A inhibition resulted in enhanced DNA damage-induced TOPBP1 and RAD51 recruitment to chromatin in mammary epithelial cells (Laine et al., 2021). These findings laid the foundation for identifying proteins responsible for mediating the coalescence of shattered chromosomes in mitotic cells. This quest led to the CIP2A and TOPBP1 complex, a significant revelation given the role of this complex in segregating chromosome fragments lacking centromeres, strongly supporting the notion that CIP2A and TOPBP1 physically bridge broken chromosomes during cell division (Adam et al., 2021; De Marco Zompit et al., 2022; Figure 3).

Recent studies show that the CIP2A–TOPBP1 complex is responsible for tethering the fragments produced by chromosome shattering during cell division (Lin et al., 2023; Trivedi et al., 2023). Although the results of both studies are remarkably consistent, they differ in some details. Perhaps the most notable divergence pertains to the conclusion about the role of MDC1 in chromosome fragment clustering. Trivedi et al. show that MDC1 has a key role upstream of CIP2A–TOPBP1 in promoting shattered chromosome tethering, whereas Lin et al. observed only a minor contribution from MDC1 (Xue and Durocher, 2023). CIP2A–TOPBP1 exhibits dual recruitment modes to mitotic DNA damage, as previously reported (Adam et al., 2021; De Marco Zompit et al., 2022): one mode relies on MDC1 and responds to chromosome breaks, whereas the other operates independently of MDC1 and is linked to defective DNA replication. Therefore, further studies of dynamic association of CIP2A–TOPBP1 with shattered chromosomes may offer insights into the origins of chromosome shattering and the identification of the elusive factors responsible for recruiting CIP2A–TOPBP1 to chromosomal DNA that has undergone abnormal replication (Xue and Durocher, 2023; Figure 3).

Chromothripsis, pathogenesis, and therapeutic intervention

Association between chromothripsis and diseases

Given that chromothripsis impacts extensive genomic regions, it possesses the potential to influence a diverse array of distinct pathological conditions (Nazaryan-Petersen and Tommerup, 2016). The association between chromothripsis and cancer has garnered significant interest due to its potential implications for tumorigenesis and disease prognosis. Recent pan-cancer studies using high-throughput sequencing have revealed a much higher prevalence of chromothripsis, affecting approximately 49% of all cases across various tumor types (Voronina et al., 2020). The presence of chromothripsis has been linked to aggressive tumor behavior and unfavorable clinical outcomes for cancer patients, emphasizing its clinical relevance (Molenaar et al., 2012; Rausch et al., 2012; Fontana et al., 2018; Rücker et al., 2018). Although initially thought to be rare, the widespread occurrence of chromothriptic events in diverse cancer genomes has become increasingly evident as more genomes are sequenced, suggesting its significant involvement in tumorigenesis (Nones et al., 2014; Notta et al., 2016; Chudasama et al., 2018; Quigley et al., 2018). Further analyses have revealed distinct chromothriptic patterns among different tumor entities, indicating the involvement of context-dependent mechanisms in chromothripsis. These entity-specific patterns may be associated with mutational signatures characteristic of each tumor type, such as APOBEC activity or HR repair deficiency, providing valuable insights into the underlying processes driving chromothripsis in specific contexts. Moreover, chromothriptic events have been found to generate a substantial number of fusion genes, which have potential clinical implications as therapeutic targets (Voronina et al., 2020).

Tumor cells exhibit abnormalities in micronuclei, and the rupture of compromised micronuclei envelopes triggers innate immunity activation through the DNA-activated cGAS–STING pathway (Mackenzie et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2018; Kwon et al., 2020). Furthermore, the genomic instability can stimulate immune surveillance (Lee et al., 2017), and the resultant chromosomal rearrangements facilitate the generation and presentation of neoantigens, activating immune cells, and promoting the clearance of tumor cells, particularly observed in mesothelioma (Mansfield et al., 2019, 2020). Conversely, cells can limit the initiation of innate pro-inflammatory signals by inhibiting the release of DNA from micronuclei (Mohr et al., 2021). Thus, chromothripsis-elicited fusion genes may offer a niche for targeted and synthetical lethality for interrogation of various cancers. As the understanding of chromothripsis in cancer continues to evolve, future directions of research could focus on deciphering the exact mechanisms driving chromothripsis in different tumor types, exploring its functional consequences on tumorigenesis and therapy response, and developing targeted therapeutic strategies to exploit chromothripsis-associated vulnerabilities. Molecular delineation of the interplay between chromothripsis and other genomic instability mechanisms in cancer progression may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the complex processes underlying cancer development and evolution.

Chromothripsis and developmental disorders

Chromothripsis can arise during various stages of spermatogenesis, including premeiotic division of diploid spermatogonia, first meiotic division involving recombination events, and spermiogenesis. Environmental stimuli, such as ionizing radiation or free radicals, as well as endogenous factors, such as DNA replication stress, may act as triggers for chromothripsis during these processes (Liu et al., 2012). CCRs, including chromothripsis, are associated with congenital abnormalities and can contribute to spontaneous abortions and male infertility (Batista et al., 1994; Pellestor et al., 2011; Madan, 2012). Studies analyzing families with chromothripsis have revealed the transmission of chromosome rearrangements from mothers to their children, resulting in completely balanced rearrangements in mothers involving multiple breakpoint junctions across several chromosomes. Some mothers showed no phenotypic abnormalities despite breakpoints affecting multiple protein-coding genes. However, in some children, unbalanced transmission led to complex copy-number changes, resulting in severe congenital phenotypes. These findings indicate that the human genome can tolerate extensive chromosomal reshuffling without significant phenotypic effects but can increase the risk of miscarriages, abortions, and severe congenital diseases (De Pagter et al., 2015).

The complex nature of chromothripsis events has led to the proposal of various causative mechanisms, such as abortive apoptosis, telomere erosion, mitotic errors, micronuclei formation, and p53 inactivation, all of which have been associated with reproductive failures and chromosomal abnormalities. Hence, understanding the impact of chromothripsis on human reproduction, especially in the context of fertilization and early embryonic development, is crucial for assessing its potential implications (Pellestor, 2014). On a separate note, it would be of great interest to assess whether chromothripsis occurs during the process of in vitro fertilization.

Chromothripsis and opportunity for new therapeutics

A strong link has been established between HR deficiency and chromothripsis in human tumors and genetic mouse models, suggesting the possibility of using synthetic lethality for managing chromothriptic tumors (Gröbner et al., 2018; Ratnaparkhe et al., 2018; Bolkestein et al., 2020; Voronina et al., 2020; Simovic and Ernst, 2022). Moreover, exploiting defects in the DDR and mitotic processes in chromothriptic tumors may offer promising therapeutic avenues, as cancer cells may rely more heavily on these pathways than normal cells (Jackson and Bartek, 2009; Lord and Ashworth, 2012).

The inherent genomic instability in rapidly dividing tumors presents an important therapeutic avenue through targeting DDR pathways, thereby inducing additional replication stress, exogenous DNA damage, and/or DDR inhibition to selectively eliminate malignant cells (Audibert and Soutoglou, 2023). This phenomenon likely accounts significantly for the initial positive outcomes observed in early applications of radiotherapy and a range of DNA-damaging chemotherapeutic agents, including alkylating agents and topoisomerase inhibitors. Nevertheless, these compounds frequently exhibit a deficiency in selectivity, leading to harmful effects on non-afflicted tissues and the development of notable adverse reactions (Audibert and Soutoglou, 2023). Several innovative compounds, targeting DDR pathways, have emerged, which include selective chemical inhibitors for ATM, POLQ, USP1, RAD51 (Groelly et al., 2023).

Chromothripsis perturbs the internal organization of chromosomes to form ecDNA. These amplified ecDNA segments can then reintegrate into chromosomal sites as a response to DNA damage induced by chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Luebeck et al. (2023) have provided exciting evidence that the ecDNA might contribute to the transformation of non-cancerous tissue into a cancerous state, offering a mechanism of action underlying chromothripsis-elicited pathogenesis and a novel niche for therapeutic interventions. These ecDNAs can carry oncogene amplifiers, providing cancer cells with a means to alter their genomes at a faster rate (Kim et al., 2020). Drugs that interfere with ecDNA replication, such as nucleoside synthetase inhibitors and ribonucleotide reductase inhibitors used in chemotherapy, could be explored for their impact on ecDNA dynamics in cancer development (Chen et al., 2023). Understanding the mechanisms of ecDNA segregation and potential vulnerabilities in this process could lead to the identification of therapeutic targets, such as PARP, which has been shown to decrease ecDNA reintegration frequency (Shoshani et al., 2021). In addition, exploiting the susceptibility of ecDNA to micronuclear expulsion and elimination may provide novel strategies for DNA damaging therapies (Wu et al., 2022a). In the future, the complexities of intra-tumor genetic heterogeneity and the diverse molecular mechanisms contributing to chromothripsis and ecDNA dynamics require further investigation for developing effective therapies (Chen et al., 2023). Future research should center on therapeutic approaches targeting ecDNA, especially in clinical scenarios, where inhibiting oncogenic ecDNA could hold significant therapeutic promise (Wang, 2023).

The identification of the CIP2A–TOPBP1 complex as a mitotic chromosome tether presents an exciting avenue for future research (Xue and Durocher, 2023). It is imperative to identify the composition and atomic structure of CIP2A–TOPBP1. The proximity-ligation coupled mass spectrometric analyses and cryo-electron microscopic analyses of the CIP2A complex, as recently documented (Tian et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023a), will undoubtedly advance our ability to interrogate the role of the complex in tumor development. Improved understanding of chromosomal instability in cancer may offer potential therapeutic targets for accurate interrogation of chromothripsis.

Perspectives

The chromothriptic processes discussed here are largely based on in vitro experimentation and reconstitution. A pressing objective is to establish whether chromothriptic processes and regulations are involved in live cell division as modeled by 3D culture systems. Several emerging technologies are suitable to delineate the molecular and cellular mechanisms that control these chromothriptic processes in mitosis. Advances in molecular imaging, optical activity reporters, chemical probes, and 3D organoid models will facilitate our understanding of the role of chromothripsis-driven dynamics in cell division, renewal homeostasis, and disease processes.

A better understanding of chromosome contacts and chromothripsis trajectory relies on real-time visualization of live cells. Fluorescent proteins and chemical probes are valuable tools for studying dynamic processes within living cells. A systems-level analysis of multiple organelle interactions has been developed using a multi-spectral image acquisition method that overcomes the challenge of spectral overlap in the fluorescent protein palette (Valm et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2020). This spectral imaging protocol achieves, for a single live cell, simultaneous measurements of five different organelles, their numbers, volumes, speeds, positions, and dynamic inter-organelle contacts (Figure 5A). Since the size and dynamics of chromosomes during mitosis, the spectral imaging is applicable to studying the spatiotemporal dynamics of CIP2A-mediated mitotic tethering of shattered chromosomes during the entire process of mitosis (Liu et al., 2020).

Recent studies in cellular and molecular biophysics, as well as biochemical reconstitutions, have revealed that mitotic phosphorylation, acetylation, and methylation of kinetochore proteins have roles in chromosome organization (Xia et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2014; Mo et al., 2016; Song et al., 2021). FRET-based biosensors can be used to measure localized post-translational modification dynamics. In general, the FRET-based sensor reports changes in intra-molecular FRET between cyan and yellow fluorescent proteins (Chu et al., 2011, 2012; Xu et al., 2021; Dou et al., 2024). It would be of interest to accomplish simultaneous visualization of phosphorylation and methylation gradients during chromothirpsis given the fact that CIP2A interacts with PP2A and BubR1. This would enable us to consolidate the post-translational modification dynamics and CIP2A-elicited protein-protein networking into a working model in which chromothripsis controls ecDNA formation and distribution in mitosis.

To date, studies of chromothripsis have relied on the use of well-documented experimental models, including primary or transformed cell lines. Such approaches have elucidated the framework of chromothripsis but have limited clarification of the functional relevance of chromothripsis to human health or disease formation. Thus, the mechanisms underlying spatiotemporal regulation of CIP2A-mediated chromothripsis and perturbation of its dynamics in pathogenesis of diseases remain poorly understood. Although chronic perturbation of CIP2A may result in profound pathological afflictions, including gastrointestinal cancers, the dynamics of oncogenesis and mechanisms of action have not been fully characterized. The development of 3D human organoids from various contexts therefore provides a powerful tool to study, in real time, epithelial development, stem cellular dynamics, and the host cell response to pathogen infection such as Helicobacter pylori, SARS-CoV-2, and EBV (Wang et al., 2008, 2022; Li et al., 2023b). Future studies, likely using patient-derived gastric organoids combined with optical imaging, will provide insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying chromothripsis and will identify small molecules for regulating LLPS in 3D cultures (Figure 5B), in particular in the chromothripsis-dependent context (Figure 5C).

Despite the progress over the past several decades in understanding the mechanism of chromothripsis, much remains to be explored. We still have a rudimentary understanding of the molecular pathways underlying chromothripsis. Advances will require cryo-electron tomographic analyses of the different states and fine cytoarchitecture of the chromothriptic cells in situ. Currently, it is unclear to what extent CIP2A-mediated chromothripsis is elicited by spindle checkpoint defects. In addition, it remains elusive as to how individual chromothripsis is regulated and how it is subjected to inter-chromosomal shattering. Thus, the salient challenges ahead are to delineate and distinguish the characteristics of chromothripsis in a context-dependent manner.

Overall, we anticipate that advances in our understanding of the molecular pathways underlying chromothripsis will enable us to amalgamate the chromothripsis-driven cellular dynamics in 3D organoids into a working model for chromosome stability control and targeted interrogation for chromothripsis-driven pathogeneses.
