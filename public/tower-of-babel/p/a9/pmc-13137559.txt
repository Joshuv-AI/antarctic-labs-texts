Multilocus genome-wide association study reveals genomic regions associated with resistance to red leaf blotch in soybean
Authors: Yechalew Sileshi, Kassahun Bantte, Abush Tesfaye, Harun Murithi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.] is a widely grown crop used for food, feed, and industrial purposes. However, a fungal disease known as Red Leaf Blotch (RLB), poses a significant threat to soybean production, and developing resistant varieties is a key management strategy. In this study, a total of 323 genetically diverse soybean genotypes of the USDA Germplasm Collection were phenotyped at RLB hotspot locations in Ethiopia (Pawe, Metu, and Asosa) in the 2024 cropping season. These genotypes were genotyped using the SoySNP50K Illumina Infinium BeadChip. A final filtered set of 38,281 SNP markers was used for multilocus genome-wide association studies to identify genomic regions associated with RLB resistance. A LOD score > 3 was used as the significance threshold. The results of the population structure analysis showed that the tested soybean genotypes were grouped into four different clusters. Linkage disequilibrium decay occurred at approximately 229 kb. ML-GWAS revealed that six quantitative trait nucleotides (QTNs) were significantly associated with RLB resistance. These QTNs are linked to 153 functional candidate genes, which are primarily involved in pathogen defense responses. Notably, key genes encode transcription factors (WRKY, HBP-1b, MYB5 repressors, bZIPs), kinases, Ser/Thr phosphatase 2 A regulatory subunits, zinc finger proteins, nodulins, amino acid transporters, and CMP-sialic acid domain-containing proteins. The identified quantitative trait nucleotides provide a valuable opportunity for consideration in advanced molecular breeding initiatives aimed at improving RLB resistance in soybean. Introduction

Soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.] is a self-pollinating annual legume that originated in China approximately 4,500 years ago [1, 2]. It belongs to the Fabaceae family and has a diploid chromosome number of 40 [3, 4]. The soybean genome size is approximately 1.1 gigabases (Gb) [5]. The importance of soybean extends beyond its role as a food and feed source; it also has important industrial applications and plays a key role in biofuel production [6, 7]. Soybean is vital for providing protein, oil, and other essential nutrients [8]. Additionally, its nitrogen fixing ability has made it an important crop for improving soil fertility [9].

Soybean production accounts for more than 6% of the world’s arable land, with 371.2 million tons produced in 2023 [7]. In Ethiopia, soybean was grown on 90,000 hectares, yielding approximately 220,000 tons in 2023 [10, 11], with a productivity of 2.49 tons per hectare. However, the productivity in Ethiopia is below its potential. Several factors impact production and productivity; among these factors, soybean diseases are major limiting factors in many areas of the world [12]. One of the primary fungal diseases affecting soybean production is red leaf blotch, caused by Coniothyrium glycines [13–15]. RLB is native to Africa [18] and was first reported in Ethiopia in 1957 [19]. Currently, it poses a serious threat to soybean production in Ethiopia as well as throughout sub-Saharan Africa [20]. Yield losses of up to 50% have been reported from Zambia and Nigeria [13, 16, 17], but under severe conditions, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, yield losses of up to 70% are expected [18]. In Ethiopia, the distribution of RLB is widespread across all soybean producing regions, with incidence ranging from 60% to 100% [21, 22].

Although fungicide applications can be used to manage plant diseases such as RLB, these chemicals impose significant costs on crop production and pose potential environmental risks. For this reason, the use of genetically resistant varieties provides the most effective, economical, ecological, and sustainable disease management approach to reduce losses due to this disease. Therefore the identification and use of RLB resistance lines are of paramount importance to protect global soybean production.

Agromorphological traits can serve as valuable tools for evaluating soybean genotypes for resistance to RLB through various statistical approaches. However, relying solely on agro-morphological characteristics presents challenges, due to the strong influence of environmental factors on the expression of quantitative phenotypic traits which compromise the predictability of trait expressions. Therefore, a feasible approach is to combine phenotypic and molecular data to identify genotypes, regions, and genes associated with specific traits of interest, such as resistance to RLB.

Genome-wide association analysis is a powerful method for identifying genomic regions associated with traits of interest [23]. GWAS utilizes collections of diverse, unrelated lines that exhibit a range of variation in specific phenotypes and have been genotyped and phenotyped for certain traits. Statistical associations between DNA sequence nucleotide polymorphisms are analyzed to detect genomic regions linked to particular traits [24, 25]. GWAS provide accurate estimates of the size and direction of the effects of alleles at identified loci for complex traits [24]. GWAS can be more accurate in identifying candidate genes than traditional methods such as biparental QTL mapping [26].

GWAS have been used in various studies in soybean to identify genomic regions associated with traits such as biomass accumulation [27], yield [28, 29]; linoleic acid, oil, and palmitic acid contents [30, 31]; protein content [32]; and seed size [33]. While only one study has been reported on the identification of quantitative trait loci associated with resistance to RLB based in Uganda, focused exclusively on Uganda pathogen isolates and soybean genotypes [34]. This Uganda study identified 19 genomic regions associated with RLB resistance across the chromosomes 1, 5, 6, 9, 10, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17, 19, and 20 [34]. The previous study employed FarmCPU GWAS models, and limited SNP coverage (6,395 SNPs). Consequently, the resistance associated loci identified in the Uganda study might be specific to the Uganda, and could limit their applicability in other regions with different isolates.

In the study presented here, we conducted a GWAS analysis of RLB resistance in Ethiopian fields, across three major soybean-growing regions, capturing diverse environmental conditions, and local pathogen isolates. The study utilized a highly diverse set of soybean genotypes collected around the world. We employed the advanced Multi-Locus Random-SNP-Effect Mixed Linear (mrMLM) GWAS Model, which has emerged as a promising approach offering higher power and better false-positive control without multiple testing corrections [35, 36]. The study utilized higher marker density than the previous studies, enabling more comprehensive detection of resistance associated loci. The aim of this study was to identify genomic regions and potential candidate genes associated with resistance to RLB disease in response to pathogen isolates found in Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Experimental sites

The field experiments were conducted at the Metu, Pawe, and Asosa Agricultural Research Centers, which are hotspots for RLB, where seasonal occurrence of the disease has been reported since 2014. A full description of the study locations is provided in Table 1.

Plant materials

In this study, 323 different soybean genotypes (Supplementary Table 1) were used, obtained from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA Soybean Germplasm Collection, Urbana, Illinois, USA). The genotypes were selected in different maturity groups and originated from more than 25 countries with the majority originally from across Asia (Supplementary Table 1).

Experimental design and trial management

The experiment was performed in an 18 × 20 alpha lattice design (ALD) with two replications per location. Each plot consisted of two rows measuring 3 m in length, with a spacing of 40 cm between rows and 5 cm within rows. To ensure high and uniform disease pressure across all genotypes, spreader rows of a highly susceptible variety, Clark 63 K, were planted at the borders of each block two weeks prior to planting the trials. To ensure uniform distribution of the disease among all the tested genotypes, a field-based supplementary inoculation was conducted by collecting leaves from plants infected with RLB, soaking them overnight in water, and applying this suspension the following morning via a knapsack sprayer. The inoculum was sprayed over all the genotypes at the flowering stage. All recommended management practices for soybean, including thinning, weeding, and fertilizer application, were performed as recommended by the Jimma Agricultural Research Center.

Phenotypic data collection

Disease severity was assessed at the R6 (full seed) growth stage for each genotype. The severity of RLB was scored on a plot basis using a 0 to 5 scale [37]. This scale evaluates observed damage, including the percentage of leaf area affected, defoliation, presence of pycnidia on blotches, and color and size of the blotches (Table 2).

Study locations weather data

Weather data for the study locations were retrieved from the NASA POWER Data Access Viewer (DAV) (https://power.larc.nasa.gov/data-access-viewer/).

Phenotypic data analyses

The phenotypic data were analyzed using SAS statistical software version 9.3 (SAS Institute, 2011) [38]. Individual location data were checked for normality, and for combined analyses, Hartley’s F-max test [39] was performed to check the homogeneity of error variances among environments, confirming that the data met the necessary assumptions. Mean separation among genotypes was performed using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at p < 0.05. The Multi-environment Trial Analysis with R (META-R) software package [40] was used to calculate the best linear unbiased estimation (BLUE) and to estimate variance components using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method, implemented via the lmer function of the lme4 package in R, with the environment and block treated as random effects while genotype considered as a fixed effect. The broad-sense heritability (H²) was also calculated via META-R software. GWAS was performed on the basis of the BLUE phenotypic data, which were averaged across the three environments.

SNP data analyses

Genotyping and quality control

The study population, SoySNP50K genotypic data was accessed from the SoyBase database (https://www.soybase.org/snps). The genotyping procedure is described by Song et al. [41]. A total of 41,095 SNPs were initially identified for the selected genotypes. Data processing was performed using TASSEL version 5.0 [42], where SNPs exhibiting missing data greater than 20% and those with a minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 1% were filtered out. Consequently, 38,281 SNPs remained and were used for the genome-wide association studies. The SNP density distribution was visualized using SRplot, a free online platform for data visualization and graphing software [43].

Principal components and kinship

Principal component analysis (PCA) and kinship analyses were performed on the genotype data to assess the genetic diversity among the soybean germplasm. These analyses were performed using the GAPIT package in R software [44], which utilizes a dataset of 38,281 high-quality SNP. The PCA and kinship results were visualized graphically to better understand the genetic relationships within the soybean collection.

Population structure analysis

In order to determine the population structure, STRUCTURE v2.3.4 [45] was utilized. The run length was set to 10,000 burn-in iterations followed by 10,000 Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) replications. Each value of K (number of ancestral populations) was tested with 5 independent runs, with K varying from 1 to 10. The optimal K value was predicted based on the method suggested by the Evanno method [46], which was implemented in Structure Harvester [47].

Linkage disequilibrium

Genome-wide linkage disequilibrium (LD) was analyzed using the sliding window approach in TASSEL 5.0 [42]. The window size was set to 50 neighboring SNPs to calculate the squared correlation coefficient (r²); this size was chosen to sufficiently capture the LD decay curve while maintaining computational efficiency. The critical (r²) threshold, beyond which LD was considered to be caused by physical linkage, was set at 0.2. To estimate the LD decay rate, (r²) values were plotted against physical distance (bp), and a smoothing spline regression was fitted to the data following the approach described by Hill and Weir (1988) using R software [48]

Genome-wide association analyses

Six multilocus GWAS models were utilized to detect association signals related to resistance to RLB, including mrMLM [49], FASTmrMLM [50], FASTmrEMMA [51], pLARmEB [51], pkwmEB [52], and ISIS EM-BLASSO [50]. All methods were implemented using mrMLM model. The GUI R package provides a graphical user interface for the multilocus random SNP-effect mixed linear model [51]. The mrMLM GUI software was used to calculate the kinship internally. Both the kinship matrix and population structure were included in each of the six multilocus models to control for false marker trait associations caused by stratification effects, thereby increasing the accuracy and power of QTN detection [49]. QTNs with an LOD score of ≥ 3.00 across all six mrMLM methods were considered significant [51]. Markers associated with resistance to RLB by at least three of the models were identified as reliable QTNs [53].

Candidate gene discovery

Candidate genes located within an estimated ± 229 kb of the significant SNPs were identified using the G. max Williams 82.a2 reference genome available in SoyBase (https://mines.legumeinfo.org/glycinemine/genomicRegionSearch.do). To determine their functions, we performed functional annotation of each gene using the SoyBase database (https://legacy.soybase.org/genomeannotation//accessed on May 16, 2025).

Distribution of SNPs across the soybean genome

A total of 41,095 SNP markers have been identified in 323 soybean accessions. The dataset was filtered and SNPs with MAF < 0.01 and SNPs with more than 20% missing data were excluded, resulting in a robust dataset of 38,281 SNPs, which were extensively distributed over 20 chromosomes of the soybean genotypes. The number of SNPs ranged from 1459 for chromosome 20 to 3030 for chromosome 03, with an average of 1914.05 SNPs per chromosome. The MAF of these SNPs ranged from 0.22 to 0.3 and was on average 0.27 (Supplementary Table 2). Genome–wide association study

A total of 59 QTNs were identified in five different chromosomes, which are significantly associated to RLB in at least one of the six ML-GWAS models with a LOD score > 3 (Fig. 8 and supplementary Table 4). Each method has demonstrated a different level of efficiency in identifying QTNs associated with RLB resistance. The pKWmEB method performed the best overall, detecting a total of 13 QTNs, followed by the FASTmrMLM method, which identified a total of 12 QTNs. The ISIS EM-BLASSO and pLARmEB methods each detected a total of 11 QTNs, whereas the mrMLM method identified 8 QTNs. The FASTmrEMMA method detected the lowest with only 4 QTNs. Among the six methods, pLARmEB had the maximum (8.26) LOD score, followed by FASTmrMLM (7.6) and mrMLM (6.08)(Table 4).

Prediction of potential candidate genes

For the candidate gene search, a 229 kb window upstream and downstream was considered on the basis of LD decay observed in our study. In this study, we identified 284 candidate genes from the most reliable QTNs (Table 5), with individual associated SNPs for 42 genes (ss715590644), 51 genes (ss715608049), 39 genes (ss715613225), 46 genes (ss715614480), 43 genes (ss715615182), and 63 genes (ss715635943) (Table 5). Notably, 131 of these genes had uncharacterized or unknown protein functions. The other 153 genes had known protein functions with molecular functions and cellular and biological processes (Supplementary Table 4). These genes are involved in several plant stress responses. The most important genes encode proteins involved in disease resistance, such as Glyma.05g123600, Glyma.19g254800, which encode WRKY domain-containing proteins; Glyma.10g276100, which encodes HBP-1b transcription factors; and Glyma.19g257400, which encodes MYB5 repressors. Other key signaling components include kinase and phosphatase enzymes such as Glyma.05g123500 (CDKA;1), Glyma.10g275400 and Glyma.19g253700 (protein kinase), Glyma.10g275800 (Ser/Thr phosphatase 2 A regulatory subunit), (Glyma.05g123700, Glyma.10g276600, Glyma.19g255600), metabolic enzymes (e.g., methyltransferases, caffeoylshikimate esterase-like, glycosyltransferases), and domain-containing regulators such as bZIP, CLE30, and zinc finger proteins. Additional genes included vacuolar iron transporters (Glyma.05g121300), nodulins (Glyma.05g121600), sugar transporters (Glyma.05g122200), amino acid transporters (Glyma.10g278100), and CMP-sialic acid transporters (Glyma.19g253600).

Discussion

Phenotypic variation

Soybean red leaf blotch (RLB) is a major threat to soybean production worldwide [18], with a particularly severe impact in Sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia, where yields were reported to be reduced by up to 70% [18]. Spread to high-producing regions, such as those in North and South America or parts of Asia, could be devastating [54]. Due to its seriousness, the US government Federal Select Agent Program has classified the causal agent of RLB disease, the fungus Coniothyrium glycines, as a select agent [55]. Despite this potential threat, there have been limited efforts at the global level in screening and evaluating the available germplasm for the response to RLB disease, including in hotspots within Ethiopia. Identifying RLB resistance genotypes and mapping associated genomic regions could facilitate the development of improved varieties.

In this study a genetically diverse panel of soybean genotypes sourced from the USDA Soybean Germplasm Collection, Urbana, Illinois, USA from different maturity groups and originated from more than 25 countries, were screened for RLB severity levels. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) for mean RLB severity revealed highly significant differences (p < 0.001) among, genotype, location, and their interaction (Table 3). The significant differences among genotypes indicate that there is considerable genetic diversity in response to RLB, which can be harnessed in breeding programs for RLB resistance. Although only a few genotypes had moderate resistance, none demonstrated complete immunity across all test environments (Fig. 3B, and Supplementary Table 1).

The study also showed that RLB infestation was significantly affected by location and genotype-by-location interactions (Table 3). Hence, resistance observed under one environmental condition may not be effective under another. In this study, genotypes performed differently across locations (Supplementary Table 1). Factors such as temperature, humidity, soil type, and rainfall influence disease development and severity [56, 57]. The presence of different pathogen strains in various locations can further alter resistance levels among genotypes, with notable variation in RLB pathogen populations in Ethiopia [58]. Despite variability in weather data (Fig. 2) across test locations, all sites had conducive conditions to RLB occurrence, especially from June to August, with optimal temperature, high soil moisture, humidity, rainfall, and wind speed. However based on the weather data, Metu was the most favorable site for RLB occurrence compared to Pawe and Asosa (Fig. 2). Previous research shows RLB infects soybean at temperatures between 20 °C and 25 °C [55, 61]. In line with these findings, earlier studies have reported significant differences among soybean genotypes tested across different locations in Africa, including Ethiopia, in response to RLB infection [34, 54, 60]. However, in the current study, a few soybean lines, including PI548178, PI068604-1, PI089775, PI407241, PI468908, PI507595, PI548490, PI578499A, and PI629014, exhibited mean RLB severity ratings below 2 across all locations, showed a consistent ability to reduce disease severity across environments (Supplementary Table 1). This indicates that these lines demonstrate pathotype-non-specific resistance or partial resistance to RLB. Such resistance is likely controlled by multiple minor genes, provide durable and broad-spectrum protection against the disease [59].

SNP distribution and LD

In the present study, genotypic data for the soybean accession revealed that the SNP distribution within 1 Mb windows exhibited considerable heterogeneity, ranging from regions with no detected SNPs to those with dense SNP clustering (Fig. 4). This variation highlights the uneven nature of SNP density across the soybean genome. Notably, chromosomes such as Chr17, 4, and 8 display higher overall SNP densities than other chromosomes such as Chr5, suggesting potential differences in genomic architecture and recombination rates among chromosomes. The heterogeneous distribution of SNP density has important implications for linkage disequilibrium, QTN mapping, and GWAS analyses, influencing the resolution and statistical power [62].

In the present study, we observed that LD decay to r² = 0.2 occurred at approximately 229 kb (Fig. 5). This LD decay indicates that the population is characterized by genomic LD blocks of 229 kb, at r2 = 0.2. This is within the range reported in previous soybean studies. The LD patterns in soybean populations are highly variable; a previous study indicated that the LD ranged from 90 to 574 kb in cultivated soybean but not more than 100 kb in wild soybeans [63]. The LD decay rate for self-pollinated crops is generally higher than cross-pollinated [64].

Principal component analysis (PCA) and kinship

PCA revealed a notable level of genetic divergence among the tested soybean accessions. The distribution of genotypes across the principal components (Fig. 6A) suggests substantial genetic variation within the population, which can be advantageous for breeding. Similarly, on the basis of the kinship matrix analysis, the genotypes demonstrated the presence of genetic diversity, as evidenced by varying degrees of relatedness. The dendrogram delineates distinct genetic clusters (Fig. 6B).

Population structure analysis

Population structure analysis (Fig. 7B) identified four distinct subpopulations within the 323 genotypes of soybeans evaluated in this study. Each subpopulation showed a unique response to red leaf blotch (RLB), which reflecting the underlying genetic diversity between genotypes. This genetic stratification provides critical information on genetic relationships and variability within the population. In terms of ancestry (Fig. 7C) the varying proportions of colors within each bar indicate differences in genetic backgrounds, suggesting levels of gene flow, hybridization, or isolation among groups. Some genotypes exhibit high genetic heterogeneity with evenly distributed colors, indicating high diversity. Others are more homogeneous, with dominant colors suggesting lower diversity. Understanding these patterns is crucial for elucidating the evolutionary processes shaping these populations and can also inform strategies for conservation, breeding, and management by identifying genetically distinct or admixed groups that may require targeted approaches. The clustering of 323 soybean accessions from over 25 countries into four groups(Fig. 7D) demonstrates high genetic diversity and extensive admixture, with no clear geographic separation. This suggests significant gene flow among different regions.

Genome-wide association study

In this study, six multilocus GWAS methods were employed. The six ML- GWAS methods detected a different number of QTNs (Fig. 8). The pKWmEB method identified the highest number of QTNs, whereas the FASTmrEMMA method detected the lowest. These differences are primarily due to variations in the underlying statistical models, assumptions, and sensitivities to diverse genetic architectures.

Among the 59 QTNs identified by the six GWAS models, only the six QTNs located on chromosomes 5, 10, 12, 13, and 19 were consistently detected by at least three models (Table 5; Fig. 9). The simultaneous detection of these QTNs across multiple approaches suggests that these loci are likely to be robust and reliable markers associated with RLB resistance. Notably, these significant QTNs were identified in regions with low marker density on chromosomes 5, 10, 12, 13, and 19 (Fig. 4). This finding indicates that their detection is driven primarily by the actual genomic positions of genes influencing resistance to RLB or by strong associations and large-effect loci rather than by marker density. Conversely, the absence of detected QTNs in regions with higher marker density, such as on chromosomes 4, 8, and 17, suggests that major functional variants for this trait are unlikely to be located in those regions within our population.

Notably, in this study, the QTNs ss715635943 and ss715613225 were simultaneous detected via five methods, whereas ss715590644, ss715615182, ss715608049, and ss715614480 were identified via three methods (Table 5). These QTNs are reliably associated with RLB resistance and explain more than 3.6% of the phenotypic variation (Table 5). A previous reports [34], also observed putative resistance QTN on chromosomes 5, 10, 12, 13, and 19, but not in the same chromosome location. However, the QTN on chromosome 5 (ss715590644) at position 31,603,860 is located in close proximity to previously reported QTNs at the position 30,968,142 on the same chromosome [34]. Similarly, the QTN (ss715608049) at position 49,809,728 on chromosome 10, identified in our study, is near the previously reported position 48,178,692 on chromosome 10, which is associated with RLB resistance [34], adding some validation that these genetic regions might be associated with RLB resistance. Other QTNs identified in this study did not co-localize near previously reported loci associated with RLB resistance, suggesting opportunities for discovering novel QTNs, or perhaps defense related genes specific to the isolates existing in different geographical locations.

Within the LD block plus or minus 229kb, most of the identified QTNs in this study colocalized with previously reported QTNs related to various traits, including QTNs on chromosome 5 at marker position 31,603,860,linked to oil and protein content [65], chromosome 10 at marker position 49,809,728, associated with water use efficiency [66], chromosome 13, related to soybean cyst nematode (SCN) resistance [67], and to full maturity [69], and chromosome 19 the marker position 50,087,176, associated with flowering time [69] and to shoot iron content [68]. While these QTNs are not directly linked to RLB resistance, their proximity or shared genetic regions to resistance associated loci may indicate linkage or pleiotropic effects that could indirectly influence disease resistance.

In the current study the top three QTNs with the highest phenotypic effects i.e., ss715615182 (6.9%), ss715614480 (6.3%), and ss715613225 (5.9%) (Table 5) highlights their potential as a valuable markers for the genetic improvement of soybean for resistance to RLB disease. The explanatory power of all the six QTNs identified in this study accounts for less than 10% of the total phenotypic variance, and none are classified as major QTN effect. It is common that QTNs typically explain less than 10% of the phenotypic variance in GWAS for complex traits, which are generally polygenic and affected by numerous genes with small individual effects and environmental influences. Nonetheless, QTNs explaining less than 7% of the phenotypic variance can still be valuable in breeding programs when combined through marker-assisted selection (MAS). Although their individual effects are modest, stacking multiple QTNs may lead to significant genetic gains. Given their limited effect sizes and potential interactions, these QTNs are most effectively utilized in conjunction with other breeding strategies, such as genomic selection.

In this study, the six significant QTNs associated with resistance to RLB in soybean revealed that accessions possessing homozygous alleles GG on chromosome 5 at marker position 31,603,860, AA on chromosome 10 at marker position 49,809,728, and CC on chromosome 12 at marker position 6,092,650, as well as both AA and TT alleles on chromosome 13 at position 27,143,270 and at position 31,063,831 and CC on chromosome 19 ,presented a greater level of resistance to RLB than accessions with alternative alleles (Table 5). This finding aligns with previous reports indicating that homozygous alleles confer greater resistance to RLB compared to heterozygous alleles [34]. Specifically, homozygous genotypes such as GG on chromosome 6 at the position Gm06_20112134 and Gm16_34649045, TT on chromosome 16, at the position, Gm16_3302971 and CC or TT on chromosome 19 at the position Gm19_44916522 have been associated with increased resistance to RLB [34].

Prediction of potential candidate genes

For the candidate gene search, a 229 kb window upstream and downstream was used based on the estimated LD decay observed in our study. A total of 284 genes were identified associated with the six significant loci. Among these genes, 131 are uncharacterized or of unknown function, while 153 encode known proteins (Supplementary Table 4). The functional annotation of the identified genes revealed the diverse roles of these genes in defense against pathogens and environmental stresses (Supplementary Table 4). Notably, genes such as Glyma.05g123900 and Glyma.05g124000 encode polygalacturonase-inhibiting proteins (PGIPs), which are vital in plant defense because they inhibit pathogen-secreted polygalacturonases (PGs) and activates defense responses [70–75]. The other genes, Glyma.05g123000, Glyma.05g123600, and Glyma.19g254800, encode WRKY domain-containing proteins that play crucial roles in plant defense responses against pathogens [76–79]. The genes Glyma.13g197800 and Glyma.19g257400 encode MYB transcription factors that play crucial roles in plant stress responses and phenylpropanoid metabolism [80]. R2R3-MYB repressors regulate lignin and flavonoid biosynthesis, whereas R3-MYB repressors control anthocyanin production [80, 81]. Soybean R2R3-MYB transcription factors are involved in responses to biotic stresses, including soybean rust infection caused by Phakopsora pachyrhizi with some genes related to lignin synthesis and the chitin response being induced in resistance genotypes [82]. Glyma.05g122400 encodes a BZIP domain-containing protein; BZIP transcription factors that play crucial roles in plant defense responses and stress signaling [83, 84]. Four soybean BZIP genes (GmbZIP62, GmbZIP105, GmbZIPE1, and GmbZIPE2) were found expressed during Phakopsora pachyrhizi infection [85]. Another BZIP gene, GmbZIP19, was reported to increase resistance to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum and Pseudomonas syringae when overexpressed [86].

CONSTANS-like (COL) zinc finger proteins are encoded by Glyma.10g274300 and Glyma.19g257500. The CONSTANS-like (COL) gene family encodes zinc finger proteins that play crucial roles in plant development and stress responses [87, 88]. A C2H2-type zinc finger protein transcription factor, GmZFP03, confers resistance to Phytophthora sojae by targeting two SOD1 promoters [89]. On chromosome 10, Glyma.10g277100 encodes a cationic amino acid transporter that could play a role in plant‒pathogen interactions, influencing both plant defense responses and pathogen nutrition [90–93 ]. Glyma.12g077500 encodes LysM domain receptor-like kinase 4. LysM is a receptor-like kinases (RLKs) that have been reported to possibly play a role in plant innate immunity by recognizing chitin, a fungal cell wall component [94, 95]. Glyma.13g155800 encodes the protein callose synthase 5. Callose, a β-1,3-glucan polymer, is important in plant development and defense responses [95] and is synthesized by callose synthases, encoded by GSL genes [96]. GSL5 is responsible for stress-induced callose deposition, forming physical barriers against pathogens [96, 97]. Other genes, Glyma.13g194900 and Glyma.13g195100, encode TIR (Toll/interleukin-1 receptor) domains, which may play crucial roles in plant immunity and stress responses. In soybean, GmTIR overexpression enhances tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses [98]. TIR-NBS and TIR-X proteins are involved in basal defense responses, interact with various proteins and potentially act in guard complexes to monitor pathogen effectors [99, 100]. Glyma.13g195600 encodes an RPP13-like protein. The RPP13 gene in Arabidopsis thaliana encodes a disease resistance protein that recognizes the ATR13 effector from the downy mildew pathogen Hyaloperonospora parasitica [101]. RPP13 exhibits extreme amino acid diversity, particularly in its leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain, which is maintained through reciprocal selection between the host and pathogen [102].

Glyma.12g077300, Glyma.12g078800, Glyma.05g123200, Glyma.05g124200, and Glyma.10g275400 encode protein kinase domain-containing proteins. These proteins are known to play roles in plant defense responses [103]. Salicylic acid-induced protein kinase (SIPK), a mitogen-activated protein kinase, can induce multiple defense responses, including gene expression and hypersensitive response-like cell death [104]. Calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs) are important Ca²⁺ sensors that transduce signals triggered by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or effectors, regulating diverse immune responses [105]. The CDPK isoform CPK5 is rapidly activated in response to PAMP stimulation and phosphorylates the NADPH oxidase RBOHD, demonstrating the importance of protein kinases in both local and systemic immunity [106–108]. GmMPK3 and GmMPK6 phosphorylate the receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase GmCDL1, enhancing soybean resistance to pathogens such as the soybean cyst nematode [109]. Additionally, GmCDPKs are involved in soybean defense, with 50 GmCDPK genes showing specific expression patterns in response to insect and drought stress, indicating their importance in both biotic and abiotic stress responses [110].

Limitations of the study

The current study was conducted across three locations in a single growing season; while this captures spatial environmental heterogeneity, which may not capture interannual variability and could limit the broader applicability of the findings across different seasons. Significant environmental heterogeneity among the sites may introduce confounding effects, making it challenging to attribute results solely to genetic factors or gene expression. Additionally, the use of two replications per site is a common constraint in high-diversity GWAS panels, though the large population size (n = 323) and multi-location data help maintain statistical power.The germplasm panel comprised exclusively introduced lines from the United States, limiting insights into how local genotypes respond to RLB and potentially hindering the identification of region-specific resistance genes. However, the inclusion of a highly diverse set of genotypes from over 25 countries provides a valuable advantage, offering insights into RLB resistance that can be leveraged in breeding programs. Given Ethiopia reliance on introducing genetic material for soybean improvement, and many of the local lines have been introduced from the USA, the materials used in this study are adaptable to local agro-ecological conditions. The extensive genetic diversity within this panel enhances the potential for discovering novel resistance genes, and the findings could be extrapolated to other regions. Addressing these limitations in future research will be essential to improve the robustness and broader applicability of the results.

Conclusion

In the present study, considerable genetic variation was observed among the evaluated genotypes for resistance to RLB. Only a few genotypes showed moderate resistance to RLB, and there were no immune genotypes. This study successfully identified six significant QTNs or genomic regions associated with resistance to red leaf blotch using multilocus genome-wide association analysis. Two of the identifed QTNs colocalized with previously reported QTNs associated with resistance to RLB. A total of 284 genes associated with the six significant QTNs were identified, among which 153 encode proteins with known functions. The functional annotation of some candidate genes indicated their involvement in defense mechanisms, signaling pathways, and stress responses. It is recommended to conduct functional validation of these candidate genes, as well as to explore potential gene interactions.
