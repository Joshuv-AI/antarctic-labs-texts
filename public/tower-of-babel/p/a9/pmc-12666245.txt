Long‐term risk and predictors of high‐risk human papillomavirus persistence after thermal ablation amongst women living with HIV in West Africa
Authors: Simon Boni, Pierre Debaudrap, Firmin N. Kabore, Evelyne Kasile‐Pooda, Armel Poda, Belarsi Ouattara, Eugene Messou, Brahima Doukoure, Antoine Jaquet, Apollinaire Horo, on behalf IeDEA West Africa Collaboration
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMounting evidence supports the use of thermal ablation in women positively screened with high‐risk Human Papillomaviruses (hrHPV) but limited data are available on the long‐term post‐treatment outcomes in women living with HIV (WLHIV). We aimed to estimate the persistence of hrHPV infection amongst WLHIV ≥24 months post treatment in West Africa.MethodsFrom October 2019 to October 2024, a cohort study was conducted amongst WLHIV in two HIV clinics in Burkina Faso and Cote d'Ivoire. All WLHIV with a positive hrHPV test who received thermal ablation were followed up ≥24 months. During follow‐up visits, DNA HPV testing, visual inspection and biopsy were systematically performed. Factors associated with ≥24 months hrHPV positivity were assessed through a logistic regression model.ResultsA total of 200 WLHIV, aged 42 years [Interquartile range (IQR): 38–45], with a nadir CD4 of 365 [IQR: 168–616] cell/mm3 received thermal ablation and were followed for a median time of 42 [IQR: 29–48] months. A positive hrHPV was detected in 40.5% of women ≥24 months post treatment. WLHIV who had a nadir CD4 count ≤200 cell/mm3 (aOR = 3.06 [95% CI: 1.23–7.59]) or had no or a primary school level (aOR = 2.25 [95% CI: 1.13–4.49]) were more likely to present at ≥24 months with hrHPV infection.ConclusionPost‐therapeutic hrHPV infection remains high beyond 2 years in WLHIV stressing the need for long‐term follow‐up, especially when diagnosed with advanced HIV disease. Future implementation research should focus on the contribution of additional tools to better track those in need of additional treatment.

INTRODUCTION

The persistence of high‐risk human Papillomaviruses (hrHPV) is the major cause of cervical cancer which is the second most common and the deadliest cancer in Sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA) in females [1, 2]. Although, most infections spontaneously cleared within 2 years, [3] women living with HIV (WLHIV) are at a higher risk of persistent hrHPV infection [4]. Thus, they are six times at a higher risk of being diagnosed with invasive cervical cancer compared to their immunocompetent counterparts [5]. The high burden of this malignancy in SSA is therefore fuelled by the high prevalence of HIV in the region [1, 2, 5].

Primary screening based on the detection of hrHPV is now recognized as the most effective approach and is being scaled up by a growing number of national cervical cancer screening programmes across resource‐limited settings [6, 7, 8]. Ablative treatment options based on cryotherapy which require extreme cold temperatures to destroy precancerous cells, have been largely used in SSA during the last two decades with demonstrated feasibility and impact on reducing high‐grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) progression [9, 10]. More recently, thermal ablation, an alternative ablative therapy based on high temperatures to destroy precancerous cells has been progressively replacing cryotherapy to address the important logistical issues of the latter [6, 11, 12]. As per the WHO global cervical cancer elimination initiative, thermal ablation is now promoted to replace cryotherapy for the treatment of eligible premalignant lesions in resource‐constraint settings [13]. Several countries in SSA have made investments in rolling out thermal ablation devices as part of their cervical cancer screening programmes [6, 14, 15, 16]. Side effects with thermal ablation especially related to bleeding and fertility, are less important compared to excision, and similar in terms of pain and vaginal discharge [6, 17, 18]. Overall the procedure is usually well tolerated, with fair satisfaction in women with eligible precancerous lesions [19, 20, 21, 22].

Previous studies reported a high risk of hrHPV and cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or higher (CIN2+) persistence following treatment in WLHIV [7, 23]. Post‐treatment persistence of hrHPV has been identified as an important predictor of persistence/recurrence of CIN2+ [24, 25]. Existing evidence has led WHO to recommend a 24‐month HPV‐based follow‐up in WLHIV after treatment [26]. However, limited information is currently available on the persistence of hrHPV beyond 12 months' post‐treatment in WLHIV, especially after thermal ablation [7, 20, 27, 28]. Treating WLHIV with thermal ablation based on partial genotyping (including HPV16/18/45) is now an option supported by WHO and might be increasingly implemented in the absence of available/affordable alternative triage options in low‐income settings such as West Africa. This approach will translate into treating a number of women with no underlying histological lesions. To what extent thermal ablation will clear hrHPV in this population is unknown. The use of antiretroviral therapy (ART) as well as low immunologic status has been previously identified as important determinants of hrHPV persistence, but there is limited information on predictors of post‐treatment persistent hrHPV in the era of universal ART [29]. Therefore, more data on long‐term outcomes after ablative treatment, and particularly thermal ablation in WLHIV is still needed to help understand the pattern of optimal post‐screening management in WLHIV. We aimed to estimate the risk of hrHPV persistence and predictors amongst WLHIV beyond 24 months following thermal ablation in West Africa.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Design and setting

From October 2019 to October 2024, a cohort study was conducted amongst WLHIV in two HIV clinics in Bobo‐Dioulasso (Burkina Faso) and Abidjan (Cote d'Ivoire). In Abidjan, the ‘Centre de Prise en Charge, de Recherche et de Formation (CePReF)’ at Yopougon is an HIV care facility which hosts an accredited lab for molecular biology for HPV detection [30]. It is one of the main HIV clinics of the urban area of Abidjan actively following over 2000 WLHIV aged 25–55 years in 2024. In Burkina Faso, the ‘Hopital du jour’ (HDJ) is part of the university hospital of Sourou‐Sanon, in charge of HIV and opportunistic infections care in Bobo‐Dioulasso. In 2024, over 2500 WLHIV were in care in this HIV clinic.

Population and procedures

Participants contributing to this ≥24 months follow‐up analysis were initially part of the AIMA‐CC study (ANRS 12375), a former research study that assessed the performances of various triage options for the management of WLHIV tested positive for hrHPV [31]. Participants were eligible if they presented with a documented HIV infection, were on antiretroviral therapy for ≥12 months, and were aged 30–49 years. Women who were pregnant, had undergone a previous hysterectomy, or had been treated for cervical precancer or cancer in the previous 12 months were excluded. All WLHIV who received thermal ablation based on a positive HPV 16/18/45 and/or a positive visual inspection were followed up at 12 months as scheduled as part of the AIMA‐CC study, and then at 24 months and beyond in the IPHOTHER (Incidence and Persistence of HPV and precancerous lesions recurrence long‐term after thermal ablation) study. In each participating HIV clinic, the clinical team comprised a senior gynaecologist and two midwives who ensured a gynaecological examination with cervical sampling for HPV testing, visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) and lugol (VILI), and a systematic biopsy.

The detection of hrHPV was done through sequencing of HPV viral DNA carried out using a multiplex real‐time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (GeneXpert®, Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) as a point‐of‐care approach. The process included DNA extraction, amplification and detection in one fully integrated cartridge. The 14 hrHPV were detected simultaneously and results were displayed following the outputs of the five fluorescent channels: HPV16; HPV18/45; HPV31/33/35/52/58; HPV51/59; HPV39/56/66/68. Biopsies were performed on acetowhite lesions or systematically at 6 and 12 o'clock when the VIA/VILI test was negative.

At baseline, thermal ablation was performed on the same day when the VIA/VILI was positive or negative but with a positive partial genotyping including one of the following hrHPV (16, 18 or 45), except in the case of extended acetowhite lesions which required loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) and cold conisation, planned secondarily at a referral facility. Post‐therapeutic recommendations included avoiding sexual activities during the following month post‐treatment, limited vaginal toilet as well as additional ART adherence counselling.

Follow‐up visits were planned at 12 and at least 24 months from the day of thermal ablation (≥24 months) at the same HIV clinics, by the same clinical team. Research assistants systematically recalled all treated WLHIV, through phone calls to honour their planned schedules. During follow‐up visits, a gynaecological examination was repeated including provider‐based cervical sampling for hrHPV detection and VIA/VILI. A colposcopy was additionally performed at ≥24 months post treatment to evaluate the new transformation zone (TZ) and to enable a better assessment of precancerous lesions according to the International Agency for Research on Cancer classification [32]. Colposcopy‐directed biopsies were systematically performed at this visit.

Data collection and outcomes

Individual data such as age, education, marital and occupation status, tobacco use and the number of living births (parity) were collected at enrolment by the research assistant in the HIV clinic. Clinical examination and colposcopy results were captured in the database at the same time, while histology was completed when pathological results were available. HIV characteristics were captured from the clinic routine HIV care database and merged with the prospectively collected data at the end of the study. Following Hoffman et al definition [33], overall hrHPV persistence was defined as having at least one hrHPV type (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66 and 68) at 12 and ≥24 months post‐thermal ablation. Type‐specific hrHPV persistence was defined as harbouring the same hrHPV channel at three consecutive time points – baseline, 12 and ≥24 months. Precancerous lesions persistence was defined as having a CIN2+ result at 12 months (biopsy systematically at 6 and 12 h) and at ≥24 months after a colposcopy‐directed biopsy considering those with CIN2+ at baseline.

Data analysis

Categorical variables were presented as counts with proportions, while continuous variables were presented as medians with their interquartile ranges (IQR). Comparisons were possible using Chi‐squared or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, or student's T‐test and Wilcoxon test for continuous variables, when applicable. Incidence and persistence/recurrence of hrHPV infection and precancerous lesions rates were calculated overall, and in each subgroup of the population study. The McNemar's Chi‐squared test was used to compare the proportions of hrHPV positivity, VIA/VILI positivity and CIN2+ between baseline and 12 months; and between 12 and ≥24 months. A logistic regression analysis was used to assess predictors of hrHPV persistence at ≥24 months following treatment. The independent variables included age at thermal ablation, educational level, number of living births, tobacco use, WHO clinical stage, CD4 count at ART initiation and at last known visit before thermal ablation. Treatment modalities (number of probe applications) were also considered. Crude and adjusted Odds Ratio (aOR) with their IC95% were computed. In bivariate analysis, variables with a p value < 0.25 and other potential confounding variables based on previous literature such as age, participating site/country (because of differences in individual and HIV characteristics), as well as the number of thermocoagulator probe applications were considered in multivariable analysis. The likelihood ratio test was applied to compare nested models and assess the contribution of additional variables. All analyses were performed using STATA 14.1.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by national ethics committees from Cote d'Ivoire and from Burkina Faso for the cohort building until 12 months [31]. An additional approval was obtained from the two countries' ethical agencies (Ref 174‐23/MSHPCMU/CNESVS‐km for Cote d'Ivoire; Ref No. 2022–07‐161 for Burkina Faso) to pursue the follow‐up until >24 months post treatment. All the medical records were anonymized. An informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

More than one hrHPV group was found in 57 (28.5%), 26 (13.0%) and 10 (5.0%) at baseline, 12 and >24 months post thermal ablation, respectively.

Type‐specific hrHPV persistence rate was found in 81 (40.5%) and 38 (19.0%) at 12 and >24 months, respectively. At endline, 38 out of the 81 (46.9%) WLHIV had the same detected hrHPV genotype when considering specifically those persisted at 12 months post thermal ablation. At ≥24 months, HPV16, HPV 18/45, HPV 31/33/35/52/58, HPV 51/59 and HPV 39/56/66/68 persistence were retrieved in 36.2% (n = 17); in 5.5% (n = 2), 25.9% (n = 28); 6.3% (n = 2) and 3.9% (n = 2) of participants, respectively (Table 3). The CIN2+ rate declined from 12.0% (n = 24) at baseline, to 4.0% (n = 8) at 12 months (p = 0.003) and 3.5% (n = 7) at ≥24 months post treatment.

Incident CIN2+ cases were retrieved in 5 (2.8%) and in 4 (2.2%) participants at 12 and ≥24 months respectively. All three persistent CIN2+ at 12 months, retreated by thermal ablation, remained at ≥24 months, including one cancer in situ.

The sensitivity analysis showed that a hrHPV positivity rate was retrieved in 88 (50%) and 17 (70.8%) at 12 months (p = 0.055); and in 65 (36.9%) and 16 (66.7%) at ≥24 months post thermal ablation (p = 0.005) amongst participants without and with CIN2+ at baseline. Amongst WLHIV with a hrHPV positive test at ≥24 months post thermal ablation, 7 (8.6%) were ATG II or III at colposcopy. VIA/VILI declined from 87.5% to 35.5% between the time of thermal ablation and the 12‐month visit (p < 0.001), before an increase to reach 48.0% at the ≥24 months visit (p = 0.005).

Predictors of hrHPV persistence at ≥24 months post thermal ablation

After adjusting for time since post thermal ablation, age, tobacco use, country, the number of probe applications, and multiple hrHPV at baseline, WLHIV who had a nadir CD4 count ≤200 cells/mm3 (aOR = 3.06 [95%CI: 1.23–7.59]), having no or a primary school level (aOR = 2.25 [95%CI: 1.13–4.49]), and the existence of a CIN2+ before thermal ablation (aOR = 4.62 [95% CI: 1.62–13.12]), were more likely to present at ≥24 months with overall hrHPV persistence (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Findings from this cohort study demonstrated a high persistence of hrHPV positivity beyond 2 years post thermal ablation in WLHIV. This risk of hrHPV ≥24 months was independently predicted by advanced HIV disease, a low educational level and the existence of CIN2+ at baseline. Most importantly, HPV16, usually considered for treatment based on partial genotyping and HPV 31/33/35/52/58 remained substantially high over time.

Data on post‐treatment hrHPV beyond 12 months is particularly scarce; however, our results seemed high compared to the existing literature. A systematic review presenting data on long‐term post‐treatment outcomes reported hrHPV persistence beyond 12 months of 18% following cryotherapy for CIN [33]. The high hrHPV persistence beyond 2 years raises concerns on the specificity of hrHPV in identifying women deserving further treatment. WHO currently recommends that women treated for a positive hrHPV and/or CIN2/3, regardless of HIV status undergo HPV DNA monitoring at 12 months instead of cytology VIA, or co‐testing. They should then transition to routine screening intervals as recommended if the result is negative [26]. This points out the question of which methods and algorithms would better identify WLHIV who should be closely followed up beyond 1 year post treatment. This may reflect the need to reconsider the role of HPV genotyping in follow‐up protocols, especially in resource‐limited settings where triage tools are limited. As some of these persistent infections may be transient, further studies are needed to consider the role of alternative algorithms for the follow‐up of treated WLHIV based on individual high‐risk factors. In settings where HPV testing screening programmes are limited, VIA performed by qualified healthcare workers or colposcopy could be systematically used during follow‐up visits and HPV testing offered to a subgroup of WLHIV with a high risk of hrHPV persistence. The main challenge is the common variation of the squamocolumnar junction and the TZ after thermal ablation, making VIA less appropriate long‐term post‐treatment [34].

Immunodeficiency probably acts as a major mediating factor supporting the lower clearance of hrHPV observed in WLHIV with low CD4 count compared to others. Indeed, those with advanced HIV disease at entry into HIV care were more likely to harbour hrHPV genotypes beyond 24 months, after adjusting for individual, lifestyle factors and follow‐up duration. Immune suppression is associated with a diminished capacity to clear hrHPV infections, resulting in a higher likelihood of persistence or reinfection over time [29, 35, 36]. A meta‐analysis of 1452 WLHIV followed until 33 months post‐CIN management, demonstrated evidence of higher recurrence of CIN in those with advanced HIV disease measured by nadir CD4+ count <200/mm3 [37]. Data suggest that this high risk persists even in the era of universal ART [38, 39]. HIV programmes should therefore reinforce national efforts to track new HIV infections and earlier ART initiation combined with an enhanced and tailored post‐cervical cancer screening surveillance for WLHIV positively screened for precancerous cervical lesions.

In our study, ART duration was not associated with hrHPV persistence as previously reported in earlier analyses [4, 23, 29, 40, 41]. Therefore, the risk of persisting hrHPV seemed not influenced by women's past history of HIV treatment stressing the need for a close post‐thermal ablation follow‐up regardless of HIV treatment status. Chronic immune activation and alterations of the cervical microenvironment relying on chronic inflammation might impair cervical mucosal immunity, leading to persistence, even in the context of prolonged ART use as demonstrated by Casselli and Mu, and al [42, 43]. More importantly, Liu and al, demonstrated a limited impact of ART in lowering the progression from normal status to high‐grade lesions [4].

As expected, WLHIV who experienced a CIN2+ at the moment of thermal ablation were more likely to harbour a persistent hrHPV beyond 24 months post treatment. Indeed it has been previously demonstrated that high‐grade lesions resulted in a disrupted cervical microenvironment, hindering viral clearance [42, 43]. In a setting where histology is more accessible, WLHIV with CIN2+ at screening might require more intensive and longer post‐thermal ablation follow‐up. In all cases, a close clinical, virological (HIV and HPV parameters) and immunological follow‐up is needed, as the association between longer post‐thermal ablation follow‐up interval and hrHPV persistence has been well demonstrated [33, 40]. While all hrHPV declined over time since thermal ablation, HPV16 declined at a slower rate compared to other hrHPV. This finding corroborates those from WLHIV in a Tanzanian report where HPV45/16/18/58 persistence was predominant [40]. HPV16 is known as the most oncogenic genotype, with a stronger ability to evade host immune responses and integrate into host DNA, particularly in immunosuppressed individuals such as WLHIV [44, 45].

The association of hrHPV persistence with lower education is potentially linked with health literacy, adherence to post‐thermal ablation recommendations, and timely care‐seeking behaviours [46, 47]. This knowledge gap may impair adherence to recommended surveillance schedules and one‐month abstinence recommendations, thereby facilitating the persistence/recurrence of hrHPV infections. However, the association between education and hrHPV persistence deserves further studies to understand how social determinants of health mediate the risk of persisting post‐therapeutic hrHPV. Addressing social determinants of health through integrated health education and differentiated support helped by community counsellors, may be critical to preventing post‐therapeutic cervical cancer premalignant recurrences. Ultimately, the concept of viral latency might be considered and questions the complex challenge of differentiating persistence or reactivation, as well as lag and immune control phases where hrHPV are detected that do not necessarily imply subclinical or clinical infection [48, 49].

Overall, the decrease in CIN2+ observed in our study (from 12% to 3.5%) suggests a positive impact of thermal ablation on the clearance of existing cervical cancer precursors, supporting its rollout by national cancer control programmes. Although our small sample size limits any accurate comparisons with other reports, the rate of 3.5% of precancerous lesions persistence was quite lower compared to the 11.3% found by Aka et al. 24 months after treatment in Abidjan [50].

While existing guidelines recommend 2‐year post‐treatment follow‐up in WLHIV [11], our findings suggest that this may be insufficient in this high‐risk population. A tailored, individual and risk‐stratified surveillance strategy involving annual hrHPV testing with potential triage with partial genotyping, colposcopy and/or artificial intelligence enhanced tools should be explored further. An HPV therapeutic vaccine for preventing persistence in WLHIV receiving ablative treatment could be raised, although initial findings did not suggest a significant capacity to enhance hrHPV clearance in women [51]. Through, CC screening programmes, WHO supports the use of thermal ablation without clear underlying evidence of histological lesions. This translates into a high proportion of women treated solely for the presence of hrHPV. In the absence of a reference group of WLHIV with hrHPV but not receiving thermal ablation we were not able to directly assess the capacity of thermal ablation to clear hrHPV beyond natural HPV clearance. Reports on natural HPV clearance in WLHIV are limited. In a North American cohort of WLHIV co‐infected with HPV, 70% spontaneously cleared their infection after a median time of 5.7 years [52]. While no formal comparison can be made with previous reports given the wide heterogeneity in terms of population characteristics, follow‐up time and HPV detection methods, this example raises concerns about the ability of thermal ablation to clear hrHPV and questions the usefulness of the hrHPV test as a monitoring tool to assess response to treatment after thermal ablation.

While this study is amongst the first including WLHIV followed up beyond 24 months in West Africa, a region highly impacted by both HIV and cervical cancer, it has some limitations. The small size of our study population did not allow the assessment of factors associated with precancerous lesions' persistence at ≥24 months post‐treatment. The lack of complete genotyping did not enable genotype‐specific persistence predictors' analysis. Some potential predictors of hrHPV persistence such as the duration of hrHPV infection before treatment were not available. In addition, although the two participating sites included large patient cohorts (essentially from urban areas) and have extensive research experience, they cannot fill the critical gap in evidence from rural areas, where health literacy and adoption of infection prevention strategies might remain limited. Ultimately, representativeness was also hampered by restricting our analysis to WLHIV accessing thermal ablation and still available for follow‐up during our study period. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into long‐term surveillance of hrHPV or premalignant lesions management in HIV programmes.

CONCLUSION

Post‐thermal ablation hrHPV infection remains high after 2 years in WLHIV, highlighting the need for long‐term follow‐up, particularly in WLHIV with a history of advanced HIV disease. These findings underscore the importance of expanding research towards appropriate monitoring algorithms in WLHIV treated with thermal ablation based on partial genotyping and/or a positive VIA. Challenges in post‐thermal ablation follow‐up including the low specificity of hrHPV or the inability to visualize the TZ/squamocolumnar junction in WLHIV, raise the need for additional tools to enhance tracking of those in need of additional treatment.
