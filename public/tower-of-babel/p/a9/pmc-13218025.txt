Factors associated with academic motivation among Tunisian medical students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Maroua El Ouaer, Amira Sallem, Wafa Dhouib, Dhekra Chebil, Siwar Abdelmoula, Hayet Ben Hamida, Sabrine Ben Youssef, Nidhal Haj Salem, Ali Majdoub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAccording to Self Determination Theory, motivation is divided into intrinsic and extrinsic types. The type desired for students is intrinsic motivation, which is associated with successful achievements. Some factors could modify the profile of motivation and then the outcomes. There is a huge lack of literature data concerning the motivation profile and its associated factors, among Tunisian and North-African medical students.ObjectivesThis study aimed to identify the academic motivate on profiles of medical students in Tunisia and assess the factors influencing their motivation.MethodologyThis cross-sectional study was conducted over four months: from August to November 2024. An auto-administered anonymous questionnaire based on the validated Academic Motivation Scale (AMS-C28) was distributed to medical students in the four faculties of medicine in Tunisia.ResultsA total of 689 medical students mainly aged between 18 and 22 years (65%) were enrolled in the current study. They were mostly female (71.8%) and in the second study cycle (64.3%). The median scores were 1.75 [1.00–3.00] for amotivation, 4.66 [3.75–5.41] for intrinsic motivation (IM), and 5.16 [4.41–5.75] for extrinsic motivation (EM), which was identified as the predominant profile in our cohort.According to the results of multivariate analysis, having a choice of medicine affected by family (aOR = 2.3 [1.4–3.7]), history of academic failure (aOR = 1.6 [ 1.1–2.7]), and lack of support from peers (aOR = 1.5 [1.3–1.8]) were identified as independent risk factors for amotivation. The three subscales of IM (to know, toward accomplishment and toward experience stimulation) were independently associated with female gender, students who perceived satisfaction with the quality of teaching, and those who chose medicine as a personal preference. When focusing on EM, we have shown that personal choice in choosing medicine was associated with external regulation (aOR = 1.8 [1.3–2.5]) and that identified regulation was higher among female students (OR = 2.1 [1.4–3.1]), in case of personal choice of studying medicine (aOR = 2.2 [1.5–3.2]) and when students were satisfied with the teaching quality (aOR = 1.4 [1.0–2.1]). Students whose first choice was not medicine had higher introjection scores (aOR = 2.211 [1.374–3.56]), while self-reported psychological distress were inversely associated with introjection (aOR = 0.594 [0.386–0.594]).ConclusionOur study revealed that extrinsic motivation is the main profile of Tunisian medical students. It also provides the basis to pedagogical interventions aiming to improve the educational environment through the analysis of factors associated with amotivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09051-8.

Introduction

Medical studies are widely recognized as one of the most challenging academic courses. It requires physical effort, intellectual rigor and emotional stability. Generally young students, with multiple reasons pushing them to pursue this curriculum, face many difficulties. Thus, due to the large amount of information with the pressure of clinical practice, medical students often develop high levels of stress and even burnout. Therefore, academic motivation plays a crucial role in students’ success and well-being. Effectively, an adequate profile of academic motivation maintained during all the cursus, could ensure best achievements, best results and more solid doctors. On the other hand, amotivation is associated with burnout and failure.

There are different theories of motivation. According to Self Determination Theory, humans have three types of motivations i.e. intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation and amotivation [1].

Intrinsic motivation (IM) reflects the engagement in an activity for its inherent satisfaction. Depending on the main interest, there is three types of IM: to know, to accomplishment and to experience stimulation. The phenomenon of intrinsic motivation reflects the primary propensity of organisms to engage in activities that interest them and, in so doing, to learn, develop, and expand their capacities. With regards to extrinsic motivation (EM), the person consider that the activity is a way to attempt specific external or internal goals in which the action is done to attempt a different consequence or outcome. It is devised into three identities: identified regulation, introjection, and external regulation. External regulation behavior is regulated through external means such as rewards and constraints. In introjected regulation, an individual behavior becomes valued and judged important for the individual, and especially that it is perceived as chosen by oneself, then the internalization of extrinsic motives becomes regulated through identification. Amotivation (AM) refers to the reduced intention towards goal-directed behavior, reflecting the absence of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation to engage in an activity [2].

Students who are extrinsically motivated could lose their motivation with upper their cursus, due to the stressful deadlines and poor outcomes, in contrast to intrinsically motivated individuals, who perform tasks for their inherent satisfaction. So, intrinsic motivation is the desired type of motivation for students, as it is associated with best outcomes, successful achievements, deep learning and well-being when compared to extrinsic motivation.

The Academic Motivation Scale (AMS), developed by Robert J. Vallerand, is widely used to evaluate student motivation, assessing intrinsic, extrinsic, and amotivation types​ [3–5]. The AMS provides a comprehensive understanding of the driving factors behind a student’s engagement in academic activities.

Although the importance of this subject, there is a lack of studies focusing on academic motivation among medical students and no literature data are available on the topic in Tunisian medicine faculties. However, Tunisian medical faculties are engaged in both accreditation and certification process where the motivation and the wellbeing of students represents a key point.

In this context, the aim of the current study was to identify academic motivation profile among medical students in Tunisia and its associated factors in order to highlight potential areas of intervention to improve student outcomes and satisfaction.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was an observational cross-sectional study with an analytic aim, using a self-administered questionnaire conducted among Tunisian medical students from August to November 2024.

Setting

Tunisia has four medical schools: one in the north, two in the central region, and one in the south. The language of instruction is currently bilingual, with courses delivered in both French and English. Students are generally educated in both languages from the early years of schooling. Recently, there has been a progressive shift toward fully anglophone medical education, with newer cohorts receiving their entire instruction in English.

Participants

Inclusion criteria

All medical students from the first to the fifth year, registered in the four medical faculties in Tunisia (Monastir, Tunis, Sousse, and Sfax) for the academic year 2024/2025, who agreed to participate by completing the questionnaire.

Non-inclusion criteria

Medical students who declined to answer the questionnaire.

Data collection

The questionnaire based on the validated Academic Motivation Scale (AMS) was distributed in its English version for four months. It was sent to the email addresses of medical students at the Faculty of Medicine of Monastir and shared in social groups for the remaining three Tunisian medical faculties using a Quick Response (QR) code. The questionnaire was constructed via Google Forms to facilitate auto-administration. Two reminders were sent to allow as many students as possible to participate in the study.

In addition to the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS-C 28), the following variables were studied:

Sociodemographic Factors: Gender, age, and year of study.Personal Factors: Personal interest in medicine, presence of mental health challenges, and academic failure.Environmental Factors: Support from peers or faculty and the quality of teaching.

Data sources/ measurement

A validated and widely used tool, the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS-C 28), was employed. Developed by Robert J. Vallerand, the AMS was originally created in French and later translated into English. It was designed to assess motivation among students, and subsequent studies have adapted it for medical students [6].

The AMS consists of 28 items scored on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = does not correspond at all, 7 = corresponds exactly). The scale evaluates three key dimensions of motivation (Table 1):

Accordingly, reliability analyses were conducted for the subscales of AMS in the present sample. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha for each dimension: intrinsic motivation to know (α = 0.848), intrinsic motivation toward accomplishment (α = 0.827), intrinsic motivation toward stimulation (α = 0.822), identified regulation (α = 0.764), introjected regulation (α = 0.836), external regulation (α = 0.790), and amotivation (α = 0.848), indicating acceptable to high internal consistency across the subscales.

Operational definition of variables

Dichotomous classification of the IM, EM and AM subscales

For the exploratory analysis of factors associated with motivational profiles, the subscales were subsequently categorized to identify students presenting high levels of each motivational dimension. This approach allowed the use of dichotomic classification to explore determinants of clearly expressed motivational profiles.

For each IM subscale (IM-to Know, IM-to Accomplish, IM-to Experience Stimulation), a subscale was considered ‘present’ if all four corresponding items scored between 5 and 7. Self-reported psychological distress (anxiety or depressive symptoms)

This variable was assessed using a single self-reported item asking students whether they had experienced symptoms of anxiety or depression during their medical studies. It reflects perceived psychological distress and does not constitute a clinical diagnosis.

Cycle of study

First cycle level: first and second year of study.

Second cycle level: third, fourth and fifth year of study.

Perception of teaching quality

Students’ perception of teaching quality was assessed using a self-reported item asking participants to evaluate the overall quality of teaching in their medical faculty. Responses were categorized into two groups: satisfied and dissatisfied with the quality of teaching.

Reasons for choosing medical studies

Participants were asked about the main reason for choosing to pursue medical studies. Responses were categorized into two groups: personal choice (decision primarily driven by the student’s own motivation or interest in studying medicine) and choice affected by family (decision influenced by family expectations, encouragement, or pressure).

Study size

A convenience sampling approach was employed, aiming to reach all eligible medical students by distributing the questionnaire via email addresses provided by the faculty of medicine of Monastir and through social media for the remaining 3 medicine faculties. The anticipated response rate and total sample size were determined based on the number of completed questionnaires received.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software package (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences), version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

The normality of distributions was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Variables that did not meet the normality assumption were reported as medians with interquartile ranges. Qualitative data were expressed as percentages.

Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were appropriately applied for qualitative variables and percentage comparisons. When the assumption of normality was not met, non-parametric tests were used: the Mann–Whitney U test for comparing two independent medians, the Kruskal–Wallis test (non-parametric ANOVA) for comparing more than two independent groups, and the Friedman test for comparing repeated measures of median scores within the same participants (e.g., intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and amotivation). A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For the exploratory analysis of factors associated with motivational profiles, AMS subscales were first categorized to identify students with high levels of each motivational dimension, allowing the use of logistic regression models to examine determinants of clearly expressed motivational profiles. A univariate analysis was initially conducted to assess the relationship between each independent variable and the motivational outcomes, and variables with a p-value < 0.25 were entered into a stepwise descending multivariate logistic regression model. This approach included all selected variables initially, progressively removing those with limited contribution, until only significant predictors remained. Variables with a significant odds ratio (p ≤ 0.05) in the final model were considered independent determinants. As a sensitivity analysis, the associations were also examined using AMS subscale scores as continuous variables, and the direction of the observed relationships remained consistent.

Results

Among the 689 medical students surveyed, most were female (71.8%) and in the second study cycle (64.3%). The majority were aged 18–22 years (65%) and from the Faculty of Monastir (68.1%). Medicine was the first study choice for 87.7% of participants, mainly by personal decision (68.3%). Anxiety or depression was reported by 78% of students, and 21.1% had experienced academic failure. Overall, 66.4% rated the quality of teaching as good, and 61.3% reported receiving support (Table 2).

Amotivation was associated with several factors. Intrinsic motivation to know was associated with several factors. In univariate analysis, it was significantly higher among students in the first cycle of study (p = 0.010), whereas it was less frequent among students with a history of academic failure (p = 0.001), those reporting anxiety or depression (p = 0.038), and students who did not receive support (p = 0.007). In multivariate analysis, intrinsic motivation to know was independently associated with choosing medicine based on personal preference (aOR[95%CI] = 3.2 [2.2–4.6]) and the perception of satisfaction with the quality of teaching (aOR[95%CI] = 2.6 [1.8–3.6]), while female students tended to have higher motivation than males (aOR[95%CI] = 1.4 [1.0–2.1]) (Table 5).

Intrinsic motivation toward accomplishment was significantly higher among female students (p = 0.003), students perceiving satisfaction with the quality of teaching (p < 0.001), and those who received support from peers (p = 0.029) in univariate analysis. In multivariate analysis, female gender (aOR[95%CI] = 1.8 [1.2–2.7]), perceived satisfaction with teaching quality (aOR[95%CI] = 2.6 [1.7–3.9]), and personal choice in studying medicine (aOR[95%CI] = 1.7 [1.1–2.5]) were independently associated with higher intrinsic motivation toward accomplishment (Table 5).

For intrinsic motivation toward experience stimulation, univariate analysis showed higher motivation among female students (p = 0.005), students aged 18–22 (p = 0.017), those perceiving satisfaction with the quality of teaching (p < 0.001), students receiving support from peers (p = 0.006), and those who chose medicine based on personal preference (p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis indicated that intrinsic motivation toward experience stimulation was independently associated with female gender (aOR[95%CI] = 2.5 [1.2–5.0]), being in the first cycle (aOR[95%CI] = 1.7 [1.1–3.0]), personal choice in studying medicine (aOR[95%CI] = 3.5 [1.6–7.7]), and perceived satisfaction with the quality of teaching (aOR[95%CI] = 3.6 [1.7–7.8]) (Table 5).

Identified regulation was significantly higher in univariate analysis among female students (p < 0.001), younger students aged 18–22 (p = 0.035), students from specific faculties (p = 0.036), and those who chose medicine based on personal preference (p = 0.000). Multivariate analysis indicated that female gender (aOR[95%CI] = 2.1 [1.4–3.1]) and personal choice in studying medicine (aOR[95%CI] = 2.2 [1.5–3.2]) remained independently associated with higher identified regulation, while perceived satisfaction with the quality of teaching also had a positive effect (aOR[95%CI] = 1.4 [1.1–2.1]) (Table 6).

For introjection, univariate analysis showed higher scores among students whose first choice was not medicine (p = 0.001) and those reporting anxiety or depression (p = 0.009). Multivariate analysis confirmed that students whose first choice was not medicine had higher introjection scores (aOR[95%CI] = 2.2[1.3–3.6]), whereas anxiety or depression was inversely associated with introjection (aOR[95%CI] = 0.6[0.4–0.7]) (Table 6).

Discussion

Our study aims to determine the motivation profile among a cohort of medical students enrolled in the four Tunisian medical faculties and to establish associated factors with motivation.

This study delineates a distinct motivational profile among medical students in Tunisia, characterized by the predominance of extrinsically-driven motivation—as operationalized by the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS). This extrinsic orientation coexists with moderate intrinsic motivation and comparatively low amotivation. Elucidating this configuration is critical, as student motivation constitutes a fundamental determinant of learning strategies, academic resilience, and the professional identity formation.

Our findings resonate with the complex motivational landscape described in the global literature [7, 8]. However, the pronounced extrinsic drive presents a nuanced contrast. Whereas research in settings like the Netherlands robustly correlates intrinsic motivation with deep learning and well-being [8, 9], investigations within other collectivist or Lower-Middle-Income Country (LMIC) contexts, such as Bangladesh and Egypt, report a similarly significant weight of extrinsic factors, including academic performance and future career stability [10, 11]. This convergence suggests that socio-educational structures exert an influence in shaping motivational outcomes.

The predominance of extrinsic motivation within this cohort is observed and could be reproduced by the socio-educational infrastructure. The competitive selection process, and a curriculum predicated on high-stakes examinations, systematically incentivize performance-oriented goals. This pedagogical environment effectively functions as an apparatus that reinforces and perpetuates the extrinsic regulatory styles measured by the AMS [12, 13]. Furthermore, systemic anxieties concerning future job prospects, remuneration, and broader healthcare system challenges in Tunisia likely foster an instrumentalist view of medical education. Collectivist cultural norms, wherein familial expectations for prestigious careers exert an influence and amplify these extrinsic pressures, potentially at the expense of intrinsic interest [7, 11].

The implication of this profile is particularly determinant for medical education outcomes. Although extrinsic motivation can be a reliable predictor of near-term academic performance, as evidenced in studies from Iran and Bangladesh [10, 13], it is more robustly associated with superficial learning strategies, emotional exhaustion, and diminished long-term professional satisfaction compared to its intrinsic counterpart [8, 14]. This is particularly disquieting for professional identity formation, given that intrinsic motivation is a stronger predictor of empathy, deep cognitive engagement, and sustained intellectual curiosity [9, 15].

To cultivate more self-determined motivation, we recommend medical curricular reforms grounded in the principles of Self-Determination Theory (SDT). This entails enhancing autonomy through the integration of student-selected projects and early clinical experiences; fostering competence via mastery-focused, formative feedback cycles; and strengthening relatedness through structured mentorship programs and collaborative learning pedagogies [9, 16].

Factors associated with amotivation

Our analysis identifies key predictors of amotivation. Although amotivation levels were low, its concentration within specific student subgroups is clinically and pedagogically significant. The multivariate model reveals that a choice of medical studies affected by family pressure, a history of academic failure, and a perceived lack of support from peers function as independent risk factors.

These findings align strongly with the theoretical framework of Self-Determination Theory. An influenced choice by family expectations typically reflects compromised autonomy at the critical juncture of career selection. Absent this personal endorsement, students lack a foundational rationale for engagement, rendering them highly susceptible to amotivation when confronted with academic rigors [8, 9].

Furthermore, repeated failures can precipitate a state of learned helplessness, wherein students become convinced of the futility of their efforts, thereby eroding motivation entirely [8, 17]. The association with self-reported anxiety and depression in the univariate analysis further underscores this vicious cycle; amotivation can be both a cause and a consequence of psychological distress, creating a maladaptive feedback mechanism that compromises academic achievement and overall mental health [14].

Factors influencing motivation

Motivation and learning are inter-related. The educational environment emerged as a powerful modifiable determinant. According to the results of the current study, perceived high teaching quality was an independent predictor for intrinsic motivation to know, intrinsic motivation toward accomplishment, and identified regulation. This finding corroborates international studies indicating that engaging, supportive pedagogy fosters competence and stimulates curiosity, thereby promoting deep learning and a drive for mastery [16, 18]. During last recent years, curriculum reforms have taken place in Tunisian medical faculties with the aim of promoting active pedagogical learning methods. The employed methods such as simulation, Problem-Based Learning (PBL), Case-Based Learning (CBL), Team-Based Learning (TBL) and other methods based on a socioconstructivist approach, could help in dealing with the complex endeavor of motivating learners. Indeed, students are directly engaged in the learning process instead of being passive recipients of information. Such an active learning aligns with educational theories that explain motivation: self-determination theory and constructivist learning theory.

Our analysis also revealed significant demographic patterns. Female gender was consistently associated with more adaptive motivational profiles, including higher intrinsic motivation toward accomplishment and experience stimulation, and greater identified regulation. This resonates with research suggesting gender differences in learning strategies and emotional engagement, though the underlying socio-cultural etiologies warrant further investigation [7, 11].

Notably, extrinsic motivations demonstrated divergent pathways. External regulation was largely unassociated with most demographic or environmental factors, highlighting its pervasive, systemic nature in a high-stakes academic context [9]. Intriguingly, a personal choice for medicine correlated with higher external regulation, suggesting that even autonomously-oriented students strategically navigate and engage with the system’s entrenched reward structures, a phenomenon observed in other competitive environments [10, 17]. Introjection was concurrently associated with reduced autonomy, reflecting a vulnerable motivational strategy in students who perceive limited personal control.

Educational implications and recommendations

The consistent positive association between perceived teaching quality and all forms of autonomous motivation (intrinsic motivation and identified regulation) underscores that the educational environment itself is the most potent lever for change.

Based on these findings and aligned with Self-Determination Theory, we propose the following evidence-informed recommendations:

Institutionalize autonomy-supportive structures for career selection and curricular engagement: the robust correlation between personal career choice and intrinsic motivation mandates the implementation of structured, pre-admission career counseling. Furthermore, the curriculum itself should be redesigned to incorporate meaningful elective components, fostering a sense of ownership and enabling students to internalize the value of their academic pursuits.Optimize Competence through mastery-oriented learning and formative feedback: the association between academic failure and amotivation, and between high-quality teaching and intrinsic motivation, demands a shift from a predominance of high-stakes summative assessment toward a culture of continuous formative feedback. Implementing mastery-based learning models, focused on skill acquisition and incremental improvement, can rebuild a sense of competence in struggling students and deepen learning for all.Foster relatedness with robust mentorship and systemic support: the identification of “lack of support” as an independent risk factor for amotivation necessitates the establishment of formal, longitudinal mentorship programs. Effective mentoring provides both academic guidance and psychosocial support, creating a sense of belonging and buffering against attrition and burnout. The strategy adopted in medical universities counselling units should be based on a proactive outreach targeting students with identified risk factors, such as a history of academic difficulty or an influenced career choice.

Study strengthens and limitations

This is at the best of our knowledge the first Tunisian and North-African study to design a detailed motivation profile among Tunisian medical students using the AMS as a validated tool. Beyond its descriptive scope, the current study goes further by providing a deeper analysis of independently associated factors with learning amotivation which could be a starting point to develop strategies aiming to improve the learning environment. The predominance of participants from Monastir, coupled with the convenience sampling approach, and the inability to calculate a precise response rate due to the distribution strategy constitute a notable limitation, potentially introducing center bias and limiting the applicability of our findings across all medical schools in Tunisia. Furthermore, the possibility of over-reporting or under-reporting due to social desirability bias cannot be excluded when using self-reported data.

Conclusion

Our findings have highlighted an extrinsic-dominant profile of Tunisian medical students. Although amotivation has shown to be a minor profile when compared to both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, targeted interventions based on the identified associated factors with amotivation should be made to improve the educational environment aiming to promote intrinsic motivation among medical students. Indeed, for LMICs like Tunisia, cultivating intrinsic motivation is not a luxury but a necessity for developing a resilient, empathetic, and adaptable physician workforce capable of meeting future healthcare challenges.
