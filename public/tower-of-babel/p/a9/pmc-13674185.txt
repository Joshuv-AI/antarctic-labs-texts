Avatrombopag for persistent and chronic immune thrombocytopenia in children: A post hoc analysis of clinical characteristics of response from a phase 3b clinical trial
Authors: Rachael F. Grace, Göksel Leblebisatan, Yesim Aydinok, Sule Unal, John Grainger, Amanda B. Grimes, Michele P. Lambert, Jessica Zhang, Brian D. Jamieson, Faizan Sattar, Scott Kolodny, Michael Vredenburg, the AVA‐PED‐301 Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAvatrombopag treatment resulted in significantly higher platelet response rates in patients when compared with placebo in a phase 3b study of patients with paediatric immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) (NCT04516967). These post hoc analyses of the 12‐week core phase further assessed the efficacy of avatrombopag, considering the influence of baseline characteristics and protocol‐defined dose adjustments on the achievement and sustainability of response. The cumulative percentage of patients who achieved a platelet response (2 consecutive platelet counts ≥50 × 109/L) by days 15 and 22 was 70.4% and 75.9%, respectively, with avatrombopag (placebo, 0% at both days). Only minor differences were observed by baseline characteristics. Among the 92.6% of patients (50/54) treated with avatrombopag who achieved a clinically meaningful platelet response (platelet count ≥30 × 109/L), the mean percentage of time with response was 62.5%. Protocol‐specified dose holds due to a platelet count >250 × 109/L occurred for 61.1% of patients, which may have impacted the efficacy outcomes. 78.3% of non‐durable responders had ≥1 dose holds prompted by a platelet count of 250–400 × 109/L, a platelet count that would not prompt a dose hold in real‐world clinical practice. Avatrombopag demonstrated a clinically meaningful platelet response across the clinical and demographic subgroups in this heavily pre‐treated and refractory paediatric ITP population.

INTRODUCTION

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is a rare autoimmune disease characterized by low platelet counts, an increased risk of bleeding and a reduced health‐related quality of life (HRQoL). 1 The incidence in children is 2–7 per 100 000. 2 In many children, ITP can be both transient and with spontaneous recovery; treatment goals are focused on raising platelet counts to treat and prevent bleeding, improve HRQoL and minimize treatment‐related toxicity. Current treatment guidelines recommend close observation for children with newly diagnosed ITP who present with no or mild bleeding, regardless of platelet count. 3 , 4 For non–life‐threatening bleeding, first‐line therapy includes corticosteroids (limited to 7 days), intravenous immunoglobulin and anti‐D immunoglobulin. 3 , 4 In children unresponsive to first‐line therapy who require treatment due to bleeding symptoms or reduced HRQoL, guidelines recommend the use of a thrombopoietin receptor agonist (TPO‐RA). Three TPO‐RAs—romiplostim, eltrombopag and avatrombopag—are approved in the United States to treat both adult and paediatric ITP. 5 , 6 , 7

Most recently, results from the core phase of the global phase 3b study evaluating the efficacy and safety of avatrombopag in children and adolescents with persistent or chronic ITP have been published. 8 Avatrombopag significantly improved durable platelet response (≥6 of 8 weekly platelet counts ≥50 × 109/L during the last 8 weeks of the 12‐week core phase treatment period without rescue therapy) and platelet response (2 consecutive platelet assessments ≥50 × 109/L over the 12‐week core phase treatment period without rescue therapy) compared with placebo (durable platelet response, 28% vs. 0% [p = 0.0077]; platelet response, 81% vs. 0% [p < 0.0001]). Secondary efficacy end‐points were also significantly improved over placebo with avatrombopag. The study protocol required a dose hold with platelet counts >250 × 109/L. This resulted in 61.1% of avatrombopag‐treated patients experiencing a dose hold during the core phase, which may have impacted the efficacy results.

Post hoc analyses of the 12‐week core phase of the phase 3b study were conducted to further assess the efficacy of avatrombopag, considering the influence of baseline characteristics and protocol‐defined dose adjustments on the achievement of response and sustainability of response to avatrombopag.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design

These are post hoc analyses of the core phase data of the randomized, placebo‐controlled phase 3b AVA‐PED‐301 study in paediatric patients with primary ITP for ≥6 months and an insufficient response to previous treatment (NCT04516967). Full details of the study design have been published. 8 Briefly, paediatric patients aged ≥1 to <18 years were initially assigned in a 2:2:1 ratio into one of three age‐related cohorts (Cohort 1, ≥12 to <18 years; Cohort 2, ≥6 to <12 years; Cohort 3, ≥1 to <6 years) and then randomly assigned in a 3:1 ratio stratified by age cohort to receive avatrombopag or placebo for 12 weeks (core phase). Eligible patients then continued on to the 2‐year open‐label extension phase. All patients or their caregivers or legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent before commencement of any trial‐related activities.

During the core phase, the study drug dose could be titrated up or down to achieve a target platelet count of 50–150 × 109/L. Patients receiving study drug in Cohorts 1 and 2 had a starting dose of 20 mg oral tablet once daily (maximum dose, 40 mg once daily; minimum dose, 20 mg once weekly). Patients receiving study drug in Cohort 3 had a starting dose of 10 mg powder for oral suspension once daily (maximum dose, 20 mg once daily; minimum dose, 10 mg once weekly). The study drug was held if a platelet count measurement was >250 × 109/L, a threshold substantially lower than the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved level of >400 × 109/L specified in the avatrombopag prescribing information. 5 Therefore, dose holds occurring within the 250–400 × 109/L range during the core phase were specifically evaluated. Following a dose hold, platelet counts were monitored twice weekly until they decreased to ≤100 × 109/L, at which point the study drug was restarted with a decrease of one dose level.

A total of 75 patients were enrolled in the core phase (avatrombopag, n = 54; placebo, n = 21) and treatment was completed by 44 patients (81%) in the avatrombopag arm and 1 patient (5%) in the placebo arm. Baseline characteristics and results of the primary and secondary outcomes in the core phase have been published. 8 Of note, 61.1% of avatrombopag‐treated patients (33/54) (Cohort 1, 52.4% [11/21]; Cohort 2, 75.0% [15/20] Cohort 3, 53.8% [7/13]) and 4.8% of placebo‐treated patients (1/21) experienced dose holds due to a platelet count >250 × 109/L during the 12‐week core phase. 8

These post hoc analyses focused on characterizing the efficacy profile of avatrombopag; safety during the 12‐week core phase has been reported previously. 8

Achievement of platelet responses

To further characterize platelet response during the core phase, different definitions for platelet response were utilized (Table S1). These included platelet response (2 consecutive platelet assessments ≥50 × 109/L over the 12‐week core phase treatment period in the absence of rescue therapy), single platelet response (at least 1 platelet count ≥50 × 109/L, in the absence of rescue therapy), clinically meaningful platelet response (at least 1 platelet count ≥30 × 109/L, in the absence of rescue therapy), achievement of 2 consecutive platelet counts ≥30 × 109/L and the cumulative percentage of patients achieving a platelet response.

Analyses included proportion of patients with clinically meaningful platelet response or single platelet response, cumulative percentage of patients who achieved a platelet response by day 15 and day 22, the influence of baseline characteristics (sex [male/female], presence of World Health Organization (WHO)‐defined bleeding [grades ≥1], ITP duration [<12 months/≥12 months], number of prior ITP medications since diagnosis [≤2/>2], prior treatment with TPO‐RA [yes/no], type of prior TPO‐RA treatment [eltrombopag only/romiplostim only/both eltrombopag and romiplostim], response to prior TPO‐RA and weight at baseline [12 to <18 years, <55 kg/≥55 kg; 6 to <12 years, <33 kg/≥33 kg; 1 to <6 years, <18 kg/≥18 kg]) on platelet response measures. Cut‐offs for weight at baseline were determined arbitrarily for each age group to ensure similar numbers of patients in each weight group.

Sustainability of response

Sustainability of platelet response in the core phase of the study was also evaluated further. Definitions for this analysis were durable platelet response (≥6 of 8 weekly platelet counts ≥50 × 109/L during the last 8 weeks of the 12‐week core phase treatment period, in the absence of rescue therapy) and late‐stage durable platelet response (percentage of patients achieving ≥3 of 4 weekly platelet counts ≥50 × 109/L during the last 4 weeks of the 12‐week core phase treatment period, in the absence of rescue therapy; Table S1).

Evaluations included late‐stage durable platelet response for the overall population and by age cohort, percentage of time patients achieved a clinically meaningful platelet response, percentage of time patients achieved a single platelet response, durable platelet response by disease duration at baseline and the influence of baseline characteristics on the durable platelet response.

Dose hold effects were assessed among durable and non‐durable responders in the avatrombopag group who experienced ≥1 dose hold. The following dose hold effects were assessed during the last 10 weeks of the core phase: proportion of patients with platelet counts <50 × 109/L for ≥15 days post‐hold, total time with platelet counts <50 × 109/L for patients who had platelet counts <50 × 109/L for ≥15 days post‐hold and median nadir post‐hold platelet count. The following dose hold effects were evaluated among non‐durable responders: the number and proportion of unique patients with dose holds due to platelet counts between 250 and 400 × 109/L, the number and proportion of dose holds attributed to platelet counts within this range, the number and proportion of patients who underwent dose cycling (dose increase to the pre‐hold dose following reinitiation) following a dose hold and the median number of days between avatrombopag reinitiation and subsequent dose escalation. Dose holds attributed to causes other than platelet counts exceeding 250 × 109/L were excluded from the dose hold analysis.

Descriptive statistics were used for all analyses, which were conducted using Statistical Analysis System (SAS) version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) or Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365).

RESULTS

Achievement of platelet responses by clinical characteristics

More patients in the avatrombopag group than in the placebo group achieved a clinically meaningful platelet response (92.6% vs. 19.1%; Figure 1A), 2 consecutive platelet counts ≥30 × 109/L (88.9% vs. 0%; Figure 1B), a single platelet response (90.7% vs. 9.5%; Figure 1C) and a platelet response 8 (81.5% vs. 0%; Figure 1D) during the core phase. The cumulative percentage of patients who achieved a platelet response by day 15 and day 22 was 70.4% and 75.9%, respectively, for patients in the avatrombopag group, compared with no patients at either time point in the placebo group (Figure 1E).

In the avatrombopag group, 81.5% of patients (44/54) achieved a platelet response, 8 including 100% of patients (13/13) with a disease duration of <12 months at baseline (persistent ITP) and 75.6% of patients (31/41) with a disease duration of ≥12 months (chronic ITP). Overall, no patients in the placebo group achieved a platelet response. Analysis of platelet response by baseline characteristics revealed that responses were similarly high across all groups (Table 1). Among those with prior TPO‐RA exposure, platelet response rates were consistent regardless of response to prior TPO‐RA treatment (response to prior TPO‐RA treatment, 82.4% [14/17]; no response to prior TPO‐RA treatment, 78.3% [18/23]).

Sustainability of response by clinical characteristics

A durable platelet response to avatrombopag was reported for 27.8% of patients (15/54) in the overall population, 8 including 30.8% of patients (4/13) who had a disease duration of <12 months at baseline and 26.8% of patients (11/41) who had a disease duration of ≥12 months at baseline. No patients in the placebo group had a durable platelet response. When analysed by baseline characteristics, durable platelet response rate was generally similar across all groups (Table 1).

A late‐stage durable platelet response was observed in 35.2% of patients (19/54) in the avatrombopag group and 0% of patients in the placebo group (Figure 2). This response was noted across all age cohorts, with 47.6% (10/21) in Cohort 1, 25.0% (5/20) in Cohort 2 and 30.8% (4/13) in Cohort 3.

Among the 92.6% of patients (50/54) treated with avatrombopag who achieved a clinically meaningful platelet response, the mean (median) percentage of time with a clinically meaningful platelet response was 62.5% (66.7%). Clinically meaningful platelet response was maintained for >25% of the time during the final 8 weeks of the 12‐week core phase in 94.0% of patients, >50% of the time for 72.0% of patients and >75% of the time for 36.0% of patients (Figure 3).

Of the 90.7% of patients (49/54) treated with avatrombopag who achieved a single platelet response, the mean (median) percentage of time with a platelet count ≥50 × 109/L was 51.0% (51.2%). A platelet count ≥50 × 109/L was achieved for >25% of the time during the final 8 weeks of the 12‐week core phase in 95.8% of patients, >50% of the time for 54.2% of patients and >75% of the time for 8.3% of patients (Figure 3).

Impact of dose holds on the durability of platelet response

Of 54 avatrombopag‐treated patients, 61.1% (33/54) (Cohort 1, 52.4% [11/21]; Cohort 2, 75.0% [15/20] Cohort 3, 53.8% [7/13]) and 4.8% of placebo‐treated patients (1/21) experienced dose holds due to a platelet count >250 × 109/L during the 12‐week core phase. 8 Among the 33 avatrombopag patients with dose holds, 10 were durable responders and 23 were non‐durable. In the final 10 weeks, durable responders had fewer dose holds per patient (0.8 vs. 1.6) and none experienced multiple holds compared with 52.2% of non‐durable responders (Table 2).

Forty percent of durable responders (4/10) and 47.8% of non‐durable responders (11/23) had post‐dose hold platelet counts <50 × 109/L lasting ≥15 days during the final 10 weeks of the core phase (Table 2). The median total duration of post‐dose hold platelet counts <50 × 109/L was 19.5 days for the 4 durable responders (mean, 19.8 days) and 25 days for the 11 non‐durable responders (mean, 29 days). During the final 10 weeks of the core phase, the median nadir platelet count post‐dose hold was 20.0 × 109/L for durable responders (mean, 20.6 × 109/L) and 11.5 × 109/L for non‐durable responders (mean, 27.3 × 109/L).

Among the 23 non‐durable responders, who accounted for 40 total dose holds, 11 had holds triggered solely by platelet counts of 250–400 × 109/L, 5 by counts >400 × 109/L and 7 by counts spanning both ranges. Over half (13/23, 56.5%) experienced >1 hold, whereas 10 (43.5%) had only 1. Overall, 25/40 holds (62.5%) were associated with platelet counts within the 250–400 × 109/L range.

Analysis of dose cycling showed that 21 of 23 non‐durable responders with ≥1 dose hold experienced post‐dose hold dose cycling, with a median interval of 14 days between avatrombopag reinitiation and subsequent dose increase.

Among the 16 non‐durable responders without a dose hold, 12 attained the cohort‐specific maximum dose and 8 completed the core phase. Of these 12 patients, 9 recorded at least one platelet count ≥30 × 109/L and 8 achieved at least one platelet count ≥50 × 109/L.

DISCUSSION

These exploratory, post hoc analyses suggest that avatrombopag treatment led to a clinically meaningful platelet response in the vast majority of paediatric patients with persistent and chronic ITP. Although platelet response is commonly defined in clinical trials as a platelet count >50 × 109/L, an International Working Group of expert clinicians recommends a threshold of >30 × 109/L, which is considered more clinically relevant and reflects a level at which bleeding risk is low.1 Findings based on alternative definitions of response and durability underscore the importance of incorporating clinically relevant outcomes in clinical trials to inform real‐world treatment decisions. Retrospective, observational studies from the United States and China demonstrated the effectiveness and safety of avatrombopag in paediatric patients with ITP. 9 , 10 A real‐world study in China found that treatment response to eltrombopag or avatrombopag was generally similar in paediatric patients with ITP but that the median time to response was significantly shorter with avatrombopag compared with eltrombopag (8 days vs. 15 days; p < 0.001). 11

Avatrombopag elicited a high response rate across diverse demographic and clinical backgrounds; however, patient numbers were low in some subgroups, necessitating cautious interpretation. Characteristics linked to disease severity or treatment refractoriness, including baseline bleeding and prior ITP therapies, were associated with only moderate variability in response with high response rates also seen in patients with these characteristics. All 13 patients with persistent ITP achieved a platelet response, indicating potential benefit from earlier avatrombopag initiation. Furthermore, patients with prior TPO‐RA exposure demonstrated high response rates, regardless of previous treatment response. These exploratory, post hoc findings support considering avatrombopag as a switch option in paediatric patients with inadequate response or for improved convenience (e.g. avoiding subcutaneous injections, hepatotoxicity or food‐type limitations), consistent with adult data supporting transitions to avatrombopag from other TPO‐RAs. 12 , 13

Per protocol, 61.1% of patients experienced a dose hold due to a platelet count >250 × 109/L. 8 Avatrombopag treatment could resume at a reduced dose once platelet counts fell to ≤100 × 109/L, which may have contributed to fewer patients achieving a durable response during the core phase both due to rebound thrombocytopenia and the failure to maintain or reach the appropriate individual dose for efficacy due to dose cycling. The high frequency of dose holds due to platelet counts >250 × 109/L reflects the efficacy of avatrombopag in children, despite many of them being classified as non‐responders, and suggests that the short duration of the core phase may have limited the opportunity for dose titration and stabilization of platelet counts in some patients. During the final 10 weeks of the core phase, non‐durable responders had twice as many dose holds per patient compared with durable responders and over half experienced multiple dose holds, whereas none of the durable responders did. Dose holds initiated early in this 10‐week period likely impacted the 8‐week window used to define durable response due to delays between (a) dose hold and platelet decline to ≤100 × 109/L permitting avatrombopag reinitiation and (b) reinitiation and subsequent platelet recovery. These exploratory, post hoc analyses found that patients with platelet counts ≤50 × 109/L for ≥15 days during this period were less likely to be durable responders, as shown by a higher proportion of non‐durable responders meeting this criterion and a longer median duration below the response threshold. Median nadir platelet counts during the final 10 weeks were also lower in non‐durable responders compared with durable responders. Single platelet responses over defined thresholds were used to demonstrate the response to avatrombopag, particularly in the context of the study's dose hold requirement, which may have limited some patients' ability to achieve consecutive responses. While the reported findings support the plausibility that dose holds may have contributed to an underestimation of avatrombopag efficacy, this observation should be interpreted cautiously and as hypothesis‐generating.

As previously noted, based on discussion with regulatory authorities, the study protocol‐mandated dose holds at platelet counts ≥250 × 109/L, a threshold substantially lower than the >400 × 109/L recommended in the avatrombopag prescribing information and used in paediatric ITP trials of eltrombopag and romiplostim. 5 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 In the phase 3b study, 78.3% of non‐durable responders with ≥1 dose hold had dose holds triggered by platelet counts of 250–400 × 109/L—levels that would not have warranted a dose hold under avatrombopag prescribing guidelines but, rather, a dose reduction. Consequently, the protocol did not reflect real‐world avatrombopag dosing practices. Indeed, a real‐world study reported that dose reductions were used more frequently than dose holds in paediatric patients who experienced thrombocytosis on avatrombopag. 10 Future studies evaluating avatrombopag outcomes in paediatric ITP using dose hold criteria aligned with prescribing information or other TPO‐RA clinical trials are warranted.

All patients experienced both rebound thrombocytopenia and sub‐response nadir platelet counts following a dose hold (according to the clinical trial protocol), highlighting the clinical impact of dose holds on efficacy due to the risk of rebound thrombocytopenia. However, future real‐world experiences may suggest dose reduction rather than dose hold to prevent the occurrence of rebound thrombocytopenia. The phase 3 clinical trial of avatrombopag in adults with ITP showed higher platelet counts in the first 1–2 weeks of treatment, followed by a decrease in the following 1–2 weeks. 18 Thus, physicians may consider dose decrease rather than dose hold for patients with platelet counts >400 × 109/L, particularly early in treatment when platelet counts have not stabilized, which may reduce the risk of rebound thrombocytopenia and dose cycling observed with dose holds. TPO‐RA dose reductions have been suggested as an effective way to eliminate thrombocytosis in clinical practice, with less platelet fluctuation than dose withholding. 19

A key limitation of this analysis was the relatively small number of patients with specific baseline characteristics included in the subgroup evaluation, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions. Baseline characteristics other than those evaluated, such as prior response to corticosteroids, may correlate with response to avatrombopag. However, a broader analysis was not possible given the small patient numbers. Further, response measures were compromised by the supratherapeutic effect, subsequent required dose holds and eventual dose cycling. Additionally, the protocol‐mandated dose holds for platelet count >250 × 109/L and subsequent reinitiation appeared to contribute to rebound thrombocytopenia, potentially impacting durability of response outcomes. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted in the context of the exploratory and descriptive nature of this post hoc analysis, including the small patient numbers in some groups. Nevertheless, they provide useful insight into avatrombopag efficacy and dosing considerations.

In conclusion, clinically meaningful and durable platelet responses were observed across a range of clinical and demographic characteristics in this paediatric population. Certain characteristics may be associated with differences in response rates and warrant further investigation. Avatrombopag induced rapid and sustained platelet responses in a heavily pre‐treated and refractory paediatric ITP population, regardless of the response definition used. Study outcomes may have been influenced by protocol‐specified dose hold criteria, which were more stringent than those used in prior TPO‐RA studies and those recommended in the avatrombopag prescribing information, and do not reflect real‐world clinical practice.
