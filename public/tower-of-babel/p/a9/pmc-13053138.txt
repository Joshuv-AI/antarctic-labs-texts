Predicting Behavior of Cutaneous Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma in Transplant Recipients
Authors: Meghana C Bhaskara, Mikayla G Hubbard, Jessica A Yesensky, Janice L Farlow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

After solid organ transplant, the risk of developing cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma increases by 65 to 250×. 1 Within the immunosuppressed patient population, solid organ transplant recipients (SOTRs) are at a uniquely increased risk of developing invasive cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas tumors, requiring independent study of this population. 2 The head and neck location is also an independent risk factor for invasion of cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas. 3 Aggressive cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas of the head and neck (cHNSCCs) require numerous cosmetically and functionally morbid surgeries and are a significant cause of mortality in SOTRs. 1 , 4

However, traditional staging systems underperform in risk stratification of cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas. AJCC and IUCC systems fail particularly at the T2 stage, which includes both high and low‐risk tumors. 5 , 6 BWH staging criteria identifies high risk tumors more efficiently than AJCC and UICC systems. 5 , 7 , 8 However, the BWH system does not account for immunosuppression, a major known risk factor and significant patient population, in risk stratification. Majority of poor outcomes of cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas in immunosuppressed patients occur in low stage tumors. 9 This reflects a need to improve staging to identify aggressive tumors that are understaged by current criteria.

With the risks of invasion, recurrence, metastasis, and death that cHNSCCs pose in SOTRs, it is vital to identify high‐risk cHNSCCs in these patients. Current staging systems underperform and do not incorporate known factors associated with aggressive cHNSCCs. Further detail on clinicopathologic factors associated with aggressive tumors may inform Identification of high‐risk cHNSCCs and guide prognostication and appropriate treatment in SOTRs. Therefore, we aim to identify the utility of clinicopathologic factors such as age at transplant, age at first post‐transplant cHNSCC, type of transplant, and level of invasion for prediction of invasion and survival in SOTRs with cHNSCCs.

Methods

Study Design

A single institution cross‐sectional study was performed for SOTRs who underwent posttransplant surgical resection of at least one cHNSCC between the years 2009 to 2019 at a tertiary academic medical center (Indiana University Institutional Review Board #22782).

Eligibility Criteria

Patients eligible for inclusion were patients who developed one or more cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma in the head and neck region after undergoing solid organ transplantation. Included patients had at least one cHNSCC resected between 2009 and 2019. Patients excluded from study had tumors other than cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas, squamous cell carcinoma in situ without invasion through the basement membrane, immunosuppression for reasons other than solid organ transplant, or only had cHNSCC resected prior to transplant and none after transplant.

Data Collection

All patients who were organ transplant recipients and had squamous cell carcinomas resected between 2009 and 2019 were identified. From this population, only patients who had cHNSCCs resected after solid organ transplant were selected. Data on age at transplant, type of transplant, age at first cHNSCC after transplant, level of invasion of each cHNSCC, age at first deeply invasive cHNSCC, if death has occurred, and age at death were collected from electronic medical records.

Outcomes

Primary outcome was overall survival. Overall survival was compared by level of invasion, age at transplant, and age at first cHNSCC following transplant. Secondary outcome was risk of developing deeply invasive cHNSCC. Risk of developing deeply invasive tumor was evaluated by ethnicity, sex, age at first cHNSCC after transplant, type of transplant, and age at transplant.

Statistical Analysis

Overall survival was evaluated using Mantel‐Cox test for level of invasion and transplant type and Cox regression for age at transplant and age at first cHNSCC. Level of invasion was analyzed by age at first transplant, age at first posttransplant cHNSCC, and overall lifespan using unpaired t‐test. Effect of transplant type on level of invasion was measured using ANOVA. Chi square test was used to evaluate effect of sex and ethnicity on level of invasion. Hazard ratios (HR) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and a P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Search criteria identified 77 patients, of which 44 patients met criteria for inclusion (Table 1). All 44 patients meeting inclusion criteria were included. Mean patient age was 69.4 years (SD = 8.8 years). All patients were white (100%), most were non‐Hispanic (97.8%), and approximately 4 in 5 patients were male (81.8%). There were 21 patients with renal transplants, 6 patients with heart transplants, 8 patients with liver transplants, 3 patients with lung transplants, and 6 patients with multiorgan transplants (1 pancreas and lung, 5 pancreas and kidney). None of the kidney transplant recipients commenced long‐term dialysis related to graft failure. Sixteen SOTRs (6 kidney, 2 liver, 3 multi‐organ, 3 heart, 2 lung) had deeply invasive cHNSCCs.

Primary Outcome

Invasion deep to the subcutis was associated with decreased overall survival (P < .0021) (Figure 1). Within deceased SOTRs, those with deeply invasive cHNSCCs (mean = 65.2, SD = 9.05) had shorter lifespans than those whose cHNSCCs were not deeply invasive (70.2, 8.0) (P = .02). There was no association between overall survival and transplant type (P = .83) (Figure 2), age at first post‐transplant cHNSCC (HR = 1.00, 95% CI = 0.96‐1.04), and age at transplant (HR = 1.02, 95% CI = 0.98‐1.07).

Secondary Outcomes

Younger age at transplant approached significance for risk of developing a deeply invasive tumor (P = .07). Patients with deeply invasive tumors received a solid organ transplant on average 5.3 years earlier, with average age at transplant being 51.2 years (SD = 8.2 years) in those without deeply invasive tumors and 56.5 years (SD = 9.8 years) in those with deeply invasive tumors. There was no association of sex (P = .12), ethnicity (P = .18), age at first post‐transplant cHNSCC (P = .15), or type of transplant (P = .21) with risk of developing a deeply invasive tumor (Table 2).

Discussion

Deeply invasive cHNSCCs are associated with decreased overall survival, while transplant type and age at first posttransplant cHNSCC are not related to overall survival. Younger age at transplant approached significance in association with risk of developing a deeply invasive tumor. Sex, ethnicity, age at first cHNSCC, and type of transplant had no relation to risk for developing a deeply invasive cHNSCC.

Correlates to invasion, recurrence, metastasis, and survival in SOTRs with cHNSCCs are extremely underreported in the literature. Only one article presented findings on a strict correlate to survival with these specific tumors in this population. Moloney et al's retrospective cohort study supported that decreasing degree of immunosuppression correlated with longer follow‐up period. 10 In SOTRs, head/neck location, older age at transplant, older age at first cSCC, local recurrence, size >2 cm, clinical ulceration, poor differentiation grade, perineural invasion, and deep invasion have been associated with increased risk for metastasis of cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas. 11 , 12 However, these correlates to metastasis of cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas are not specific for the head and neck location, which itself is associated with increased risk for invasion and necessitates independent study. 3

Our finding of deeply invasive cHNSCCs being associated with decreased overall survival is supported by the known relation between deep invasion and increased risk for metastasis. 12 While Genders et al. identify that later age at transplant was associated with increased risk for metastasis, we found that younger age at transplant approaches significance in relation to increased risk of developing deeply invasive cHNSCCs. 11 Longer duration of immunosuppression, a result of younger age at transplant, has been associated with increased risk of developing aggressive head and neck nonmelanoma skin cancers (NMSC) and increased frequency of NMSC in SOTRs. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16

Though we found no relation of ethnicity to risk of developing invasive cHNSCCs or overall survival, analysis was likely limited by our ethnically homogenous group of 100% white and 97.8% non‐Hispanic patients. Cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas occur most commonly in individuals with lower Fitzpatrick skin types representing fair skin colors. 17 This trend is also observed in SOTRs and follows a strong linear association. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 However, cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas still occur in patients with higher Fitzpatrick skin types and this population is likely underrepresented in the literature. 22 Presentations of skin cancers in sun protected sites and at later stages in patients with higher Fitzpatrick skin types also necessitates intentional study in these patients as part of future investigations of cHNSCCs in SOTRs. 22 , 23

Decreasing immunosuppression has been associated with longer survival in patients with aggressive cHNSCCs. 10 However, besides this study in 9 patients in which immunosuppression regimen was reduced or stopped completely, there have been no other studies presenting granular data on immunosuppression relating to survival, risk of invasion, or progression of cHNSCCs. Not specific to the head and neck region, trends exist along with proposed mechanisms to compare outcomes of cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas in relation to immunosuppressant dosage and types in SOTRs. 24 Logically, decreasing immunosuppression is associated with lower incidence and better prognosis related to cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas. 15 , 24 Antimetabolites and calcineurin inhibitors are associated with decreased cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma incidence in comparison to mTOR inhibitors. 24 Within antimetabolites, azathioprine is associated with increased incidence over mycophenolic acid. 24 Further data on immunosuppressant medications, such as whole blood concentration, area under curve concentrations, average trough levels, type of individual immunosuppressant, combination of immunosuppressants, cumulative dose, current daily dosage, duration of use, and use of antilymphocyte or antithymocyte globulin as induction or rejection therapy specifically in SOTRs with cHNSCCs may inform clinical practice regarding immunosuppression regimens or surveillance in SOTRs with cHNSCCs. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Autoimmune disease comorbidity and reason for transplant are further variables mediating degree and duration of immunosuppression that have not been studied in relation to aggressively behaving cHNSCCs in SOTRs but may prove useful.

Genetic analysis is an additional factor with potential to identify high‐risk tumors. The 40 gene expression profile has been shown to independently predict metastatic risk of cHNSCCs at 3 years postdiagnosis. 25 cHNSCCs display dysregulation of extracellular matrix interactions, epithelial to mesenchymal transition, cell adhesion, cellular motility, angiogenesis, and cellular differentiation, and a panel for just 10 genes was found to be 95% specific and 96% sensitive in predicting extensive perineural invasion. 26 , 27 In addition to analyzing gene expression within cHNSCCs, assessing tissue surrounding the tumor may identify microenvironments permissible to recurrent and aggressive tumors. 28 Though genetic analysis has emerged as a promising tool for risk stratification, it has not yet been utilized to identify high‐risk cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas within a patient population exclusive for SOTRs. 29 , 30

As location specific criteria and genetic testing has improved staging criteria for mucosal head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, inclusion of similar clinicopathologic factors may improve staging of cHNSCCs especially in SOTRs. 31 Improved staging can inform clinical prognostication and allow patients with aggressive cHNSCCs to receive appropriate counseling and treatment. SOTRs with aggressive cHNSCCs may benefit from adapted treatment paradigms with elements such as early sentinel lymph node biopsy, reduction of immunosuppression, substitution of promitogenic immunosuppressants, and perioperative immunotherapy with cemiplimab. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 Though immunotherapy has conventionally been contraindicated in SOTRs due to risk of transplant rejection, preferential use of certain immunotherapy types, such as anti‐CTLA‐4 over anti‐PD‐1 medications, and targeted immunosuppressant treatments, such as the Harvard regimen incorporating pulse doses of prednisone and mTOR inhibitors, may decrease risk of graft rejection and allow SOTRs to benefit from better disease control with immunotherapy. 24 Transplant type may also affect candidacy for immunotherapy, as kidney transplant recipients and their providers may be more amenable to the risk of graft rejection with use of immunotherapy, as they can return to hemodialysis if their kidney fails.

Limitations

This single‐center study is subject to limitations inherent to retrospective cohort study methodology. Data collection was limited to the quality of information recorded and available. Data on immunosuppression regimens and changes over time was unable to be collected. As such, we were not able to account for changes to immunosuppressant regimens in recent years, such as reductions in steroid and calcineurin inhibitor use, incorporation of alternate immunosuppressants such as mTOR inhibitors, and updated induction and anti‐rejection treatments. 37 , 38 , 39 Cause of death was not available in deceased patient medical records or in public records for majority of included patients. Additionally, follow up for surveillance was highly variable, as some patients followed up with providers outside our center. These factors limited our ability to present findings on disease‐specific mortality and survival. The patient population was also majority non‐Hispanic, White, and male, which may limit generalizability. Including patients as part of a multi‐center study would increase both the sample size and diversity, improving generalizability of findings.

Conclusion

cHNSCCs pose significant risk for aggressive behavior and poor outcomes in SOTRs. Current staging criteria underperforms for risk stratification of cHNSCCs in SOTRs, and clinicopathologic information, such as younger age at transplant, may be useful for identification of high‐risk tumors. Further large prospective studies providing granular data on factors such as degree and duration of immunosuppression and genetic analysis are warranted.
