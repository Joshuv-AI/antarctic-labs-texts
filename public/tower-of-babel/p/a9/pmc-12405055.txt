Falls Among Individuals With Systemic Lupus Erythematosus: An Observational Study
Authors: Chelsea R Perfect, C Barrett Bowling, S Sam Lim, Charmayne Dunlop‐Thomas, Courtney Hoge, Jinoos Yazdany, Patricia P Katz, Laura C Plantinga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is thought to accelerate the aging process. However, there is limited research on geriatric syndromes, such as falls, in this potentially vulnerable population. This study aimed to describe the prevalence of and contributors to falls in the SLE population.MethodsParticipants were recruited from an ongoing population‐based cohort of individuals with validated SLE. Falls (number of falls and fall‐related injuries requiring medical attention over the past year) and perceived contributing factors were self‐reported. Descriptive statistics were calculated, and age‐, sex‐, and race‐adjusted odds ratios (aORs) of participant characteristics with falls were estimated using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsNearly one‐third (30.7%) of participants (overall N = 447; 40.9% aged ≥50 years, 91.7% female, and 82.6% Black) reported falling in the past year; 19.2% fell twice or more. Loss of balance (78.1% of falls), slipping/tripping (64.2%), and weakness (53.3%) were the most commonly reported contributing factors. Age and sex were not associated with falls, but higher physical performance (aOR, 0.78; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.71–0.87) was associated with lower odds of falls. Higher SLE activity (aOR, 1.78; 95% CI, 1.44–2.21) and damage (aOR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.00–1.49), greater depressive symptoms (aOR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.14,1–80), and taking fall risk‐increasing drugs (antidepressants: aOR, 1.82; 95% CI, 1.13–2.93; pain medications: aOR, 2.60; 95% CI, 1.68–4.00; opioids: aOR, 4.52; 95% CI, 2.39–8.56) were associated with higher odds of falls.ConclusionFalls were common in our cohort, regardless of age. Our results suggest potential interventions for reducing falls, like better control of SLE, physical therapy, depression screening, and medication review.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a condition that affects more than 200,000 Americans a year—a number that is expected to increase in the coming years. 1 , 2 Most new diagnoses occur among women in the first five decades of their lives. 3 However, the majority of individuals diagnosed with SLE will go on to live with the disease for decades. 4 For many, these years are not without complications. Like many other chronic systemic conditions, SLE is thought to accelerate the aging process, 5 , 6 leading individuals to develop a biological age that exceeds their chronological age. 7 This “accelerated aging” is thought to be due to stress, hormone alterations, and inflammation and may place aging adults with SLE at increased risk for geriatric syndromes years before their age‐matched peers. 7 Despite interest in geriatric syndromes in other chronic conditions (eg, HIV, chronic kidney disease) and an increasing focus on frailty in adults with SLE, 8 , 9 , 10 there has been limited focus on the prevalence of or risk factors for other geriatric syndromes among the SLE population—including falls.

This study aimed to describe the burden of and risk factors for falls—an important geriatric syndrome associated with significant morbidity and mortality among older adults 11 —in a population‐based cohort of individuals living with SLE. We were interested in understanding if falls were common among individuals with SLE regardless of age. As a geriatric syndrome, falls may be due to multiple contributing factors related to the person (intrinsic) and to the environment (extrinsic). 12 Therefore, we were also interested in understanding what intrinsic and extrinsic factors contribute to this geriatric syndrome. By understanding the prevalence of and contributors to falls, we hope this research contributes to increased attention to and prevention of a syndrome that can compromise an individual's quality of life or even lead to an early death.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study population and data sources

Participants for the Approaches to Positive, Patient‐centered Experiences of Aging with Lupus (APPEAL) study were recruited from the ongoing, population‐based Georgians Organized Against Lupus (GOAL) cohort in metropolitan Atlanta. GOAL participants 13 , 14 are adults (≥18 years) with SLE, defined as at least four revised American College of Rheumatology (ACR) SLE criteria 15 or three ACR criteria plus a diagnosis of SLE by a board‐certified rheumatologist. GOAL participants who were not actively participating in GOAL, were unable to speak English, did not have sufficient vision and hearing to undergo study testing, were unable to consent, or were living outside of Georgia at the time of recruitment were excluded from APPEAL. The Emory Institutional Review Board approved the APPEAL (IRB00110977) and GOAL (IRB00003656) study protocols. APPEAL participants provided informed consent before completing study visits.

A total of 451 participants completed an APPEAL visit between October 8, 2019, and May 12, 2022. For this analysis, we excluded participants whose overall survey response patterns were potentially invalid (n = 4), leaving 447 participants for analyses. 16 Participants were considered to have potentially invalid survey patterns if they consistently selected extreme Likert‐scale responses for measures that contained reverse‐scored items. Study data were obtained from performance tests and self‐administered questionnaires during the APPEAL visit. Data from the GOAL annual survey closest to the APPEAL visit date were additionally linked to the APPEAL data.

Variables

Falls

Participants were asked if they had fallen in the past year, how many falls they had had in the past year, and whether they had sought medical attention for any of their falls. Additionally, a list of potential intrinsic (lost balance, dizziness, weakness, fainted/blacked out, lightheadedness, unstable joint, sudden leg weakness, confusion, pain, heart racing) and extrinsic (slipped/tripped, shoes/footwear, low lighting, problem in environment [such as rugs], rushing/running, other) fall‐related factors—compiled from prior publications describing falls risk factors—were presented to participants who reported falls, and participants reported whether these factors contributed to their fall(s). 12 , 17 , 18

Other variables

Age, sex (at birth), race, and education were self‐reported by the participant via the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Toolbox 19 from a fixed set of categories. Age was additionally categorized into groups based on the distribution of participating individuals (18–34 vs 34–49 vs 50+; 18–34 vs 34–59 vs 60+). Race was categorized as Black (as a single or multiple race), White, and other. Education was the highest level attained and categorized as high school graduate/equivalency or lower, some college/associate degree, and college graduate or higher. Current working status was assessed with the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire: General Health v. 2.0. 20 , 21 , 22 SLE‐related factors included measures of disease duration, activity, and damage. Disease duration was self‐reported during the GOAL assessment and adjusted for the date of the APPEAL assessment. Current SLE activity was assessed during the APPEAL visit via the Systematic Lupus Activity Questionnaire (SLAQ; range 0–44; higher scores indicating greater SLE‐related disease activity). 23 The Brief Index of Lupus Damage (BILD) score (range, 0–46; higher scores indicate greater cumulative SLE‐related organ damage) 24 , 25 closest to the APPEAL visit was obtained from linked GOAL data; cumulative damage to individual systems was also defined by the BILD at the study visit, and individual medications were categorized. Other factors included body mass index (BMI), which was calculated from measured (in‐person visits) or reported (remote visits) weight and height. 2 Physical activity (including definitions of low, moderate, and high activity) was assessed with the International Physical Activity Questionnaire – Short Form. 26 In brief, individuals were considered to have low (vs moderate or high) activity if they did not have one of the following: three or more days of vigorous activity for ≥20 minutes per day, five or more days of moderate‐intensity physical activity or walking for ≥30 minutes per day; or five or more days of any combination of walking, moderate‐intensity, or vigorous intensity activities achieving a minimum of at least 600 metabolic equivalent of task‐minutes per week. 27 Depression was assessed via the validated 8‐item Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Depression Short Form‐8a 28 , 29 and reported as T‐scores (where 50 = mean score and 10 = 1 SD). Participants’ perceived stress was assessed using the 10‐item Perceived Stress Scale (range 0–40; higher scores indicate greater perceived stress, with scores of ≥20 considered high levels of stress). 30 , 31 Basic activities of daily living (BADLs; eg, bathing, dressing, transferring [such as from bed to chair]) 32 and instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs; eg, shopping, managing finances) 33 scales were reported as scores, with higher scores representing more dependence. The PROMIS Physical Functioning‐Short Form 12a 34 was used to assess participants’ perceptions of their physical functioning, and raw scores were scaled to T‐scores. We used the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) to assess physical performance (balance, gait speed, and lower body strength) among APPEAL participants. 35 , 36 SPPB scores range from 0 to 12, with higher scores representing better performance; subscores for each component range from 0 to 4. Fluid cognition was measured using the NIH Toolbox among those who completed in‐person visits; we defined cognitive impairment as an age‐corrected standard score >1.5 SD below the mean score. 16 Current use of medications was self‐reported by the participant, and individual fall risk–increasing drugs were assigned to categories of interest as appropriate, including antihypertensives, antidepressants, sedatives/antianxiety medications, and pain medications (opioid and nonopioid). 37 We defined polypharmacy and hyperpolypharmacy as reporting ≥5 and ≥10 medications, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of participants were described overall, and percentages reporting falls, number of falls, and falls requiring medical intervention were tabulated. Percentages of participants reporting various fall‐related intrinsic and extrinsic factors associated with their falls were also calculated. Associations of any falls with selected characteristics were assessed with multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for age, sex, and race. In sensitivity analyses, we additionally explored factors associated with falls that were reported by participants to be associated with lost balance or muscle weakness, the two most common intrinsic factors associated with falls reported by participants. Complete case analysis was used. The statistical significance threshold was set at 0.05. All analyses were conducted using Stata v. 18.0.

RESULTS

Characteristics of study participants

Overall, the mean age of our study participants was 46.2 years; 40.9% were ≥50 years of age, 91.7% were female, and 82.6% were Black (Table 1). Almost one‐quarter (23.0%) had a high school degree or less. The median duration of disease was 14.8 years, with median SLAQ and BILD scores of 11 and 2, respectively. Participants had a mean BMI of 30.1, with nearly half (47.2%) being obese, and 73.9% reported low physical activity. The mean overall SPPB scores was 9.0; subscores for balance and chair stands were 3.8 and 1.7. Most had few or mild BADL and IADL limitations, with median scores of 6 and 9, respectively. Nearly half of participants (42.8%) reported currently taking steroids, 61.5% reported taking antihypertensives, 55.0% reported taking pain medications, and 21.9% reported taking antidepressants (Table 1).

Reported falls among individuals with SLE

About one‐third of participants (30.7%) reported falling in the past year, and 19.2% fell twice or more (Table 2). Among those who reported falls, the median number of falls was two, and one‐quarter (25.6%) reported needing medical intervention for one or more of their falls (Table 2). When asked about the intrinsic and extrinsic factors associated with their falls, more than half suggested that loss of balance (78.1%), slipping or tripping (64.2%), and weakness (53.3%) were factors in their fall(s) (Figure 1). Other common factors were unstable joints (eg, ankles, knees, hip; 49.6%), sudden leg weakness (46.7%), pain (44.5%), dizziness (29.9%), lightheadedness (25.6%), environment (eg, rugs, uneven sidewalks; 23.4%), rushing or running (19.7%), shoes or footwear (13.1%), or other factors (12.4%); <10% reported heart racing, confusion, low lighting, or fainting/blacking out as factors in their fall(s) (Figure 1).

Participant characteristics associated with falls

Although the older participants (≥50 years) were more likely to report falls than the youngest participants (18–34 years), this association was not statistically significant (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.82; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.22–3.07) (Table 3). A sensitivity analysis using a threshold of 65 years likewise did not show a statistically significant relationship (aOR, 1.85, 95% CI, 0.41–8.36). Similarly, male versus female participants were not statistically significantly less likely to report falls (aOR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.27–1.36). Race and education were not associated with falls. Additionally, the presence of cognitive impairment was not associated with a difference in falls (aOR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.28–1.32). Higher disease activity (aOR, 1.78; 95% CI, 1.44–2.21) and damage (aOR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.00–1.49) scores were associated with higher odds of reporting falls, as were obesity (aOR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.04–2.41), higher depressive symptom scores (aOR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.14–1.80), and higher BADL (aOR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.16–1.43) and IADL (aOR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.07–1.21) scores. Higher physical functioning (aOR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.38–0.66) and performance (aOR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.71–0.87) scores were associated with lower odds of reported falls. Taking ≥10 medications (compared with <5 medications) was associated with a higher risk of falls (aOR, 3.36; 95% CI, 1.83–6.16). With regard to specific medication classes, current use of antidepressant (aOR, 1.82; 95% CI, 1.13–2.93), pain (aOR, 2.60; 95% CI, 1.68–4.00), as well as opioid medications (aOR, 4.52; 95% CI, 2.39–8.56) were associated with higher odds of reported falls, but steroids, sedatives, and antihypertensive medications were not (Table 3). Being currently employed versus not was associated with substantially lower odds of reporting falls (aOR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.40–0.94); similarly, high (vs lowest) physical activity was associated with lower odds (aOR, 0.33; 95% CI, 0.14–0.76).

In sensitivity analyses examining only those falls that were attributed by participants to loss of balance (Supplemental Table 1), the associations of disease activity (aOR, 2.11; 95% CI, 1.66–2.76), damage (aOR, 1.44; 95% CI, 1.17–1.78), employment (aOR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.30–0.78), depressive symptoms (aOR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.20–1.95), perceived stress (aOR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.07–1.71), and medications—including antidepressants (aOR, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.12–3.06), pain medications (aOR, 2.75; 95% CI, 1.70–4.44), as well as opioid medications (aOR, 4.74; 95% CI, 2.51–8.97)—with these falls were stronger than in the primary analysis. Performance (aOR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.73–0.89) and increased physical activity (aOR, 0.39; 95% CI, 0.16–0.98) were statistically significantly associated with falls, but this relationship was not stronger than in the primary analysis. In contrast, obesity was not statistically significantly associated with falls attributed to lost balance. For falls attributed to weakness, results were similar. However, education (some college vs college graduate aOR, 2.07; 95% CI,1.12–3.79) and White versus Black race (aOR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.09–0.95) were statistically significantly associated with falls attributed to weakness, whereas increased physical activity was not statistically significantly associated with falls (aOR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.17–1.48). Furthermore, although pain medications (aOR, 2.40; 95% CI, 1.38–4.16) and opioids (aOR, 4.10; 95% CI, 2.06–8.16) remained significantly associated with falls, this relationship was not as strong as the primary analysis (Supplemental Table 1).

A sensitivity analysis was also performed for those falls attributed to slipping/tripping—the most commonly reported extrinsic factor (Supplemental Table 2). Among falls due to slipping/tripping, the associations with employment (aOR, 0.45; 95% CI, 0.26–0.75), physical activity (high versus low) (aOR, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.09–0.83), and depressive symptoms (aOR, 1.46; 95% CI, 1.12–1.91) were stronger than in the primary analysis. The associations with disease activity (aOR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.26–1.99), physical performance battery (aOR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.79–0.97), physical function (aOR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.41–0.77), BADL score (aOR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.09–1.37), IADL score (aOR, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.05–1.21), pain medications overall (aOR, 2.05; 95% CI, 1.24–3.39), and opioid pain medications (aOR, 2.37; 95% CI, 1.21–4.63) were statistically significant but not stronger than in primary analyses.

DISCUSSION

Around one‐third of SLE study participants reported a fall in the past year—a number that exceeds the prevalence of falls among the general US population over the age of 65 (27.6%). 38 Multiple intrinsic factors appear to be contributing to these falls, including loss of balance, weakness, joint instability, and pain—a finding that is consistent with known SLE pathology leading to many musculoskeletal and neurologic manifestations. This relationship is supported by the study's statistically significant associations found between falls and disease activity or damage. Extrinsic factors likely contributed to the high prevalence of falls, with nearly two‐thirds of study participants reporting that they tripped or slipped during their fall. Specific medications and polypharmacy may have also been a factor in our participants’ reported falls. Taking ≥10 medications was associated with a substantially higher odds of reported falls among participants. However, although all of the examined medication classes in this study were associated with higher odds of reported falls, the relationship was only statistically significant for antidepressants and pain medications. In contrast, our results were not statistically significant but did trend toward those with cognitive impairment having fewer falls—which was unexpected. One possible hypothesis is that people living with cognitive impairment may be more sedentary leading to fewer falls—but this would need to be studied further. Another possibility is that those with cognitive impairment may have forgotten about prior falls (particularly given the risk for recall errors).

These results are largely consistent with other previously published literature in the aging population. Prior research around falls in older adults found that half of those reporting falls noted extrinsic factors (eg, slippery floors, insufficient lighting), in addition to intrinsic risk factors (eg, impairment in mobility and balance, weakness, pain, comorbidities such as arthritis). These findings are additionally consistent with falls research in older adults that found falls are often multifactorial in nature and suggests that interventions to address falls for those with SLE may need to target multiple risk factors outside of treating SLE alone. 17 Prior research also found trends in the general aging population where antidepressants, sedatives, opioids, and nonopioid pain medications were associated with an increased risk of falls. 37 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 Therefore, efforts to reduce falls risk in the SLE population could target medications including deprescribing, considering alternatives, or counseling patients on risk.

These results are also consistent with other previously published literature on SLE that found increased rates of cognitive changes, functional changes, and frailty associated with SLE 8 , 36 , 43 , 44 —even among middle‐aged adults. These findings agree with a prior smaller study of 78 patients (48 SLE; 30 control) in Turkey that found a higher prevalence of falls among adults with SLE (15%), relative to a comparison group who did not have SLE (3%). 45 Although this earlier study found a relationship between disease characteristics and falls, it did not note a relationship between falls and other patient characteristics. The current study extends the examination of falls prevalence in SLE to a diverse US population and expands upon prior research by further examining the contributing role of several intrinsic and extrinsic factors in participants’ falls. Several of these risk factors—including issues with balance, weakness, joint instability, and dizziness—are conditions often found more frequently in older adults, raising concerns that syndromes more common in older adults may play a major role in falls for those living with SLE.

This high estimated prevalence of falls among the SLE population suggests a need for increased clinical attention to screening for falls—and potentially other geriatric syndromes—during clinical assessments for those living with SLE. SLE providers could consider modeling their practice after other subspecialties (eg, oncology and nephrology), 46 , 47 which have started to incorporate geriatric assessments (both rapid and comprehensive) into their practice. Alternative approaches could include integration of an interdisciplinary perspective or adopting an age‐friendly care position (eg, 5Ms framework: mobility, medications, mind, matters most, and multicomplexity) 48 to their patient care models, which would address several risk factors for falls identified in this study (eg, mobility [balance issues, weakness, joint instability, and dizziness] and medications [antidepressants, pain medications, and opioids]).

There were several limitations to this study. First, as noted above, participants were asked to remember falls that had occurred in the prior year, which could lead to recall bias. For example, those with cognitive issues or with less severe falls may be less likely to remember falls or when they occurred. Additionally, participants may have similarly forgotten details around their falls (including all of the potential contributing intrinsic and extrinsic factors). Second, the cross‐sectional nature of the study limits our ability to distinguish risk factors from outcomes; for example, it could be argued that lack of employment and lower physical activity are a result of falls. Third, although data around potential contributing factors were collected, the observational nature of the study inevitably carries the risk of residual confounding. We attempted to mitigate this risk by asking about a number of socioeconomic, pharmacologic, and medical factors; however, future studies could expand by seeking additional information, including data around home environment and medication dosing (eg, prednisone). Fourth, the questionnaires did not collect information on fractures or the severity of these falls. As falls are the leading cause of injury and death in adults older than 65 years of age, these clinical outcomes will be important to examine in future studies to better understand the implications of this geriatric syndrome among the SLE population. 49 Additionally, although the study enrolled around two dozen adults over the age of 65 years, this number was underpowered for looking specifically at outcomes in older adults; future studies could focus on enrolling more patients older than 65 years of age to better understand how SLE affects older adults. Finally, although the questionnaire included items about the factors surrounding these falls, it did not delineate how different factors could have contributed to each individual fall.

This study demonstrated the prevalence of and contributing factors to falls in the SLE population. These findings support a relationship between SLE and falls. Future studies could focus on understanding if there are differences between SLE individuals and the general population regarding severity of the falls, incidence of injury, and sequelae of falls. Future studies could additionally target, develop, and implement interventions to prevent falls among those with SLE.
