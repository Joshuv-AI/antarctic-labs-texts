Role of deformation twins in magnetic heterogeneity of MC 1100 and Barkhausen noise emission
Authors: M. Neslušan, J. Veselý, R. Čep, P. Minárik, J. Čapek, M. Jacková
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper deals with the surface heterogeneity in the near-surface region of the high strength low alloyed steel MC 1100. The remarkable surface thermal softening takes place due to decarburization during hot rolling, which in turn avoids very fine short twins within the grain interior during the accelerated cooling. The depth and extent of this twin-free region strongly vary along the sheet width, whereas this effect only fringes along the sheet length. The high sensitivity of the Barkhausen noise technique against the variable depth extent of short twins free region originates from the interaction among the domain walls in motion and lattice imperfection in which very fine short twins play a strong role due to their high pinning strength. The study demonstrates that the influence of residual stresses can be neglected. On the other hand, evolution between the tensile stress and Barkhausen noise is strongly altered as a function of microstructure heterogeneity. For this reason, employing the Barkhausen noise technique to monitor the stress state of components made of MC 1100 in real operation can be a quite complex task.

Introduction

High strength low alloyed (HSLA) steels are often employed for automotive, civil or petrochemical components1,2. Typical applications include building constructions, bridges, offshore platforms, pipelines, strengthening parts of car bodies, etc.1,2. These bodies are capable of bearing high stresses and represent quite cheap materials due to a low fraction of alloying elements. An excellent strength-to-weight ratio makes them competitive materials for construction weight lightening. Their high strength is a product of plastic deformation (usually rolling) at elevated temperatures (exceeding the austenitizing temperature) and the synergistic effect of accelerated cooling. The final mechanical properties can be customized when the diversity of hot rolling and cooling conditions are varied with their microalloying. The mechanism by which the functional properties are produced is quite complex, along with their microstructure. The mixture of bainite and ferrite for HSLA steels of lower strength is replaced by a mixture of bainite and martensite of variable fraction for higher-strength HSLA3,4. However, the improved strength is at the expense of the reduced toughness. Accelerated cooling also engenders carbide precipitation of very fine size. Microalloying elements such as Ti, Nb and V can also strengthen the matrix via their dissolution in the Fe matrix and/or the formation of fine precipitates1,5,6. The newly developed HSLA can also contain Mo, Ni, La, Ce and Cu to improve corrosion resistance, toughness, etc.7,8.

HSLA steel chemistry and processing are still subjected to extensive development to customize their functional properties with respect to specific requirements during their real usage. Li et al.5 improved HSLA steel strength by adding Ti and explained the matrix strengthening by the formation of circular TiC carbides. Zhu et al.9 investigated the effect of rapid heat treatment on mechanical properties and precipitation behavior of medium carbon low alloy steel ing. Yu et al.10 linked mechanical properties with the crystallographic features. Zhang et al.11 compared the microstructure and mechanical properties of HSLA fabricated by additive manufacturing and conventional casting. Geng et al.12 applied the powder metallurgical strategy to improve the performance of low alloyed steels. Cui et al.13 found different structures in the near-surface and bulk as a result of tempering treatment. Xi et al.14 investigated the influence seasonal Ciona macrofouling on corrosion and hydrogen permeation behavior of a HSLA. Li et al.15 investigated the flow stress–strain curves and dynamic recrystallization behavior of high carbon low alloy steels during hot deformation.

Components made of HSLA steels usually play a major role with respect to the bearing capacity of the system in which these steels are integrated. Their damage due to long-term operation can be a result of a complicated stress state accelerated by corrosion attack together with the synergistic contribution of heating. In order to avoid their failures during real operations, suitable and reliable monitoring techniques of acceptable sensitivity would be beneficial. HSLA steels are ferromagnetic structures with magnetic substructures within the grains’ interior. For this reason, magnetic Barkhausen noise (MBN) might be considered a promising candidate for the mentioned task. The acoustic component of Barkhausen noise emission can also be utilized in some cases16. However, the acoustic technique can suffer from over-helmeting disruptive background noise, which makes the signals obtained peculiar and difficult to understand. Having a much better signal-to-noise ratio, the electromagnetic component of Barkhausen emission preserves information about the interaction of Domain Walls (DWs) with microstructure and/or stress state. In contrast, the contribution of background noise is well-distinguishable and can be omitted or easily subtracted17. MBN originates from irreversible and discontinuous DWs motion when the electromagnetic and acoustic pulse is emitted as soon as the altering external magnetic field exceeds the strength of pinning sites anchoring DWs in their static positions18,19. The sensitivity of MBN emission concerning microstructure is based on the aforementioned interaction between lattice imperfections and DWs20–22. However, MBN is also sensitive to the stress state when DWs in Fe alloys tend to align along the direction of tensile stress, increasing MBN23,24 (DWs align perpendicularly against the compressive stress and decrease MBN). The synergistic contribution of microstructure and stress state might cause difficulties in unwrapping their prevailing roles, especially in the case of residual stresses. However, DWs alignment under external stress can clearly mediate information about stress amplitude24,25.

HSLA steels undergo a processing route in which hot rolling is performed without the presence of a protective atmosphere. Therefore, the surface can suffer from the noticeable depletion of carbon, which has an ineluctable role in the intricate phase transition during cooling. This study elucidates that the surface softening and its depth extent can be monitored by MBN. However, good sensitivity of MBN regarding microstructure and/or stress might blend in the acquired signal, and superior contribution could be difficult to single out during the tensile test. The systematic pilot study in the field of low alloyed steels3 revealed that MBN is mainly governed by DWs density and their alignment, whereas the residual stress state takes on the fringing effect. It was also reported that the systematic evolution in MBN and the extracted features are linked to the emission originating from the deeper bulk, whereas the surface signal can vary remarkably among the neighbouring regions. This study elucidates the intrinsic aspects contributing to MBN with respect to the strong heterogeneity of Barkhausen noise emission and their repercussions regarding the evolution between MBN and tensile stress.

Finally, the samples of length 200 mm of the gauged shape (as depicted in Fig. 2—left upper corner) were cut off to check the influence of the variable degree and extent of thermal softening and the corresponding MBN on mechanical properties. These samples were cut along RD (according the standard EN ISO 6892–1), and the variable MBN is linked to the gauged region, which is 50 mm in length and 12 mm in width. Further 2 series of 7 samples emitting the variable MBN along RD were cut for the metallographic, EBSDS and TEM observations as well as the analysis of relationship between MBN and tensile stress. The samples were loaded by the uniaxial tensile test on the Instron 5895 device.

In order to explain MBN in terms of altering microstructure futures, the small specimens were cut in the regions of variable MBN. Seven different positions emitting the MBN in the range from 555 to 1440 mV were cut off. Metallographic observations of the near-surface regions and bulk were carried out on the specimens of length 20 mm cut along RD (hot moulded, ground, polished, and etched by 3% Nital for 5 s). Metallographic images were analyzed on the light microscope Neophot 2 with the use of Niss Elements software. In order to investigate the degree and depth extent of thermal softening, the microhardness measurement HV0.1 was also performed (Innova Test 400TM device, 100 g load, dwell time 10 s, 3 repetitive measurements). In order to explain MBN in terms of altering residual stresses, residual stress depth gradient was analyzed using the X-ray diffraction (XRD) technique (Proto iXRD Combo diffractometer, {211} planes, CrKα radiation, sin2ψ method, ½s2 = 5.75 TPa−1, s1 = − 1.25 TPa−1).

TEM observations were carried out on JEOL JEM-2200FS operated at 200 kV. The Nanomegas Astar system was used for orientation mapping. Samples for TEM were prepared by FIB lamella lift-out from designated areas.

SEM observation was performed using a ZEISS Aurica Compact microscope on mechanically polished samples, with the final step of electropolishing using Struers Lectropol and Struers A3 solution. EBSD measurements were performed on the samples with a reasonable step size using an EDAX EBSD camera installed in SEM. The EBSD data post-processing was performed in OIM Analysis 8 software, comprising spherical re-indexing.

The Empyrean diffractometer (Malvern Panalytical B.V., Almelo, Netherlands) with cobalt radiation was used for the tetragonality and texture (preferred orientation) analyses by XRD. The tetragonality was determined from the XRD patterns using the Rietveld refinement performed in MStruct software26. Texture analysis was performed based on ODF calculation from experimental pole figures, which were obtained on three diffraction lines {200}, {211}, {220} of the ferrite/martensite phase. The MATLAB™ toolbox MTEX software was used to process the data and plot inverse pole figures.

MBN measurements were carried out using RollScan 350. MBN signal was processed by MicroScan 600 software. The raw MBN signals were filtered by the bad pass filter 20–1000 kHz. The effective value, referred to as MBN, was calculated on the base of the filtered signal. Furthermore, MBN envelopes were constructed, and PP was extracted afterwards. All aforementioned MBN features together with the signal and envelopes visualization, were obtained directly from the MicroScan 600. PP values (Peak Position) are the magnetic field in which an envelope attains the maximum. Magnetizing conditions are as follows: magnetizing frequency 125 Hz (of sine profile), magnetizing voltage 5 V, serial sensor S1-18-12-01. The calculated penetration depth of magnetic field produced by this sensor (in this particular case) is about 0.64 mm. All MBN features were obtained by averaging 12 consecutive MBN bursts. MBN measurements were carried out along the rolling direction (RD) as well as in the transverse direction (TD), see Fig. 2.

MBN as a function of matric microstructure—metallographic, SEM, EBSD and TEM observations

Mechanical and corresponding magnetic softening in the near-surface region is governed by decarburization during hot rolling and subsequent quenching. During the hot rolling above the austenitic temperatures with the absence of a protective atmosphere, surface decarburization cannot be avoided. During hot rolling, carbon is fully dissolved in the austenite matrix in this case. However, during the rapid cooling, when the martensitic transformation occurs, C in this phase is above the soluble limit27. The quenching cycle initiates C migration when the long-range C motion is suspended due to rapid cooling. The Microstructure of the MC1100 after quenching is usually composed of martensite having the lath morphology. These laths have a variable length of several hundreds of nanometres, and the length is in tents of micrometres28. The long twins are very often formed and neighbouring along with the laths, see Fig. 8b,c. The long twins have a uniform orientation following the parental laths. On the other hand, short twins can also be observed and reported as internal twins29–31. These short twins are formed inside the laths and/or the long twins, and all crystallographically equivalent twin systems are equally present, see Fig. 8a,b. As contrasted against the long twins, the short twin boundaries can be of curved shape due to the different speeds of boundary motion during the detwinning process28. It was also reported that the absence of twins is due to decarburization when the carbon content falls below the critical threshold28. Such behaviour can be found on the sample surfaces depicted in Fig. 8c.Fig. 8TEM observations—short and long twins.

One can see that the valuable differences can be reported for the free surface only in RD when the amplitude of residual stresses drops down when the stronger MBN is emitted, see Fig. 15a and Fig. 16a. Erratic microstructure and the corresponding divergences in MBN raise the question of how the alterations mentioned above affect mechanical properties as those indicated in Tab. 2. To answer such question, the series of 17 gauged samples of the size and shape described in Chapter 2 were prepared for the uniaxial tensile test. MBN versus tensile stress

To address the question of whether the MBN sensitivity against the external load is inevitably lost, the series of 7 samples (emitting the MBN as those ascribed in chapter 3.1) underwent the uniaxial loading with the controlled load and the corresponding tensile stress. The MBN in-situ measurements revealed strong divergences in the relationship between MBN and the assessed stress, see Fig. 17.Fig. 17MBN versus tensile stress (tensile stress along RD).

MBN versus tensile stress (tensile stress along RD).

The evolution of MBN in ferromagnetic bodies versus stress is driven by the competition between the energy of magnetocrystalline anisotropy Ea and the magnetoelastic energy Eσ19,36,37.

On the other hand, the magnetoelastic energy Eσ can be expressed as follows19,36,37:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{\sigma } = \, \left( {{-}3\lambda_{s} cos^{2} \varphi } \right)/2,$$\end{document}where λs is the isotropic magnetostriction, and φ defines the angle between the direction of magnetization and the direction of stress σ.

Having the positive magnetostriction of Fe polycrystalline alloys at lower magnetic fields18, the MBN grows with tensile stress due to the predomination of Ea when DWs become aligned into the direction of the applied stress, see Fig. 17a. However, when the matrix contains the grains being preferentially oriented along the direction of tensile stress (the region nearby the sheet edges emitting the high MBN, see Fig. 11c), this mechanism is fully consumed, all movable DWs are aligned within the MBN sensing volume and the evolution of MBN versus tensile stress exhibits the descending trend. Furthermore, Fig. 17 also demonstrates that the ascending MBN measured along RD is compensated by the dropping region in TD and vice versa. Figure 17 depicts that the sensitivity of MBN versus tensile stress is fully lost as soon as the stress of magnitude 400 MPa is attained.

The different sensitivity of the MBN technique against the exerted stress with respect to the variable surface decarburization in this particular case represents a quite serious obstacle with respect to the applicability of the MBN technique for monitoring components made of this steel. The non-destructive monitoring of components due to their surface damage, such as corrosion, fatigue load, etc. and the superimposing stress state requires the initial information about MBN as well as the direction of sheet rolling are known. Otherwise, the acquitted MBN cannot be interpreted straightforwardly, and a complementary technique should be considered. Further, MBN signals post-processing to extract additional MBN parameters also do not provide evolutions in which certain surface states are unique and exclusive concerning decarburization degree and stress state.

Short discussion concerning the surface decarburization

Decarburizing of the surface is generally the thermally activated diffusion process of C depletion due to the chemical gradient between the sheet and the surrounding environment. The aforementioned diffusion process considers C diffusion through the lattice only. However, the complementary contribution of grain boundaries and the accelerated diffusion along them should also be accounted for flux since the activation energy for diffusion through the grain boundaries is lower than that through the lattice only38. Furthermore, the superimposing contribution of plastic work and accelerated diffusion flux due to the presence of dislocations should not be overlooked as well. Being so, the time t to achieve the full decarburization at a certain depth x is shorter than that depicted in Fig. 18.

Finally, the mechanism by which the final surface of a hot rolled sheet is obtained should be discussed. A hot rolled sheet grows with respect to its width and length, which is inevitably linked to its increasing surface area. The new surface is erratic and produced when the mass lying in a certain depth below the surface becomes the new surface. For this reason, the regions decarburized during the hot rolling at a specific phase of this process are neighbouring with those less affected by decarburization extruded from the depth towards the surface, see Figs. 19, 20 and 21. Being so, the regions of the different degree of decarburization are integrated in the MBN sensing volume (sensing area 2 × 2 mm2 and the estimated depth about 0.1 mm) which in turn explains strong MBN fluctuation through the sheet width. As noted earlier, this mechanism also elucidates remarkably higher MBN at the sheet edges and their exclusive behaviour. The surface on the sheet edges is not supplemented by the “fresh” mass unaffected (or less affected) by decarburization. Its long-term exposure with respect to decarburization and superimposed plastic work results in relatively high decarburized depth and preferential grain alignment, see Fig. 21.Fig. 19Metallographic images along TD.Fig. 20Extrusion of the bulk matrix towards the free surface.Fig. 21IQ images of the surface.

Conclusions

The main findings of this study can be summarized as follows:hot rolling of MC 1100 initiates the surface decarburizing of variable extent,short twins are produced in bulk during rapid cooling after hot rolling,due to surface decarburization and carbon depletion, the near-surface region of variable depth is free of short twins,the short twins take a strong role in DWs pinning and the corresponding MBN emission,the regions emitting low MBN are those less affected by the decarburizing process and vice versa,the non-uniform decarburizing process strongly alters the evolution of MBN versus tensile stress,the decarburizing has no influence on the guaranteed mechanical properties of MC 1100.

The strong fluctuation of MBN through the sheet surface represents a serious complication regarding monitoring components made of MC 1100 in real applications. When a component made of MC 1100 is subjected to reliable and repetitive monitoring using the MBN technique, the initial MBN screening should be carried out. These pilot measurements should be performed on the unloaded component before use, and the information about the rolling direction should be known. MBN provides a very fast response and potentially can be integrated into automated cycles or robotic cells. A deep understanding of the obtained signals is vital in order to extract true information about a component state.
