Association between calcitonin gene-related peptide and substance P in saliva and pulp according to anxiety levels in patients with dental pain: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Aliye Kamalak, Esra Balkanlıoğlu, Hilmi Taş, Rabia Hurşitoğlu, Hasan Dağlı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental pain is influenced by both local inflammatory processes and neurobiological and psychological factors. This study compared substance P (SP) and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) levels in pulp tissue and unstimulated saliva between patients with dental pain and painless controls, and investigated their relationships with pain severity, pulpal inflammation, and psychological distress.MethodsFifty-two adult patients indicated for primary endodontic treatment were enrolled and allocated into two groups: 26 patients with dental pain and 26 painless controls with no pain complaint and a VRS score of 0. Pain intensity was assessed using the Verbal Rating Scale (VRS). Depression, anxiety, and stress were evaluated using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21). Pulp tissue and unstimulated saliva samples were collected and analyzed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Pulpal inflammation was graded histopathologically. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Effect sizes were calculated using eta squared (η²). Correlations were assessed using Pearson or Spearman coefficients to examine associations between neuropeptide levels, pain severity, and inflammation grade.ResultsPulp calcitonin gene-related peptide levels were significantly higher in patients with dental pain than in painless controls (p = 0.007; η² = 0.146). Salivary substance P levels were also significantly increased in the painful group (p = 0.044; η² = 0.080). No significant between-group differences were observed for pulp substance P or salivary calcitonin gene-related peptide (p > 0.05). Depression, anxiety, and stress scores were all significantly higher in patients with dental pain (all p < 0.001). Pain severity showed positive correlations with pulp calcitonin gene-related peptide (r = 0.332) and salivary substance P (r = 0.324), whereas no significant association was found between pain severity and histopathological inflammation grade.ConclusionsPulp calcitonin gene-related peptide may be associated with local neurogenic activation in symptomatic pulpal pain, while salivary substance P may represent a potential noninvasive candidate marker for pain-associated responses. Background

The dental pulp is a highly specialized connective tissue characterized by dense vascularization, rich sensory innervation, and a strong immunologic capacity. Owing to these features, it plays a central role in inflammation, nociception, and tissue repair. Pulpal injury resulting from caries, trauma, or iatrogenic irritation initiates an inflammatory cascade often accompanied by microbial invasion, leading to increased vascular permeability, hemodynamic alterations, and enhanced neurogenic activity [1, 2].

Clinically, pulpitis is largely diagnosed based on symptoms and sensibility testing; however, numerous investigations have demonstrated that these methods do not reliably correlate with histopathological findings. This clinical–histological discrepancy underscores the need for objective biomarkers capable of more accurately reflecting the biological status of the pulp and the degree of inflammation [3]. In this context, molecular biomarkers have emerged as promising tools for assessing pulpal activity beyond conventional diagnostic tests.

Neuropeptides released from peripheral sensory nerve endings—particularly Substance P (SP) and Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP)—are key mediators of pulpal inflammation and dental pain. These molecules contribute to neurogenic inflammation through vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and amplification of pro-inflammatory signaling. Clinical studies have consistently reported elevated SP and CGRP levels in inflamed pulp tissue, with associations observed between neuropeptide expression, caries severity, and pulpal pathology. Furthermore, systematic evidence indicates that SP levels in gingival crevicular fluid are significantly increased in painful teeth compared with healthy controls, suggesting that neuropeptide diffusion into peripheral biological fluids may reflect active nociceptive processes [4]. However, methodological heterogeneity and limited standardization have restricted the clinical translation of these findings.

Neuropeptide expression is not exclusively triggered by microbial inflammation. Mechanical stimuli such as occlusal trauma and orthodontic force have been shown to significantly increase pulpal SP and CGRP expression, along with angiogenic mediators such as VEGF, indicating activation of neurovascular defense mechanisms independent of overt histopathological severity [1, 5]. These observations suggest that neuropeptide levels may reflect functional neurogenic activation rather than purely structural inflammatory damage.

The clinical applicability of pulpal biomarkers remains limited despite their diagnostic potential due to the need for invasive sampling procedures [3]. Consequently, interest has shifted toward non-invasive biological media such as saliva, which can be collected easily and repeatedly. Elevated salivary CGRP levels have been documented in conditions involving trigeminal system activation, including migraine and other headache types such as tension-type headache [6, 7], supporting the concept that salivary neuropeptides may reflect nociceptive activation. Nevertheless, studies simultaneously evaluating SP and CGRP levels in both pulp and saliva in the context of dental pain—and examining their relationships with pain severity, histopathological inflammation, and psychological status—remain scarce [3, 4].

Dental pain itself is increasingly recognized as a multidimensional experience that cannot be explained solely by peripheral inflammatory mechanisms. Psychological stress has been shown to influence neuropeptide release and modulate pain perception by enhancing both central and peripheral sensitization [1]. However, clinical data integrating dental pain, neuropeptide expression, histopathological findings, and psychological parameters within a unified model are still insufficient.

The aim of this study was to compare SP and CGRP levels in the pulp and saliva of individuals with and without dental pain and to evaluate their associations with pain severity, pulpal inflammation, and psychological status (depression, anxiety, and stress). The null hypothesis was that pulp and salivary SP/CGRP levels would not differ according to pain status and would not be associated with psychological stress. By integrating neurobiological and psychosocial components within a single analytical framework, this study seeks to address a critical gap in the current understanding of dental pain mechanisms.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted at the Faculty of Dentistry, Kahramanmaraş Sütçü İmam University, Kahramanmaraş, Türkiye. Ethical approval and informed consent procedures are described in the Declarations section. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07330024; Registration date: December 12, 2025).

Fifty-two adult patients indicated for primary endodontic treatment were consecutively recruited and allocated into painful and painless groups according to their pain status. Each participant contributed only one tooth to the study, and no multiple teeth from the same individual were included in the analysis. The aim of the study was to compare pulp and salivary substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptide levels between patients with dental pain and painless controls and to examine their association with anxiety levels, pain severity, and pulpal inflammation.

Sample size calculation (G*Power analysis)

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 (two independent groups, two-tailed) [8]. Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.79) for differences in SP and CGRP levels between groups, with α = 0.05 and 80% power, the minimum required sample size was 26 participants per group. Accordingly, a total of 52 participants (26 per group) were included in the final analysis. The sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome variables (SP and CGRP levels). However, given the number of secondary analyses performed, including correlation and structural modeling, the results should be interpreted with caution.

Study group

Participants were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure a homogeneous study population and to control for potential confounding factors.

Inclusion criteria

Adult individuals aged 18–65 years; having ASA I–II systemic health status, presence of permanent teeth requiring endodontic treatment due to caries or restorative needs, not having used analgesics in the last 24 h, not having consumed food or drink in the last 2 h before measurement, willingness to participate in the study, and providing informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Periodontal disease (probing depth > 4 mm or BOP > 20), systemic inflammatory, autoimmune, neuropathic or chronic pain diseases, acute infection, fever, or antibiotic use within the last 7 days, pregnancy or lactation, regular psychiatric medication use (antidepressants, anxiolytics, antipsychotics, antiepileptics, etc.), use of cigarettes or tobacco products, alcohol consumption or high caffeine intake within the last 24 h, previous endodontic treatment, history of trauma or pulp involvement due to restorative procedures, systemic diseases that may affect neuropeptide levels (e.g., diabetes, thyroid diseases, renal/hepatic failure), medication use affecting salivary flow (anticholinergics, antihistamines, beta-blockers, etc.). In addition, potential confounding factors known to influence salivary neuropeptide levels, including smoking, systemic inflammatory conditions, medication use, and recent food intake, were controlled through the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Demographic data, pain assessment results, and DASS-21 scores were recorded in the study form.

Pain assessment

Pain intensity was assessed using the Verbal Rating Scale (VRS), categorized as 0 = no pain, 1 = mild pain, 2 = moderate pain, and 3 = severe pain. The VRS was selected due to its simplicity, ease of administration, and suitability for clinical settings, allowing patients to reliably categorize their pain intensity without requiring numerical or visual interpretation.

Psychological assessment

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) was used to assess depression, anxiety, and stress levels. The validated Turkish version of the scale was applied. DASS-21 consists of 3 subscales: Depression (7 items); Anxiety (7 items); Stress (7 items). Each item is scored from 0 to 3, and the scores of the seven items in each subscale were summed to obtain subscale scores. The scale was administered to the participants under the supervision of a researcher in a clinical setting.

Sample collection and laboratory analyses

In teeth requiring endodontic intervention, local anesthesia was provided using Ultracain DS Forte (Sanofi Aventis, Paris, France), and rubber dam isolation was achieved with a DermaDam Synthetic Rubber Dam (Ultradent Products Inc., South Jordan, UT, USA). Access cavities were prepared using a sterile diamond bur (Komet FG Diamond Bur, Gebr. Brasseler GmbH & Co. KG, Lemgo, Germany).

Pulp tissue was obtained from each study tooth using sterile paper points and an excavator. Immediately after collection, the entire pulp specimen from the same tooth was divided into two portions. One portion was used for ELISA-based neuropeptide analysis, whereas the remaining portion was fixed in formalin and processed for histopathological examination. No additional pulp specimens or separate teeth were collected for the two analyses. Samples were collected in sterile Eppendorf microcentrifuge tubes, transported on ice, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. This approach ensured that both biochemical and histopathological analyses were performed on tissue derived from the same tooth.

In addition, all saliva samples were collected under standardized conditions, including fasting status and controlled collection time, to minimize variability. These measures were implemented to reduce the influence of circadian variation and recent dietary intake.

Saliva samples

Approximately 1.5 mL of unstimulated saliva was collected from each participant for 5 min before and after the procedure. The samples were placed in 2 mL round-bottom Eppendorf microcentrifuge tubes (Camgen, CMGN276000002), centrifuged, and the supernatants were stored at − 80 °C.

Neuropeptide analysis (SP and CGRP)

The pulp tissue portions designated for ELISA analysis were individually homogenized in phosphate buffer prior to analysis. All pulp tissue samples were processed under standardized conditions and homogenized in a consistent buffer volume to reduce variability related to tissue quantity. The homogenization process was performed using a MICRA RT D-8 tissue homogenizer (Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Each sample was homogenized until complete cellular disruption was achieved, and the resulting suspension was stored under appropriate conditions for subsequent biochemical analysis.

SP ELISA kits in pulp and saliva samples (BT LAB (Bioassay Technology Laboratory; Cat. No: E1528Hu; Measurement range: 5–2000 ng/L; Sensitivity: 2.39 ng/L; Intra-assay CV: < 8%; Inter-assay CV: < 10%); and CGRP ELISA kits (BT LAB (Bioassay Technology Laboratory; Cat. No: E1061Hu; Measurement range: 2–600 ng/L; Sensitivity: 1.12 ng/L; Intra-assay CV: < 8%; Inter-assay CV: < 10%); were measured using ELISA kits in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Within the scope of the ELISA procedure, standards and samples were prepared in a 96-well microplate. After completing the incubation and washing steps, the optical density values were measured at 450 nm. Absorbance measurements were performed using a MULTISCAN GO microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Concentrations were calculated in ng/L using a standard curve. All samples were tested in duplicates. All assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and standard curves were generated for each run.

Evaluation of pulpal inflammation

After removal for histopathological examination, the pulp tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formaldehyde for at least 24 h. Following fixation, the samples were subjected to routine tissue processing and embedded in paraffin blocks. Serial sections of 4–5 μm thickness were prepared from each sample using a microtome, and then the sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) according to the standard protocol. The stained slides were then examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX51, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

The samples were divided into two separate groups as control and painful pulp, in accordance with the study design. In each group, acute and chronic inflammatory responses were graded using a semi-quantitative scoring system. The level of inflammation was scored as follows: 0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, and 3 = severe. Acute inflammation was defined by the predominance of neutrophilic infiltration, vascular congestion, edema, and, when present, focal microabscess formation. Chronic inflammation was characterized predominantly by mononuclear inflammatory cell infiltration, including lymphocytes, plasma cells, and macrophages, with varying degrees of fibrosis [9]. Semi-quantitative grading was based on both the density and distribution of inflammatory cells: grade 0 = no inflammatory infiltrate; grade 1 = sparse focal inflammatory infiltrate; grade 2 = moderate multifocal inflammatory infiltrate; and grade 3 = dense diffuse inflammatory infiltrate involving a substantial portion of the pulp tissue [9]. All histopathological evaluations were performed by two independent, blinded pathologists who were unaware of the study groups, and the scores were recorded (Fig. 1).

Blinding procedure

All biological samples were labeled with randomly coded ID numbers. The laboratory staff conducting the ELISA measurements were blinded to group information. The histopathologists were completely independent of the clinical data. The researcher performing the statistical analysis learned the group codes at the end of the study.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Group comparisons were conducted using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman coefficients based on data distribution.

Effect sizes were calculated using eta squared (η²), and structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using AMOS version 24.0. Due to multiple comparisons and the exploratory nature of the analyses, no formal correction for multiple testing was applied; therefore, results should be interpreted with caution.

When examining pulpal neuropeptide levels, the pulp CGRP level was found to be significantly higher in the painful group (129.59 ± 42.74 vs. 90.47 ± 56.38; p = 0.007, η² = 0.146). No significant difference was observed in the pulp SP levels between the two groups (p > 0.05), indicating that SP levels did not differ according to pain status. In saliva analyses, the salivary SP level was significantly higher in the pain group (600.88 ± 304.71 vs. 435.79 ± 270.40; p = 0.044), whereas salivary CGRP levels did not differ significantly (p > 0.05) (Table 3; Fig. 2A). However, no significant correlations were observed between corresponding pulp and salivary neuropeptide levels, including pulp SP–salivary SP and pulp CGRP–salivary CGRP associations (Table 2). Additionally, correlation analyses revealed that pulpal CGRP was not only associated with pain severity (r = 0.332, p < 0.05) but also showed a significant positive relationship with depression scores (r = 0.355, p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study advances a biopsychosocial interpretation of dental pain by simultaneously integrating pulpal and salivary neuropeptide profiles, histopathological inflammation scores, and psychological parameters. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, the observed relationships between psychological variables, pain, and neuropeptide levels should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. In this study, pain, histopathological inflammation, and psychological status were evaluated as distinct but interacting components of the dental pain experience. These variables were not considered interchangeable; rather, they represent different dimensions of a multifactorial process. Our findings suggest that dental pain cannot be explained solely by peripheral inflammatory processes; rather, it represents a dynamic neurobiological process modulated by psychological and systemic factors. The null hypothesis—that neuropeptide levels would not differ according to pain status and would not be associated with psychological variables—was largely rejected. An important consideration is that the painless group in the present study should not be regarded as a true healthy control because all included teeth required endodontic treatment. Previous studies have shown that SP and CGRP are present at physiological levels in healthy pulp but are generally upregulated in inflamed pulpal conditions [2, 3, 5]. Therefore, the higher pulpal CGRP level observed in the painful group is consistent with the existing literature. Similarly, the increased salivary SP level observed in the painful group is consistent with previous studies reporting elevated salivary SP concentrations in patients with dental pain [10].

The significantly elevated pulpal CGRP levels observed in painful cases suggest that CGRP may be associated with symptomatic pulpal stress and neurogenic inflammation. However, given the sample size and the absence of detailed pulpal diagnostic stratification, these findings should not be interpreted as establishing the diagnostic utility of CGRP. Within this framework, pulpal CGRP levels in the present study are interpreted primarily as indicators of local neurogenic activity associated with symptomatic pulpal conditions, whereas salivary substance P may reflect a broader systemic response related to pain perception and psychological stress. These biomarkers should therefore be considered context-dependent rather than specific to a single biological process. Caviedes-Bucheli et al. demonstrated that both mechanical stress and inflammatory stimuli induce SP and CGRP release in human pulp [5], findings that directly align with our results. Importantly, our data suggest that pulpal pain is not solely proportional to the density of inflammatory cell infiltration, but is critically dependent on neuropeptidergic activation at the level of sensory nerve endings. Inflammatory injury in periradicular tissues is known to trigger cascades of cytokines, arachidonic acid metabolites, nitric oxide, and neuropeptides, increasing vascular permeability and neural compression [11]. Within this framework, CGRP emerges not merely as a byproduct of inflammation, but as an active participant in the amplification of pulpal nociception. The concept of “microbial endocrinology” further reinforces this paradigm, proposing that SP and CGRP may function as interkingdom signaling molecules capable of modulating bacterial virulence and inflammatory responses [12], thereby positioning neuropeptides at the intersection of host–microbe interactions.

The absence of group differences in pulpal SP, accompanied by significantly elevated salivary SP levels in painful individuals, suggests compartmentalized neuropeptide regulation. Importantly, the present findings do not demonstrate a consistent direct correspondence between salivary and pulpal neuropeptide levels. Pulpal CGRP was increased in painful teeth, whereas salivary CGRP was not; conversely, salivary SP was increased in painful patients, whereas pulpal SP did not differ significantly between groups. Therefore, salivary biomarkers should not be interpreted as direct surrogates of pulpal neuropeptide activity. Rather, salivary SP may represent a pain-associated candidate marker influenced by broader oral, neurogenic, and systemic factors. While CGRP appears to reflect localized pulpal neurogenic activation, SP may represent a systemic stress-responsive biomarker associated with overall pain burden. Chavarría-Bolaños et al. reported early increases in SP during orthodontic intrusion, with more limited changes in CGRP and endogenous opioids [13], and further emphasized that SP regulation may extend beyond local tissue events to systemic stress responses [14]. Our findings are consistent with this dual-modulation hypothesis.

Morphological evidence indicates that a substantial proportion of pulpal axons co-express SP and CGRP [15], underscoring their integration within a bidirectional peripheral–central neural network. The previously reported reduction in salivary SP following endodontic treatment [16], supports the interpretation that salivary SP reflects active nociceptive states. However, heterogeneity highlighted in systematic analyses [4] underscores the necessity of methodological standardization in salivary biomarker research. Furthermore, associations between salivary neuropeptides and psychological stress in periodontal disease [17] strengthen the argument that SP may act as a stress-sensitive mediator within the broader neuroimmune axis.

A critical observation in our study is the weak association between histopathologically inflammation grades and subjective pain intensity. This finding reinforces the concept that neurogenic inflammation may precede or amplify classical cellular infiltration [18]. Pain perception may be more closely associated with dynamic neurochemical modulation than with static histological severity. Tissue-specific compartmentalization is further supported by findings in burning mouth syndrome, where persistent neuropathic pain occurs without elevated salivary CGRP [19], suggesting that neuropeptide expression is pathophysiological context-dependent. Moreover, previous studies have reported inconsistent elevations of SP in gingival crevicular fluid [4], indicating that SP diffusion into peripheral fluids may reflect active nociception rather than mere inflammatory burden [10].

This study provides clinical and biochemical evidence supporting elevated CGRP levels in symptomatic pulpal cases and reinforces the concept that pulpal pain is not the consequence of a single neuropeptide surge, but rather the result of coordinated activation within a complex neuroimmune network. Neuropeptides operate synergistically with inflammatory mediators, vascular responses, and peripheral neural sensitization. Previous studies demonstrating increased neuropeptide levels in the periodontal ligament following endodontic instrumentation indicate that mechanical irritation amplifies neurogenic inflammation [20, 21]. In line with these observations, the increased pulpal CGRP levels detected in painful cases in our study may reflect converging biological mechanisms involving apical irritation, mechanical loading, microbial stimuli, and inflammatory cascades acting simultaneously.

Beyond local triggers, systemic inflammation and oxidative stress are known to influence tissue homeostasis and nerve–tissue interactions. Within this broader biological context, pulpal tissue cannot be considered immunologically isolated; rather, it may respond dynamically to systemic inflammatory and neuroendocrine signals. Considering the well-established influence of psychological state on pain modulation, it is plausible that stress and anxiety are associated with elevated salivary SP levels; however, the present findings do not allow conclusions regarding causality or underlying biological mechanisms. The significantly higher depression, anxiety, and stress scores observed in painful individuals in our study support this interpretation and suggest an interaction between psychological burden and neuropeptide expression. The observed association between pulpal CGRP levels and depression scores may suggest a potential interaction between psychological factors and neuropeptide expression.

Previous studies have reported elevated SP levels in gingival crevicular fluid of painful teeth [22], supporting the notion that SP may disseminate into peripheral biological fluids during active nociceptive states. Although our investigation focused specifically on pulp and saliva, these collective findings strengthen the argument that SP functions as a stress-sensitive, systemically modulated biomarker of dental pain rather than a strictly localized inflammatory mediator.

Experimental animal models further clarify the mechanistic underpinnings of these findings. Okazaki et al. demonstrated that intense chemical irritation in rat pulp leads to odontoblast loss and increased neuropeptide expression [23]. In a separate study, Caviedes-Bucheli et al. reported that near-infrared diode laser application modulates SP and CGRP levels in experimental tooth movement and is associated with pain reduction [24]. Together, these studies illustrate that SP and CGRP are not passive byproducts of inflammation; instead, they represent dynamic neuromodulators responsive to both injury and therapeutic intervention.

One of the most clinically relevant observations in our study is the absence of a direct relationship between histopathologically pulpal inflammation scores and subjective pain intensity. Conversely, pain intensity showed a strong association with psychological status. However, due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, these associations cannot be interpreted in terms of directionality, and it remains unclear whether psychological distress influences pain perception or is a consequence of it. This dissociation supports prior evaluations emphasizing that endogenous opioid mechanisms may partially mask inflammatory severity and that pain perception cannot be reduced to histological parameters alone [24]. While inflammatory cell infiltration reflects structural tissue response, the subjective experience of pain appears to be governed more prominently by neurochemical modulation, psychological stress, and central sensitization processes.

The observed correlation between pulpal CGRP levels and stress–depression scores may be consistent with the biopsychosocial “mood–pain cycle” described in the literature; however, this relationship should be interpreted cautiously and does not imply a causal interaction. Within this framework, increasing pain intensifies psychological distress, and escalating psychological distress further enhances neuropeptide-mediated nociceptive signaling. This bidirectional amplification loop highlights that dental pain management should extend beyond somatic intervention to include psychological evaluation and supportive strategies.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that dental pain emerges from the interaction of biological (CGRP/SP), psychological (depression–anxiety–stress), and histological factors within an integrated neuroimmune–psychological network. The findings of this study should be interpreted within a multidimensional framework, in which pain perception, tissue-level inflammation, and psychological factors contribute through different but interacting mechanisms. Therefore, biomarker differences observed in this study cannot be attributed to a single explanatory pathway. While CGRP primarily reflects localized pulpal neurogenic activation, salivary SP appears to function as a non-invasive indicator of systemic stress-modulated pain burden. These results support the use of a biopsychosocial framework in the assessment and management of dental pain. In addition, confidence intervals were not systematically reported, which may limit the precision of effect estimates.

Future studies should validate these findings in larger longitudinal cohorts using standardized sampling and analytical protocols. Evaluating SP and CGRP together with additional inflammatory biomarkers and monitoring their changes before and after endodontic treatment may further improve their diagnostic and prognostic value. Ultimately, integrating molecular biomarkers with conventional clinical assessment may contribute to the development of reliable chairside diagnostic tools for endodontic practice [3, 16, 18].

Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes causal interpretation of the relationships among pain perception, neuropeptide expression, and psychological parameters. Although statistically powered, the sample size and inter-individual variability in neuropeptide expression may limit broader generalizability. The exclusive evaluation of SP and CGRP restricts insight into the wider network of pain-related neuromediators, including cytokines, endogenous opioids, and central sensitization markers. Additionally, salivary SP levels are known to be influenced by stress reactivity and circadian rhythms, factors that may not have been entirely controlled despite standardized sampling procedures. Histopathological assessment reflects structural pulpal inflammation but does not capture peripheral neural sensitization or central pain processing mechanisms, which are integral components of the pain experience.

Although all participants were recruited from the same clinical setting and evaluated using the same clinical and radiographic protocol, detailed group-wise matching for variables such as tooth type, pulpal diagnosis, and clinical status was not available. Therefore, the potential influence of underlying pulpal pathology or other clinical differences between groups cannot be completely excluded.

In addition, although a priori sample size calculation was performed based on the primary outcomes, the relatively small sample size may limit the stability of findings derived from multiple comparisons, correlation analyses, and structural equation modeling. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger cohorts. Furthermore, no correction for multiple testing was applied, which may increase the risk of type I error given the number of comparisons performed.

The lack of consistent correlations between pulpal and salivary neuropeptide levels also limits the interpretation of saliva as a surrogate medium for pulpal neuropeptide activity. Another limitation is the absence of a true healthy control group consisting of sound teeth extracted for orthodontic reasons. Therefore, physiological baseline levels of pulpal SP and CGRP could not be established directly, and interpretation of the findings relied on comparisons with previously published healthy reference data [2, 3, 5].

Despite these precautions, it was not possible to control all potential confounding factors affecting salivary neuropeptide levels, such as subtle variations in oral hygiene, subclinical periodontal conditions, and individual biological variability. Therefore, the influence of these factors cannot be completely excluded.

In addition, sex-related differences and body mass index (BMI), which may influence pain perception, were not specifically analyzed in this study. Future studies should consider these variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of pain mechanisms.

Conclusions

Dental pain should be conceptualized as a multidimensional biopsychosocial phenomenon rather than a purely peripheral inflammatory response. Elevated pulpal CGRP levels appear to reflect localized neurogenic activation within symptomatic pulp tissue, whereas increased salivary substance P levels may reflect pain-associated responses influenced by broader systemic factors and cumulative pain burden. The limited correspondence between histopathologically severity and subjective pain intensity, together with the significant influence of psychological factors, underscores the necessity of integrating biological and psychological assessment in dental pain evaluation.

Pulpal CGRP may be associated with local neurogenic activation in symptomatic pulpal conditions, while salivary SP may represent a potential non-invasive candidate marker related to pain-associated responses. However, the absence of consistent correlations between pulpal and salivary neuropeptide levels indicates that salivary measurements should not be regarded as reliable substitutes for pulpal biomarker assessment. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies incorporating broader neuroimmune and central sensitization markers are warranted to further clarify causal pathways and validate the translational relevance of these biomarkers in clinical endodontic practice. Given the cross-sectional design and limited sample size, these findings should be interpreted as associations and do not establish diagnostic utility or causal mechanisms. Future research should also consider the potential influence of demographic and physiological variables, such as sex and body mass index, on pain perception and biomarker expression.
