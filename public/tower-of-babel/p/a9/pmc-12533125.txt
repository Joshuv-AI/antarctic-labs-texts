Effects of manure application on paddy soil phosphorus in China based on a meta-analysis
Authors: Shuangfeng Liu, Chaoyang Liang, Jintian Zhang, Siying Chen, Jian Cheng, Minghui Chang, Jinling Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLimited phosphorus (P) availability in paddy soil significantly constrains rice growth, which can be enhanced by manure amendments. Although the effects of manure on paddy soil P fractions and availability have been extensively studied, different studies have yielded inconsistent results. Therefore, we conducted a meta-analysis of 1,799 published experimental datasets from 58 references. The results demonstrated that manure application increased the total, available, and microbial biomass P (MBP) by 46%, 134%, and 78%, respectively. The enhancing effect of manure on different P fractions followed the order of labile-P (221%) > moderately labile-P (145%) > stable-P (53%). Manure also significantly enhanced the activity of soil phosphatase, particularly that of alkaline phosphatase, by improving soil properties and enhancing microbial activities. In terms of manure types, pig manure could more significantly enhance the soil P availability and labile-P. Soil available potassium (AK), soil total nitrogen (TN), and mean annual temperature (MAT) were significant factors influencing soil P availability. Compared with non-fertilization treatments, manure amendments also significantly increased TP loss. Under application rates of 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1, the runoff loss of TP (101%, 95% CI: 45 to 158%) was minimized. Overall, this study recommends applying pig manure at a rate of 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1 in southern China’s paddy soils to enhance soil P utilization efficiency and reduce P runoff loss. Introduction

Phosphorus (P), especially available phosphorus (AP), is essential for plant growth and crop yield. Agriculture is the primary driver of global P depletion, with over 80%–90% of global P reserves being consumed by agriculture by the 2010s1. Approximately 70% of croplands in Asia exhibit soil AP concentrations that fall below the threshold for optimal rice production, with an even greater severity observed in China2. This condition necessitates the substantial application of P fertilizers3. Excessive application of chemical P fertilizer reacts with metal ions such as calcium (Ca), aluminum (Al) and iron (Fe) to form insoluble Ca-phosphate (Ca3(PO4)2), Al-phosphate (AlPO4) and other compounds. This reaction significantly reduces the concentration of AP in soil solution, thereby limiting P uptake by plants and subsequently triggers long-term effects on soil fertility4. Excessive application of chemical P fertilizers may also disrupt soil structure5 and accelerate P runoff, thereby triggering water body eutrophication. In addition, it can cause soil heavy metal contamination and inhibit the activity of beneficial P-mobilizing microorganisms6. Therefore, there exists an urgent need to develop strategies aimed at enhancing P accessibility for plants, which would reduce reliance on chemical fertilizers and mitigate their environmental impacts. One promising approach is to utilize manure as a P fertilizer, which has been shown to be more effective in improving soil fertility than chemical fertilizers7. To effectively utilize P and sustainably manage manure in paddy soil, a better understanding of P behavior under manure application and its relationships with potential regulatory factors is necessary.

Soil P availability is mainly evaluated by P fractions8. Soil P is present in both inorganic (Pi) and organic (Po) fractions, where Pi constitutes 20%–60% of soil total phosphorus (TP), with less than 1% being found in the soil solution9, and Po can be converted into Pi accessible to plants by microbial activity10. Pi and Po can be characterized as either labile-P, moderately labile-P, or stable-P in terms of availability11. This classification system was modified from Hedley et al.12 by Tiessen and Moir13. Hedley et al.12 classified soil P into Resin-P, NaHCO3-P, CHCl3/NaHCO3-P, NaOH-P, Sonicate/NaOH-P, HCl-P, and Residual-P. The availability of different P fractions varies, with labile-P being more readily converted into AP for direct uptake by plants5. AP comprises solution P and various phosphates that can be released into soil solution, playing crucial roles in crop growth and resource management14. Bioavailable P in solution, reactive P on soil particle surfaces, and strongly immobilized inactive P can transform into one another, forming a dynamic equilibrium15. The transformation of these P fractions can be influenced by many factors such as soil properties, microbial activity, or climatic conditions. For example, complex microbial activities may influence P availability by altering processes such as P solubilization and transformation16.

Extensive research has elucidated the pivotal role of manure application in enhancing soil P availability through dual mechanisms: direct supplementation of AP to soils and transformation of P fractions17,18. While proven effective, this practice’s outcomes differ significantly across experiments, influenced by manure properties, soil properties, and climate conditions. Experimental evidence from different studies demonstrates different responses of P fractions to manure types. Comparative analyses quantify this differentiation, revealing 37.4% and 58.5% elevations in labile-P concentrations following pig and cattle manure applications, respectively19. Neutral soils exhibit greater increase of labile-P with minimal stable-P alteration, whereas acidic systems show negligible response to equivalent manure inputs. This suggests that soil properties such as soil pH exert regulatory effects on P dynamics. This pH-dependent behavior interacts jointly with climate drivers. Regional comparative studies in Jiangxi and Sichuan provinces quantify climatic regulation on P dynamics. Equivalent pig manure applications induced 27.3% and 15.8% AP enhancement in these regions respectively, demonstrating precipitation-driven variability20,21. These differences across areas highlight the necessity for targeted management strategies that integrate soil and climate factors with application patterns.

Paddy soil is a significant soil type in China22predominantly distributed in the northeastern and southern regions. Periodic artificial irrigation and drainage lead to intense soil-water interface processes in paddy soil systems, resulting in significant alterations to the soil environment compared to natural soils. These changes greatly influence the migration and transformation of P, altering its fractions and consequently affecting P availability23. Currently, the seasonal P utilization rate in rice cultivation remains restricted to 10–20% in China14. Most current studies evaluate the effects of manure on P in paddy soils under controlled conditions, lacking comprehensive analysis of cross-regional comparisons and multifactor interactions, and there is still no consensus. Therefore, there is a need for comprehensive quantitative studies into the mechanisms by which manure application affects soil P availability and the relevant driving factors involved in China’s paddy soils.

In this study, we assessed the impact of manure and various factors on soil P fractions through a meta-analysis and random forest model while considering regional disparities. The findings are expected to provide some important guidance for agricultural manure management. Specifically, this study aimed to (1) quantify the effects of manure application on soil P fractions in China’s paddy soils; (2) identify the key drivers and their effects on soil P fractions; (3) elucidate the mechanisms through which manure influences P fractions; and (4) determine the optimal manure application strategies.

Results and discussion

Response of soil phosphorus and enzyme activity to manure application

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the meta-analysis results revealed significant increases in soil TP (46%) and AP (134%) after manure application. PAC exhibited a significant increase of 58%. Manure brings a large amount of nutrients into the soil, especially OM, which is crucial for enhancing P availability for several reasons: (1) Po in manure can be mineralized to Pi24. (2) OM can affect phosphate adsorption by competing for adsorption sites and altering the surface charge of adsorbent25. (3) Organic acids can solubilize insoluble soil P26. (4) Organic acids can chelate or complex with multivalent cations (Fe, Al, Ca), forming stable metal-organic complexes that remove P adsorption sites on metal oxides, thereby releasing strongly-bound P27. Additionally, Po in manure typically exists in the forms of phosphomonoesters and diesters of inositol, phospholipids, and nucleic acids. Compared with mineral P, it is more readily decomposed by microbial activity into bioavailable P28. In contrast, the solubility of mineral P is significantly influenced by soil pH and mineral composition, which often results in its presence in insoluble forms4thereby reducing leaching and hindering plant uptake. Notably, labile-P showed a significant increase of 221%, followed by a moderate increase in moderately labile-P (145%), and a slight increase in stable-P (54%) (Fig. 1b). Labile-P is the main P component in manure, which can directly release AP15. Moderately labile-P can be converted to labile-P by microorganisms19.

MBP increased by 78% under manure application (Fig. 1a). The previous meta-analysis results also showed that manure application improved the abundance of bacteria and fungi by 60.0% and 27.7%, respectively, as well as increased soil bacterial diversity29. The abundant carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) sources present in manure provide ample nutrients for microorganisms, significantly increase microbial biomass, particularly that of P-solubilizing microorganisms30. These microorganisms can secrete organic acids to dissolve insoluble phosphates. The input of a substantial amount of easily degradable organic C from manure increases microbial biomass C, resulting in the rapid proliferation of P-solubilizing bacteria, which upregulate the copy number of the phoD gene to secrete more alkaline phosphatase31. The effect of manure application on enzyme activities is illustrated in Fig. 1c. Manure application significantly increased the activities of phosphatase (97%), urease (22%), sucrase (22%), and dehydrogenase (109%). Notably, the activities of alkaline phosphatase and acid phosphatase exhibited remarkable increases by 114% and 91%, respectively. Pig manure is generally weakly alkaline, and its application in acidic paddy soils in the southern region leads to an increase in pH. The increase in pH directly reduces the activity of Al3+ and Fe3+, minimizing their toxicity to microbial cell membranes and thereby providing a more favorable environment for the survival of microorganisms and enzyme proteins32. These extracellular enzymes specifically act on particular organophosphorus compounds to release soluble P33. Qaswar et al.21 suggested that alkaline phosphatase catalyzes the hydrolysis of phosphate bonds, thereby facilitating the acquisition of AP by plants and microorganisms.

Factors affecting available phosphorus under manure application

The application of pig manure substantially increased the AP in paddy soil by 211% (95% CI: 194 to 227%) (Fig. 2a), which was much more significant than the effect of other manure treatments. The composition of manure shows considerable differences due to differences in animal nutrition and mineral supplementation34. Pigs have sufficient daily food to meet their nutritional needs. The C/P ratio of pig manure is approximately 15–20, with a moderate decomposition rate and steady P release35. In contrast, cattle manure has a higher C/P ratio and slower decomposition rate, while chicken manure has a lower C/P ratio and insufficient energy, both of which perform less effectively than pig manure (Fig. S7a). Additionally, pig manure exhibits the lowest TN and TK levels among the three types of manure (Fig. S7b and c), which can mitigate soil acidification caused by excessive N36 and reduce competition for K and P37, thereby decreasing P fixation. Its OM content (200–300 g kg-1) is at a moderate level, which can ensure microbial activity while avoid acidification33 (Fig. S7d). The pig gut also secretes enzymes that facilitate the mineralization of Po into Pi and increase soil P availability. Application of poultry manure may result in higher ammonium nitrogen (NH4+-N) concentrations in the soil, leading to microbial cytotoxicity and inhibition of enzyme activities38, thereby inhibiting the utilization of P.

Although pig manure can significantly enhance P availability, it is important to note that it may contain some non-essential heavy metals and antibiotics39. Therefore, it is crucial for controlling the application rate of pig manure in practical production. Manure application exhibited a positive effect on soil AP regardless of the applied quantity. The optimal results were observed with the addition amount lower than 10 t ha⁻¹ yr-1, which could contribute to the highest increase in AP by 498% (Fig. 2a). Further categorization of manure application at < 10 t ha⁻¹ yr-1 demonstrated markedly significant enhancement of AP within the range of 2–6 t ha⁻¹ yr-1 (1,207%) (Fig. S2). Further analysis of pig manure application rates based on soil types indicated that in the red soil-derived paddy soil (typical of low hills south of the Yangtze River, particularly in Jiangxi and Hunan), the clay minerals are primarily kaolinite, with a high clay content and significant acidity40. The application of pig manure at 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1 resulted in a significantly higher increase in AP compared with other application rates. In hydromorphic paddy soil (plains of the middle and lower Yangtze River), which has a coarser texture and higher porosity41, there was no significant difference in AP enhancement between the 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1 and ≥ 6 t ha− 1 yr− 1 treatments. From a resource conservation perspective, the application rate of 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1 is still recommended.

Paddy soils are characterized by alternating redox reactions, which creates a special environment for nutrient transformation. Decreased soil redox potential drives the reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+, enhancing the solubility of Fe2+-associated phosphates42, and the reduction of Fe colloidal film encapsulated on the surface of phosphoric acid increases the availability of occluded P. These processes also trigger a counteracting mechanism: it can transform crystalline Fe/Al oxides into amorphous forms with larger reactive surface areas, thus enhancing P fixation43. The application of manure can influence the paddy soil environment to some extent. The pH analysis of the manure samples showed that most were neutral (6.5–7.5) or alkaline (pH ≥ 7.5). The manure samples with pH ≥ 7.5 resulted in more significant enhancement of AP (144%) than those with pH 6.5–7.5 (60%) (Fig. 2a). For acidic paddy soils in southern China, alkaline manure can increase soil pH, reducing the binding of P with Fe3+ and Al3+, and decreasing the formation of insoluble compounds4. This alkaline manure is typically rich in cations such as Ca2+ and Mg2+, and under flooded conditions, Ca2+ may form soluble Ca-P complexes instead of insoluble Ca-phosphate, thereby maintaining P availability. Although alkaline manure can increase pH of acidic paddy soil to some extent, high concentrations of ammonium from pig manure may lead to soil acidification, so it is recommended to apply it in conjunction with acidic ameliorants such as lime.

When the soil clay content is lower than 20%, the application of manure has a better effect to enhance the availability of P in the soil (Fig. 2b). Soils with clay contents < 20% have a low density of P adsorption sites, allowing the P provided by manure to be more readily retained in solution or exchangeable forms, thus reducing its irreversible fixation by Fe/Al oxides. These soils have good aeration, creating an optimal environment that enhances microbial activity, thus significantly enhancing P availability44. Soils with initial TN < 1 g kg⁻¹ (165%) and TP concentrations of 0.5–1.0 g kg⁻¹ (170%) exhibited the most substantial increase in AP after manure application. This is consistent with the above results, where the lower TN concentrations of manure also had significant increase in AP. For the initial AP content in the soil, a lower level of < 10 g kg− 1 showed the most significant improving effect, while soils with high P contents demonstrate a weaker response. After the application of manure, due to the concentration effect, soils with lower AP contents receive relatively higher additional P. Furthermore, the addition of organic matter enhances microbial activities, which also promotes the release of AP19. In soil with a pH range of 6.5–7.5, manure application resulted in significantly higher AP by 182% (95% CI: 155%–210%) compared with that under other soil pH conditions (Fig. 2b). This may be because phosphate reacts with metal ions to form insoluble phosphate compounds in acidic soils or in alkaline soils, which reduces the solubility and availability of P4. The AP content exhibited a significant increase across all experimental periods. The results for periods exceeding twenty years showed a markedly elevated level of P availability (620%) (Fig. 2c) compared with those observed over shorter experimental durations. This is because the slow-release nature of manures, which have a lower release rate and longer retention in soil compared with chemical fertilizers, requiring more time for mineralization and exchange with mineral-bound P45. Therefore, it is necessary to adopt long-term fertilization practices. The increase in AP was particularly pronounced under conditions of 10 °C ≤ MAT < 20 °C and MAP exceeding 800 mm. This can be attributed to increased carbon availability and stimulated microbial activity under warmer, wetter conditions46.

Factors affecting soil phosphorus fractions under manure application

Following the application of manure, the 95% CI for stable-P under various manure and soil properties demonstrated that the value was nearly all overlapped with zero, suggesting that manure exerted a comparatively limited impact on stable-P (Fig. 3c). The application of pig manure led to a significant increase in the concentrations of all P fractions, which followed an order of labile-P (254%) > moderately labile-P (182%) > stable-P (62%) (Fig. 3). However, the application of cattle and chicken manure did not yield significant effects on various P fractions. The reason is consistent with the effect of pig manure on AP. Previous studies have demonstrated that co-application of chemical and manure is the most effective strategy to improve soil P availability7,18but our meta-analysis results indicated that chemical fertilizer may limit the effect of manure (Fig. 3a). The application of manure alone increased the labile-P and moderately labile-P by 321% and 225%, while the combined application of chemical fertilizer and manure enhanced only by 71% and 44%, respectively. This could be due to the fact that chemical fertilizer can inhibit the enhancement of AP content through mechanisms such as changing soil properties and affecting microbial abundance. For example, excessive N inputs may reduce soil pH and increase the content of insoluble Fe- and Al-phosphates, leading to a reduction in AP47. Huang et al.48 reported that the application of chemical fertilizers can inhibit bacterial growth and reproduction and reduce soil bacterial abundance, which in turn negatively affects the transformation of P fractions.In soils with a clay content greater than 40%, the application of manure has a significantly greater effect on the enhancement of labile-P and moderately labile-P compared with its effect in soils with a clay content of 20%–40%. Under manure application, labile-P (263%) and moderately labile-P (283%) showed significantly greater increases under neutral soil pH conditions than under other pH conditions (Fig. 3a and b), which was consistent with the findings related to AP.

Factors affecting phosphatase activity under manure application

The increase in phosphatase activity was significantly greater in the presence of pig manure (125%) when compared with that of earthworm (47%), cattle (14%), and chicken manure (29%) (Fig. 4a). Phosphatase activity was elevated upon application of manure with TK < 15 g kg⁻¹ (82%). Direct application of manure resulted in a significantly greater enzyme activity response (121%) than manure application combined with chemical fertilizer (41%). The increase in enzyme activity was notably more pronounced under neutral soil pH conditions (120%) (Fig. 4b). These results are consistent with the findings on the impact of pig manure on AP and labile-P, indicating that manure may enhance P availability by increasing phosphatase activity. The field experiments indicated that MAT of 10–20 °C and MAP ≥ 800 mm were more favorable for enzyme activity (Fig. 4c). While thermal and hydrologic conditions initially promote enzymatic P mobilization, excessive temperatures can reduce microbial and enzymatic activity49 and increase the adsorption of P to soil particles. PLS-PM shows that MAT and MAP directly enhance phosphatase, accelerating organic-P mineralization and thereby increasing AP. These climatic variables also act indirectly via soil OM (0.81), while AK exerts a strong negative effect (–0.65). Manure OM and TN do not significantly influence phosphatase; instead, high TN directly suppresses AP (–0.42) by intensifying P sorption or microbial immobilization. Thus, warm-wet conditions and high OM soils synergistically boost enzymatic P release, whereas elevated AK or TN levels constrain AP regardless of enzyme activities. Integrating these insights, we speculate that the pathway from enzyme stimulation to P availability is more governed by region-specific climate and soil chemistry, and the intrinsic composition of the manure itself exerts a comparatively minor influence.

Key factors influencing soil available phosphorus and phosphatase activity

Fig. 5a shows that soil AK, MAT, soil AP, MAP, soil TN, manure pH, and manure TN are the significant influencing factors on soil AP, suggesting that soil properties, manure properties, and experimental conditions have significant influence on P availability. A correlation analysis was conducted on the quantitative data of four key influencing factors, including soil AK, AP, MAT, and soil TN (p < 0.01). The results demonstrated that soil AP content had a negative correlation with initial soil AK, AP and TN while a positive correlation with MAT (Fig. S3), which was consistent with the results of the meta-analysis. As illustrated in Fig. S3c, the relative increase in AP following pig-manure application declines significantly as the initial AP level rises. This may be because high-P soils suppress microbial and phosphatase activity via feedback inhibition6thereby attenuating organic-P mineralization. Additionally, plant uptake saturates at > 25 mg kg− 1 AP50. Therefore, additional P contributes less to crop and is accumulated in stable fractions or lost through runoff. Soil AK was the most significant factor affecting soil AP content among the selected factors (Fig. 5a). It is likely that soil AK can influence microbial activity, alter the release rate of organic acids in manure, and then affect the release of P51. K exists in the soil as exchangeable cations and can induce soil acidification that lower pH, which promotes chemical fixation of P by Fe/Al oxides52. Manure pH is mostly alkaline, which can neutralize the acidity of paddy soils in southern China and reduce the formation of insoluble minerals such as Fe phosphate and Al phosphate53. P-solubilizing microorganisms exhibit a competitive advantage in low-N environments, mineralizing organic P and releasing AP. Additionally, low N levels reduce nitrification, thereby decreasing the acidification effects of NO3− on the soil6. Experimental duration, manure type, soil TN, MAT, MAP, manure TN, and manure TP, which can primarily represent the characteristics of manure and the experimental conditions, were identified as the most influential factors on phosphatase activity (Fig. 5b). Correlation analysis of the quantitative data for the above indicators revealed that soil TN, MAT, and MAP (p < 0.01) had positive correlations with soil phosphatase activity (Fig. S4). The increase in soil TN can enhance P fixation, while also drive microorganisms to secrete phosphatases in response to P limitation47. Manure application introduces a N source into the soil, and N is a key element for the growth and reproduction of microorganisms. The increased N supply may stimulate microbial metabolic activity, thereby enhancing the synthesis and activity of phosphatase54. However, excessively high concentrations of TP in manure may lead to an increase in AP content, which could inhibit the synthesis of phosphatase31. There exists a certain interaction between TN and TP, and an improper N-to-P ratio may affect the growth of soil microorganisms and the activity of phosphatase.

Phosphorus loss and influencing factors

Subgroup analysis in Fig. 6a shows that TP runoff loss is the lowest under 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1 pig manure, whereas soluble P does not differ significantly between this rate range and ≥ 6 t ha− 1 yr− 1. Hence, 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1 represents the optimal threshold for both emission reduction and nutrient conservation. This outcome arises from three convergent mechanisms. (i) The P load introduced by this rate is only 60%–80% of that from conventional fertilizers55, reducing the runoff P pool at the source; (ii) soil P saturation remains low, keeping particulate and dissolved P losses in the low-risk zone56; and (iii) increased soil OM enhances aggregate mean weight diameter, markedly reducing sediment-bound runoff57. Collectively, 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1 pig manure can better balance high crop demand and low environmental risk, minimizing runoff P loss without sacrificing soil P availability. The loss of TP was significantly influenced by the concentrations of TN, TP, soil OM, and MAP (Fig. 6b). Correlation analysis of the quantitative data for the above indicators revealed that TP loss had a negative correlation with soil TN, TP and OM while a positive correlation with MAP (Fig. S5). Greater precipitation can enhance TP loss through increased runoff and soil erosion. High N fertilization may boost plant growth, increase ground cover, and reduce runoff erosion58. Humic substances in OM act as key cementing agents for soil aggregates, improving soil stability and reducing particulate P loss through erosion and runoff59.

Research implications and limitations

The results of this study suggested that manure affected soil P mainly through chemical and biological mechanisms. From a chemical perspective, manure affected various processes such as the dissolution and adsorption of Pi; from a biological perspective, manure can enhance the mineralization of Po by directly supplying mineralizing enzymes and boosting microbial biomass and diversity, which can increase the activities of enzymes such as phosphatase. Combining the results of meta-analysis and random forest model, it can be speculated that manure was more suitable for application in paddy soil in southern China. It is recommended to regulate the application rate to 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1, as this rate is the optimal choice for maximizing P utilization efficiency under manure application. Based on our results, manure can exacerbate P loss in paddy soils. Therefore, we need to improve P utilization while controlling P loss. We should determine the soil properties before planting rice. If AP < 10 mg kg− 1, TN < 2 g kg− 1, AK < 100 mg kg− 1, or initial pH < 6.5, applying 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1 pig manure is most effective. Manure application rates can be adjusted appropriately under other soil conditions. In regions where MAT < 10 ℃ or precipitation < 800 mm, the same total amount is maintained but split into 70% basal plus 30% mid-tillering topdressing to align with slower mineralization. In the southern regions of China, where rainfall is abundant, P runoff loss is more likely to occur. During the rainy season, the application of manure should be delayed until after the rain, and the amount of manure applied should be appropriately reduced to decrease P runoff loss.

This study elucidated the effect of manure on the P fractions in soil and identified the key influencing factors. However, studies of manure application exhibit considerable variability, leading to fragmented data distribution, which hinders the subgroup analysis of several properties. The included literature lacked relevant data on paddy cultivation history (years of rice cropping), heavy metal content in manure (Cd, Cu, Zn), antibiotics content in manure (oxytetracycline and chlortetracycline), manure P fractions (labile-P and stable-P), organic acids (citric acid and oxalic acid), and solubility. The absence of data on these variables prevented further analysis of their regulatory effects through subgroup analysis or meta-regression in this study. Additionally, due to the limited data available in the literature on soil Fe, Al, Ca and CEC, these factors were not incorporated into the random forest model. We recommend that future experiments should comprehensively record and analyze various factors to further explore how regional disparities affect soil P availability and to develop more refined manure application strategies.

Conclusion

This study employed meta-analysis to evaluate the impact of manure application on AP, P fractions, and phosphatase activity in paddy soils. The results indicated that manure application can significantly increase the TP, AP content and soil phosphatase activity in the soil. Manure application significantly enhanced the labile-P and moderately labile-P fraction, whereas showed a less significant effect on stable-P. Among different types of manure, pig manure contributed to the most substantial improvement of soil P availability. Furthermore, the random forest model and correlation analysis revealed that the AP content was negatively correlated with the AK and TN levels of the soil, while positively correlated with MAT. Among various climatic conditions, MAP was also an important indicator and its increase tended to enhance AP. Therefore, manure is particularly beneficial to be utilized in southern paddy soil of China. An application rate of 2–6 t ha− 1 yr− 1 is recommended for long-term application. These findings are important for assessing the sustainable supply and bioavailability of soil P under manure application and for elucidating the mechanisms of soil P transformation.

Materials and methods

Data collection

A comprehensive search was performed utilizing the following retrieval formula: TS = (manure* OR organic fertili* OR fertili*) AND TS = (phosphorus OR phosphorus availability OR phosphorus fraction* OR phosphorus form* OR available phosphorus OR Olsen-P) AND TS = (paddy) to identify all articles published prior to January 1, 2024 in the Web of Science (http://apps.webofknowledge.com/) and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (https://www.cnki.net/) databases. Subsequently, the literature was subjected to a screening process in which the titles and abstracts were reviewed to ensure the accuracy and general applicability of the selected articles. A total of 238 articles were chosen for further screening to enhance the relevance and comparability of the data. The screening criteria are as follows: (1) Duplicate entries were eliminated; (2) Experiments conducted on paddy soils within China were included; (3) Both experimental (application of manure or combination of chemical fertilizer and manure) and control (no application of manure or chemical fertilizer) groups were included, with each sample containing at least three replicates; (4) The literature needed to include at least one of the following indicators: experimental type, experimental duration, manure type, soil texture, soil pH, soil organic matter (OM), total nitrogen (TN), TP, total potassium (TK), manure pH, experimental site, mean annual temperature (MAT), mean annual precipitation (MAP), and other relevant indicators; (5) For articles with multiple experiments, each experiment would be treated as a separate study within the dataset; (6) The experimental data must be presented as specific data points or graphs and should include both the mean and standard deviation; (7) Soil P fractions were measured by using sequential fractionation techniques developed by Hedley et al.12 and modified by Tiessen and Moir13 and Jiang and Gu60. The soil P fractions were assigned into three P fractions according to its stability, including labile-P (Resin-P + NaHCO3-Po + NaHCO3-Pi), moderately labile-P (NaOH-Po + NaOH-Pi), and stable-P (Residual-P + HCl-P). The determining methods for P fractions are described in Text S1. Ultimately, a total of 58 articles investigating the effects of manure application on paddy soils were included in the analysis, yielding 1,799 data sets (Fig. S1 and Table S1). Influencing factor analysis

The random forest model can predict the outcomes by integrating multiple decision trees and utilizing a voting or averaging mechanism to arrive at the final result, demonstrating a high degree of prediction accuracy63. Data were selected for the random forest model, and the ‘randomForest’ and ‘rfPermute’ packages in R (v.4.3.1) were utilized to analyze the relative importance of the factors. All data pertaining to AP, phosphatase activity and P loss, along with the associated experimental conditions, were collated. The random forest model used 1,000 decision trees (ntree = 1,000). Each tree was trained on about 80% of the data from bootstrap sampling, with the remaining 20% used for testing. Datasets with too many missing values were excluded, and remaining missing values were filled using the random forest’s median imputation method. Finally, the top fifteen datasets were selected for mapping, and each dataset’s importance was labeled to identify key factors.
