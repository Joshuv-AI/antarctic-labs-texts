Engineered PD1-NKG2D Dual-CAR NK92 cells broaden antitumor target recognition in preclinical tumor models
Authors: Xinru Jin, Mingfeng Li, Mengjun Wang, Jin Zhao, Zhiyuan Niu, Changjiang Guo, Lingtong Zhi, Norfarazieda Hassan, Wuling Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo address challenges such as the complex manufacturing of CAR-T and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), CAR-NK cells offer greater potential as an “off-the-shelf” therapy. To broaden tumor recognition and reduce the risk of immune escape associated with single-target approaches, we developed a single-promoter-driven multicistronic CAR-NK92 system that employs NKG2D for broad recognition of stress ligands and combines a PD1-CAR to reverse PD-L1 inhibitory signaling, thereby significantly enhancing antitumor efficacy.MethodsA multicistronic construct co-expressing PD1-CAR and NKG2D-CAR was generated using a P2A peptide under the control of a single CMV promoter and introduced into NK92 cells. Background

According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), the global cancer burden is projected to rise by 77.6% by 2050, highlighting an urgent need for innovative and effective therapeutic modalities [1]. While conventional interventions such as surgery, radiotherapy, and targeted therapies have extended patient survival, achieving curative outcomes remains challenging due to systemic limitations and drug resistance [2, 3]. In the past decade, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has revolutionized the treatment of hematological malignancies [4, 5]. However, its clinical scalability is severely constrained by the complexities and high costs associated with personalized, autologous manufacturing [6–8]. Moreover, the robust activation of CAR-T cells often triggers life-threatening toxicities, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), primarily driven by the systemic surge of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IFN-γ [9–11]. These safety and logistical hurdles necessitate the development of more accessible, “off-the-shelf” cellular immunotherapies.

CAR-engineered natural killer (NK) cells have emerged as a promising alternative, particularly for solid tumors, due to their favorable safety profile, allogeneic applicability, and compatibility with “off-the-shelf” therapeutic strategies [12–16]. Unlike T cells, NK cell activity is independent of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC), which significantly mitigates the risk of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) and allows for immediate use from healthy donors [17–20]. Despite these advantages, the efficacy of single-target CAR-NK cells against solid tumors is often undermined by the intrinsic heterogeneity of the tumor landscape and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which remain major barriers to durable therapeutic responses [21, 22]. Single-antigen targeting frequently leads to “antigen blind spots,” where tumor subclones lacking the primary marker evade immune detection, ultimately resulting in therapeutic resistance and disease relapse [9, 23, 24]. Thus, engineering NK cells to simultaneously recognize multiple tumor-associated antigens has become a pivotal strategy for enhancing clinical robustness.

To achieve an effective dual-targeting strategy, the selection of recognition modules must be strategic. NKG2D is a cornerstone activating receptor in NK cells, known for its ability to recognize a broad spectrum of stress-induced ligands expressed across diverse malignancies, thereby providing an attractive strategy for broad-spectrum tumor targeting in solid tumors [25–28]. This property makes the NKG2D signaling pathway an ideal candidate for broad-spectrum tumor sensing [29, 30]. However, potent recognition alone is insufficient in the face of checkpoint-mediated suppression. PD-1/PD-L1 axis is a dominant mechanism of tumor immune escape, where PD-L1 expression on tumor cells “brakes” the effector functions of infiltrating immune cells [31–34]. In the context of synthetic biology, converting this inhibitory signal into a stimulatory one—rather than merely blocking it—provides a sophisticated solution to maintain effector fitness within the hostile TME [35, 36].

To counteract immune evasion in solid tumors, we developed the PN-CAR-NK92 system, which leverages a dual-targeting strategy to achieve synergistic broad-spectrum recognition and immunosuppressive reversal. Using P2A-mediated co-expression, we integrated an NKG2D sensor with a PD-1 signal-reversal receptor, ensuring uniform receptor distribution on effector cells. This design not only eliminates the “antigen blind spots” typical of single-target CARs but also reprograms inhibitory PD-L1 signals into activating impulses, significantly enhancing NK92 cell fitness within suppressive microenvironments. Validation in the H1299 model confirmed the superior persistence and efficacy of PN-CAR-NK92, providing a promising translational approach for treating complex solid tumors.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell cultures

The human NK92 cell line and HEK-293T cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). Human tumor cell lines MGC-803, U2OS, A549, H1299, and KATO-III, as well as the murine mammary carcinoma cell line EMT-6, were purchased from Shanghai Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). NK92 cells were cultured in α-MEM medium (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 12.5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone), 12.5% horse serum (Solarbio, Beijing, China), 2 mmol/L L-glutamine, 100 U/mL recombinant human IL-2 (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). HEK-293T, MGC-803, U2OS, and A549 cells were maintained in DMEM (HyClone), while EMT-6, H1299, and KATO-III cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (HyClone). All tumor cell culture media were supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

CAR construct design

The CAR expression vectors used in this study were designed based on lentiviral backbones. The NKG2D-CAR construct consisted of the NKG2D extracellular domain, an IgG4 hinge region, a CD28 transmembrane domain, and intracellular signaling domains derived from 4-1BB and CD3ζ. The PD1-CAR construct contained the extracellular domain of PD-1 fused to the intracellular signaling domains of DAP10 and 4-1BB. All CAR constructs were synthesized and cloned into the lentiviral vector backbone derived from pCDH-CMV-MCS-P2A-copGFP-T2A-Puro (Addgene) by GenScript Biotech Corporation (Nanjing, China). During cloning, the copGFP expression cassette was replaced by CAR expression cassettes. As a result, no GFP is expressed in the final constructs. To achieve coordinated expression of the two receptors, the PD1-CAR and NKG2D-CAR sequences were linked via a self-cleaving P2A peptide. In the PN configuration, PD1-CAR was positioned upstream of NKG2D-CAR (Fig. 1G), whereas in the NP configuration, the order of the two CAR modules was reversed, with NKG2D-CAR positioned upstream of PD1-CAR (Fig. 1H).

Lentiviral production and transduction of NK92

Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK-293T cells using a third-generation packaging system. Briefly, cells were co-transfected with the CAR expression plasmid together with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h after transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, and concentrated by ultracentrifugation. NK92 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were transduced with concentrated lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20 in the presence of polybrene (1.5 µL). Viral titers were determined using a qPCR-based titration kit (Applied Biological Materials Inc., Richmond, BC, Canada). After 12 h, the medium was replaced, and cells were cultured for an additional 72 h. CAR expression in transiently transfected HEK-293T cells and stably transduced NK92 cells was subsequently confirmed by flow cytometry.

Quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR)

To evaluate CAR expression in engineered NK92 cells, the expression levels of NKG2D and PD-1 were analyzed in parental NK92, PD-1-CAR-NK92, NKG2D-CAR-NK92, and PN-CAR-NK92 cells at 72 h post-infection and after cell purification. Single-target CAR-NK92 cells were included as reference controls to verify the specificity of PD-1 and NKG2D transcript expression derived from the corresponding CAR constructs. Total RNA was extracted from cells using the UNIQ-10 Total RNA Extraction Kit (Beyotime Biotech Inc., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using an RNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biological Materials Inc., Canada). QPCR was subsequently performed using 5× All-in-One qPCR Master Mix (Applied Biological Materials Inc., Canada). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal control for normalization of gene expression levels.

Flow cytometry analysis of car expression and cell phenotype

Flow cytometry was performed to evaluate CAR expression in transiently transfected HEK-293T cells and transduced NK92 cells, characterize the phenotype of CAR-NK92 cells, and determine ligand expression on the surface of tumor cells. Briefly, cells (1 × 10⁶) were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% FBS and resuspended in 100 µL of FACS buffer to generate single-cell suspensions. Cells were incubated on ice for 30 min with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies, followed by two washes with FACS buffer before acquisition.

To assess CAR expression on both HEK-293T and NK92 cells, cells were stained with BV421-conjugated anti-human NKG2D antibody (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA; Cat. No. 320822) and APC-conjugated anti-human PD-1 antibody (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No. 17-9969-42). Unstained cells were used to establish baseline gating, whereas wild-type parental HEK-293T or NK92 cells stained with antibodies were used as negative staining controls. For phenotypic characterization of CAR-NK92 cells, cells were stained with BV421-conjugated anti-human CD56 antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 362552), APC-conjugated anti-human CD3 antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 317317), and APC-conjugated anti-human CD16 antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 302012). To evaluate ligand expression on tumor cells, APC-conjugated anti-human PD-L1 antibody (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. 17-5983-42) and PE-conjugated anti-human MICA/B antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 320908) were used.

Cell counting kit-8

Cell proliferation was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; MCE, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Parental NK92 cells and CAR-modified NK92 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in 100 µL of complete medium and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. At 24, 48, and 72 h after seeding, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation for 1–2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm (OD₄₅₀) was then measured using a microplate reader to evaluate cell viability and proliferation. Blank wells containing medium without cells were included to determine background signals. Each experimental condition was performed in triplicate.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic activity of CAR-NK92 cells against tumor cells was evaluated using a luciferase-based assay. MGC-803, KATO-III, EMT-6-PD-L1, U2OS, A549, and H1299 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and co-cultured with CAR-NK92 cells at effector-to-target (E: T) ratios of 1:1, 2.5:1, 5:1, and 10:1. Target cells alone, effector cells alone, and medium-only wells were included as controls.

To determine maximal target cell lysis, 10 µl of lysis buffer was added to positive control wells. After 3–5 h of co-culture, 30 µl of ONE-Glo™ Luciferase Assay Reagent (Promega, USA) was added to each well, and luminescence was measured using a microplate reader. Cytotoxicity was calculated using the following formula:

Cytotoxicity (%) = [(RLU min – RLU sample) / (RLU min – RLU max)] × 100%.

Cytokine release assay

Cytokine secretion during CAR-NK92 cell and tumor cell co-culture was quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). CAR-NK92 cells were co-cultured with tumor cells at an E: T ratio of 5:1 in 96-well plates with a total volume of 200 µL per well and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 3–5 h.

In vivo antitumor efficacy in the H1299 xenograft model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xinxiang Medical University. Female NCG mice (6–8 weeks old; NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdc^em26Cd52 Il2rg^em26Cd22/Gpt) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. To establish the xenograft tumor model, 5 × 10⁶ H1299 cells suspended in 100 µL PBS were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. When tumors reached approximately 65–100 mm³, mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 6 per group): PBS control, NK92, and PN-CAR-NK92. For treatment, 2 × 10⁷ effector cells (or an equal volume of PBS) were administered via tail vein injection every other day. To support NK92 cell persistence in vivo, recombinant human IL-2 (1000 U per mouse) was administered via intraperitoneal injection during the treatment period. Tumor growth was monitored throughout the experiment, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula:

V = (length × width²) / 2. Body weight was recorded to evaluate systemic toxicity. When tumor volume approached the predefined humane endpoint (~ 1500 mm³) or when animal welfare considerations required earlier intervention, mice were euthanized, and tumors were harvested, weighed, photographed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde.

Immunohistochemistry

Antibodies against CD56 were used for conventional Immunohistochemistry (IHC). Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde; the paraffin-embedded tissue Sect. (4 μm) were deparaffinized, dehydrated, and treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase. The images were acquired at 200× magnification using a ZEISS Axioskop2 plus advanced positive microscope.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for in vitro experiments and as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) for in vivo tumor growth curves, unless otherwise indicated. All experiments were performed at least in triplicate. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Tumor growth curves were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test. * indicates p < 0.05, ** indicates p < 0.01, *** indicates p < 0.001 and **** indicates p < 0.0001.

Results

Rational design of PN-CAR-NK92 cells

To overcome tumor immune escape and the immunosuppressive TME, a modular PN-CAR system was engineered by integrating a PD-1–based chimeric switch receptor with a reoriented NKG2D-CAR. The PD1-CAR component was designed to convert inhibitory PD-1 signaling into a functional activating signal. We retained the extracellular domain of PD-1 to ensure specific recognition of PD-L1 on tumor cells. Based on evidence that DAP10 mediates NKG2D surface stabilization and relays activating signals in NK cells [37, 38], its transmembrane and intracellular domains were incorporated into the PD-1-CAR scaffold. This configuration ensures that PD-L1 engagement triggers direct stimulatory signals within the engineered NK92 cells, effectively bypassing the natural inhibitory checkpoint. (Fig. 1A) To enhance tumor recognition, an optimized NKG2D-CAR was constructed. Because native NKG2D is a type II transmembrane protein, its structure was reoriented to a type I configuration to ensure compatibility with conventional CAR architectures. The functional ectodomain of NKG2D (amino acids 82–216) was fused to an IgG4 hinge region to improve ligand accessibility. The receptor was further linked to tandem 4-1BB and CD3ζ intracellular signaling domains to provide synergistic activation signals for NK92 cells (Fig. 1B). To evaluate the structural feasibility of the engineered receptors, three-dimensional protein structure modeling was performed. The predicted structures indicated that the PD1-CAR and NKG2D-CAR maintained spatial conformations similar to those of their native counterparts upon ligand engagement (Fig. 1C–F). Structural similarity was further supported by high TM-scores, indicating that the essential ligand-recognition scaffolds of the receptors were preserved. To further clarify the complete element arrangement, we provided a comprehensive schematic of the multicistronic PN and NP constructs (Fig. 1G-H). The design utilizes a single CMV promoter to drive one multicistronic transcript, which is subsequently translated into separate PD1-CAR, NKG2D-CAR, and puromycin resistance protein via P2A and T2A-mediated cleavage. Consequently, these results indicate that the constructed PN-CAR system can effectively convert the original inhibitory signaling pathways into activating ones, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor activity of NK92 cells, and also maintains good structural stability and functionality.

Construction of PN-CAR-NK92 cells

To validate receptor expression and determine the optimal configuration for dual-CAR co-expression, HEK-293T cells were transiently transfected with either individual CAR constructs or multicistronic CAR constructs. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed surface expression of PD-1 and NKG2D following transient transfection with the individual PD1-CAR and NKG2D-CAR constructs, respectively (Fig. 2A). These individual CAR constructs were subsequently used as single-CAR control groups in later functional experiments. Two multicistronic configurations were then generated for head-to-head comparison: PN-CAR (PD1–P2A–NKG2D) and NP-CAR (NKG2D–P2A–PD1). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that HEK-293T cells transiently transfected with the PN construct exhibited a higher proportion of PD-1+/NKG2D+ double-positive cells compared with the NP construct (Fig. 2B).

Based on these findings, the PN configuration was selected for subsequent NK92 cell engineering experiments. Quantitative PCR analysis was first performed 72 h after lentiviral transduction to evaluate transcription of the CAR-associated PD-1 and NKG2D sequences (Fig. 3A). Parental NK92 cells showed minimal endogenous PD-1 expression, whereas PD1-CAR-NK92 and PN-CAR-NK92 cells exhibited markedly increased PD-1 transcript levels. Similarly, elevated NKG2D transcript expression was detected in NKG2D-CAR-NK92 and PN-CAR-NK92 cells. Single-target CAR-NK92 cells were included as reference controls to confirm the specificity of transcript expression associated with each CAR construct. To enrich CAR-positive cells, puromycin selection was subsequently applied. Quantitative PCR analysis demonstrated further enrichment of both PD-1 and NKG2D transcripts after selection (Fig. 3B). Flow cytometry analysis was subsequently performed to monitor surface co-expression of PD-1 and NKG2D throughout the selection process (Fig. 3C). At early post-transduction time points, only a small fraction of cells co-expressed both receptors. Following 14 days of puromycin selection, the PD-1+/NKG2D+ population increased markedly and reached 94.7%, confirming the successful establishment of a stable PN-CAR-NK92 cell line.

Phenotypic characterization and proliferation of PN-CAR-NK92 cells

To determine whether CAR expression affected the biological properties of NK92 cells, the phenotype and proliferation of PN-CAR-NK92 cells were evaluated. Flow cytometry analysis showed that the characteristic phenotype of NK92 cells was maintained after genetic modification. Both parental NK92 cells and PN-CAR-NK92 cells exhibited a CD3⁻/CD16⁻ phenotype. In addition, high expression of the NK cell marker CD56 (> 99%) was observed in all groups (Fig. 4A). Cell proliferation was assessed using a CCK-8 assay over a 72 h period. PN-CAR-NK92 cells showed growth kinetics comparable to those of parental NK92 cells and single-target CAR-NK92 cells (Fig. 4B). No significant differences in OD₄₅₀ values were observed among the groups (P > 0.05), indicating that CAR expression did not affect the proliferation capacity of NK92 cells. These results indicate that the expression of the PN-CAR construct does not alter the characteristic phenotype or proliferation capacity of NK92 cells.

PN-CAR-NK92 cells exhibit enhanced cytotoxicity across heterogeneous antigen-expressing tumor cells

To evaluate the antitumor activity of PN-CAR-NK92 cells, the expression of PD-L1 and MICA/B was first analyzed in a panel of six tumor cell lines by flow cytometry (Fig. 5A). MGC-803 and H1299 cells showed high MICA/B expression with relatively low PD-L1 levels, whereas EMT-6-PDL1 cells exhibited high PD-L1 expression and low MICA/B levels. U2OS and A549 cells expressed both PD-L1 and MICA/B, while KATO-III cells showed minimal expression of both ligands. The cytotoxic activity of CAR-NK92 cells was then evaluated at different E: T ratios. PN-CAR-NK92 cells exhibited stronger cytotoxic activity across most ligand-positive tumor cell lines compared with parental NK92 cells and single-target CAR-NK92 cells (Fig. 5B). In contrast, PD1-CAR-NK92 cells showed limited killing activity against MICA/B-high tumor cells, whereas NKG2D-CAR-NK92 cells showed reduced cytotoxicity against PD-L1-high EMT-6-PDL1 cells. PN-CAR-NK92 cells achieved the highest cytotoxic activity at an E: T ratio of 5:1. Altogether, these results indicate that PN-CAR-NK92 cells exhibit enhanced cytotoxic activity against a broad range of tumor cell lines, particularly those expressing either PD-L1 or MICA/B ligands, demonstrating the functional advantage of dual-targeting CAR engineering in NK cells.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of PN-CAR-NK92 cells in an H1299 xenograft model

To evaluate the antitumor activity of PN-CAR-NK92 cells in vivo, a subcutaneous xenograft model was established using H1299 cells, which were selected based on their concurrent expression of PD-L1 and MICA/B and their robust in vitro responsiveness to PN-CAR-NK92-mediated cytotoxicity. Mice were treated with systemic infusions of effector cells according to the experimental schedule (Fig. 7A). Tumor growth was monitored throughout the study. Both NK92 and PN-CAR-NK92 cells inhibited tumor progression compared with the PBS control group, with a more pronounced effect observed in the PN-CAR-NK92 group (Fig. 7B). A clear divergence in tumor growth between the PN-CAR-NK92 group and control groups became evident around day 15 after tumor inoculation. At the experimental endpoint (day 17), tumors in the PN-CAR-NK92 group were significantly smaller than those in the PBS and NK92 groups. At the end of the experiment, tumors were excised and photographed (Fig. 7C). Tumors in the PN-CAR-NK92 group were smaller than those in the PBS and NK92 groups. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that tumor weights in PN-CAR-treated mice were significantly lower than those in the control groups (Fig. 7D), with an average tumor weight of approximately 0.2 g. The infiltration of NK92 cells into tumor tissues was further evaluated through immunohistochemical staining analysis. The results showed a marked increase in NK92 cell infiltration in the PN-CAR-NK92 group compared to the control NK92 group, with approximately a two-fold higher level observed in the former (Fig. 7E-F). Collectively, these findings indicate that PN-CAR-NK92 cells exhibit enhanced antitumor activity and increased intratumoral NK-cell infiltration in the H1299 xenograft model.

Discussion

The clinical landscape of oncology has been transformed by CAR-T cell therapy, which has demonstrated remarkable therapeutic efficacy in several hematological malignancies [39]. The approval of multiple CAR-T products by regulatory agencies such as the FDA and EMA represents a major milestone in the development of cellular immunotherapies [40–42]. However, the high cost of individualized manufacturing and the challenges associated with large-scale production remain significant barriers. In addition, the therapeutic application of CAR-T cells in solid tumors continues to face critical obstacles, including poor tumor infiltration and the highly immunosuppressive TME [43–45]. Consequently, NK cell–based immunotherapies have emerged as promising alternatives due to their inherent antitumor activity and compatibility with “off-the-shelf” therapeutic strategies [46, 47].

A deeper understanding of tumor immune evasion mechanisms is essential for improving the efficacy of these therapies. One of the most important pathways involved in tumor-mediated immune suppression is the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling axis, which inhibits the activation of immune effector cells and promotes immune tolerance within the TME [48–50]. To counteract PD-1–mediated inhibitory signaling, the extracellular domain of PD-1 was fused with the transmembrane and intracellular signaling domains of DAP10, thereby converting inhibitory signals into activating cues. DAP10 is a critical adaptor molecule in NK-cell signaling and is known to recruit PI3K-dependent pathways that promote cytotoxic degranulation while also supporting the stability of endogenous NKG2D expression [51, 52]. In parallel, the NKG2D-CAR module was designed using the extracellular recognition domain of NKG2D linked to an IgG4 hinge and intracellular signaling domains derived from CD28, 4-1BB, and CD3ζ. This configuration enables the recognition of MICA and MICB, stress-induced ligands that are frequently expressed on tumor cells but can evade immune detection through proteolytic shedding within the TME [26]. By integrating these two receptor modules, the PN-CAR system provides a dual-antigen recognition strategy that may reduce the limitations associated with single-target therapies.

Consistent with this design, our in vitro cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that PD1-CAR-NK92 cells exhibited stronger activity against PD-L1–high tumor cells, whereas NKG2D-CAR-NK92 cells preferentially targeted MICA/B-high tumor cells. In contrast, PN-CAR-NK92 cells maintained robust cytotoxicity across tumor cell lines with distinct ligand-expression profiles, including A549, U2OS, and H1299. Furthermore, PN-CAR-NK92 cells exhibited enhanced secretion of IFN-γ, TNF-α, granzyme B, and perforin following tumor cell engagement. These findings suggest that the integration of DAP10 signaling with CD3ζ-mediated activation may contribute to enhanced NK-cell effector function under diverse antigenic conditions.

In the present study, NKG2D ligand expression was evaluated based on MICA/B staining only. whereas additional NKG2D ligands, including ULBP family members, were not assessed and may also contribute to tumor recognition by the PN-CAR system. Furthermore, the current study utilized homogeneous tumor cell lines with distinct ligand-expression profiles rather than models of intratumoral antigen heterogeneity or antigen-negative subclonal escape. Therefore, although the dual-target strategy broadened tumor recognition coverage compared with single-target CAR-NK92 cells, its ability to overcome antigen escape within heterogeneous tumor populations remains to be further investigated. Future studies using mixed tumor populations, patient-derived models, or heterogeneous tumor models will be necessary to more comprehensively evaluate the translational applicability of the PN-CAR strategy. In addition, the in vivo antitumor activity of PN-CAR-NK92 cells was validated only in an H1299 xenograft model, and further validation across additional tumor models with distinct ligand-expression patterns will be required.

Despite these encouraging findings, several challenges remain in the treatment of solid tumors. The complex and immunosuppressive nature of the TME often results in reduced efficacy compared with hematological malignancies [53, 54]. Future therapeutic strategies may therefore benefit from combining CAR-NK cell therapy with biomarker-guided precision oncology approaches and other multimodal treatment strategies to further enhance antitumor responses [55–59]. In addition, although NK92 cells were used in this study as a proof-of-concept platform, the modular PN-CAR design may be readily adapted to other clinically relevant NK-cell sources, including peripheral blood-derived, umbilical cord blood-derived, or induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived NK cells. Overall, this dual-target CAR-NK strategy provides a promising framework for broadening tumor recognition coverage in solid tumors.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully established a multicistronic dual-target PN-CAR-NK92 system that co-expresses a PD1-based switch receptor and an NKG2D-CAR through a single-promoter lentiviral platform. The engineered PN-CAR-NK92 cells maintained stable receptor expression and preserved characteristic NK-cell phenotypes during long-term expansion. Functional analyses demonstrated that PN-CAR-NK92 cells exhibited enhanced cytotoxic activity and increased effector molecule secretion across tumor cell lines with distinct ligand-expression profiles compared with single-target CAR-NK92 cells (Fig. 8). In addition, PN-CAR-NK92 cells displayed enhanced antitumor activity in an H1299 xenograft model. Collectively, these findings suggest that dual-target CAR engineering may broaden tumor recognition coverage and provide a promising strategy to reduce limitations associated with single-target CAR therapies in solid tumors.
