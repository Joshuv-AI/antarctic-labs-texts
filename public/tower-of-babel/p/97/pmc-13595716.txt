Factors associated with symptom relief and lesion formation in acute ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack
Authors: A Hyeong Kim, Si Won Kim, So Young Park, Byoung-Soo Shin, Hyun Goo Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient symptoms have not been a primary consideration in ischemic stroke management. However, symptom evaluation in ischemic stroke may enhance the quality of care and improve prognosis. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to determine the factors associated with symptom relief and lesions in ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack (TIA).MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with ischemic stroke or TIA between April 2020 and February 2024 in a tertiary regional general hospital. The 2436 patients who met the inclusion criteria were classified based on symptom persistence and the presence of stroke lesions. Factors related to symptom relief and lesion formation were analyzed using comparative and multivariate regression analyses.ResultsThrough multivariate regression analysis, we identified significant factors, including age, stroke size, absence of white matter hyperintensities, absence of microbleeds, and the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at admission, that affect symptom relief in patients with ischemic stroke lesions. Significant factors affecting lesion formation in patients with ischemic stroke and symptom relief were the NIHSS score at admission, atrial fibrillation, and alcohol intake. The results demonstrated that pathological changes in the brain, pre-existing brain damage, and stroke lesions can prevent symptom relief. The NIHSS score, which indicates stroke severity, was associated with symptom relief and the presence of stroke lesions. Stroke lesion formation was associated with alcohol intake and the presence of atrial fibrillation.ConclusionsThese findings provide valuable insights for the development of management strategies that improve the treatment and prognosis of patients with acute ischemic stroke.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Transient ischemic attack, Neurologic manifestations, Spontaneous remission

Introduction

Thrombolysis and antiplatelet therapy are effective and widely implemented treatments for patients with ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) [1]. Treatments after a stroke or TIA focus on preventing stroke recurrence and improving prognosis [2]. However, stroke symptoms are crucial for determining the specific location of stroke lesions, predicting outcomes after treatment, and selecting the appropriate therapy. Therefore, predicting the clinical course of patients with acute ischemic stroke is important to make a therapeutic approach. Despite the importance of stroke symptom assessment, limited research has been conducted on this topic.

The clinical presentation of TIA is similar to that of ischemic stroke. Several studies have shown that the risk of recurrent ischemic stroke increases after TIA [3, 4]. This could be interpreted as similar pathophysiological mechanisms in ischemic stroke and TIA. Several risk factors associated with ischemic stroke have been identified [5]; however, the mechanisms by which these risk factors cause stroke remain unclear. Patients with TIA experience resolution of neurological deficits within 24 h, as demonstrated by no acute infarction in brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [6]. While typical TIA episodes often resolve within minutes or less than one hour, this study adhered to the criterion of symptom resolution within 24 h to clearly differentiate the TIA group from the persistent ischemic stroke group in a real-world clinical context. The presence and size of stroke lesions on MRI are important to predict prognosis [7]. Patients with ischemic stroke and remarkable stroke lesions on MRI experience mitigated or resolved symptoms during the acute phase of the disease.

In this study, we aimed to identify stroke-related factors to predict symptom relief in patients with acute ischemic stroke. Therefore, to elucidate the role of the ischemic lesion in symptom resolution, we focused on the relationship between the lesion and symptom relief by comparing two groups: ischemic stroke patients with symptom resolution and patients with TIA.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective study of 2647 patients with ischemic stroke or TIA who were admitted to a regional hub tertiary general hospital between April 1, 2020, and February 1, 2024. Among these patients, we excluded 185 whose stroke lesions or National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores were blanked, and 26 without stroke lesions who experienced persistent symptoms. A total of 211 patients were excluded from the initial cohort of 2,647: 185 due to missing NIHSS scores or stroke lesion data, and 26 who showed persistent symptoms in the absence of a stroke lesion. Therefore, 2436 patients were included in the present study. We categorized the participants into the following groups: symptom relief without lesions (137 patients), symptom relief with lesions (324 patients), and symptom persistence with lesions (1975 patients). Thereafter, we conducted a comparative analysis of the groups (Fig. 1).

Definition and classification

The NIHSS is a valid method for evaluating the severity of neurological symptoms after a stroke episode [8, 9]. In our study, the severity of symptoms experienced by patients with stroke was compared based on NIHSS scores. The NIHSS provides a rapid and accurate primary evaluation of stroke-related impairments by quantifying neurological evaluation [10]. However, the NIHSS score is inadequate as a single index for evaluating outcomes after stroke because it only evaluates impairment and not daily life functions [11]. Therefore, we used the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) to overcome these limitations. The mRS score is an index used to assess stroke-related disorders [11] that classifies patients with stroke into seven grades (0–6) based on functional independence, particularly ambulatory ability [12]. The mRS score at discharge was used to assess the prognosis of patients with stroke. To establish a criterion consistent with symptom resolution observed in patients with TIA, symptom relief was defined as a neurological symptom at admission subsiding to “NIHSS score at discharge = 0.” In this study, symptom relief was strictly defined as an NIHSS score of 0 at discharge. While we recognize that significant clinical improvement (partial recovery) is clinically important, our primary research focus was to distinguish the clinical and radiological characteristics of patients with complete neurological resolution (TIA) from those with persistent deficits (ischemic stroke). By applying this stringent criterion, we aimed to eliminate interpretative ambiguity arising from the high variability in patients’ baseline severity and clinical courses, thereby ensuring a clear and consistent comparison between the two distinct clinical entities. Stroke lesions were determined only by the presence of acute lesions on MRI.

Microbleeds are hypointense, tiny, round spots that are usually found on susceptibility-weighted images [13]. Microbleeds were classified as follows: grade 0 (absent), 0; grade 1 (mild), 1–2; grade 2 (moderate), 3–10; grade 3 (severe), > 10 [14]. White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are hyperintense signals detected using T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery imaging. These lesions indicate the accumulation of tissue damage in the white matter. We assessed WMHs using the Fazekas scale, a widely used classification method for evaluating WMHs [15]. The classification was as follows: periventricular white matter lesions, no lesion = 0, caps or pencil-thin lining = 1, smooth halo = 2, and irregular periventricular signals extending into deep white matter = 3; separate deep white matter lesions, no lesion = 0, punctate foci = 1, beginning confluence of foci = 2, and extensive confluent areas = 3 [16]. The Fazekas scale score for each patient was defined as scoring the maximal periventricular or deep white matter lesion [15].

Bone mineral density (BMD) was measured using dual-energy radiographic absorptiometry, which uses T-scores to compare the BMD of young and healthy patients [17]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) / height (m2) [18].

Risk factors

The widely known risk factors for stroke include hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke, ischemic heart disease, congestive heart failure, smoking, and alcohol intake. Patients with hypertension were defined as those whose systolic blood pressure at rest was ≥ 140 mmHg and whose diastolic blood pressure at rest was ≥ 90 mmHg [19]. Patients with diabetes mellitus were defined as those whose 2 h postprandial blood sugar level in the 75 g oral glucose tolerance test was ≥ 200 mg/dL, whose 8 h fasting blood sugar level was ≥ 126 mg/dL, or whose Hb1Ac level was ≥ 6.5% [20]. Patients with dyslipidemia were defined as those who were previously diagnosed with dyslipidemia or met the following diagnostic criteria: fasting blood low-density lipoprotein cholesterol level ≥ 130 mg/dL or total cholesterol level ≥ 200 mg/dL [21]. Patients with atrial fibrillation were defined as those who were previously or newly diagnosed using electrocardiography. A previous stroke was defined as a hypointense lesion on diffusion-weighted MRI. Patients with ischemic heart disease or congestive heart failure were defined as those who were previously diagnosed. Alcohol intake and smoking status were defined as a history of drinking or smoking in the last 5 years.

Stroke size measurements

Stroke size was measured using the ABC/2 method [22]. The stroke size was directly measured and calculated using MR images. Two trained researchers conducted measurements using the average of three trials for each scan, and neurologists interpreted debated cases.

Initially, we measured the largest cross-sectional area of the stroke lesion by multiplying A = the maximum length (cm) in the transverse plane by B = the maximum width (cm), which was perpendicular to A in an identical plane. C is the approximate number of 10-mm slices of the stroke lesion. To calculate C, we counted the slices according to the following criteria: as a standard for the largest stroke lesion, a slice with a stroke size of > 75% was considered 1, a stroke size of 75–25% was considered 0.5, and a stroke size of < 25% was considered 0. Statistical analysis

Patients with ischemic stroke were categorized into two groups: those with symptom relief and those with persistent symptoms. We analyzed and compared the demographic characteristics, risk factors, and clinical outcomes of the two groups. In addition, we divided the patients in the symptom relief group into those with and without ischemic stroke for comparative analysis. Descriptive statistics for all variables were presented as frequencies (%) for categorical variables and as means ± standard deviations for continuous variables. To compare differences between categorical variables, Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used, and continuous variables were analyzed using the Student’s t-test.

Logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the factors affecting symptom improvement in patients with ischemic stroke and to identify the factors affecting the presence of ischemic stroke in patients whose symptoms improved. Univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent variables with a p-value < 0.05. Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression analysis including these variables was performed. The results of the logistic regression analysis are presented as p-values, adjusted odds ratios (aORs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and the sample size was determined based on a power calculation to detect a statistically significant difference at a two-sided α level of 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Differences between patients with and without symptom relief

We analyzed the differences between the patients with stroke lesions who experienced symptom relief and those whose symptoms persisted by comparing their clinical and demographic characteristics (Table 1). Our results demonstrated that patients in the symptom relief group were significantly younger (68.16 ± 13.83 years vs. 72.80 ± 12.26 years, p < 0.001) and had a smaller stroke size (1.85 ± 5.12 mL vs. 11.87 ± 32.36 mL, p < 0.001) compared to the group with symptom persistence. In addition, the symptom relief group included a higher proportion of male patients (64.2% vs. 57.5%, p = 0.024). The group without symptom relief had significantly higher rates of WMHs (Fazekas scale score 3, 15.8% vs. 36.7%, p < 0.001) and microbleeds (3.9% vs. 9.1%; p < 0.001). In addition, the symptom relief group had lower NIHSS scores at admission (1 [0-2] vs. 4 [2–7], p < 0.001) and lower mRS scores at discharge (0 [0-1] vs. 3 [2–4], p < 0.001). Regarding stroke risk factors, the symptom persistence group had a higher rate of previous strokes (19.8% vs. 26.0%, p = 0.016); however, the symptom relief group showed a higher rate of alcohol intake (27.8% vs. 21.9%, p = 0.018) and had a higher BMD (–1.06 ± 1.25 SD vs. − 1.84 ± 1.46 SD, p = 0.004). Laboratory findings showed that the symptom relief group had a significantly higher albumin level (4.29 ± 0.45 g/dL vs. 4.14 ± 0.50 g/dL, p < 0.001) and glomerular filtration rate (81.93 ± 21.87 mL/min vs. 78.97 ± 23.47 mL/min, p = 0.046); meanwhile, the symptom persistence group had a higher level of fibrinogen (321.60 ± 74.03 mg/dL vs. 335.62 ± 82.64 mg/dL, p = 0.006).

Factors associated with stroke lesion formation

We analyzed the differences between the presence of lesions in patients with ischemic stroke and the absence of lesions in patients with TIA (Table 3). The group with lesions had a longer onset-to-arrival time (992.66 ± 4401.19 min vs. 4537.57 ± 18429.55 min, p = 0.003) and a significantly higher NIHSS score at admission (0 [0–0] vs. 1 [0–2], p < 0.001). Regarding stroke risk factors, the group with lesions had higher rates of atrial fibrillation (6.6% vs. 15.8%, p = 0.007) and alcohol intake (17.9% vs. 27.8%, p = 0.026). Laboratory findings showed that the group without lesions had a significantly higher level of albumin (4.40 ± 0.34 g/dL vs. 4.29 ± 0.45 g/dL, p = 0.006).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that age, stroke size, and the NIHSS score inversely affected symptom relief, and microbleeds grade 0 and WMH grade 0 affected symptom relief in patients with stroke. In addition, stroke lesions in symptomatic patients with ischemic stroke were associated with the NIHSS score at admission, atrial fibrillation, and alcohol intake.

Older patients have higher rates of mortality and incidence and show a poorer prognosis [23, 24]. Aged brains exhibit increased immune responses and immune-related cells infiltration, as well as reduced DNA repair and genomic stability [25]. In addition, the aged brain is associated with increased stroke severity, which may be related to atrophy and decreased brain capacity [24]. These characteristics of aged brains delay the recovery of function following brain damage. Therefore, in this study, the reduced recovery of the damaged brain worsened symptom relief.

Evidence clearly demonstrates that ischemic stroke size affects prognostic outcomes and that a large stroke size is associated with increased severity of neurological deficits [7, 26]. In particular, the ischemic stroke lesion volume at 24 h is highly significant for prognosis and functional outcomes [27]. Boes et al. [28] showed that damaged areas are associated with the extent neurological deficits, because each brain region performs specific functions. Because the brain network allows brain communication, a large stroke size can result in poor prognostic outcomes [29, 30]. In the present study, we measured and calculated the size of stroke lesions associated with primary symptoms. When measuring symptom-related stroke volume, it was observed that symptoms affected by the damaged brain region and associated brain networks were alleviated when the stroke lesion was large, even though the poor outcomes generally associated with a large lesion size.

Hong et al. [31] reported that excessive WMHs decrease the probability of brain functional outcomes in patients with stroke, especially in those with mild stroke. Moreover, the WMH volume is related to cognition in patients with ischemic stroke, particularly those with large infarctions [32]. These/Our findings show that a small WMH volume is related to the recovery of brain functions. Moreover, a large WMH volume is associated with poor restoration of brain function after stroke. This poor restoration interrupts symptom relief in patients with ischemic stroke. Likewise, cerebral microbleeds significantly affect symptom relief in patients with ischemic stroke. Microbleeds can induce in situ thrombosis and reduce arterial blood flow [33]. This characteristic of microbleeds delays brain recovery and symptom relief after ischemic stroke.

The NIHSS score has been used to predict prognosis in clinical settings because a high NIHSS score is associated with poor prognosis [34]. The present study confirmed that the NIHSS score at admission is an important factor for symptom relief, suggesting that the severity of neurological deficits at onset can be used as a factor for predicting symptom relief in patients with acute ischemic stroke. In addition, our study showed that a higher NIHSS score was associated with stroke lesions rather than with TIA. This result demonstrates that pathological changes in the brain, pre-existing brain damage, and stroke lesions affect the severity of symptoms in patients with ischemic stroke and can interrupt symptom relief.

The present study showed that atrial fibrillation is associated with stroke lesions in patients with ischemic stroke and symptom relief. This suggests that microparticles originating from atrial fibrillation are associated with stroke lesions, although they have minimal effects on the patient’s chief symptoms. As one of the most significant stroke risk factors, some mechanisms have been described that explain how atrial fibrillation causes ischemic stroke [35]. Stroke recurrence can be reduced by atrial fibrillation care and continuous monitoring [36]. However, in some studies, strokes remained common in patients with atrial fibrillation, even in those with oral anticoagulation therapy [37, 38]. Further research is required to determine the mechanisms underlying atrial fibrillation in patients with stroke.

Alcohol intake can cause ischemic stroke [39]. Increased levels of hepatic enzymes are associated with decreased blood flow velocity; liver lesions are associated with ischemic stroke related to inflammation, coagulation disorders, and viral hepatitis; and liver lesions can cause impaired circulation [40, 41]. Kim et al. demonstrated that the fatty liver index is related to nonfatal ischemic stroke [42]. The significant association between alcohol intake and liver lesions may be related to the development of stroke lesions, as demonstrated in the present study.

Acute interventions for ischemic stroke, including intravenous tissue plasminogen activator (IV-tPA) and intra-arterial thrombectomy (IAT), are well established to be closely associated with symptom resolution [43]. Accordingly, we evaluated whether these acute interventions influenced symptom relief or lesion formation in our cohort. Among patients with confirmed lesions (Table 1), the distribution of acute interventions did not differ significantly between the symptom relief and persistence groups (p = 0.089), suggesting that treatment alone did not primarily drive clinical recovery. In contrast, among patients with symptom relief (Table 3), acute interventions were more frequently performed in the lesion-present group than in the lesion-absent group (p = 0.001). Rather than indicating a causal role of intervention in lesion formation, this finding more plausibly reflects greater baseline clinical severity or ischemic burden among patients selected for aggressive therapy. Consistent with this interpretation, Alexandre et al. [44] reported that age and baseline NIHSS score are more decisive determinants of outcome than interventional treatment in severely affected patients. Moreover, Winkelmeier et al. [45] demonstrated that lesion persistence despite aggressive treatment reflects intrinsic tissue vulnerability rather than treatment failure. Taken together, in this cohort, acute interventional therapy is better interpreted as a marker of baseline disease severity rather than a primary confounding factor.

This study has certain limitations. First, it was conducted retrospectively, and selection bias is a potential factor in retrospective studies. In addition, obtaining a complete database was challenging because the patients had not been examined previously. Second, this was a single-center study. As the authors’ hospital is a regional hub tertiary general center, the patients were limited to local inhabitants. However, this does not seem to interrupt the generalization of our finding in ischemic stroke to other population because patients with ischemic stroke usually do not frequently visit primary or small hospitals. Third, the use of a stringent definition for symptom relief (NIHSS = 0) may have resulted in the classification of patients with dramatic clinical improvement - who nonetheless had a residual NIHSS ≥ 1-into the symptom persistent group. This approach potentially excludes a clinically meaningful subgroup that responded well to treatment but did not achieve total resolution. Consequently, the distinct characteristics of these ‘partial responders’ might have been underrepresented in our analysis. Further research employing broader definitions of recovery, such as major neurological improvement, is necessary to fully capture the spectrum of clinical success in acute stroke management. Fourth, functional outcomes were evaluated only up to 3 months after stroke onset due to the retrospective nature of the dataset and substantial missing long-term follow-up data. Future prospective studies with systematic follow-up are needed to assess longer-term functional outcomes. Finally, alcohol intake was classified as either present or absent without information on the type of alcohol consumed, duration of drinking, or alcohol intake.

Conclusions

Treating symptoms during the early management of acute ischemic stroke is crucial. This study result can be used to determine association between the severity and prognosis of patients. Moreover, it might be considered during the early management of patients with ischemic stroke intensive care and close monitoring needs for the patients with these factors. Also, early rehabilitation can be supportive to promote functional recovery in these patients. The study findings offer potential targets to a consistent management of the underlying disease and the patient’s lifestyle. Further prospective research is necessary to investigate the association between these factors and the efficacy of acute ischemic stroke treatment such that controlling these risk factors affects symptom relief or reduces stroke size.
