A dataset of categorized features for the study of transient and seasonal phenomena on the Martian surface
Authors: Adriano Nardi, Antonio Piersanti, Carlotta Petrucci, Alessandro Pignatelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a dataset of utilities developed to address the study of transient phenomena on the Martian surface through a seasonal approach. To this aim we selected high resolution HiRISE/MRO images. For each of these images (catalogue updated as of December 2024), we have compiled data records that are partly extracted from the archive itself but are supplemented with many other, associated or computed, new parameters. Further processing of this primary dataset has yielded two lists of image clusters related to a selection of Martian sites. Based on the spatio-temporal distribution of HiRISE images, these sites are ideal for studying: (i) seasonal surface variations through a ’virtual sampling’ of the Martian year, and (ii) transient or seasonal changes through non-conventional stereoscopic pairing.

Background & Summary

Martian space missions of the 21st century are revealing an increasingly dynamic and changing Martian environment. The Viking 2 lander (1976–1980) had already photographed transient atmospheric phenomena, such as frost, on the surface. Starting in 2000, the Mars Global Surveyor orbiter, thanks to the 1.5 meter-per-pixel resolution of its Mars Orbiter Camera, showed the first gullies1, almost immediately highlighting morphological variations and newly formed channels, especially on dune gullies2. The action of liquid water as an agent for these phenomena was proposed from the beginning1 and has been repeatedly contested and reproposed, despite its poor compatibility with the current physical conditions on the surface. Currently, liquid water on the surface of Mars is not stable. However, as ice thaws, transient liquid water may appear if the surface temperature is between the triple point and the boiling point. These conditions can occur on Mars at the current surface pressure due to the large diurnal range of surface temperatures. This produces a “transient liquid water potential” (TLWP)3.

The geological features known on Mars as “gullies” are often much larger than similar terrestrial structures of the same term. In size, they are more comparable to terrestrial ravines or gorges4. In terrestrial terminology, “gully” refers exclusively to the erosional channel; however, in common usage in the Martian literature, the term includes the entire complexity of the structure, including apical alcoves and basal aprons, when present5. Studies of Martian gullies, both linear (dunal gullies) and those not associated with dunes, show significant evidence that their activity is ongoing and that the most significant morphological changes are strongly influenced by seasonal control. In particular, they appear to be associated with the seasonal cycle of freezing and thawing5.

Thus, the seasonal approach can be considered the only key to hypothesizing a role for water in many seasonal transient phenomena, including gullies, despite the limited stability of its liquid phase under current surface conditions. Here, we have developed utilities to facilitate a task that previously could have been performed only by collecting or calculating parameters by means of ad-hoc and non-systematic approaches. Images acquired by the High-Resolution Imaging Science Experiment (HiRISE) on board the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO) are well-suited for the study of Martian transients, thanks to a resolution that reaches 25 cm per pixel. Unlike CTX, HiRISE operates strictly as a targeted instrument; consequently, a substantial fraction of its acquisitions (approximately 30–40%) consists of repeat imaging designed for seasonal monitoring and/or the generation of stereopairs. As of December 2021, the official spatial coverage accounted for 2.6% of the Martian surface6. Following the milestone of 100,000 acquired observations reached in 2025, current estimates place the global coverage between 4.5% and 5%. Nevertheless, the mapping products presented herein demonstrate that this seemingly modest coverage is remarkably well-distributed across all latitudes, leaving no significant observational gaps across the major Martian climate bands.

In this paper, we present a dataset composed by a wide selection of parameters related to HiRISE images, which are useful for a seasonal approach to the study of the Martian surface geology, and we briefly outline the methods for the collection and production of this dataset. We have compiled all the parameters into three main lists, which we have named “Martian Utilities For Future Analysis” (MUFFA). These are associated with two lists of HiRISE image clusters. We will also explain how to use these utilities through a practical example.

Computation of seasonal parameters

The orbit of Mars exhibits a much higher eccentricity compared to Earth’s. This results in a more pronounced variation in the duration of the four seasons. Furthermore, all Martian seasons are longer than their terrestrial counterparts, with a difference of up to 51 sols between the length of spring and autumn. Consequently, evaluating how many local days, years, or seasons have elapsed between two Martian images, based on solar longitude (Ls) and especially when starting from the terrestrial date of a HiRISE image, is not a straightforward task. It is also not immediate to estimate which season was locally in progress in the study area and especially to compare seasonal periods starting from the Ls.

In the presented dataset, we also express Martian time through the parameters calculable with the IMDcalc algorithm, specifically designed for this purpose7. We adopt April 11, 1955 (Ls = 0°) as the beginning of Martian year 18 in accordance with the Planetary Society. Starting from the terrestrial date associated with the HiRISE images, the IMD algorithm provides us with the Martian year (MYear), the Martian day counted from the beginning of the year (Sol), the local season (Season) and the percentage of this season already passed (PercSeason). Finally, under the “IMD” heading, we propose the Martian dating system proposed with this algorithm. It is a five-digit code, one of which is a decimal, that immediately expresses: the Martian year, the season, and its percentage elapsed. This numerical code was designed to be mapped onto natural numbers in a unique and monotonic, though non-linear, way. It can therefore be used as a time scale for a graph. Here’s an example: an event that occurred during Martian year 8, specifically at 31.6% of northern spring (or southern autumn, in any case season 1), is encoded as: IMD = 8131.6. Being accustomed to Earth’s seasonal cycles (where the near-circular orbit makes angle and time nearly coincident), the IMD removes the cognitive distortion introduced by Martian eccentricity, providing a comprehensible linear (0–100%) “progress bar” for analyzing decay/growth rates. Unlike an Ls approach or a “Julian-type” approach”, IMD dating offers the opportunity to compare Martian years and seasons accurately and intuitively. It is also versatile because, being a single numerical entity, it can be directly used in quantitative tasks (plot scales, etc.). By adopting the IMD time scale, in our previous works it was possible to highlight the specific recurrence of transient phenomena and to hypothesize predictions of their repeatability7,9. Altimetric Data Extraction Using GIS

The original HiRISE dataset provided the geographic coordinates of the image center but not its elevation. However, this parameter is crucial in the study of seasonal transients, which are linked to temperature and pressure variations7,9. We used a Digital Elevation Model (DEM) to extract the elevation values corresponding to the geographic coordinates of all HiRISE images in the Classification list. This process has been executed by means of the QGIS application on the “Mars MGS MOLA - MEX HRSC Blended DEM Global 200m” USGS DEM (credits: USGS Astrogeology Science Center; accessed on 19 Feb 2026). This is a combination of DEM data from NASA’s Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter (MOLA/MGS) and ESA’s High Resolution Stereo Camera (HRSC/MEX). This blend yields a resolution of 200 meters per pixel instead of the 400 m resolution of MOLA. The obtained elevations refer to the official reference coordinates of the HiRISE images and have therefore been incorporated into the MUFFA lists as an additional coordinate and classification element.

The utility of stereoscopy in studying transient phenomena

The images classified in the MUFFA lists were acquired by the HiRISE instrument onboard MRO. This instrument does not produce native stereopairs. HiRISE stereo pairs are obtained from two separate, closely-spaced, orbital passes over the same target. An official list of these pairings is available on the HiRISE Website (credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/UArizona) and has been included in the Classification list. Each image in the Classification list is considered a potential ‘left’ image, which can be paired with a ‘right’ component (RightStereoComb), along with its corresponding stereo convergence angle (StereoConvAng). These high-resolution pairs enable a three-dimensional view of the surface, which is extremely useful for geomorphological interpretation9,10.

The stereoscopic technique involves using images of the same area captured from two different angles (a ‘left’ image and a ‘right’ image). In our case, the same effect is achieved by using images acquired during two different orbiter passes. Traditional optical stereoscopes required the images to be printed. For digital images, stereoscopic viewing is enabled by active or passive stereoscopic 3D image displays. Passive systems combine the images via a semi-reflective mirror but separate the view for each eye by shifting the light’s polarization angle by 90 degrees. This requires viewing through glasses with polarized lenses. Active systems involve the use of shutter glasses that alternately block each lens, synchronized with the appearance of the corresponding image on the same screen. The high alternation frequency between the two eyes renders the vision of a single image stable.

An alternative method, better suited for print media, is to generate anaglyphs. These are images colored with two complementary tints, designed to be viewed through special glasses which also feature colored lenses. This popular method results in a loss of resolution compared to the original image; however, just to give some practical exemplification of stereoscopy we will be show this technique in some figures.

The use of stereoscopy offers several advantages compared to a single HiRISE image. The first is the ability to obtain a perspective view with depth perception, which significantly enhances geomorphological interpretation. Direct stereoscopic viewing (i.e., through a stereoscope) also has the advantage of utilizing the maximum possible resolution. Indeed, when generating a Digital Terrain Model (DTM) from the same stereopair, the resolution is reduced by a factor of 411. A second advantage, which we propose specifically for studying Martian transient phenomena, can be obtained by leveraging the technical limitations of stereoscopy. Typically, a stereopair is acquired in a single session, and the stereoscopic baseline is defined by the distance between the two objectives of the stereoscopic camera. HiRISE pairs, as previously stated, are instead obtained via two separate flybys over the same region. The official HiRISE stereoscopic dataset consists of images that are optimally paired. This means that, beyond the required geometry, the two images must be acquired at the same local time and be separated by a short time interval (in sols); otherwise, different shadowing on the surface could impede stereoscopic fusion. However, images separated by a long time interval will exhibit a defect in the 3D view wherever a surface detail has been displaced or modified in shape or color. The visual perception of this defect produces an “ethereal” detail, which allows for the immediate recognition of any difference existing between the two images. This new technique has helped us highlight transient phenomena or surface mutations, such as gullies that have changed their path over time (Fig. 2a and Fig. 2b). Pairing of Non-Native Stereoscopic Images

Given that the HiRISE camera is not natively stereoscopic, specific flybys were planned to obtain image pairs geometrically suitable for stereoscopic pairing. The images catalogued in the MUFFA lists (HiRISE/MRO) can have three resolution levels. In the “map projected” re-sampled format, they have the following image scale range: 25, 50, and 100 cm/pixel. Stereoscopically pairing heterogeneous images (i.e., acquired at distant times, not perfectly centered, or of different resolution) may be necessary to improve the long-term sampling of Martian transients. To pair heterogeneous pairs, we adopted a specific graphical procedure that necessarily reduces the overall resolution to that of the lower-resolution image in the pair. First, it is necessary to crop an area from the lower-resolution image (LRI) that outlines the same portion of the Martian surface captured in the higher-resolution image (HRI). This operation can be performed using GIS or by means of graphics editing software. Subsequently, the HRI is processed to match the resolution of the LRI. This operation can be performed with graphics editing software in several ways: either by nominally resizing the “row x column” dimensions of the HRI to match the LRI, or by downsampling the HRI by a factor of 0.5 (or 0.25, as appropriate).

“Seasonal clusters” dataset

This dataset contains 190 HiRISE image clusters, classified according to the parameters reported in Table 2. Each record is classified into 12 categories, drawn either from an entry in the MUFFA_HiRISE_Classification.csv table or from a product derived by processing these entries. Each record represents a cluster of images acquired within a nominal area of 1000 km2, corresponding to a radius of approximately 17.8 km from the reference coordinates. In the case of Seasonal Clusters (SC), a minimum of 28 images was set to define a “cluster”. This value is not a geometric or statistical absolute, but an empirical boundary derived from a retrospective analysis of the HiRISE catalog up to December 2024. Observing the distribution of image counts against their temporal spread within the nominal 1000 km2 area, N=28 emerged as a robust heuristic lower bound. Within the current operational history of MRO, no cluster with 28 or more elements is sampled over a period shorter than one Martian year. Consequently, this filter effectively isolates regions with high potential for inter-annual monitoring. Figure 4 highlights that no cluster falls below 1 year. The utility of each SC will also be related to how the sols of its constituent images are distributed throughout the Martian Year (MY). The relative sol for each image is marked within the specific cluster. Let us consider two exemplary cases. If, in a cluster of only 28 elements acquired over 9 Martian years, the images were taken on 28 different sols, this would provide seasonal monitoring with 28 samples across the Martian year. If, instead, the images in the same cluster were found to be acquired always on the same 4 sols, this would result in monitoring over a maximum of 9 years, composed of a minimum of 7 images for each of those 4 seasonal markers, which could potentially represent all four seasons. The higher the image count, the more suitable the cluster will be for both types of analysis. “Extended Stereoscopy” dataset

This dataset includes 11,407 elements classified into 12 categories, analogous to the SC. The specific legend for the Extended Stereoscopy Clusters (ESC) categories is provided in Table 5. In this case, the goal is to identify pairs of HiRISE images that can potentially be used in stereoscopy for the purposes illustrated in Section “Pairing of Non-Native Stereoscopic Images”. For this reason, ESC clusters are defined as having a minimum of two elements with a maximum distance of 5 km between them, in both latitudinal and longitudinal terms. In the longitudinal (along-track) direction, image alignment within a digital stereoscope can be manually restored. Conversely, the 5 km latitudinal (cross-track) threshold is fundamentally dictated by the sensor’s swath width. At a nominal orbital altitude of 300 km, the RED CCD sensor array of the HiRISE camera generates a footprint with a cross-track width of approximately 6 km. By constraining the maximum centroid-to-centroid distance between two images to 5 km, a theoretical minimum lateral overlap of 1 km is inherently guaranteed by simple geometric subtraction. At a standard resolution of 0.25 m/pixel, a 1 km cross-track intersection equates to approximately 4,000 pixels. Because digital stereogrammetry software (or a human operator in a digital/anaglyph environment) requires a significantly smaller subset of homologous pixels to successfully generate tie-points and clear Y-parallax, this 5 km threshold effectively functions as a spatial low-pass filter, reliably ensuring the minimum overlap required for stereoscopic viewing. The coordinates of the ESC clusters refer to the centroid of a group of at least two elements detected within a polygon with a nominal area of 25 km2, constrained by a 5 km latitudinal extent. The clusters selected for ESC can contain as few as two elements, which means a single cluster may represent a monitoring period shorter than one Martian year. There are 5,948 clusters with this characteristic. The ESC selection criterion therefore necessitates highlighting the temporal range in sols, if necessary, and in such cases, specifying the first and last sol of acquisition within the same year (“FirstSol” and “LastSol”).

The effective number of useful elements in an ESC cluster can be subjective, as we have already seen with SC, but for ESC, it is further limited by the geometric constraints of the stereoscopic technique. Indeed, the pairing of stereopairs cannot follow a single, precise rule, as it depends on the artifacts (or offset) one is willing to accept in the partial overlap between the images. For example, in the limiting case where the horizontal distance between the photo centers equals the horizontal width of the photo, the two images can no longer be superimposed. For this reason, a 5 km side limit was imposed for the ESC count. With this parameter, the “Count” value will be proportionate to, and close to, the number of reasonably pairable images.

The maximum number of elements detected in an “ESC” cluster was 157 (Cluster11010.csv), corresponding to 12,246 potential ESP (EStereoPairs)—that is, stereopairs usable at least in virtual mode. The total number of ESP identified using these criteria, summing all the ESC clusters, is 249,027, compared to the 9,185 original HiRISE pairs. The original HiRISE pairs are, of course, included in the ESC list and are identifiable in their respective Cluster lists by the indication of their corresponding right component (RightStereoComb).

The maximum number of years elapsed between two images in an ESC is 10. Fig. 7 plots the number of cluster elements against the mean virtual sampling rate (VSolRate) across the Martian year. Clusters exhibiting a rate between zero and a few days (where the exact threshold is a function of both season and latitude) can contain numerous stereopairs featuring negligible shadow discrepancies, even when the constituent images were acquired several years apart. The same principle applies to the virtual annual coverage (VSolRange), although this specific filtering yields a selection with fewer elements. These stereopairs remain fully suitable for analytical study, albeit strictly within the boundaries of their effective overlapping area. Conversely, stereoscopic processing of clusters sampled at broader temporal rates (or extracted from a wider VSolRange) will inherently suffer from shadow-induced artifacts, the severity of which scales directly with topographic roughness. Nevertheless, even within these sub-optimal pairs, the visual perception of a stereoscopic mismatch readily highlights dynamic surface changes, such as displaced boulders or diverted gullies.Fig. 7Graph showing, for each ESC, the correlation between the number of images within the cluster (“Counting in cluster”) and the mean virtual sampling rate across the Martian year (VSolRate). Satellite observation of transient phenomena on Mars surface, is not limited to geological interest (wind erosion and sedimentation) but may extend to other fields, such as meteorology (wind and phase transitions) or, one day, even biology (indigenous or non-indigenous). We hope that our Martian Utilities For Future Analysis, made available to the scientific community in the dataset presented here, will represent a good starting point and a solid foundation for the remote exploration of the Red Planet.
