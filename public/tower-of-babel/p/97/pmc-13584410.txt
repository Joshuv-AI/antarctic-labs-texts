Integrated WGCNA and TO-GCN analysis reveals pathways and genes underlying salt tolerance in Sesbania cannabina
Authors: Mingzhu An, Xianguo Wang, Wei Wang, Yawen Hou, Xiangyu Wu, Wei Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Salt stress seriously threatens the growth and development of plants. To investigate how Sesbania cannabina responds to salt stress, we constructed a temporal regulatory response network under salt stress by integrating the physiological and transcriptomic data from ‘Zhongke Jing No.1’ at five time points (0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h, n = 3) after salt treatment. The results indicated that salt stress increased the relative electrical conductivity (REC) and malondialdehyde (MDA) content, induced the accumulation of osmotic regulators, and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities. A total of 688,424,778 raw reads were generated by RNA-seq, and 21,422 DEGs were identified using |log2FC| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.01. WGCNA and TO-GCN were constructed using β = 20 and PCC cutoff of 0.91, respectively, revealing three response stages: normal (0 h), early stage (6 h and 12 h), and late stage (24 h and 48 h). The MEturquoise module was associated with early osmotic and oxidative responses, while the MEbrown and MEblack modules were associated with late-stage antioxidant defense and secondary metabolism reprogramming. Early-stage genes were mainly involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and plant hormone signal transduction, whereas late-stage genes were enriched in glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis. Based on co-expression and network analyses, 9 putative candidate TFs, including ERF71, WRKY53, and bZIP9, were predicted to be associated with key salt-responsive pathway genes in the pathway subnetworks. These findings indicate that S. cannabina undergoes a transition from early carbon metabolism and hormone-mediated responses to late antioxidant defense and secondary metabolic reprogramming under salt stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09359-z.

Introduction

Salt stress caused by soil salinization is one of the major abiotic stresses affecting plant growth. Approximately 6% of the world’s arable land is affected by salinization and alkalization, causing serious economic losses [1, 2]. High salt concentrations reduce water availability and disturb cellular osmotic balance, leading to dehydration, wilting, and even death [3]. Rapid accumulation of Na+ in plants can lower the membrane potential below the resting potential, thereby activating K+ efflux channels and disrupting K+/Na+ homeostasis [4]. Subsequently, salt stress induces excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and superoxide anion (O2−), which can damage cellular structures by inducing membrane lipid peroxidation and protein denaturation [5].

Plants have evolved various metabolic pathways to cope with salt stress, including ion transport, compatible solute synthesis, and activation of antioxidant enzymes [6]. Plants synthesize osmotic regulators, including proline, soluble sugars, and polyamines, which help maintain cell turgor, reduce water loss, and enhance salt tolerance [7–9]. The antioxidant enzyme system, including peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), is induced and contributes to the detoxification of excess ROS [10]. These responses are usually accompanied by broad metabolic and transcriptional reprogramming, particularly in pathways related to hormone signaling, glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and carbon metabolism [11–13].

Halophytes can grow normally under high-salinity conditions, such as 200 mM NaCl, making them valuable materials for investigating salt-tolerance mechanisms and identifying stress-resistance genes [14]. Sesbania cannabina is an annual leguminous halophyte with strong nitrogen-fixing ability, high yield, and considerable stress resistance [15, 16]. It is commonly used as a pioneer plant and green manure in saline-alkali soils to alleviate soil salinization and improve soil fertility [17, 18]. Previous studies on S. cannabina have mainly focuses on antioxidant defense, ion homeostasis, and salt stress regulation mediated by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi [17, 19]. However, systematic analysis of how S. cannabina responds to salt stress remain limited, which restricts its wider application in the improvement of saline-alkali soils.

Transcriptome analysis has become a standard method for deciphering plant responses to salt stress and has been widely applied to uncover the relationships among hormone regulation, reactive oxygen species scavenging, osmotic adjustment, and salt tolerance [20, 21]. However, existing studies typically focus on a single analytical method and still lack multidimensional integrative analyses. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) can identify gene modules that are correlated with specific physiological responses and identify candidate hub genes [22]. In the halophyte Nitraria sibirica, WGCNA effectively identified key salt-responsive genes from transcriptomics data and provide systematic insights into ion homeostasis, oxidative stress, and signaling regulation under salt stress [23]. WGCNA was also used to identify candidate genes that regulate salt tolerance during soybean germination [24]. Time-ordered gene co-expression networks (TO-GCN) analysis can characterize temporal co-expression patterns and identify potential associations between transcription factors and genes during stress responses [25]. TO-GCN has been used to analyze the response patterns of mung bean under different salt concentration gradients [26]. These two methods demonstrate the value of analyzing different aspects of salt responses from complementary perspectives. Therefore, integrating these two methods can provide a more systematic understanding of plants responses to salt stress. In recent years, the combined analysis of WGCNA and TO-GCN has been applied to studies on cotton fiber development and peach fruit ripening [27, 28]. To our knowledge, the integrated application of WGCNA and TO-GCN in halophytes or legumes has not yet been reported.

This study aims to systematically analyze the dynamic responses of S. cannabina to salt stress by integrating physiological and transcriptome data. Through integrated WGCNA and TO-GCN analyses, candidate transcription factors putatively associated with salt-responsive pathway were identified based on co-expression and network analysis. This is the first study to apply WGCNA and TO-GCN to investigate the response of S. cannabina to salt stress, providing a systematic characterization of salt stress-associated co-expression networks. These findings provide valuable directions for further research into the mechanisms underlying the response of S. cannabina to abiotic stress.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

Seeds of the Sesbania cannabina cultivar ‘Zhongke Jing No.1’ were used in this study. This cultivar was bred by the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. This cultivar exhibits excellent growth performance in saline-alkali soil with pH ≥ 9. The seeds were disinfected with 0.5% NaClO for 30 min, thoroughly rinsed several times with deionized water, and subsequently treated with concentrated H2SO4 for 30 min to break dormancy. Finally, they were rinsed thoroughly with deionized water and soaked in deionized water for 5 h. The seeds were then cultivated in a plant growth chamber under a 14 h/10 h light/dark photoperiod, with day/night temperatures of 27/20 ℃, relative humidity of 50%, and a light intensity of 15,000 lx. After seven days of germination, uniformly growing seedlings were transferred to 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution. Each treatment included six hydroponic boxes (12.7 cm × 8.7 cm × 11.4 cm), with nine seedlings per box. Each hydroponic box represented one biological replicate. The nutrient solution was replaced every seven days, and each box contained the same volume of nutrient solution. Based on preliminary experiments, 250 mM NaCl was determined as a near-threshold stress concentration for subsequent physiological and transcriptomic analyses, and salt treatment was initiated when the seedlings reached the three-leaf stage. Root samples were collected at 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after salt treatment and stored at -80 °C for subsequent physiological measurements and transcriptome sequencing, with three biological replicates at each time point.

Measurement and analysis of the physiology indicators

Roots from different treatments were cut into 1 cm segments, placed in centrifuge tubes containing 20 mL of distilled water, and shaken for 24 h before conductivity was measured and recorded as S1. The samples were then heated in a boiling water bath for 30 min, and conductivity was measured again and recorded as S2. Distilled water was used as the blank control, and its conductivity values before and after boiling were recorded as S01 and S02, respectively. Relative Electrical Conductivity (REC, %) was calculated as follows: REC (%) = [(S1 -S01) / (S2-S02)] × 100%. The contents of MDA, proline, and soluble sugar were determined using the thiobarbituric acid method [29], acidic ninhydrin method [30], and anthrone method [31], respectively. Soluble protein content and the activities of POD, SOD, CAT, and APX were determined spectrophotometrically according to previously reported methods [32]. The physiological indices were expressed on a fresh weight (FW) basis. Root samples were dried at 60 ℃ to a constant weight, ground into a fine powder, and digested in a mixture of concentrated HNO3 and H2O2 (3:1, v/v). The concentrations of Na+ and K+ in the digested samples were then determined by ICP-OES and expressed as mg·g− 1 DW. All measurements included appropriate blank controls and standard calibration procedures and were conducted with three biological replicates.

RNA extraction and transcriptome analysis

RNA-seq was performed on root samples treated with 250 mM NaCl (0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h, n = 3). The RNA concentration, purity, and integrity of the samples were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. Then, mRNA was isolated, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Adapters were ligated to both ends of the cDNA, and the adapter-ligated PCR products were purified for library quality control. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. The entire experimental process was conducted by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw RNA-seq data were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject accession number PRJNA1393928.

Clean reads from each sample were separately aligned to the S. cannabina reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0) [33]. The S. cannabina reference genome and annotation files were obtained from the Figureshare platform (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.Figureshare.24420706.v1) [34]. The mapping rate ranged from 78.63% to 95.77% (Table S1). Gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM and normalized as transcripts per million reads (TPM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was identified using the DESeq2 package (http://bioconductor.org/packages/stats/bioc/DESeq2/) based on raw read count data, and genes with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.01 were considered DEGs. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed using R scripts, and GO enrichment analysis was performed using Goatools (https://pypi.org/project/goatools/). GO terms and KEGG pathways with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched among the DEGs.

Establishing and analysis WGCNA

WGCNA was employed to investigate co-expression relationships between genes and phenotypic traits and to facilitate the identification of hub genes within the network [22]. Based on the filtering criteria of TPM > 1 and coefficient of variation > 0.1, a total of 17,116 DEGs were retained for WGCNA. The soft-thresholding power was determined according to the scale-free topology criterion [35]. When the scale-free topology fit index approached saturation at 0.75, the soft-thresholding power was selected to construct the adjacency matrix. The soft-thresholding power was set to 20 to construct a scale-free co-expression network (Figure S1). The minModuleSize, minKMEtoStay and the mergeCutHeight were set on 30, 0.3, and 0.25,respectively. Correlations between module eigengenes and physiological traits were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Modules with |r| > 0.6 and p < 0.01 were defined as salt-responsive modules, and key genes within these modules were identified based on high module membership (MM) and gene significance (GS) [24]. The gene co-expression network was visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.3.

Establishing and analysis TO-GCN

TO-GCN analysis was used to characterize temporal co-expression patterns across different time points and to infer putative associations between genes [25]. DEGs with TPM > 1 were used for TO-GCN analysis. Pairwise co-expression connections were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC), and the threshold for network construction was set to 0.91. According to the methods of Chang et al. [25] and Zhao et al. [36], bait genes were selected from TFs identified by MFSelector as monotonically downregulated during salt treatment. Finally, the hierarchical structure of the transcriptome-based co-expression network was constructed using a breadth-first search algorithm [36]. The gene co-expression network was visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.3.

qRT-PCR validation of RNA-seq

20 DEGs, including 4 TFs and genes involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and plant hormone signal transduction, were selected from the RNA-seq dataset for qRT-PCR validation. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qRT-PCR was performed on a qTOWER3G real-time quantitative PCR system (Analytik Jena) using cDNA as the template and Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The β-actin gene of S. cannabina was used as the internal reference gene [37]. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with three biological replicates for each sample [38]. Pearson correlation analysis was performed using the Log2 fold-change values of these genes to verify the reliability of the transcriptome data.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences among groups, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test, with a significance level of p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Figures were created using GraphPad Prism 9.5.

Results

Effects of salinity stress to S. cannabina

To investigate the effect of salt stress on S. cannabina, seedlings were treated with 250 mM NaCl for 48 h, and phenotypic changes were observed at 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h. After 6 h of salt treatment, S. cannabina seedlings exhibited visible wilting symptoms, likely associated with osmotic water loss. Partial recovery was observed after 12 h. However, with prolonged salt treatment, the cotyledons gradually turned yellow and fell off (Fig. 1A). Salt stress led to an increase in Na+ content and a decrease in K+ content in the roots, resulting in a significant decline in the K+/Na+ ratio (Fig. 1B–D). REC and MDA content also increased significantly, indicating that ion homeostasis and membrane integrity in the roots are affected (Fig. 1E, F). Meanwhile, soluble sugar content and SOD activity in S. cannabina roots increased significantly after 6 h of salt stress (Fig. 1H, J). As the duration of salt stress increased, soluble protein and proline contents, as well as the activities of POD, CAT, and APX, also increased significantly at 24 h and 48 h (Fig. 1G, I,K–M). These results suggests that S. cannabina displayed a dynamic response to salt stress, characterized by early osmotic and oxidative responses followed by later antioxidant and osmoprotective responses.

Transcriptome analysis and identification of DEGs

After filtering out adapters, poly-A/T sequences, and low-quality reads, a total of 682,291,022 clean reads were obtained, corresponding to 102.16 Gb of clean data. The Q20 and Q30 were above 98.00% and 95.00%, respectively, and the GC content ranged from 43.16% to 44.65% (Table S1). Principal component analysis (PCA) and sample correlation analysis showed high consistency among biological replicates within each of the five time points, indicating that the transcriptome data were reliable (Fig. 2A, B).

Transcriptome analysis of S. cannabina under salt stress. A PCA analysis. B Correlation analysis between samples. C Numbers of upregulated and downregulated DEGs in different comparisons. D Venn diagram of DEGs at different time points under salt stress

A total of 21,422 DEGs were identified by comparing the 0 h group with each salt-treatment time. In the four comparisons, 13,578 (6,336 up-regulated and 7,242 down-regulated), 15,444 (7,433 up-regulated and 8,011 down-regulated), 12,312 (5,712 up-regulated and 6,600 down-regulated), and 10,330 (5,278 up-regulated and 5,052 down-regulated) DEGs were found, respectively (Fig. 2C). Among these, 5,652 DEGs were differentially expressed at all time points, including 2,427 up-regulated genes and 3,189 down-regulated genes (Fig. 2D). To validate the reliability of the identified DEGs, 20 genes were selected for qRT-PCR analysis. The expression trends of these genes were consistent with the RNA-seq results (Figure S2), and Pearson correlation analysis showed a strong correlation between RNA-seq and qRT-PCR datasets (r = 0.86, R2 = 0.75, p < 0.01) (Figure S3). Detailed primer information is shown in Table S2.

Identification of key modules by WGCNA

A total of 17,116 DEGs were ultimately clustered into 17 co-expression modules (Fig. 3A). The MEbrown, MEblack, and MEturquoise modules showed significant positive or negative correlations with several physiological traits (|r| > 0.6, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3B). MEturquoise was rapidly induced at 6 and 12 h after salt treatment and was mainly enriched in plant hormone signaling transduction, MAPK signaling pathway, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis. This module was closely associated with REC, MDA, soluble sugars, and SOD, indicating its possible involvement in the early response to salt stress. In contrast, MEbrown and MEblack were significantly elevated mainly at 24 and 48 h after salt treatment. These two modules were enriched in glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis, and were closely associated with proline, soluble proteins, POD, CAT, APX, and Na+ and K+ contents, suggesting their involvement in the late-stage responses to salt stress (Fig. 3C). These results indicated that S. cannabina roots underwent distinct transcriptional reprogramming at different stages of salt stress.

TO-GCN construction and analysis

To investigate how DEGs were dynamically regulated under salt stress, the LED family transcription factor gene SC.4BG001384.V1 was selected as a bait gene for the construction of TO-GCN (Fig. 4A). A total of 12,655 DEGs, including 870 TFs, were divided into 10 hierarchical networks (Levels 1–10) (Table S3). The temporal expression heatmap revealed three distinct stages: the normal stage (0 h), early stage (6 h and 12 h), and late stage (24 h and 48 h) (Fig. 4B, C). Levels 1–4, containing 193 TFs and 2,334 non-TFs, were highly expressed at 0 h and showed decreased expression after salt treatment. Levels 5–7, containing 330 TFs and 3,867 non-TFs, exhibited increased expression at 6 h and 12 h, suggesting their association with early responses to salt stress. Finally, Levels 8–10, containing 175 TFs and 2,021 non-TFs, showed the highest expression at 24 h and 48 h, corresponding to the late-stage response to salt stress.

A joint analysis was performed between genes in the MEturquoise, MEbrown, and MEblack modules and early- and late-stage genes identified by TO-GCN. Enrichment analysis showed that in the early-stage genes were mainly enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, and plant hormone signaling transduction. In contrast, late-stage genes were primarily enriched in glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and downstream metabolic pathways (Figure S4).

Salt stress induces glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and pyruvate metabolism

A total of 33 genes involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and pyruvate metabolism were significantly upregulated during the early stage, especially at 6 h and 12 h, and their expression gradually decreased at later time points (Fig. 5A). To identify TFs potentially associated with the early activation of these pathway-related genes, we constructed a co-expression subnetwork by integrating these genes with TFs assigned to the same TO-GCN stage. The subnetwork contained TFs from several major families, including MYB-related (20), MYB (9), AP2/ERF (8), and WRKY (8), suggesting that these families may participate in the transcriptional coordination of early glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and pyruvate metabolism under salt stress (Fig. 5B). Based on integrated network topological parameters, including MM, degree, and betweenness centrality, four TFs were selected as candidates: SC.1BG005084.V1 (AP2/ERF, ERF71), SC.4AG002835 (bZIP, bZIP9), SC.6AG004798.V1 (MYB, PHR1), and SC.1AG000847.V1 (BES1/BZR1, BES1) (Table S4). In addition, promoter analysis of the pathway-related DEGs identified several cis-elements associated with the TF families of the four select candidates, including ABRE, MBS, and ERE elements (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that the four selected TFs may be involved in the transcriptional coordination of early salt-responsive glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and pyruvate metabolism in S. cannabina.

Salt stress induces plant hormone signal transduction

A total of 39 DEGs associated with plant hormone signal transduction showed significant transcriptional changes under salt stress. Most of these genes were strongly induced at 6 h and 12 h, reaching their highest expression levels during the early response stage (Fig. 6A). The corresponding co-expression subnetwork contained multiple TF families, including MYB-related (19), MYB (12), bZIP (10), and WRKY (7), indicating that these TFs may contribute to the transcriptional coordination of plant hormone signaling transduction under salt stress (Fig. 6B). Based on TF ranking within the co-expression subnetwork and promoter cis-element information, SC.6AG000595.V1 (B3, ARF19) and SC.6AG004798.V1 (MYB, PHR1) were identified as candidate TFs potentially associated with the transcriptional coordination of plant hormone signal transduction (Table S4, Fig. 6C).

Salt stress induces the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and its downstream pathways

A total of 70 DEGs associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and its downstream pathways were markedly induced by salt stress, and most of these genes reached their highest expression levels at 24 h and 48 h (Fig. 7). The constructed co-expression network showed that several TFs families, including WRKY (12), MYB (11) and NAC (9), were co-expression with genes involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and downstream metabolic pathways (Table S5). Based on the co-expression subnetwork analysis, SC.1BG003236.V1 (WRKY, WRKY53), SC.3BG002583.V1 (WRKY, WRKY24), and SC.6BG005369.V1 (WRKY, WRKY28) were selected as candidate TFs associated with the phenylpropanoid-related subnetwork (Figure S5A, Table S4). Furthermore, cis-elements analysis of the promoter regions of these DEGs revealed W-box elements, which are potential binding sites for WRKY TFs. These findings suggest that the three WRKY genes may be associated with the transcriptional coordination of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and its downstream pathways in S. cannabina under salt stress (Figure S5B).

Salt stress induces glutathione metabolism

In this study, a total of 25 genes related to the glutathione pathway were identified as salt-responsive genes. Compared with 0 h, the expression levels of genes encoding GST, PGD, G6PD and gshA were significantly increased at 24 h and 48 h. The subnetwork showed that TFs from the MYB (13), NAC (9) and WRKY (9) families were co-expressed with genes involved in glutathione metabolism (Fig. 8A). In addition, cis-element analysis of the promoter regions of DEGs revealed the presence of W-box and MYB-binding elements, further suggesting that SC.5BG001307.V1 (MYB, MYB39), SC.1BG003236.V1 (WRKY, WRKY53), and SC.6BG005369.V1 (WRKY, WRKY28) may be associated with transcriptional coordination of glutathione metabolism under salt stress (Fig. 8B–D).

Discussion

High salinity can disrupt physiological and metabolic processes in plants, thereby effecting their growth and development [39]. As organs directly exposed to saline environments, roots can rapidly sense salt stress and initiate a series of adaptive responses [40]. In this study, salt stress led to Na+ accumulation and a decrease K+ content in the roots, while REC and MDA levels increased. These changes indicate that salt stress caused membrane damage, oxidative stress, and ion imbalance in S. cannabina roots (Fig. 1B–F). To investigate dynamic changes in the root physiology and transcriptional reprogramming in S. cannabina under salt stress, we inferred potential regulatory networks combined WGCNA and TO-GCN analysis.

In the early stages of salt stress, plants usually shift carbon use from growth to defense and produce osmoprotective compounds to alleviate osmotic stress and maintain redox balance [41]. Soluble sugars act as osmoprotectants that help maintain cellular homeostasis. In addition, they can function as signaling molecules involved in plant growth and development [42]. In our study, soluble sugar content significantly increased at 6 h and 12 h under salt stress, which was accompanied by the recovery of S. cannabina from a wilted state to an erect posture, suggesting that carbon metabolic pathways in S. cannabina may have undergone similar changes (Fig. 1A, G). Previous studies have shown that soluble sugars can enter glycolysis after conversion into hexoses, thereby providing carbon substrates for glycolysis [43, 44]. Glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and pyruvate metabolism are major carbon metabolic pathways that provide energy and carbon skeletons for downstream metabolic processes [45]. Salt stress stimulates the phosphorylation of glycolysis-related proteins in the halophyte Lycium barbarum, thereby enhancing energy supply and supporting the maintenance of cellular homeostasis [46]. In the halophyte Salvadora persica, the expression of glycolysis-related genes significantly increases under salt stress, suggesting that growth slows down and energy is reallocated to the biosynthesis of osmolytes [47]. Similar regulation of carbon metabolism under salt stress was also observed in alfalfa [48]. In this study, genes encoding key enzymes involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and pyruvate metabolism were significantly upregulated at 6 h and 12 h (Fig. 5A). These results suggest that carbon metabolic pathways in S. cannabina roots are rapidly adjusted during the early response to salt stress. On the one hand, soluble sugars are actively accumulated to regulate cellular osmotic pressure and maintain water uptake. On the other hand, glycolysis may be enhanced to provide energy and carbon skeletons for downstream metabolic pathways. The expression of genes encoding these enzymes can be regulated by TFs such as MYB, bZIP, NAC, and ERF [49–51]. In this study, ERF71, bZIP9, PHR1, and BES1 were co-expressed with genes involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and pyruvate metabolism (Table S4, Fig. 5B, C).

Early-stage genes were significantly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction under salt stress (Figure S4). Thalmann et al. [52] suggested that abscisic acid (ABA)-regulated starch degradation contributes to soluble sugars accumulation and osmotic stress tolerance, indicating that ABA signaling may be associated with the regulation of early carbon metabolism. ABA signaling is an important pathway in plant responses to abiotic stress, and studies in Arabidopsis thaliana and Limonium bicolor have shown that ABA signaling is closely related to plant stress responses [53, 54]. Under salt stress, genes encoding components of the PYL-PP2C-SnRK2 signaling module showed peak expression at 6 and 12 h, suggesting that the ABA signaling may participate in early salt responses in S. cannabina (Fig. 6A). JAZ proteins are core repressors in the jasmonic acid (JA) signaling pathway. Previous studies have shown that ABA receptor PYL proteins can interact with JAZ proteins to regulate the reprogramming of plant metabolism and growth [55]. In addition, salicylic acid (SA) and ethylene signaling pathways are also closely related to plant stress responses and show crosstalk with the ABA and JA signaling pathways [56].

Auxin, cytokinin, and brassinosteroid (BR) signaling pathways play important roles in root growth and development. GH3 and SAUR are typical early auxin-responsive genes and may be involved in SA/ABA-mediated plant defense response under salt stress [57]. Type-B ARRs are core transcriptional regulators of cytokinin signaling [58]. Studies have shown that LpARR10 may enhance salt tolerance in Arabidopsis by activating the SOS1/SOS3 pathway [59]. The high expression levels of BSK, BZR1/2, and TCH4 further indicate that BR signaling may contribute to early growth regulation, cell wall modification, and stress adaptation [60]. In this study, hormone signaling-related genes were significantly upregulated at 6 h and 12 h under salt stress (Fig. 6A), suggesting that S. cannabina may coordinate root growth and stress resistance through multiple hormones signaling pathways during the early stages of salt stress. A similar phenomenon has also been observed in the roots of the halophytic Clerodendrum inerme, suggesting that plant signal transduction may contribute to the complex regulatory network underlying salt stress resistance in Clerodendrum inerme [61]. In addition, TFs are involved in regulating hormone signaling and hormone-related gene expression under salt stress, and MYB, WRKY, NAC, and AP2 family members have been reported to be associated with ABA and JA signaling pathways [62–65]. In this study, ARF19 and PHR1 were co-expressed with genes involved in plant hormone signal transduction, suggesting that these TFs may be associated with early hormone-mediated responses under salt stress (Table S4, Fig. 6B, C).

At the late stage of salt stress, the contents of proline and soluble protein, as well as the activities of POD, CAT, and APX, increased. These changes indicate that osmotic protection and antioxidant defense were continuously enhanced during the late response. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs at the late stage of salt stress were mainly enriched in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, isoflavonoid biosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism pathways (Figure S4). Phenylpropanoid metabolites includes phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and downstream pathways, such as flavonoid and isoflavonoid biosynthesis, and plays an important role in plant responses to salt stress [66]. PAL, 4CL and C4H are pivotal enzymes in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and play essential roles in the production of various downstream phenylpropanoid metabolites [67]. CAD and POD are key enzymes in lignin biosynthesis. CAD and POD genes have been reported to be significantly upregulated under abiotic stress, thereby enhancing lignin accumulation and maintaining cell structure and integrity [68, 69]. CHS and CHI are key enzymes involved in flavonoid and isoflavonoid biosynthesis. As non-enzymatic antioxidants, flavonoid can help reduce oxidative damage [70, 71]. In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to the role of isoflavonoid metabolism in maintaining ROS homeostasis and promoting plant stress adaptation. In plants such as Medicago sativa and Lotus japonicus, isoflavonoid metabolism is significantly induced under salt stress and contributes to enhance salt tolerance [65, 66]. In this study, genes associated with phenylpropanoid, flavonoid, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis were significantly upregulated at the late stage of salt stress (Fig. 7). NAC and AP2/ERF family members have also been shown to regulate lignin and flavonoid biosynthesis [72, 73]. In this study, WRKY53, WRKY24 and WRKY28 were co-expressed with genes involved in phenylpropanoid, flavonoid, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis (Table S4, Figure S5A, B).

Glutathione metabolism is an important component of plant redox regulation under salt stress. GSH participates in ROS detoxification, which enzymes such as GST, G6PD, and PGD are closely associated with antioxidant defense and NADPH-dependent redox balance [74]. In the halophyte Hordeum marinum H559, the expression levels of glutathione S-transferase-related genes under salt stress were significantly higher than those in two less salt-tolerant genotypes, suggesting a stronger capacity for ROS detoxification and oxidative damage mitigation [75]. In this study, glutathione metabolism was enriched during the late phase of the response. Genes encoding GST, gshA, G6PD, and PGD were significantly upregulated (Fig. 8A), indicating that antioxidant protection may be enhanced during the late stage of salt stress. In addition, a co-expression subnetwork between glutathione metabolism-related genes and TFs was constructed. WRKY53, MYB39 and WRKY28 were co-expressed with glutathione metabolism-related genes, suggesting that these TFs may be associated with the transcriptional coordination of glutathione metabolism under salt stress (Table S4, Fig. 8B, C).

In the early phase of the response to salt stress, carbon metabolic reprogramming and hormone-mediated signaling pathways are rapidly induced to maintain osmotic regulation, energy supply, and signal transduction. As the stress persists, the response gradually shifts toward sustained antioxidant protection and maintenance of cell structure, as reflected by the enhanced reactive oxygen species scavenging and the upregulation of genes associated with glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and downstream flavonoid and isoflavonoid pathways. However, this study has some limitations. First, time-matched control samples were not included during salt stress treatment, and therefore potential effects of time-dependent factors, such as circadian rhythms, cannot be completely excluded. Second, only a single genotype was used in this study, and the relevant conclusions need to be further validated in additional S. cannabina genotypes. Third, this study mainly focused on short-term responses to salt stress, and further research is needed to investigate the mechanisms of long-term salt stress adaptation. Fourth, the networks constructed in this study were mainly inferred from co-expression relationships and temporal associations. Therefore, the putative regulatory associations inferred between candidate transcription factors and downstream genes require further experimental validation.

Conclusions

High salinity is a severe threat to plant growth and development. This study integrated physiological analyses with time-series transcriptome sequencing to analyze the dynamic of S. cannabina to salt stress, aiming to reveal its underlying adaptive mechanisms. By constructing WGCNA and TO-GCN networks, we identified three modules significantly associated with physiological traits and revealed the potential molecular mechanisms underlying salt stress responses. This transition from early metabolic and signaling adjustment to late-stage defense-related metabolic reprogramming may represent a coordinated dynamic adaptation strategy in S. cannabina roots under salt stress. This study is the first to integrate WGCNA and TO-GCN with time-series transcriptomic analysis in S. cannabina, providing a preliminary map of its salt stress response network and an important data foundation for the in-depth analysis of putative transcriptional regulatory mechanisms under stress conditions.
