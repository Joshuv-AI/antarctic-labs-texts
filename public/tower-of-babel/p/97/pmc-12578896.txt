Elucidating the mechanism by which Aloe-emodin from Cassiae semen treats diabetic retinopathy via network pharmacology and experimental verification
Authors: Jing Chen, Daiying Zhou, Lina Zhang, Xiangxiang Zhang, Zhigang Lv, Na He, Jing Bao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract The aim of this study was to investigate the possible mechanism by which Cassiae Semen treats DR via network pharmacology and to validate this mechanism via in vitro experiments. The active ingredients and potential DR-related targets of Cassia Semen were screened using the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) platform and other disease-related databases. A protein‒protein interaction network for these common targets was subsequently established via the STRING database. Key active ingredients and core targets were selected through network topology analysis using Cytoscape software. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the DAVID database. In vitro experiments were designed to further validate the key active components and targets of Cassiae Semen identified by network pharmacology analysis. Twenty-one common targets were selected. Aloe-emodin (AE) was speculated to be the main active ingredient of Cassiae Semen in the treatment of DR. Cassiae Semen might act through various biological processes, including by regulating apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation. In vitro, AE effectively inhibited lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced BV2 cell proliferation and downregulated the mRNA expression of M1 microglia-related genes (COX2, iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6). Furthermore, AE inhibited proinflammatory cytokine (TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6) secretion and the expression of the inflammatory protein COX2 in LPS-stimulated BV2 cells. Network pharmacology and in vitro experiments revealed that AE, the key active component of Cassiae Semen, suppresses inflammation by inhibiting M1 microglial polarization, making it a potential therapeutic agent for DR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21963-x.Keywords: Diabetic retinopathy, Cassiae semen, Network pharmacology, Microglia polarization, InflammationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Drug discovery

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a chronic retinal disease caused by hyperglycemia and is a common and severe microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus (DM)1,2. DR can lead to visual impairment and potentially irreversible vision loss, severely affecting DM patients’ quality of life. The effective treatment methods for DR mainly include laser and anti-VEGF therapies but have several limitations, such as financial burdens, obvious side effects and long treatment durations3–5. Thus, more people have turned their attention to herbal medicine, seeking safer and more cost-effective complementary medicine for treating DM and DR6,7. Chronic inflammation acts as a critical driver of DR progression. Under hyperglycemic conditions, macrophage homeostasis is disrupted, leading to the activation and M1 polarization of macrophages. M1 macrophages secrete abundant proinflammatory factors and proangiogenic stimuli, which promote abnormal endothelial cell activation, resulting in vascular leakage, pathological angiogenesis, and retinal cell damage8,9. Thus, M1 polarization represents an important research focus for identifying strategies to prevent and treat DR.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulations have been widely used to treat and prevent DM and DR. Sun et al. reported that the injection of traditional Chinese medicines significantly improved the clinical effectiveness in patients with DR10. Li et al. reported that Mimeng flower decoction could ameliorate DR11. Moreover, Wang et al. explored the antihyperglycemic effects of Rhizoma Coptidis and its main component berberine and their possible mechanisms of action12.

Cassiae Semen is the dry and mature seed of the leguminous plants Cassia obtusifolia and Cassia tora, which are among the traditional herbs classified by the Chinese Ministry of Health as “medical food homologues”13. It is plain, bitter and salty in flavor and has a slight natural cooling effect, with meridian tropism in the liver, kidney and intestine. Its multifaceted functions include liver detoxification, vision improvement, wind and heat dispelling, liver and kidney nourishment, and intestinal moisturization to alleviate constipation. Evidence has shown that Cassiae Semen can exert a variety of pharmacological effects, such as lowering blood glucose, alleviating hyperlipidemia and high blood pressure, and relieving oxidative stress14–17.

Recent pharmacological studies revealed that Cassiae Semen has significant preventive and therapeutic effects on diabetes and its complications, as well as various ophthalmic diseases18–20. Xu et al. reported that Obtusin—an extract derived from Cassia seeds—significantly lowers blood glucose levels, ameliorates dyslipidemia, increases the retinal layer thickness, attenuates oxidative stress, and suppresses the elevation of inflammatory cytokine levels in diabetic mice, supporting the therapeutic potential of Cassiae Semen in DR21. Additionally, Cassiae Semen-derived compounds have been shown to exert protective effects in dry eye disease and glaucoma models, primarily by inhibiting inflammatory signaling pathways and alleviating oxidative stress22,23. Our research group previously found that polysaccharides isolated from Cassia seeds protect human retinal endothelial cells under high-glucose conditions by reducing ROS production and activating the antioxidant HO-1/Nrf2 pathway, further underscoring the role of Cassiae Semen in alleviating diabetes-related endothelial damage20. However, the effects of Cassiae Semen against DR have not been fully elucidated. The aim of this study was to clarify the effective pharmaceutical constituents and principal targets of Cassiae Semen in the treatment of DR using network pharmacology methods, and to further investigate and validate the molecular mechanism of action of aloe-emodin (AE), the predicted key component of Cassiae Semen, through in vitro experiments, providing novel evidence and theoretical guidance for the application of TCM-derived active ingredients in DR therapy. Results

Shared Cassiae Semen-DR targets

To systematically elucidate the mechanism of action of Cassiae Semen in the treatment of DR, we first identified its bioactive ingredients and potential targets using the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) database. A total of 14 active pharmaceutical ingredients (Table 1), corresponding to 64 potential targets were selected (Fig. 2). Simultaneously, 1476 DR-related targets were retrieved from multiple public databases including the GeneCards, TTD, DisGeNET, and DrugBank databases. By cross-referencing the drug-related targets with DR-related targets, we identified 21 overlapping targets, which represent potential key molecules in the treatment of DR by Cassiae Semen.

Key ingredients and target prediction

To elucidate the core bioactive compounds in Cassiae Semen and their targets in the treatment of DR, a compound–target network was constructed for the 21 common targets shared between Cassiae Semen and DR, along with the 11 active ingredients of Cassiae Semen (Fig. 3A). Further topological feature analysis revealed that MOL000471 AE was the most highly connected node, suggesting its central role as a key bioactive component of Cassiae Semen in the treatment of DR. Subsequently, the common targets were uploaded to the STRING platform to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network (Fig. 3B). Using the CytoHubba plug-in of Cytoscape, the top 10 core proteins were extracted based on the maximal clique centrality (MCC) algorithm (Fig. 3C). These core targets included CASP3, TP53, PTGS2, TNF, IL1B, ESR1, MAPK14, PRKCA, PRKCD and NOS2. These hub genes encode proteins with critical roles in inflammation, apoptosis, oxidative stress, and metabolic regulation, including Caspase-3, tumour protein 53, COX2, TNF-α, IL-1β, estrogen receptor1, mitogen-activated protein kinase 14, protein kinase Cα, protein kinase Cδ and iNOS. These findings suggest that Cassiae Semen, particularly through AE, may mitigate DR pathology by simultaneously targeting multiple inflammatory, apoptotic, and oxidative stress pathways.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed to explore the potential functions of the 21 DR-related target genes of Cassiae Semen. The results of the GO enrichment analysis are illustrated in biological process, cell component, and molecular function terms.

Using the DAVID database, 116 biological process terms were enriched, and the top 20 terms with the highest degree values were visualised (Fig. 4A). The results indicated that the potential mechanisms of Cassiae Semen in the treatment of DR are predominantly associated with various biological processes, including apoptosis, the inflammatory response, hypoxia, and the regulation of insulin secretion. In terms of cell components, 13 terms with a frequency ≥ 2 are presented (Fig. 4B), and the enriched cell component terms mainly included the cytosol, membrane, plasma membrane, endoplasmic reticulum and macromolecular complex. Among the molecular functions, twenty terms had a frequency ≥ 2 (Fig. 4C). The enriched molecular function terms included enzyme binding, protein binding, and protease binding.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the DR-related targets of Cassiae Semen. (A) Significantly enriched biological process terms. (B) Significantly enriched cellular component terms. (C) Significantly enriched molecular function terms. (D) Top 20 significantly enriched KEGG pathways. A-D were sorted according to -log P values (P < 0.05).

In addition, a total of 68 metabolic pathways were identified, and the top 20 enriched KEGG pathways are shown in Fig. 4D. Notably, significant enrichment was detected in the AGE-RAGE signalling pathway, the IL-17 signalling pathway, the lipid and atherosclerosis pathway, the MAPK signalling pathway, neurodegeneration pathways associated with various diseases, and the TNF signalling pathway. These results suggest that Cassiae Semen may ameliorate DR through multiple pathways involving inflammation, apoptosis, metabolic regulation, and neuroprotection.

AE inhibited the activation and proliferation of LPS-induced BV2 cells

LPS is always used to mimic inflammation and induce apoptosis in vitro. On the basis of previous studies, BV2 cells were treated with 1 µg/ml LPS to induce the secretion of inflammatory-related cytokines24–26. BV2 cells were co-treated with LPS (1 µg/ml) and various concentrations of AE for 12 h, and cell viability was measured via the CCK8 method, as shown in Fig. 5. No significant difference was observed between the LPS group and the group treated with AE at concentration below 4 µM. However, AE effectively inhibited LPS-induced BV2 cell activation and proliferation at concentrations ranging from 4 to 10 µM, with statistical significance. Although a non-monotonic response was observed at 8 µM, which may reflect inherent biological variability or complex concentration-dependent effects, the overall trend demonstrated clear growth inhibition at higher AE concentrations. Based on these findings and supported by previous reports, cells treated with 10 µM AE were selected as the model group and used in subsequent experiments.

AE reduced the mRNA levels of M1 microglia markers

To further investigate the effect of AE on the regulation of BV2 cell polarization, we measured the levels of iNOS, an M1 microglia biomarker, and proinflammatory cytokines including COX2, TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6. Proinflammatory cytokine levels were significantly elevated after LPS treatment for 12 h, which indicates that the M1 polarization of BV2 microglia was successfully induced by LPS treatment. After AE treatment, the mRNA expression of iNOS was significantly lower than that in the LPS group, and the mRNA levels of proinflammatory cytokines (COX2 and TNF-α) were decreased (Fig. 6A). AE significantly decreased LPS-induced IL-1β and IL-6 production in BV2 cells (Fig. 6B).

Effect of AE on the mRNA expression of COX2, iNOS and TNF-α (A) and IL-1β and IL-6 (B) after LPS treatment. The LPS + AE group was compared with the LPS group, and the LPS group was compared with the control group. **P < 0.01. ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

AE decreased the protein levels of M1 polarization markers

To further demonstrate the above results in BV2 cells, the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 were measured by ELISA. As shown in Fig. 7A, increased levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the cell culture medium were observed in the LPS group, which demonstrated that stimulation of BV2 cells with LPS induced M1 polarization. However, AE significantly reduced the release of these inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, Western blot analysis demonstrated that LPS increased the expression of COX2, whereas AE inhibited its expression (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

Network pharmacology leverages high-throughput omics data and network topological structure analysis and is characterized by its holistic and systematic approach. The processes include active ingredient screening, potential component identification, pharmacological activity prediction and drug‒gene‒disease co-module analysis. These methods have progressively expanded the modern scientific understanding of TCM. Clinical studies have confirmed that TCM prescriptions have significant therapeutic effects on DR27. However, the molecular pharmacological mechanism by which Cassiae Semen treats DR is unknown. Therefore, we constructed a multilevel network to further clarify the underlying regulatory mechanism by which Cassiae Semen protects against DR.

In this study, 21 common targets of Cassiae Semen-DR were identified, with the top 10 targets including COX2, iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-1β, which act not only as inflammatory mediators but also as important biomarkers of M1 microglial polarization. Moreover, GO enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes were associated with the apoptotic process and the response to hypoxia and inflammation, which was also consistent with the pathological mechanism of DR28–30. Furthermore, we predicted that AE is the key ingredient of Cassiae Semen involved in in the treatment of DR. AE is a natural anthraquinone compound with numerous pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, antiapoptotic, anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties31–33. Previous studies have shown that AE alleviates sepsis-related tissue damage, inflammation, and oxidative stress34. AE prevents nerve injury and neuroinflammation caused by ischaemic stroke via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and NF-κB pathways35. Moreover, AE inhibits microglial pyroptosis and induces the polarization of M1 to M2 microglia in ischaemic injury model animals36. Research has shown that AE inhibits hypoxia-induced retinal neovasculogenesis via the HIF-1α/VEGF pathway, providing theoretical support for the treatment of AE in DR37.

GO enrichment analysis revealed that the inflammatory process was a top enriched biological process term. TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 exhibit cytotoxic properties, leading to the downregulation of components associated with the tight junction protein complex while simultaneously promoting the production of VEGF in DR38. Inhibition of the IL-1β pathway prevents the development of DR in diabetic animals39. According to the KEGG pathway enrichment results, AE may modulate DR by regulating multiple pathways, such as the AGE-RAGE and MAPK signalling pathways. The accumulation of AGEs results in increased mitochondrial production of free radicals, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress28. These findings indicate that the AE affects DR via multiple pathways and multiple targets. In the present study, the levels of inflammatory molecules, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, were dramatically decreased under AE stimulation, as measured by ELISA. Therefore, we confirmed the ability of AE to suppress the inflammatory response.

Traditionally, the clinical diagnosis and grading of DR rely on retinal microvascular changes, reinforcing the notion that DR is solely a microvascular complication of diabetes. This perspective is now being challenged, and DR is increasingly recognized as a disease that affects retinal neurovascular units (NVUs)38,40. Recent studies revealed that chronic retinal inflammatory injury and neurodegeneration manifest earlier than microangiopathy does38,41,42. Inflammatory processes, including resident immune cell activation and the production of inflammatory molecules, are found in the retinas of diabetic patients in the early phases of DR43,44. TNF-α and IL-1β have been implicated in contributing to key pathologic endpoints in DR, including capillary drop-out and vascular hyperpermeability39,45,46. Chronic hyperglycaemia is a critical factor in the polarization of microglia and the secretion of inflammatory mediators, with microglial changes manifesting prior to the onset of microvascular abnormalities9,38. Abnormal activation of microglia has been identified as a critical initiating factor in NVU dysfunction, which then leads to the development of DR47,48. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the effects of AE on inflammation regulation mediated by microglia in DR. Microglia are crucial immune cells in retinal tissue characterized by their high plasticity and rapid responsiveness to alterations in the microenvironment. These cells exhibit a dual phenotype process, known as microglial polarization, which encompasses both proinflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) states9. During DR development, retinal microglia switch their polarization from M2 to M1 in response to a constant proinflammatory insult49, with consequent secretion of deleterious cytokines related to retinal neurodegeneration, including COX2, iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-650. Agents such as LPS and γ-interferon can induce the M1 polarization of microglia. We investigated the significant upregulation of both the mRNA and protein expression of the aforementioned markers in BV2 cells after LPS treatment. These findings of this study are consistent with these in vitro observations9. Consequently, inhibiting microglial M1 polarization can effectively mitigate retinal inflammation, alleviate oxidative stress, and suppress neovascularization and is therefore a novel strategy for preventing the progression of DR.

Despite the prospective findings, this study had several limitations. First, in vitro models may not comprehensively reflect the complex processes of inflammation in vivo. Further validation in animal models and clinical trials is necessary for translating the findings of this study into practical therapeutic applications. Second, although we elucidated how AE regulates inflammatory factors, other components of Cassiae Semen may exert effects against DR. Further studies on other components are encouraged to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of Cassiae Semen and evaluate its potential therapeutic effects on DR.

In conclusion, the results demonstrate that 10 µM AE inhibited the LPS-induced proliferation of BV2 cells; downregulated the relative mRNA expression of COX2, iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6; and decreased the expression of the corresponding proinflammatory proteins. These findings confirm that AE, a significant natural constituent of Cassiae Semen, possesses anti-inflammatory biological activity and holds promise as a potential therapeutic agent for DR by modulating the M1 polarization of microglia.

Conclusion

We identified AE as the primary active constituent of Cassiae Semen involved in DR treatment and screened 10 core targets through network pharmacology analysis. In in vitro cellular experiments, we confirmed that AE downregulated the mRNA and protein expression of the predicted targets, including COX2, iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. AE inhibited the LPS-induced M1 polarization of BV2 cells, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for the use of Cassiae Semen in the prevention and management of DR. However, further research and clinical studies are necessary to explore and validate its therapeutic potential.

Materials and methods

Screening the active ingredients and targets of Cassiae semen

We screened the TCMSP database (http://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php)51 to determine the active pharmaceutical ingredients and targets of Cassiae Semen using the following criteria: oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug likeness (DL) ≥ 0.1852. All the targets were submitted to the UniProt protein database (https://www.uniprot.org) for validation of their gene names53. Cytoscape 3.9.0 software was used to generate a network diagram of the targets of Cassiae Semen.

Cassiae semen ingredient-DR target acquisition

The key word “diabetic retinopathy” was searched in the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), DisGeNET (https://disgenet.com/), DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/), and Therapeutic Targets (TTD) databases (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). After applying filters and removing duplicate entries, a list of DR-related targets were obtained.

Key ingredients and target prediction

The shared DR-related and Cassiae Semen targets were imported into the STRING 11.5 platform54 to construct a protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network. The common targets, their corresponding active ingredients and the above PPI network were imported into Cytoscape. Then, a topology analysis of the network nodes was subsequently performed to select the most active pharmaceutical components in the network. Ten key targets were obtained through the Cytohubba plugin.

Functional enrichment analysis

To enrich the functions of the predicted targets, we further performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis via the DAVID database (2021 update)55–57. The criterion for filtering was P < 0.05. We then visualize the obtained information with histograms and bubble charts via bioinformatics software.

Experimental verification

Cell culture and treatment

Mouse microglial BV2 cells (Mcellbank Biotechnology, China) were cultured overnight in high-glucose DMEM (Biological industries, Israel) supplemented with 10% foetal calf serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (PS) (Gibco, USA) under standard conditions at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. The control group was maintained under standard culture conditions. Aloe-emodin (AE) (Shanghai Source Leaf Biological Company, China) was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). In the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (MedChemExpress, USA) and AE groups, BV2 cells were treated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 12 h in 96-well plates. The AE group was treated with different concentrations (1, 2, 4, 6, 8 or 10 µM) of AE at the same time as LPS (1 µg/mL) treatment for 12 h. Given that the vehicle control (PBS at the volume used) was confirmed to have no measurable impact, its data were omitted from presentation.

Cell viability assay

Cell proliferation was measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Dojindo, Japan). Then, 10% CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm by spectrophotometry.

Measurement of the mRNA expression of inflammation-related genes

Total cellular RNA was extracted using RNA-Solv Reagent (Omega Bio-tek, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from each RNA sample using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was used to measure mRNA expression using TB Green Fast qPCR Mix (Takara, Japan). Interleukin 6 (IL-6), interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β), tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX2) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (Beijing Tsingke Biotech, China) expression levels were normalized to those of the endogenous control glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) via the 2 − ΔΔCt cycle threshold method. Measurement of inflammatory factor levels

The concentrations of IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). BV2 cell suspensions from each experimental group were centrifuged at 4 °C and 3000 rpm for 20 min, after which the supernatant was collected. The concentrations of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were determined in accordance with the ELISA kit instructions (Jingmei Biological Technology, China). The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a multifunction microplate reader (Rayto, China).

Western blot analysis

The expression levels of COX2 were assessed through Western blotting. Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer with protease inhibitors, and the concentration of each protein sample was quantified using a BCA assay. An equal sample (20 µg/lane) was subsequently separated on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for one hour at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies against COX2 (1:1000 dilution, Proteintech, China) and β-actin (1:2000 dilution, Proteintech, China) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse/rabbit secondary antibodies at room temperature for one hour. After incubation and repeated washes, the expression of COX2 and β-actin was visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) Western blotting detection kit (NCM Biotech, China). The protein bands were visualized with a ChemiDoc system (Bio-Rad, USA). The grayscale values of the bands were analysed using ImageJ software (v1.52a, National Institutes of Health, USA).

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated in triplicate. Statistical analysis and mapping were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v9.0, GraphPad Software, USA). One-way ANOVA was used for comparisons between multiple groups, Tukey’s method was used for multiple comparisons of components, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
