Effects of COVID-19 pandemic on incidence of asthma exacerbation in an urban population
Authors: Sonya S Henry, Kevin E Duong, Michael D Cabana, Tim Q Duong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe long-term effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on asthma exacerbations across sociodemographic groups is unknown. We conducted a retrospective cohort study of 162,113 asthma patients within the Montefiore Health System from March 2018 to February 2024 to evaluate pandemic-related changes in exacerbation risk across demographic, socioeconomic, and social determinant subgroups. Difference-in-differences logistic regression compared pre-pandemic (2018–2020) and late-pandemic (2022–2024) periods by age, sex, race and ethnicity, income, insurance, and unmet social needs. Interrupted time series regression assessed level and trend changes in March 2020, with sensitivity analyses confirming robustness. Before the pandemic, exacerbation odds were higher among Black, Hispanic, and Other race patients, females, Medicaid-insured individuals, and children. After pandemic onset, risk increased disproportionately for patients with unmet social needs, children, males, Medicaid, and low-income groups, while racial and ethnic disparities persisted. Interrupted time series showed immediate decreases among children and adults, with subsequent rebound to baseline in children but sustained suppression among adults. The pandemic was associated with widened socioeconomic disparities, a transient reduction in pediatric exacerbations, and a lasting decline among adults, underscoring the need for interventions addressing socioeconomic and social drivers of asthma outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41311-x.Keywords: asthma exacerbation, COVID-19, socioeconomic status, health disparitiesSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic profoundly disrupted global healthcare delivery and posed unique challenges for managing chronic diseases, including asthma1. Asthma is one of the most prevalent chronic respiratory conditions, particularly in urban and underserved populations, and is influenced by environmental exposures, healthcare access, and socioeconomic factors2. Previous pandemics, such as the 2009 H1N1 influenza outbreak, demonstrated that respiratory viral infections can exacerbate asthma symptoms and increase healthcare utilization for asthma3. Similarly, the COVID-19 pandemic introduced biological and social stressors that likely altered patterns of asthma exacerbation and care.

Effective asthma management requires routine outpatient visits, appropriate pharmacotherapy, and regular monitoring of symptoms and triggers2,4. During the early COVID-19 pandemic, public health measures, including lockdowns, school closures, and social distancing, have led to reductions in viral transmission and outdoor air pollution, which might have contributed to early decreases in asthma exacerbation rates reported in some settings5. However, these potential benefits were offset by disruptions in access to primary and specialty care, delays in preventive services, and hesitancy to seek in-person medical attention due to fear of SARS-CoV-2 exposure6. The net effects are unknown.

Importantly, the pandemic disproportionately impacted urban and socioeconomically disadvantaged communities, which already bear a high burden of asthma-related morbidity. In the Bronx, for example, asthma prevalence and hospitalization rates are among the highest in the United States7. The pandemic intensified pre-existing disparities by worsening housing instability, food insecurity, transportation barriers, and gaps in insurance coverage8. Such social determinants of health may have compounded the risk of uncontrolled asthma and exacerbations during the pandemic, particularly in marginalized groups. Despite these concerns, relatively few studies have comprehensively examined how the COVID-19 pandemic influenced asthma outcomes in racially and socioeconomically diverse urban populations, nor how unmet social needs have shaped disparities in asthma care and outcomes during this period. Furthermore, the long-term impact of pandemic-related healthcare disruptions on asthma exacerbations remains unclear.

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study aims to investigate trends in asthma exacerbations before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic using real-world data from the Montefiore Health System, which serves a large, diverse population in the Bronx, one of the earliest U.S. epicenters of the pandemic. The study will evaluate how exacerbation rates varied over time, and how these trends interacted with social determinants of health, including age, race/ethnicity, insurance status, income level, and documented unmet social needs. The findings may inform efforts to build more resilient and equitable asthma care systems in anticipation of future public health emergencies.

Methods

Data sources

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Einstein-Montefiore Institutional Review Board (2021–13658). Our data came from the Montefiore Health System, which consists of multiple hospitals and outpatient clinics in the Bronx, the lower Hudson Valley, and Westchester County. Informed Consent was waived by the Albert Einstein College of Medicine-Montefiore Medical Center Institutional Review Board (#2021–13658) with an exception for HIPAA waiver. All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Electronic medical records were extracted automatically between March 2018 and February 2024 as described previously9–15. We validated this COVID-19 dataset by routine chart reviews of all key variables on a selected group of patients. The data consisted of 162,113 unique asthma patients.

Variables

Asthma and asthma exacerbations were defined by ICD-10 codes J45.1-J45.9. Specific codes for exacerbations included J45.21, J45.31, J45.41, J45.51, and J45.901. Demographic variables (age, sex, race, and ethnicity) were tabulated.

Social determinants of health (SDOH) data, including neighborhood-level median incomes, insurance coverage, and unmet social needs, were also extracted. The Montefiore-Einstein Social Determinants of Health Screen, a standardized instrument adapted from the Health Leads Toolkit (https://healthleadsusa.org/), has been offered by healthcare providers since 2016. Completion of the one-page screener is voluntary. The screener assesses 8 domains of unmet social need: housing, food insecurity, utilities, health, transportation, medications, child or elder care, legal services, and family safety. Based on this screener, patients were categorized as having either no unmet social needs or at least one unmet social need.

Annual median household income was assigned by matching the patient’s ZIP code to U.S. Census Bureau data (https://data.census.gov). Income was categorized into quartiles based on the study population distribution: Q1 <$38,066; Q2 $38,066–$48,472; Q3 $48,472–$63,048; and Q4 >$63,048. Insurance status was obtained from the electronic medical record and categorized as private, Medicaid, Medicare, self-pay, or missing/uninsured.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of asthma exacerbations, expressed as the percentage of exacerbation events (per 100 asthma patients annually). Exacerbation rates were calculated for yearly periods defined as March through February (rather than calendar years), with each patient contributing once to the denominator if active during that period. Differential changes in rates following the COVID-19 pandemic were evaluated across sociodemographic subgroups (race/ethnicity, sex, age group, income quartile, insurance type, unmet social needs) using difference-in-differences (DiD) logistic regression comparing pre-pandemic (March 2018–February 2020) and late-pandemic (March 2022–February 2024) periods. This DiD framework was utilized to isolate the disproportionate impact of the pandemic on specific vulnerable subgroups by comparing their change in risk over time against the change observed in a reference group.

The secondary outcome was overall temporal changes in monthly asthma exacerbation rates per 100 patients, assessed using interrupted time series (ITS) analysis with March 2020 as the interruption point.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using Python version 3.13.1. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Asthma exacerbation rates were computed monthly and annually from March to February to align with pandemic interruption timing in our region (March 2020). Patients with missing or unknown values for income, insurance, or unmet social needs were excluded from subgroup-specific models.

Difference-in-differences was implemented using logistic regression because the outcome was binary, defined as the presence or absence of at least one exacerbation within each study period. To satisfy the requirement of each patient contributing once to the denominator, data were collapsed to a single observation per patient for the pre-pandemic and post-pandemic periods. Models were unadjusted, as the primary goal was subgroup stratification rather than causal inference. Regression models included main effects for the post-pandemic period, sociodemographic subgroup indicators, and their interactions. By utilizing an interaction term between the study period and the subgroup indicator, the model effectively accounts for both baseline group differences and general temporal trends that affected the entire population. Total post-pandemic odds ratios for each subgroup were derived from the logistic regression models by summing the subgroup main effect and its interaction with the post indicator on the log-odds scale and exponentiating the result. 95% confidence intervals and P values for these estimates were calculated using the model covariance matrix to obtain the variance of the combined coefficients.

Interrupted time series analysis used ordinary-least-squared regression with heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors to evaluate temporal changes in monthly exacerbation rates per 100 patients. Autocorrelation was assessed using Durbin–Watson and Ljung–Box tests, as well as autocorrelation function plots, and HAC corrections were applied accordingly. For this analysis, March–May 2020 was excluded due to healthcare disruptions at the pandemic onset. All model adequacy was assessed through residual diagnostics and formal tests.

Sensitivity analysis

As sensitivity analyses, we conducted unadjusted difference-in-differences logistic regression models comparing asthma exacerbation odds across sociodemographic subgroups using multiple year-pair comparisons spanning pre-pandemic and post-pandemic onset interruption periods (Supplementary Fig. 1). This assessed whether the observed disparities were consistent across different time windows. We also tested alternative interrupted time series specifications, including models with varied exclusion windows (no exclusion, March-April only, March-June) (Supplementary Table 1) and used autoregressive integrated moving average models with exogenous covariates (ARIMAX) (Supplementary Table 2). ARIMAX models explicitly handled autocorrelation in the error structure, with model selection guided by the Akaike Information Criterion.

Results

Regarding median annual household income, 27.12% were in the lowest quartile (<$38,066), 26.57% in the second quartile ($38,066 to $48,472), 23.78% in the third quartile ($48,472 to $63,048), and 18.68% in the highest quartile (>$63,048). For social needs, 4.46% had at least one unmet need, 24.79% had no unmet needs, and 70.75% had no available records.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of primary findings. Difference-in-differences models using multiple year-pair comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 1) showed consistent results, with racial and ethnic disparities remaining stable while socioeconomic and social determinant disparities (Medicaid, income, unmet needs) widened across all time windows. Interrupted time series models with varying exclusion periods (no exclusion, March–April, March–June) confirmed significant immediate level drops in children and adults across specifications (Supplementary Table 1). ARIMAX models that explicitly accounted for serial correlation produced estimates similar in magnitude and direction to the primary ITS ordinary-least-squared model with HAC (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study of asthma exacerbation trends in a diverse urban, Bronx population from 2018 to 2024 identified significant variation by age, sex, race and ethnicity, insurance type, income, and social determinants of health, consistent with the influence of multiple underlying factors. Seasonal patterns were evident, with exacerbation peaks in September and October and troughs in June and July. These patterns were abruptly disrupted at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, with a marked early decline in exacerbation rates. Interrupted time series analyses demonstrated immediate reductions in exacerbation rates among both children and adults, with a larger initial decline in children. However, by 2023, only pediatric rates rebounded toward baseline, while adult rates remained persistently lower.

Pre-pandemic, disparities in asthma exacerbation risk were evident, with higher odds among Black and Hispanic patients, female individuals, children, Medicaid beneficiaries, those in the middle-income quartiles, and individuals with unmet social needs. In contrast, Medicare beneficiaries and those in the lowest income quartile had lower odds of exacerbation. Following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, racial and ethnic disparities remained stable; however, difference-in-differences analyses revealed disproportionately greater increases in exacerbation risk among patients with Medicaid coverage, unmet social needs, lower or middle income, and pediatric age. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings. Overall, the pandemic disrupted baseline patterns and disproportionately affected vulnerable subgroups, amplifying existing socioeconomic disparities in asthma outcomes.

A more granular examination using interrupted time series modeling delineated distinct recovery trajectories by age group, highlighting a return to baseline among children and persistently suppressed exacerbation rates among adults. This sustained reduction among adults is supported by prior literature. The Characteristics, Treatment and Outcomes With Severe Asthma in the United States (CHRONICLE) study of U.S. subspecialty-treated asthma patients reported sustained reductions in asthma exacerbations, emergency department visits, and hospitalizations from April 2020 through mid-202116. Similarly, a population-based analysis in Ontario found that acute care utilization among adults with asthma remained below projected levels during the first pandemic year, despite reduced outpatient and pulmonary function testing services17. In Hong Kong, a territory-wide analysis reported a 53% decline in asthma-related admissions between January and April 2020 compared to the 2015–2019 average, with reductions linked to increased masking and seasonal temperature changes18. Together, these findings suggest that reductions in adult asthma exacerbations observed during the pandemic were sustained across settings and may be attributable to behavioral adaptations (e.g., universal masking, remote work), environmental changes, and reduced viral transmission.

Pre-pandemic racial disparities in asthma exacerbation risk remained largely stable after COVID-19 onset. This persistence, rather than amplification, may reflect entrenched baseline inequalities, such that pandemic-related disruptions did not substantially shift relative risk. Our findings occurred within a broader context in which the pandemic disproportionately affected marginalized populations,19,20. and are consistent with prior research demonstrating the influence of socioeconomic status and social determinants of health on asthma outcomes21,22. Other studies have shown that racial disparities in asthma-related emergency department use did not worsen during the pandemic despite overall reductions in visit volume, possibly owing to protective factors such as masking, remote work, and expanded telemedicine access23. Nevertheless, the continued presence of these disparities highlights the enduring impact of systemic barriers that persisted despite substantial changes in health care delivery.

In contrast, the pandemic disproportionately worsened outcomes among economically vulnerable populations. Medicaid beneficiaries, who already faced elevated pre-pandemic risk, experienced widening disparities following pandemic interruption. This trend may reflect heightened financial strain, reduced medication access, and limited preventive care among safety-net populations. Income-related patterns mirrored these findings- while pre-pandemic disparities were mostly limited to the second income quartile, post-pandemic periods showed elevated odds of exacerbation among the lowest and lower-middle income groups. Thanik et al. reported that families with public insurance were significantly more likely to report indoor asthma triggers, income loss, and housing instability compared to privately insured counterparts24. Publicly insured families were also less likely to have access to remote work, more reliant on public transportation, and more likely to test positive for SARS-CoV-2 despite lower testing rates24.

The most pronounced disparity amplification occurred among patients with unmet social needs. Individuals with at least one unmet need —such as housing, food insecurity, or transportation—faced both elevated baseline risk and a substantial increase in exacerbation burden after the pandemic onset. These findings are consistent with longstanding evidence that racial and ethnic minority populations in the U.S. face disproportionate asthma burden due to systemic inequities, including higher exposure to environmental triggers (e.g., mold and pests), housing instability, and limited access to preventive care25,26.

The pandemic’s amplification of economic disparities underscores the critical role of systemic and structural factors in asthma management. Addressing these inequities requires targeted interventions, including improved access to preventive care, enhanced asthma education programs, and policies addressing broader social determinants of health. Our findings highlight the pandemic’s role as both a disruptor and a magnifier of inequities. Examining the relationship between social determinants of health and long COVID could shed light on the likely challenges faced by this population and could help to address the nuanced needs of individuals experiencing prolonged health consequences post-SARS-CoV-2 infection27.

Limitations

There are several limitations. Our patient cohort is diverse, consisting of large proportions of underserved minorities, and our findings may not be generalizable to other populations that are less diverse. Larger studies including multiple health systems would enhance generalizability. Median household incomes were based on ZIP codes, which might not accurately reflect individual patient income. Electronic medical records could have errors in documentation. Our records did not capture patients who went to urgent care outside of our health system for treatment of exacerbation and thus could underestimate the extent of exacerbation. However, we expect that this limitation is likely to affect similarly across time and should not affect the overall conclusions. As with any retrospective study, there could be other unintended patient selection biases and confounds.

Conclusions

The COVID-19 pandemic onset was associated with an immediate reduction in asthma exacerbation incidence, likely due to decreased exposure to triggers, followed by return to pre-pandemic exacerbation levels. However, the return to pre-pandemic exacerbation levels was uneven across population subgroups. While racial and ethnic disparities remained stable, marked disparities in recovery were observed among groups defined by income, Medicaid insurance, age, and unmet social needs, with these gaps widening during the pandemic period. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions that address social determinants of health, ensure equitable access to care, and support sustained preventive strategies to reduce disparities and improve asthma outcomes in diverse urban populations.
