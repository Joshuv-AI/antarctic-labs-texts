Total Synthesis of Rohitukine and Dysoline and Their Anticancer Analogues Flavopiridol and IIIM-290
Authors: Madhusmita Nayak, Kailas V. Kallurkar, Tejaswi Somarowthu, Jagadeesh Babu Nanubolu, Sandip B. Bharate
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rohitukine is a naturally occurring piperidine alkaloid that has led to the discovery of several potent CDK9/T1 inhibitors, including flavopiridol and IIIM-290. (−)-(3S,4R)-1-Methyl-4-(2,4,6-trimethoxyphenyl)­piperidin-3-ol serves as a crucial chiral intermediate in the synthesis of rohitukine and its analogues. In this paper, we describe the asymmetric synthesis of the chiral alcohol through the diastereoselective reduction of (+)-(R)-1-methyl-4-(2,4,6-trimethoxyphenyl)­piperidin-3-one in >90% yield and ≥99.5% de using N-selectride without the requirement of chromatographic purification. The (−)-(3S,4R) diastereomer intermediate was successfully employed for the gram-scale synthesis of rohitukine, flavopiridol, and IIIM-290. Additionally, herein, we report an acid-promoted ring-opening/ring-closure equilibrium process of the rohitukine core, giving thermodynamically more stable regioisomer dysoline. The CDK9/T1 inhibition study indicates that a piperidine ring at the C8 position of the chromone nucleus is crucial, as C6-regioisomers show significantly reduced or no inhibition. The developed method for producing clinically important piperidine alkaloids is straightforward, is scalable, and involves only a few chromatographic purification steps.

Introduction

Rohitukine (1a) is a piperidine alkaloid that primarily occurs in Meliaceae plants, Amoora rohituka, and Dysoxylum binectariferum. It is extensively studied for its pharmacological properties, including anticancer, antiinflammatory, antidyslipidemic, and gastroprotective activity. Despite numerous pharmacological properties, its unique ability to precisely occupy the ATP-binding pocket of protein kinases has been extensively studied. The medicinal chemistry efforts targeted at cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) on rohitukine pharmacophore have led to the discovery of anticancer clinical candidates, flavopiridol, riviciclib, TP-1287, voruciclib, and IIIM-290. Rohitukine and all its inspired candidates are potent inhibitors of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) with better selectivity for CDK9/T1. The regioisomer of rohitukine is dysoline (2a), which also occurs naturally in Dysoxylum binectariferum. Flavopiridol (3a) is the pioneering candidate in the kinase drug discovery program and is the first kinase inhibitor to enter a clinical trial. It has received orphan drug status from the FDA for CML and AML. , Its phosphate prodrug, TP-1287, is currently under clinical development as an oral drug in patients with advanced solid tumors. Piramal’s P276-00 (riviciclib) bears a pyrrolidine ring instead of piperidine, and it was investigated in clinical trials for several cancer types. MEI Pharma is pursuing the clinical development of another candidate, voruciclib, a structural analog of riviciclib. We discovered a semisynthetic derivative, IIIM-290 (4a), which inhibits CDK9/T1 and shows antiproliferative activity in pancreatic cancer, in vitro and in vivo. Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) have been crucial targets in cancer drug discovery, with significant medicinal chemistry efforts being undertaken worldwide leading to several molecules entering clinical trials. Among these, the chromone and flavone alkaloids represent an important class of CDK inhibitors, yet there are only a few reports on their synthesis. The gram-scale synthesis reported for flavopiridol is by Teleha and colleagues, who achieved the synthesis of racemic flavopiridol with an overall yield of 7%. The synthesis of rohitukine by Naik et al. does not include an enantioselective step to selectively introduce a chiral center and relies on the separation of alcohol diastereomers through column chromatography. The only total synthesis of dysoline (2a) is reported by Coffin and Ready that comprises a 12-step-long enantioselective procedure. In this paper, we present the gram-scale synthesis of the natural product rohitukine (1a) and its two analogues, flavopiridol (3a) and IIIM-290 (4a), utilizing N-selectride for asymmetric reduction with over 99% diastereomeric excess for the cis-(3S,4R)-isomer. Notably, this is the first complete synthesis of IIIM-290 (4a), which was previously obtained through a semisynthetic method from natural rohitukine. Additionally, we also report an unprecedented formation of dysoline (2a) from the rohitukine skeleton via chromone ring opening followed by cyclization with the p-OH group. Thus, the present study introduces a new approach for the total synthesis of dysoline. The absolute stereochemistry was established using single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Rohitukine, Flavopiridol, and IIIM-290

The synthesis began with the acid-catalyzed condensation of 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene (5) with N-methyl piperidone (6) in acetic acid, yielding an olefin derivative 7 in 94% yield. The olefin 7 underwent hydroboration with BH3, followed by oxidation with H2O2, producing arylpiperidinol rac- 8 with a yield of 72%. Swern oxidation of rac-8 produced arylpiperidone, rac-9, in 72% yield. The mixture of enantiomers, (R)-9a and (S)-9b, was separated via resolution with (+)-dibenzoyl-d-tartaric acid. The completion of the resolution was verified using chiral HPLC and by comparing the specific rotation with literature values. In the first round, two crops were collected, producing the desired (R)-9a isomer, which exhibited a specific rotation of +27° and had an enantiomeric purity of 80%, as indicated by chiral HPLC. Repeating the resolution process on the obtained crop resulted in the desired isomer (R)-9a, achieving greater than 99% enantiomeric excess and a specific rotation of +31.5° (c 1.0, MeOH), as previously reported (Lit. +31.27°). The optimization of the asymmetric reduction of (+)-(4R)-9a to its cis-alcohol, (−)-(3S,4R)-8a, is detailed in Table . DIBAL-H was used previously , for the reduction of the C3-ketone 9a to the corresponding alcohol, culminating in the formation of a mixture of cis- and trans-isomers (diastereomers 3S,4R-8a and 3R,4R-8c), which were subsequently separated by silica gel column chromatography. In our experiments, using DIBAL-H (2 equiv.) yielded diastereomers 8a and 8c in an 85:15 ratio (entry 2). The diastereomeric ratio of cis-8a and trans-8c was estimated through 1H NMR analysis, as the C3-proton appears at significantly different chemical shift values in the cis and trans isomers. Specifically, the 1H NMR chemical shift values were found to be 3.85 ppm for cis-(3S,4R)-8a and 4.42 ppm for trans-(3R,4R)-8c. This diastereoselectivity was further confirmed by chiral HPLC analysis, with results from both methods being consistent. In our attempts to separate the mixture of diastereomers 8a and 8c obtained via DIBAL-H using silica gel column chromatography, 43% yield of 3S,4R-8a was obtained (entry 4). Additionally, treating 9a with NaBH4 produced diastereomers 8a and 8c in a 49:51 ratio (entry 1). Recognizing the significance of obtaining the enantiomer 8a through asymmetric reduction, we explored a variety of reducing agents (Table ).

The successful results were achieved using borohydride reagents. The application of N-selectride (2 equiv.) at −78 °C for 16 h led to the exclusive formation of the cis-diastereomer 8a in a ≥99.5% de; however, a significant amount of the starting material remained unreacted, resulting in only a 9% isolated yield (entry 5). Increasing the equivalents of N-selectride to 10 resulted in a modest yield improvement to 30%, while still maintaining dr. Further optimization of the reaction temperature yielded promising results. By adding 10 equivalents of N-selectride at −78 °C and then allowing the reaction to continue at −20 °C for 8 h, we observed substantial improvement, achieving an isolated yield of 85% with a ≥99.5 de (entry 7). Additional adjustments, with the addition at −78 °C followed by a reaction at −5 °C for 4 h, resulted in an 89% yield (entry 8). It was further found that the addition of N-selectride at −78 °C was not necessary; conducting the entire process at −5 °C for 6–8 h provided a 91% yield of the product with ≥99.5 de (entries 10 and 11) using only 3 equiv. of N-selectride. Similar results were obtained with K-selectride (entry 13). However, considering the pricing difference between K- and N-selectride, the use of N-selectride is preferable. The lower than 3 equiv. of N or K-selectride produced lower yields. cis-(3S,4R)-8a exhibited a specific rotation of −54.13° (c 1.0, MeOH), consistent with the earlier report (Lit. −53.4°). The additional reduction experiments using rac-9 indicated that N-selectride selectively produces only cis-products with no formation of any trans-isomer, which was also proved via XRD analysis (Section S14 of the Supporting Information). Scale-up studies indicated that this diastereoselective reaction could be successfully done at the 25 g scale. While we were at the end of our investigations, a careful literature search revealed that selectrides were previously employed for cis-diastereoselective reduction of piperidinones, specific reports comprising the use of K- or L-selectrides in DME/THF solvents yielding >95:5 dr. The current approach demonstrated the use of a relatively cheaper N-selectride in THF solvent and a simple workup, affording cis-8a in yields of up to 91% and ≥99.5% de. The excellent cis-diastereoselectivity observed with N-selectride in the reduction of piperidine-3-one 9a to piperidine-3-ol 8a is due to its bulky, noncoordinating nature, which promotes hydride delivery from the less hindered axial face of the ketone, leading to formation of the equatorial alcohol and thus the cis-product (relative to the axial substituent at nitrogen). In contrast, DIBAL-H, being less sterically hindered and capable of coordinating with both the carbonyl oxygen and the ring nitrogen, allows hydride attack from both faces, resulting in reduced selectivity and a mixture of cis- and trans-diastereomers. Hence, the steric bulk and lack of chelation in N-selectride drive its superior cis-selectivity.

The acylation of arylpiperidinol (−)-(3S,4R)-8a with acetic anhydride in the presence of boron trifluoride etherate, followed by saponification, produced the acetophenone intermediate (+)-(3S,4R)-10a in 82% yield. The subsequent reaction of the hydroxy acetophenone (+)-(3S,4R)-10a with 2-chlorobenzoyl chloride 11 in pyridine yielded the benzoylated flavone (+)-12a via diketone intermediate that upon cyclization yielded a benzoyled chromone (+)-12a in 84% yield (from 10a). The debenzoylation of (+)-12a using MeOH and 50% NaOH afforded dimethyl-flavopiridol (+)-13a in 72% yield. Finally, demethylation of 13a using pyridine HCl resulted in the formation of (+)-3a (flavopiridol) in 71% yield (Scheme ).

The obtained acetyl intermediate (+)-10a was utilized for the total synthesis of rohitukine (1a) and IIIM-290 (4a), as depicted in Scheme . The reaction of hydroxy acetophenone (+)-10a with ethyl acetate and sodium metal provided the diketone intermediate, which was treated with concentrated HCl to afford the dimethoxy-chromone (+)-15a in 65% yield. The treatment of (+)-15a with pyridine.HCl yielded (+)-1a (rohitukine) in 65% yield. The Claisen–Schmidt condensation of (+)-1a with 2,6-dichloro benzaldehyde (16) afforded (+)-4a (IIIM-290) in 90% yield. The 1H and 13C NMR data of natural rohitukine perfectly match with synthetic rohitukine. The obtained specific rotation for 1a differs from that reported for natural rohitukine, likely due to differences in the degree and nature of salification, a known factor influencing optical rotation in piperidine alkaloids. The semisynthetic IIIM-290 and synthetic IIIM-290 display similarity in all aspects, including 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and specific rotation. Using the synthetic approach shown in Scheme , we were able to synthesize 1.5 and 1.96 g of flavopiridol and IIIM-290, respectively.

To ascertain the absolute stereochemistry, flavopiridol 3a obtained in Scheme was crystallized using methanol and the X-ray crystal structure was recorded (Figure ). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first single-crystal structure of flavopiridol, although there are several reports on its enzyme-bound cocrystallized structures (bound to glycogen phosphorylase, CDK9/T1, CDK2/A, CDK1/B, and bromodomain-containing protein 4). In X-ray crystal, 3a adopted monoclinic chiral space group C2 confirming its chiral nature. The asymmetric unit of the crystal contains two molecules (Z′ = 2) and 8.5 water molecules. Water molecule O9W has a partial site occupancy of 0.5, while the rest of the water molecules have a full site occupancy of 1.0. Water molecules O3W, O6W, O7W, and O8W were disordered over two sites and were modeled. The absolute configuration for molecule A (C1A, R and C2A, S) and molecule B (C1B, R and C2B, S) was established by anomalous dispersion effects in diffraction measurements on the crystal with the Flack parameter value refined to 0.02(3). Molecule 3a exists in a zwitterionic state, with a proton from hydroxyl O2A/O2B transferred to hydroxypiperidine nitrogen N1A/N1B of the hydroxypiperidine ring. This we say by locating the electron density for the proton on the nitrogen atom rather than on the hydroxyl oxygen atom. Second, there is a shortening in the C–O– bond (1.315(5) Å) compared to C–OH (1.350(4)­Å) once the proton is transferred. As a result of the anionic charge, the oxygen atom O2A/O2B is involved in stronger hydrogen bonding with the adjacent hydroxyl of the hydroxypiperidine ring (O1A–H···O2A, 2.523(4)­Å, 171°; O1B–H···O2B, 2.617(4), 159°). An interesting feature of this crystal structure is the presence of multiple water molecules in the crystal lattice, which bind with the molecules and also occupy the voids created by their close packing, by forming water channels along the b-axis, and thus play a crucial role in the stabilization of crystal itself (Figure ).

Acid-Catalyzed Demethylation and Rearrangement of Chromone

While executing the gram-scale synthesis of rohitukine (1a), we observed an intriguing phenomenon at the demethylation step (conversion of 15a to 1a). A side product with a similar UV chromophore (222, 265, and 328 nm) and a slightly higher TLC R f than rohitukine (R f 0.6 vs 0.4) was detected. Furthermore, the LCMS analysis of the reaction mixture revealed the same molecular weight as rohitukine (m/z 305) (Sections S17 and S18, Supporting Information). The isolation of the side product using silica gel chromatography followed by spectral characterization revealed it to be the regioisomer, dysoline (2a). The NMR data of 2a perfectly matched with the literature values. The formation of 2a from dimethyl rohitukine 15a was an unexpected observation as this transformation requires a cascade of reactions involving a key step of pyrone ring opening (C–O bond cleavage). The pyridine hydrochloride is highly hygroscopic in nature and easily captures moisture. The moisture captured by the pyridine hydrochloride is likely to be involved in the nucleophilic ring opening. The reaction of 15a when repeated using a fresh bottle of pyridine.HCl while handling it under an inert atmosphere, the formation of 2a as a side product was not observed (entry 1, Table ); however, we noticed the formation of monomethyl rohitukine 17a as a side product. The higher equivalents of pyridine·HCl resulted in the formation of a side product 2a (entries 3–4). However, the reaction of 15a with pyridine·HCl with the addition of 10 volumes of water resulted in the formation of a 37:63 ratio of 1a:2a (entry 5), clearly indicating the primary role of water as a nucleophile in the transformation of 15a to 2a. Furthermore, the use of higher equivalents of pyridine HCl and 10 volumes of water resulted in a 22:78 ratio of rohitukine:dysoline (entry 6). The use of quinoline as an additive in the reaction mixture yielded rohitukine as a major product (87:13 ratio of 1a:2a); however, when the reaction was continued for 16 h, a higher percentage of dysoline was formed (entries 7 vs. 8). Thus, the optimization studies presented in Table indicate that the use of 10 equivalents pyridine HCl for 3 h reaction time (entry 2) is useful for the synthesis of rohitukine whereas the 30 equiv. of pyridine HCl with 10 volumes of water (entry 6) is ideal for dysoline synthesis. A similar type of chromone rearrangement has been reported previously; however, those studies involved different core structures and were conducted under distinct reaction conditions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of such rearrangement occurring under pyridine·HCl-mediated conditions.

The plausible mechanism involved in the transformation of 15a to 2a is shown in Figure . The oxygen from aromatic methyl esters of dimethyl rohitukine (15a) undergoes protonation via HCl followed by the removal of the methyl group by pyridine to produce rohitukine (1a). The presence of moisture in the reaction or external addition of water in the acidic condition forms hydronium ion, which protonates the carbonyl group making it more electrophilic-centered and thus initiating chromone ring opening at a high temperature. The nucleophilic attack by OH2 at the C2 position of the pyrone ring produces a hydroxy intermediate III that stabilizes to a ring-opened keto–enol intermediate V. The protonation of the intermediate V followed by the ring closure preferentially with the C4–OH (thermodynamically more stable) group produces C2-hydroxy chromane intermediate VII that loses a water molecule to yield dysoline 2a, as depicted in Figure . The mechanism clearly shows that the transformation of dimethyl rohitukine 15a to dysoline 2a proceeds via rohitukine 1a as a key in situ intermediate. To prove this, we performed a reaction of pure rohitukine 1a under the optimized reaction condition (pyridine HCl and 10 V of water) and observed the formation of dysoline in 78% yield. Furthermore, to check whether dysoline also undergoes ring opening and forms rohitukine, we treated dysoline with pyridine HCl in the presence of water. Dysoline did undergo chromone ring opening and the formation of rohitukine 1a was observed (25: 75 ratio of 1a: 2a); however, the cyclization of the diketone intermediate preferred C4-OH because of the less stearic hindrance compared to that of C2-OH. Thus, the careful selection of the condition as optimized in Table can be employed in order to preferentially access either rohitukine or dysoline.

Plausible mechanism for pyridine·HCl-mediated demethylation to form rohitukine (1a) followed by water-mediated chromone ring opening and rearrangement to its regioisomer, dysoline (2a).

The structure of 5a was confirmed by the single-crystal X-ray crystal analysis (Figure ). This X-ray structure further validates the unprecedented transformation of dimethyl rohitukine 15a to dysoline 2a, as described in Figure . The rohitukine/dysoline regioisomeric pair exhibits key differences in chemical shift values. Notably, the aromatic protons C3-H and C6-H/C8-H and the piperidine C4′-H show significant variations. In Vitro and In-Silico Analysis for CDK9/T1 Inhibition

The synthesized piperidine alkaloids, rohitukine (1a), flavopiridol (3a), and IIIM-290 (4a), were evaluated for their CDK9/T1 inhibitory activity and compared with their respective reference compounds. The IC5 0 values of the synthesized molecules closely matched those of the standards, as shown in the dose–response curves in Figure A. To further explore preliminary structure–activity relationships, the inhibitory activities of both regioisomeric pairs, 1a vs 2a and 4a vs 5a, were compared. The regioisomer dysoline (2a) was found to be 40-fold less active than rohitukine (1a), while compound 5a, the regioisomer of IIIM-290, was completely inactive (IC5 0 >100 μM) compared to the highly potent activity of IIIM-290 (IC5 0 = 10 nM). To rationalize these differences, molecular docking studies were performed using the ATP-binding site of CDK9/T1 (PDB: 3BLR). As shown in Figure B, rohitukine (1a) fits perfectly into the active site of CDK9/T1, closely mimicking the binding pose of flavopiridol (the cocrystallized ligand of 3BLR). Notably, its C4-carbonyl and C5-hydroxyl groups establish a tridentate H-bonding network with CYS106, a key hinge region residue. In contrast, the binding orientation of its regioisomer, 2a, is distorted due to the altered position of the piperidine moiety on the chromone nucleus (shift from C8 to C6). As a result, the C4-carbonyl group of dysoline is displaced from the hinge pocket and does not participate in H-bonding with CYS106, being located at a distance exceeding 5 Å (Figure C). Like rohitukine (1a), its styryl analogue, IIIM-290 (4a), also perfectly occupies the active site wherein the C4-carbonyl offers H-bonding with CYS106 with a 1.95 Å distance. The orientation of the regioisomer of IIIM-290, 5a, is distorted completely in the active site with flipping of the chromone ring by 180°. Thus, there is no H-bonding of C4 carbonyl or C5-OH with the CYS 106 and its distance is more than 8 Å (Figure F) from the hinge pocket. The observed docking interactions of the 1a/2a and 4a/5a regioisomer pairs show a perfect correlation with their experimental IC5 0 values against CDK9/T1 (Figure B). Furthermore, we investigated whether similar differences in active site binding exist for other pairs of regioisomers. A comparative analysis of interaction patterns was performed for analogues of compound 4a, bearing various substitutions (halogen, hydroxyl, methoxy) on the styryl moiety, along with their corresponding regioisomeric counterparts. These studies consistently revealed trends analogous to those observed for the 4a vs. 5a pair. Collectively, these findings suggest that the precise positioning of the piperidinyl substituent on the chromone nucleus is critical for optimal binding orientation and effective occupancy within the active site of CDK9/T1 enzyme.

To evaluate the stability of docked complexes, 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed for both pairs of regioisomers. In each case, the C8-piperidinyl regioisomer exhibited sustained H-bonding with the hinge region of CDK9/T1. For the 4a/5a pair, isomer 4a demonstrated superior complex stability, reflected by lower protein RMSD (2.4 Å) and ligand RMSD (4.0 Å) values compared to 5a (P-RMSD: 4.5 Å; L-RMSD: 5.0 Å). Although the protein RMSF for 4a was slightly higher (7.2 vs. 5.6 Å), the ligand RMSF was lower (1.7 vs 2.0 Å), suggesting a more stable and consistent binding pose for 4a. Interaction fraction analysis further confirmed that 4a maintained persistent contacts (fraction > 0.9) with both CYS106 and ASP167, residues critical for CDK9/T1 inhibition. In contrast, 5a retained interaction with CYS106, but not through its C4/C5 functionalities; instead, the interaction occurred via the C7-hydroxyl group. Additionally, it formed a water-mediated contact with ASP109, a residue not critical for catalytic inhibition. The lack of consistent interaction with ASP167, along with the absence of C4/C5-mediated H-bonding with CYS106, likely accounts for the biochemical inactivity of 5a, while 4a’s stable engagement with both key residues support its potent inhibitory activity. In the 1a/2a pair, both isomers showed similar protein RMSD values (∼4.8 Å), indicating comparable overall protein stability. However, 1a exhibited a more stable ligand pose, as shown by lower ligand RMSD (4.2 Å) and RMSF (1.1 Å), compared to 2a (L-RMSD: 7.2 Å; L-RMSF: 1.9 Å). Notably, 1a maintained strong interactions with CYS106 and ASP109 and sustained a moderate ionic interaction with ASP167 (interaction fraction: 0.45). In contrast, 2a lacked stable interaction with ASP167 (fraction <0.05), attributed to the shift of the piperidinyl group from C8 to C6. Interestingly, 2a underwent conformational reorientation during the simulation, allowing it to establish stable H-bonding with CYS106 via its C4/C5 substituents, an interaction absent in its XP-docked pose (Supporting Information, Section S21). This dynamic adaptation may partially explain the moderate inhibitory activity observed for 2a. However, unlike 2a, its counterpart 5a from other pairs failed to reorient during the simulation to establish compensatory interactions with CYS106 via C4/C5 groups, which likely explains its complete lack of CDK9 inhibition. Overall, the MD simulation results are consistent with the biochemical data, highlighting the critical role of sustained interactions with CYS106 and ASP167 in determining the inhibitory activity of these regioisomeric compounds.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have reported a gram-scale synthesis of two clinically relevant piperidine alkaloids, flavopiridol and IIIM-290, with defined absolute stereochemistry wherein for IIIM-290, it is the first complete synthesis. This approach is practical, achieving an overall yield of 11–12% and involving chromatographic purification in only a few steps. A key aspect of the synthetic route is an asymmetric reduction step using N-selectride, which selectively produces the cis-isomer in >90% yield with ≥99.5% de, without the need for chromatographic purification, and it is scalable to multigram quantities. The unprecedented acid-catalyzed tandem transformation of dimethyl rohitukine to dysoline described herein provides a new route for the total synthesis of dysoline natural product. The impact of the change in the position of piperidine on the chromone nucleus on biological activity was also assessed. The gram-scale synthesis presented here has the potential to facilitate the preparation of these compounds at a kilogram scale for clinical development.

Absolute Stereochemistry by Single-Crystal XRD

X-ray data for VA74 (10a), KB1705 (3a), and KB1752 (5a) was collected at room temperature on a Bruker D8 VENTURE instrument with an IμS Mo microsource (λ = 1.54184 Å) and a PHOTON III C14 detector. X-ray data for the KB1705 were collected at room temperature on a Bruker D8 QUEST instrument with an IμS Mo microsource (λ = 0.71073 Å) and a PHOTON III C7 detector. The raw data frames were reduced and corrected for absorption effects using the Bruker Apex 3 software suite programs. The structure was solved using an intrinsic phasing method and further refined with the SHELXL , program and expanded using Fourier techniques. Anisotropic displacement parameters were encompassed for all non-hydrogen atoms. All C-bound H atoms were placed geometrically and treated as riding on their parent C atoms [C–H = 0.93–0.97 Å, and Uiso­(H) = 1.5Ueq­(C) for methyl H or 1.2Ueq­(C) for other H atoms]. The O bound H atoms were located in the difference Fourier map, and their positional coordinates were allowed to be refined. For modeling the structural disorder, PART, FVAR, DELU, SIMU, and DFIX instructions were utilized as appropriate and the site occupancies for the major and minor components were refined.
