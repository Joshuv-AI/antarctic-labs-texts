Rare primary small intestinal infection: a case report of Mycobacterium kansasii enteropathy in an immunocompetent patient and literature review
Authors: Lang Wu, Jiayao Wang, Jin Zhu, Teng Li, Yueying Chen, Limin Luo, Ye Zhang, Shoubin Ning, Bairong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are important opportunistic pathogens that most commonly infect the lungs. Primary involvement of the gastrointestinal tract—especially the small intestine—is exceedingly rare, and small-bowel infection caused by Mycobacterium kansasii (M. kansasii) has seldom been reported.Case presentationWe describe an extremely rare case of primary small-intestinal M. kansasii infection in an immunocompetent young man who presented with prolonged chronic diarrhea and fever. After an extensive but unrevealing diagnostic work-up, the etiology was finally established by microbial metagenomic sequencing of tissue obtained by double-balloon endoscopy. Building on the initial regimen of ethambutol hydrochloride, rifampicin, and clarithromycin—and with subsequent antibiotic adjustments tailored to the patient’s evolving symptoms—clinical symptoms resolved completely, and follow-up endoscopy showed mucosal improvement.ConclusionThis case underscores that NTM infection should be considered in the differential diagnosis of unexplained chronic gastrointestinal symptoms and highlights the pivotal role of modern molecular techniques in reaching a precise diagnosis. Detailed analysis of the case together with a review of the literature aims to raise clinicians’ awareness and improve management of this rare entity.

Introduction

Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are environmental acid-fast bacilli found worldwide in water, soil and dust [1, 2]. Although generally of low virulence, they can cause severe, chronic opportunistic infections in susceptible hosts—most commonly those with underlying pulmonary disease (e.g., bronchiectasis, COPD) or immunosuppression (e.g., HIV infection, organ transplantation, immunosuppressive therapy) [3, 4].

Pulmonary involvement accounts for the vast majority of NTM infections [1]. Extrapulmonary disease is uncommon and may affect lymph nodes, skin and soft tissue, bones and joints, blood, or disseminate systemically [5–7]. Gastro-intestinal (GI) NTM infection is particularly rare, usually occurring as part of disseminated disease in advanced AIDS; the typical organism in that setting is Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) [8, 9].

Mycobacterium kansasii (M. kansasii) is one of the most clinically significant NTM pathogens [10]. Since it shares major antigens with Mycobacterium tuberculosis, M. kansasii infection closely mimics pulmonary tuberculosis and is frequently misdiagnosed as such. While sporadic cases have been reported, primary, localized—especially small-intestinal—M. kansasii infection is considered an exceptional clinical event. To our knowledge, no fully documented report describing the affected small-bowel segment, detailed diagnostic work-up, and definitive treatment outcome in an immunocompetent patient has been published.

The rarity of this entity, combined with non-specific clinical features, often leads to prolonged diagnostic delay; patients may undergo repeated investigations and misdiagnoses for months or even years. We herein present a case of an immunocompetent young man with chronic diarrhea and fever who was ultimately proven to have primary small-intestinal M. kansasii infection. By detailing the clinical course, diagnostic strategy, and final outcome, and by reviewing the relevant literature, we aim to offer clinicians a new perspective when confronted with obscure chronic small-bowel disease.

Case report

A 23-year-old man was admitted on 29 April 2025 with a 9-month history of recurrent abdominal pain, diarrhea, and fever. Illness onset was 9 months prior to presentation, when he developed sudden severe periumbilical colic accompanied by profuse sweating; the pain subsided after oral analgesics. Within days, watery diarrhea (7–8 stools/day) and high fever (peak 38.6 °C) appeared without rigors. Over the ensuing 9 months the patient visited more than 10 hospitals and was hospitalized > 10 times.

Repeated laboratory work-ups showed elevated antistreptolysin-O (ASO); antinuclear antibody, five-item vasculitis screen (anti-proteinase 3 antibody, anti-myeloperoxidase antibody, anti-cardiolipin total antibody, anti-β2 glycoprotein 1 total antibody, anti-glomerular basement membrane antibody), and Epstein–Barr virus serology were all negative, five-item immunoglobulin profile (IgM, IgA, C3, C4 no abnormality observed; IgG↑) mild abnormality, while thyroid function was normal. T-SPOT.TB and PPD skin test were positive; nasopharyngeal swab suggested possible Mycoplasma. MRI of the pituitary and MR angiography of the brain were unremarkable, EEG was normal, and PET-CT revealed no space-occupying lesions. Cerebrospinal fluid LDH was 18 U/L, IgG 35.8 mg/L, with negative smears for fungi, bacteria and Cryptococcus. Stool “diarrhea four-item (Norovirus, Adenovirus, Rotavirus, Stool routine examination)” PCR panel was negative; growth hormone, two-item cortisol, insulin and C-peptide were normal. Anti-nRNP/Sm was weakly positive; dengue NS1 antigen/antibody, HSV-1, EBV DNA, “rheumatic seven-item” panel, autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, Clonorchis antibody, CMV antibody, M. tuberculosis antibody, four-item ANCA and full autoantibody screen were all negative. Chest CT, abdominal ultrasound, carotid plus lower-limb Doppler, and ocular ultrasound were normal. Multiple gastroscopies showed only chronic non-atrophic gastritis, and colonoscopies were repeatedly normal.

Empiric levofloxacin plus symptomatic treatment transiently improved abdominal pain and fever, but diarrhea persisted. From October 2024 he developed low-grade afternoon fever (peak 37.6 °C). Subsequent courses of ceftriaxone, azithromycin, piperacillin and penicillin, as well as short glucocorticoid bursts, brought no sustained benefit. In February 2025 presumptive anti-tuberculosis therapy (isoniazid 0.3 g qd plus rifapentine 0.45 g bid) was started; after more than two months symptoms remained unchanged.

Past history was unremarkable except for Helicobacter pylori (HP) infection and has undergone HP eradication therapy, anal fistula surgery. He denied any autoimmune disease or use of immunosuppressive drugs. On admission vital signs were stable, and physical examination of chest, heart and abdomen revealed no abnormalities.

Investigations at our hospital in May 2025 showed normal procalcitonin, IL-6 9.15 pg/mL, mild thrombocytosis, normal leukocyte and erythrocyte counts, CRP 12.59 mg/L, negative respiratory-virus and SARS-CoV-2 PCR, ASO 407 IU/mL, negative rheumatoid factor, negative Legionella antibody, weakly positive M. tuberculosis antibody, negative M. tuberculosis-specific IFN-γ release assay, and TBNK subset analysis revealing CD4/CD18 2.84%. CT enterography demonstrated no structural small-bowel disease. Double-balloon endoscopy achieved complete small-bowel visualization: the retrograde approach was normal; the antegrade approach revealed mucosal lesion (Fig. 1A). Biopsies showed chronic mucosal inflammation, negative CMV/EBER in-situ hybridisation, and negative acid-fast bacilli stain (Fig. 1B). Targeted metagenomic sequencing of small-intestinal mucosa detected HP and Mycobacterium kansasii (relative abundance 1.09%). Drug resistance genes detected: None detected. A diagnosis of primary M. kansasii enteritis was made. The patient received ethambutol hydrochloride (1 g qd) combined with rifampicin (0.6 g qd) and clarithromycin (0.5 g qd) for 2 months, after which fever subsided and diarrhea improved. Because diarrhea with low-grade fever recurred at the end of July, the regimen was adjusted to ethambutol hydrochloride, azithromycin, rifampicin, and isoniazid. Small-bowel endoscopy in August 2025 is shown in Fig. 2A and 2B. Targeted metagenomic sequencing of small-bowel mucosa no longer detected M. kansasii. After complete symptom resolution, a third surveillance endoscopy in November 2025 confirmed sustained endoscopic remission (Fig. 3) with no pathogens detected on repeated metagenomic analysis.

Discussion

The central value of this case lies in its extreme rarity. To the best of our knowledge it is the first fully documented report of primary small-intestinal M. kansasii infection occurring in an immunocompetent host. The 9-month diagnostic odyssey provides practical lessons and prompts wider reflection on NTM enteropathy, especially that caused by M. kansasii.

M. kansasii is a photochromogenic mycobacterium that typically causes pulmonary disease after inhalation of contaminated aerosols [11]. Contaminated water systems—especially urban distribution networks—are well-documented environmental reservoirs [12]. We hypothesize that our patient acquired the organism by drinking contaminated water, allowing direct mucosal colonization of the small intestine. The patient had no definitive systemic immunodeficiency, but had a history of HP infection, and NGS testing revealed positive intestinal HP. Although there is currently no evidence to suggest that HP directly induces NTM infection, a significant association exists between HP infection and the development or exacerbation of certain autoimmune diseases. Their shared mechanisms include molecular mimicry, chronic inflammation, epitope spreading, and cytokine dysregulation. HP shares pathways with other pathogen infections and may serve as a unifying factor in the pathogenesis of various autoimmune diseases [13]. We hypothesize that HP alters the gastric microenvironment and affects the mucosal immune system; these immunological and microbiota alterations may theoretically exert an indirect influence on the gastrointestinal tract’s ability to resist other pathogens. Therefore, the patient may have had localized intestinal mucosal immune dysfunction, or exposure to a large bacterial load at one time, leading to this rare clinical infection. The low relative abundance (1.09%) of M. kansasii in the biopsy most likely reflects sampling site and depth rather than organism burden. Unlike the disseminated MAC infection commonly seen in patients with severe immunodeficiency, this patient showed no evidence of pulmonary involvement. During the treatment course at our hospital, multiple sputum induction tests were performed for pathogen detection, with no respiratory secretions produced and no signs of systemic dissemination, supporting the diagnosis of “primary small intestinal infection.”

The clinical picture—cramping abdominal pain, watery diarrhoea and low-grade fever—overlaps with almost every small-bowel disorder, notably intestinal tuberculosis, Crohn’s disease, small-bowel lymphoma and coeliac disease. Repeated positive T-SPOT and PPD tests repeatedly steered us toward tuberculosis, yet chest imaging was clear and typical ulcerovegetative lesions of TB were absent. The endoscopic finding of pale, oedematous mucosa also raised consideration of coeliac [14, 15] and ischaemic enteropathy [16]. Neither the patient’s complaints nor the physical findings offered a single clue specific to M. kansasii infection. The final diagnosis was achieved only through a step-wise, multimodal strategy in which each technique built on the previous one. Double-balloon endoscopy was pivotal: it supplied direct visualization and, more importantly, reliably yielded tissue. Although conventional acid-fast staining of the biopsy was negative, the availability of mucosal samples allowed us to proceed to targeted PCR and metagenomic sequencing, ultimately providing the decisive evidence of M. kansasii DNA.

Management of M. kansasii infection is among the best-standardised of all NTM diseases. Current ATS/IDSA guidelines recommend a rifampicin-based triple regimen—rifampicin, ethambutol plus either isoniazid or a macrolide—for pulmonary disease caused by rifampicin-susceptible strains [17, 18]. Therefore, the patient was treated with rifampicin, ethambutol and clarithromycin. The prompt resolution of fever, marked reduction in diarrhoea, endoscopic mucosal healing and clearance of the organism on repeat sequencing confirm that this schema is equally effective for the rare entity of small-bowel infection. Another point worth noting is that clarithromycin, a commonly used drug for eradicating HP infection and treating NTM disease, inadvertently suppressed or eradicated HP in some patients, exerting a dual effect of drug selection and improving intestinal mucosal immunity. This may also be a key factor contributing to the patient’s clinical improvement after the addition of clarithromycin following diagnosis. Interestingly, the second biopsy showed intensified inflammation with focal villous atrophy; this is not a sign of therapeutic failure but most likely represents immune reconstitution—an expected “by-product” of successful antimycobacterial therapy.

Literature review yields only a single case of suspected Mycobacterium avium complex infection producing extensive jejunal atrophy, the mucosa appearing white, edematous and finely serrated [9]. Reports describing jejunal involvement by M. kansasii are virtually non-existent, underscoring the uniqueness and academic value of the present case. Strikingly, both patients had disease confined to the jejunum and shared almost identical endoscopic mucosal features—raising the intriguing possibility that pale, edematous mucosa features may be a shared endoscopic signature of certain NTM enteritides. Larger case series are required to confirm this hypothesis.

As a single-case report, our observations cannot be used to define the epidemiology or clinical spectrum of small-intestinal M. kansasii infection. The presumed water-borne route remains hypothetical, because environmental sampling was not performed and microbiologic proof of contamination is lacking. Moreover, despite an extensive immunologic work-up that revealed no overt defect, we cannot exclude an as-yet-unrecognised, unconventional, localised immune deficit that predisposed this otherwise healthy individual to infection.

Conclusion

We present the first meticulously documented case of primary small-intestinal M. kansasii infection in an immunocompetent host. By sharing this unique diagnostic and therapeutic journey we aim to broaden clinicians’ awareness of the expanding spectrum of NTM disease, minimise future misdiagnosis or oversight of such rare entities, and ultimately improve patient outcomes. Larger case series and prospective studies are now needed to elucidate the pathogenesis, clinical phenotype and optimal management of NTM enteropathy.
