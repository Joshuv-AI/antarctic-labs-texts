The ‘72-Hour Divergence’ in severe hyperbilirubinemia: sepsis is associated with altered renal and hematologic adaptation in a 10-year exchange transfusion cohort
Authors: Emine Ergül Sarı, Özgül Salihoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the association between concurrent sepsis and physiological recovery trajectories in neonates undergoing exchange transfusion (ET) for severe hyperbilirubinemia.Study designA 10-year longitudinal cohort study (2010–2020) of 54 neonates requiring double-volume ET. Patients were stratified into Sepsis (n = 26) and No-Sepsis (n = 28) groups. Renal and hematologic adaptation markers were analyzed over the first 72 h post-procedure.ResultThe initial severity of hyperbilirubinemia was comparable between the groups (admission TSB: 23.8 vs. 23.1 mg/dL; p = 0.42). Sepsis, predominantly driven by Klebsiella and E. coli, was identified in 48.1% of the cohort. While ET effectively lowered bilirubin in all infants, concurrent sepsis was associated with a “72-Hour Divergence” an altered physiological state characterized by severe thrombocytopenia (85 vs. 240 × 10³/µL; p < 0.001) and persistent creatinine elevation (1.1 vs. 0.4 mg/dL; p < 0.001) at 72 h. Furthermore, sepsis was linked to a prolonged hospitalization of 7.8 days (p < 0.001), independent of admission bilirubin levels.ConclusionSepsis is associated with altered renal and hematologic adaptation in neonates undergoing ET. The “72-Hour Divergence” represents a distinct high-risk phenotype requiring clinical vigilance beyond isolated bilirubin clearance.

Introduction

Despite universal screening programs and advances in phototherapy, severe neonatal hyperbilirubinemia requiring exchange transfusion (ET) remains a critical challenge, particularly in tertiary referral centers serving diverse populations [1, 2]. While the incidence of ET has declined in developed healthcare systems, it retains its status as a definitive “rescue therapy” for preventing kernicterus spectrum disorders (KSD) in infants presenting with fulminant jaundice [3, 4]. Contemporary literature has largely focused on the neurotoxic thresholds of bilirubin and the safety profile of the procedure itself [4–6]. However, this bilirubin-centric view often overlooks a “double burden” increasingly observed in high-acuity settings: the coexistence of sepsis and severe hyperbilirubinemia [7, 8].

In neonates with hemolytic disease, the physiological trajectory is often predictable. However, when hyperbilirubinemia is compounded by sepsis, the clinical picture becomes distinctively complex. Emerging data suggest that sepsis particularly multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteremia acts as a systemic disruptor, associated with organ dysfunction that extends far beyond the liver [9, 10]. Recent studies have identified specific windows of vulnerability, noting that sepsis-associated thrombocytopenia typically reaches its nadir between 48 and 72 h [9, 11], a timeline that dangerously overlaps with the delayed rise in serum creatinine seen in neonatal acute kidney injury (AKI) [12, 13].

We hypothesize that in neonates requiring ET, sepsis is associated with a phenomenon of “altered physiological adaptation,” where renal and hematologic adaptations fail simultaneously around the third day of admission. We operationally define this phenomenon as the “72-Hour Divergence.” Leveraging a comprehensive 10-year dataset (2010–2020) from a high-volume tertiary center, this study aims to: (1) define the microbiological profile of sepsis in infants undergoing ET, with a focus on Klebsiella and E. coli [10, 14]; and (2) characterize the “72-Hour Divergence” phenotype to determine how sepsis-associated organ dysfunction relates to length of stay and procedural morbidity [15, 16].

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Bakırköy Dr. Sadi Konuk Training and Research Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval Number: 2019-07-07). The study methodology and patient selection process adhere to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines, with the patient inclusion and exclusion cascade detailed in Fig. 1 (STROBE Flowchart). As a major government tertiary referral center, our unit manages complex neonates transferred from a wide network of peripheral hospitals and emergency departments. The study population included all neonates (≥ 35 weeks of gestation) who underwent double- volume exchange transfusion (DVET) for severe hyperbilirubinemia during the study period. Infants with major congenital malformations, chromosomal anomalies, or incomplete medical records were excluded. In total, 54 neonates met the inclusion criteria.

Clinical protocol and decision for exchange transfusion

Upon admission, a standardized diagnostic workup was performed for every neonate to identify the underlying etiology of hyperbilirubinemia. This mandatory screening panel included complete blood count (CBC) with peripheral smear, reticulocyte count, maternal and neonatal blood grouping, Direct Coombs Test (DCT), and quantitative glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) assay. Furthermore, given the high prevalence of comorbidities in our referral population, blood cultures and inflammatory markers (CRP) were routinely obtained prior to the procedure to rule out sepsis. The decision to perform DVET was strictly based on the total serum bilirubin (TSB) thresholds defined by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines applicable during the study period [5, 17].

Data collection and definitions

Demographic data, etiology of jaundice (ABO/Rh incompatibility, G6PD deficiency), and laboratory parameters (TSB, hemoglobin, platelet counts, serum creatinine) were extracted from electronic medical records.

Sepsis Stratification: Patients were stratified into two groups based on their sepsis status:

Sepsis group

Defined according to the NeoPIns criteria and EMA definitions [18]. This included: Proven Sepsis: Positive blood culture (bacteremia). Clinical Sepsis: Presence of clinical signs (hemodynamic instability, temperature instability) and abnormal inflammatory biomarkers (CRP > 10 mg/L, I/T ratio > 0.2) with a negative culture.

No-Sepsis (control) group

Infants undergoing ET solely for hemolytic or metabolic causes without clinical or laboratory evidence of infection.

The “72-Hour Divergence” Definition: The “72-Hour Divergence” was operationally defined as the simultaneous occurrence of a platelet nadir (< 150,000/µL) [9] and a persistent or rising serum creatinine level (AKI stage ≥ 1) at 72 h post-procedure [19]. To evaluate this, we analyzed the change (Δ) in markers between admission (Day 0) and Day 3.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or Student’s t-test depending on normality. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. To determine independent predictors of hospital length of stay (LOS), a multivariable linear regression model was constructed. Variables included in the model were sepsis status (binary), gestational age (continuous), admission TSB (continuous), and birth weight (continuous). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the 10-year study period (2010–2020), 54 neonates met the criteria for double-volume exchange transfusion (DVET). The cohort was predominantly male (n = 29, 53.7%) with a mean gestational age of 38.8 ± 1.4 weeks and a mean birth weight of 3207 ± 432 g. The etiological profile of severe hyperbilirubinemia was heterogeneous (Table 1). While ABO incompatibility remained the most frequent primary diagnosis (n = 31, 57.4%), comprehensive pre-procedural screening identified significant contributions from Rh isoimmunization (18.5%) and G6PD deficiency (5.6%). Notably, in 12 cases (22.2%), no specific hemolytic or metabolic cause could be identified (idiopathic) despite exhaustive workup.

Crucially, the admission Total Serum Bilirubin (TSB) levels were comparable between the Sepsis (23.8 ± 3.5 mg/dL) and No-Sepsis (23.1 ± 2.8 mg/dL) groups (p = 0.42). Furthermore, there was no significant correlation between admission TSB levels and admission platelet counts (Pearson r = 0.05, p > 0.05), confirming that both groups presented with a similar baseline hyperbilirubinemia burden prior to the procedure (Fig. 2).

The Sepsis group (n = 26) comprised neonates with culture-proven sepsis as well as those with strong clinical and laboratory evidence of systemic infection. Among the culture-positive cases, the microbiological profile was dominated by multi-drug resistant Gram-negative bacteria, primarily Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli. Notably, there was no statistically significant difference in the magnitude of Day 3 thrombocytopenia or creatinine elevation between the proven and clinical sepsis subgroups (p > 0.05), justifying their combined analysis as a single high-risk phenotype.

While DVET successfully reduced TSB levels significantly in all patients (p < 0.001), the physiological recovery trajectory diverged sharply based on sepsis status, a phenomenon we term the “72-Hour Divergence.”

At admission, platelet counts were comparable between groups. However, by Day 3 post-procedure, the Sepsis group exhibited a precipitous drop in platelet counts (85 ± 28 vs. 240 ± 42 × 10³/µL, p < 0.001), whereas the No-Sepsis group showed stability (Table 2). Similarly, while serum creatinine levels normalized rapidly in the No-Sepsis group following hydration and DVET, the Sepsis group demonstrated a persistent elevation or a secondary rise in creatinine by Day 3 (1.1 ± 0.3 vs. 0.4 ± 0.1 mg/dL, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). This divergence indicates a distinct physiological trajectory: despite the successful clearance of bilirubin, the concurrent septic process was associated with independent renal and hematologic deterioration.

The presence of this “Septic-Exchange Phenotype” had a profound impact on resource utilization. Neonates with sepsis had a significantly longer total length of hospital stay (LOS) compared to the non-septic group (17.6 vs. 9.8 days, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Multivariable linear regression analysis confirmed that sepsis was the primary independent predictor of prolonged hospitalization, independent of admission TSB levels, gestational age, or birth weight (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this 10-year longitudinal cohort of neonates requiring exchange transfusion (ET), we identified a critical physiological divergence that we have termed the “72-Hour Divergence.” While exchange transfusion remains the gold standard for rapidly lowering serum bilirubin [1, 4], our findings demonstrate that the procedure alone does not halt the systemic cascade associated with concurrent sepsis. We show that sepsis predominantly driven by Gram-negative pathogens like Klebsiella and E. Coli is associated with altered recovery, linked to a paradoxical deterioration in renal and hematologic function even as bilirubin levels normalize. Furthermore, we quantified the “hidden cost” of this phenotype, revealing that sepsis is associated with extended hospitalization by nearly 8 days independent of the initial bilirubin severity.

The hallmark of our study is the identification of the “72-Hour Divergence,” characterized by a nadir in platelet counts and a peak/plateau in serum creatinine 72 h post-procedure. This timing is biologically consistent with the kinetics of sepsis-associated organ dysfunction. Recent literature confirms that in Gram-negative sepsis, endotoxin-mediated bone marrow suppression and peripheral platelet destruction typically peak between 48 and 72 h [9, 11]. Simultaneously, sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) often manifests with a 48-hour lag in serum creatinine elevation due to the delayed reduction in glomerular filtration rate [12, 15]. In our “No-Sepsis” group, the removal of bilirubin and antibody-coated red blood cells via ET restored homeostasis rapidly. However, in the “Sepsis Group,” the removal of the toxin (bilirubin) was insufficient to reverse the inflammatory storm. This suggests that for a septic neonate, ET is a necessary “bridge” to prevent neurotoxicity, but it must be coupled with aggressive renal and hematologic support to navigate this divergence period [13, 19].

Our microbiological profile challenges the traditional view of early-onset sepsis. While Group B Streptococcus is often highlighted in Western guidelines, our cohort was dominated by Gram-negative organisms (Klebsiella pneumoniae and E. coli), consistent with emerging data from developing countries [10, 20]. This shift is critical because Gram-negative sepsis is associated with more severe thrombocytopenia and higher endotoxin loads compared to Gram-positive infections [9, 14]. The prevalence of these pathogens in our “crash-cart” admissions suggests that many of these infants likely suffered from community-acquired or healthcare-associated infections that progressed rapidly prior to referral, turning a manageable case of jaundice into a multi-organ crisis [8, 21].

While previous large cohorts from developed and developing countries [4, 8, 22] have primarily focused on epidemiological trends, risk factors, and procedural safety, our study moves beyond descriptive analysis to define a specific physiological phenotype. Unlike Hemmati et al. [22] and Hakan et al. [8], who reported demographic profiles and general outcomes over similar timeframes, we specifically isolated the mechanistic impact of sepsis. We demonstrate that sepsis is not merely a comorbidity but is associated with a distinct “72-Hour Divergence,” characterized by altered renal and hematologic recovery, which correlates with the hospital length of stay independent of admission bilirubin levels.

Our study expands this by quantifying the resource burden. We found that sepsis was the single strongest predictor of prolonged length of stay (LOS), associated with an additional average of 7.8 days to hospitalization. This finding aligns with the “double burden” concept described by Olusanya et al. [2, 23], where the intersection of infection and jaundice consumes disproportionate NICU resources. In resource-limited settings where bed capacity is scarce, recognizing this “Septic-Exchange Phenotype” early is vital for accurate prognostication and resource allocation [6].

A potential criticism of high ET rates is the failure of screening systems. However, our institution enforces a strict pre-discharge screening protocol [5, 17]. The fact that these infants presented with fulminant hyperbilirubinemia despite screening supports the “Biological Evasion” theory proposed by Bhutani et al. [24]. In cases of sepsis-associated hemolysis or G6PD deficiency, the rate of bilirubin rise can exponentially exceed the predictive power of nomograms [22, 25]. Furthermore, although the 2022 AAP guidelines raised the thresholds for ET [5], the majority of our cases presented with TSB levels significantly exceeding even these updated limits, validating the continued necessity of ET in this high-risk population.

The strength of this study lies in its 10-year homogeneous dataset and the granular analysis of physiological markers. Limitations include its retrospective design and single-center nature. As a high-volume tertiary referral unit, our population represents a “high-risk” subset; therefore, findings may be most applicable to tertiary centers managing late-presenting severe jaundice rather than primary birth hospitals.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we propose that the management of severe hyperbilirubinemia must evolve from a “bilirubin-centric” approach to a “system-centric” approach. The “72-Hour Divergence” characterized by thrombocytopenia and renal stress is a distinctive signature of the septic infant undergoing exchange transfusion. Clinicians should recognize that lowering bilirubin is only half the battle; the subsequent 72 h require vigilant monitoring for this altered adaptation phenomenon, particularly in settings where Gram-negative sepsis is prevalent.
