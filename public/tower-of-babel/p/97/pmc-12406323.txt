Inhibition mechanism and behaviour of wedelolactone against α-glucosidase
Authors: Jianjun Zhang, Min Zhao, Fan Wu, Zheng Shi, Rui Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe quest for effective and safe treatments for diabetes mellitus has led to the exploration of natural metabolites as potential α-glucosidase inhibitors. This study delves into the inhibition mechanism of wedelolactone against α-glucosidase and its hypoglycaemic activity. Activity assay results discovered that wedelolactone functioned as a mixed-type inhibitor, with an IC50 of 39.12 ± 2.54 μM, surpassing the potency of the standard drug acarbose. Employing multi-spectra methods, our findings indicated that wedelolactone binding induced conformation changes in α-glucosidase to attenuate its enzymatic activity, as evidenced by fluorescence quenching, synchronous fluorescence, 3D fluorescence, CD spectra, and ANS assay. Molecular docking studies provided insights into the specific interactions between wedelolactone and α-glucosidase. Collectively, these results laid the groundwork for the potential application of wedelolactone as a natural therapeutic agent in diabetes management.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM), a metabolic disorder of growing global concern, has seen a staggering rise in its prevalence1–3. Epidemiological analysis estimates that approximately 425 million adults are currently living with DM, and DM numbers would increase to around 700 million by 20454,5. Persistent hyperglycaemia, a hallmark of DM, lead to significant internal environmental disruptions, metabolic anomalies, and a myriad of complications6,7. Notably, the correlation between chronic hyperglycaemia and elevated mortality rates has been observed8–10.

In the therapeutic landscape of type 2 DM (T2DM), a variety of chemical inhibitors have been sanctioned for clinical use. Among these, α-glucosidase inhibitors have garnered attention for their cost-effectiveness and therapeutic efficacy11–13. α-Glucosidase, an enzyme bound to the intestinal epithelium, facilitates the hydrolysis of glycosidic bond from carbohydrate substrates14–16. This enzyme plays a pivotal role in carbohydrate digestion post-ingestion, converting them into absorbable glucose, which is intrinsically linked to the rise in blood glucose levels. Therefore, inhibition of α-glucosidase can delay the digestion and absorption of dietary carbohydrates, mitigating postprandial hyperglycaemia17–19. There are some α-glucosidase inhibitors in clinical practice, like acarbose. Although acarbose has been proven the efficacy in inhibiting α-glucosidase, it may lead to the accumulation of undigested carbohydrates and the production of gas after fermentation by colonic bacteria. This has stimulated the pursuit of potential α-glucosidase inhibitors derived from natural sources, which may enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimising adverse reactions20–22.

Wedelolactone (Figure 1), one important metabolite of edible plant Eclipta prostrata L. in south China, has received a lot of attention due to its positive bioactive properties23,24. Especially its inherent activities inducing antioxidant, anti-tumor, and anti-inflammatory make wedelolactone have the capable of serving as a human health supplement25–27. Previous studies also have revealed that wedelolactone possesses inhibitory activity against α-glucosidase28. In this context, the natural metabolite wedelolactone, abundant in food plants, has emerged as a promising candidate for its potential α-glucosidase inhibitory property. However, the detailed inhibitory activity and mechanism of wedelolactone against α-glucosidase and its hypoglycaemic activity were still not invesitgated.

Chemical structure of wedelolactone.

This study delves into the inhibitory effects of wedelolactone on α-glucosidase using multi-spectroscopic and docking methods. The findings aim to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of wedelolactone’s action against α-glucosidase and its implications for diabetes management.

Materials and methods

Materials

Wedelolactone (purity 98%) was procured from Macklin. α-Glucosidase from Saccharomyces cerevisiae and p-Nitrophenyl-α-D-galactopyranoside (pNPG) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Other reagents were of analytical grade.

Anti-α-glucosidase activity

In this study, the capacity of wedelolactone to inhibit α-glucosidase was assessed based on previous methods29,30. The assay was conducted by mixing α-glucosidase (10 μL) with varying concentrations of wedelolactone (10 μL, 8–128 μM) into 130 μL of PBS, followed by incubation for 10 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, 50 μL of substrate pNPG was introduced to above mixture and measured the absorbance at wavelength of 405 nm.

Inhibitory kinetic

Reversibility

To assay reversibility, reaction rate was plotted towards α-glucosidase concentration31,32. Then, wedelolactone concentration was set to 0 ∼ 64 μM, pNPG volume was maintained, and α-glucosidase volume was set to 7.5 ∼ 12.5 μl, respectively.

Kinetics

Lineweaver-Burk plots revealed the inhibition kinetics33,34. Samely, wedelolactone concentration was set to 0 ∼ 64 μM, α-glucosidase volume was maintained, and pNPG concentration was added, respectively.

Fluorescence spectra

Fluorescence spectra were conducted to assay the interaction between wedelolactone and α-glucosidase35,36. Fluorescence spectra of α-glucosidase free or with wedelolactone were recorded at 304, 307, and 310 K with excitation wavelength of 280 nm and slit width of 5.0 nm. α-Glucosidase was added wedelolactone and then measured fluorescence spectra. Moreover, synchronous and 3D fluorescence spectra of mixture were similarly assayed37,38.

CD spectra

CD spectra of α-glucosidase free were determined with scanning wavelength of 190–260 nm. Then α-glucosidase was added wedelolactone and measured CD spectra. CDNN software was used to calculate the secondary structure.

ANS

Fluorescence spectra of ANS-α-glucosidase mixture free or with wedelolactone were determined at excitation wavelength of 350 nm. 1 µL of wedelolactone was continuously added into 2 ml of mixture of ANS-α-glucosidase mixture (0.1 mg/mL) and recorded fluorescence spectra39.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking between wedelolactone and α-glucosidase was conducted using SYBYL software40–43. Wedelolactone structure was optimised with adding hydrogen, adding charge, and minimising energy. Homologous α-glucosidase was conducted according to previous reports15. In brief, the homology modes were built using modeller 10.1 software (http://salilab.org/modeller/), and the generated modes was verified the quality using Ramachandran plot (http://services.mbi.ucla.edu/PROCHECK/). Then, the most reasonable homology mode was selected for docking. Then α-glucosidase also was optimised with removing water, adding hydrogen, and adding charge, followed by generation of active pocket. Then, docking was simulated in default program.

Results and discussion

Inhibitory activity of wedelolactone

Our investigation results of wedelolactone’s effect on α-glucosidase (Figure 2) unveiled its effective inhibitory potential that was directly proportional to the concentration of wedelolactone. Wedelolactone (128 μM) resulted in about 90% inhibition against α-glucosidase. The IC50 value of wedelolactone was calculated to be 39.12 ± 2.54 μM, which was remarkable lower than that of positive control acarbose (674.62 ± 10.34 μM).

Inhibition rate of wedelolactone.

Inhibition kinetics

The reversibility of wedelolactone was confirmed by observing the reaction velocity at varying concentrations of α-glucosidase (Figure 3A). The linear plots of reaction velocity against α-glucosidase concentration demonstrated that the inhibition was reversible. The kinetic interaction between wedelolactone and α-glucosidase was elucidated using Lineweaver-Burk plot (Figure 3B). The enzyme kinetics graphical representation provided the clear evidence of a mixed-type inhibition16, which was characterised by the intersection of Lineweaver-Burk plots in the second quadrant.

(A) Reversibility assay of wedelolactone; (B) Kinetics assay of wedelolactone.

The kinetic parameters were further deduced from the slopes (Figure 4A) and intercepts (Figure 4B) of Lineweaver-Burk plots, yielding the Ki value of 0.03 mM and Kis value of 0.11 mM.

(A) Ki assay of wedelolactone; (B) Kis assay of wedelolactone.

Fluorescence quenching

The interaction between wedelolactone and α-glucosidase was further explored through fluorescence quenching studies. The intrinsic fluorescence of α-glucosidase was monitored in the presence of ­wedelolactone. Our observations revealed fluorescence intensity of α-glucosidase at the characteristic emission peak of 340 nm gradually declined with the introducing wedelolactone at 304, 307, and 310 K (Figure 5A–C). These phenomena indicated the binding event between α-glucosidase and wedelolactone. Moreover, blue-shifts in α-glucosidase fluorescence peak were also observed, suggesting conformation alteration within the α-glucosidase’s structure.

(A–C) Fluorescence quenching assay of wedelolactone at 304, 307, and 310 K (curve a: wedelolactone only; curve b to n: α-glucosidase with wedelolactone (0.5 ∼ 6 μM)); (D) The Stern-Volmer plots of wedelolactone.

The Stern-Volmer plots (Figure 5D), constructed at varying temperatures (304, 307, and 310 K), demonstrated a linear relationship, signifying a single quenching process. The calculated quenching rate constant (Kq) values (Table 1) at the respective temperatures were significantly higher than the maximum diffusion collision quenching rate constant (2 × 1010 mol L−1S−1), supporting a static quenching of wedelolactone, although Stern-Volmer quenching constant (Ksv) values decreased with temperature (Table 1). In addition, the association constant (Ka) values (Table 1) were found to be higher than 104, reflecting the stability of the enzyme-inhibitor complex. The binding site number (n) values (Table 1) were all approximately equal to 1, suggesting only one binding site for wedelolactone on α-glucosidase.

Synchronous fluorescence spectra

The synchronous fluorescence was employed to probe fluorophores micro-environmental changes within α-glucosidase upon interaction with wedelolactone. Our investigation focused on the spectral shifts at Δλ of 15 and 60 nm, indicating the microenvironments alterations surrounding tyrosine and tryptophan residues, respectively. As observed that the fluorescence intensities of these residues in α-glucosidase diminish with increasing concentrations of wedelolactone (Figure 6A and B). Especially, the characteristic peak of tyrosine residues exhibited slight blue-shift, while that of tryptophan residues unchanged. The decrease in intensity coupling with spectral shift provided the evidence of interaction of wedelolactone with α-glucosidase.

(A–B) Synchronous fluorescence spectra of wedelolactone with Δλ of 15 and 60 nm.

3D fluorescence spectra

The conformation changes of α-glucosidase in response to wedelolactone were investigated using 3D fluorescence spectra. Our examination focused on the two characteristic peaks, Peak 1 predominantly reflecting spectral attributes of tryptophan and tyrosine residues and Peak 2 attributed to the π→π* transitions within the polypeptide backbone. Upon the introduction of wedelolactone, a discernible decrease in the intensity of Peak 1 was recorded (Figure 7), suggesting the microenvironment alteration surrounding aromatic residues. Furthermore, the intensity of Peak 2 also exhibited a decline (Figure 6), implying a profound impact on the secondary structure of α-glucosidase.

(A-B) 3D fluorescence spectra of α-glucosidase and α-glucosidase-wedelolactone.

CD spectra

The secondary structure of α-glucosidase was scrutinised in the presence of wedelolactone using CD spectra. α-Glucosidase exhibited characteristic CD negative bands around 212 ∼ 222 nm, indicative of α-helices structure (Figure 8). While, upon the addition of wedelolactone, a noticeable reduction in the intensities of these bands was observed (Figure 8), pointing towards conformation changes in α-glucosidase. As wedelolactone increased, there was a decrease in α-helix content, accompanied by increases in β-sheet, β-turn and random coil contents (Table 3), which was consistent with the results that cryptolepine affected α-glucosidase conformation5.

ANS assay

ANS assay was conducted to investigate the binding of protein and ligand on the hydrophobic environment. As shown in Figure 9A that, fluorescence peak of ANS-α-glucosidase was at a relative low level, while, that could be obviously increased by wedelolactone addition, accompanied by a blue shift from 510 to 500 nm. From Figure 9B, wedelolactone addition (0.5 μM) would cause an increase in fluorescence intensity by 26.8%.

(A) ANS assay of α-glucosidase with wedelolactone; (B) Fluorescence peak intensity against wedelolactone.

Docking analysis

The molecular underpinnings of wedelolactone’s interaction with α-glucosidase were elucidated through molecular docking. After docking procedure, the most stable pose of wedelolactone was selected was chosen to analyse. As shown in Figure 10A, wedelolactone nestled within the active pocket of α-glucosidase with a binding energy of −4.1 kcal/mol. Furthermore, wedelolactone not only formed hydrogen bonds with key amino acid residues Arg212, Asp214, and Gln350, but also made hydrophobic interactions with Phe300 and Asp349 (Figure 10B).

Discussion

Natural products have the characteristics of diverse molecular structures44–46, rich pharmacological activities47–49, and low toxicity50–52, making them as an important source of clinical drugs. With the processing of scientific research on diabetes pathogenesis, natural product development, and people’s demand for safe medicine, natural products as α-glucosidase inhibitors for diabetes treatment have received growing attention53–55.

Due to the low toxicity and multi-activity, wedelolactone was investigated α-glucosidase inhibition mechanism using multi-spectroscopic and docking methods. Our results demonstrated that wedelolactone presented its potential on α-glucosidase inhibition with IC50 value of 39.12 ± 2.54 μM, ∼17-fold stronger than control acarbose (674.62 ± 10.34 μM). Inhibition kinetics results revealed wedelolactone as a reversible mixed-type inhibitor, which suggested that wedelolactone bound to α-glucosidase with non-covalent binding, and could bind to free enzymes and enzyme substrate complexes56. Then the binding constants (Ki and Kis) were calculated, and lower Ki (0.03 mM) than Kis (0.11 mM) indicated stronger affinity of wedelolactone to free enzyme than the enzyme-substrate complex57.

Subsequently, the effects of wedelolactone on enzyme structure were studied. Fluorescence quenching method is widely used to study the effects of inhibitors on enzyme conformation. Our fluorescence quenching results confirmed the binding of wedelolactone to α-glucosidase would quench endogenous fluorescence of enzymes in the static process, which was evidenced by by linear Stern-Volmer plots and higher Kq values (higher than 2 × 1010 mol L−1S−1). Especially, thermodynamic processes analysis indicated both hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces as the main binding forces between α-glucosidase and wedelolactone. These similar enzyme quenching phenomena had also been found in other natural products, like quercetin58 and gallocatechin gallate59.

The effects of wedelolactone on enzyme structure were further assayed by synchronous/3D fluorescence, CD, and ANS spectra. Monitoring synchronous fluorescence spectra of α-glucosidase treated with wedelolactone found that wedelolactone would bind to tyrosine and tryptophan residues to quench enzyme fluorescence, which caused the microenvironment changes around tyrosine and tryptophan residues. 3D fluorescence spectra can response tryptophan and tyrosine residues, and polypeptide backbone. The changed 3D fluorescence spectra of α-glucosidase by wedelolactone revealed that wedelolactone perturbed the spectral characteristics of α-glucosidase, indicating the conformational rearrangements within the enzyme to disrupt the enzyme’s catalytic activity. CD spectra were used to monitor the secondary structure of α-glucosidase, and our results indicated that the binding of wedelolactone to α-glucosidase caused the changes in secondary structure contents of α-glucosidase, by intercalating into the enzyme’s internal structure and obstructing the active site, to inhibit its activity. Moreover, the hydrophobicity of α-glucosidase was assayed by monitoring ANS fluorescence, and the increasing ANS fluorescence with the wedelolactone concentration suggested that the binding of wedelolactone would expose enzyme hydrophobic region, then, to reduce the enzyme activity.

Finally, the binding detail of wedelolactone to α-glucosidase was investigated by molecular docking analysis, and the results found that wedelolactone not only formed hydrogen bonds with key amino acid residues Arg212, Asp214, and Gln350, but also made hydrophobic interactions with Phe300 and Asp349. These interactions contributed to the conformation change of α-glucosidase, thus, inhibiting enzyme activity60.

All above spectra analysis not only corroborated the binding of wedelolactone to α-glucosidase but also provided a deeper understanding of the thermodynamic nature of this interaction.

Conclusion

In general, we investigated the inhibitory effects of wedelolactone, a bioactive metabolite in Eclipta prostrata L., on α-glucosidase and its subsequent hypoglycaemic effects. Activity assay results discovered that wedelolactone functioned as a mixed-type inhibitor, with an IC50 of 39.12 ± 2.54 μM, surpassing the potency of the standard drug acarbose. Employing a multi-spectra method, our findings indicated that wedelolactone binding induced conformation changes in α-glucosidase to attenuate its enzymatic activity, as evidenced by fluorescence quenching, synchronous fluorescence, 3D fluorescence, CD spectra, and ANS assay. Molecular docking studies provided insights into the specific interactions between wedelolactone and α-glucosidase. Collectively, these results laid the groundwork for the potential application of wedelolactone as a natural therapeutic agent in diabetes management. This work only investigated the inhibition mechanism of wedelolactone on α-glucosidase in vitro, but did not involve the hypoglycaemic activity in vivo. Thence, further clarification of the hypoglycaemic activity of wedelolactone in vivo will strengthen its application potential as α-glucosidase inhibitor.
