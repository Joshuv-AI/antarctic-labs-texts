Introduction

Metastatic tumors of the pancreas are rare, making up just 2 % of all pancreatic malignancies [1]. Renal tumors are the most common source of pancreatic metastases, with lung, melanoma, breast, and colorectal cancers as other less common sources, and colon cancer cases accounting for just 1.3 % of metastatic tumors of the pancreas [2]. Metastatic pancreatic tumors often present without specific clinical manifestations, which complicates their differentiation from primary pancreatic tumors [3]. Furthermore, a standardized treatment consensus for these tumors has yet to be established [4]. This report presents a case of secondary pancreatic head metastasis from colon cancer treated via radioactive iodine-125 seed implantation. We hope that this case report provides valuable clinical insights and therapeutic experience for physicians managing similar conditions. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [5].

Case report

An 81-year-old female presented to an external hospital with persistent abdominal pain for one month. She had a 10-year history of grade 3 hypertension, with no other comorbidities and no prior surgical history. After diagnosis, she underwent radical surgery for right-sided colon cancer in May 2021. Her postoperative pathology results for right-sided colon tissue samples revealed a case of ulcerative moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma with a lesion size of 4 cm × 3 cm × 1 cm. Deep infiltration of the muscle layer was observed to the surrounding adipose tissue, with no serosa involvement, no vascular or nerve involvement, the proximal and distal margins were tumor free, and evidence of metastasis to the pericolic lymph nodes was observed (3/16), greater omentum (−). Immunohistochemistry results were as follows: MSH2 (+), MSH6 (+), MLH1 (+), PMS2 (+), Ki-67 (+75 %). Her pathological stage was pT3N1bM0. She did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery. Following the initial surgery, the patient underwent regular follow-up with abdominal CT scans every six months, and no pancreatic mass or enlarged abdominal lymph nodes were detected during these examinations.

In October 2023, this patient underwent a follow-up examination at an external hospital during which a space-occupying lesion was detected in the head of the pancreas. Her family refused surgical resection, and she was admitted to our hospital in November 2023 with a pancreatic mass. Physical examination results revealed that her abdomen was soft, with no tenderness, rebound tenderness, or abdominal masses. Tumor marker results were as follows: CA199, 24.69 U/mL (<30 U/mL); CEA 3.84 ng/mL (<4.5 ng/mL); CA242, 7.54 U/mL (<20 U/mL). Abdominal enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a hypodense mass measuring approximately 3.50 cm × 3.08 cm × 3.17 cm in the head of the pancreas, with mild arterial phase enhancement of the edges and poor demarcation from the surrounding tissues, together with greater delayed phase enhancement. Lymph nodes were visible in the surrounding area, with the largest exhibiting a short diameter of approximately 0.8 cm. The dilatation of the main pancreatic duct was evident. These results were consistent with the occupying lesion of the pancreas, such that the presence of a neoplastic lesion was considered likely (Fig. 1A). Contrast-enhanced ultrasonography results were suggestive of pancreatic head mass consistent with secondary tumor changes and peripheral lymph node metastases, not excluding the gastrointestinal tract as the potential origin of this metastatic lesion (Fig. 2). Subsequent ultrasound-guided true-cut needle biopsy of the pancreatic head mass was conducted, and the biopsy tissue exhibited features consistent with adenocarcinoma (Fig. 3), supporting its identification as a metastatic adenocarcinoma likely derived from the intestinal tract based on the combination of patient history, histological features, and the following immunohistochemical results: CK7 (−), CK20 (small foci of +), PMS2 (+), MLH1 (+), MSH2 (+), and MSH6 (+). Based on these results, a clinical diagnosis of pancreatic head metastasis stemming from primary colon cancer was made. Although a PET scan would have been advisable for a more detailed assessment of potential small metastatic lesions, the patient and her family declined this examination because of economic factors.

In December 2023, this patient underwent treatment for this pancreatic head lesion and the associated metastatic lymph nodes via the ultrasound-guided percutaneous puncture-based implantation of radioactive Iodine-125 seeds. The preoperative Treatment Planning System (TPS) was used to develop a treatment plan, leading to the administration of a prescribed dose of 120 Gy, and 23 Iodine-125 seeds with an activity of 29.6 MBq were implanted. Compliance with this preoperative plan was confirmed through postoperative validation. The procedure was successfully completed in 45 min. After this procedure, the patient is kept nothing per mouth, and receives anti-inflammatory treatments, acid-suppressive therapies, enzyme-suppressive therapies, and fluid replacement therapies. Follow-up abdominal CT imaging on day 2 postoperatively revealed no evidence of bleeding, exudate formation, or other complications. A satisfactory radioactive seed distribution was observed, with no evidence of cold spots (Fig. 1B). On day 2 after the procedure, the patient was discharged and did not undergo any further antitumor treatments. She underwent regular follow-up evaluations. At 7 months postoperatively, follow-up abdominal CT imaging revealed the complete resolution of the pancreatic head lesion. According to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) [6], the patient had achieved a clinical complete response (CR) (Fig. 1C). The patient remained in good condition at 9 months postoperatively without any complication.

Discussion

Colon cancer is among the most frequently diagnosed digestive tract malignancies, with incidence rates that continue to rise annually. Prognostic outcomes for colon cancer patients are closely related to whether or not the primary tumor metastasizes [7]. Colon tumors most often metastasize to the regional lymph nodes, liver, lungs, and bones, whereas metastasis to the pancreas is a rare event [8]. Pancreatic metastases often present with a variety of nonspecific clinical symptoms that are often detected during follow-up after primary tumor resection. Roughly half of patients remain asymptomatic [9]. It can be difficult to distinguish between primary pancreatic cancer and isolated pancreatic metastasis based solely on clinical symptoms and imaging results [3]. Pancreatic biopsy, however, can be an effective means of diagnosing these tumors, with immunohistochemical staining results aiding in the identification of the tumor of origin [4,10].

Therapeutic decision-making for pancreatic metastases is complex and necessitates taking the primary tumor of origin, the presence or absence of extra-pancreatic metastases, overall patient condition, and the suitability of the lesion for surgical resection into account. Studies have shown that the resection of isolated pancreatic metastases from renal cell carcinoma can improve survival, but there are no definitive treatment recommendations for pancreatic metastases derived from other primary tumors [4].

Radioactive Iodine-125 seed implantation therapy is commonly used to treat a range of solid tumors including pancreatic, lung, liver, prostate, and colorectal cancers, achieving good therapeutic efficacy [11]. The implantation of radium in the pancreas for the treatment of pancreatic cancer is not a new technique, as it dates back to the 1930s. What is novel in the present context is its application for metastatic cancer in the pancreas. An increasing number of studies have investigated and confirmed the feasibility and safety of US- and CT-guided radioactive Iodine-125 brachytherapy in head and neck, thoracic, abdominal, pelvic, spinal, and even metastatic cancers over the past two decades. This approach entails the delivery of radioactive Iodine-125 seeds, which are sealed radioactive nuclide sources encased in a titanium alloy, with a half-life of 59.6 days. These seeds continuously emit low-energy gamma rays that cause cytotoxic tumor cell death as a form of internal radiotherapy. Wang et al. performed a study in which they determined that Iodine-125 seed implantation brachytherapy is safe and effective when used to treat colorectal cancer patients with abdominal incisional metastases [12]. Research published by Yu et al. has further demonstrated the safety and efficacy of Iodine-125 seed implantation as a treatment for hepatic colorectal cancer metastases [13]. Few reports to date, however, have focused on using Iodine-125 seed implantation as a means of treating pancreatic colorectal cancer metastases. In the present case, the patient was treated via ultrasound-guided percutaneous puncture radioactive Iodine-125 seed implantation radiotherapy. The procedure itself was quick, as was the postoperative recovery period, and the patient experienced no significant complications. At 7 months after this procedure, the pancreatic mass had fully disappeared, achieving a clinical CR. Iodine-125 seeds are thus a very effective treatment for pancreatic metastases secondary to colorectal cancer, in line with what has been reported for metastases in other abdominal sites including the liver and incisional metastases when treated via Iodine-125 seed implantation. At 9 months postoperatively, the patient remained in good condition without any evidence of tumor recurrence.

Conclusion

In summary, Iodine-125 seed implantation is a safe, effective, and minimally invasive approach to treating cases of colon cancer with pancreatic head metastasis. The rapidity of this procedure and the short postoperative recovery for treated patients make it a promising therapeutic option for similar cases.
