Mapping and optimization of a patient-centred care pathway for treatment of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis: a qualitative study
Authors: Thomáy-Claire Ayala Hoelen, Ghislaine A.P.G. van Mastrigt, Annemarijn Weber, Zoë Vankan, Linda Klerks, Bram Stevelmans, Selaine F. C. Soons, Aggie T.G. Paulus, Mark van den Boogaart, Eva Jacobs, Paul C. Willems
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent idiopathic scoliosis refers to a complex three-dimensional deformity of the spine, with lifelong physical, psychological and healthcare burden. To improve and help achieve value-based care, this study mapped the integrative patient-centred adolescent idiopathic scoliosis healthcare pathway and investigated potential avenues for improvement from multiple perspectives.MethodsCare processes were mapped and evaluated through a brown paper session and focus group with hospital staff, semi-structured interviews with patients and parents, and a brown paper session with scoliosis-specific exercise therapists and orthotists. Data was analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsSix themes were identified by staff: reachability, involvement, communication, information provision, organization/coordination and accessibility, while seven themes emerged after the interviews with patients and parents: treatment, information provision, communication, involvement, facilities, organization/coordination and availability. The first brown paper session engaged 42 hospital staff, of whom 12 participated in the focus group. In addition, 31 scoliosis-specific exercise therapists and 2 orthotists contributed to the second brown paper session. Interviews were conducted with 11 patients and 15 parents. Interviewees consistently valued commitment, positive interactions between patients and clinicians, and shared decision making. Key improvement areas included addressing treatment-related burdens, harmonizing patient information, strengthening communication pathways, incorporating peer support and creating standardized protocols to overcome organizational constraints.ConclusionThe adolescent idiopathic scoliosis pathway is characterized by professional commitment and high-quality interpersonal care but hampered by treatment burdens, inadequate information provision, communication gaps and organizational inefficiencies. Standardized information provision, enhanced communication lines and the integration of peer support could enhance patient experience and streamline care delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14267-x.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a three-dimensional deformation of the spine characterized by a lateral curvature and axial rotation of the spine. With a general population prevalence of 1–3% [1, 2], this condition presents in adolescents aged 10–18 years old, predominantly affecting females [2]. Several treatment options exist depending on severity of the curve depicted by the Cobb angle on radiographs, progression of the curve, and age of the patient. Non-operative management aims to halt progression of the spinal curve and includes scoliosis specific exercise therapy (Cobb angle 10–45°) and bracing (25–45°) [2, 3]. If non-operative treatment is ineffective in halting progression of the curvature (Cobb angle ≥ 40–45°) [2, 3], operative correction is often indicated.

AIS affects the physical and psychological wellbeing of individuals into adulthood, with patients facing a higher risk of developing chronic pain, osteoporosis and decline in functional and pulmonary capacity [3–6]. Increasingly, evidence underscores the lifelong burden AIS poses on health-related quality of life [3, 4]. The lifelong impact further increases the substantial utilization of healthcare resources, resulting in high costs [4, 7, 8]. Consequently, the need for value-based healthcare is increasingly relevant since it describes the measured improvement in a patient’s health relative to the costs required to achieve such improvement [9]. This optimizes value by prioritizing patient health, enhancing alignment between patient and healthcare provider and reducing healthcare utilization [10, 11].

The mapping and optimization of healthcare pathways has the potential to further value-based healthcare [12]. Healthcare pathways are structured, evidence-based frameworks that help depict the continuum of care from prevention to recovery or palliative care for a defined patient population [13]. Mapping these pathways supports understanding healthcare processes, identify problems and opportunities for improvement, and fosters coordination, co-creation and shared ownership [14]. Additionally, it facilitates standardization of healthcare pathways both nationally and internationally. Optimization of care pathways thereby improves quality of care, reduces variability in clinical practice, improves outcomes and reduces condition-associated costs as well as the use of healthcare services [15].

Care pathways need to be considered from multiple perspectives, including the patient, parent and healthcare provider journeys [16, 17]. Prior studies have predominantly focused on improving pathway efficiency from a healthcare provider perspective. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively map the entire healthcare pathway and to evaluate challenges and opportunities for improvement from multiple perspectives. Accordingly, this study aims to provide an integrative, patient-centred analysis of the AIS healthcare pathway, identifying problem areas and opportunities for improvement from the perspectives of patients, parents, healthcare professionals and extramural collaborators.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative study was conducted consisting of process mapping the AIS healthcare and evaluating the healthcare pathway from multiple perspectives using a brown paper analysis i.e. collaborative process-mapping technique used to identify inefficiencies, bottlenecks and opportunities for improvement, a focus group and semi- structured interviews (Fig. 1). The study was conducted at Maastricht University Medical Centre (MUMC+) in the Netherlands. Informed consent was obtained prior to the study. Ethical approval was obtained by the Medical Ethical Testing Committee and the ethical board of Faculty of Health, Medicine and Life Sciences (FHML-REC) at Maastricht University (#2024 − 0394).

This study employs the definition and integrative conceptual framework of a care pathway by Gartner et al. (2022), integrating patient experience, the supporting role of professionals, evidence-based and best-practice approaches, as well as the infrastructure needed to continuously improve the care pathway [13]. The quality criteria identified in the conceptual framework by Antonacci et al. (2021) were used for process mapping [14]. The thematic analysis framework depicted by Braun and Clarke (2006) was used for the qualitative analysis of the brown paper sessions, focus group and semi-structured interviews [18]. This study was written in accordance with the Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) and the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) checklists [19, 20] (Appendix I).

Participants and setting

The healthcare pathway was mapped in close collaboration with the multidisciplinary MUMC+ scoliosis core team, consisting of a paediatrician, a critical care physician, an orthopaedic nurse, a physician assistant and surgeon, a paediatric intensive care nurse, a paediatric nurse and pedagogue, and two quality and safety officers. Employees from all departments involved in the AIS healthcare pathway were eligible for inclusion and invited to partake in the employee brown paper session [21]. Furthermore, one representative from each department was asked to participate in the subsequent focus group. A second session was planned for extramural collaborators, including scoliosis-specific exercise (SSE) therapists and orthotists, directly involved in the integrated “Scoliosis care” network, which works closely with the MUMC + to provide integrated care for patients with AIS.

For the semi-structured interviews, eligible participants included AIS patients aged 10–18 years under treatment at MUMC+, and their parents/primary caregivers. Patients were contacted by the research team to provide additional information, discuss enrolment and informed consent forms, after their treating physician had requested consent to share contact information. Interviews were scheduled in line with outpatient clinic visits. Purposive sampling was used until data saturation was reached. Theoretical data saturation was defined as the point in data collection and analysis when new information no longer resulted in new codes [22].

Data collection

The AIS healthcare pathway was generated during three interdisciplinary meetings with the scoliosis core team and consisted of five phases as described by Antonacci et al.: (1) preparation, planning and process identification; (2) data and information gathering; (3) map generation; (4) process analysis and (5) taking it forward [14].

The employee brown paper session lasted four hours and allowed for walk-ins, two researchers were present (TH/ZV). A visual depiction of the AIS healthcare trajectory was presented. Each employee was provided with coloured sticky notes and asked to identify strengths (green), challenges (red) and opportunities for improvement (yellow) within the healthcare process. An identical approach was implemented for the second brown paper session held with SSE therapist and orthotists.

The focus group of employees allowed for in-depth discussion of themes that emerged from the earlier brown paper session with employees. A nominal group technique was utilized to gather additional feedback, enabling structured qualitative data collection whilst ensuring that all participants were heard [23, 24]. Participants were asked to rank the themes that arose during the brown paper session based on importance and subsequently reflect on them. The focus group was scheduled for 60 min and was guided by two researchers (TH/ZV).

Semi-structured interviews were conducted between February and May 2025 by three independent investigators (SS/RK/NM) with no prior relation to the patient or parent, and who had no conflicts of interest with respect to study outcomes [25]. Further details of interviewer characteristics and reflexivity are provided in Appendix II. Children and adolescents were allowed to have the interview individually or in the presence of their parents/primary caregivers. The topic guide was developed by the scoliosis core team based on expert knowledge (Appendix III). The interviews were semi-structured to allow participants to raise issues and address topics that were not listed in the predefined topic guide. All interviews were audio recorded.

Data analysis and synthesis

The AIS healthcare trajectory was mapped by the scoliosis core team (PW/EJ/MvdB/SS) on paper and then digitalized using Microsoft Visio. Each core team member discussed the mapped healthcare pathway with their colleagues, and feedback was considered during the core team meetings.

All coloured sticky notes collected during the two brown paper sessions were organized digitally in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365), assigned to the corresponding healthcare trajectory phase and grouped, describing strengths (green), areas for improvement (yellow), or problem areas (red). Two researchers (TH/ZV) independently analysed the sticky notes using a reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) approach to inductively identify themes and subsequently cluster them [18, 26]. A third researcher (PW) was consulted in case of disagreements.

The RTA approach guided the generation of a topic list for the focus group. The fieldnotes and audio recording were transcribed verbatim by one researcher (ZV) and checked by an independent researcher (TH) on quality and completeness. The transcripts were analysed in ATLAS.ti (Version 25, ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin, Germany) using a hybrid thematic analysis approach [18]. Inductive coding (open coding) was used to identify themes. Subsequently, axial coding was used to cluster individual codes into larger themes. This was followed by selective coding to integrate the findings and link them to central concepts deductively identified during the brown paper session [27]. The coding structures were presented in a code tree. All coding was performed independently by two researchers (ZV/TH).

A similar approach was taken for the analysis of the semi-structured interviews. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim by two independent researchers (LK/BS) who were not involved in conducting the interviews. Transcripts were analysed using a hybrid thematic analysis approach in ATLAS.ti. Two researchers (LK/BS) jointly coded the first interview to generate a code book. The code book was discussed with the rest of the research team (TH/AW/PW/EJ/MB) to ensure alignment and consistency [25]. The remaining interviews were split coded by the researchers and subsequently, a cross-check of 25% of the transcripts was performed. Any discrepancies or uncertainties during subsequent interviews were discussed iteratively during research meetings.

Findings were compared and synthesized into overarching themes to provide an integrative overview from multiple perspectives.

Reflexivity

The team consisted of members with diverse professional and academic backgrounds, including health sciences, clinical care, health services research and health education. The team’s diverse backgrounds and perspectives enhanced the depth of the study. Interviews were conducted by independent researchers (TH/ZV/SS/RK/NM) with no prior relation to the interviewee. Further details on the interviewers are provided in Appendix II. Participants were reassured that the interview would remain anonymous and that they were free to share both positive and negative lived experiences. To enhance objectivity, transcripts were analysed by researchers (LK/BS/AW), who had not conducted the interviews. Regular team meetings, independent analysis and cross-checking of code were used to reflect on potential biases, challenge assumptions and ensure the findings accurately presented the narrative of participants. GM and AP had extensive qualitative research experience and contributed an external perspective as well as ensuring methodological rigor. AW had prior experience in qualitative research and provided an independent view of the data to challenge assumptions and maintain objectivity. Although several team members (MB/EJ/PW) had prior professional relations with clinical staff, patients and parents as well as external collaborators they were not directly involved in conducting or analysing the collected data but rather provided contextual insight into clinical practice and care processes during team discussions.

Trustworthiness of the data

Trustworthiness of the qualitative data was ensured in accordance with the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba (1985, 1986) [28, 29]. Credibility was addressed through independent data analysis and triangulation of data sources and methods, combining brown paper sessions, focus groups and semi-structured interviews across multiple participant groups. The criterion of dependability was reinforced through the maintenance of a clear audit trail detailing data collection, analysis and iterative team discussion. Confirmability was enhanced through reflexive practices, independent coding of data by researchers not involved in data collection and regular team meetings to challenge interpretations and assumptions. Transferability was supported by providing a rich description of the study context, participant characteristics and care setting, allowing readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other contexts and healthcare settings.

SSE therapists and orthotists

During the brown paper session for SSE therapists (n = 31) and orthotists (n = 2), a simplified version of the healthcare pathway was presented. The brown paper session generated a total of 73 sticky notes: n = 29 depicting strengths, n = 24 illustrating areas for improvement, and n = 20 representing challenges. The healthcare pathway phases identified as relevant were referral, intake, pre-operative, post-operative, and follow-up. Using the RTA approach, five themes were identified: communication, referral letter, information provision, brace, and policy (Table 2).

Patients and parents

Interviews were conducted with seven patients who had undergone surgery and four patients who had received brace treatment. Additionally, 15 parents were interviewed. The semi-structured interviews lasted an average of 27 min. Seven main themes emerged: treatment, information provision, communication, involvement, facilities, organization/ coordination, and availability (Table 3).

Treatment-specific burdens

The treatment-specific burdens were primarily articulated by patients and parents, highlighting their direct experiences with diagnostic assessments, brace treatment and surgical procedures. A prominent finding was that brace wear and post-operative recovery were described as physically and emotionally demanding, with patients often underestimating their impact.

Diagnostics

Patients reported satisfaction with the radiographic process prior to their consultation with the treating physician. However, patients and parents were not informed that they would need to undress for the radiograph, and parents expressed that, in addition to this information, having a radiological staff member of the same gender would provide greater comfort and security for their child.

Shared decision-making

Patients and parents indicated they felt sufficiently involved in deciding the course of treatment and were able to express what was important to them. Patients felt that physicians took their personal preferences into account, which gave them confidence in the chosen treatment plan. However, they acknowledged that the severity of their curvature may limit the extent of shared decision making.

Brace

Negative experiences with wearing a brace were frequently reported. Braces caused discomfort, such as pain from pressure points and excessive heat, and resulted in trouble sleeping. Additionally, some patients disclosed that wearing a brace caused social discomfort. Moreover, clear instructions on how to properly put on the brace and information on the expected duration of wear were lacking. Although parents saw clear benefits to brace treatment, they confirmed the pain and difficulties children experienced when adjusting to the brace. Brace discomfort seriously affected patients’ motivation and compliance with treatment.

Operative

Patients and parents were satisfied with the pre-operative multidisciplinary carousel meeting as it helped them feel prepared for surgery. Additionally, parents indicated that they appreciated the PICU tour prior to surgery. However, some patients found it too long, as the sessions lasted all day with waiting periods between consultations with different specialists. Additionally, some parents indicated that the amount of information received during the consultations was overwhelming, particularly for their child. Although the time after surgery was physically and psychologically challenging, patients were satisfied with post-operative care. Patients emphasized the importance of adequate pain management, especially before physiotherapy sessions. Parents valued being kept informed directly after the operation. Patients and parents raised concerns about the discharge procedure, noting a lack of structure that led to unclear explanations of post-surgical home care and missing medication lists and materials.

Overall, the burdens associated with treatment went beyond mere physical discomfort and included emotional and social challenges. This underscores how AIS affects the daily lives of both patients and their parents.

Information provision

Employees

The lack of uniformity in the information provided to patients and parents by specialists was considered the most important problem, as it can lead to confusion and dissatisfaction. Additionally, employees raised concerns about the limited information on pain management in the pre-operative trajectory.

SSE therapists and orthotists

SSE therapists and orthotists were satisfied with the effectiveness of the treatment provided at the hospital. However, for the brace treatment specifically, SSE therapists and orthotists indicated that more feedback from the medical specialist is necessary to ensure adequate brace fit. In addition, SSE therapists expressed a lack of adequate information on the referral letter for health insurance acceptance. Furthermore, additional information and feedback after intake or hospital consultations would be appreciated.

Patients and parents

Patients also reported insufficient information provision, describing uncertainty about expectations for brace wear, timing of medication and discharge criteria. In addition, three parents expressed a desire to have received more written information, such as brochures or a website referral, and one parent stated that the information in the brochure was outdated. Moreover, most parents reported not having received questionnaires prior to the first consultation, whereas one parent reported receiving the same questionnaire three times from different departments. Regarding verbally communicated information, parents were positive about the way the physician explained it to them, emphasizing the clear, personal, and calm manner of communication. Nevertheless, parents requested more clarity on expectations regarding treatment and especially on their personal role once their children switch from the PICU to the children’s ward.

Across all participant groups, issues with information provision were primarily related to inconsistency, timing, and insufficiency.

Communication and involvement

Employees

Most people were pleased with the level of involvement and commitment of colleagues concerning patient care. Problems related to accessibility and communication with employees included dissatisfaction with long referral times and a lack of inter-department communication. Departments such as physiotherapy that were not directly involved in the pre-operative multidisciplinary meeting (carousel consultation) often missed crucial information on protocol or patient updates. These departments felt disconnected due to a lack of involvement. Moreover, criticism was also raised on the discontinuation of the pre-operative PICU (Paediatric Intensive Care Unit) tour, as this was considered beneficial for easing patients and parents.

SSE therapists and orthotists

SSE therapists and orthotists were satisfied with the clear and easy communication.

Patients and parents

Patients expressed their satisfaction with being addressed directly despite their young age, and the professional’s ability to communicate in an age-appropriate and understandable manner. Moreover, parents felt that healthcare professionals were empathetic and approachable, taking the time to answer any questions. Notwithstanding, parents raised concerns regarding inconsistencies in communication between departments and healthcare providers. This resulted in contradictory instructions leading to confusion.

The support provided by parents and the direct involvement of medical staff were greatly appreciated and gave patients significant comfort and much-needed reassurance. In addition, patients emphasized the importance of having their parents accompany them during appointments, procedures, and overnight stays. Parents appreciated being allowed to stay with their child after surgery and indicated this helped them feel involved. Nevertheless, two parents reported feeling overlooked because personnel did not fulfil their commitments. Furthermore, three parents expressed their desire for peer contact. They would have appreciated insight from other parents or children who had gone through similar experiences, noting that this may also benefit their child.

Communication was perceived as empathetic and patient-centred by all participants, while deficiencies in professional communication contributed to feelings of disconnection and confusion throughout the healthcare journey.

Organization and coordination

Employees

Most problem areas concerned the logistics of care, including uncertainty about who is primarily responsible for timely radiograph and surgery orders, a lack of flexibility in surgical planning that led to postponements, bed capacity issues, and the absence of standardized discharge protocols. The capacity of available PICU beds is the predominant limiting factor for surgery planning, with postponement or cancellation. The risk of this is insufficiently communicated to patients and parents, often leading to frustration. Additionally, the problem of incorrect or delayed surgical orders for equipment/instrumentation was raised, as these cause stress and dissatisfaction. Furthermore, patients are often discharged without receiving the necessary instructions due to a lack of standardized protocol.

SSE therapists and orthotists

Problem areas concerned ambiguous policies on exercise therapy post-operatively or following brace treatment, and no debriefing on the carousel consultation.

Patients and parents

Patients and parents indicated that appointments were scheduled in a timely manner. Patients noted structural delays, with prolonged waiting times between radiological imaging and the subsequent consultation with their treating orthopaedic surgeon. Parents appreciated the timely appointments and the flexibility in scheduling, which accommodated their personal circumstances. Nonetheless, one parent expressed frustration over the lack of time indications or communications about delays. Additionally, both patients and their parents described the pre-operative admission as being scheduled earlier than necessary. Parents indicated this led to distress in addition to waiting times.

In addition to challenges with scheduling and coordination, organizational factors in the hospital setting also affected patients’ and parents’ experiences. Patients who stayed in a single hospital room after surgery valued their privacy, noting that it aided their rest and recovery, while those in shared rooms reported discomfort. Parents shared mixed experiences regarding the hospital environment. Concerns were raised about food quality, cleanliness, and uncomfortable waiting areas, including the lack of quiet seating and adequate distractions. Additionally, parents experienced technical difficulties with check-in at the kiosks when multiple appointments were scheduled on the same day. Experiences with the PICU ranged from parents praising the attentiveness and organized care to others describing it as hectic and stressful, negatively impacting their child’s recovery. The Ronald McDonald House was consistently noted as a valuable resource for families, although the inability to reserve a room in advance was identified as a source of stress.

Although most patients and parents were satisfied with the time between their referral and first consultation, two patients reported long waiting times, which caused concern and uncertainty. One patient waited three months and described this time as particularly stressful, stating that some reassurance would have helped. This was emphasized by their parent, who found the situation frustrating. Generally, patients and parents were aware of the possibility of contacting the hospital after surgery and felt that the treating physician and nurse were accessible. Parents felt they received clear instructions and contact information upon discharge, which they found extremely helpful and reassuring.

Across the different participant groups, challenges such as limited capacity, unclear roles, and logistical problems were identified as the primary contributors to uncertainty, delays, and stress in the organization and coordination of the healthcare pathway.

Discussion

This study comprehensively mapped the complex, integrative patient-centred AIS healthcare pathway at Maastricht University Medical Centre + in the Netherlands, encompassing multiple trajectories from referral through non-operative and surgical treatment. Subsequently, opportunities for pathway optimization using a multiple perspective approach were identified. Across all respondents, high professional commitment, positive interactions between patients and clinicians, shared decision making as well as parental involvement were identified as major strengths of the current pathway. Areas for improvement focused on treatment-specific burdens, aligning information provision, filling communication gaps, incorporating peer support and standardizing protocols to tackle organisational bottlenecks.

Treatment-specific burdens

Although patients and parents valued shared decision-making, the severity of the spinal curvature often constrained viable treatment options, leaving AIS patients and parents struggling to maintain agency [30]. Regarding non-operative treatment, brace wear was described as physically and psychologically taxing. Patients reported feeling pain from pressure points, heat and sleep disturbances as well as social discomfort. These findings are consistent with prior studies that report discomfort, altered body image, and reduced participation as common barriers to brace wear adherence [30, 31]. These problems emphasize the need for psychosocial support, with literature showing that psychosocial interventions are feasible and positively impact psychosocial outcomes in patients with AIS [32, 33].

All respondents valued the comprehensiveness of the pre-surgical consultations, in line with studies indicating that structured preoperative education improves preparedness and reduces anxiety [34]. However, the long duration and amount of information were overwhelming for some patients. Furthermore, dissatisfaction with the lack of standardized discharge procedures was reported. Enhanced discharge pathways have shown high satisfaction among patients [35] as well as the incorporation of discharge post-operative education programs that guide them after surgery [36]. This was echoed by SSE therapists, who indicated a need for clear policies on post-operative SSE treatment.

Aligning information provision

Inconsistent and unmet information needs were the most prominent concerns across interviewees. Patients and parents described discrepancies in the verbal information they received from healthcare providers, outdated written materials and insufficient guidance regarding brace use and discharge criteria. Employees acknowledged that inconsistency and incomplete information can lead to confusion and distress. This is consistent with prior studies, which found that inconsistency or unmet information needs can result in non-adherence, confusion, anxiety and a loss of confidence in the ability of healthcare providers amongst patients and their parents [37–39]. Additionally, it prompts families to seek supplementary potentially unreliable information elsewhere [37, 40, 41]. The importance of effective communication between orthotists and patients was found to be crucial in brace wear compliance [42]. The absence of consistent information may be exacerbated in multidisciplinary care [43]. Information provision that is well tailored and of high quality, allows for more effective participation in shared decision-making, helps patients feel more in control and can ultimately lead to increased satisfaction [37, 39, 40, 44].

Filling communication gaps

Patients felt involved and appreciated age-appropriate communication from healthcare professionals. Research shows that positive, frequent patient-provider interactions improve outcomes, treatment adherence, and satisfaction in AIS patients [33, 45, 46]. Patients and parents valued the empathic communication and approachability of healthcare professionals but would have appreciated more guidance on their own role. In a systematic review, the importance of parental support is emphasized, considering the challenges that parents face navigating their child’s diagnosis and treatment [39]. Active parental involvement leads to faster recovery and better overall health outcomes for children [41, 47]. It is therefore crucial for healthcare providers to adequately support and involve parents. Furthermore, parents suggested the inclusion of peer contact for additional support [40], with research underscoring that peer mentorship facilitates coping in AIS patients and also has perceived benefits for parents [48].

Organization and coordination

The most dominant organizational constraint causing frustration was related to surgical scheduling, with limited PICU bed capacity leading to postponement or cancellation of surgery. This disruption causes dissatisfaction and frustration for patients, parents and hospital staff [49]. Additionally, cancelling elective operations causes significant strain on healthcare resources [50]. Furthermore, unclarity concerning discharge procedures causes frustration and dissatisfaction amongst patients, signalling the need for a more structured discharge protocol.

Clinical and research implications

To increase satisfaction amongst involved parties and improve pathway efficiency, there is a need for standardized protocols concerning information delivery, discharge procedures and post-surgical interventions. In the short term, it is recommended to update and standardize patient information packets to ensure high-quality counselling. Information should be provided at various points along the care continuum, including at discharge. Additionally, reinstating live or virtual PICU tours and implementing pre-discharge checklists will increase parent and employee satisfaction. The development of centralized communication across disciplines will strengthen the commitment of relevant parties. In addition, the exploration of digital health tools for patient engagement and interdisciplinary coordination such as a personal health environment can help streamline communication between collaborators. Long-term strategies should focus on continuous improvement of the care pathway by establishing feedback loops to monitor adherence to and satisfaction with the pathway through regular checks with the involved parties. In parallel, systematic dissemination of study findings can promote mutual understanding and alignment, thereby supporting the effective implementation of pathway improvements. In addition, incorporating peer-support programs for patients and parents should be explored. Furthermore, (inter)national comparison of healthcare pathways can help harmonize and standardize best practices. Future research should investigate the most effective approaches for implementing improvement strategies, ensuring the involvement of all involved parties, as this is essential for achieving effective and sustainable change [51].

Strengths and limitations

The major strengths of this study are the utilization of a collaborative perspective to evaluate the healthcare trajectory of patients with AIS and the use of state-of-the-art research methods i.e. integration of process mapping with qualitative thematic analysis. This enabled the triangulation of experiences and identification of overlapping priorities across the entire care continuum in both professional and patient domains. Additionally, the interviews and analysis were done by independent researchers to limit potential bias. However, this was a single-center study in a specialized academic hospital, potentially limiting its generalizability to other contexts. Additionally, participant selection may have introduced bias towards individuals with more extreme experiences. Another limitation is that extramural collaborator analysis was limited to the brown paper session. If requested by the child, parents joined the interview, which may have influenced the child’s openness or may have led to socially desirable responses. However, to minimize influence, interviewers first asked the child to respond before parents could answer.

Conclusion

The integrative, patient-centred AIS pathway is characterized by high professional commitment, adequate shared decision making as well as positive parental involvement and demonstrates high-quality interpersonal care. However, current challenges remain in managing treatment-specific burdens; ensuring consistent and reliable information; bridging communication gaps and addressing organizational constraints. Addressing these opportunities for improvement by standardizing information, strengthening interdisciplinary communication, and incorporating psychosocial and peer support programmes, may improve patient and parent experience as well as streamline care delivery. Incorporating these improvements within the framework of value-based healthcare will ensure that improving patient outcomes remains central while fostering shared decision making, strengthening alignment between patients, families, and providers as well as reducing healthcare resource utilization.
