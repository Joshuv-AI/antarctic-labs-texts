Dysregulation of the miR-4286 in diabetic kidney disease and its role in inflammatory response
Authors: Jiayong Zhang, Qilin Tang, Mengmeng Pu, Lin Yan, Yijun Chen, Bin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe aimed to investigate the expression level and diagnostic value of miR-4286 in serum of DKD patients and validate its effect on podocyte inflammatory response and apoptosis in vitro.MethodsWe detected the expression level of serum miR-4286 and CADM1 in DKD patients and healthy individuals. Clinical correlations of miR-4286 with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), and KDIGO risk categories were analyzed. We detected the expression change of miR-4286 in podocytes treated. ROC analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic value of miR-4286. Dual-luciferase assay and RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) were used to validate the targeting relationship between miR-4286 and CADM1. TNF-α and IL-1β in supernatants were detected by ELISA, CADM1 protein expression was assessed by Western blotting, and apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry.ResultsIn DKD patients, upregulation of serum miR-4286 and downregulation of CADM1 were observed and negatively correlated. MiR-4286 levels were negatively correlated with eGFR, positively correlated with UACR, and increased across KDIGO risk categories. In vitro, mimicking diabetic conditions revealed that miR-4286 was upregulated and CADM1 was downregulated at both mRNA and protein levels in podocytes in HG group. Functionally, suppressing miR-4286 could ameliorate the HG-induced inflammation and apoptosis. While CADM1 knockdown aggravated both two kinds of damages. Importantly, suppressing CADM1 partly rescued the apoptosis from co-silencing miR-4286.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that miR-4286 could regulate inflammatory response and podocyte function in DKD by targeting CADM1. Upregulation of miR-4286 would suppress CADM1, further enhancing the release of inflammatory factors and apoptosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13098-026-02230-z.Keywords: Diabetic kidney disease, miR-4286, CADM1, Inflammatory response, Podocyte apoptosis

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a common complication and the main cause of end-stage renal failure [1], accounting for nearly one-third of all cases of end-stage renal failure in the world [2]. It affects a large proportion of diabetes, and the high diabetes incidence in developing countries is likely to increase [3]. The main causes are chronic high blood sugar, high blood pressure, abnormal lipid profile, and genetic susceptibility [4]. African and Indigenous people are more prone to it [5, 6]. It greatly affects quality of life, and dialysis and transplantation will bring financial and emotional burden. It also causes worse cardiovascular disease and infection, ultimately leading to reducing life expectancy [7, 8].

Treating DKD consists of glucose, blood pressure, and cholesterol levels management and RAS-blocking medicines (ACEIs/ARBs) prescription [9, 10]. Even if these treatments slow down the deterioration, they can’t stop the disease and cause unwanted reactions including low high blood pressure and increased potassium [11]. SGLT2 inhibitors seem to provide new expectations, although their long-term efficiency needs to be further investigated [12]. Understanding the actual development of DKD is important for finding new therapeutic targets. Persistent high glucose leads to oxidative stress and inflammation [13, 14], tubulointerstitial fibrosis [15], and podocytes dysfunction characterized by cell adhesion impairment [16, 17]. Better understanding of these processes may provide new insights for understanding DKD and improve patients’ health.

MicroRNAs are involved in the development of DKD and have attracted extensive attentions. Based on systematic reviews, we found that consistently miRNAs were aberrantly expressed, and six significantly altered molecules were involved in major pathological processes [18]. Urinary miR-3137, miR-4270 and miR-223-3p were reported as diagnostic molecules [19, 20]. Mechanistically, miR-802 promoted renal inflammation [21] and fibrosis while miR-214 inhibited fibrosis by targeting DIAPH1 [22]. Interestingly, miR-4286 was reported to be up-regulated in elevated glomerular miRNAs of DKD [23]. However, the further detailed functional properties and governing molecules of miR-4286 remain unclear and need further investigation.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the functional role and molecular mechanisms of miR-4286 in DKD. Firstly, bioinformatics analysis was used to identify potential target molecules. The differential expression and diagnostic value in patient sera of these molecules were validated. Furthermore, we investigated the effect of miR-4286 knockdown on apoptosis and inflammatory markers in high glucose stressed podocytes. Subsequently, we verified that miR-4286 directly bound to the 3’UTR of cell adhesion molecule 1 (CADM1). In addition, by changing the CADM1 level, we also investigated the effect of miR-4286/CADM1 downstream signaling pathway on controlling high glucose induced inflammation and apoptosis were investigated.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The downstream target genes of miR-4286 were predicted using two independent databases with their respective default settings. First, TargetScanHuman (version 8.0) was employed with the human species option, and predictions were performed based on the cumulative weighted context + + score, utilizing the default results table which lists the most prevalent transcript per gene. Second, the prediction system of miRDB (version 6.0) was applied with the human species parameter to identify potential target genes. To identify genes with direct relevance to the disease context, a list of DKD-associated genes was retrieved from the GeneCards database (version 5.16) using the keyword “diabetic kidney disease” and default relevance filters, yielding a comprehensive list of DKD-associated genes. The union of predicted targets from TargetScanHuman and miRDB was then intersected with the DKD gene set from GeneCards to obtain a focused list of candidate genes that are both predicted targets of miR-4286 and implicated in DKD.

The Gene Ontology biological processes (GO BP) and KEGG pathways enrichment analysis for these overlapping target genes were performed based on Metascape platform.

Clinical sample collection

This study comprised 78 DKD patients and 78 healthy individuals from Xingtai People’s Hospital. The inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study were formulated with reference to published literature [24]. Inclusion criteria for the DKD group: (1) Aged ≥ 18 years with confirmed type 2 diabetes mellitus; (2) DKD defined as persistent albuminuria (urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, UACR ≥ 30 mg/g) and/or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL·min⁻¹·(1.73 m²)⁻¹ for over 3 months, with chronic kidney disease from other etiologies ruled out by clinical and laboratory examinations. Inclusion criteria for the healthy control group: (1) Age- and gender-matched to the DKD group; (2) No history of diabetes, chronic kidney disease or cardiovascular disease; (3) Unremarkable results in physical and laboratory examinations. Exclusion criteria: (1) Confirmed non-diabetic kidney diseases (e.g., primary glomerulopathy, lupus nephritis, obstructive nephropathy); (2) Type 1 diabetes, gestational diabetes or other specific types of diabetes; (3) Uncontrolled hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 160 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 100 mmHg); (4) Severe hepatic insufficiency (Child-Pugh class C), malignant tumors, active tuberculosis or hematological disorders; (5) Occurrence of diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state, acute cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events or severe infections within the recent 3 months; (6) Pregnant or lactating women; (7) Participation in other clinical drug trials within the recent 3 months; (8) Poor compliance or incomplete clinical data.

According to the approval of the ethics committee and the written consent of all subjects, fasting venous blood was collected. Serum samples were prepared and stored in aliquots at -80℃. We documented baseline parameters including age, gender, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, laboratory values including HDL-C and LDL-C, as well as key renal function parameters: eGFR and UACR. Based on these values, patients were classified according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) risk categories [25] (low, moderately increased, high, and very high risk), which stratify disease severity by combining eGFR and albuminuria levels.

Cell culture

Human glomerular podocytes were purchased from Yaji, China. The cells were cultured at 37 ℃, 5% CO2 in a humid atmosphere. The culture medium (RPMI 1640, Gibco, USA) was supplemented with 10% FBS (Invitrogen, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio, China). In addition, 25 mM glucose was used to simulate high glucose (HG) condition, while 5.5 mM glucose was used for normal glucose (NG) condition.

Cell transfection

We transfected human glomerular podocytes (plated in 6-well plates at ~ 70% density) with various constructs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA), including miR inhibitor, miR mimic, si-CADM1 (small interfering RNA targeting CADM1), their corresponding controls (inhibitor-NC, mimic-NC, si-NC), and combined treatments. The transfection medium was then replaced with complete RPMI 1640 after 6 h, and cells were collected 48 h later for functional investigations of miR-4286 and CADM1.

Real time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Gene and miRNA expression were quantified by RT-qPCR in quadruplicate. This process involved RNA extraction from clinical serum samples and cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and subsequent cDNA generation with a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan). The PCR reactions, carried out on a real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA), used a SYBR Green PCR kit (Applied Biosystems, USA), with GAPDH and U6 serving as normalization controls for mRNA and miRNA, respectively.

Luciferase reporter experiment

Both wild-type and mutated versions of the CADM1 3’UTR were cloned into reporter vectors (GenePharma, China). Cellular transfection with either miR-4286 mimics or control mimics was followed by dual-luciferase measurement (Promega, USA), with firefly luciferase values being calibrated against Renilla luminescence. Quadruplicate independent experiments were conducted.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

The endogenous interaction between miR-4286 and CADM1 mRNA was analyzed by RIP using a commercial kit (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Cell lysates were incubated with anti-AGO2 antibody (Abcam) or control IgG at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with Protein A/G beads. After washing, the bound RNA was extracted using TRIzol LS, and the enrichment of miR-4286 and CADM1 was detected by RT-qPCR and expressed as a percentage of Input. The experiment was independently repeated four times.

Western blotting

Cells from each treatment group were lysed to extract total protein. Protein samples were separated by electrophoresis and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against CADM1 (Abcam) and GAPDH (Proteintech), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime). Signals were developed using ECL substrate (Tanon), captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon). CADM1 protein expression was normalized to GAPDH. All experiments were independently performed four times.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in supernatants were measured using ELISA kits (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The optical density at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader and the concentration was calculated based on the standard curves in four independent experiments.

Cell apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis was measured using flow cytometric analysis. After treatment, cells were harvested and stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI kit (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Finally, the cell pellets were resuspended in binding buffer and incubated in the dark with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide sequentially for 15 min. The whole process was repeated in four independent experiments.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed using a statistical strategy involving GraphPad Prism 10.1 and SPSS 27.0 software. Metric data were presented as mean ± SD and analyzed using t-tests for two groups or ANOVA with Sidak’s/Dunnett’s post-hoc analyses for multiple groups. Categorical variables and continuous variables were analyzed using the chi-square test and Student’s t-test, respectively. Furthermore, Spearman’s correlation, ROC curves and binary logistic regression models were implemented to analyze the associations, diagnostic efficiencies and predictive factors using a significance level of p < 0.05.

Effects of miR-4286 on cell apoptosis and inflammatory factor expression

As shown in Fig. 2A, successful establishment of miR-4286 overexpression and knockdown models was achieved. Under HG conditions, the expression level of miR-4286 increased with prolonged exposure time (Fig. 2B). Functional experiments demonstrated that HG treatment promoted the release of inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β, while knockdown of miR-4286 reduced the expression levels of these factors (Fig. 2C-D). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that the HG-induced apoptosis rate showed a declining trend following miR-4286 knockdown (Fig. 2E).

Identification and validation of CADM1 as a target of miR-4286

Bioinformatic screening, based on the overlap of miR-4286 target predictions (TargetScanHuman: 4,674 transcripts; miRDB: 1,242 genes) with a DKD-associated gene set from GeneCards (12,799 genes), identified 258 candidate genes common to both (Fig. 3A). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these target genes were significantly enriched in the cell adhesion molecules pathway from the KEGG analysis (Fig. 3B), as well as in biological processes related to cell junction organization and cell-cell adhesion from the Gene Ontology analysis, with a Sankey diagram detailing the gene-process associations (Supplement Fig. S1).

A dual-luciferase reporter assay confirmed that miR-4286 directly binds to the wild-type 3’UTR sequence of CADM1, but not to the mutant sequence (Fig. 3C-D). The RIP assay further confirmed the interaction, showing significant enrichment of both miR-4286 and CADM1 mRNA in AGO2 immunoprecipitates compared to IgG controls (Fig. 3E). In cellular models, overexpression of miR-4286 reduced CADM1 expression, while inhibition of miR-4286 increased its expression (Fig. 3F). Under HG conditions, CADM1 expression was downregulated with increasing induction time (Fig. 3G). The expression level of CADM1 was significantly lower in the serum of DKD patients compared to controls (Fig. 3H). Correlation analysis in clinical samples revealed an inverse relationship between the serum levels of miR-4286 and CADM1 (Fig. 3I).

Regulatory effects of CADM1 on inflammatory response and cellular apoptosis

The expression levels of CADM1 mRNA and protein across different treatment groups are shown in Fig. 4A and B, respectively. When examined in inflammatory factor assays, the knockdown of CADM1 in miR-4286 inhibitor cells increased the suppression of these inflammatory factors induced by miR-4286 inhibitor (Fig. 4C-D). In the miR-4286 inhibitor model, the knockdown of CADM1 partially reversed the apoptosis-inhibiting effects induced by miR-4286 inhibitor (Fig. 4E).

Discussion

Increased levels of miR-4286 are expected in the DKD context. Experimentally, suppression of miR-4286 could decrease the inflammatory response and cell death induced by HG. Furthermore, the mechanism of action of miR-4286 was investigated, and CADM1 was identified as a direct target. Through repression of CADM1, miR-4286 increased the release of inflammatory factors and altered cellular function.

Recent reports have demonstrated that miR-4286 displays highly pleiotropic biological functions with remarkable context-dependency in different pathological conditions. The pleiotropic functions of miR-4286 are well illustrated by its tumor-promoting activities in prostate and esophageal carcinomas through targeting SALL1 and INPP4A [26, 27], which is in sharp contrast to its tumor-suppressive function in glioblastoma mediated via the TGF-β pathway [28] and its protective function in alcohol-induced osteopenia achieved through inhibiting HDAC3 [29]. Our present findings are consistent with its tumor-promoting activity. Our results establish a pathogenic mechanism for the miR-4286/CADM1 axis in DKD that is consistent with its previously documented cancer-promoting functions [26, 27].

The decline of kidney function in DKD is intimately linked to the depletion of glomerular podocytes [30]. As critical components of the filtration barrier, the loss of these specialized cells represents a key pathological event. This loss is primarily driven by two interrelated processes: programmed cell death (apoptosis) [31] and chronic low-grade inflammation [32]. In the diabetic milieu, persistent metabolic disturbances create a hostile microenvironment. Inflammatory signals, including elevated cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, are not only markers of injury but also active perpetrators that can directly compromise podocyte integrity and trigger apoptotic signals [33, 34]. In this established pathogenic framework, our study elucidates a novel regulatory axis, the miR-4286/CADM1 pathway, which crucially contributes to this injurious cycle. We show that CADM1, a key adhesion molecule maintaining cell-cell contact and intracellular signaling, is directly and functionally targeted by miR-4286 in DKD podocytes. The hyperglycemia-induced upregulation of miR-4286 leads to the transcriptional repression of CADM1. Downregulation has two pathogenic effects: it directly weakens podocyte adhesion structures, promoting cell apoptosis, and initiates an intrinsic inflammatory cascade in podocytes, amplifying the production of factors such as TNF-α and IL-1β. Thus, the miR-4286/CADM1 axis acts as a key intracellular amplifier that translates diabetic metabolic stress into a potent activation of both core injury pathways, namely apoptosis and inflammation, hastening podocyte loss and disease progression.

Our identification of CADM1 as a target in DKD podocytes aligns with its role as a regulator across tissues and pathologies. Tubular damage and apoptosis in diabetic nephropathy correlate with this molecule’s α-shedding [35]. While pancreatic β-cells contain glucose-sensitive CADM1 that restricts insulin release [36]. Meanwhile, in pancreatic α-cells, CADM1 promotes gap junctional communication crucial for regulating glucagon secretion [37]. CADM1 frequently acts as a miRNA target: miR-21 triggers cardiac fibrosis by downregulating CADM1 [38], miR-214-3p speeds osteosarcoma development through the CADM1/MAPK cascade [39], and miR-423-5p enhances lung adenocarcinoma by aiming at CADM1 [40]. Our work adds DKD to the growing list of diseases where miRNA-mediated dysregulation of CADM1 drives pathology, specifically by magnifying inflammatory signals and promoting apoptosis in podocytes.

Significant therapeutic potential exists in developing strategies focused on the miR-4286/CADM1 axis, such as exploring miR-4286 blockers in pre-clinical models. Nevertheless, the diagnostic value and functional relevance of this axis require further confirmation in large, multi-center DKD groups. It should also be noted that the present study has several limitations. First, it is limited to in vitro and clinical serum analyses; thus, the pathological role of the miR-4286/CADM1 axis remains to be validated in mouse models of diabetic kidney disease. Second, although our dual-luciferase reporter assay confirmed the direct targeting of CADM1 by miR-4286, we were unable to validate the expression correlation between miR-4286 and its predicted targets (including CADM1) in renal tissues using independent, published DKD transcriptomic datasets (e.g., from the GEO database). Third, while our bioinformatic enrichment analysis highlighted the association of miR-4286 targets with cell adhesion pathways, their specific connections to other pivotal DKD pathological pathways, such as TGF-β signaling, inflammatory response, and fibrosis, were not fully elucidated and warrant further investigation. Fourth, significant differences in LDL-C levels were observed between the DKD group and healthy control group, which may act as a potential confounding factor for the detection and analysis of inflammatory markers. Abnormal LDL-C is closely correlated with systemic inflammation and renal injury [41], which may interfere with the differential expression of inflammatory indicators between groups and affect the interpretation of relevant experimental results. Subsequent studies will conduct rigorous propensity score matching or multivariate regression adjustment to eliminate the interference of LDL-C and other baseline confounding factors, to further improve the accuracy and reliability of the research conclusions. Fifth, the optimal diagnostic cut-off value of serum miR-4286 was determined as 1.23, with a sensitivity of 91.0% but only a moderate specificity of 69.2%. The relatively unsatisfactory specificity restricts its standalone application as a precise diagnostic biomarker in routine clinical screening, which may be attributed to heterogeneous baseline metabolic profiles among enrolled subjects and limited sample size in our single-center cohort. Moreover, given that current standard options like RAS/SGLT2 inhibitors have constraints, studying combination therapies that mix these new axis-targeting methods with proven drugs could yield enhanced, synergistic results.

To further elevate the translational value of this research, we outline structured future research directions and clinical transformation plans here. For preclinical verification, genetically engineered DKD mouse models with miR-4286 overexpression or knockout will be constructed to observe long-term renal function, podocyte survival and renal fibrosis status after miR-4286 antagonist intervention. Clinically, we will expand multi-center cohorts to verify the diagnostic performance of serum miR-4286, and conduct subgroup analysis stratified by blood lipid and blood glucose levels to eliminate the interference of LDL-C and other confounders on its predictive efficacy for DKD progression. In therapeutic translation, we will optimize chemically modified miR-4286 antisense oligonucleotides to improve renal tissue targeting efficiency and biosafety, and test synergistic curative effects when combined with routine SGLT2 or RAS inhibitors. Mechanistically, follow-up work will explore crosstalk between the miR-4286/CADM1 axis and TGF-β fibrotic signaling to complete the upstream and downstream regulatory network of podocyte injury. Collectively, this phased research plan provides a solid foundation for translating the miR-4286/CADM1 axis into novel diagnostic biomarkers and targeted therapeutic agents for clinical DKD management.

Conclusion

In the present study, it was demonstrated that increased levels of miR-4286 in DKD promote inflammatory cytokine release and podocyte apoptosis via suppression of CADM1. This axis is involved in high glucose-induced renal injury, and thus, miR-4286 acts as a diagnostic marker and a potential therapeutic target. Therefore, therapies targeting miR-4286 may thus provide new approaches for the treatment of DKD by preserving renal cell function, and consequently, improve the long-term clinical outcomes of patients.
