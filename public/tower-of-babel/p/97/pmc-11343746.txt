Twin support vector regression for characterizing uncertainty in surface reconstruction
Authors: ShiCheng Yu, JiaQing Miao, FeiLong Qin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface reconstruction plays a pivotal role in various fields, including reverse engineering, and oil and gas exploration. However, errors in available data and insufficient surface morphology information often introduce uncertainty into the reconstruction. It is crucial to accurately characterize and visualize the uncertainty in surface reconstruction for risk analysis and planning further data collection. To this end, this paper proposes an uncertainty characterization method based on twin support vector regression. First, various modeling data are effectively integrated and the information contained in the high-confidence sample is efficiently utilized through the uncertainty interval generated by quantiles and upper/lower bound constraints. Second, well-path points are incorporated by imposing inequality constraints on the corresponding prediction points. Finally, in order to reduce computation time, the problem of uncertainty characterization is formulated as two smaller-scale quadratic programming. The results obtained from a real fault dataset and a synthetic dataset validate the effectiveness of the proposed method. When well data are available, the generated uncertainty envelopes are constrained by well data, which can partially mitigate reconstruction uncertainties.Subject terms: Geophysics, Applied mathematics

Introduction

Surface reconstruction reveals the surface morphology and spatial distribution of objects and plays a crucial role in various fields1–3, such as industrial design, virtual reality, computer graphics, and oil and gas exploration. However, uncertainty in surface reconstruction can arise from the sparsity and imprecision of data, especially a lack of surface morphology information, and improper selection of modeling methods4–8. These sources of uncertainty interact with each other and collectively influence the simulated results generated by the reconstruction model9. The effective identification and quantification of uncertainties within the model space are essential for risk analysis and decision-making. In response to model structural uncertainty stemming from the limitations of knowledge and information scarcity, Cao et al.10 established a framework for inverse uncertainty quantification on the basis of evidence theory. This framework effectively enhances computational efficiency by employing a similar system analysis method, thereby resolving the issue of high computational costs associated with evidence theory in inverse problems. Additionally, Cao et al.11 utilized the Karhunen-Loéve (KL) expansion and a nonintrusive stochastic smoothed finite element method to analyze the random fields in homogeneous and multimaterial structures under uncertain conditions, effectively quantifying uncertainties in structural responses. The method based on polynomial chaos expansion12,13 also represents a significant research direction for uncertainty quantification. These studies not only improve the efficiency and accuracy of uncertainty quantification but also bolster the robustness of models, offering a solid theoretical foundation and technical support for risk assessment and decision-making in engineering practices.

Geological surface reconstruction plays an important role in understanding underground structures14. It is the basis of research work, such as oil and gas migration path analysis and reserve calculations in oil and gas exploration and development. Consequently, in the oil and gas exploration industry, extensive attention has been given to effectively characterizing the uncertainty in geological surface reconstruction with the ultimate goal of maximizing the success rate of oil and gas exploration. There are two main methods used to characterize the uncertainty of geological surface reconstruction. The first method uses Monte Carlo stochastic models to generate many equally probable 3D surface models15–19. This method comprehensively accounts for model structural errors and effectively explores the uncertainty space of the data; however, it incurs a high computational time cost. On the other hand, the other main method generates envelopes that represent the surface’s uncertainty on the basis of the distribution of sampling points20,21. The envelopes divide the simulated space of geological surfaces into two distinct subspaces, enabling the simulation of geological surfaces in one subspace while excluding their appearance in the other, and providing spatial constraints for the subsequent reconstruction of geological surfaces. By considering the range of the envelopes, one can readily discern the spatial regions or input data that contribute more significantly to the uncertainty in surface reconstruction. Concurrently, considering the uncertainty in geological surface reconstruction through the envelopes can aid geologists in more effectively analyzing geological structures and in more comprehensively assessing the potential risks involved in oil and gas exploration. In addition, the selection of well locations is pivotal to oil and gas exploration, as it directly influences both the cost and the success rate. Characterizing the uncertainty in geological surface reconstruction by the envelopes allows for the identification of areas with high uncertainty. This facilitates an analysis of the probability of oil and gas accumulation. Caers22 established envelopes by specifying a specific interval around a geological surface, within which the geological surface changes in a particular manner. This method amplifies the uncertainty in well data. Røe et al.20 established standard multivariate normal theory and kriging interpolation to generate envelopes, effectively integrating seismic interpretation data and well data. However, this method is overly cumbersome because it requires converting the modeling data into the normal domain and subsequently transforming the simulation results back to the fault surface domain. Yu et al.21 generated envelopes via the confidence-constrained support vector interval regression (CCSVIR) model. This model is a large-scale quadratic programming problem with high complexity.

Considering the limitations of existing methodologies, this paper presents a surface uncertainty representation model based on twin support vector regression (TSVR)23. TSVR is an improvement on support vector regression, and when dealing with uncertain data, variants of TSVR quantify or characterize the uncertainty of the data by introducing additional mechanisms. Xiao et al.24 proposed an uncertain-data-based least squares twin support vector machine (ULSTSVM). The core idea of the ULSTSVM is to simulate the uncertain information of each sample by introducing a noise vector. These noise vectors are optimized during model training to reduce the impact of noise on model performance. Han et al.25 proposed the fuzzy chance constrained least squares twin support vector machine (FCC-LSTSVM) for processing data distributions with statistical uncertainty. FCC-LSTSVM minimizes the influence of data measurement noise through chance-constrained programming and a fuzzy membership function. Liang and Zhang26 proposed uncertainty-aware twin support vector machines (UATSVMs) for processing uncertain data. In UATSVM, each uncertain sample is modeled as a random vector with a Gaussian distribution. These methods improve the robustness of the model to noise and outliers by taking uncertainty into account during optimization. However, owing to the characteristics of support vector methods, the above uncertainty representation methods based on TSVR treat high-confidence sample points as points within the insensitive band and are thus unable to effectively utilize high-confidence sample points to reduce the uncertainty of reconstruction. To address this issue, this paper implements the integration of high-confidence samples through uncertainty intervals generated by quantiles and upper/lower bound constraints. Additionally, TSVR optimizes the time efficiency by decomposing the surface uncertainty characterization model into two smaller-scale quadratic programming problems27,28. Experimental results on real fault datasets and synthetic datasets show that the geological surface uncertainty representation method based on TSVR has a lower time cost and better performance than the methods proposed in8 and9, demonstrating good applicability. The surface uncertainty characterization model based on TSVR

This paper uses two nonparallel surfaces, e1x,y=x,y·w1+b1 and e2x,y=x,y·w2+b2, to characterize the uncertainty of a reconstructed surface. These two surfaces are called the upper and lower envelopes, which are specific regions around the reconstructed surface22.

The linear model

Suppose the set of sampling points used for reconstructing the geological surface is xi,yi,zi∣i=1,⋯,n, where xi,yi,zi is the coordinate of the sampling point. Denote Ai=xi,yi,i=1,⋯,n, which is referred to as the input of the i-th sampling point, and zi is called the output. Due to the limitations of data acquisition methods and equipment, the output zi of the sampling points is uncertain, usually following a certain distribution. The higher the confidence of the sampling point output, the smaller the variance of zi. The α1 quantile of zi is denoted as q1i (Pzi≤q1i=α1), and the α2 quantile is denoted as q2i, where α1 is a constant very close to 1 but less than 1, and α2 is a constant very close to 0 but greater than 0. Here, q2i,q1i is referred to as the uncertainty interval for the output of the sampling point. The length of the interval q2i,q1i is small for sampling points with high confidence, such as well-pick points. In addition, although the shape and position of the reconstructed surface are uncertain, they are typically limited by specific constraints. For instance, the position of a fault is determined by the termination points of the stratigraphic horizons during fault reconstruction. Therefore, the output zi of the sampling point usually has an upper bound ui and a lower bound li. li,ui is referred to as the interval that represents the upper and lower bounds for the output of the sampling point. The length of the interval li,ui is extremely small for sampling points with high confidence. Finally, well-path points play a crucial role in reconstructing the true geological surface, as they are known points on a specific side of the fault surface20. The input of the well-path point is assumed to be Aip=xip,yip,i=n+1,⋯,n+m, and the output is zip,i=n+1,⋯,n+m. Applying the specific inequality constraints to the interpolated points (with the input Aip,i=n+1,⋯,n+m) that correspond to the well-path points ensures that the reconstructed surface is correctly positioned relative to the well-path. Experiments

The effectiveness of the proposed method is validated through experimental simulations using a real fault dataset and a synthetic dataset. The real fault dataset contains 773 discrete points, of which 771 are fault points (including 2 well-pick points) obtained from seismic interpretation, and 2 are well-path points. Due to the uncertainty of the x-coordinates of these scattered points, their y-coordinates and z-coordinates are used as inputs, and their x-coordinates are used as outputs. The outputs of the fault points are assumed to follow a normal distribution Nxi,70, while the outputs of the well-pick points are assumed to follow a normal distribution Nxi,1. xi represents the x-coordinate sampling value of the i-th fault point. The values α1 and α2 corresponding to quantiles q1i and q2i are taken as 0.999 and 0.001, respectively. This will result in the probability of the reconstructed fault surface being within the uncertainty interval of approximately 0.998 at the sampling point. In addition, we used Gaussian kernel functions in the following experiments.

The envelopes are subsequently adjusted using well-path points and well-pick points to investigate the impact of well data on the uncertainty in surface reconstruction. The revised turquoise fault uncertainty envelopes reveal that well-pick points possess minimal uncertainty, and the updated fault envelopes consistently remain within the initial uncertainty envelopes, even in areas devoid of well data. Furthermore, the updated envelopes indicate that well data play a pivotal role in mitigating the uncertainty in surface reconstruction. Consequently, if the budget allows, drilling could be utilized to reduce uncertainty in high-uncertainty areas.

The proposed uncertainty characterization method based on TSVR (referred to as UCTSVR) and CCSVIR, as well as the uncertainty characterization method using standard multivariate normal theory (referred to as UCMSMNT) proposed by Røe et al.20, can effectively utilize well data when generating envelopes. Moreover, UCTSVR incurs a certain increase in computational time cost compared to CCSVIR when obtaining upper and lower envelopes based on Eqs. (17) and (18) due to the involvement of large matrix multiplication operations. Overall, the average total time of UCTSVR for generating initial envelopes is reduced by 47.090% compared to that of CCSVIR.

The inclusion of well data complicates the solution of quadratic programming, resulting in a significant increase in solving time for quadratic programming in UCTSVR and CCSVIR, as well as an increased proportion of model solving time during the construction and solving of quadratic programming. However, the strategy employed by UCTSVR, which involves solving two smaller-scale quadratic programming problems, results in a 57.693% reduction in the average time required for constructing and solving the quadratic programming compared to CCSVIR, as shown in Table 2. This ultimately leads to a decrease in the total time. Furthermore, compared to CCSVIR, UCTSVR eliminates the need for calculating kernel functions between well data and between well data and interpolation points, thereby reducing the computation time required for kernel functions.

Finally, according to Tables 1 and 2, the Kriging interpolation in UCMSMNT consumes a significant amount of time, resulting in the highest computational time cost. In comparison to UCMSMNT, the proposed UCTSVR method reduces the computational time cost for generating initial envelopes by 91.977% and for updating envelopes using well data by 85.917%.

As shown in Tables 3 and 4, although the time required for conversion between Cartesian space and normal space in UCMSMNT is only slightly affected by the amount of data, its proportion of the total time is small. Therefore, UCMSMNT is not an appropriate method for datasets with large data volumes. Although the time required to construct and solve quadratic programming in UCTSVR and CCSVIR also increases nonlinearly with increasing data volume, the proportion of time required to construct and solve quadratic programs is not particularly large, so UCTSVR and CCSVIR are more suitable for large datasets than UCMSMNT is.

This phenomenon shows that when UCTSVR is used to represent the uncertainty of geological surface reconstruction, because the real shape of the underground geological surface is not known, it is only necessary to set the hyperparameters C1, C2, C3 and C4 to large values, and satisfactory model performance can be obtained. Discussion

In this paper, a novel method is proposed to characterize the uncertainty in geological surface reconstruction effectively. This method generates the envelopes that characterize the uncertainty of the reconstructed surface through TSVR. However, the classical TSVR has a limitation when dealing with high-confidence sample points. It regards high-confidence sample points as points in the insensitive band and cannot integrate these sample points. To overcome this problem, this paper introduces the uncertainty intervals and upper and lower bound constraints generated by quantiles, not only effectively integrating various heteroscedasticity data, but also effectively utilizing information related to high-confidence sampling points, thereby reducing the uncertainty of surface reconstruction. The addition of regularization terms to measure the complexity of the model enables the proposed method to adopt structural risk minimization criteria, thus exhibiting good generalization performance. Additionally, the uncertainty envelopes generated by the proposed method are consistently constrained by well data, without significantly increasing the computational time. More importantly, these envelopes provide valuable positional constraints for subsequent surface reconstruction, helping to reduce uncertainty and enhance the reliability of geological surface reconstruction. Through the analysis of three experiments, this paper proves that the proposed method is superior to the existing methods in characterizing the uncertainty of geological surfaces. Therefore, the method proposed in this paper not only provides a new perspective on uncertainty characterization in surface reconstruction, but also theoretically extends the application of TSVR in uncertainty characterization.

Although the proposed model effectively reduces the modeling time by decomposing uncertainty characterization into two smaller-scale quadratic programming problems, several limitations remain. First, the proposed method has room for improvement in reducing the complexity of the model and time cost. Secondly, the characterization of the uncertainty of geological surface reconstruction in current studies relies mainly on parametric methods, which limits the universality of the model. To overcome this challenge, nonparameterized surface uncertainty characterization methods constitute a potential research direction worthy of attention in the future.
