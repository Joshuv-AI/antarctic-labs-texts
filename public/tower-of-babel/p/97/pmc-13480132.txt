Beta-Blockers for Vasovagal Syncope in Aging Subjects: A Randomized Controlled Trial and Individual Patient Data Meta-Analysis of Clinical Trials
Authors: Nariman Sepehrvand, Sanaz Hatami, Satish R. Raj, Lisa Semeniuk, Rasha Hamzeh, Felix Ayala-Paredes, Giuliano Becker, Teresa Kus, Panagiota Flevari, Angel Moya-Mitjans, Robert S. Sheldon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies provided conflicting evidence of benefit of beta-blockers (BBs) in preventing vasovagal syncope (VVS), with possible benefit in older adults, leading to North American guidelines accepting BBs as a reasonable but weak option in patients aged ≥ 42 years with VVS (class IIb). In the Prevention of Vasovagal Syncope Trial 5 (POST 5), we conducted a randomized clinical trial of metoprolol in the prevention of VVS in patients aged ≥ 40 years.MethodsPatients aged ≥ 40 years with ≥ 1 syncopal spell in the prior year and ≥ –2 points for the Calgary Syncope Symptom Score were randomized to receive either metoprolol or matching placebo for 12 months. Patient-level meta-analysis of patients with age ≥ 40 years was done for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) studying the effect of BBs in VVS.ResultsA total of 51 patients (age 64 ± 11 years, 59% female) were included in POST 5 (26 metoprolol, 25 placebo). Treatment arms were balanced in demographic and clinical characteristics. Eight patients (31%) in the metoprolol arm and 10 (40%) in the placebo arm experienced syncope during a median follow-up period of 12 months (P = 0.69). In the pooled analysis of 173 patients from 4 RCTs, 29 of 85 patients on BB (34%) and 30 of 88 patients (34%) on placebo had at least one syncope recurrence (odds ratio 0.99, 95% confidence interval 0.43, 2.29).ConclusionBBs showed no effect on reducing syncope recurrence in older patients with VVS. These results may inform practice guidelines by removing their recommendations for using BBs in this specific context.Clinical Trial RegistrationNCT02123056.

Vasovagal syncope (VVS) is a syndrome of transient loss of consciousness with paroxysmal reflex hypotension and/or bradycardia that poses a significant healthcare burden.1 One of the earliest treatments to be attempted was beta receptor blockade, with the rationale that animal studies suggested beta-receptor involvement in ventricular baroreceptor reflexes,2 isoproterenol could trigger syncope during tilt testing,3,4 and beta-blockers could counteract the isoproterenol effect.5 This treatment was investigated in a number of randomized clinical trials (RCTs),6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 including the Prevention of Vasovagal Syncope Trial 1 (POST 1), which appeared to show no benefit of metoprolol in preventing recurrence of syncope.10

In a pooled analysis of data from an observational study (N = 153)12 and the POST 1 trial,10 the hazard ratio estimate for the effect of beta-blockers was reported as 1.58 (confidence interval [CI] 1.00-2.31) for patients aged < 42 years, and 0.52 (CI 0.27-1.01) for patients aged ≥ 42 years (P for interaction = 0.007), suggesting a benefit in patients aged ≥ 42 years.13 The 2018 European Society of Cardiology guideline recommended that beta-adrenergic blocking drugs not be considered in the treatment of VVS (class III, Level of Evidence A).6 The 2017 American College of Cardiology, American Heart Association, and Heart Rhythm Society guidelines14 and the 2020 Canadian Cardiovascular Society syncope guidelines15 suggested that beta-blockers might be reasonable to use in patients aged ≥ 42 years with recurrent VVS (class IIb, Level of Evidence B-Non-Randomized). Although both recommendations were based on relatively weak evidence, these transatlantic variations were likely due to minor differences in the interpretation and weighing of the existing evidence.16

Given these uncertainties, we designed and conducted the Prevention of Vasovagal Syncope Trial 5 (POST 5) to inform management of VVS with metoprolol in older patients. POST 5 aimed to assess the effect of metoprolol on the likelihood of syncope recurrence in older adults with VVS. To complement these results, we also performed a systematic review with an individual patient-level meta-analysis of RCTs of the effect of beta-blockers in reducing recurrences of syncope in older adults with recurrent VVS.

Methods

POST 5 randomized clinical trial

The study rationale and methods were previously published.17 In brief, POST 5 (NCT02123056) was a prospective, parallel design, 2-arm, placebo-controlled RCT that enrolled patients aged ≥40 years, with a score ≥ -2 on the Calgary Syncope Symptom Score for Structurally Normal Hearts,18 and ≥1 syncopal spells in the year preceding enrollment, from 7 sites in Canada and the US.

Patients were excluded if they had other causes of syncope, were unable to give informed consent, had important noncardiovascular or cardiovascular diseases or an implanted defibrillator, glaucoma, hepatic or renal disease, urinary retention, seizure disorder, or a contraindication to beta-blockers or another clinical need for a beta-blocker. Patients with pacemakers were eligible provided that the pacemaker was not implanted for syncope. All patients provided informed consent, and the study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki (Research Ethics Board file #13-1110).

Patients were allocated randomly on a 1:1 basis using a central computerized algorithm in a double-blind manner via research electronic data capture (REDCap)19 for a prespecified period of 1 year. Medication containers were centrally filled and labeled with the randomization code number. Metoprolol was started with a dose of 50 mg twice daily, with the intent to increase to 100 mg twice daily over a maximum of 2 weeks, if tolerated. Patients who were intolerant of the study medication had it reduced to a level that permitted remission of their side effects to a minimum dose of 25 mg/d. If intolerable symptoms persisted, the medication was discontinued, and the patient was withdrawn from the study. All patients received teaching on the importance of maximizing salt and fluid intake, were reassured about the etiology and outcome, and were coached on counterpressure maneuvers (Fig. 1). Continuation of co-treatment with fludrocortisone or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors was permitted if patients were stable on these medications prior to randomization. No additional therapies were initiated specifically for the management of VVS during the follow-up period.Figure 1Prevention of Vasovagal Syncope Trial 5 (POST-5) design. design BID, twice daily; CSSS, Calgary Syncope Symptom Score; PO, orally.

The primary endpoint was the first syncope recurrence. Patients were interviewed within 1 week of the syncopal spell. The secondary endpoints were the frequency of recurrent syncopal spells and the frequency, duration, and severity of presyncopal spells as well as quality of life, measured by the Impact of Syncope on Quality of Life scale (ISQL)20 and the EuroQol—5 dimensions, 3 levels (EQ-5D-3L)21 (at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months postrandomization).

Unfortunately, insufficient funding, changing practice patterns, and slow recruitment led the trial to be terminated prematurely.

Individual patient-level data meta-analysis

Data search

We searched for RCTs (both cross-over and parallel groups designs), in any language, that assessed the effect of beta-blockers compared to placebo with a minimum treatment duration and follow-up of 3 months in patients with VVS, with or without a positive tilt table test, with any outcome measure. The PICO (population, intervention, comparison, and outcomes) that was used in this meta-analysis is provided in Supplemental Table S1.

MEDLINE, PubMed, EMBASE, Ovid, CINAHL Plus, and the Cochrane Central Register for Controlled Trials in the Cochrane Library databases were screened for eligible RCTs published from inception until March 31, 2025. The search strategy for each database is provided in Supplemental Table S2. The bibliographies of the included articles were also reviewed to identify potential eligible studies. No language restriction was applied for the search, and no specific restrictions on the publication date were used. Per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement,22 2 investigators (N.S. and S.H.) independently screened the study titles and abstracts and selected potential studies. In case of ambiguity regarding the eligibility of an individual study, based on title and abstract, the full-text article was reviewed. Any uncertainty or discrepancy was resolved upon further discussion and via input from R.S.S.

Data extraction

After selecting the final set of included studies, 2 investigators (N.S. and S.H.) independently extracted data regarding study characteristics into a pre-designed electronic form. The discrepancies were resolved through discussion and with input from the third investigator (R.S.S.). For all included studies, we contacted the authors to request access to the individual patient-level data for patients aged ≥ 40 years. No patient-level data were available for trials by Brignole et al.6 and Theodorakis et al.23 For the remaining studies, we focused on the outcome of syncope recurrence. Data were extracted for the following variables: study design, setting, inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, sample size, intervention, comparator, studied outcomes, results.

Quality assessment

RCTs were evaluated in terms of the risk of bias, using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool. The quality of evidence for each outcome was evaluated with the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) method,24 exploring the 5 different GRADE domains, including study limitations, consistency of effect, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias. Study quality assessment also was done independently by N.S. and S.H., and the discrepancies were resolved through discussion and via input from a third investigator (R.S.S.).

Baseline patient characteristics and hemodynamics were comparable between the treatment arms. The metoprolol arm had a median lifetime number of 12 syncope episodes (interquartile range [IQR]: 3, 42.5) compared to 6 episodes (IQR: 4, 25) in the placebo group. The median number of VVS episodes in the previous year was 3 (IQR: 2, 5.25) for the metoprolol vs 3 (IQR: 2, 4) for the placebo arm, respectively.

Prior to enrollment, 7.6% of metoprolol patients and 12.0% of placebo patients had received fludrocortisone. Serotonin reuptake inhibitors had been used by 15.3% and 16% of patients in the metoprolol and placebo groups, respectively. No patients had previously used midodrine or disopyramide.

Eight patients (31%) in the metoprolol arm and 10 patients (40%) in the placebo arm experienced syncope during a median follow-up period of 12 month. The 1-year actuarial syncope recurrence rate was identical to that reported above. Survival analysis showed no significant difference between metoprolol and placebo in syncope-free survival (log-rank P = 0.69; Fig. 2).Figure 2Kaplan–Meier curves for the effect of metoprolol as compared to placebo in patients with vasovagal syndrome aged ≥ 40 years in the Prevention of Vasovagal Syncope Trial 5 (POST-5).

The ISQL score improved from baseline to 12-months follow-up in both the metoprolol (26.3 ± 12.6-16.4 ± 15.7) and placebo (20.4 ± 12.4-11.5 ± 7.9) study arms. No significant difference between study arms was present. No significant change over time occurred in EQ-5D-3L scores in each study arm, and no difference was observed between the metoprolol and placebo arms (Supplemental Fig. S1).

The primary inclusion criterion across studies was a history of recurrent VVS, with at least 1-3 previous syncopal episodes. The median number of syncopal episodes in the year prior to randomization ranged from 3 to 5 across studies.

In the pooled cohort, 29 of the 85 patients in the beta-blocker arm (34%), and 30 of 88 patients (34%) in the placebo arm had recurrence of syncope (odds ratio [OR] 0.99, 95% CI 0.43, 2.29, I2 = 25%; Fig. 4).Figure 4Forest plot comparing beta-blockers vs placebo in patients with vasovagal syncope with age ≥ 40 years for the outcome of syncope recurrence. Risk of bias was mostly low-to-unclear (Supplemental Table S3). However, one study had a high risk of bias for blinding of participants and personnel due to different dosing schedules between treatment arms; a second study had a high risk of bias for incomplete outcome data due to a substantial number of discontinuations (Supplemental Table S3; Supplemental Figs. S2 and S3).

Sensitivity analyses, including comparisons between fixed- and random-effects models, as well as leave-one-out analyses (Supplemental Table S4), yielded consistent results.

The funnel plot (Supplemental Fig. S4) for the comparison of beta-blockers vs placebo in patients with VVS aged ≥ 40 years showed reasonable symmetry, suggesting an absence of significant publication bias. However, with only 4 studies included, this assessment of publication bias should be interpreted with caution.

A subgroup analysis between studies investigating the effect of nonselective vs β1-selective beta-blockers against placebo in patients with VVS aged ≥ 40 years showed no significant difference across those subgroups (P = 0.18), although analysis was limited with the presence of only a single small cross-over RCT in the nonselective beta-blocker group (Supplemental Fig. S5). Similarly, no significant difference occurred across sex groups in the effect of beta-blockers in preventing recurrent syncope in older patients with VVS (P = 0.32; Supplemental Fig. S6).

Discussion

The POST 5 trial reported comparable syncope recurrence rates in patients treated with either metoprolol or placebo. In addition, a meta-analysis of pooled individual patient data from 4 randomized clinical trials revealed very similar odds of recurrence.

Addressing guidelines gap

Although the 2018 European Society of Cardiology guidelines6 recommend against use of beta-blockers (class III, LOE A), the 2017 American College of Cardiology, American Heart Association, and Heart Rhythm Society Guidelines14 and the 2020 Canadian Cardiovascular Society Clinical Practice Update15 maintained class IIb recommendations for its possible use (LOE B-NR) in patients aged ≥ 42 years with recurrent VVS. Previous uncertainties arose from underpowered subgroup analyses and observational studies. Our results might inform future North American guidelines and position statements. The use of beta-blockers could be relegated to class III (no benefit) across all age groups.

Clinical implications

In this meta-analysis, beta-blockers showed no therapeutic benefit in preventing VVS in adults aged ≥ 40 years, a result similar to findings from the general VVS nonrandomized cohort.32 A recent meta-analysis of trials in the field of syncope showed no significant reduction in syncope recurrence with beta-blockers in patients without any age restriction,33 similar to the results shown in Supplemental Figure S7.

Current conventional treatment begins with explanation and reassurance, and then includes (where not contraindicated or impractical) increased salt and fluid intake34,35 and physical counterpressure maneuvers.36, 37, 38 Their supporting evidence is at best of low to moderate quality. Fludrocortisone is used, but the evidence is restricted to prespecified secondary analyses of the foundational clinical trial.39 Only the use of midodrine is supported by a positive clinical trial.40,41 The evidence for the use of a specific dual-chamber pacemaker is very strong, but it is restricted to the small population of highly symptomatic older patients with documented asystole during VVS.42, 43, 44 Other options, such as atomoxetine,45 cardioneuroablation,42,43,46 and physiologically selective treatment strategies have only low degrees of evidence and determinations regarding their use await pivotal randomized trials.

Limitations

Several limitations merit consideration. Even though both the POST 5 trial and the meta-analysis report no benefit of beta-blockers in reducing the likelihood of recurrences of VVS, results were limited by early trial termination and restricted individual patient data availability as well as high risk of bias in 2 of the included studies. The POST 5 trial was stopped early due to recruitment fatigue and financial shortages, compounded by changing practice patterns. Even though pooling data from multiple trials improved our statistical power for evaluating beta-blocker effects in VVS, the aggregated sample size was insufficiently powered to detect modest treatment benefits. In this context, the absence of a statistically significant effect should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence of no effect. Several randomized studies were excluded due to short follow-up periods. Finally, we were unable to obtain patient-level data for patients aged ≥ 40 years from 2 trials,7,23 precluding pooling of data from all eligible RCTs.

Summary

Beta-blockers do not significantly reduce the likelihood of syncope recurrence in patients with VVS and age of at least 40 years. Future practice guidelines and position statements may wish to include this in recommendations concerning beta-blockers in older adults with VVS.
