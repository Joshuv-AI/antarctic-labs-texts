An in vitro study of fluoride-preloaded halloysite nanotubes to enhance the fluoride release in conventional and resin-modified glass ionomer cements
Authors: Martha Esperanza García-Moreno, Elias Nahum Salmerón-Valdés, Adriana Alejandra Morales-Valenzuela, Ulises Velázquez-Enríquez, Víctor Hugo Toral-Rizo, Edith Lara-Carrillo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe purpose of the study was to compare the fluoride release in two conventional glass ionomer cements (Ionobond, Ketac Molar) and two resin-modified glass ionomer cements (Vitrebond, Fuji II LC) adapted with halloysite nanotubes preloaded with sodium fluoride at different concentrations.MethodsIn total, 96 samples were prepared and distributed into four control groups and eight experimental groups (5 % and 10 %). Totals of 10 % and 5 % of the total weight of ionomer powder needed to prepare the samples were replaced with nanotubes, preloaded at 2,000 parts per million, respectively. The experimental groups were followed for 120 days at seven time intervals. All the samples were stored at 37 °C.ResultsAll the experimental groups showed significant differences compared with the control groups; likewise, differences were observed between the concentrations of 5% and 10%.ConclusionThe experimental groups (conventional and resin-modified glass ionomer cements adapted with preloaded nanotubes) released a higher and more constant amount of fluoride compared to the control groups.Keywords: Nanotubes, Fluoride release, Ionomers, Sodium fluoride

Introduction

Sodium fluoride is an anticariogenic agent because it can replace hydroxyapatite crystals with fluorapatite crystals with a lower solubility in acid conditions. (Zhang et al., 2023) This element has been added to different dental products to reduce or inhibit demineralization and, consequently, caries lesions. (Bahsi et al., 2019) Subsequently, sodium fluoride has been added to preventive and restorative materials with the aim of protecting the enamel surface around these materials. (Porenczuk et al., 2019) Previous studies have mentioned that the presence of fluoride on the enamel surface, in sustained concentrations for a long enough period, decreases the risk of demineralization and is able to promote the remineralization of the enamel (Garoushi et al., 2018, Patil et al., 2020).

Glass ionomer cements (GICs) are formed by an acid-based reaction of fluoroaluminosilicate glass and polyalkenoic acid; (Francois et al., 2020, Sasidharan Lathikumari and Saraswathy, 2024) they can be used as liners, sealants, bases, restorations, and adhesives for orthodontic devices. (Hasan et al., 2019) GICs are biocompatible with the pulp and are capable of chemically bonding with enamel and dentin. Nevertheless, the main advantages of GICs are their fluoride-releasing properties maintaining a fluoride-rich environment adjacent to the restoration. (Alsunbul et al., 2023b, Alsunbul et al., 2023a) However, some disadvantages, such as a low compressive and flexural strength, poor esthetics and difficult handling have been reported. (Alsunbul et al., 2023b, Francois et al., 2020, Sasidharan Lathikumari and Saraswathy, 2024) Resin-modified glass ionomers (RMGI) were developed to improve said disadvantages. (Francois et al., 2020, Nicholson et al., 2023) Several studies suggest that fluoride release from GICs and RMGI occurs during the first 24-hours, and a marked decrease is observed after this period (Barakat and Abdelrahim, 2022).

Therefore, it is necessary to create new materials that allow a larger and more constant fluoride release. Recently, nanotechnology has been used in dentistry to improve the properties of dental materials, (Malik and Waheed, 2023) including attempts to incorporate different nanoparticles (titanium dioxide, zirconia, aluminum oxide, nanohydroxyapatite, amorphous calcium phosphate, etc.) into GICs and RMGI (Fierascu, 2022, Gjorgievska et al., 2020, Karimi et al., 2019, Murugan et al., 2021).

Drug delivery could be an alternative to enhance the fluoride release of GICs and RMGI, because halloysite nanotubes are a hollow tubular structure with a length of approximately 1 micra, an external diameter of 50 to 80 nm, and a lumen diameter of 10 to 15 nm, which acts as a nanocontainer to encapsulate several substances or drugs. They are biocompatible, with low toxicity, and high mechanical properties. In addition, they are a low-cost alternative compared to other drug delivery systems (Barman et al., 2020, Bertolino et al., 2020, Danyliuk et al., 2020, Fakhruddin et al., 2021, Massaro et al., 2021).

Previously, antiseptics have been loaded into halloysite nanotubes and incorporated into different dental materials. (Degrazia et al., 2018) The incorporation of fluoride-preloaded halloysite nanotubes to GICs and RMGI, could be an alternative to enhance the fluoride release of these materials. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the fluoride release of GICs and RMGI adapted with fluoride-preloaded halloysite nanotubes. In this study, the principal hypothesis was that GICs and RMGI adapted with fluoride-preloaded halloysite nanotubes in two different concentrations would release a higher and more constant amount of fluoride, and the null hypothesis would be accepted if the experimental groups did not show statistical differences regarding the fluoride release compared to the control groups.

Materials and methods

For this study, fluoride-preloaded nanotubes were prepared. First, we weighed 1gr of halloysite nanotubes (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis Missouri, USA) using an analytical balance (OHAUS Pioneer, New Jersey, USA). Subsequently, the nanotubes were placed into a drying oven (HERAtherm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham Massachusetts, USA) at 30 °C for 12-hours. After this period, 3,560 Âµl of a 5 % solution from 3-(Trimethoxysilyl)propil methacrylate 98 % (Sigma–Aldrich) was added, and the nanotubes were moved back to the drying oven for 24-hours at 110 °C.

The incorporation of sodium fluoride (Sigma-Aldrich) into the halloysite nanotubes was carried out, placing the nanotubes into 10 ml of fluoride solution at 2,000 ppm (parts per million) under magnetic stirring for 1-hour. Subsequently 10 ml of ethanol at 96 % was added, and this preparation was sonicated for 1-hour. After this period, the nanotubes were dried in the oven at 30 °C for 10 days.

Sample preparation

Two types of GICs (Ketac Molar, 3 M ESPE, Minnesota, USA and Ionobond, VOCO GmbH, Cuxhaven, Germany) and two types of RMGI (Fuji II LC, GC Corporation, Tokio, Japan and Vitrebond 3 M ESPE) were used to create 96 samples with a diameter of 3 mm and a thickness of 1 mm in a Teflon matrix. Fig. 1 shows the samples, which were divided into four control groups and eight experimental groups subdivided in two different fluoride-preloaded nanotube concentrations (5 % and 10 %), with eight samples for each analyzed group. The sample size was calculated according to the methodology of similar previous studies, where between 5 and 8 samples were analyzed for each study group. (Brenes-Alvarado and Cury, 2021, Garoushi et al., 2018, Nagi et al., 2018) These studies designed a power analysis to calculate the sample size resulting in a requirement of a total of 9 samples per group to obtain a statistical power of 80 %, which was very similar to that used in previous studies, where a power test was not designed (Wassel and Allam, 2022).

Sample distribution diagram.

For the experimental groups, the powder amount recommended by the manufacturer was weighed 10 times to obtain a mean, and 5 % or 10 % of the powder was substituted with fluoride-preloaded halloysite nanotubes.

The GICs and RMGI were handled according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For photopolymerizable materials, a dental LED Photopolymerization Lamp was employed (Elipar, 3 M ESPE) for 30 s for Vitrebond and 20 s for Fuji II LC, verifying the minimum wavelength of 400 mW/cm2 with an LED radiometer (Demetron, Kavo Kerr, Charlotte, USA). The obtained samples were placed in plastic bottles with 5 ml of deionized water at 37 °C to simulate a buccal temperature. The fluoride-release readings were taken after 1-hour; 24-hours; and 5,15,30,60, and 120 days.

Potentiometer calibration

A potentiometer (HI-3222, Hanna Instruments, Woonsocket, Rhode Island, USA) and a fluoride ion selective electrode (HI-4110, Hanna Instruments) were used to determine the fluoride release from the samples analyzed in this study. A calibration curve is required to use the equipment. Therefore, fluoride solutions in concentrations of 1,2,10,100, and 1,000 ppm were prepared. First, 2.2106 g of sodium fluoride was dry-weighed in the analytical balance and dried in the oven at 105 °C for 1-hour. Then, the sodium fluoride was placed in a graduated flask with 1,000 ml of deionized water, obtaining a 1,000 ppm solution. To obtain the 100 ppm solution, 100 ml of the 1000 ppm solution was mixed with 900 ml of deionized water. To obtain the 10 ppm solution, 10 ml of the 1000 ppm solution was mixed with 990 ml of deionized water. Finally, to obtain the 2 and 1 ppm solution, 20 ml and 10 ml, respectively, were taken from the 100 ppm solution, and 1,000 ml was obtained through the addition of deionized water. Subsequently, 6 ml from these fluoride solutions was mixed with 6 ml of TISAB (Total Ionic Strength Adjustment Buffer) solution to establish the calibration curve registering the readings.

Fluoride release determination

Fluoride determination was carried out at the end of each period previously established for this study. The samples that were placed in plastic bottles with 5 ml of deionized water were rinsed with 1 ml of deionized water in the same plastic bottle, obtaining 6 ml that were mixed with 6 ml of TISAB II solution, because this solution works in a 1:1 proportion. The sample was deposited in a new plastic bottle with 5 ml of deionized water until the next established period of study. The preparation of 12 ml to be analyzed was magnetically stirred, introducing the electrode for 3 min until the reading was stable.

Results

After 1-hour, all the groups analyzed in this study released fluoride. The control groups, Ionobond and Fuji released higher values with 7.57 ppm and 7.48 ppm, respectively. The experimental groups with 5 % fluoride-preloaded halloysite nanotubes increased the fluoride release in all the study groups between 1 and 3 ppm. The highest values were shown by the Fuji 5 % (9.09 ppm) and the Ionobond 5 % (8.52 ppm). The experimental groups with 10 % halloysite nanotubes showed an increase of up to 5 ppm in the experimental study groups compared to the control groups; this increase was more marked between the Vitrebond control and the Vitrebond 10 %, which increased from 3.44 ppm to 8.92 ppm, respectively, and for the Ketac control and Ketac 10 % that increased from 4.82 ppm to 8.70 ppm, respectively. The group with the highest fluoride release in this period was Fuji 10 % with 10.88 ppm.

The highest fluoride release was observed after 24-hours in all the study groups analyzed. Regarding the control groups, Fuji released 8.13 ppm, and the lowest values were observed in the Ketac group with a mean of 5.75 ppm. For the groups with 5 % nanotubes, the highest fluoride release was observed in the Fuji 5 % with a mean of 9.46 ppm, and the rest of the experimental groups released concentrations between 8.15 and 8.26 ppm. The Fuji 10 % group had the highest values after 24-hours, with a mean of 11.28 ppm.

A decrease was observed in all the study groups from day 5, which was more evident in the control groups. The best performance during this period was observed in the groups with 10 % halloysite nanotubes.

After 15 days, the decrease in fluoride continued in all the study groups, although the experimental groups (5 % and 10 %) showed a higher and more consistent fluoride release, mainly the groups with the highest content (10 %). This behavior in the study groups was maintained until day 30.

The fluoride release decreased considerably after day 60 in all the study groups. Nevertheless, the highest values were observed in the groups with 5 % nanotubes. At day 120, all the groups showed a severe decrease in the fluoride release except the Vitrebond 5 %, which maintained 4.27 ppm and showed the most constant fluoride release (Table 1).

During the first hour, differences were observed between all the study groups, and after 24-hours, no differences were observed between the control and experimental groups with 5 % fluoride-preloaded halloysite nanotubes for the Vitrebond and Ionobond groups.

On day 5, differences were observed between all the study groups except for the Vitrebond, when the experimental groups were compared. For day 15, differences were found between the control and experimental groups (5 %–10 %). During day 30, the experimental group with 10 % content showed differences with respect to the control groups in all the GICs and RMGI analyzed.

For day 120, the groups with 5 % content showed differences with respect to the Vitrebond and Ionobond control groups. The groups with 10 % content presented differences with respect to the Ionobond and Ketac control groups.

Discussion

These materials have a measurable anti-caries effect when they are used as an alternative restorative, and when they release enough fluoride, suggesting that a higher fluoride content in the material is better as long as it does not interfere with its mechanical properties (Barakat and Abdelrahim, 2022, Choi et al., 2024).

Previous studies have mentioned that the fluoride release decreases considerably after 24-hours, (Patil et al., 2023) although other studies mention that this decrease was observed after 72-hours, which can reduce the ability of GICs to inhibit secondary caries (Bahsi et al., 2019).

Several studies have tried to enhance different properties of GICs and RMGI through the incorporation of titanium dioxide and zirconium nanoparticles to increase the compressive strength (Gjorgievska et al., 2020) or the incorporation of nanohydroxyapatite at 2 %, 5 %, and 10 % to reduce the porosity of these materials (Genaro et al., 2020).

Previously, nanostructures have been incorporated into dental materials at a range of 2 to 20 %.(Gjorgievska et al., 2020, Kalagi et al., 2020, Karczewski et al., 2021, Wassel and Allam, 2022) However, studies have suggested that to improve the properties of dental materials with different structures, it is necessary to use a percentage of 10 %. Specifically, halloysite nanotubes have been added to dental materials in previous studies at concentrations of 10,15, and 20 %. (Karczewski et al., 2021) In this study, we considered the lowest concentrations of halloysite nanotubes with the aim of altering the materials as little as possible.

Previous studies added 1 %,5%, and 10 % hydroxyapatite nanoparticles to GICs to increase the fluoride release, obtaining better results with the incorporation of 10 % nano-hydroxyapatite particles. (Mahmoud and Metwally, 2021) The results obtained in this study were similar until day 30, because all the experimental groups with 10 % nanotubes showed a higher fluoride release with respect to the control groups. However, the 5 % concentration showed a higher and more constant fluoride release than the control groups in all the periods analyzed in this study with the exception of the initial 24-hour period.

Regarding the control groups, the Fuji II LC released a higher amount of fluoride with respect to the other materials, whereas the Ketac Molar showed the lowest fluoride release at almost all periods of time. Previous studies reported that Fuji LC showed the highest release compared with different restorative materials. (Garoushi et al., 2018) Nevertheless, the Ketac Molar and Fuji II LC groups were the groups that most benefited from the incorporation of the 5 % and 10 % nanotubes, because this increased the fluoride release up to 4 ppm with respect to the control groups in different periods.

The periods for the analysis of the samples in this study were established based on previous similar studies where fluoride release was evaluated from 5 min after setting until 6 months of follow-up. (Dziuk et al., 2021) On the other hand, maximum periods of 28 and 42 days have been evaluated for other studies. (Choi et al., 2024, Nagi et al., 2018) Monitoring the samples for longer periods allowed us to better understand the behavior of these materials.

Previous studies modified GICs and RMGI with various nanostructures without evaluating or reporting alterations in the setting time of these materials. (Gjorgievska et al., 2020, Wassel and Allam, 2022) In this study, the setting time in all experimental groups was not observed to be altered by the incorporation of nanotubes at 5 and 10 %. Nevertheless, studies have reported a decrease, with respect to the working time, when amorphous calcium phosphate nanoparticles were added to RMGI, attributing these alterations to the large size of the nanoparticle. (Karimi et al., 2019) Therefore, it is advisable to carry out future studies to assess the setting time with a specific methodology.

These findings could benefit the development of new materials that allow maintaining a restoration free of caries recurrence for a longer time, while increasing the material longevity. Furthermore, these new materials could inhibit the demineralization process in the adjacent tooth structure avoiding the future presence of carious lesions.

Conclusion

The null hypothesis was rejected because the conventional and resin-modified glass ionomer cements adapted with fluoride-preloaded halloysite nanotubes had an enhanced fluoride release, with a higher and more constant release, which could be beneficial against demineralization and secondary caries. On the other hand, it was confirmed that nanotechnology is an alternative capable of enhancing the beneficial properties of different dental materials.

Limitations

In this study, the fluoride release from GICs and RMGI modified with halloysite nanotubes was analyzed for 120 days and in controlled laboratory conditions. Therefore, it is important to develop future studies to analyze these materials for longer periods of time, evaluating the possible variability in the fluoride release and the alterations that these materials may undergo in mechanical and setting properties, as well as their interaction with other materials and the behavior of these modified materials under clinical conditions.
