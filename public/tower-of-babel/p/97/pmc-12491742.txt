Disentangling unobserved heterogeneity: Novel application of a statistical method to evaluate a multilevel healthcare intervention for hispanic individuals living on the United States-Mexico border with comorbid diabetes and hypertension
Authors: MinJae Lee, Juliana Z. Lopez, Natalia I. Heredia, Maria Zolezzi, Soo Kyung Park, Paul G. Yeh, Lisa Mitchell-Bennett, Belinda M. Reininger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIndividuals with diabetes and hypertension represent a high-risk population with poor prognosis and high healthcare costs. Clinicians recognize the complexities of treating patients with multiple chronic conditions; however, the research literature shows little attention to characterizing change patterns in hypertension outcomes for participants of health interventions. The aim of this study was to characterize individual-level longitudinal trajectories of heterogeneity in blood pressure among a Hispanic population with uncontrolled type II diabetes and hypertension enrolled in a multi-level intervention.MethodsThis study was implemented along the US-Mexico border. Using blood pressure results collected longitudinally between 2013 and 2020 from 2476 participants with diabetes and high blood pressure at baseline, we conducted group-based trajectory modeling to identify latent groups for systolic blood pressure (SBP). We evaluated participant engagement characteristics and other individual-level characteristics and elucidated how these factors were associated with group membership.ResultsWe identified four distinct SBP trajectory groups. Three groups demonstrated improvement, two of which had clinically significant changes in SBP that were maintained through 24 months. One group was resistant to SBP improvements despite the intervention. While all groups maintained higher than normal SBP levels, the group characteristics elucidate factors that contribute to improvement or lack thereof.ConclusionUnderstanding SBP trajectories in the context of population heterogeneity can inform tailoring approaches undertaken by interventions. These findings inform approaches for clinics to more effectively match screening of non-medical drivers of health to individual patient needs.

Introduction

Hypertension is the leading risk factor for cardiovascular disease (Lewington et al., 2002), the primary cause of death in the United States (U.S.) (Martin et al., 2024). Hypertension affects 29 % of the U.S. population, with less than half (48.3 %) of adults meeting recommended blood pressure targets (Fryar et al., 2017). Among individuals with diabetes, hypertension prevalence rises to 70–80 % (National diabetes statistic report, 2024). While the rate of diabetes mortality decreased 0.9 % per annum between 2000 and 2019, the rate of hypertension-related diabetes mortality increased 2.0 % per annum (Ouyang et al., 2022). Individuals with comorbid hypertension and diabetes have a poorer prognosis. Guidelines vary on optimal blood pressure targets for individuals with diabetes (Passarella et al., 2018). Reducing systolic blood pressure (SBP) by 10 mmHg reduced mortality (Emdin et al., 2015). In a study of populations with and without diabetes, a five mmHg reduction in SBP reduced the risk of cardiovascular events by 10 % (Rahimi et al., 2021).

The Chronic Care Model (CCM) provides a framework for health systems to deliver effective chronic care (Wagner, 1998). The Expanded CCM is multilevel, incorporating community support for chronic disease management (Barr et al., 2003). CCM programs have varied widely in their implementation of the framework's components (Stellefson et al., 2013), with self-management support the most common (Davy et al., 2015). Studies on the CCM have demonstrated its effectiveness (Stellefson et al., 2013) across conditions, including hypertension (Mohler and Mohler, 2005; Goh et al., 2022). A meta-analysis found CCM programs improved hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) by 0.5 % and reduced SBP by 2.8 mmHg, but individual characteristics were not studied (Elissen et al., 2013).

Recognizing that patients are not homogenous, healthcare providers have looked for solutions to manage large and diverse patient panels (Mohler and Mohler, 2005). Stratifying patients into risk groups with similar medical and social needs profiles allows clinicians the ability to better organize care teams and tailor services (Wagner et al., 2019; Savitz and Bayliss, 2021), but evaluation results using this approach are limited. Latent class modeling approaches, such as group-based trajectory modeling, allow the identification and characterization of distinct groups (Nagin, 1999; Sands et al., 2017). Characterization of this heterogeneity could guide resources to high-need patients to improve care (Imes et al., 2018; Nagin and Odgers, 2010; Vadhariya et al., 2019). Additionally, blood pressure improvements seen at the group level during an intervention are not equally distributed across all participants. Accordingly, the purpose of this study is to characterize individual-level heterogeneity in blood pressure using latent class modeling among a Hispanic population with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and hypertension enrolled in a multi-level intervention based on the expanded CCM.

Methods

Parent study

Salud y Vida is a multi-level intervention implemented in a low-income Hispanic population along the U.S.-Mexico border. In collaboration with clinical care, the intervention expanded care to community and home settings (Zolezzi et al., 2022; Lopez et al., 2024). Clinicians implemented the Medical Home model, a care delivery model using both a patient-centric and collaborative approach to care provision and coordination (Adamson, 2011). Furthermore, they identified and referred high-risk patients with T2DM and uncontrolled HbA1c (>8 %) to the intervention for additional chronic care management support. The Salud y Vida intervention was implemented between 2013 and 2020. The initial focus was on T2DM and, over time, was expanded to simultaneously support management of high blood pressure. Participants received self-management support for up to two years through community health worker (CHW) visits and phone calls, Diabetes Self-Management Education and Support (DSMES), and additional services to address barriers such as transportation and financial support for medications and clinical visits. The intervention has demonstrated improvement in HbA1c and blood pressure within three months, and results were maintained through 24 months (Reininger et al., 2022; Lopez et al., 2024). While the program demonstrated SBP reduction of 6.49 mmHg, the variation in baseline SBP and SBP changes was not detectable with mean-based statistical modeling.

Study design

The present study is a secondary analysis of the intervention using data collected between 2013 and 2020. This study was approved by UTHealth Houston Institutional Review Board, and all participants completed written informed consent.

We conducted blood pressure screenings and used interviewer-administered questionnaires to assess medication use and adherence, depression, medical home status, and other important covariates described in detail below. All aspects of implementation, including visits and DSMES class attendance, were documented in a case management database, from which our analytic database was drawn.

Measures and data collection

Blood pressure

Trained staff conducted blood pressure screenings using the WelchAllyn ProBP2400 or Omron HBP1300. Baseline screenings were measured in a clinical or community setting. Follow-up screenings were measured by trained CHWs, usually in the participant's home. Follow-up was provided up to 12 months for participants who achieved the target HbA1c (≤9 %) and up to 24 months for participants who required additional time. Between 2013 and 2018, the intervention collected one blood pressure screening at each visit. Starting 2019, two measurements were collected and recorded at each visit. For the analysis, the average of the two recorded blood pressure screenings was used for visits with two recorded values. Results were categorized according to the American Heart Association (AHA) blood pressure categories: normal (blood pressure of <120/80 mmHg), elevated (combination of systolic between 120 and 129 mmHg and diastolic <80 mmHg), stage 1 hypertension (systolic between 130 and 139 mmHg or diastolic between 80 and 89 mmHg), stage 2 hypertension (systolic ≥140 mmHg or diastolic ≥90 mmHg), severe hypertension (systolic ≥180 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥120 mmHg). SBP and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were used to determine pulse pressure (=SBP - DBP).

Participant engagement

The number of CHW visits and DSMES classes were used to measure participant engagement. The number of DSMES classes/follow-up months was also assessed, given that follow-up time differed by participants.

Depression

Two separate screeners were used to measure depression. The first involved the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2), a twoitem scale assessing depressive symptoms over the past two weeks. The second involved asking participants if they had been diagnosed with depression. The latter was used to generate a binary depression variable (yes/no) for use when the PHQ-2 score was missing (30 %).

Demographic variables

Baseline demographic characteristics were used for longitudinal modeling, including age, sex (male/female), Spanish as a primary language, employment status (yes/no), marital status (married/other), education level (greater than eighth grade/other), insurance status (yes/no), and having a medical home (yes/no).

Statistical analysis

Among study participants, 3806 had blood pressure screenings of >120/80 mmHg at baseline and had blood pressure data collected in at least two visits. We excluded 159 records due to having a pulse pressure > 100 (Tang et al., 2020). These individuals (2.4 % of sample) represented a high-risk population needing care beyond the scope of this intervention. The resulting sample of 2476 participants had a baseline blood pressure within the AHA's cut points for stage 1 hypertension, stage 2 hypertension, and severe hypertension. We compared the characteristics of 3806 participants (full sample) to the 2476 participants (final sample) and the results were similar (data not shown).

To characterize longitudinal trajectories of SBP, we conducted group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) (Nagin, 2005), a nonlinear regression modeling approach accounting for individual participant-level variability. To identify distinct SBP trajectories, we estimated the posterior probability of group membership based on individual SBP profiles and assigned each participant to their highest probability trajectory group. Mean group assignment possibilities (i.e., theoretical proportion) were then computed and compared to the actual group assignment proportion for calculating misclassification error. A higher mean group assignment possibility indicated a better fit with a predefined minimum cutoff of >0.7 considered acceptable. The number of trajectories was determined by the lowest Nagin's Bayesian information criterion (BIC) (Nagin, 2005), average posterior group membership probabilities, and the number of patients in each group. Trajectory groups with more than 50 participants were used for model cutoff.

Each variable included in our GBTM was evaluated to see how they were associated with the identified group membership. In addition to baseline data including demographic characteristics (age, sex, preferred language, marital status, insurance, education, employment), depression, self-reported medication use, and medical support as well as baseline DBP levels, we assessed engagement level-related variables including both time-invariant variables (number of CHW visits, completion rates of DSMES sessions per month, follow-up period) and time-variant variables such as the number of DSMES classes at each time point (baseline, month three, six, eight, …, 24) for evaluating their longitudinal associations with trajectory shape. As a sensitivity analysis, we conducted the same modeling after excluding the participants who had imputed binary depression variables due to missing a PHQ-2 score at baseline (30 %). We evaluated underlying assumptions, such as linearity of associations, while conducting the aforementioned statistical analysis. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary NC) and statistical significance was assumed at the 0.05 level.

Results

Demographic and baseline characteristics

The mean age was 53 years, 31.7 % were male, and 67.2 % reported Spanish as their preferred language (Table 1). Only 37.2 % of participants were employed, 51.7 % had higher than eighth grade education, 59.4 % were married, and 22 % were insured. At enrollment, 80 % self-reported they had a medical home, 26.7 % self-reported taking antihypertensive medications and 37.7 % reported T2DM-related medications. Mean SBP and DBP levels at enrollment were 138 mmHg (SD = 15.9) and 81.2 mmHg (SD = 10), respectively. The mean number of DSMES classes and CHW visits was 6.4 and 4.4, respectively. The optimum number of trajectory groups identified was four groups (Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 1). The posterior probability of each group was very high, with the lowest average posterior probability being >0.87, greater than the recommended value of 0.7, which suggests a good fit for the model. All four groups had distinct shapes, with curvilinear/cubic functions identified as significant in all trajectory groups with an adequate number of participants in each group (Table 2, Table 3, Fig. 1). All groups had hypertension at baseline and follow-ups, but transitioned between stages uniquely. Group 1, named “maintained”, included 1245 (50.3 %) participants and had the lowest mean baseline SBP (127.8 mmHg) and DBP (78.7 mmHg). Group 2, named “vacillating”, had 388 (15.7 %) participants with a mean baseline SBP of 132.7 mmHg and DBP of 79.8 mmHg (i.e., stage 1 hypertension). SBP levels increased in early follow-up periods, followed by a decrease after nine months (note a slight increase was found from 21 to 24 months, but this finding was based on a small number of participants retained in the program longer to receive additional support). Group 3, named “rapid decrease”, had 592 (23.9 %) participants with a mean baseline SBP of 150.8 mmHg and DBP of 84.2 mmHg (i.e., stage 2 hypertension) and showed a rapid decrease (about 20 mmHg) in SBP levels from baseline to nine months. Group 4, named “slow decrease” included 251 (10.1 %) participants with the highest mean baseline SBP of 167.3 mmHg and DBP of 90.6 mmHg (i.e., stage 2 hypertension) and demonstrated slow decrease in The parameter estimates and corresponding 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) for each trajectory curve are shown in Table 2, where parameter estimates of linear, quadratic, and cubic terms reflect non-linear trajectories. Adding the predetermined time-varying variable, number of DSMES classes improved the overall model fit (BIC = −44,920 [with time-varying] vs. -44,958 [without time-varying]). A higher number of DSMES classes was associated with lower SBP levels over time in Groups 1–3, and this association was strongest in Group 3 “rapid decrease.”

In comparison with Group 1, Group 2 “vacillating” was the only group with lower engagement levels. Both Group 3 “rapid decrease” and Group 4 “slow decrease” had higher engagement levels compared to Group 1, especially Group 3 “rapid decrease”, which had a stronger association with higher number of DSMES classes and a higher completion rate of DSMES classes per month. For example, the log-odds increase by 0.04 per class completion. i.e., exp (0.04) = 1.04 times higher odds of being in Group 3 versus Group 1 (odds ratio (OR) =1.04, 95 % CI = [0.9, 1.2]). Both Group 3 “rapid decrease” (OR = exp(−4.55) = 0.01, 95 % CI = [0.001, 2.3]) and Group 4 “slow decrease” (OR = exp(−4.21) = 0.02, 95 % CI = [0.001, 3.39]) were less likely to be on antihypertensive medication at baseline, while participants on antihypertensive medication at baseline had 2.53 times significantly higher odds of being in Group 2 “vacillating” (vs. Group 1 “maintained”) compared to those with no antihypertensive medication use. Group 4 “slow decrease” was strongly associated with having a medical home (OR = exp(3.28) = 26.6, 95 % CI = [1.22, 578]) and having depression at baseline (OR = exp(2.01) = 7.46, 95 % CI = [0.3, 172.4]), being unemployed at baseline (OR = exp(2.45) = 11.6, 95 % CI = [0.53, 252.1]), and being male (OR = exp(2.10) = 8.2, 95 % CI = [0.54, 124.0]). Group 3 “rapid decrease” was associated with younger age, and Group 2 “vacillating” with older age compared to Group 1. Group 2 “vacillating” was more likely to be unmarried, uninsured, and higher educated compared to Group 1. Group 1 “maintained” had more Spanish speakers, shorter follow-up months, fewer males, more employed individuals, fewer with a medical home, and fewer participants with depression. Higher baseline DBP and pulse pressure levels were found in Groups 2–4 compared to Group 1, but these associations were found to be the strongest in Group 4 “slow decrease” (OR = 4.5, 95 % CI = [1.1, 18.0]).

We also conducted post-hoc univariable comparisons of demographic and baseline characteristics among the identified trajectory groups using ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, Chi-square test for categorical variables (Supplemental Table 2). We observed similar findings to what we had in trajectory analysis.

We conducted a sensitivity analysis involving exclusion of the sample of participants who had the imputed binary depression variable due to missing a PHQ-2 score at baseline. The two depression variables were not comparable; therefore, we needed to assess how the analysis results could differ without this group. After excluding those who had PHQ-2 data missing (30 %), we identified three groups: Group 1 “maintained”, Group 2 “vacillating”, and Group 3 “decrease”. While these results do not differentiate a rapid decrease and a slow decrease group, we found similar longitudinal trajectories to our main analysis. In comparison with Group 1 “maintained”, Group 2 “vacillating” had lower engagement levels, had higher antihypertensive medication use at baseline, and was characterized as more likely to be older, unmarried, uninsured, and higher educated compared to Group 1. Group 3 “decrease” was associated with having a medical home, having insurance at baseline and being male. Group 1 had shorter follow-up months, fewer males, more employed individuals, and fewer participants with depression. Higher baseline DBP and pulse pressure levels were found in both Groups 2 and 3, compared to Group 1. To broaden our sample to encompass participants with a history of depression, regardless of whether they experienced symptoms in the past two weeks measured by the PHQ-2, our final results included the group with missing PHQ-2 scores.

Discussion

This study disentangled unobserved heterogeneity by identifying distinct trajectories in blood pressure trends among intervention participants and characterizing the factors shared among individuals in each group. Examination of heterogeneity provides important insights into priority groups that may need distinct intervention/care, which cannot be identified using traditional statistical models producing aggregate-level data, that inherently ignore individual variation. Prior longitudinal analysis of blood pressure results from this intervention sample did not capture the major findings observed in this study (Lopez et al., 2024).

Heterogeneous patterns of blood pressure control

We observed an improvement in blood pressure in three of the four groups. Group 3 “rapid decrease” and Group 4 “slow decrease” achieved SBP reduction ≥10 mmHg by six months, and improvement was maintained through 24 months. From the trajectories, we observed early engagement in DSMES may be important to obtaining blood pressure reduction quickly.

Healthcare services delivery

Although all participants had T2DM with poor HbA1c control and high blood pressure at enrollment, less than half self-reported taking T2DM medications and only a quarter self-reported using antihypertensive medications. This was surprising since 78 % of participants were referred from a medical home. Possible explanations for these findings may be related to participants not reporting their medications correctly (lack of awareness of the medications and reasons for taking medications) or participants not actively taking prescribed medications due to other barriers. Participants in Group 3 “rapid decrease” and Group 4 “slow decrease” were least likely to report antihypertensive medication usage at baseline, despite having dangerously high SBP. This highlights the importance of identifying challenges and needs impacting medication adherence and providing support to mitigate these factors. Group 2 “vacillating” participants tended to have more blood pressure medication use at baseline compared to the reference group (Group 1 “maintained”), which meant they were receiving treatment for hypertension. Yet this group had the slowest reduction of SBP of all groups. These individuals may have more treatment-resistant blood pressure. Indeed, individuals with refractory blood pressure need closer and more intentional primary care follow-up to optimize their blood pressure and multiple chronic condition regimen (Yaxley and Thambar, 2015). For this group reporting more blood pressure medication usage, future research should explore if better care coordination and other patient-centered care approaches can improve outcomes.

Influence of depression

Group 3 “rapid decrease” participants had a lower prevalence of depression at baseline, whereas Group 4 “slow decrease” participants had a higher prevalence. Depression causes anhedonia which in turn affects a person's motivation to initiate and execute any changes to their daily routine (Elderon and Whooley, 2013; Peyrot and Rubin, 2007). Thus, higher levels of depression in Group 4 may have initially caused a lack of motivation and limited behavior change, while not impacting attendance itself. While we see a delayed intervention effect in blood pressure for individuals with depression, we found this multi-level intervention was associated with gradual SBP improvements in these participants.

Impact of socioeconomic indicators on blood pressure

Group 3 “rapid decrease” and Group 4 “slow decrease” were more likely to be employed, have health insurance and a medical home at baseline compared to Group 1. These participants had more access to healthcare providers yet had uncontrolled T2DM and hypertension and less use of their needed medication. Engagement in the intervention, which helps to align access, medication usage, and improve social needs and health behaviors, may be associated with improved SBP.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is its lack of recorded changes in medication over time. The intervention focused on addressing behaviors and barriers with medication self-management. Whereas, the dose and types of medication changes over time were not self-management behaviors and thus not recorded. Furthermore, capturing the change in medications was managed and recorded by the participant's medical home. Similarly, we did not assess longitudinal changes in sociodemographic variables such as insurance, employment, and medical home status. Baseline PHQ-2 data were incomplete, so we supplemented as needed with self-reported depression history. Some argue that GBTM may generate spurious findings, especially when the average posterior probability is used as the sole criterion to evaluate model fit (Mésidor et al., 2022). Future studies could perform other latent class modeling approaches such as growth mixture modeling (Muthén, 2004). The trajectories identified in this study may not apply to other populations. Trajectory modeling is most effective in identifying and monitoring clusters within a population sharing similar clinical trajectories (Nagin and Odgers, 2010). Since our findings are data-driven, replication of results is needed to assess the generalizability of patterns of change, which also requires large or multiple samples (Frankfurt et al., 2016).

Conclusion

This study characterized individual-level heterogeneity in blood pressure trajectories among an underserved population with T2DM and comorbid hypertension. We identified four heterogeneous and distinct groups of participants with similar clinical trajectories in their SBP levels and assessed associated factors. As a retrospective study, further research is needed to examine group patterns across populations and conditions. With more such studies, the findings could lead to proactive approaches to tailor interventions based on individuals' characteristics, non-medical health needs, and closer monitoring of disease management patterns. Furthermore, this study expands our knowledge of how an expanded CCM intervention impacts blood pressure outcomes. This multi-level intervention that incorporated both the health system and CHW support in the home and community settings demonstrated clinically significant improvement in SBP among patients with both T2DM and hypertension. These findings can help inform the risk stratification tools utilized in primary care (Girwar et al., 2021) and add to the evidence base used to drive decisions. We believe the findings from our study provide a novel application of a statistical method to demonstrate how an intervention could be tailored for population subsets.
