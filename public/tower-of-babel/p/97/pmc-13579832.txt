Background

Seminoma is a malignant testicular tumor classified as a germ cell neoplasm and may, in rare cases, arise in the mediastinum or other extragonadal sites. It accounts for approximately one‑third of testicular germ cell malignancies and typically affects males between 15 and 34 years of age. Seminoma is among the most curable malignancies, with survival rates approaching 99% in early stages [1–3]. The typical clinical presentation is a painless testicular mass, although severe pain may occur in uncommon cases [1]. Diagnosis relies on a combination of laboratory and radiological assessments, including testicular ultrasound and tumor marker evaluation. However, the gold standard for diagnosis remains histopathological examination of the tumor following radical orchiectomy, which is the primary treatment at all stages, with adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy added when clinically indicated [1, 2]. Seminoma rarely metastasizes to the central nervous system (CNS). When relapse occurs after treatment, CNS involvement typically represents part of a systemic recurrence, whereas isolated CNS relapse is exceedingly rare [3, 4]. In this article, we present a unique case of solitary seminoma metastasis to the brain, manifesting as a large intracranial tumor four years after completion of treatment for a testicular seminoma.

Case presentation

A 34‑year‑old Syrian male was referred to the Neurology Department after experiencing recurrent headaches over the past five months, progressive weakness in his right hand, and a recent onset of persistent nausea and vomiting. The patient reported a family history of migraine and initially attributed his headaches to this predisposition; however, his symptoms had become incessant over the preceding two months. He had no prior history of brain tumors, trauma, or head injury. Notably, he had a history of left testicular seminoma (Stage IIB), which had been effectively treated four years earlier with radical orchiectomy and resection of three retroperitoneal lymph nodes, the largest measuring 35 mm in maximum diameter, followed by chemotherapy consisting of 4 cycles of cisplatin and etoposide, and adjuvant radiotherapy. Remarkably, the patient had a complete clinical and radiological response to these treatments. At the time of initial diagnosis of testicular seminoma, metastatic involvement was confined to the retroperitoneal lymph nodes, with no evidence of spread to other organs, especially the brain, since a brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) conducted at the time of diagnosis revealed unremarkable findings, supporting the classification of Stage IIB disease. Over the subsequent four years, the patient underwent rigorous surveillance in accordance with established follow‑up protocols for Stage II seminoma, including regular clinical evaluations and abdominal and pelvic computed tomography (CT) scans, none of which indicated recurrence. Serum tumor markers data during the initial diagnosis were unavailable due to the long elapsed period. On physical examination, mild muscle weakness was observed in the right hand. Fundoscopic evaluation revealed bilateral papilledema. These findings, combined with the patient’s symptoms, raised concern for an intracranial space‑occupying lesion. MRI of the brain demonstrated a large, spherical, intra‑axial mass in the left frontal region measuring approximately 40 × 45 × 55 mm. The lesion exhibited heterogeneous signal characteristics, appearing hyperintense on T2‑weighted images and hypointense on T1‑weighted images, with scattered areas of enhancement following contrast administration. Correlation between the histopathological findings, immunohistochemical analysis, and the patient’s remarkable history of seminoma confirmed the diagnosis of metachronous seminoma metastasis to the brain. Additional imaging studies, including CT and MRI, were conducted to evaluate the presence of metastases in other organs, but no other lesions were identified. The patient subsequently received appropriate postoperative treatment, consisting of whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT) at a total dose of 30 Gy delivered in 10 fractions using opposed lateral fields encompassing the entire cranial vault, and cisplatin-based chemotherapy consisting of 4 cycles of cisplatin and etoposide. Moreover, the patient was given memantine to reduce the neurological side effects of WBRT. Notably, his symptoms improved rapidly, and he completed his postoperative adjunctive therapy without any significant complications other than some expected side effects such as headache, nausea, vomiting, and alopecia, with no long-term complications such as memory impairment. The patient underwent rigorous clinical follow-up every three months, and radiological assessment every six months. A follow-up brain MRI performed six months after surgery showed no evidence of recurrence. The patient remains in good health within 18 months of regular follow-up with no clinical or radiological evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

Seminoma is a malignant tumor that primarily affects the testicles and, less commonly, the mediastinum or other extragonadal sites. It typically occurs in men between 15 and 34 years of age. It is considered one of the most curable and highly treatable forms of cancer, with survival rates exceeding 95% when diagnosed at an early stage [1]. Our patient had a notable history of left testicular seminoma (Stage IIB) and received appropriate management. He subsequently underwent rigorous surveillance in accordance with established follow‑up guidelines for stage II seminoma, with no evidence of relapse. In cases of testicular seminoma, surveillance remains essential to monitor for synchronous and metachronous metastases. The term “synchronous metastases” commonly refers to metastases that are discovered during the initial diagnosis of the primary tumor or within 6 months of its diagnosis, while metastases discovered after that period are defined as “metachronous metastases” [5]. Various malignancies tend to result in brain metastases, with lung cancer identified as the leading primary source. Other significant contributors include melanoma, colorectal cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and breast cancer [6]. Notably, brain metastases are rare in testicular malignancies, occurring in only about 1% to 2% of all patients with testicular cancer. These metastases develop almost exclusively in nonseminomatous tumors, whereas their occurrence in seminomas is minimal, accounting for only about 5%, and is typically associated with high stages of seminoma with widespread systemic involvement. The five‑year survival rate for patients who present with synchronous brain metastases at initial diagnosis of the primary tumor is approximately 45%, whereas for those who develop brain metastases during primary treatment or at recurrence, the survival rate decreases to about 12% [7]. Darren R. Feldman et al. concluded in their study that males with brain metastases originating from germ cell tumors have a notably low overall survival rate, particularly when additional risk factors are present [8]. MRI represents an effective imaging modality, providing excellent and precise anatomical localization of brain metastases. On T1‑weighted images, parenchymal metastases typically appear iso‑ to hypointense relative to normal brain tissue, while on T2‑weighted images they demonstrate variable signal intensity. These metastases are typically spherical in shape and are frequently accompanied by extensive vasogenic edema that is often disproportionate to the size of the tumor, usually affecting the white matter while sparing the cortex [9]. In our patient, MRI revealed a large spherical intra‑axial space‑occupying lesion. Therapeutic approaches for brain metastases encompass surgical intervention, radiotherapy, and systemic chemotherapy. The choice of treatment is influenced by several factors, such as the location and size of the metastasis, as well as the severity of both extracranial and intracranial involvement [6]. Surgical intervention has recently been recognized as a standard treatment for selected patients with brain metastases. Resection provides immediate relief from mass effect, often resulting in faster clinical improvement compared with radiotherapy, and reduces the need for high‑dose corticosteroids, thereby minimizing steroid‑related adverse effects. Additionally, surgical excision allows for histopathological examination of the specimen, which cannot be achieved through systemic chemotherapy or radiotherapy [6]. However, there remains a lack of conclusive evidence regarding optimal management strategies for seminoma metastasizing to the brain, and treatment decisions are generally determined independently on an institution‑specific basis. According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines, cisplatin‑based chemotherapy is recommended as first‑line management for brain metastases, with radiotherapy considered as an additional option. However, surgical intervention should be pursued when appropriate and feasible [7]. Most brain tumors represent metastases from a previously identified primary malignancy. In a minority of cases, however, the lesion may be a primary brain tumor, a non‑cancerous process, underscoring the need for further histological evaluation to guide management [6]. Patients with good preoperative overall status and solitary brain metastases are often considered suitable candidates for surgery [6]. Our patient was deemed suitable for surgical intervention and subsequently underwent successful resection of the intracranial tumor. The total specimen was submitted for histopathological examination. Typically, histopathology reveals a dispersed configuration of pale tumor cells interspersed with fibrovascular septa encompassing lymphocytes. The tumor’s cellular background consists of cells with distinctive features, including pale or clear cytoplasm, polygonal nuclei with flattened edges and fine granular chromatin, one or more centrally situated prominent nucleoli, and well‑defined cytoplasmic membranes. The nuclei are generally non‑overlapping and evenly spaced; however, in areas with denser cytoplasm, nuclear crowding may be observed. Additionally, nearly all cases exhibit variable lymphocytic infiltration, and more than half demonstrate a granulomatous response [1]. In our case, the histopathological findings were suggestive of metastatic seminoma to the brain. Immunohistochemistry was performed to confirm the diagnosis. Radiotherapy represents one of the main management options, aiming to inhibit further tumor growth and recurrence while achieving local control. Various radiations are employed, either independently or in combination with surgical intervention. One of the primary methods is WBRT [6]. WBRT is utilized to address intracranial metastases, providing considerable advantages by targeting the entire brain and ensuring treatment of all metastatic sites, including microscopic lesions that may not be detectable through imaging modalities [6]. However, WBRT affects both healthy and cancerous brain cells. This increases the risk of neurological side effects, such as vomiting, headaches, and nausea in the short term, and memory impairment in the long term. It is recommended that individuals undergoing WBRT receive memantine to help alleviate these neurological effects [6]. Our patient received post‑surgical treatment with cisplatin‑based chemotherapy and WBRT, with no recurrence or complications noted during a 6‑month follow‑up period. To the best of our knowledge, only three published case reports describe isolated CNS metastases arising from testicular seminoma in the absence of systemic dissemination. Notably, each of these cases differs from our patient’s presentation in important ways. Choi et al. described a case with multiple intracranial metastases identified at the time of initial diagnosis. Their patient received radiotherapy but declined chemotherapy, and his status had declined as of their last follow-up [10]. The case reported by Mackey & Venner involved extensive leptomeningeal spread affecting both the brain and spinal cord, but without metastasis within the brain parenchyma itself, and their patient exhibited no further recurrence within 30 months after completing radiotherapy and chemotherapy [4]. Fujii et al. documented a case of solitary brain metastasis with associated Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, detected one year after treatment of an intra‑abdominal seminoma. Their patient received radiotherapy to reduce the tumor size, and his condition improved, though long-term follow-up was not reported [11]. In contrast to these cases, our patient’s parenchymal metastasis emerged approximately four years after completion of primary therapy. Remarkably, our article documents the largest solitary brain metastasis reported in the literature, presenting after a relatively long time after initial treatment. Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of considering seminoma metastasis to the brain in the differential diagnosis of intracranial space‑occupying lesions in patients with a prior history of testicular seminoma, although such metastases are extremely rare, even within an extended period after initial treatment. The long interval between the primary tumor diagnosis and the development of brain metastasis in our patient further emphasizes the value of maintaining clinical awareness. New‑onset neurological symptoms in seminoma survivors should prompt consideration of this possibility. Current surveillance guidelines for stage II seminoma do not include routine brain imaging, reflecting the low incidence of CNS relapse. However, this case underscores that such events, while rare, do occur and can present years after treatment with isolated neurological symptoms. This highlights the need for a high index of clinical suspicion and a low threshold for brain imaging in symptomatic patients. Future research should focus on identifying potential risk factors (for example, initial stage, presence of vascular invasion, specific genetic markers) that could define a high-risk subgroup of survivors who might benefit from more targeted CNS surveillance. Brain MRI remains a key tool in the diagnostic assessment of suspected intracranial involvement.
