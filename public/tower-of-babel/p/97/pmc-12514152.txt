Independent pathfinding with collision avoidance for visually impaired individuals
Authors: Tadeh Ghahremanians, Hossein Mahvash Mohammadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractComputer vision tasks such as image segmentation, object detection, and face recognition have been crucial in developing assistance systems for visually impaired individuals. Among these, image segmentation plays a vital role in helping them navigate safely. However, this task is more complex as it requires detailed spatial information. In this article, we propose a novel panoptic segmentation framework that serves as the foundation for an effective pathfinding system, combining robust collision avoidance with high performance. Our contribution includes a single-stage instance segmentation method built on a ResNet101-FPN encoder-decoder architecture. Additionally, we created a customized panoptic labeled dataset to meet the specific needs of visually impaired individuals, aiming to support future integration with real-time feedback in visual prostheses. We evaluate our model both qualitatively and quantitatively using the Panoptic Quality (PQ) metric. Results show that our method surpasses recent panoptic segmentation techniques, achieving a PQ score 4.092 points higher. It also outperforms existing pathfinding systems, demonstrating greater accuracy and efficiency under varied weather conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19387-8.Keywords: Assisting visually impaired individuals, Pathfinding, Panoptic segmentationSubject terms: Engineering, Eye diseases

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there are around 45 million blind individuals1 and about 2.2 billion visually impaired people in the world2. The WHO started the “Vision 2020” project in an effort to eliminate avoidable blindness and significantly reduce the number of visually impaired cases3–5. Despite these lofty goals, the WHO’s universal eye health programs are difficult to implement due to increases in world population and life expectancy6. Still, blindness and visual impairment cause many individuals to lose access to the most basic rights, such as mobility, environmental interaction, information access, and many others7,8. In their study, Duckett and Pratt emphasize the importance of mobility for visually impaired individuals: “The lack of proper transportation for many individuals with visual impairments causes these individuals to live in isolation and seem it turns out that transportation is a key issue for visually impaired individuals so that they can use their abilities and play an active role in society”9.

Developing a device that enables independent pathfinding with collision avoidance assistance has proven extremely challenging insofar as such a device would require the recognition of a wide range of potential objects to provide high-level auxiliary awareness of scene perception. But a potential solution to this challenge may rest in computer vision research such as object detection, image segmentation, particularly that which relates to scene understanding. Image segmentation methods among others, offer especially important insight in providing potential solutions for those who struggle getting from one place to another due to visual impairment10. Related to image segmentation models, consider the input image in Fig. 1(a). Semantic segmentation models aid in recognition of parts of a whole scene with similar textures11–13 (e.g. ground, sky, trees, and buildings) and can also recognize obstacles for visually impaired individuals. However, within this approach, obstacles belonging to the same class (e.g. persons) cannot be distinguished from one another14 Fig. 1(b). This can have a negative impact on the behavioral decisions of individuals15. On the other hand, typical instance segmentation models have the ability to distinguish between obstacles of the same class, but they do not have the ability to identify areas with similar textures16 Fig. 1(c). These regions include areas without clearly defined boundaries—such as pavements, roads, mountains, or the sky. For individuals with visual impairments to achieve independent pathfinding with reliable obstacle avoidance, it is essential to accurately delineate traversable paths at the pixel level. Notably, such paths often fall within the areas with similar textures, which lie beyond the segmentation capabilities of instance segmentation models.

Different types of image segmentation methods: (a) input Image17, (b) semantic segmentation17, (c) instance segmentation17, (d) panoptic segmentation17.

A new task called panoptic segmentation solves this problem. Panoptic segmentation integrates the two tasks of semantic and instance segmentation18, such that it becomes possible to provide more comprehensive perceptual information19 to visually impaired individuals Fig. 1(d). Panoptic segmentation, depicted in Fig. 1 (d), divides the scene into “things” and “stuff” classes20–22. “Things” refer to countable objects, such as person, bicycle, etc., whereas “stuff” refers to regions with similar texture that lack clear instance demarcations (e.g., street, sky). Panoptic segmentation provides more comprehensive scene information13,19,23 and effectively improves the performance of scene analysis, making it widely applicable.

Feedback for visually impaired individuals can be delivered through three primary methods: visual enhancement, substitution, and replacement24. Vision enhancement involves processing a captured image and displaying it on a monitoring device24. In contrast, vision substitution delivers the output through vibrations or auditory signals, providing less detailed information than visual feedback. Vision replacement, which employs advanced medical technology, is more sophisticated, as it directly transmits information to the stimulator implanted in retina or visual cortex24.

While audio and haptic feedback are commonly employed in assistive navigation systems, recent studies (e.g25,26., indicate that integrating visual prostheses with conventional audio and haptic feedback improves navigational efficiency and accuracy, especially in dynamic and unstructured environments.

Through the scene understanding approach presented in this study (see Fig. 2b, c), critical environmental information, including obstacle detection and path identification, can be relayed to visually impaired individuals using a camera-based system embedded in smart glasses (see Fig. 2a). This information is then processed and transferred via a visual prosthesis (see Fig. 2d, e)27,28. Additionally, the proposed method can be integrated into the video processing unit of vision implant systems, such as the Argus II29.

During the development and implementation phases, we consulted with visually impaired individuals to evaluate the efficiency and usability of our proposed method. The study involved 100 participants with visual impairments and 200 sighted participants using blindfolds. Through these consultations, we identified significant navigation challenges, including difficulties in pathfinding and sound-based navigation in crowded environments. To further understand their needs, we referenced Sankaran’s survey1, which includes qualitative interviews with visually impaired individuals using Witzel’s problem-oriented interview method1. It is worth noting that we adopted the same interview methodology for our data collection. Our findings align with those from Sankaran’s study, reinforcing the real-world applicability of our approach. Additionally, we reviewed studies involving sighted individuals using blindfolds or simulated visual impairment environments, which provided valuable insights for refining the system’s features for practical deployment.

In 2017, Malūkas et al.35 presented a computer vision-based system that provides real-time pathfinding for visually impaired individuals. They introduced a semantic segmentation technique leveraging a convolutional neural network (CNN), capable of identifying patterns within the image’s features. Robertas and Agnes used a 19-layer VGG model due to its simple neural network and optimal performance to classify and localize tasks.

Hongmin Mu et al. (2024)51 proposed a dynamic obstacle avoidance (OA) system tailored for partially sighted and visually impaired (PSVI) users. Their approach utilizes a fast instance segmentation network enhanced by a novel Multi-scale 2D convolution unit, which captures both local and global contextual features through a hierarchical residual-like structure. A regional safety analysis module is incorporated to provide timely vibration alerts, enabling effective collision avoidance.

Yun Gao et al. (2024)52 introduced WOAD (Wearable Obstacle Avoidance Device); a wearable system specifically designed for visually impaired individuals. WOAD employs depth-aided video compression to minimize transmission delay and leverages cross-modal learning for robust obstacle detection. The system achieved impressive real-world performance, including 100% collision avoidance, a response time under 320 ms, and an 11-hour battery life.

Sunnia Ikram et al. (2025)53 presented a practical and low-cost smart knee glove designed to assist visually impaired users through real-time obstacle detection using IoT sensors and machine learning. The collected sensor data is processed using an ensemble of classifiers—Decision Tree, SVM, KNN, Random Forest, and Gaussian Naïve Bayes—combined through a Voting Classifier to enhance accuracy. The system achieved a detection accuracy of 98.34% within a 4-meter range. We also examined several deep learning-based navigation methods that were not explicitly developed for visually impaired assistance but demonstrate relevance to autonomous mobility. Abhishek Mukhopadhyay and Harshitha Br et al. (2025)54 developed a semantic segmentation framework capable of detecting objects in complex, unstructured backgrounds. The system integrates two distinct segmentation models: an encoder-decoder architecture for abstract feature learning and a dilated convolutional branch to manage variable object scales. A dynamic fusion strategy based on branch-wise confidence scores enables adaptability to dynamic environments. The model was evaluated using the Indian Driving Dataset (IDD) and Cityscapes.

In a related study, Mukhopadhyay and Pradipta Biswas et al. (2024)55 proposed a hybrid semantic segmentation framework tailored for object detection in challenging environments. Their model incorporates a dual-branch architecture consisting of a VGG16-based encoder-decoder network and a parallel dilated convolutional branch, with outputs fused via learned confidence weights. The framework was implemented in a real-world autonomous aircraft taxiing system, demonstrating high segmentation accuracy and responsiveness.

Gaikwad and Mukhopadhyay et al. (2025)56 introduced an autonomous visual taxiing system for aircraft that combines lane detection and object avoidance. The system employs a dual-branch segmentation network for lane detection—consisting of a VGG16-based encoder-decoder and a dilated convolutional branch—fused through confidence-weighted merging. The resulting lane segmentation is fed into a steering control module. Methods developed for domains such as autonomous driving, aircraft navigation, lane detection, and object tracking in complex environments are highly task-specific and not designed to meet the specialized requirements of assistive systems for visually impaired individuals. In contrast, our proposed system integrates a custom-designed, fast panoptic segmentation model, real-time pathfinding capabilities, and a dedicated dataset—each component specifically optimized for the unique challenges of visually impaired navigation.

Panoptic segmentation, which merges the benefits of semantic and instance segmentation, was initially introduced by Kirillov et al.57. Kirillov et al. called this approach Panoptic FPN (Feature Pyramid Network). In the proposed panoptic FPN (Feature Pyramid Network)31 approach, Kirillov et al.57 begin with an FPN backbone to extract comprehensive multi-scale features. Similar to Mask R-CNN, their approach incorporates a region-based branch atop FPN for instance segmentation. Concurrently, a lightweight dense prediction branch is added to the same FPN (Feature Pyramid Network) features for semantic segmentation. Kirillov et al.57 produced the final model of panoptic segmentation of the image with the corresponding encoder-decoder architecture. Subsequent advancements in panoptic segmentation further developed from this approach, integrating instance and semantic segmentation within an encoder-decoder architectural framework. While conventional segmentation methods are limited in their ability to provide comprehensive scene understanding, panoptic segmentation distinguishes between obstacles more effectively. However, its computational complexity has been a major limitation—a challenge that has been addressed in this study.

Approach

The proposed approach

This article aims to assist blind individuals in navigating and avoiding obstacles. By segmenting safe areas, the system identifies clear paths for movement, thereby preventing collisions. Instead of focusing on detecting obstacles, the system highlights safe zones for walking, treating anything outside these zones as potential obstacles. This approach simplifies the problem by emphasizing space segmentation rather than object recognition, which can be challenging. Obstacle detection methods lack’s the ability to provide precise, pixel-level boundary information for individuals with visual impairments which results in incomplete spatial understanding of their surroundings. Moreover, object detection methods are incapable of identifying traversable paths. While these methods are faster and suitable for simple approaches which provides feedback such as audio cues. These approaches are inadequate for advanced methods which provide visual feedback approaches intended for integration with high-end visual prostheses. Therefore, the problem statement has been revised to focus on segmenting safe paths in images, with any area outside these paths considered an obstacle.

Building on the semantic segmentation pathfinding approach by Malūkas et al.35 and the integration of semantic and instance segmentation by Facebook’s AI research team’s57, we introduce a distinct panoptic segmentation model customized for the specific needs of visually impaired individuals. This model utilizes a custom database to address individuals’ daily challenges, extracting detailed texture information and ensuring robustness against occlusion and poor lighting conditions. Our aim is to develop a solution resistant to challenging scenarios, specifically focusing on the specialized application of pathfinding with collision avoidance for visually impaired individuals. Subsequently, we will explore the implementation intricacies of our proposed approach.

General structure of panoptic segmentation

As implicitly outlined in the abstract, we make three key contributions: (1) a custom-designed, lightweight panoptic segmentation framework based on a single-stage instance segmentation architecture that enables high-resolution scene understanding for real-time pathfinding and obstacle avoidance under varying lighting and weather conditions. (2) a panoptic-labeled custom dataset that captures navigation-critical scene components tailored to the needs of visually impaired individuals; and (3) a novel integration of semantic and instance segmentation modules. Instance segmentation

In this part of the panoptic segmentation approach, we aim to employ a single-step method for instance segmentation by leveraging the ResNet101-FPN architecture (see Fig. 6). This facilitates easy integration with the semantic segmentation architecture, ultimately leading to the creation of a fast panoptic segmentation. The single-step instance segmentation approach relies on the RetinaNet network architecture. As shown in Fig. 6, layers from the ResNet110-FPN structure are utilized as input for the instance prediction head module. A series of prototypes of the attention map related to instance segmentation are generated using the ResNet110-FPN encoder-decoder architecture. Additionally, the ResNet101-FPN encoder-decoder architecture, in conjunction with the instance prediction head block, generates a set of mask coefficients. These coefficients are used to select the most suitable prototype created by the attention map. The instance attention map block consists of Multiple convolution layers responsible for producing prototypes of instance attention maps, which correspond to Instance segmentation masks. In this way, the single-step instance segmentation task will be done well. Further implementation details, are available in Supplementary Code 1.

Semantic segmentation

The output of semantic segmentation with the same backbone as instance segmentation has been utilized with the architectural block shown in Fig. 5. Its output in the final part of semantic segmentation consists of a stuff head and things head. After applying Up Convolution to the output layers of the FPN backbone these blocks must ultimately convolve, such that the number of its channels should be equal to the number of its classes. In the following, a combination of two semantic and instance segmentation methods to provide a panoptic segmentation model will be examined.

To enable panoptic fusion, relevant channels in are selectively masked based on the instance segmentation output. During training, the corresponding regions in are aligned with ground truth masks to create a semantic-instance overlap region, denoted as . During inference, these regions are predicted using the output of the instance segmentation branch. Each captures the semantic footprint of an object instance and is aligned with its corresponding channel.

Since the spatial dimensions of and may differ, we apply resizing and zero-padding to ensure compatibility. The aligned tensors are then combined element-wise to generate the instance-level panoptic representation , where N is the number of detected instances. Each channel in corresponds to a semantically enriched mask of a distinct object.

Simultaneously, the background regions from semantic segmentation are compiled into the tensor , preserving class-wise information about texture-based areas.

Together, and form the final input to the panoptic logits head. The number of channels in each tensor is dynamic varies with the number of detected instances, while depends on the number of background semantic classes present in the image.

This modular tensor fusion strategy ensures that object-specific and background-specific information are both preserved and accurately represented in the panoptic output, balancing precision with computational efficiency.

To ensure an optimal accuracy–speed trade-off, particularly for real-time applications tailored to visually impaired individuals, we fine-tuned the model’s hyperparameters based on ablation studies. The model was trained using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with a base learning rate of 0.0025, momentum of 0.9, and weight decay of 0.0005. A polynomial learning rate decay schedule was applied. The ResNet-101-FPN backbone, pre-trained on ImageNet, was employed for feature extraction.

The training environment was configured using Python 3.10.12, PyTorch 2.1.0, NumPy 1.23.5, and OpenCV 4.8.0. All experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, with checkpointing enabled every 5,000 iterations to monitor model performance throughout the training process.

Types of datasets for panoptic segmentation

General datasets

Several key datasets are commonly utilized for panoptic segmentation, which can aid in pathfinding and collision avoidance for visually impaired individuals. The Cityscapes dataset includes 30 classes and is valuable for training such systems60. The Mapillary Vistas dataset contains 25,000 high-quality images and is available in two versions: v1.2 with 66 objects in 37 categories, and v2.0 with 124 objects in 70 categories17. The COCO 2020 dataset, widely used in object detection, provides over 200,000 images with both instance-level and semantic-level annotations, making it a key resource for panoptic segmentation research61. Lastly, the BDD 100 K dataset includes 100,000 videos from various U.S. cities, with annotations for 10 object categories and 30 texture categories, offering rich data for diverse environments62.

Custom dataset

Here we introduce a custom dataset containing 1200 Panoptic labeled pictures and 5-hour videos which can be used for validation and test of the proposed method. This dataset provides control over data quality and annotation accuracy. By carefully curating and annotating the dataset, we can ensure that it meets the desired standards for training and evaluating the proposed panoptic segmentation model. Moreover, using this custom dataset allows us to address data privacy concerns and ethical considerations and ensure that the sensitive information is handled correctly and the rights and privacy of individuals in the images are protected. Figure 11 shows some instances of the introduced dataset which has been created under various lighting conditions and challenging modes, including occlusion or weather different conditions.

To ensure image segmentation accuracy, evaluation is done by IoU (Intersection over Union), and, generally, the average or mean of IoU is also reported by m. IoU (mean Intersection over Union). This component evaluates how well the algorithm demarcates the boundaries of each area. Secondly, in evaluating the accuracy of the prediction of the classes, this will be measured using appropriate accuracy metrics.

Panoptic quality combines these two aspects into a single metric, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the algorithm’s performance in both object and stuff segmentation. A higher panoptic quality score indicates better overall performance in accurately segmenting both objects and stuff in an image36.

P.Q. (Panoptic Quality) S.Q. (Segmentation Quality) m. IoU R.Q. (Recognition Quality)

In this equation, the numerator of fraction is the sum of the intersection over union (IoU), which is calculated and evaluated between each area on the ground truth and prediction; it is divided by the number of True Positives (TP), which is the denominator of the fraction. This means those areas that are correctly segmented, and their mean IoU is calculated, which is the m.IoU. In the recognition quality, the numerator of the fraction is the number of TPs, while the denominator of the fraction corresponds to the number of TPs plus half the sum of False Positives (FP) and False Negatives (FN). By computing this fraction, recognition quality is derived. By simplifying the number of TP numerator of the fraction of recognition quality with the denominator of the fraction of segmentation quality, panoptic quality of Eq. 1 is obtained.

The ablation study further highlights the effectiveness of our architectural choices and dataset design. Specifically, the ResNet101-FPN backbone provides an optimal trade-off between accuracy and efficiency, the single-stage segmentation method enhances real-time applicability, and the custom dataset significantly improves robustness in real-world navigation scenarios for visually impaired individuals.

To Further assess the generalization ability of our model on unseen data, we performed 10-fold cross-validation on a subset of the COCO dataset. This evaluation strategy, which involves training and testing on distinct data partitions, reduces the risk of overfitting and provides a robust measure of the model’s generalization capability.

As illustrated in figure, our approach consistently maintains high segmentation quality across diverse environments, further validating the effectiveness of both the model architecture and dataset design.

To ensure the statistical reliability of our results, we report all performance metrics with standard deviations calculated over five independent runs, each initialized with different random seeds. For core metrics—including mean Intersection over Union (mIoU), panoptic quality (PQ), and inference time—we provide 95% confidence intervals. We also perform statistical significance testing using paired t-tests and, where applicable, ANOVA to confirm that observed improvements over baseline models are not due to random variation. This rigorous evaluation protocol enhances the credibility and reproducibility of our findings.

It is worth noting that despite the promising performance of the proposed system, certain limitations remain. While a lightweight panoptic segmentation design was adopted—featuring a single-stage instance segmentation backbone based on ResNet101-FPN—the overall complexity of panoptic segmentation remains considerably higher than conventional object detection or semantic segmentation methods. Furthermore, the reliance on a portable GPU presents a challenge for real-time inference on low-power edge devices or mobile assistive platforms that lack GPU resources or are constrained by processing and battery capacity.

Conclusion

This article proposes a new panoptic segmentation approach to address the most significant challenges encountered by visually impaired individuals. The proposed method, designed to overcome the limitations of current image segmentation models, introduces a novel structure that effectively tackles these challenges. It can segment images under difficult conditions such as poor lighting and obstruction, while expanding the range of supported environments and scenarios. Additionally, it optimizes computational efficiency. By leveraging panoptic segmentation databases and our customized panoptic labeled dataset tailored to specific needs of visual impaired individuals, this system offers personalized guidance. In the future, incorporating real-time feedback loops with visual prostheses could further enhance its capabilities. As research in this field continues to evolve, there is significant potential for further refinement and innovation. Future advancements may focus on improving the accuracy and robustness of detecting instances and obstacles. Moreover, the integration of wearable glasses and computing unit with visual prosthesis will enhance accessibility and usability, empowering individuals to navigate with confidence and autonomy. Ultimately, the goal of these efforts is to promote inclusivity and accessibility, enabling visually impaired individuals to navigate the world with greater freedom and safety. Through ongoing collaboration between researchers, engineers, and the visually impaired community, we can continue to advance assistive technologies and pave the way towards a more inclusive society.
