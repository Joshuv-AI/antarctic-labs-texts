Seasonal surge for mental health demand in the emergency department of Eradah complex for mental health in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia: a retrospective study
Authors: Shrief Y. Afifi, Ahmed E. Sallam, Abdulkhaliq S. S. Alghamdi, Mohammed S. Almuqahwi, Hosam M. Aly, Moatazbellah I. Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeasonal and climatic variations are known to influence patterns of mental illness presenting to emergency departments; however, few studies—particularly in Saudi Arabia and the Eastern Mediterranean region —have examined these effects. This study investigates whether seasonality and gender differences are associated with variations in the number of emergency department visits for mental health disorders at Eradah Complex for Mental Health in Dammam, Saudi Arabia.MethodsWe analyzed data from patients presenting to the emergency room (ER) between January 2024 and December 2024 with mental health diagnoses according to ICD-10 criteria. Data were categorized into four consecutive quarters (Q1 = January–March; Q2 = April–June; Q3 = July–September; Q4 = October–December) and analyzed by sex. Chi-square analysis was used to assess seasonal variation.ResultsSeasonal time-series assessment demonstrated significant variation across all disorders studied, including personality disorders, mixed anxiety–depressive disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), and psychotic disorders. Most patients with psychotic disorders were male, whereas most patients with the other diagnoses were female. There was a significant seasonal variation (P ≤ 0.01) in emergency department visits for psychotic disorders and mixed anxiety–depressive disorder in both males and females.ConclusionSignificant seasonal variation exists in ED visits for psychotic disorders, mixed anxiety–depression, OCD, and personality disorders, with the highest volume occurring in Q4 (October–December). Females accounted for most non-psychotic disorders. Therefore, emergency and outpatient psychiatric services should enhance winter/autumn preparedness, and further research should examine underlying climatic drivers.

Introduction

The survival of many species, including humans, depends on their ability to adapt to external environmental changes such as light–dark cycles [1]. Modern humans evolved near the equator, where day and night lengths remain relatively constant throughout the year [2]. As early human populations migrated to higher latitudes, they encountered substantial variations in photoperiods. Genetic adaptations of the human biological clock to differences in day length and latitude may contribute to susceptibility to mental health conditions, according to geographic descriptive models [3]. Indeed, the prevalence of depressive disorders [4, 5] and other psychiatric conditions [6, 7] increases with latitude [8]. High-latitude regions show more pronounced seasonality in depressive symptoms compared with regions near the equator [9]. Impaired biological adaptation to environmental variations—particularly light exposure—may increase vulnerability to specific psychiatric disorders [3].

Seasonal fluctuations in psychiatric disorders have long been of interest. Recent research highlights associations between seasonal changes and various mental health indicators, with the highest rates of suicide, hospitalizations, and emergency presentations for self-harm occurring in spring and summer [10]. These patterns have been observed across diverse cultures and geographic regions [10]. Unlike many physical illnesses—for example, heart failure and pulmonary infections, which peak in winter [11, 12]—psychiatric disorders tend to show different seasonal patterns. Neurological disorders such as ischemic stroke and meningitis also exhibit winter peaks, although other neurological conditions vary [13].

Individuals with psychiatric disorders often experience behavioral, emotional, and cognitive impairments that significantly limit functioning in social, occupational, and interpersonal domains [14]. Surveys assessing neuropsychiatric and physiological variables are commonly used to investigate seasonal patterns of mood and behavior [15]. Seasonality has been identified as a feature of several psychiatric disorders [16], with more severe clinical manifestations associated with higher global seasonality scores [14]. Biological factors, including gender, likely contribute to the observed seasonality, although social and environmental stressors related to the seasons may also play a role [17].

Few studies have examined the impact of seasonal variation on mental health patients in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, this study was designed to determine the potential effects of seasonal changes on mental health presentations at Eradah Complex for Mental Health in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective, cross-sectional design was employed. Aggregated ED data from January to December 2024 were extracted from the electronic health record of Eradah Complex for Mental Health in Dammam, Saudi Arabia. Data were grouped by quarter (Q1–Q4), sex, and ICD-10 psychiatric diagnosis. No patient identifiers were accessed.

Sample size

The minimum required sample size was calculated using MedCalc Software (Acacialaan 22, Belgium) based on the method described by Della and colleagues [18]. Assuming an expected total annual psychiatric emergency volume of approximately 30,000 patients, a precision of 5% at a 95% confidence interval yielded a minimum sample size of 26,500 patients. Our study included all eligible patients (N = 27,292) during the study period (January–December 2024), which exceeds the required minimum. Simple random sampling was applied to the eligible ED records after applying inclusion/exclusion criteria [18].

Data collection

We reviewed aggregated emergency department (ED) data from Eradah Complex for Mental Health in Dammam. This descriptive retrospective study included all psychiatry-related ED presentations from January 2024 to December 2024. Diagnoses were coded according to the ICD-10 system and extracted from the Medica CloudCare electronic database. “Mental health” was defined as any presentation with a primary diagnosis within ICD-10-chapter F (F00–F99). Data included age, sex, diagnosis, and quarter of presentation (Q1–Q4), corresponding roughly to winter, spring, summer, and autumn. Data were stratified by sex.

A total of 27,292 adult patients (≥ 18 years), including first-time and relapse cases, were included in accordance with the hospital’s admission policy. We included all patients presenting to the ED after receiving medical and neurological clearance. Exclusion criteria were known substance use disorders with an addiction file at Eradah Complex for Mental Health or presentations for voluntary admission.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from Eradah Complex for Mental Health Institutional Review Board in Dammam (approval number MED013, dated 14 January 2025). Data analysis and reporting adhered to Eastern Health Cluster regulations on healthcare information use [19].

Statistical analysis

Data were coded, entered, and analyzed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For non-parametric numerical data, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were reported. While, for parametric numerical data, means, standard deviations (SD), and ranges were presented. Also, categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Associations between categorical variables (season/quarter and psychiatric diagnosis) were examined using the chi-square test of independence. The threshold for statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant; p-values ≤ 0.01 were reported as highly significant where applicable.

A total of 27,292 patients were included in this study. The highest number of presentations occurred in Q4 (October–December), accounting for 45.2% of all cases, followed by Q1 (January–March) with 26.59%. The lowest number of presentations occurred in Q3 (July–September), representing 10.63% of all cases. These fluctuations reflect clear seasonal variations, with a peak in winter and a marked decline in summer.

Discussion

In this study, nationally representative data from Saudi Arabia were used to investigate seasonal patterns among patients presenting with four major psychiatric disorders. We identified clear seasonal fluctuations in hospital presentations across nearly all disorders examined, including several for which seasonality has not previously been evaluated in the Saudi population. Chi-square analyses demonstrated seasonal shifts and gender patterns; however, the chi-square test’s sensitivity limits more nuanced interpretation. Nonetheless, our findings have important implications for translational and ecological epidemiology, healthcare planning, and future research examining psychiatric seasonality in Saudi Arabia.

Several biological mechanisms may explain seasonal variability in psychiatric manifestations. Proposed contributors include neurotransmitter fluctuations, especially in dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways, which influence mood, reinforcement, and cognitive functioning [20–24]. Seasonal changes in daylight directly affect circadian rhythm regulation, and disruptions in circadian adaptation may increase vulnerability to emotional and behavioral dysregulation [25, 26]. Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) is a well-known example, with winter depression potentially leading to delayed presentations in emergency settings due to circadian dysregulation. Geographic differences in photoperiod, such as those documented in northern Australia, may further amplify circadian-related seasonal patterns [27, 28].

Our results revealed significant seasonal variations in psychotic disorders and mixed anxiety–depressive disorders among patients visiting the ER at Eradah Complex for Mental Health. While earlier research from the Netherlands reported little seasonal influence on anxiety [29], depressive symptoms have consistently been shown to rise in winter [30]. In bipolar disorder, abnormalities in circadian rhythm, melatonin secretion, and light sensitivity may drive seasonal patterns of exacerbation [31–34].

Both male and female groups in our study demonstrated higher climate-related emotional vulnerability in winter, possibly reflecting the heightened sensitivity of psychiatric patients to environmental fluctuations, which may exacerbate symptoms and complicate management [35, 36]. Cold temperatures, typical of winter and parts of autumn and spring, can also affect mood, daily functioning, interpersonal interactions, and depressive symptoms [37]. Lukmanji et al. similarly reported higher depressive symptoms during winter months among climate-anxious individuals [38].

Although seasonal influences on psychiatric disorders are well documented globally, comparisons across studies can be challenging due to differing climates, data sources, and diagnostic criteria. By utilizing a large national database, our study validates prior findings while contributing new evidence on seasonal variation across several disorders in Saudi Arabia. Recognizing seasonal peaks is crucial for healthcare preparedness, including staffing, logistics, and the allocation of neuropsychiatric emergency resources.

Suicidal and parasuicidal behaviors are more prevalent in seasonal psychiatric conditions [39], with global data showing peaks in spring and summer that correlate with distance from the equator and variations in daylight [40]. In contrast, we observed the highest number of male psychotic disorder presentations in winter. Other studies have shown disorder-specific seasonal patterns, such as a spring peak for homicides among individuals with alcohol-use disorders and an autumn peak for those with severe depression. Future research should determine whether suicidal behaviors show disorder-specific seasonal variability in Saudi Arabia, which could enhance suicide-prevention strategies.

Although some research suggests seasonal variability in major depressive disorder, this may reflect a broad population shift toward lower affective states during certain times of the year [41–43]. In Saudi Arabia, several recent studies have investigated risk factors for depressive disorders [44, 45]. Our findings showed that mixed anxiety–depressive disorder was most common in females during winter. Broader population-based studies are needed to further explore the interplay between seasonality and depression [46].

Research on seasonal variation in OCD is limited, and no previous study has examined this question in Saudi Arabia. One Turkish study found that over half of OCD patients experience seasonal mood changes, though without affecting OCD severity [47]. This suggests that seasonal factors influence affective rather than compulsive symptoms—yet our findings show a clear seasonal pattern in OCD presentations, particularly among females, peaking in Q3 (July–September). Changes in sunlight alter serotonergic activity, a pathway implicated in OCD [48]; therefore, the intense summer daylight and heat in Saudi Arabia could plausibly trigger or worsen symptoms. Sinha et al. reported higher OCD incidence in autumn than summer, but their sample was small and inconclusive [49]. In contrast, our large cohort reveals a late-summer female peak, highlighting the need for region-specific seasonal research.

Many studies have highlighted seasonal influences on personality disorders [50–52]. In our study, personality disorders were most common among females, with the highest frequency occurring between October and December (Q4).

Limitations

This study was conducted in a major city in Saudi Arabia, and the ages of patients were not specifically categorized. Only four mental health conditions were examined. Further research is needed to investigate seasonal fluctuations across Saudi Arabia’s major cities and to correlate them with specific climatic patterns. Such studies could support more targeted preventive measures and service-planning strategies. Additionally, limitations in the electronic medical record prevented us from distinguishing bipolar disorder from psychotic disorder, and suicidal patients could not be reliably identified.

Conclusion

This study, along with future prospective research, is essential for improving the quality of emergency mental health services. Identifying potential relationships between seasonal variations and ER presentations for psychiatric disorders is critical for resource allocation and service planning. Given the possible 3–6-month lag between symptom development and ER presentation in some disorders, strengthening primary care and outpatient psychiatric resources—particularly in autumn and winter—could help reduce emergency visits. Further research is needed to examine the underlying etiological factors contributing to seasonal variability and to determine whether similar patterns occur across major Saudi cities and the broader Arabian region, especially in other high-income countries with similar climates.
