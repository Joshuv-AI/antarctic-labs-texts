Association of regional lymph node resection with survival in patients with locally advanced-stage cervical cancer: A retrospective SEER-based cohort study
Authors: Aiyuan Wu, Remila Rezhake, Ziyin Huang, Guzhalinuer Abulizi, Li Li, Youlin Qiao, Shaojie Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to validate the prognostic discriminatory power of the revised International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) 2018 staging system and to evaluate the association between regional lymph node resection (RLNR) and survival in cervical cancer (CC) patients who underwent RLNR followed by chemotherapy and radiotherapy.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study utilizing the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database (2000–2018), patients were categorized into early-stage (IA/IB1-IB2/IIA1) and locally advanced-stage (IB3/IIA2-IIB/III/IVA) groups. Survival outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier, univariate and multivariate Cox regression (including time-dependent), and year-of-diagnosis stratified analyses, applied to crude, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)-weighted, and propensity score matching (PSM)-matched models.ResultsThe FIGO 2018 system revealed significant survival differences between IB1 vs IB2 and IIIC1 vs IIIA/IIIB (all p < 0.001). In early-stage patients, RLNR conferred no significant survival benefit. However, in locally advanced-stage patients, RLNR with primary surgery was associated with better survival than RLNR alone. In the cohort without primary surgery, RLNR alone was consistently identified as a prognostic factor versus non-surgery across all three analytical models (IPTW as primary, PSM as sensitivity; all p<0.001). The hazard ratios for RLNR alone versus non-surgery were all below 1.000, and year-of-diagnosis stratified analyses further supported this protective association, with consistently directional estimates.ConclusionThis study suggested the prognostic value of the FIGO 2018 staging system and hypothesized that RLNR is a prognostic factor for better survival compared with chemotherapy and radiotherapy alone in locally advanced-stage CC, although the limitations of a retrospective study must be acknowledged.Keywords: cervical cancer, regional lymph node, SEER, surgery, survival analysis

Introduction

Accurate clinical staging is fundamental for guiding treatment decision-making, and predicting prognosis. A major revision of the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) 2018 staging system for cervical cancer (CC) involved the formal incorporation of radiologically- or pathologically-confirmed lymph node metastasis as stage IIIC (Supplemental Table S1). 1 This update aimed to enable more precise risk stratification and increase prognostic discrimination. 2 However, validation in large-scale populations remains limited, and the effect of this revision on therapeutic strategies requires further investigation.

Regional lymph node resection (RLNR) or sentinel lymph node biopsy was standard for early-stage CC.3,4 However, in patients with locally advanced-stage CC treated with chemotherapy and radiotherapy, the role of RLNR remains undefined. Current clinical guidelines recommend chemotherapy and radiotherapy as the standard of care.1,5 However, an increasing number of retrospective studies have suggested that the addition of RLNR, particularly in patients with bulky metastatic nodes, might confer a survival advantage.6–8 Given the guideline recommendations and the 2018 FIGO staging update, we hypothesized that RLNR followed by chemotherapy and radiotherapy would be associated with increased survival among patients with locally advanced-stage CC compared with chemotherapy and radiotherapy alone.

The explicit definition of node-positive CC as stage IIIC under the FIGO 2018 staging system provided a new framework for reevaluating RLNR. Therefore, the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database was utilized in this study to conduct a retrospective cohort analysis with two objectives: i) to further evaluate the current 2018 FIGO staging system, with the goal of accurately reflecting the nature of the different stages of CC, and ii) to study the effect of RLNR on survival for patients with clinically early-stage or locally advanced-stage CC.

Methods

Study participants

In this retrospective cohort study, we selected patients with CC between 2000 and 2018 from the National Cancer Institute’s SEER online database using SEER*Stat software (version 9.0.42; https://seer.cancer.gov/). Patients were included if they met the following criteria: (1) had the cervix uteri, endocervix, exocervix, or overlapping lesion of cervix uteri as primary site (based on the site recode of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3)); (2) had available data for the following key prognostic variables: FIGO stage, American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage, regional lymph node status, and tumor size; and (3) had available and valid data on survival time and status.

Specifically, to further evaluate the FIGO 2018 staging system for dataset 1 (Figure 1), patients were excluded if they had unknown AJCC stage, unknown/unclear lymph node status, or unknown survival interval. The stage IB group included women with stage IB CC and accessible tumor size information, whereas the stage III group included women with stage IIIA, IIIB, and IIIC1 CC with known regional lymph node status information. From 2004 to 2018, only regional lymph node status was reported, as para-aortic data were not available in the SEER database. The regional lymph nodes included the common iliac, external iliac, obturator, internal iliac, paracervical, parametrial, presacral, and sacral nodes. Squamous carcinoma (SCC), adenocarcinoma (AC), adenosquamous (AS), and other histological categories were identified using the ICD-O-3 codes.

To evaluate the effect of RLNR on the survival rate for dataset 2 (Figure 1), patients were excluded if they had an unknown cancer stage, unknown node status based on radiologically or pathologically confirmation, unknown/unclear surgery at the primary site, unknown/unclear regional lymph node surgery, unknown/no chemotherapy, unknown/no radiotherapy, radiation before surgery, or an unknown survival interval. In accordance with the 2018 FIGO staging system (Supplemental Table S1), 1 early-stage CC was defined as stage IA, IB1, IB2, and IIA1, whereas locally advanced-stage disease included stages IB3, IIA2, and IIB-IVA (excluding stage IIIC2). 9 In accordance with the SEER program coding and staging manual, surgeries at the primary site included trachelectomy, total hysterectomy, radical hysterectomy, and pelvic exenteration. No primary surgery included no surgery at the primary site, local tumor destruction, biopsy, or electrosurgical excision on the basis of Appendix C-Site-Specific Coding Modules about surgery codes cervix uteri. RLNR was defined as regional lymph node removal, whereas sentinel lymph node biopsy alone or biopsy/aspiration of regional lymph nodes limited nodal assessment without RLNR [RX Summ--Scope Reg LN Sur].

Study design and procedures

The SEER database contains comprehensive information on patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment, and survival. From a single SEER extraction, we constructed two analytic datasets. For the specific purpose of this study, data on the following variables were extracted: age, race, year of diagnosis, primary site, histological type and grade, FIGO stage, AJCC stage, regional lymph node status, tumor size, treatment modality, survival time, and survival status. Given that the SEER database provides publicly available, deidentified patient data, this study was granted an exemption from the informed consent requirement by the institutional review board. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the 1975 Helsinki Declaration as revised in 2024. The reporting of this study conforms to the STROBE guidelines (https://www.equator-network.org/guidelines/strobe/). 10

In dataset 1, the FIGO 2014 stage was converted to the FIGO 2018 stage based on the AJCC stage, lymph node status, and tumor size recorded in the database (Figure 1). With respect to the stage IB group, this study aimed to evaluate the cancer survival rate and tumor-related characteristics of patients with CC based on the revised 2018 FIGO staging system. For the stage III group, survival outcomes were compared between women with 2018 FIGO stage IIIC1 CC and those with stage IIIA and IIIB disease, and survival outcomes were compared among women with 2018 FIGO stage IIIC1 CC stratified by T stage (T1, T2, and T3).

In dataset 2, all eligible patients underwent chemotherapy and radiotherapy and were classified into groups with early-stage and locally advanced-stage CC according to the 2018 FIGO staging system. Complete records of surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy were available for all patients (Figure 1). Notably, in this study radiotherapy refers to postoperative radiotherapy, where “postoperative” encompasses not only after primary tumor excision but also after RLNR. For consistency, we uniformly used the term “radiotherapy” throughout the study. Among patients with early-stage CC, those who underwent RLNR were classified by primary site resection (RLNR with primary surgery; RLNR alone), while those who underwent primary site resection were classified by RLNR status (RLNR with primary surgery; primary surgery alone), with the primary objective of assessing their survival rates and hazard ratios. However, the present study focused primarily on the role of RLNR in locally advanced-stage patients. Among these patients, those undergoing RLNR were classified according to primary site resection status (RLNR with primary surgery; RLNR alone), whereas those did not undergo primary site resection were classified according to RLNR status (RLNR alone; non-surgery). Overall survival (OS) curves and hazard ratios were evaluated in crude, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)-weighted, and propensity score matching (PSM) -matched cohorts.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was overall survival in each group, including 3-year OS and 5-year OS. OS was defined as the time interval from CC diagnosis to the date of the last follow-up or death from any cause. Patients deemed alive at the last known follow-up were censored.

The demographic and clinical characteristics for the categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (percentages). Intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-square test. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed to identify confounding pathways between exposure and overall survival. Pretreatment covariates were identified as confounders with direct paths to both the exposure and outcome, and were subsequently incorporated into the propensity score model. The covariates included age, race, marital status, year of diagnosis, primary site, tumor size, histology, FIGO 2018 stage, grade, and lymph node status. We stratified patients with locally advanced-stage CC who received chemotherapy and radiotherapy into two cohorts based on the exposure of interest. One cohort underwent RLNR, with primary surgery as the exposure. The other cohort did not undergo primary surgery, with RLNR as the exposure. Owing to the highly imbalanced distribution of baseline characteristics across cohorts (e.g., RLNR alone vs a non-surgery ratio of 1:9.5), IPTW was employed as the primary analytical approach to balance baseline characteristics while minimizing information loss compared with PSM. Propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression. Stabilized weights were calculated as follows: for the exposed group, sw = p/e(x); for the unexposed group, sw = (1−p)/[1−e(x)], where p denotes the marginal probability of exposure and e(x) is the estimated propensity score. Weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to enhance stability. For sensitivity analyses, 1:1 nearest-neighbor PSM with a caliper of 0.1×standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score was performed to balance baseline characteristics. Covariate balance was evaluated using absolute standardized difference (ASD), with |ASD| < 10% considered indicative of adequate balance. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan‒Meier and compared with the log-rank test. Cox regression models were fitted to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariable Cox regression model. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption was assessed using the Schoenfeld residual method, with chi-square statistics and corresponding p-values. Covariates satisfying the PH assumption were analyzed using standard multivariable Cox regression. For covariates violating the PH assumption, a time-dependent Cox regression model incorporating interaction terms between the covariate and time was constructed, decomposing the covariate effect into time-fixed and time-varying components. When the interaction term was defined as covariate×time, exp(β+γt) was interpreted as the relative change in the hazard ratio per unit increase in the covariate over follow-up time t. 11 To evaluate potential period/cohort effects, we performed multivariable Cox regression analyses stratified by year of diagnosis (2004-2008, 2009-2013, and 2014-2018). Statistical power was calculated based on the observed sample size and event rate, with a two-sided α of 0.05. The E-value was computed to quantify the potential effect of unmeasured confounding. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.0.0 software, and statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 and R software (version 4.0.5).

Results

Clinicopathological and survival analyses of patients with early-stage CC according to the 2018 FIGO staging system

As shown in Figure 1, a total of 16,232 individuals with CC were identified from the SEER database between 2004 and 2018. The baseline characteristics of dataset 1 were listed in Supplemental Table S2. Tumor histology and grade distribution were compared across the 2018 FIGO stage subgroups. Squamous cell carcinoma was more prevalent in stage IB3 than in stage IB1 (60.5% vs 47.9%, p < 0.001), whereas adenocarcinoma was more common in stage IB1 than in stage IB3 (47.0% vs 30.9%, p < 0.001). Among the subgroups, adenosquamous carcinoma had the lowest incidence (Supplemental Table S3). Stage IB3 had slightly higher percentage of grade III and grade IV than stage IB1 (42.7% vs 25.5%, 2.7% vs 1.9%, p > 0.05 for both), whereas stage IB1 had higher percentages of grade I and grade II than stage IB3 (22.2% vs 9.8%, p = 0.042, 43.1% vs 36.2%, p = 0.086) (Supplemental Table S4). The 2018 FIGO stage IB subgroup survival curves were presented in Figure 2(A) (except for the unknown stage). The survival curves for the 2018 FIGO stage subdivisions (IB1 vs IB2) differed significantly (p < 0.001).

Survival analysis of patients with locally advanced-stage CC

Oncologists defined pelvic lymph node metastases as stage IIIC1 in the revised 2018 FIGO system based on radiological or pathological diagnosis. People with stage IIIC1 disease would have been categorized into different groups in the 2014 FIGO system. When utilizing the 2014 FIGO criteria, patients classified as stage IIIC1 were most generally assigned to stages IB1, IB2 and IIB (Figure 2(B)). Using the 2018 FIGO staging method, stage IIIC1 patients had better survival than stage IIIA and IIIB patients (p < 0.001) (Figure 2(C)). We further subclassified patients in stage IIIC1 by primary cervical mass T status and found significant variations in survival curves for T1, T2 and T3 patients (all p < 0.001) (Figure 2(D)). Those with stage IIIC1 CC (T3a and T3b) had substantially worse survival than those with stage IIIA (no nodal metastasis) (p = 0.008 for IIIA vs IIIC1 (T3a) and p = 0.008 for IIIA vs IIIC1 (T3b)) (Figure 2(E)).

Effect of RLNR on survival in patients with early-stage CC

As shown in Figure 1, among the patients who received chemotherapy and radiotherapy for the early-stage CC (stage IA/IB1-IB2/IIA1), 758 underwent RLNR, and a total of 1044 underwent primary surgery at the primary site. In the RLNR cohort, the 5-year OS rates were comparable between RLNR with primary surgery and RLNR alone (83.4% vs 77.2%; HR for death: 0.843; 95% CI, 0.394 to 1.800; p = 0.086 by the log-rank test; Figure 3(A)). In the cohort with primary surgery, the 5-year OS rates were comparable between RLNR with primary surgery and primary surgery alone (83.4% vs 80.7%; HR for death: 1.099; 95% CI, 0.368 to 3.280; p = 0.254 by the log-rank test; Figure 3(B)). Evaluation of baseline characteristics and covariate balance among patients with locally advanced-stage cervical cancer

We categorized patients with locally advanced-stage CC (stage IB3/IIA2-IIB/III/IVA) who received chemotherapy and radiotherapy into two cohorts: 3048 patients who underwent RLNR and 8009 patients who did not undergo primary site surgery. In the RLNR cohort, significant differences were observed in marital status, primary site, tumor size, histology, grade, and FIGO stage (all p < 0.001), whereas age, race, year of diagnosis, and lymph node status were comparable between the groups (Table 2). In the cohort without primary surgery, the RLNR alone group were more likely to be diagnosed during 2004–2008 (34.8% vs 26.9%), were more frequently White (77.8% vs 71.6%), were younger (≤39 years: 32.9% vs 20.4%), had tumors ≤40 mm (33.2% vs 16.1%), presented with more advanced FIGO stages (stage III: 71.3% vs 48.5%), and had a higher rate of positive lymph node status (65.1% vs 34.4%) than the non-surgery group (all p < 0.05). These findings suggest that younger patients with imaging or pathology suggesting regional lymph node involvement and more advanced FIGO stages were more likely to receive RLNR followed by chemotherapy and radiotherapy (Table 3). Using a directed acyclic graph (Supplemental Figure S1), we identified 10 pretreatment covariates as confounders, all with direct paths to both the exposure and outcome. The minimal sufficient adjustment sets for both the RLNR and no primary surgery cohorts included all 10 covariates, with no colliders or mediators identified. These variables were subsequently incorporated into the propensity score models for both cohorts.

Covariate balance was assessed using ASD across the crude, IPTW-weighted, and PSM-matched cohorts. In the crude cohort, substantial imbalances were observed for multiple covariates: in the RLNR cohort, marital status, primary site, tumor size, histology, grade, and FIGO 2018 stage were unbalanced; in the no primary surgery cohort, age, race, year of diagnosis, tumor size, FIGO 2018 stage, and lymph node status showed notable disparities. After PSM, most covariates achieved satisfactory balance, although some residual imbalance remained (Supplemental Table S6). Following IPTW, all covariates achieved an adequate balance (|ASD| < 10%) in both cohorts, indicating that IPTW-weighted models provided superior covariate balance compared with the crude data and were at least comparable to PSM-matched models, while the full sample size was preserved (Supplemental Table S6). Thus, we used IPTW-weighted models as the primary analysis, PSM-matched models for sensitivity analyses, and crude models as an unadjusted reference.

Effect of RLNR on survival in patients with locally advanced-stage CC in the crude and IPTW-weighted cohorts

In the crude cohort, the survival rates of treatment modalities for locally advanced-stage CC (stage IB3/IIA2-IIB/III/IVA) are summarized in Table 1. In the RLNR cohort (Figure 4(A)), the 5-year OS rate was significantly lower in the RLNR alone group than in the RLNR with primary surgery group (62.9% vs 73.2%, HR for death: 1.491; 95% CI, 1.294 to 1.717; p < 0.001 by the log-rank test). Variables with p<0.05 in the univariate Cox analysis, including the exposure (primary surgery) and covariates (age, race, tumor size, histology, 2018 FIGO stage, and lymph node status) were entered into the multivariable model. Among them, the PH assumption was violated for age, tumor size, and histology (all p < 0.05; Supplemental Table S7), for which time-interaction terms were created. The time-dependent Cox regression model indicated that primary surgery may be a protective prognostic factor (Table 4, Supplemental Table S8). Additionally, patients with black race, larger tumor size, non-squamous cell histology, and more advanced stage had a higher mortality risk, except for those aged 40–64 years. HRs for age increased over time, whereas those for tumor size decreased (Supplemental Table S8). In the no-primary-surgery cohort (Figure 4(B)), the RLNR alone group showed better OS than the non-surgery group (62.9% vs 56.5%, HR for death: 0.776; 95% CI, 0.686 to 0.877; p < 0.001 by the log-rank test). All variables with p<0.05 in the univariate Cox analysis, including RLNR and covariates (age, race, marital status, year of diagnosis, tumor size, histology, 2018 FIGO stage, grade, and lymph node status), were entered into the multivariable model. Among them, the PH assumption was violated for age, marital status, tumor size, histology, 2018 FIGO stage, grade, and RLNR (all p < 0.05; Supplemental Table S7), prompting time-dependent Cox regression, which suggested RLNR as a protective prognostic factor (Table 4, Supplemental Table S8). Moreover, patients diagnosed in 2014–2018, aged 40–64 years, of other races, or married/single status now had a lower mortality risk, whereas those with black race, larger tumor size, non-squamous histology, poor differentiation, and advanced stage had a higher mortality risk. In addition, the HRs for age and marital status increased over time, while those for tumor size and 2018 FIGO stage decreased (Supplemental Table S8).

To mitigate the influence of confounding factors, IPTW was employed to balance baseline characteristics between the RLNR alone and other cohorts in patients with locally advanced-stage CC receiving chemotherapy and radiotherapy. After IPTW, all covariates were well balanced (Tables 2 and 3; all p > 0.05). In the RLNR cohort (Figure 4(C)), the RLNR alone group had a significantly lower OS than the RLNR with primary surgery group (HR for death: 1.428; 95% CI, 1.203 to 1.695; p < 0.001 by the log-rank test). Conventional multivariable Cox regression, including primary surgery and covariates with p<0.05 on univariate analysis, confirmed primary surgery as a prognostic factor (HR for death: 1.467; 95% CI, 1.231 to 1.748, p < 0.001; Table 4, Supplemental Table S9), with the PH assumption satisfied for all covariates (Supplemental Table S7). Similarly, in the no-primary-surgery cohort (Figure 4(D)), the RLNR alone yielded better OS than the non-surgery (HR for death: 0.705; 95% CI, 0.622 to 0.799; p < 0.001 by the log-rank test). Conventional multivariable Cox regression analysis confirmed RLNR as a prognostic factor (HR for death: 0.762; 95% CI, 0.663 to 0.876, p < 0.001) (Table 4, Supplemental Table S9).

Sensitivity analysis in the PSM-matched cohorts

After PSM, among patients with locally advanced-stage CC, 1166 underwent RLNR and 962 did not undergo primary surgery. In the RLNR cohort, OS remained significantly lower in the RLNR alone group than in the RLNR with primary surgery group (HR for death: 1.521; 95% CI, 1.240 to 1.865; p < 0.001 by the log-rank test; Figure 4(E)). Variables with p<0.05 in univariate analysis, including primary surgery and covariates (age, tumor size, 2018 FIGO stage, grade, and lymph node status), were entered into the multivariable model. The PH assumption was violated for age and tumor size (Supplemental Table S7), prompting time-dependent Cox regression, which confirmed primary surgery as a protective factor (HR for death: 1.674; 95% CI, 1.348 to 2.078, p < 0.001; Table 4, Supplemental Table S10). In the cohort without primary surgery, the OS was greater in the RLNR alone group than in the non-surgery group (HR for death: 0.631; 95% CI, 0.514 to 0.776; p <0.001 by the log-rank test; Figure 4(F)). Variables with p<0.05 in univariate analysis, including RLNR and covariates (age, race, 2018 FIGO stage, and lymph nodal status), were entered into the multivariable model, with the PH assumption violated only for age (Supplemental Table S7). Time-dependent Cox regression confirmed RLNR as a prognostic factor (HR for death: 0.606; 95% CI, 0.495 to 0.743, p < 0.001; Table 4, Supplemental Table S10). Overall, the post-PSM findings were consistent with those from the IPTW-adjusted and crude analyses.

Analysis stratified by year of diagnostic based on crude, IPTW-weighted, and PSM-matched cohorts

Analyses stratified by year of diagnosis were performed using multivariable Cox regression models (Supplemental Figure S2). In the RLNR cohort, the RLNR with primary surgery group consistently suggested a survival benefit over RLNR alone across all three periods (2004–2008, 2009–2013, and 2014–2018), with consistent findings in both the crude and adjusted models. In the IPTW-weighted cohort, the HR point estimates were 1.265, 1.861, and 1.636, respectively, all of which exceeded 1.2. In the cohort without primary surgery, the RLNR alone group also exhibited a consistent survival benefit compared with the non-surgery group across the three periods. In the IPTW-weighted cohort, the HR point estimates were 0.630, 0.874, and 0.677, respectively, all below 1.0. Although the 95% CIs for some period-specific estimates cross 1.0, the consistent trend across periods and across different analytical models supports the robustness of these findings.

Discussion

The incidence of locally advanced-stage CC remains substantial, and the optimal treatment strategy continues to warrant further investigation, particularly in the context of the updated FIGO staging system. In this population-based analysis, after IPTW adjustment, patients who received RLNR followed by chemotherapy and radiotherapy had better survival than those who received chemotherapy and radiotherapy alone (HR for death: 0.705; 95% CI, 0.622 to 0.799; p < 0.001 by the log-rank test), although this survival benefit was less pronounced than that observed with RLNR with primary surgery. These findings were consistent in crude analyses and were further validated by PSM sensitivity analyses, confirming the robustness of our findings. Collectively, among patients with locally advanced-stage CC who did not undergo primary surgery, RLNR followed by chemotherapy and radiotherapy might be associated with better survival relative to chemotherapy and radiotherapy alone.

Radiotherapy is a mainstay for locally advanced-stage CC, 12 and the addition of chemotherapy increases 5-year survival by 6% (HR=0.810; p < 0.001). 13 Technological advances, including intensity-modulated radiotherapy and MRI-guided adaptive brachytherapy, have enhanced local control and reduced toxicity. 14 Although primary site surgery showed a survival benefit in our study, its role remains controversial and largely confined to selected cases12. Therefore, this population-based analysis investigated whether RLNR could further improve outcomes in locally advanced-stage CC patients receiving chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

With the incorporation of lymph node status into the 2018 FIGO staging system, imaging has inherent limitations in detecting micrometastases and metastases within non-enlarged lymph nodes. 15 Therefore, the clinical value of RLNR in locally advanced-stage CC merits renewed evaluation. Although previous studies have confirmed that reducing lymph node tumor burden prolongs survival, their primary focus has been on para-aortic lymphadenectomy,16,17 with limited attention given to the independent survival effect of RLNR. Due to the registry-based nature of the SEER database, detailed information on specific surgical procedures was not available, such as dissection, debulking, or staging. Nevertheless, we emphasize that this heterogeneity does not represent statistical confounding bias, but rather reflects genuine clinical reality. In routine real-world practice, the boundaries among these three surgical approaches are inherently indistinct. The extent of lymph node resection by surgeons is highly dependent on intraoperative findings: when multiple positive node groups are identified, the dissection range naturally expands; conversely, when fixed and confluent unresectable lesions are encountered, the procedure more closely resembles debulking rather than formal dissection.18–20 Therefore, viewing RLNR as a continuous operative spectrum rather than discrete mutually exclusive categories aligns more closely with clinical practice.

The unavailability of surgical subtype information was not considered a limitation. Instead, the analysis focused on the objectively recorded extent of lymph node resection in the SEER database. Core confounding factors were rigorously controlled through careful statistical design. Key clinical determinants were incorporated into propensity score models including lymph node status, FIGO stage, and primary tumor size. IPTW (primary analysis) and PSM (sensitivity analysis) were used for modeling. These variables were also included in multivariable Cox regression models to achieve additional adjustment. Consistent findings were obtained across IPTW, PSM, and crude multivariable Cox regression analyses. The IPTW-adjusted HR was 0.762 (95% CI: 0.663-0.876; p < 0.001). The addition of RLNR to chemotherapy and radiotherapy may be associated with a survival benefit relative to chemotherapy and radiotherapy alone in patients with locally advanced-stage CC.

Notably, the use of RLNR is not without cost. The spectrum of potential complications of RLNR includes lower extremity lymphedema, 21 pelvic lymphocele, 22 and obturator nerve or vascular injury. 23 Among these, lower extremity lymphedema is the most common. This complication significantly impairs quality of life. A prospective study in 2025 reported a 5-year cumulative incidence of 39.6%. 24 Moreover, this condition is difficult to reverse completely. To address this, we further performed subgroup analyses based on baseline characteristics to identify the population that might benefit most from RLNR. The results showed that among patients with radiologically or pathologically confirmed regional lymph node metastasis, those aged ≤39 years (reflecting better surgical tolerance) and those with FIGO stage III disease (reflecting higher tumor burden) were more commonly observed. This finding provides a reference for precise selection of surgical candidates in clinical practice.

Our study also found that, among patients with FIGO IIIC1 (regional lymph node metastasis), further stratification by T stage (T1, T2, T3) revealed a significant gradient in survival curves (all p < 0.001; Figure 2(D)). This finding indicates that the current IIIC1 category represents a highly heterogeneous group. Its prognosis is influenced not only by lymph node status, but also by local tumor burden. This is consistent with previous reports. 25 A large SEER-based study by Matsuo et al. (n = 11733) confirmed that 5-year survival rates in IIIC1 patients decreased significantly with higher T stage (T1: 74.8%, T2: 58.7%, T3: 39.3%). 26 Similarly, a 2025 Japanese multicenter study found that survival differed significantly across IIIC1 subgroups (T1, T2, T3AB). 27 Notably, the prognosis of IIIC1-T3AB was comparable to that of stage IIIAB. Together, these findings suggest that further sub-stratification of IIIC1 patients by T stage (e.g., IIIC1a, b, c) is necessary and reasonable in both clinical practice and future research. 28 Such refinement would allow more precise prognostic assessment and individualized treatment planning.

Our study enrolled patients over nearly 20 years, during which imaging, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and surgical techniques advanced substantially.29–31 Such temporal heterogeneity is a potential source of confounding in observational studies. To address this, we stratified analyses by year of diagnosis (2004–2008, 2009–2013, 2014–2018) and reported results from IPTW-weighted (primary), PSM-matched (sensitivity), and crude models. In the cohort without primary surgery, the RLNR alone group consistently showed better survival than the non-surgery group, with HRs as follows: IPTW-adjusted 0.630, 0.874, and 0.677; crude 0.768, 0.833, and 0.654; and PSM-adjusted 0.601, 0.667, and 0.655. All estimates were below 1.0, indicating a consistent survival benefit. Although some 95% CIs crossed 1.0 in certain periods, this reflects reduced statistical power, not directional instability. Notably, the key finding is not the significance of any single period, but the consistent direction of effects across all periods and all three analytical approaches. 32 This consistency effectively rules out confounding and temporal fluctuations, supporting the robustness of the RLNR survival benefit. Therefore, even in the context of rapid medical progress, our conclusions remain temporally stable and relevant to current clinical practice.

Recently, integrating anatomic staging with functional imaging and molecular profiling has been considered a promising approach for selecting candidates for adjunctive lymph node resection.33–35 Studies have shown that [18F]FDG-PET/CT serves as a potential predictor of outcome in node-positive CC. 36 Furthermore, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes have emerged as important prognostic and predictive biomarkers.37–39 Consequently, lymph node resection was hypothesized to yield survival benefits in patients with nodal metastases exhibiting radioresistant features or an immunosuppressive phenotype, as identified through integrated PET/CT metabolic parameters. Although our SEER-based study could not directly evaluate these factors, it provided a foundation for future translational and personalized research in this direction. 40

The conclusion had several limitations. First, the SEER database lacked detailed information on patient performance status, detailed intraoperative information, the sequence of surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and more nuanced clinical details. Consequently, the DAG-based covariate set was inherently constrained by what SEER records. Key plausible confounders such as performance status, detailed nodal burden, and specific biological markers were absent. Based on the observed sample size and number of events, this study achieved >98% statistical power to detect the observed hazard ratio (two-sided α = 0.05). In IPTW-adjusted analyses, the E-value was 2.410 (lower CI: 1.720) for the RLNR cohort, and 2.150 (lower CI: 1.780) for the cohort without primary surgery. These E-values imply that unmeasured confounders would need to be associated with both exposure and outcome by HRs of ≥ 2.400 and ≥ 2.150, respectively, to fully explain away the observed associations. Although HRs of ≥ 2-fold or 3-fold are occasionally reported, they are uncommon; variables with such strong effects on both treatment and outcome are likely even rarer. 41 Importantly, receiving RLNR without primary surgery likely reflects unmeasured factors directly associated with prognosis, such as surgical fitness and intraoperative resectability, which are not fully captured by the E-value alone. Second, lymph node status information was incomplete. Aortic lymph node involvement was not recorded. This restricted our analysis to IIIC1 patients alone, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Although the SEER database has since been updated to 2023, we chose pre-2018 data because the IIIC2 subgroup had a median follow-up of only about 5 years, which is likely insufficient to capture long-term survival differences. More importantly, from a clinical practice perspective, RLNR is more accessible and widely adopted than para-aortic lymphadenectomy in institutions beyond highly specialized centers in China. Therefore, our evaluation of RLNR followed by chemotherapy and radiotherapy in locally advanced-stage CC offers more directly relevant evidence for clinicians managing this disease in real-world settings. Future studies using updated SEER data with longer follow-up and detailed IIIC2 stratification are needed to validate these findings and assess the survival benefit in the para-aortic subgroup. Third, the retrospective study design and reliance on a U.S.-based population limit the generalizability of our findings to other settings, such as developing countries. Nevertheless, by using the SEER database, we validated the hypothesis that this treatment modality confers a survival benefit. This analysis provides valuable real-world data that may help guide clinical decision-making and inform future prospective trials.

We propose a prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) to provide higher-level evidence. Beyond the limitations inherent to registry-based analyses, the observed survival benefit of RLNR should be considered hypothesis-generating. Inclusion criteria would be: histologically confirmed cervical cancer, 2018 FIGO stage IB3, IIA2, or IIB-IVA,9,42 and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 0–1. 43 Exclusion criteria would include para-aortic lymph node metastasis (IIIC2) or distant metastasis (IVB), prior pelvic radiotherapy, or severe comorbidities. Stratified randomization would be performed according to baseline imaging-confirmed regional lymph node status (CT/MRI/PET-CT) to ensure balanced distribution of node-positive patients (IIIC1) across arms. 44 The experimental arm would receive RLNR, which encompasses systematic lymphadenectomy, debulking, or staging surgery,42,45,46 with detailed documentation of the extent and type of procedure, followed by standard chemotherapy and radiotherapy. The comparator arm would receive standard chemotherapy and radiotherapy alone with imaging-guided surveillance, without nodal surgery. The primary outcome would be OS, with secondary outcomes including 3-/5-year progression-free survival (PFS), lower extremity lymphedema, pelvic lymphocele, and quality of life (QoL). Pre-specified subgroup analyses would examine whether the RLNR benefit is predominantly driven by the IIIC1 population. 28 Although logistically challenging, such a trial would definitively clarify the therapeutic role of surgical nodal intervention in the FIGO 2018 era.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this finding suggested that, in this real-world SEER analysis, RLNR followed by chemotherapy and radiotherapy might be associated with better survival outcomes compared with chemotherapy and radiotherapy alone in locally advanced-stage CC. However, the retrospective study design and potential unmeasured confounders render this finding hypothesis-generating, warranting prospective validation.
