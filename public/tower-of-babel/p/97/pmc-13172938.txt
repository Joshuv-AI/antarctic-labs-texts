Is Saturation Biopsy Still a Viable Alternative to Fusion Biopsy in the Era of Multiparametric MRI? A Comparative Analysis in Patients With Prior Negative Biopsy
Authors: Arda Taşkın Taşkıran, Yusuf Şenoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWe aimed to compare the diagnostic accuracy of transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)‐guided saturation biopsy (SB) and multiparametric MRI (mpMRI)‐TRUS fusion‐guided combined biopsy (CB) in patients with prior negative prostate biopsies.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from 160 patients who underwent transrectal prostate biopsy between January 2014 and March 2021. All had at least one prior negative biopsy. 80 patients underwent SB with a 20‐core TRUS‐guided approach. The remaining 80 patients, with mpMRI‐detected PIRADS ≥ 3 lesions, underwent CB including 12‐core systematic plus 2–4 targeted cores per lesion. Prostate cancer and clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) detection rates, and clinical parameters were compared between groups.ResultsThe groups had no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics. The PCa detection rate was 20% in the CB group and 16.3% in the SB group (p = 0.682). csPCa detection rates were also similar: 11.3% in the CB cohort and 7.5% in the SB cohort (p = 0.589). Notably, the CB subgroup with PI‐RADS ≥ 4 lesions had a significantly higher csPCa detection rate (28.6%) than SB group (7.5%) (p = 0.016). Patients diagnosed with PCa had significantly lower free PSA and free/total PSA ratios (p < 0.05). Complication rates were low and similar in both groups.ConclusionsCB demonstrates the highest diagnostic yield for detecting csPCa, particularly in patients with PI‐RADS ≥ 4 lesions. However, in resource‐limited settings lacking mpMRI, systematic saturation biopsy remains a viable, safe, and effective alternative. PSA derivatives may serve as complementary tools to refine biopsy decisions.Keywords: MRI‐US fusion biopsy, prostate cancer, repeat biopsy, saturation biopsy

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most diagnosed malignancy among men worldwide [1]. According to the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines, the standard method for diagnosing PCa is transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) or transperineal‐guided biopsy utilizing 10–12 core samples [2]. However, standard biopsy protocols sample less than 0.05% of the prostate tissue on average. Furthermore, over 30% of cancers are located in the anterior horns, the apex, or the transitional zone—areas frequently missed by standard TRUS‐guided techniques [3, 4]. Consequently, the standard 12‐core TRUS biopsy is associated with a false‐negative rate of up to 20% [5].

In patients with persistently elevated serum prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) levels despite an initial negative biopsy, saturation biopsy (SB)—which involves obtaining more than 20 cores in a systematic manner—has traditionally been proposed to improve diagnostic yield [6, 7, 8]. However, increasing the number of cores may lead to greater procedural discomfort, higher rates of temporary erectile dysfunction, and the overdiagnosis of indolent, low‐risk cancers, potentially complicating treatment decisions [9, 10].

In recent years, advances in multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) technology and interpretation have enhanced the detection of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) in the repeat biopsy setting. MRI‐targeted biopsy (TBx) has emerged as a valuable technique [11, 12]. The PRECISION trial demonstrated that mpMRI‐targeted biopsy detects csPCa more effectively than standard 10–12 core TRUS biopsy (38% vs. 26%, p = 0.005) [13]. Although approximately 10% of cancers may still be missed with targeted biopsy (TBx) alone, current international guidelines advocate for pre‐biopsy mpMRI followed by a combined biopsy (CB) approach to maximize detection rates [14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

Despite the clear diagnostic advantages of mpMRI and targeted biopsy, the universal implementation of these modalities remains limited by significant barriers. Accessibility issues, the high cost of mpMRI equipment, and the requirement for experienced radiologists and specialized fusion software often restrict the use of TBx to high‐volume tertiary centers. In resource‐constrained settings or institutions where mpMRI is unavailable or contraindicated, clinicians face a dilemma regarding the optimal strategy for repeat biopsy. In such scenarios, systematic saturation biopsy may still represent a viable and necessary alternative to ensure significant cancers are not overlooked.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate and compare the overall and csPCa detection rates of SB and CB in patients with prior negative biopsies. Given the ongoing disparities in access to advanced imaging and concerns regarding overdiagnosis, our objective was to determine whether SB retains clinical utility as an effective diagnostic strategy in the absence of mpMRI availability.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary referral center in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the committee.

Study Population

Patients who were referred for repeat prostate biopsy due to clinical suspicion based on elevated or persistently high PSA levels, abnormal digital rectal examination (DRE), or mpMRI findings were retrospectively reviewed.

Between January 2014 and March 2021, 827 patients underwent TRUS‐guided prostate biopsy at our institution. Among them, 212 had a history of prior 12‐core negative biopsy and were candidates for repeat biopsy. 52 patients were excluded due to: previous diagnosis of PCa (n = 23), repeat biopsy with 12‐core only (n = 16), PSA > 20 ng/mL (n = 8), or age > 75 years (n = 5).

Biopsy Protocols and mpMRI Acquisition

Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) has been available at our institution since 2018. Therefore, prior to 2018, repeat biopsies were performed using SB with 20 TRUS‐guided cores (10 per lobe). After 2018, fusion‐guided biopsies, combining MRI‐targeted and systematic cores were performed according to contemporary guidelines.

mpMRI was performed using a 3‐T MAGNETOM Skyra system (Siemens) with T2‐weighted, diffusion‐weighted, and dynamic contrast‐enhanced sequences. All mpMRIs were interpreted by two radiologists with 5 and 7 years of experience using PI‐RADS v2.1 criteria. Patients with PI‐RADS ≥ 3 lesions underwent TBx plus 12‐core systematic biopsy, defined as CB.

All biopsies were performed under TRUS guidance using a biplane transrectal probe (GE Healthcare LOGIQ S8, IC5‐9‐D) and an 18 G, 20‐mm side‐notch semi‐automatic core biopsy needle (Geotek Maxicore‐U). All patients received pre‐biopsy antibiotic prophylaxis (1 g intramuscular ceftriaxone) and periprostatic nerve block with 10 mL of 2% prilocaine.

Fusion biopsies were performed using the V Nav electromagnetic tracking system (GE Healthcare), which utilized rigid registration and real‐time synchronization of pre‐acquired DICOM mpMRI with live ultrasound. To prevent impairment of sonographic visibility due to potential bleeding or artifacts, targeted biopsies were performed first. At least two targeted cores were obtained from each lesion using MR‐US fusion guidance, followed by a 12‐core systematic biopsy to complete the CB procedure.

Saturation biopsies (20 cores total) were performed by four experienced urologists (> 50 procedures/year) using the same equipment and technique (TRUS‐guided) under local anesthesia and antibiotic prophylaxis. On each side of the prostate, two cores were obtained from the lateral base, three from the lateral mid‐section, three from the apex, one from the parasagittal mid‐section, and one from the parasagittal base.

Histopathological Evaluation

All biopsy specimens were evaluated separately by two experienced genitourinary pathologists (7 and 10 years of experience). PCa was classified utilizing both the traditional Gleason scoring system and the contemporary ISUP (The International Society of Urological Pathology) grade group system. csPCa was defined as a Gleason score (GS) of ≥ 3 + 4 = 7 (ISUP grade ≥ 2); Gleason score 3 + 3 = 6 (ISUP grade 1) cancers were considered clinically insignificant prostate cancer (ciPCa). The primary endpoint was the comparison of csPCa and ciPCa detection rates between SB and CB groups.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome of the study was to assess and compare the diagnostic performance of SB and CB in detecting clinically significant PCa. Secondary variables included PSA, free PSA (fPSA), prostate‐specific antigen density (PSAD), digital rectal examination (DRE) findings, prostate volume (PV), and cancer detection rate.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using visual methods (histograms, probability plots) and the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and minimum–maximum values, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Chi‐square test was used for categorical data, and Student's t‐test for continuous variables. McNemar's test was employed to evaluate differences in biopsy methods within the CB group. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we compared the diagnostic efficacy of saturation biopsy (SB) and MRI‐US fusion‐guided combined biopsy (CB) in patients with a prior negative prostate biopsy. Our results showed an overall PCa detection rate of 20% in the CB group and 16.3% in the SB group, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.682). Similarly, the overall csPCa detection rates were comparable between the two cohorts (11.3% vs. 7.5%, p = 0.589). These detection rates are lower than those reported in the landmark PRECISION trial (38% for MRI‐targeted biopsy) [13]. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the difference in study populations; while PRECISION included biopsy‐naïve men, our cohort consisted exclusively of patients with prior negative biopsies, a group widely recognized to present greater diagnostic challenges.

A critical finding was the superior csPCa detection in the CB subgroup with PI‐RADS ≥ 4 lesions (28.6% vs. 7.5%, p = 0.016), despite similar overall outcomes. This divergence is likely twofold. First, the inclusion of patients with PI‐RADS 3 MRI findings in the general analysis dilutes the statistical advantage of targeted biopsy, masking its true efficacy which is prominent in high‐grade lesions. Second, the absence of pre‐biopsy mpMRI in the SB group prevents risk stratification; comparing a confirmed high‐risk CB subset against a heterogeneous SB cohort (with unknown MRI status) inherently widens this performance gap. These results confirm that the diagnostic superiority of mpMRI is driven primarily by its ability to identify and target high‐grade lesions.

In recent years, the criteria for repeat biopsy have shifted from relying solely on elevated PSA levels or abnormal DRE findings to incorporating mpMRI‐based risk stratification. The PROMIS trial demonstrated the high negative predictive value of mpMRI, suggesting its utility in reducing unnecessary biopsies [19]. Similarly, Zattoni et al. reported that 36% of patients with persistent PI‐RADS ≥ 3 lesions after a prior negative biopsy harbored csPCa, underscoring the importance of re‐biopsy in this subgroup [20]. Our csPCa detection rate of 28.6% in high‐risk lesions aligns with these findings, reinforcing the EAU guidelines advocating for mpMRI before repeat biopsy.

Beyond mpMRI, the emergent role of Prostate‐Specific Membrane Antigen (PSMA) Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography (PET/CT) is gaining significant attention in the diagnostic pathway of csPCa. Recent evidence suggests that PSMA PET/CT‐guided targeted biopsy could further refine patient selection and minimize biopsy‐related morbidity. For instance, a recent study by Pepe et al. evaluated the accuracy of 68Ga‐PSMA PET/CT in men enrolled in active surveillance protocols [21]. They reported that PSMA PET/CT demonstrated a superior diagnostic accuracy compared to mpMRI (83.3% vs. 70.2%) and a notably lower false‐positive rate (17.5% vs. 40%). Although the occurrence of false negatives indicates that it may not yet completely replace systematic sampling such as saturation biopsy, the incorporation of PSMA PET/CT could safely spare a substantial number of unnecessary scheduled biopsies (up to 77.5%). Therefore, PSMA PET/CT represents a highly promising, emergent modality for targeted biopsy, particularly in challenging clinical scenarios or when mpMRI yields indeterminate results.

Nevertheless, mpMRI is not universally accessible across all healthcare settings. In such resource‐limited environments, alternative diagnostic strategies remain crucial. Tonttila et al. demonstrated that cognitive fusion biopsy could effectively detect anterior tumors even in standard clinical settings [22]. Our anatomical zone‐specific analysis aligns with this observation; we identified 3 anterior csPCa in the CB group and 2 in the SB group. This demonstrates that while MRI guidance facilitates the targeted identification of anterior tumors, saturation biopsy maintains a robust systematic capability to sample these often‐missed regions. In addition to biopsy techniques, Modi et al. highlighted the continued utility of PSA dynamics in environments lacking advanced imaging [23]. Consistent with their findings, our study observed significantly lower fPSA levels and f/t PSA ratios in patients diagnosed with PCa, reaffirming the critical role of PSA derivatives as complementary tools for risk stratification when mpMRI is unavailable.

Beyond imaging modalities, incorporating PSA derivatives and genetic markers plays a pivotal role in minimizing unnecessary repeat biopsies. Pepe and Aragona demonstrated that combining the PCA3 score with the free‐to‐total PSA ratio safely avoided 32.4% of unnecessary repeat saturation biopsies while detecting 96.3% of prostate cancers. Given its high negative predictive value (88.9%), PCA3 serves as a robust exclusion tool [24]. Consequently, utilizing these molecular markers offers a highly effective strategy to refine patient selection and reduce biopsy‐related morbidity, especially when advanced imaging is unavailable.

Diagnostic challenges also persist regarding indeterminate MRI findings; although PI‐RADS 3 lesions are often managed conservatively, Araújo et al. reported that up to 26.2% of these cases harbored ISUP ≥ 2 tumors upon fusion biopsy, supporting a more cautious approach even when mpMRI is utilized [25].

On the other hand, saturation biopsy remains a clinically relevant method, particularly in settings where mpMRI and fusion technology are not readily available. Amasyalı et al. reported that 30,9% of patients with a prior negative biopsy were diagnosed with csPCa using 20‐core SB, reinforcing the value of SB as a cost‐effective and accessible diagnostic tool [26]. Our data contributes to this perspective; in the absence of mpMRI guidance, the SB protocol yielded an overall cancer detection rate of 16.3% and a csPCa detection rate of 7.5%. While these rates are lower than those achieved with targeted strategies in high‐risk cases, they demonstrate that SB remains a viable salvage strategy to detect missed cancers in centers lacking advanced imaging infrastructure.

Furthermore, saturation biopsy maintains a crucial role in the management of patients enrolled in active surveillance (AS) protocols. Accurate histological monitoring is essential in AS to prevent misleadingly low biopsy grades that may lead to the underestimation of aggressive disease. As demonstrated by Pepe et al., the integration of scheduled transperineal saturation biopsy, particularly when combined with targeted cores, significantly reduces the risk of missing tumor upgrading during follow‐up [27]. This is strongly supported by a recent prospective, randomized study by Baba et al., which evaluated patients meeting active surveillance criteria. They found that an extended 20‐core biopsy protocol significantly reduced the ISUP Grade 1 upgrade rate after radical prostatectomy compared to a standard 12‐core biopsy (35.6% vs. 62.5%, p = 0.020) [28]. These findings confirm that extended systematic sampling increases the accuracy of identifying clinically insignificant prostate cancer and decreases the likelihood of upgrading after radical prostatectomy. Consequently, active surveillance can be implemented more safely, and unnecessary overtreatment may be significantly reduced.

A randomized prospective study by Kars et al. demonstrated that, when targeted fusion biopsy is not feasible due to technical limitations, the addition of a simple prostate mapping diagram to mpMRI reports significantly increases the diagnostic success rate of cognitive‐targeted biopsies, particularly for csPCa [29]. This increase is believed to be due to the reduction of operator‐related variability by providing visual standardization of lesion localization. Adding such a simple and applicable mapping support to cognitive biopsy, a widely preferred method due to its low cost and ease of use, may be an effective and cost‐effective approach to improving diagnostic success, similar to saturation biopsy. These findings support the integration of schematic mapping into routine practice to improve biopsy accuracy, particularly in centers that do not perform software‐assisted fusion or MRI‐guided biopsy.

Currently, transperineal biopsy is increasingly preferred and becoming the standard approach for PCa diagnosis due to its low risk of infection, feasibility under local anesthesia, and advantages of anatomic access. This method allows for the safe and effective application of a combination of systematic and targeted biopsies. In a prospective study by Kaufmann et al., which used the transperineal approach, 83% of csPCa were detected with targeted biopsy alone in patients with positive mpMRI findings, while the remaining 17% were detected with 20‐focus systematic saturation biopsy (SBx) [30]. This finding suggests that TBx alone may miss a significant proportion of csPCa. Furthermore, the fact that csPCa was detected with SBx in 15% of cases even in mpMRI‐negative cases demonstrates that systematic biopsies remain an important diagnostic tool. However, this group also exhibits a high rate of clinically insignificant cancers. Therefore, avoiding biopsy may be a reasonable option, especially in patients with low PSA density (< 0.1 ng/mL²) and negative mpMRI findings. These data, as observed in our study, support the idea that the combination of systematic and targeted biopsies increases diagnostic accuracy and that the use of TBx alone may be insufficient in some cases.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. The primary limitation is the lack of pre‐biopsy mpMRI in the SB cohort. Consequently, patients in the SB group could not be stratified according to PI‐RADS scores, preventing a direct comparison of high‐risk lesions between the two groups and leaving the true prevalence of csPCa in the SB arm unknown. In the MRI‐US fusion‐guided CB group, rigid image registration and potential patient or probe movement may have affected targeting accuracy. The retrospective, single‐center design introduces possible selection bias and limits generalizability. Additionally, the absence of radical prostatectomy specimens prevented correlation with final pathology. Group heterogeneity, particularly the low proportion of PI‐RADS ≥ 4 lesions in the CB group, and the relatively small sample size may have limited statistical power, highlighting the need for larger, prospective multicenter studies.

Conclusions

In patients with a prior negative biopsy, MRI‐US fusion‐guided combined biopsy demonstrates superior diagnostic accuracy for clinically significant PCa, particularly in those with PI‐RADS ≥ 4 lesions. However, in resource‐limited settings where mpMRI availability is restricted, saturation biopsy remains a viable and cost‐effective alternative to ensure significant cancers are not overlooked. Additionally, PSA derivatives, specifically free PSA and the f/t PSA ratio, serve as valuable complementary tools in refining re‐biopsy decisions. The integration of PSA derivatives with appropriate imaging modalities may enhance the accuracy of patient selection in this context. To confirm these findings and further refine patient selection criteria, prospective, multicenter studies with long‐term follow‐up are warranted.
