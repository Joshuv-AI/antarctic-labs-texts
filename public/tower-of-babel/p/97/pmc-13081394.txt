Fluoroalkylated antimicrobial peptides enables cytosolic delivery and eradication of intracellular Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Tao Gao, Tiexin Liu, Junjie Shen, Bingbo Bao, Saiyu Shi, Junqing Lin, Xianyou Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intracellular bacteria survive are difficult to eradicate by conventional antibiotics. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have garnered increasing attention due to their ability to exert broad-spectrum antibacterial effects through various mechanisms. However, they are also unable to effectively penetrate cell membranes to enter the cytoplasm. Fluorination has been proven to effectively enhance the ability of biopeptides to penetrate cell membranes. We designed a fluoroalkylated Omiganan (PFC-OMN) by conjugating a perfluorocarbon tag to a cysteine-terminated Omiganan (OMN) via a disulfide bond, enabling self-assembly into ~ 59 nm nanoparticles and glutathione-triggered release of active OMN in the cytosol. PFC-OMN retained extracellular activity against S. aureus (MIC = 12.5 µM) and exhibited markedly enhanced intracellular bactericidal potency (IMBC₉₉.₉ = 50 µM vs. > 800 µM for unmodified peptide). FITC-PFC-OMN showed 2.6–4.2× higher cellular uptake and broader cytosolic distribution compared with FITC-C-OMN. PFC-OMN displayed low hemolysis (HC₅₀ ≈ 325 µM) and macrophage cytotoxicity (IC₅₀ ≈ 450 µM). In a murine peritonitis model, PFC-OMN significantly reduced intracellular S. aureus counts. These results indicate that fluoroalkylation can enable AMP-mediated eradication of intracellular pathogens.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03978-8.

Introduction

Intracellular bacteria can invade and survive within mammalian host cells, allowing them to evade both host immune responses and the efficacy of antibiotics [1–3]. Within these protected niches, bacteria replicate and form reservoirs, often leading to chronic and recurrent infections. This highlights that eradicating intracellular bacteria is crucial for the clinical success of treating refractory infections. However, most antimicrobials face significant challenges during the intracellular invasion phase due to poor cellular penetration and inability to achieve therapeutic concentrations within the cytosol, resulting in limited efficacy and common clinical failures in treating intracellular infections. Furthermore, while some small-molecule antibiotics can penetrate host cells, they often face challenges such as bacterial efflux pumps, enzymatic inactivation, and the rapid emergence of resistance due to their single-target mechanisms [4].

The development of new strategies to combat intracellular bacteria is urgently needed, given the severe and escalating global health burden of bacterial infections and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, bacterial infections are the second leading cause of death worldwide, with millions of fatalities annually linked to AMR [5, 6]. A forecast estimates that by 2050, AMR could be responsible for up to 10 million deaths per year, posing a catastrophic threat to modern medicine [7]. The misuse of antibiotics and the increasing resistance of pathogens further complicate effective treatment [8]. Among these pathogens, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), the pathogen employed in this study, is a leading cause of both community-acquired and healthcare-associated infections globally [9]. Beyond the rapid emergence of drug-resistant strains, treatment failure in S. aureus infections is also frequently attributed to its ability to invade and persist within host cells [10, 11]. This intracellular lifestyle, once underappreciated, is now recognized as a key mechanism for immune evasion and is strongly associated with recurrent and severe infections, such as osteomyelitis and bacteremia [12]. Therefore, innovative therapeutic platforms capable of effectively penetrating host cells and eradicating these intracellular reservoirs are of paramount importance.

AMPs, also known as host-defense peptides (HDPs), are naturally occurring peptides present in animals, insects, and plants, and generally exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial activity [13]. Owing to their diverse mechanisms, including direct disruption of bacterial membranes and biofilms, as well as modulation of host immune responses—AMPs have emerged as promising alternatives to traditional antibiotics [14–16]. Notably, the membrane-lytic mechanism of AMPs provides a distinct advantage over conventional antibiotics by presenting a higher barrier to resistance development. While bacteria can develop resistance to single-target antibiotics through specific mutations, altering the fundamental composition of their cell envelope to evade AMP action is genetically more challenging and often comes with fitness costs. Nonetheless, clinical translation of AMPs is impeded by several challenges, including potential toxicity, enzymatic degradation resulting in a short half-life, poor in vivo bioavailability, limited serum stability, and rapid clearance—largely due to their cationic, amphipathic nature and low molecular weight [13, 14, 17]. Moreover, most therapeutic AMPs are primarily effective against extracellular bacteria, as they generally cannot cross cell membranes [18]. Recently, the most common strategy to enhance cytosolic delivery of peptides has involved conjugating cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), such as the transactivator of transcription (TAT) or oligoarginines, to cargo peptides [19, 20]. However, CPP-conjugated peptides face notable limitations, such as entrapment in degradative or recycling vesicles following cellular internalization [20, 21]. Additionally, these peptides remain vulnerable to enzymatic degradation and frequently require chemical modifications, such as cyclization, to enhance proteolytic stability and membrane permeability [22, 23].

Owing to the unique properties of fluorine, fluorination is widely employed in medicinal chemistry, chemical biology, and materials science to modulate the characteristics of organic molecules [24–27]. Over the past decade, nearly 50% of all U.S. FDA-approved drugs contained at least one fluorine atom [28], a trend that continued in 2023 with 12 new fluorinated drugs introduced to the market [29]. Owing to the low polarizability of the C–F bond, fluorinated ligands usually exhibited both hydrophobic and lipophobic [27]. This utility stems from the low polarizability of the C–F bond, which confers simultaneously hydrophobic and lipophobic (or fluorophilic) character to fluorinated ligands [30]. These properties provide significant advantages for intracellular drug delivery [31]. First, the low surface energy of fluorinated compounds promotes efficient self-assembly, enhancing cargo loading capacity and complex stability [32]. Second, their pronounced hydrophobicity facilitates efficient cell membrane penetration and subsequent endosomal escape [33]. Third, their lipophobicity prevents fusion with phospholipid bilayers during endocytosis, thereby minimizing premature cargo release prior to cytosolic entry and reducing material cytotoxicity [34]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that fluorination strategies enhance the intracellular delivery of diverse biomolecules, including nucleic acids, proteins, and peptides [35–37]. This technique has been used to efficiently deliver pro-apoptotic peptides (KLAKLAKKLAKLAK) to cancer cells, inducing apoptosis and demonstrating potent therapeutic efficacy in vivo [38].

Herein, we hypothesized that incorporating perfluorocarbon (PFC) into AMPs would improve cytosolic delivery efficiency and thereby enable AMP-mediated eradication of intracellular bacteria. To test this, we appended a cysteine residue to the N-terminus of Omiganan (OMN), a widely used AMP with broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, and subsequently conjugated PFC to OMN via an intracellularly cleavable disulfide bond, yielding PFC-OMN (Fig. 1). The in vitro and in vivo analysis demonstrated the anti-intracellular bacterial ability of PFC-OMN.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cysteine terminated OMN (C-OMN, CGILRWPWWPWRRK-NH2) and FITC labeled C-OMN (FITC-OMN, FITC-(acp)-CGILRWPWWPWRRK) with over 95% purity were synthesized by QYAOBIO (Wuhan, China) using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis method. OMN was purchased from MCE and used without further purification. 1 H,1 H,2 H,2 H-1-perfluorooctanethiol, 2,2’-dithiodipyridine, and other solvents (methanol, diethyl ether, ethyl acetate, and petroleum ether) were purchased from Macklin and used without further purification.

Synthesis and characterization of PFC-OMN

PFC-OMN were synthesized by a two-step procedure. To prepare thiopyridine-ended PFC, 2,2’-Dithiodipyridine (2.20 g, 10 mmol) was dissolved in 10 mL methanol, and then 1 H,1 H,2 H,2 H-1-perfluorooctanethiol (1.92 g, 4 mmol) in 10 mL methanol was added dropwise to the above solution. The mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature. After that, the solvent was evaporated under vacuum, and the product of thiopyridine-ended PFC (1.4 g, 72% yield) was obtained as a pale-yellow oil by column chromatography (ethyl acetate/petroleum ether 1/4 v/v). Next, 10 mg HS-OMN was dissolved in 2 mL methanol and added to 2 mL methanol containing 20 mg thiopyridine-ended PFC. The solution was stirred for 4 h under nitrogen atmosphere. Finally, PFC-OMN was obtained by participating from diethyl ether for three times as a pale-yellow powder (10.8 mg). The successful preparation of PFC-OMN was verified by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time of flight mass spectrometry (Maldi-Tof-MS, Ultraflextreme, Bruker, Germany) HPLC analysis. HPLC analysis was performed on LC3000 (Chuangxintongheng, China) with Kromasil 100-5C18 column (4.6 mm*250 mm,5 micron). The mobile phase was the mixture of 0.1% TFA in acetonitrile and 0.1%TFA in water. The flow rate was 1 mL/mL, and the column temperature was 30 ℃.

DLS measurement

1 mg PFC-OMN was dissolved in DDW, and the solution was sonicated (20 kHz, 20 W) for 2 min. Then the solution was diluted by DDW to get a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. Finally, the size distribution data was evaluated by a Nanoparticle Analyzer (DelsaMax Pro, Beckman Coulter).

Cell and bacterial culture

RAW264.7 macrophage cell line were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, high glucose; ZQ-100 and ZQ-120, Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), with or without 1% penicillin–streptomycin and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 29213; American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) was used for bacterial experiments. The bacterial inoculum was prepared by subculturing on tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates supplemented with 5% sheep blood (Millipore-Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) under standard conditions, followed by transfer into tryptic soy broth (TSB) and incubation in a shaking incubator at 37 °C for 18 h.

Cytotoxicity and hemolysis assays

The cytotoxicity of OMN, C-OMN and PFC-OMN toward RAW264.7 cells was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China). Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and incubated for 24 h. The cells were then exposed to various concentrations of OMN, C-OMN or PFC-OMN for an additional 24 h, followed by the addition of 10 µL CCK-8 solution per well. After incubation in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Untreated cells were used as the control (defined as 100% viability). The IC₅₀ value was determined as the lowest concentration causing 50% reduction in cell viability. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

The hemolytic activity of OMN, C-OMN and PFC-OMN was assessed according to established protocols [39]. Serial two-fold dilutions of each sample were prepared in PBS. Fresh rabbit red blood cells (RBCs, BioChannel, Nanjing, China) were washed twice with PBS and resuspended to ~ 1.0% (v/v). Equal volumes of diluted RBC suspension and sample solutions were mixed and incubated at 37 °C with shaking (250 rpm) for 1 h. PBS and 0.1% Triton X-100 served as negative and positive controls, respectively. Following centrifugation (1000 × g, 5 min), the supernatant was collected and transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance was recorded at 405 nm. The percentage of hemolysis was calculated, and the HC₅₀ value was defined as the concentration causing 50% hemolysis.

Cell viability assays

Cell viability after treatment with OMN, C-OMN, and PFC-OMN was evaluated using a Calcein-AM/PI Double Staining Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China). RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/well and cultured for 24 h. Cells were then treated with OMN, C-OMN or PFC-OMN at twice the MIC concentration (without FITC) for 24 h, washed once with sterile PBS, and incubated with Calcein-AM/PI solution for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained cells were observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (DMI 8, Leica, Germany). Live cells emitted green fluorescence (Calcein-AM, Ex/Em = 494/517 nm), whereas dead cells exhibited red fluorescence (PI, Ex/Em = 535/617 nm).

Cytosolic delivery of peptides

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/well, each containing a sterile coverslip, and cultured for 24 h. Cells were then treated with FITC-labeled C-OMN or PFC-OMN at various concentrations diluted in 200 µL serum-free medium for 0.5–2 h. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 15 min, washed again, and mounted on glass slides using Antifade Gold. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM, TCS SP5, Leica, Germany). For quantitative analysis, fluorescence intensity of cells treated for 4–12 h under the same conditions was measured by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa™, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) prior to fixation.

To further investigate the endocytic mechanism of PFC-OMN, RAW264.7 cells were pretreated with chlorpromazine (CPZ, 20 µM)), an inhibitor of caveolae-mediated endocytosis, for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were then washed twice with PBS and subsequently incubated with peptides for 2 h under the conditions described above. After treatment, peptide uptake was visualized by LSCM as described.

In vitro antibacterial activity against extracellular and intracellular bacteria

The minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of OMN, C-OMN, PFC-OMN and PFC-OMN (with 10 mM GSH) against S. aureus were first determined by broth microdilution. Briefly, 100 µL of tryptic soy broth (TSB) containing two-fold serial dilutions of each compound was dispensed into 96-well plates. S. aureus suspensions were adjusted to 5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL and added to each well. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, bacterial growth was quantified by measuring OD₆₀₀ using a microplate reader.

To establish the intracellular infection model, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) containing sterile coverslips and incubated for 24 h. Prior to infection, the culture medium was replaced with antibiotic-free DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 30 min. Logarithmic-phase S. aureus cells were harvested by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 5 min), washed, and resuspended in PBS. Bacteria were adjusted to 5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL (OD₆₀₀ = 0.6) and stained with SYTO9 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (5 µL, 5 mM; λex/λem = 483/503 nm) for 15 min, followed by triple PBS washing. Macrophages were infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 for 1–4 h. After infection, extracellular bacteria were removed by PBS washing and eradicated by treatment with gentamicin (50 µg/mL, 1 h). Fluorescence intensity was quantified by flow cytometry (λex/λem = 483/503 nm), and infected cells (2 h post-infection) were visualized by laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) to confirm intracellular colonization.

The minimum bactericidal concentration required to kill 99.9% of intracellular bacteria (IMBC₉₉.₉) was determined as follows. RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 10 cm dishes (1 × 10⁶ cells/well) for 24 h and then infected with S. aureus for 2 h. After extracellular bacteria were eliminated as described above, cells were washed and evenly redistributed into 96-well plates. Predetermined concentrations of peptides were added, and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. Cells were then washed, lysed with HBSS containing 0.1% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100, and serial 10-fold dilutions were prepared within 15 min. Aliquots were plated on TSA and incubated overnight to enumerate CFUs. Bacterial killing efficiency was calculated relative to PBS-treated controls. Each assay was performed in triplicate on independent days.

For confirmatory assays, RAW264.7 cells infected with SYTO9-labeled S. aureus were treated with PBS, or with 2×MIC concentrations of C-OMN or PFC-OMN (without FITC) in antibiotic-free DMEM. After 24 h, fluorescence intensity was quantified by flow cytometry, and intracellular bacteria were visualized by LSCM. At 12–48 h post-treatment, cells were lysed, and lysates were serially diluted, plated on TSA, and incubated for 24 h for CFU enumeration.

In vivo antibacterial activity and toxicity

Animal care and ethics

Female C57BL/6 mice (10 weeks old) were maintained under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a temperature- (20–23 °C) and humidity-controlled (50%) facility with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Mice were housed four per cage with free access to food and water.

Biosafety analysis

Mice were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 3 per group) and treated every 2 days for 2 weeks with PBS, or 2×MIC concentrations of PFC-OMN or C-OMN (without FITC) diluted in PBS, via tail vein injection (intravenous injection, i.v.). Body weights were monitored every two days. On day 7, blood samples were collected from the orbital sinus for biochemical analyses, including serum albumin (ALB), total protein (TP), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CRE), and total bilirubin (TBIL). Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) were harvested, fixed, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for histopathological evaluation.

Peritonitis infection model

An S. aureus–induced peritonitis model was used to evaluate in vivo antibacterial efficacy. Mice were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with S. aureus (5 × 10⁷ CFU in 100 µL PBS). After 24 h, mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 3) and treated (i.p.) with PBS, C-OMN, or PFC-OMN (without FITC) for 24 h. Subsequently, 2 mL of cold HBSS was injected intraperitoneally, and mice were sacrificed to collect peritoneal lavage fluid. One-third of the lavage was used to quantify total CFUs. Another third was centrifuged, and the supernatant was plated to determine extracellular CFUs. The remaining third was incubated with gentamicin (50 µg/mL) to eliminate extracellular bacteria, followed by lysis with HBSS containing 0.1% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 to quantify intracellular CFUs.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were applied. For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was employed to evaluate statistical differences. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed before applying parametric tests. Exact p values are reported, and values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated in figures as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****).

Results

Synthesis and characterization of PFC-OMN

In this study, to investigate whether PFC modification enables AMP-mediated intracellular bacterial elimination, we designed PFC-OMN by forming a disulfide bond between the PFC and OMN moiety. The synthetic procedure, illustrated in Fig. 2A, involved conjugating cyste`ine-modified OMN with PFC via disulfide exchange reaction. The molecular weight of PFC-OMN determined by MALDI-TOF-MS was 2319 Da ([M + H+], Fig. 2B, C), matching theoretical value and confirming successful synthesis. Furthermore, we have also evaluated the purity of final PFC-OMN by HPLC analysis (Figure S1). The result showed a 90.5% purity of the final product of PFC-OMN. We observed that PFC-OMN could not be directly dispersed in PBS. To prepare aqueous nanoparticle dispersions, the compound was first dissolved in DMSO and then subjected to at least 100-fold dilution in water. The DLS analysis revealed the formation of nanoparticles with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 57.6 nm (Fig. 2D) with a PDI of 0.28, while TEM imaging further confirmed their spherical morphology in aqueous environments (Fig. 2E). Then, the in vitro cell toxicity of OMN, PFC-OMN and C-OMN was evaluated by the hemolysis of rabbit red blood cells and the cell viability of RAW 246.7 cells (Table 1; Fig. 3A, B). All peptides exhibited minimal toxicity, with HC₅₀ values of approximately 320–440 µM and IC₅₀ values of 450–520 µM. Live/dead staining further demonstrated that the toxicity of all antimicrobial peptides at a concentration of 25 µM toward RAW 264.7 cells was comparable to that of PBS (Fig. 3C, D, E).

PFC-OMN showed enhanced intracellular distribution

We next investigated the cytosolic delivery of C-OMN and PFC-OMN in macrophages. Previous reports have shown that fluoroalkylation facilitates peptide endocytosis and endosomal escape, attributed to the strong tendency of fluorinated chains to form nanostructures and their unique amphiphobic nature that prevents fusion with cell membranes [38]. To examine intracellular uptake, both peptides were labeled with FITC. RAW264.7 cells were incubated with FITC–C-OMN or FITC–PFC-OMN for 0.5–2 h at varying concentrations, followed by confocal microscopy to visualize peptide distribution and flow cytometry to quantify intracellular fluorescence after 4–12 h.

Both peptides displayed dose-dependent increases in cellular fluorescence (Fig. 4A, B). However, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of macrophages treated with FITC–PFC-OMN was ~ 2.6–4.2-fold higher than that of FITC–C-OMN across all concentrations (Fig. 4B). Notably, fluorescence signals from PFC-OMN were diffusely distributed throughout the cytoplasm, suggesting efficient endosomal escape.

At a concentration of 2×MIC (25 µM), intracellular labeling with FITC–PFC-OMN was evident after only 0.5 h of incubation, whereas FITC–C-OMN uptake remained negligible (Fig. 4C). Quantitative analysis showed that the MFI of FITC–PFC-OMN–treated macrophages was ~ 23.2-fold higher than that of FITC–C-OMN at this concentration (Fig. 4D), highlighting the rapid membrane penetration promoted by fluoroalkylation. Moreover, the intracellular MFI of both peptides increased in a time-dependent manner up to 2 h, but further incubation (4–12 h) revealed distinct kinetics: FITC–PFC-OMN uptake continued to rise, whereas FITC–C-OMN reached saturation at 4 h (Fig. 4E). These findings suggest a sustained and specific internalization pathway for PFC-OMN.

To elucidate the uptake mechanism, macrophages were pretreated with endocytosis inhibitors before exposure to FITC–PFC-OMN. As shown in Fig. 4F, internalization was markedly reduced in the presence of chlorpromazine (CPZ), an inhibitor of clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Consistently, the MFI of CPZ-pretreated macrophages decreased to ~ 2.3% of control levels (Fig. 4G). These results indicate that PFC-OMN uptake is energy-dependent and primarily mediated through the clathrin-dependent endocytosis pathway, a classical route enabling peptide entry into host cells [40].

PFC-OMN exhibited enhanced antibacterial activity against intracellular S. aureus

The antimicrobial activity of PFC-OMN and C-OMN against extracellular bacteria was first evaluated using S. aureus (ATCC 29213) as the model pathogen. PFC-OMN and C-OMN showed similar antimicrobial activity against extracellular S. aureus (both MIC = 12.5 µM) (Fig. 5A and Tabel 1). Interestingly, the OD600 of cultures treated with PFC-OMN was higher than that of those treated with C-OMN at a concentration of 6.25 µM. However, the OD600 of cultures treated with PFC-OMN combined with 10 mM GSH was comparable to that of cultures treated with C-OMN alone. This phenomenon may be attributed to the enhanced stability of PFC-OMN, which arises from its assembled nanostructures, potentially inhibiting the adequate activity of its antimicrobial fragment. The addition of GSH facilitates the disintegration of the nanosphere structure, thereby exposing the antimicrobial fragment and restoring its functionality.

Based on the results of extracellular antibacterial and cellular internalization assays, we examined the effectiveness of PCF-OMN in killing intracellular bacteria. Compared with C-OMN, PFC-OMN demonstrates significantly stronger antimicrobial activity against intracellular S. aureus, with IMBC99.9 values of 50 µM and > 800 µM, respectively (Table 1and Figure S2). This striking difference is directly attributable to the enhanced cytosolic delivery and subsequent release of active OMN. Meanwhile, it should be noted that both HC50 and IC50 values of PFC-OMN are 6–9 times higher than its IMBC99.9 against intracellular S. aureus (Table 1). At the IMBC99.9 of PFC-OMN against intracellular S. aureus, macrophages have a viability > 80% after treatment with C-OMN or PFC-OMN.

Then, RAW264.7 cells were infected with SYTO9-stained S. aureus for 1 to 4 h. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that S. aureus successfully invaded macrophages and accumulated over time (Fig. 5B). The proportion of S. aureus-infected cells reached 96.13% at 2 h, which was not significantly lower than that at 4 h (97.50%, p = 0.8530) (Fig. 5C). This suggests that the intracellular infection model was successfully established at 2 h. Consequently, we investigated RAW264.7 cells infected with SYTO9-stained S. aureus for 2 h using a confocal microscope. The results further confirmed that S. aureus invaded macrophages and distributed within the cytoplasm (Fig. 5E, F). RAW264.7 cells infected with SYTO9-stained S. aureus were treated with PFC-OMN for 24 h and then analyzed by confocal microscope and flow cytometry. C-OMN and PBS were used as control groups. The concentration of peptide was set at 25 µM (2×MIC). Fluorescence imaging showed that intracellular S. aureus was significantly suppressed in the presence of PFC-OMN, while slightly in C-OMN (Fig. 5G), compared to PBS. The MFI of cells treated with PFC-OMN was 36.6% of that of the control (p < 0.0001), while the MFI of cells treated with C-OMN was 86.0% of that of the control (p = 0.0031) (Fig. 5H). Flow cytometry analysis exhibited similar results (Fig. 5I-K). Finally, we performed colony counting following the lysis of infected RAW264.7 cells treated with C-OMN, PFC-OMN, and PBS for 12 to 48 h. Furthermore, while C-OMN initially suppressed bacterial growth at 12 h, its efficacy waned by 48 h, due to insufficient intracellular accumulation within the cells and peptide degradation after increasing the co-incubation time to 48 h. In contrast, PFC-OMN maintained potent bactericidal activity throughout the 48-hour period (Fig. 5L, M), underscoring the role of the nanoparticle formulation in enhancing peptide stability and enabling sustained release.

PFC-OMN has excellent biocompatibility and in vivo antibacterial efficacy

Encouraged by the in vitro results, we further investigated the in vivo biosafety and antibacterial efficacy and biosafety of PFC-OMN in mouse. In mice, intravenous administration of a therapeutic dose (25 µmol/kg) for two weeks did not cause weight loss or elicit significant toxicity, as assessed by blood biochemistry and histopathological examination of major organs (Fig. 6A–C), confirming the favorable safety profile of PFC-OMN.

The in vivo antibacterial efficacy of PFC-OMN was assessed in a peritonitis model induced by S. aureus, a representative intracellular infection categorized as a “serious threat” by the U.S. CDC (Fig. 6D). Mice infected intraperitoneally with S. aureus (5 × 10⁷ CFU/mouse) were treated with PBS, C-OMN, or PFC-OMN (2×MIC, i.p.).

Following 24 h of treatment, the total, extracellular, and intracellular S. aureus colony-forming units (CFUs) in the peritoneal fluid were measured (Fig. 6E, F). Both C-OMN and PFC-OMN demonstrated excellent efficacy in eliminating extracellular S. aureus. The CFUs of surviving extracellular bacteria in the PFC-OMN group (1.169 log10 CFU) were significantly lower than those in the C-OMN group (1.934 log10 CFU; **, p = 0.0055), which can be attributed to the higher stability of PFC-OMN in the peritoneal fluid, resulting from its self-assembled nanoparticles. PFC-OMN also showed significant intracellular bactericidal activity, whereas C-OMN had only a minor inhibitory effect on intracellular S. aureus, likely due to its limited intracellular concentration and poor stability. This in vivo result further confirms that PFC-OMN exhibits greater potency in eradicating both intracellular and extracellular bacteria compared with unmodified peptides, which may be attributed to its self-assembly properties and enhanced cellular internalization.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that fluoroalkylation of the antimicrobial peptide Omiganan (PFC-OMN) enables efficient cytosolic delivery and eradication of intracellular S. aureus. The parent AMP, omiganan (OMN), was selected due to its potent broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, especially against Staphylococcus aureus, and its established clinical safety profile in topical applications [42, 43]. Its cationic amphipathic structure, featuring a modifiable N-terminal cysteine, allowed site-specific conjugation without disrupting the intrinsic antimicrobial pharmacophore. Incorporation of a redox-sensitive disulfide bond was designed to exploit elevated intracellular glutathione (GSH) levels, enabling selective release of free OMN within infected host cells.

This modification addresses a major limitation of CPPs and other peptide delivery systems, which often fail to achieve effective intracellular concentrations. Compared with earlier approaches, PFC-OMN demonstrated significantly enhanced uptake in macrophages relative to unmodified peptides (C-OMN), as confirmed by flow cytometry and confocal microscopy, showing up to a 4.2-fold increase in intracellular fluorescence intensity. These results are consistent with previous reports indicating that fluoroalkylation promotes endocytosis and facilitates endosomal escape, attributed to the dual hydrophobic and lipophobic character of the PFC tag, which destabilizes endosomal membranes without direct fusion [33], thereby improving intracellular delivery. Furthermore, the in vitro antibacterial activity of PFC-OMN against intracellular S. aureus was markedly superior to that of C-OMN, with more than a 16-fold reduction in the minimal bactericidal concentration (IMBC99.9). This enhanced intracellular activity is likely attributable to the improved stability and sustained release of the peptide from nanostructures formed by fluorous modification. Notably, the antibacterial activity of PFC-OMN was restored upon the addition of GSH, which disrupted the nanostructures, further confirming the role of fluorous modification in peptide stability and controlled release. Finally, vivo evaluation demonstrated both favorable biocompatibility and robust antibacterial efficacy, with PFC-OMN showing minimal toxicity in mice and significant bactericidal activity in a peritonitis model. Collectively, these findings highlight PFC-tagged AMPs as a promising therapeutic strategy for treating intracellular bacterial infections. Importantly, the major advantage of this platform is not merely its ability to achieve cytosolic delivery, but its capacity to deliver cargos with mechanisms inherently less prone to resistance development. By overcoming the delivery barrier of AMPs, this approach enables their full potential as durable and efficacious therapeutics against intracellular pathogens.

Despite the promising findings, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration in future research. First, the fluorous modification increases production costs, which may hinder large-scale clinical translation. Future efforts should focus on optimizing the synthesis process to reduce costs while maintaining the efficacy of PFC-tagged peptides. Second, although in vitro assays demonstrated minimal toxicity toward RAW264.7 cells and human red blood cells (HRBCs), and short-term biosafety studies in mice revealed no significant toxicity, the long-term safety profile and potential immunogenicity in vivo remain unclear. Comprehensive evaluations—encompassing chronic toxicity and immunogenicity—are essential to establish long-term biocompatibility. Third, although PFC-OMN demonstrated potent antibacterial activity against S. aureus, its effectiveness against other bacterial species, particularly Gram-negative pathogens, was not examined. Given the increasing threat of multidrug-resistant organisms, future investigations should systematically evaluate the antibacterial spectrum of PFC-tagged AMPs across diverse clinically relevant pathogens, especially those resistant to conventional antibiotics. Furthermore, while the antibacterial efficacy of PFC-OMN was primarily validated using a standard antibiotic-sensitive S. aureus strain (ATCC 29213), assessing its performance against drug-resistant strains such as methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) will be crucial to determine its potential in combating antimicrobial resistance. Finally, the in vivo antibacterial evaluation in this study focused primarily on bacterial load, rather than ultimate clinical outcomes. Survival analysis in chronic infection models will therefore be an important next step to further validate the therapeutic potential of our platform. Collectively, addressing these limitations through structural optimization, comprehensive biosafety evaluation, expanded antibacterial testing, and combinatorial therapeutic strategies will be key to fully realizing the translational and clinical potential of this delivery platform.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a fluorination strategy that significantly enhances the cytosolic delivery and efficacy of the antimicrobial peptide Omiganan against intracellular S. aureus. The PFC tag enables self-assembly into nanoparticles, promotes efficient cellular uptake via endocytosis, facilitates endosomal escape, and finally allows glutathione-responsive release of the active peptide in the cytoplasm. This approach effectively addresses key limitations of conventional AMPs, including poor membrane permeability, low intracellular bioavailability, and rapid degradation.

While this study focused on S. aureus, the same strategy could be applied to combat other intracellular pathogens, including Gram-negative bacteria and mycobacteria. Future work will focus on optimizing the fluorous tag to reduce synthetic complexity, evaluating the platform against a broader spectrum of resistant pathogens, and exploring the long-term pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity of these novel biomaterials. The versatility of this fluorination approach also suggests its potential applicability for delivering other therapeutic peptides, thereby offering a general strategy for the treatment of intracellular infections and beyond.
