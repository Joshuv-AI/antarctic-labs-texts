Forming, selecting and updating cognitive maps to support flexible behaviour: relevance for anxiety
Authors: Yuheng Shi, Mona M. Garvert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In dynamic environments, relying solely on simple stimulus–response associations is often insufficient because identical experiences rarely recur. Instead, we need internal models that allow us to flexibly generalize past knowledge and plan for future events. The hippocampus plays a crucial role in this process by constructing cognitive maps that guide adaptive, goal-directed behaviour. However, in complex and ever-changing real-world situations, no single map can capture all the relevant information. The brain must therefore maintain multiple cognitive maps, each tied to its own latent context. Moreover, since uncertainty about the hidden context underlying the observations often exists, the brain must continually decide which map to rely on for guiding behaviour and to what degree it should be updated or renewed. Here we review the role the hippocampus and orbitofrontal cortex play in providing cognitive maps and signalling inferred contexts and how prediction errors and neuromodulators drive their continual arbitration. Disruptions in these processes, such as altered map formation, distorted map content and disrupted map arbitration, may drive overgeneralization of fear and resulting avoidance behaviours common in anxiety.This article is part of the theme issue ‘The role of hippocampal predictions in cognition: bridging perception and memory’.

Introduction

Cognitive maps are internal representations that encode the relational structure among elements in an environment [1,2]. Originally introduced by Tolman [3] in the context of spatial navigation, the concept has since evolved to encompass a broader class of structured representations. In the hippocampus, for example, cognitive maps have been demonstrated in rodents navigating space [4], but similar principles extend to non-spatial domains such as sound frequencies [5], odours [6], temporal structure of experience [7,8], conceptual feature space [9–11], semantic [12] and social relationships [13,14]. In contrast to other representations such as cached values [15], a cognitive map provides a flexible representational format which can be combined with different reward functions to enable novel inferences based on learned knowledge [16]. Cognitive maps need not be built only through explicit reinforcement: latent learning demonstrates that relational knowledge can be acquired incidentally, without immediate reward and later recruited when the context demands it [3].

These kinds of representations are useful because they support flexible behaviour in several key ways (figure 1A). First, by allowing the propagation of value and other features across structurally related states, they vastly reduce the learning demands in complex, high-dimensional environments [1,19,20]. Second, by encoding state-to-state transitions, they can be used to simulate potential future trajectories, facilitating flexible planning beyond simple stimulus–response learning [1,21]. The generalization of value across related states and the forward-looking evaluation of possible action sequences in planning are thus complementary functions of cognitive maps underlying flexible behaviour.

Importantly, for these functions to work in a way that is appropriate for a given task, it is necessary that the brain builds cognitive maps that accurately reflect the relational structure of the world, and uses the appropriate one for guiding behaviour. This can be challenging in noisy and uncertain situations where the mapping between states and reward is ambiguous and constantly changing.

Here, we review how the hippocampus forms multiple cognitive maps that can be used for guiding flexible behaviour. We first describe how cognitive maps support value generalization and planning. We then discuss that multiple types of cognitive maps are represented in the hippocampus in parallel, effectively making it a library that houses a repertoire of maps. Under uncertainty, flexible and adaptive behaviour depends on accurate latent-state inference in the OFC, which is hypothesized to select the hippocampal map on which generalization and planning operate. Importantly, the context-sensitive selection of a cognitive map is not a one-shot operation. Depending on prediction errors and uncertainty, the brain continuously decides whether to update the current map, switch to another or construct a new map. We then show how disruptions at any stage of this process can lead to maladaptive cognition and behaviour. Specifically, using anxiety as a case study, we illustrate how its characteristic cognitive and behavioural symptoms might arise from failures in cognitive map formation or arbitration. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms may advance representational and mechanistic accounts of behaviour and guide the development of more precisely targeted clinical interventions.

Cognitive maps enable value generalization

Generalization allows the value of a novel state to be inferred based on its similarity to previously experienced states. This kind of generalization is useful in our complex and high-dimensional world where few of our experiences repeat in exactly the same shape or form [22], because it significantly reduces the learning problem we face [23]. It operates over a learned relational structure such as a cognitive map of stimulus relationships, yet does so without necessarily invoking a specific forward-looking trajectory. Instead, the relational structure acts as a kernel providing a similarity rule that specifies how strongly each pair of states is related, and therefore how much reward learned at one state should influence reward estimates at other states [23–27]. For example, a diffusion kernel spreads value more strongly between states that are closely connected in the map, with a diffusion parameter controlling how broadly this influence propagates [23]. This approach is particularly useful in tasks where feedback is sparse, and decisions are made in a non-sequential or one-shot manner.

Indeed, recent evidence suggests that humans can leverage the similarity structure defined by a cognitive map to generalize and search for correlated rewards in structured environments [25]. In both spatial and conceptual multi-armed bandit tasks, participants’ choices reflected sensitivity to the spatial or conceptual distances embedded in the underlying relational map.

Cognitive maps enable planning

Planning is a hallmark of adaptive behaviour, enabling individuals to anticipate future outcomes and select actions accordingly. In its idealized form, planning involves model-based tree search, where an agent iteratively performs one-step lookahead, simulating the states they are going to end up in based on the actions they take, to identify policies that maximize expected value [15]. While this approach affords full flexibility, it is computationally demanding and impractical for real-world environments of high complexity [28].

One approach that simplifies the planning problem is the successor representation (SR) [29]. The SR can be understood as a predictive map that captures how states relate to each other under the agent’s typical behaviour (its policy). For each state, it encodes the expected discounted occupancy of all other states, i.e. how often the agent is likely to visit them in the future. Formally, the SR decomposes the long-run value function V into the product of a matrix M (the expected future state occupancies) and a reward vector r, which specifies the reward associated with each state. The value function that provides the basis for decision-making is then given by V = Mr. Therefore, SR simplifies planning significantly by caching multi-step transition structure into a compressed predictive map so that values can be read out without the need for step-by-step model-based tree search. This also enables rapid adaptation when reward contingencies change, because only the payoff vector r needs to be updated instead of the successor matrix M that specifies the predictive map [19,30]. Consistently, humans show greater sensitivity to reward revaluation than to transition revaluation [21,30,31].

Meanwhile, some computational and empirical studies have linked the SR to hippocampal and entorhinal coding of predictive relationships [7,19], suggesting that place and grid cell representations may reflect such a predictive map of future state occupancies. While successor representations encode predictive relationships under the agent’s experienced policy, this policy dependence limits their ability to generate novel shortcuts that were never directly experienced. A key mechanism that overcomes this limitation is hippocampal replay, the rapid reactivation of place cell sequences during rest or sleep that recapitulate past trajectories or simulates potential future ones [32–34]. Through replay, place cell activations can link otherwise disjoint experiences into a coherent relational structure [34,35], effectively stitching together separate episodes into a unified map [35–37] (figure 1A).

Multiple maps coexist

Regardless of whether cognitive maps support prospection, generalization or other functions, their utility depends on selecting an appropriate map for the relevant context. Specifically, generalization requires maps that capture the relevant structural similarities that enable the transfer of values across related states. Planning, on the other hand, benefits from representations of state-to-state relationships to optimize goal-directed behaviour. If the wrong cognitive map is used, the resulting plans may fail, or generalizations may be maladaptive.

This makes it unlikely that a single, unified map suffices for guiding adaptive behaviour. Indeed, the brain maintains multiple representations that differ in temporal horizon or spatial scale [38–41] (figure 2A), representational content [12,43,44] (figure 2B,C) and abstraction level [42] (figure 2D), and can flexibly recruit the relevant metric depending on the behavioural task [24].

One prominent dimension of differentiation is the spatial and temporal horizon. For example, using virtual navigation tasks, Brunec and Momennejad demonstrated a posterior-to-anterior gradient in the hippocampus, where the posterior hippocampus encoded near-future navigational states, while anterior hippocampus and mPFC represented longer predictive horizons and more abstract, goal-directed planning trajectories [39] (figure 2A). Near-future prediction during navigation requires high spatial and temporal resolution, enabling discrimination between nearby locations and states. In contrast, far-future prediction relies on representations that integrate information over broader spatial and temporal spans. Consistent with this distinction, anterior regions exhibit greater voxel-wise similarity and higher temporal autocorrelation than posterior regions in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI [38]).These findings align with rodent data showing larger, overlapping place fields in ventral hippocampus (corresponding to anterior in humans) and smaller, more distinct fields in dorsal hippocampus (corresponding to posterior in humans [40,41]).

Beyond differences in predictive horizons, distinct dimensions of relational content can coexist in parallel. Within the hippocampal–entorhinal system, fMRI data revealed representations of both spatial relationships between items and policy-dependent predictive relationships, reflecting how often states are expected to follow one another under an individual’s typical behaviour during navigation [24] (figure 2B). These predictive relationships define a relational structure over which value can generalize, analogous to diffusion-based generalization on a transition graph. Furthermore, after performing a graph learning task, the hippocampal formation represented the newly learnt transition structure between objects [7] (figure 2C) as well as their semantic relationships in anatomically separable subregions [12].

While some maps are housed in anatomically separable parts of the hippocampal formation, the orthogonalization of activity patterns in the dentate gyrus also enables the storage of multiple decorrelated maps within the same circuitry [45–47]. This pattern separation allows overlapping experiences to be encoded as distinct representations and prevents interference when contexts are similar. CA3 attractor dynamics can then enable map-specific pattern completion from partial or contextual cues [48–51]. CA1 may integrate sensory input from entorhinal cortex with retrieved structure from CA3 to generate task-relevant predictions and track the mismatch between expected and observed states and outcomes [52–54], which can then drive map updating or switching.

In contrast to studies demonstrating parallel mapping of distinct dimensions, other work highlights how multiple stimulus features can also be integrated into a single representational geometry: grid-like codes in entorhinal cortex capture two-dimensional conceptual spaces defined by perceptual features such as visual [9], auditory [5] and olfactory dimensions [6], but also by more abstract features, including values [55] or different dimensions of social hierarchies [14].

Importantly, map-like codes are not restricted to the hippocampal formation. After consolidation, the mPFC represents graph structures as stimulus-independent relational codes in parallel to a cognitive map encoding relationships between specific stimuli, potentially providing the neural basis for structural generalization to novel experiences [42] (figure 2D) Consistent with this broader view, Wittmann et al. showed that dmPFC/ACC encodes multiple abstract dimensions of different social relationships (basis functions) in parallel during decision-making and dynamically adjusts their weights according to task demands [56]. Together, these findings suggest that the brain maintains multiple maps reflecting different stimulus dimensions across different circuits.

Hidden state inference and context-sensitive map selection

When multiple cognitive maps are maintained in parallel, a fundamental challenge is determining which map should guide behaviour at a given moment (figure 1B). This is especially difficult in situations where sensory inputs are ambiguous and insufficient to infer the underlying cause. Selecting the wrong map risks maladaptive planning, misattributing outcomes to irrelevant states or maps and inappropriately generalizing values across unrelated states.

Formally, classical models of latent cause (or hidden state) inference frame learning as the problem of inferring which hidden contexts give rise to observed cues and outcomes [57,58]. On each new trial, incoming evidence is used to update the posterior distribution over the belief of the current context [57,59]. Importantly, this update not only operates on our belief about previously known contexts but also allows for the inference of novel ones when current contexts fail to explain the data [60–62] figure 1B,C).

In practice, these models typically deal with simple associative learning settings where a tone or light (cue) is followed by an outcome like reward or omission [57,58]. The goal is to infer whether a shift in cue-outcome contingencies reflects the emergence of a new context. For example, if a previously rewarded cue suddenly fails to yield reward, the animal might infer that a new latent cause is now in effect, rather than simply updating the strength of the association [58,63–65].

Classical formulations have operationalized latent causes as cluster labels [57,58] that indicate which context is active, but say little about what is being reinstated when a particular context is inferred. We suggest that each inferred context indexes a cognitive map, most likely represented in the hippocampus, which specifies the relational structure among states that is currently relevant (figure 1B). Notably, arbitrating between cognitive maps can occur both within and across different types of maps. For example, the brain might switch between different predictive maps depending on the inferred latent context. Alternatively, it might instate other types of maps such as spatial maps if they are considered more task-relevant.

When a contextual change occurs, it can trigger remapping of hippocampal place cells. The remapping may take the form of global remapping, where no place fields are shared across contexts; partial remapping, where some overlap exists; or rate remapping, in which the spatial tuning remains but the firing rates differ [45,59]. Such contextual changes are typically induced by alterations in sensory cues like visual landmarks, lighting conditions, auditory stimuli, odours or the geometry of the environment [66–69]. However, internal states (e.g. fear or hunger) of the animal and the task to be performed can also induce remapping [70–72].

Especially when the context is ambiguous or only partly observable, the relevant map needs to be inferred. Converging evidence points to the OFC as a key structure in this process [17,18,73] (figure 1B). For example, Schuck et al. [17] showed that inferred task states within a learnt transition structure could be decoded from medial OFC (mOFC) in the absence of sensory evidence [17], consistent with the idea that OFC represents a cognitive map of task space [18]. We hypothesize that the hippocampus stores multiple potential relational structures, while the OFC infers the latent context and biases which map to express. The selected hippocampal map then supports generalization and planning (figure 1A,B).

Supporting this view, Garvert et al. [24] found that the OFC carried a moment-by-moment signal reflecting which one of multiple candidate maps best explained observed outcomes, which was associated with a strengthening of the corresponding hippocampal map representation and change in behaviour [24]. This suggests that OFC might evaluate the reliability of each potential hippocampal map through the reward feedback it receives from the environment and gate hippocampal map representations accordingly.

One thing to note is that Garvert et al. [24] did not experimentally manipulate the relevance of different maps to behaviour. As such, the study highlights how OFC performs internal model comparison by tracking the utility of candidate hippocampal maps, but does not capture the process of latent context inference. Complementary evidence comes from Riceberg et al. [74]: in a plus-maze task, rats first learned to navigate towards one rewarded goal location (e.g. ‘go west’) [74]. Without any external cue, the rewarded goal later reversed (e.g. ‘go east’), requiring inference of a latent context change. During this transition, hippocampal (CA1) population activity predicted trial-by-trial changes in OFC representations and led OFC by approximately 30 ms in theta phase. This effect was not observed in the reverse direction, and it was only found during the first reversal. After the new contingency became familiar, CA1 ensembles stably represented the individual spatial paths, while OFC ensembles instead reflected a more generalized coding of the goal arms across different paths leading to the same reward. This asymmetric influence suggests that the hippocampus provides important signals that enable the OFC to develop representations of the underlying task state.

From map selection to arbitration under uncertainty

Under uncertainty, adaptive behaviour requires deciding whether and how to update internal models of the environment. One possibility is that learners revise an existing map to incorporate new information. Alternatively, behaviour might reflect winner-take-all-switching, whereby the map that best explains observed outcomes dominates behaviour and new maps are created when observations are not consistent with predictions. Lastly, it is also conceivable that at any moment the system maintains a posterior over multiple candidate maps and weights their value predictions according to how well they predict outcomes [60,75].

Hard switching and graded arbitration are best viewed as two ends of a continuum, with the brain flexibly shifting between them depending on the strength and clarity of contextual evidence. We propose that uncertainty and prediction errors drive this arbitration, steering the system from incremental map updating to categorical switching, and in some cases toward constructing a new map. In the following sections, we describe how prediction error magnitude and volatility determine whether the system updates an existing map, switches between alternatives or instantiates a new one.

Updating the current map

When prediction errors, i.e. the discrepancy between expected and experienced outcomes, are modest, the system maintains the current map and incrementally adjusts it (figure 1C). New evidence is interpreted as local noise or drift, prompting calibration of expected rewards or minor edits to the transition structure. The degree of updating is modulated by volatility estimates tracked by the anterior cingulate cortex [76]. In more stable environments, prediction errors are downweighed; in volatile ones, recent outcomes are weighted more heavily [76].

Notably, updating a cognitive map requires information not only about whether to update, but also what to update. Separable types of prediction errors provide this specificity. Reward prediction errors (RPEs) engaging the ventral striatum [77] signal a mismatch between the expected and received reward. They drive updates to the values attached to states or state–action pairs while leaving the map’s structure unchanged. In contrast, state prediction errors (SPEs) represented in the posterior parietal and lateral prefrontal cortex [77] signal a mismatch between the predicted next state and the state that is actually encountered. These errors target the transition structure itself and incrementally reshape the topology of the cognitive map. Chan et al. demonstrated that OFC activity carried a cue-specific learning signal for transitions [78]. Trial-by-trial OFC activity at the time of outcome predicted more optimal predictions on the next trial [78]. Interpreted within a cognitive map framework, these findings suggest that the OFC supports fine-grained updates to the transition structure.

Crucially, prediction errors also determine whether new information is integrated into an existing memory or lead to the creation of a new one (figure 1C). Consistent with this idea, gradual changes in contingencies are more likely to cause updates within an existing memory as opposed to creating a new one. For example, compared with a traditional fear extinction procedure, gradually reducing the shock voltage paired with a conditioned stimulus elicited more sustained suppression of the fear response including less spontaneous recovery and less reinstatement of fear when shock was introduced again [79,80] (figure 3), suggesting that the existing memory was overwritten. In contrast, abrupt changes through the sudden omission of an expected shock were more likely to produce reinstatement of the fear memory, suggesting the formation of a new contextual memory [57,58,81,82]. By analogy, we propose that in map-based representations, small errors drive incremental updating within a current map, while large errors prompt switching to, or creation of, a new map.

Switching between maps

Large prediction errors can carry different computational meanings depending on the context. In stable environments, they may be attributed to noise, but in volatile or structured settings, large errors can signal a possible shift in the underlying latent state rather than variability within it, thereby increasing unexpected uncertainty [83–85].

Switching between maps in response to prediction error and uncertainty is well illustrated by hidden change-point tasks [73,84,86] in which participants observe Gaussian-distributed outcomes whose mean occasionally shifts. This creates a dynamic context in which the current state is stable for a time, followed by abrupt transitions to a new latent state. Importantly, there is inherent uncertainty in this task as the prediction error could arise from noise (variances) or a genuine change of the state (mean). Learners solve this by dynamically adjusting their learning rates in response to surprise [84,86]. Specifically, when a large prediction error signals a possible change point, participants elevate their learning rates, allowing new observations to rapidly update beliefs. Over subsequent trials, as uncertainty resolves, the learning rate gradually decreases.

Recent work has linked unsigned prediction errors (reflecting the magnitude rather than the direction of surprise) with the locus coeruleus (LC), which concurrently releases dopamine and norepinephrine (NE) to shape hippocampal memory [87,88]. According to the network reset theory of the LC/NE function [89], phasic NE activity facilitates cognitive flexibility by enabling the brain to reset and reconfigure active representations in response to unexpected environmental change. When the probability that a change point has occurred is high, NE may provide a switch signal that prompts the system to move from the retrieval state to a state that is ready for encoding the new information [90,91]. In our framework, it could mean that the contextual probability of each potential hippocampal map is flattened, allowing elevated learning from new observations after the change point. It then facilitates the selection or construction of an appropriate map for the new state, whose ongoing representation is supported by activity of the OFC [73] (figure 4A).

This process of switching, however, is not always instantaneous. Following abrupt shifts in the environment, the brain may enter an unstable phase in which multiple maps compete for expression [92,93]. For instance, Jezek et al. showed that hippocampal ensembles flickered between distinct context-associated spatial maps across theta cycles during quick transitions between two learnt contexts (defined by different light cues) and settled into one representation after a few seconds [92]. Interestingly, despite the fast flickering of candidate maps, the location of the animal could still be robustly decoded [93] (figure 4B), suggesting that the hippocampus might represent the spatial and contextual information independently and accurately.

Creating a new map

Drawing on latent cause framework, we hypothesize that the brain instantiates a new map when prediction errors are large and hard to reconcile with any existing map (figure 1B,C). This results in the system shifting the posterior weight to a previously uninstantiated latent cause [60,81] and kickstart the construction of a fresh map. Empirical support comes from fear extinction and renewal paradigms. For instance, according to latent state models, the conditioned stimulus–unconditioned stimulus association survives extinction because the unexpected removal of the unconditioned stimulus induces a ‘state split,’ prompting storage of extinction learning in a newly inferred context [94]. The common phenomenon of hippocampal remapping can also be viewed as the result of inferring a new hidden cause [59]. The benefit of instantiating a new latent cause is that the memories formed under the old cause can be protected against interference from new memories and be readily retrieved under appropriate context, thus facilitating continual learning and minimizing catastrophic interference [95–97].

The process of constructing the new map likely involves orthogonalization of hippocampal codes, such that the new representation becomes uncorrelated with the old one. In a multi-day virtual reality paradigm [98], head-fixed mice learned a two-alternative cue–delay–choice task, navigating linear tracks where early visual cues predicted whether reward would appear near or far along the path. Solving the task required associating each cue with a reward location and maintaining that memory across a brief delay. As learning progressed, hippocampal population activity gradually orthogonalized across latent task states, yielding a cognitive map with increasingly distinct and stable representations [98] (figure 5). These neural changes also mirrored improvements in behaviour and memory performance.

While the hippocampus may lead map creation when environmental structure is ambiguous or must be discovered from scratch, the OFC appears critical for formatting learnt associations into precise task-state representations. In a Pavlovian devaluation paradigm, Costa et al. [99] showed that silencing lateral OFC (lOFC) during learning impaired later outcome-specific inference, despite intact general devaluation and associative learning. Computational modelling attributed this deficit to poor state discrimination, suggesting that lOFC supports the formation of finely partitioned associative ‘slots’ needed for flexible inference [99].

Complementing this, Zong et al. found that OFC can even instantiate a generalized map when hippocampal output is transiently blocked [100]. In a rodent schema-transfer task, temporary inactivation of ventral subiculum during learning of a new but structurally familiar problem accelerated the emergence of schema-like coding in OFC neurons [100]. These results suggest that when the abstract structure is already known, OFC can compose a new map by recombining existing relational scaffolds with novel sensory inputs without requiring detailed hippocampal pattern separation.

Taken together, prediction errors and volatility estimates determine whether experience incrementally updates an active map, induces competition among alternatives or triggers the construction of a new representation. These operations are likely implemented through coordinated interactions between hippocampal maps, state inference in the OFC and neuromodulatory systems that regulate learning and representational stability.

Implications for dysfunction

Disruptions in the formation, use, update and selection of cognitive maps can undermine adaptive behaviour and may contribute to psychopathology. The ability to generalize and infer outcomes is especially important in aversive contexts, where direct contact with a harmful stimulus carries risk [101]. In such settings, cognitive maps allow individuals to anticipate and minimize exposure to danger before it becomes imminent [101,102]. However, if map-based planning and generalization go awry, the preparation towards potential threat can turn into anxiety and unnecessary avoidance of safe situations.

Classical cognitive-behavioural theories mainly describe anxiety as arising from excessive threat expectancies maintained through avoidance and reduced by exposure [103,104]. Recent work additionally emphasizes altered responses to uncertainty, risk and ambiguity [105–108] and the overgeneralization of aversive outcomes [109–113] as well as changes in planning and latent-state inference [114–118].

We propose that a cognitive map perspective provides a unifying representational account of these symptoms, linking overgeneralization, distorted anticipation of future trajectories and context-inappropriate persistence of fear to alterations in how hippocampal maps are formed, structured and selected via interactions with the OFC.

Specifically, the known structural and physiological effects of stress and anxiety on these brain regions [119–123] may affect map-based behaviour in separable ways: (i) by impacting the formation of distinct maps, (ii) by altering the content and geometry of maps, and (iii) by dysfunctional updating and selection of maps under uncertainty. We organize existing findings along these three dimensions and discuss how each could give rise to characteristic cognitive and behavioural symptoms observed in anxiety as follows.

(1)Altered map formation: reduced repertoire of distinct cognitive maps. Stress- and anxiety-induced reductions in dentate-gyrus neurogenesis may limit the formation of new decorrelated representations in the hippocampus [124–126]. Behaviourally, this predicts reduced context specificity, increased interference between learning episodes and unstable generalization across environments that share overlapping features.

Behaviourally, this predicts reduced context specificity, increased interference between learning episodes and unstable generalization across environments that share overlapping features.

(2)Distorted map content: biased geometry and threat-centric representations. Heightened risk sensitivity, intolerance of uncertainty and overestimation of the likelihood and magnitude of rare, but highly aversive events [105–108,127] might give rise to internally generated maps that are disproportionately skewed toward adverse outcomes, such that threat-related states are represented as subjectively closer, more probable or more strongly connected than they are objectively. Beyond altered encoding, this might be driven by biased offline hippocampal replay towards aversive trajectories, leading to disproportionate strengthening of threat-related paths within the map [128,129]. This not only results in excessive and uncontrollable worry [130] but also encourages avoidance behaviour [131]. Furthermore, even when the relational structure is intact, fear may spread excessively across the map due to overly broad generalization gradients [110,112].

(3)Altered map arbitration: misattribution of context and maladaptive map selection. Predicting danger adequately depends on extracting and using the relevant stimulus dimensions for prediction and generalization—that is organizing experience along the features that truly predict danger rather than surface-level or irrelevant ones. Anxiety disorders are often characterized by overgeneralization along irrelevant or overly broad dimensions [111,112]. Physiologically, this might result from disrupted LC–NE signalling [132,133] or impaired prefrontal–hippocampal interactions [134], leading to inappropriate weighting of the evidence when selecting or switching between maps. This could then lead to incorrect inference of the current context, the selection of the wrong map, persistent reliance on threat-associated maps, premature switching or inappropriate inference of new contexts [135]. While being adaptive in certain situations, this partitioning of experience can impair the update of the original aversive experience with safety outcomes [57,136]. Consistent with this account, computational models of fear learning showed that individuals who are more likely to form new extinction memories tend to exhibit greater spontaneous recovery compared to those who appear to update a single memory trace [82]. By contrast, recent latent-cause modelling work in clinically characterized PTSD samples has suggested a reduced tendency to infer additional causes during extinction, consistent with over-generalized threat representations [117,118,137]. A full reconciliation of these different modelling results is beyond the scope of this review, but they illustrate that anxiety-related alterations in map arbitration may take multiple forms.

Together, these observations suggest that therapeutic interventions should not only target the direct fear association, but also the biased processes that govern how fear generalizes and influences planning. Indeed, many cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) techniques do exactly this. For example, exposure therapy can be understood as encouraging exploration in areas that are typically avoided, which provides the disconfirmatory evidence needed to rectify maladaptive maps. Exposure strategies that produce gradual prediction errors may be particularly effective, as they encourage updating of the original aversive map with safety information [79]. Abrupt changes in contingencies, on the other hand, might be more likely to induce new latent cause inference [73,116], resulting in the formation of a new map. This increases the risk of spontaneous reinstatement of the existing map associated with a threat memory [57,82].

Similarly, cognitive reappraisal and other interventions that diminish the tendency to overweight threat can be understood as a strategy for reshaping the evaluation of trajectories within a predictive map. Rather than letting offline simulation be dominated by worst-case outcomes, which can distort cognitive maps through repeated simulations of negative trajectories, reappraisal makes neutral or positive trajectories more salient. This changes how the current state is represented and updates related states, so the map more accurately reflects environmental contingencies.

Together, this suggests that existing therapeutic approaches can be framed as methods that restore the adaptive use, selection and updating of cognitive maps. Importantly, these mechanisms are dissociable at the behavioural and neural level. For instance, our framework predicts that two patients with comparable avoidance may differ in treatment response depending on whether avoidance reflects distorted map geometry versus impaired map arbitration, predicting differential sensitivity to gradual versus abrupt exposure. A better understanding of the precise cognitive-computational processes involved in the maintenance of anxiety disorder in different individuals might enable even more targeted interventions [137–139].

Notably, alterations in cognitive maps can be both a cause and a consequence of anxiety. On the one hand, changes in hippocampal, prefrontal and neuromodulatory function can bias map construction, latent-state inference and uncertainty-weighted arbitration mechanisms, biasing planning and generalization towards threat and thereby increasing vulnerability to anxiety. On the other hand, anxiety-related cognitive and behavioural patterns—particularly avoidance, threat-focused attention and biased offline simulation [128,129]—could themselves lead to distortions in cognitive maps. This creates a self-reinforcing loop where a skewed map leads to anxiety-related behaviour, and that behaviour further reduces the opportunity to update the skewed map based on experience, contributing to the maintenance of the anxiety disorders [140,141].

Conclusion

Cognitive maps provide structured representations of the world that enable two core functions underlying flexible behaviour: generalization beyond immediate experience and planning. These functions critically depend on selecting the right map for the current context. Selecting the appropriate map requires latent context inference, a dynamic process that balances updating, switching between and creating new maps guided by uncertainty signals and prediction errors. The interplay between the hippocampus and the OFC plays a central role in this process, with the OFC evaluating which candidate map explains current outcomes and gating hippocampal maps accordingly.

Disruptions in these processes likely have profound implications for psychiatric disorders like anxiety. For example, heightened offline stimulation of unlikely aversive outcomes, maladaptive generalization and biased planning based on a distorted map and altered hidden state inference can lead to core symptoms of anxiety disorders like avoidance and worry. Reframing the origin and maintenance of these symptoms in terms of the neurocomputational mechanisms underlying the adaptive use, updating and selection of cognitive maps might offer new directions for the development of targeted therapy.
