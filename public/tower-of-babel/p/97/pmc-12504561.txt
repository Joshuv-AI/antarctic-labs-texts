Using social reinforcement in online Language learning to foster motivation through self-determination theory
Authors: Qingxia Zhang, Goodarz Shakibaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the effects of social reinforcement on Iranian EFL learners’ motivation (i.e., autonomy, competence, and relatedness) within online language learning environments. Adopting an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, the research involved 100 intermediate-level Iranian EFL learners aged 24–39. Participants were randomly assigned to either an experimental group, which received targeted social reinforcement during online activities, or a control group, which engaged in the same activities without specific reinforcement. Quantitative data, gathered via pre- and post-intervention administrations of a validated motivation scale, were analyzed using independent samples t-tests. These analyses revealed statistically significant improvements in scores for autonomy, competence, and relatedness among learners in the experimental group compared to their counterparts in the control group. Complementary qualitative findings, derived from content analysis of semi-structured interviews, further indicated that participants who received social reinforcement perceived it as positively influencing their autonomy, competence, and relatedness. These convergent results underscore the considerable potential of strategically implemented social reinforcement to foster enhanced learner motivation in online EFL contexts, offering valuable insights for pedagogical practice and the design of more supportive virtual learning spaces.Keywords: Social reinforcement, Online language learning, Self-determination theory, Motivation, Autonomy, Competence, RelatednessSubject terms: Physiology, Psychology

Introduction

The global proliferation of digital technologies has significantly reshaped educational landscapes, and the field of English as a Foreign Language (EFL) in Iran is no exception. In recent years, Iranian EFL contexts have witnessed a notable integration of new technologies, ranging from learning management systems and mobile applications to interactive whiteboards and online communication platforms1,2. This technological surge has offered unprecedented opportunities, altering how Iranian EFL learners engage with English by providing increased access to authentic materials, fostering interactive learning environments, and potentially promoting greater learner autonomy3–5. Consequently, these advancements have begun to influence traditional pedagogical approaches and have created new avenues for language acquisition, though the precise nature and extent of their impact on learner outcomes continue to be an area of active investigation.

Within these evolving online learning environments, learner motivation emerges as a critical factor for sustained engagement and successful language acquisition, particularly in the Iranian EFL context where maintaining learner impetus can be challenging6–8. Online settings, while offering flexibility, can sometimes lack the direct, face-to-face encouragement found in traditional classrooms; this situation places greater emphasis on learners’ intrinsic drive. Self-determination Theory (SDT), a prominent framework for understanding human motivation, posits that supporting learners’ basic psychological needs for autonomy (a sense of control and volition), competence (a feeling of effectiveness and mastery), and relatedness (a sense of belonging and connection) can enhance intrinsic motivation9,10. New technologies possess the potential to positively influence these needs; for instance, customizable learning paths can bolster autonomy, interactive exercises with immediate feedback can enhance competence, and online discussion forums can foster relatedness11–13. However, the mere presence of technology does not guarantee motivational enhancement, and specific pedagogical approaches within these digital spaces require closer examination.

One such approach, rooted in behavioral psychology, is social reinforcement (SR). It, primarily derived from14 principles of operant conditioning, suggests that behaviors followed by positive social consequences, such as praise, recognition, or positive feedback from peers or instructors, are more likely to be repeated and strengthened14–16. In an educational context, according to17, SR can take various forms, including verbal commendations, positive written comments, or even non-verbal cues of approval within synchronous online sessions. It is plausible that such reinforcement could significantly affect EFL learners’ motivation by positively impacting their feelings of competence when their efforts are acknowledged, their sense of relatedness when they receive supportive feedback from their learning community, and even their autonomy if reinforcement encourages self-directed learning behaviors. Despite the intuitive appeal and established efficacy of SR in traditional settings, its specific effects on the motivation, particularly the SDT constructs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness of Iranian EFL learners within online language learning environments have remained largely unexplored in the empirical literature.

Therefore, there is a compelling necessity to investigate the effects of SR strategies on motivation among Iranian EFL learners engaging in online language learning. Understanding this dynamic is theoretically significant as it allows for an examination of how external factors, such as SR, interact with and potentially nurture the intrinsic motivational components outlined by SDT in a technologically mediated, culturally specific context. Practically, the findings from such an inquiry hold considerable importance for EFL educators and instructional designers in Iran. Insights gained could inform the development of more effective online pedagogical practices and intervention strategies designed to bolster learner motivation, ultimately leading to improved engagement and language proficiency. This study, consequently, aims to address this lacuna by exploring the effects of SR in online language learning on Iranian EFL learners’ motivation, specifically concerning their perceptions of autonomy, competence, and relatedness, employing a mixed-methods approach to provide a comprehensive understanding of this phenomenon.

Review of literature

Social reinforcement in L2 learning

Social Reinforcement Theory (SRT) fundamentally explains how behaviors are learned and modified through social interactions and their resulting consequences, such as approval, praise, or attention from others18,19. Defined as an interpersonal exchange where actions are encouraged by positive social feedback, SRT distinguishes itself from material rewards through its inherent reliance on social dynamics20,21. Rooted in B.F. Skinner’s14 principles of operant conditioning, the theory was significantly expanded by Bandura’s15 Social Learning Theory (SLT), which highlighted the importance of observational learning, vicarious reinforcement, and cognitive processes, solidifying the “social” component of reinforcement paradigms. Accordingly, SRT has found broad application across diverse fields, including clinical psychology, education, and organizational behavior to foster desirable actions and enhance interpersonal engagement22. Within the context of L2 learning, SRT is particularly salient, emphasizing the strategic use of positive social feedback, such as verbal praise or affirmative gestures contingent upon learners’ communicative attempts23. This approach suggests that encouragement for language use, even imperfectly, increases persistence and skill refinement, while fostering a supportive, low-anxiety environment where learners feel secure experimenting with the language and benefiting from vicarious reinforcement24. The effectiveness of this reinforcement hinges on its perceived sincerity, immediacy, and appropriateness to the learner’s effort and developmental stage25.

The proliferation of online learning environments introduces new dimensions to SRT’s application, particularly for EFL learners. While offering flexibility and accessibility26,27, online platforms can risk isolation if social elements aren’t intentionally integrated. This underscores the critical role of SRT in designing online interactions that provide robust SR. Timely and personalized feedback on discussion posts, digital badges for achievements, and peer-to-peer encouragement in collaborative tasks can significantly bolster EFL learners’ motivation28. This is particularly crucial for supporting the fundamental psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness, as outlined in SDT29,30. SR in these digital spaces enhances perceived competence through acknowledgment of progress, fosters autonomy by encouraging active participation, and cultivates relatedness by building a sense of community and connection with instructors and peers, ultimately positively influencing engagement and persistence in online language acquisition31,32.

Motivation in the lens of Self-determination theory

Motivation serves as a fundamental element in comprehending human behavior, frequently regarded as the driving force that begins, guides, and maintains efforts aimed at reaching objectives23–33. In the realm of second language (L2) learning, SDT provides a thorough framework that transcends the conventional separation of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Deci and Ryan10 present a framework in which motivation is viewed as a continuum. At one end lies amotivation, where individuals experience a complete absence of drive, while at the other end is fully intrinsic motivation, marked by authentic interest and enjoyment. Between these extremes exist different types of extrinsic motivation. These include external regulation, which is influenced by rewards or punishments; introjected regulation, stemming from internal pressures like guilt; identified regulation, which aligns with personal values; and integrated regulation, which is fully assimilated into one’s identity. The extent of self-determination is contingent upon the satisfaction of three essential psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. When these needs are met, extrinsic motivations may be internalized, leading to more autonomous forms of engagement. In second language learning, motivation signifies the learner’s inclination to participate in and continue the process of acquiring a new language. The most effective motivation stems from autonomous regulation, wherein the learning experience is perceived as personally meaningful or intrinsically enjoyable. In contrast, controlled motivation arises from external pressures or rewards12–34. SDT highlights the importance of fostering autonomous motivation, which is based on the learner’s intrinsic curiosity and internalized objectives, to encourage ongoing commitment and resilience. This method demonstrates significant value in online learning contexts, where maintaining persistence can frequently be difficult35.

SDT provides a robust framework for examining motivation within the context of online language learning. This theory emphasizes the significance of fulfilling three fundamental psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Autonomy enables learners to feel a sense of choice and control over their studies, such as when they choose tasks or set their own learning pace36,37. Competence emerges from a sense of accomplishment and confidence as learners tackle language challenges38,39. Relatedness emphasizes the importance of establishing meaningful connections with others, fostering a sense of belonging and mutual support within the learning community38,40,41. Meeting these needs contributes to improved emotional well-being and increased engagement levels. Such outcomes are particularly significant for managing the challenges associated with remote education10,37. Concepts derived from operant conditioning, social learning theory, and social reinforcement theory highlight the significance of external and social factors in shaping behavior, particularly through mechanisms such as feedback and recognition. Theories emphasize environmental factors that enhance participation in learning activities. SDT elaborates on these ideas and demonstrates how social or external influences can foster intrinsic motivation, rather than relying exclusively on external factors. Supportive reinforcements that do not exert control facilitate the integration of external elements into individual belief systems. Positive social interactions in digital environments consequently fulfill the three needs and steer motivation towards self-regulation.

Social support fosters autonomy by promoting self-initiated actions among learners and refraining from imposing constraints. Feedback from peers or teachers may validate a learner’s choices, such as endorsing the selection of a topic during an online discussion. This approach contrasts with the predetermined rewards associated with operant conditioning, as they may undermine the sense of free choice when perceived as coercive. SDT suggests that such reinforcements assist learners in assimilating external concepts and transforming them into personal objectives9,42. In the realm of language education, this process may occur when students receive recognition for exploring topics that are personally meaningful, leading to sustained engagement. External contributions in this way evolve into internal motivations, leading learners to perceive their work as directed by their own agency and imbued with significance.

Social support further enhances competence by fostering learners’ confidence in their abilities through validation and evidence of progress. Teachers or fellow students might provide specific and constructive feedback on improvements in aspects such as verbal communication or vocabulary usage during online discussions. This input affirms growth and diminishes dependence on external rewards. According to SLT, observing the successes of others and receiving constructive feedback can motivate similar efforts. SDT elucidates the rationale for fostering intrinsic motivation. The satisfaction of the need for competence transforms challenges into achievable objectives43,44. In online collaborative sessions, acknowledging a learner’s contributions enhances self-confidence and fosters deeper engagement driven by genuine curiosity rather than obligation. Over time, the internalization of this affirmation transforms external validation into a personal acknowledgment of achievement, sustaining motivation during independent endeavors.

Furthermore, social support fosters a sense of relatedness by establishing connections and cultivating a supportive community that alleviates feelings of loneliness in online environments. Initiatives like collective praise or mutual recognition in forum discussions foster a sense of belonging and respect among participants. SRT perceives this as shaping influenced by the environment through advantageous interactions. SDT emphasizes its role in intrinsic motivation45. The connection to the need for relatedness fosters a sense of respect among learners and promotes their active participation. In L2 contexts, this evolution may involve continuous patterns of mutual support, where students assist each other with language tasks, fostering a commitment that deepens internally. The transformation of external influences into enduring engagement, propelled by the individual, enhances resilience throughout the entire learning process46.

Empirical foundations of the study

The empirical landscape concerning motivation in L2 learning has long recognized the pivotal role of reinforcement. Early investigations, such as those by47, established a critical link, suggesting that teacher attention could foster student engagement, and this creates a virtuous cycle of motivation and learning. Over subsequent decades, this understanding has been refined and expanded. More recent scholarship, including48,49, has consistently demonstrated the positive impact of student engagement on motivational outcomes. Complementing this50, noted that positive reinforcement directly enhances student engagement, while51 empirically validated that teacher-applied reinforcement positively influences learners’ performance, particularly in English language contexts, a sentiment echoed by52 regarding the general function of reinforcement in language acquisition. These studies, spanning several decades, collectively underscore a consistent theme: reinforcement strategies are fundamental in cultivating an engaged and consequently motivated learning environment.

Building upon this general understanding, another significant strand of research has focused more specifically on the nuanced effects of personalized feedback and social support within learning ecologies. For instance53, argued that personalized feedback not only serves as an outcome of motivation but crucially bolsters learners’ sense of competence and autonomy, enabling persistence and leading to improved academic performance. This perspective is strongly supported by a cluster of recent studies;54,55 provided evidence for the positive influence of personalized feedback messages on student performance and motivation respectively, while56 highlighted its impact on fostering self-regulated learning. In parallel, investigations into social dimensions have yielded consonant findings57. demonstrated that social support significantly improved the motivation of EFL learners. Similarly58, found a robust relationship between academic encouragement and students’ academic self-efficacy, and59 identified emotional well-being and SR as strong predictors of motivation. Furthermore, the efficacy of individualized approaches in specific language skill development was confirmed by60 who reported a positive impact of such feedback on listening and reading skills in EFL learning. Collectively, this body of work, particularly from the last decade, emphasizes how tailored and socially embedded reinforcement can directly nurture key psychological antecedents of motivation, aligning with constructs like competence and autonomy.

While the reviewed literature robustly establishes the benefits of various forms of reinforcement and feedback on learner motivation and engagement, several nuances warrant further investigation. Specifically, much of the existing research tends to examine these constructs either in general educational settings or with a focus on specific types of feedback without always deeply integrating the unique dynamics of online language learning environments, nor do they consistently explore the collective impact on all three core psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness as a holistic motivational framework. Furthermore, a comprehensive, mixed-methods exploration explicitly targeting how SR within these digital spaces influences this tripartite of motivational components, particularly among Iranian EFL learners, appeared less prevalent. The present study, therefore, aimed to fill this gap by systematically investigating the effects of SR in an online language learning context on these specific motivational dimensions (e.g., autonomy, competence, and relatedness) for Iranian EFL learners, employing an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design to provide both breadth and depth of understanding. The following research questions (RQs) were posed to meet these objectives:

RQ1: Does social reinforcement in online language learning significantly improve EFL learners’ motivation in terms of autonomy, competence, and relatedness?

RQ2: In what ways does social reinforcement in online language learning affect EFL learners’ motivation in terms of autonomy, competence, and relatedness?

Method

Research design

This investigation employed an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. This approach, as outlined by prominent methodologists61, is characterized by a two-phase process. Initially, quantitative data are collected and analyzed. This is then followed by a second phase where qualitative data are gathered and interpreted to help explain, elaborate on, or enrich the initial quantitative findings. The primary rationale for selecting this particular design was its inherent strength in facilitating methodological triangulation. While classic conceptualizations of triangulation often involve the concurrent use of different methods to cross-validate findings62, within an explanatory sequential framework, triangulation is achieved by using the qualitative findings to provide depth, context, and richer explanation for the statistical patterns identified in the quantitative data63. This sequential integration, therefore, allows for a more robust and comprehensive understanding of the research phenomenon involved in this study.

Participants

The present study was situated within the context of online English language learning environments accessible to learners in Iran. Access to digital platforms is increasingly central to EFL acquisition in the country, driven by factors such as limited access to traditional classroom settings and growing internet penetration, as evidenced by recent reports indicating that over 70% of Iranians have access to the internet and utilize online resources for educational purposes. Currently, Iranian EFL learners commonly utilize a range of online resources, including virtual learning environments offered by language institutes like Iran Language Institute, mobile applications such as Duolingo and Memrise, and social media platforms like Telegram and Instagram for language practice and interaction.

Participants were recruited from several established online EFL learning communities, including a large Facebook group dedicated to IELTS preparation and a forum hosted by a private language academy. The sample comprised 100 adult Iranian learners (38 male, 62 female) aged 24 to 39 years (M = 33.12, SD = 3.15), all of whom had Persian (Farsi) as their first language. All participants were concurrently enrolled in an online English course at the time of data collection; these courses primarily focused on general English skills development, with a secondary emphasis on conversational fluency. To ensure a degree of homogeneity in language ability relevant to the study’s focus, participants were required to demonstrate intermediate-level English proficiency. This proficiency level, corresponding to the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) B1/B2 level, signifies the ability to generally understand everyday conversations and texts, and to communicate with reasonable accuracy on familiar topics. This proficiency was confirmed via scores on the Oxford Quick Placement Test, a widely used assessment tool demonstrating strong validity and reliability for placement purposes. Furthermore, to mitigate potential confounding variables, participants self-reported that they were not concurrently enrolled in any other formal English language courses or receiving intensive private tutoring during the study period. A convenience sampling method was employed to initially identify potential participants within these online communities, acknowledging the limitations inherent in this non-probability sampling technique, specifically the potential for selection bias64.

Following the initial screening and selection, the 100 qualifying participants were then randomly assigned to one of two experimental conditions. This random assignment helps to ensure that, on average, the groups were comparable at the outset of the intervention. The first group (n = 50) received SR specifically designed to foster autonomy, competence, and relatedness. A second group (n = 50) served as a control, receiving no specific SR interventions related to the study, thus providing a baseline for comparison. Ethical considerations were paramount throughout the research process. Approval for the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee at a run-state university. Before committing to participation, all individuals received a comprehensive information sheet detailing the study’s objectives, the procedures involved, the expected duration of their involvement, and the voluntary nature of their participation. It was explicitly stated that they could withdraw at any time without penalty. Furthermore, assurances were provided regarding the confidentiality and anonymity of their data; all data collected were to be de-identified and reported in aggregate form. Written informed consent was subsequently secured from every participant prior to the commencement of any research activities. All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Instruments

To assess the EFL learners’ motivation, the instrument utilized was the Self-Determination Motivation Scale developed by65. This particular scale was selected due to its direct alignment with the theoretical underpinnings of SDT and its established capacity to comprehensively measure the three core psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. The instrument, in its original design by65, consists of 15 items. These items are evenly distributed across the three aforementioned dimensions, with 5 items specifically designed to gauge perceived autonomy, 5 items to assess feelings of competence, and a further 5 items dedicated to measuring relatedness. One item example included “I feel that I have a lot of choice in how I learn English in this online environment”. Participants indicated their level of agreement with each statement using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (Totally Disagree) to 5 (Totally Agree). Prior to its administration in the main study, several steps were undertaken to ensure the instrument’s suitability and psychometric soundness for the Iranian EFL context. First, the English version of Chiang’s65 scale was carefully translated into Persian, the participants’ first language (L1), by two bilingual language experts. Following this, the translated Persian version of the questionnaire was subjected to a pilot study involving 25 Iranian EFL learners with characteristics similar to the main study sample, but who did not participate further. The data from this pilot administration were analyzed to ascertain the internal consistency reliability of the translated scale. This analysis yielded a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.87. Concurrently, the content and face validity of the translated instrument were evaluated through expert judgment. The questionnaire was reviewed by three university professors specializing in EFL, motivation, and psychometrics who provided feedback on the clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness of the items for Iranian EFL learners. Their consensus affirmed the instrument’s suitability for measuring the intended constructs within the target population.

To complement the quantitative findings and gain deeper insights into the participants’ lived experiences, this study employed semi-structured interviews as a key qualitative data collection tool. This approach was chosen for its capacity to provide a flexible yet guided framework, allowing participants from the experimental group to articulate their personal perceptions, challenges, and reflections concerning the SR interventions they encountered within the online language learning communities. The development of the interview questions was theoretically grounded in the core dimensions of SDT (e.g., autonomy, competence, and relatedness). Specifically, the questions were designed to explore how various forms of SR, as implemented in the online environment, may have influenced these psychological needs. For instance, the authors aimed to understand how targeted praise or peer recognition (elements of SR) might foster a greater sense of volitional engagement (autonomy), perceived capability (competence), or connection with others (relatedness).

To ensure consistency across interviews while still allowing for emergent themes, an interview protocol was developed and utilized by the researchers. This protocol outlined the main areas of inquiry and provided a set of open-ended, guiding questions. Examples of such questions included:

Could you describe how receiving badges or positive comments from peers made you feel about your freedom to choose learning activities or pace?

Could you describe how receiving badges or positive comments from peers made you feel about your freedom to choose learning activities or pace?

Can you recall a specific instance where SR from the platform or peers influenced your confidence in your language abilities? How so?

Can you recall a specific instance where SR from the platform or peers influenced your confidence in your language abilities? How so?

In what ways did the interactions involving SR help you feel more connected to your classmates or the learning community?

In what ways did the interactions involving SR help you feel more connected to your classmates or the learning community?

How did SR you experienced affect your overall motivation to participate and learn in this online environment?

How did SR you experienced affect your overall motivation to participate and learn in this online environment?

These interviews were conducted in Persian, the participants’ native language, to facilitate richer and more articulate responses. Each interview session typically lasted between 20 and 30 min and took place in a quiet, mutually agreed-upon virtual setting (e.g., a private online call) to ensure participant comfort and confidentiality. With explicit informed consent from each participant, all interviews were audio-recorded. These recordings were subsequently transcribed verbatim to ensure accuracy and provide a reliable dataset for in-depth qualitative analysis. Data saturation was reached after interviewing 11 participants (males, n = 5; females, n = 6) from the experimental group, which provided a comprehensive understanding of the phenomena under investigation.

Data collection and analysis procedures

The data collection for this study proceeded through a meticulously planned sequence of phases, ensuring systematic implementation of the research design. The initial phase involved comprehensive training for the instructors responsible for facilitating the online language learning communities. This training, conducted by the authors, focused on the precise implementation of the different SR strategies designated for each experimental group. Instructors were familiarized with the theoretical underpinnings of SDT, the specific types of feedback to provide (or withhold for the control group), and the operational definitions of targeted versus generic reinforcement to ensure fidelity to the research design. Following instructor training, the second step was the administration of the pre-test. All participants across the two groups completed Chiang’s65 Self-Determination Motivation Scale to establish baseline levels of autonomy, competence, and relatedness prior to the intervention period.

The intervention phase subsequently commenced and spanned a period of seven weeks. The interventions for the experimental group were grounded in the principles of the SRT, which emphasizes the role of positive feedback, recognition, and encouragement in shaping behavior and enhancing motivation. This approach aligns with key psychological theories, such as SDT, which highlights the importance of autonomy, competence, and relatedness in fostering intrinsic motivation. By providing personalized and specific feedback, the interventions aimed to address these three psychological needs. For instance, acknowledging individual progress (e.g., “Your use of the subjunctive tense was excellent, Ali!“) reinforced competence, while public recognition of achievements (e.g., congratulating Reza on his first paragraph) fostered relatedness by creating a sense of community. Private messages offering tailored encouragement further supported autonomy by making learners feel their efforts were self-directed and valued. SR also draws on Bandura’s15 SLT, which posits that observational learning and reinforcement from peers and authority figures can significantly influence behavior. By incorporating feedback from both instructors and peer moderators, the interventions created a multi-layered support system that modeled desired behaviors and reinforced positive outcomes through social validation.

To tangibly raise participants’ motivation, the interventions included a variety of structured activities designed to maximize engagement and reinforcement. For example, weekly spotlight posts were used to publicly highlight exceptional contributions, such as well-constructed sentences or creative use of vocabulary, which not only recognized individual effort but also set benchmarks for others. Personalized progress reports were sent biweekly, detailing specific improvements (e.g., “Sara, your pronunciation accuracy has increased by 20% this month”) and setting achievable short-term goals. Peer-to-peer praise sessions were organized where participants exchanged positive feedback on each other’s work, fostering a collaborative environment. Additionally, gamified elements, such as badges for milestones (e.g., “Grammar Master” for consistent accuracy in verb conjugations), were introduced to provide tangible rewards. Live Q&A sessions with instructors allowed for real-time, specific feedback, while goal-setting workshops helped participants articulate and share their learning objectives, creating accountability. These activities were carefully designed to ensure that reinforcement was immediate, specific, and socially embedded. By combining public recognition, private encouragement, and interactive tasks, the interventions created a dynamic and supportive learning ecosystem that continuously reinforced participants’ efforts and achievements.

For the control group, the participants engaged in the same online language learning activities as the experimental group but did not receive any structured SR interventions from the trained instructors beyond the standard functionalities and interactions typical of the online platform. While participants could still post questions or share achievements, any feedback or recognition arose from naturally occurring, uninfluenced peer responses. This group served as a crucial baseline to measure the distinct effects of the targeted and generic SR strategies. Upon completion of the four-week intervention period, the post-test was administered. All participants once again completed Chiang’s65 Self-Determination Motivation Scale. To mitigate potential practice effects from re-administering the same instrument, a suitable intervening period of four weeks, filled with active learning and the intervention itself rather than test preparation, was maintained. Finally, following the collection of post-test quantitative data, the qualitative phase commenced. Semi-structured interviews, as detailed in the Instruments section, were conducted with a subset of 11 participants. This sequencing allowed for the quantitative results to inform the selection of interviewees and provided a rich layer of understanding regarding their experiences with the different SR conditions.

Data analysis procedures

The quantitative data gathered were analyzed using SPSS Statistics, Version 27. Initially, descriptive statistics, specifically means (Ms) and standard deviations (SDs), were computed for the baseline scores on autonomy, competence, and relatedness for both groups. Subsequently, inferential statistical methods were applied, with independent samples t-tests selected to explore any differences between the experimental group and control group prior to and after the intervention. Before conducting t-tests, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were checked. The Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed normal data distribution (all ps > 0.05), and Levene’s test showed equal variances across groups (all ps > 0.05). Since both assumptions were met, independent samples t-tests were used with a significance level of p < .05.

For the qualitative phase, the interview transcripts were analyzed using content analysis. This research method was selected as it facilitates a systematic and objective interpretation of textual data through a structured process of coding and identifying patterns or themes66,67. The primary rationale for employing content analysis was its suitability for systematically exploring and understanding the nuances of Iranian EFL learners’ lived experiences and perceptions concerning the effects of SR on their motivational constructs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. The analytical process adhered to the three-stage coding procedure advocated by68. The first step was open coding in which the interview transcripts were meticulously read and re-read to break down the data into discrete conceptual units. Each meaningful segment of text pertaining to learners’ experiences SR and its perceived impact on their motivation was assigned an initial descriptive code. This process generated a comprehensive list of preliminary codes directly grounded in the participants’ narratives. The second step was axial coding. Following open coding, the focus shifted to systematically developing connections between the initial codes. Similar codes were grouped into more abstract categories, and the relationships between these categories and their subcategories were explored. This stage involved re-examining the data with these emerging categories in mind, specifically looking for how various dimensions of SR intersected with learners’ feelings of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. The last step was selective coding which involved identifying a core category or central theme that integrated the major categories developed during axial coding. This step aimed to formulate a coherent explanatory framework that captured the essence of how SR in the online language learning environment influenced learners’ motivation.

To assess the reliability and trustworthiness of the qualitative findings, several measures were implemented. The consistency of the coding process was primarily established through inter-coder reliability. A second researcher, familiar with qualitative methodology, independently coded a significant portion (e.g., approximately 25%) of the interview transcripts. Subsequently, Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was calculated to assess the level of agreement between the two coders, with a target value of 0.80 or higher indicating substantial agreement69. Any discrepancies in coding were discussed and resolved through consensus, refining the codebook and its application. Furthermore, peer debriefing sessions were held periodically, allowing for critical reflection on the analytical process and emerging interpretations.

Similar patterns of change were observed for competence scores (Experimental group: from M = 10.08 to M = 19.92; Control group: from M = 10.38 to M = 13.58) and relatedness scores (Experimental group: from M = 11.30 to M = 20.62; Control group: from M = 10.74 to M = 14.46). Furthermore, the narrower confidence intervals and smaller SDs recorded for the experimental group post-intervention suggest a greater consistency of improvement among participants who received SR.

As presented in Table 4, post-test comparisons revealed statistically significant differences favoring the experimental group on all measured dimensions: autonomy (t(98) = 6.715, p < .001, d = 1.35), competence (t(98) = 8.208, p < .001, d = 1.65), and relatedness (t(98) = 8.495, p < .001, d = 1.71). The magnitude of these effects, with all Cohen’s d values surpassing the 0.80 criterion for large effects, underscores the substantial practical significance of the intervention.

Qualitative results

This section presents the findings from the qualitative analysis. For confidentiality, all participants are referred to by pseudonyms throughout the results reporting.

Positive effects of social reinforcement approach on the EFL learners’ autonomy

The qualitative data obtained from semi-structured interviews with participants in the experimental group offer a more detailed understanding of the quantitative results. A significant portion of the findings identified in the interview data concerned the enhancement of learner autonomy through experiences with SR. Participants indicated that positive verbal cues and acknowledgements from instructors and peers not only increased their confidence but also encouraged them to explore learning paths more independently. This observation aligns with established theories of SDT, proposed by Deci and Ryan10, which suggest that feelings of competence, fostered through positive feedback, are essential for intrinsic motivation and autonomous behavior. For example, Reza highlighted a newfound willingness to self-direct his learning activities:

Before, I used to just do the exercises the teacher assigned. But when the teacher praised my comment in the forum, saying it was a ‘very insightful point,’ I felt more confident. So, for the next module, I actually searched for some extra videos on YouTube about the topic myself, even before the teacher suggested any. I wanted to understand it better on my own.

This excerpt approves that targeted positive feedback functioned as a catalyst, prompting the learner to move beyond tasks that were specifically assigned. The reinforcement appears to have validated the participant’s capability, which can encourage proactive and self-initiated learning behavior, a key indicator of enhanced autonomy88. Furthermore, the perception of a supportive and encouraging online atmosphere, cultivated through SR, seemed to diminish anxieties associated with making independent learning decisions. This resonates with research on the impact of classroom climate on learner agency, conducted by12, demonstrating how a positive learning environment can empower students to take ownership of their learning. Shima elaborated on how consistent encouragement altered her approach to tackling challenging tasks:

I was always hesitant to choose my own topics for the speaking tasks, worried I might pick something too difficult or not relevant. But the instructor often said things like, ‘That’s a great idea, pursue it!’ or ‘Don’t worry, give it a try, and we can discuss.’ This made me feel it was okay to take risks. Now, I actually prefer to select my own topics because I feel more in control and more invested in what I’m learning. It wasn’t just about the grades anymore; it was about my own choice.

This account indicates that consistent SR created a psychologically safe space for experimentation. Thus, the learner reported a shift from task selection guided by external sources to a more intrinsically motivated and self-determined approach, underlining a significant development in her autonomy. The instructor’s supportive stance, reinforced through positive communication, appears to have been crucial in this transition, mirroring findings in23’s work on the motivational role of teacher behavior. Moreover, participants reported an increased tendency towards independent problem-solving as a result of feeling affirmed by SR. This suggests a move away from over-reliance on the instructor towards a more self-sufficient learning stance. As Sima articulated:

In the past, if I didn’t understand a grammar point immediately, I would just wait for the teacher to explain it again or ask a direct question. But after getting positive comments on my contributions, like ‘good effort in figuring that out,’ I started trying harder on my own first. I’d re-read the material, maybe look it up online, before asking. It felt good to solve it myself sometimes.

This narrative demonstrates a clear shift towards greater self-reliance. The reinforcement, by acknowledging effort rather than solely correct answers, seemingly fostered the participant’s confidence in her ability to independently navigate learning challenges.

Positive effects of social reinforcement of EFL learners’ competence

The qualitative data further illuminated the mechanisms through which SR positively influenced participant motivation, particularly by fostering an enhanced sense of competence. This theme underscores how targeted positive feedback and peer recognition within the online learning environment contributed to learners’ belief in their capabilities and their effectiveness in the language learning process, a crucial component of SDT9. The development of such a sense of competence is central to understanding motivation;15 emphasized the role of perceived self-efficacy in driving engagement and achievement. Karim articulated how specific encouragement directly impacted his self-perception of ability:

Before, I was always hesitant, thinking my grammar was weak. But after the instructor praised my sentence construction in the online forum task, saying it was ‘clear and effectively used the new vocabulary,’ I felt a significant shift. It wasn’t just a general ‘good job’; it was specific. That made me believe I could actually master these structures.

This excerpt illustrates how personalized and detailed positive feedback can directly affirm a learner’s skill. The instructor’s comment did not merely acknowledge effort but validated a specific linguistic achievement, nurturing the participant’s confidence in his grammatical proficiency. This finding resonates with research on formative assessment92, which highlights the importance of feedback focused on specific aspects of performance rather than general praise. Additionally, Sedigheh highlighted the motivating effect of peer validation on her developing skills:

When we did the small group discussions online, and my classmates would react with a ‘thumbs up’ or a ‘great point!’ after I shared my opinion, it really helped. It showed me that my contributions were valuable and that I was understanding and using English effectively enough to communicate my ideas. I felt more capable.

This reflection emphasizes the power of peer-based SR. Such affirmations, even if brief, served as immediate indicators of successful communication and comprehension, reinforcing the participant’s belief in her growing communicative competence within the target language. This supports83’s Socio-cultural Theory, which posits that learning is fundamentally a social process and that interaction with peers plays a vital role in cognitive development. In addition, the consistency of such positive interactions appeared to build a more resilient sense of capability, as expressed by Farshad:

It wasn’t just one comment that made the difference. It was the regular positive feedback from both the teacher and other students. If I posted a question and got a helpful answer with a ‘good question!’ or if I helped someone else and they thanked me, all these small things added up. I started to feel like I truly belonged in the learning environment and that I was making real progress, becoming a more competent English speaker.

This narrative demonstrates that the cumulative effect of varied SRs, ranging from instructional praise to peer acknowledgments cultivated a robust and internalized sense of competence. The regularity of these positive experiences appeared instrumental in shifting learners’ self-appraisal from uncertainty toward a more confident assessment of their language abilities. Accordingly, these findings strongly advocate that SR in online settings can be a powerful tool for enhancing EFL learners’ feelings of competence, positively influencing their overall learning motivation. These results contribute to a growing body of literature demonstrating the efficacy of positive psychology interventions in L2 learning contexts93.

Positive effects of social reinforcement of EFL learners’ relatedness

The qualitative findings also offer compelling insights into how SR within the online learning environment positively influenced participants’ sense of relatedness. These results indicated that interactions perceived as supportive and affirming fostered a stronger sense of connection among learners and with the instructor. This observation aligns with SDT, where9 identify relatedness (i.e., the feeling of belonging and connection) as a fundamental psychological need crucial for intrinsic motivation and well-being. Moreover, the importance of interpersonal bonds for both psychological health and academic performance is well-documented in research on social connectedness93. In this regard, Faranaz from the experimental group articulated the impact of direct encouragement:

Getting those little ‘well done’ messages from the tutor after I posted, or even just a ‘good point,’ made me feel like I wasn’t just shouting into the void. It felt like someone was actually listening and that my contributions mattered.

This brief reflection highlights how even minimal, positive affirmations from an authority figure can significantly enhance a learner’s perception of being acknowledged and valued. Such acknowledgement appears to be a direct pathway to feeling more connected within the learning environment, a finding consistent with the concept of teacher immediacy and its positive effects on student engagement94,95. Farhad expanded on the role of peer-initiated reinforcement, indicating a broader sense of community:

It wasn’t only the teacher. When other students would react to my answers in the forum with a thumbs-up, or when someone would say, ‘I had the same thought!’ it was really motivating. It made the online class feel less like a solo mission and more like we were a team, helping each other out. I definitely felt more part of a group than in other online courses I’ve taken where there was no such interaction.

The experience described here suggests that reinforcement from peers played a vital role in cultivating a supportive atmosphere. The feeling of shared understanding and mutual encouragement seemingly transformed the individual learning experience into a collective one, strengthening interpersonal bonds and a sense of belonging crucial for relatedness. This finding echoes the benefits of peer interaction for fostering a sense of community and shared responsibility, as demonstrated in research on collaborative learning96. Moreover, the cumulative effect of varied positive social cues was emphasized by Ahmad Reza, who contrasted this experience with previous, less interactive online settings:

In the past, online learning felt very isolated. You submit work, you get a grade. But here, the regular positive feedback, the encouraging emojis from classmates, and the instructor highlighting good examples from our discussions—all of it together created a much warmer, more human atmosphere. I found myself looking forward to logging in and seeing what others had said, and I felt comfortable asking questions because the environment was so supportive. It made a huge difference to how connected I felt to everyone.

This more detailed account underscores how a consistent and multifaceted approach to SR can fundamentally alter the perceived nature of the online learning space. The shift from an impersonal, task-focused environment to one characterized by warmth and mutual support directly addressed the learners’ need for relatedness, making them feel like integral members of a learning community rather than isolated individuals. These narratives collectively demonstrate that the strategic integration of SR can be a powerful tool for fostering relatedness in online EFL contexts.

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of SR within an online language learning context on the motivation, specifically autonomy, competence, and relatedness, of Iranian EFL learners, utilizing a mixed-methods explanatory sequential design. The principal findings from both the quantitative and qualitative phases converged, demonstrating that strategically implemented SR significantly and positively influenced all three dimensions of learner motivation within the Iranian EFL context. These results advocate that the intentional integration of supportive social interactions can be a potent catalyst for enhancing learners’ intrinsic drive in digital learning environments, a finding particularly relevant given the increasing prevalence of online learning in Iran.

The outcomes of this study, particularly the enhanced motivation and performance observed in the experimental group that benefited from personalized feedback and encouragement within an online community, align with a growing body of literature. However, it is important to consider these findings in light of the specific socio-cultural context of Iranian EFL learning. For example, the positive effects of learner engagement on motivation, demonstrated in recent studies such as those conducted by48, are reinforced by our results. This also supports the foundational work of47, who posited that positive teacher attention fosters a cycle of increasing motivation and learning. Furthermore, the observed increase in learner motivation resonates with findings regarding positive reinforcement, as noted by researchers like50] and [51, and with research concerning social support for EFL learners conducted by57, acknowledging that cultural norms surrounding teacher-student interaction in Iran may uniquely influence the effectiveness of these approaches. Moreover, this study’s emphasis on the motivational power of SR is consistent with research identifying emotional well-being and SR as predictors of motivation and academic expectations, a point made by58, and with studies demonstrating the positive function of reinforcement in English language learning, such as the work of52.

Beyond the general impact of reinforcement, the specific positive effect of personalized feedback on learners’ motivation, competence, and autonomy is strongly supported by prior research, including the work of53. This is particularly noteworthy considering the potential for personalized feedback to address individual learning needs within the often-large class sizes that are common in Iranian educational settings. The connection between academic encouragement and self-efficacy observed in this study echoes findings reported by58. Similarly, the efficacy of personalized feedback messages strongly corroborates the work of54,55, which demonstrated positive influences on student performance and motivation. The observed enhancement in autonomy through tailored feedback further aligns with research on personalized feedback and self-regulated learning, as highlighted by56. Finally, the study’s indication that tailored feedback can enhance various dimensions of learners’ language competence is well supported by research confirming the positive impact of individualized feedback on listening and reading skills in EFL contexts, a crucial area for Iranian EFL learners preparing for standardized tests and academic pursuits, as confirmed by60.

The findings of this study are best understood by considering the specific context of English language learning in Iran and can be illuminated through several prominent theoretical lenses. From the perspective of Skinner’s theory of Operant Conditioning, the observed enhancements in autonomy, competence, and relatedness likely stem from the application of principles governing operant conditioning70. Within the Iranian educational system, where positive reinforcement from instructors often carries considerable weight, the positive feedback, encouragement, and peer recognition experienced by participants in the experimental group likely functioned as powerful reinforcers. These reinforcers likely increased the frequency and intensity of desired learning behaviors – active participation, effortful engagement, and persistence – a factor particularly relevant given the potential challenges Iranian learners may face in accessing consistent support outside of the classroom71–73. For example, specific praise for a contribution, acting as a positive stimulus, likely encouraged further participation, strengthening engagement and, consequently, motivation. Contemporary research applying reinforcement principles in educational technology74–76 suggests that such digital affirmations can effectively shape online learning conduct and foster a more positive disposition toward the learning process, a benefit particularly valuable in the expanding online learning landscape within Iran.

Furthermore, the results align convincingly with the tenets of SDT, which posits that motivation is optimized when the fundamental psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are satisfied9,77. The SR strategies employed in this study appear to have directly nurtured these three needs. The enhanced sense of relatedness is perhaps the most evident outcome, as positive interactions, peer support, and instructor acknowledgement would cultivate feelings of connection and belonging within the online learning community12,78. The positive feedback on performance and contributions likely bolstered learners’ perceived competence, leading them to feel more capable and effective in their language learning processes79. While the link to autonomy might be more nuanced, a supportive and reinforcing environment can empower learners by reducing anxiety and fostering a sense of psychological safety, potentially encouraging them to take more initiative and make self-directed choices in their learning which is a particularly important consideration given potential constraints on learner agency within the Iranian educational context80. The confluence of these satisfied needs, as suggested by SDT, predictably resulted in the heightened intrinsic motivation observed.

Finally, when viewed through the lens of Socio-cultural Theory, the findings underscore the inherently social nature of learning, even in online environments81–83. This theory emphasizes that higher cognitive functions develop through social interaction, with learning occurring as individuals participate in culturally valued activities and utilize appropriate tools and signs, including language itself84. In this study, the online platform served as the social context, and SRs (i.e., instructor feedback and peer comments) can be conceptualized as mediational tools and forms of scaffolding provided by more knowledgeable others or capable peers. These interactions likely facilitated learners’ movement through their Zone of Proximal Development, enabling them to achieve more than they could independently, a process potentially amplified by the collaborative learning opportunities afforded by the online environment85.

Pedagogical implications

The findings of this study offer several pertinent implications. Iranian EFL learners, these results suggest, might adopt a proactive approach to their online studies; they could actively seek out and participate in digital platforms fostering interaction and reciprocal support, cultivating not only linguistic competence but also a stronger sense of autonomy86–88 and connection with peers, which is crucial for social presence in online environments89. EFL educators in Iran are encouraged to design and implement online pedagogical strategies systematically incorporating positive social feedback. This could involve structuring collaborative tasks where peer encouragement is integral, aligning with sociocultural learning theories83, or providing personalized affirmations specifically acknowledging learners’ progress and effort. Such feedback mechanisms may prove crucial in bolstering self-efficacy15 and relatedness within the virtual classroom, key components of motivated learning10.

School administrators could play a pivotal role by championing such pedagogical shifts, perhaps through supportive institutional policies, facilitating professional development focused on leveraging social dynamics in online settings90, on e-moderating skills), and ensuring access to technologies that enable rich, interactive learning experiences. These efforts would promote a culture where student motivation is actively nurtured, consistent with broader institutional goals for student success. Material developers targeting the Iranian market, this research underscores the value of embedding features that promote SR directly within digital learning resources. It appears advisable to move beyond traditional content delivery by designing materials incorporating spaces for peer-to-peer interaction, mechanisms for positive feedback exchange, and perhaps even gamified elements rewarding collaborative achievements, strategies known to enhance engagement91. Such an approach, which aligns with principles of SDT10, holds considerable potential, enhancing learners’ intrinsic motivation by nurturing feelings of competence, autonomy, and relatedness. Ultimately, this could contribute to more sustained engagement and success in their online English language learning endeavors in Iran, as supported by extensive research on motivation in educational settings.

Conclusions, limitations and suggestions for further research

Employed a mixed-methods approach, this study investigated the effects of SR on the motivation, including autonomy, competence, and relatedness of Iranian EFL learners within an online learning environment. The results clearly demonstrate that SR significantly enhanced learners’ motivation across all three dimensions, a finding corroborated by qualitative data revealing participants experienced SR as fostering self-direction, confidence, and a stronger sense of connection. These findings demonstrate that the deliberate integration of SR mechanisms into online EFL settings can cultivate a more supportive and engaging learning environment, ultimately bolstering intrinsic motivation. The theoretical contributions of this research reside in providing robust empirical support for established motivational frameworks, notably SDT and Socio-cultural Theory, within the relatively under-explored context of Iranian online EFL education. The study reinforces the applicability of these theories to virtual language learning and expands our understanding of how these constructs interact within this unique learning environment.

Acknowledging the scope of this investigation, several limitations warrant consideration. The four-week intervention period may have been insufficient to fully capture sustained motivational shifts, and the sample, which comprised 100 intermediate-level EFL learners aged 24–39 enrolled in Teaching English courses, lacked broader demographic diversity, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. While the study produced statistically meaningful results, the sample size per group (n = 50) was slightly below the recommended minimum of 64 for detecting medium effect sizes at 80% power. This limitation suggests the need for caution in generalizing the findings, and future studies are encouraged to use larger samples to enhance statistical power. Furthermore, unmeasured individual differences, such as prior language learning experience, personality traits, or external motivations could have influenced learner responses. Future research should prioritize addressing these limitations. Specifically, longitudinal studies with extended intervention periods are needed to assess long-term effects. Investigations should also broaden the participant base to include learners who vary in proficiency, age, and learning objectives. Exploring the effectiveness of SR strategies that are specifically tailored to Iranian cultural norms represents another crucial avenue for inquiry, as does examining how empowering learners to co-create their own peer support systems impacts motivation. Finally, exploratory research utilizing neuroimaging techniques could offer deeper insights into the neurological basis of SR in online learning.
