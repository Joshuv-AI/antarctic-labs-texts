Isoliquiritigenin alleviates ulcerative colitis by enhancing the intestinal barrier and improving intestinal microbiota via inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway
Authors: Ruifang Li, Yan Sun, Mingzhi Feng, Drolma Kelsang, Hanhui Cai, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUlcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic disease characterized by intestinal inflammation, mucosal damage, and immune dysregulation. Current treatments are often inadequate, driving the search for alternatives with fewer side effects. Isoliquiritigenin (ISL), a flavonoid from licorice, has demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects, but its efficacy in UC remains uncertain. Male C57BL/6 mice were divided into six groups: Control, DSS model, low-, medium-, and high-dose ISL (25, 50, 100 mg/kg), and 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA, 100 mg/kg). UC was induced by administering 3% DSS, followed by ISL or 5-ASA treatment. Disease activity index (DAI), histopathology, real-time PCR, Western blotting, immunohistochemistry, and 16 S rRNA sequencing were used to assess inflammation, intestinal barrier integrity, and gut microbiota (GM) composition. a Caco-2 cell-based model of UC was developed. After transfection to induce the overexpression of TLR4 and its adaptor protein MyD88, the cells were incubated with ISL. The effects of this intervention were evaluated by measuring the expression of inflammatory cytokines, tight junction-associated proteins, and components of the TLR4/MyD88 signaling pathway. ISL significantly alleviated DSS-induced UC symptoms, such as weight loss and colonic shortening. ISL reduced colonic mucosal damage and inflammatory cell infiltration, downregulated TLR4, MyD88, and phosphorylated NF-κB expression, and lowered TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels. It also improved intestinal barrier function by upregulating ZO-1, Claudin-1, and Occludin expression. ISL modulated GM by increasing beneficial bacteria (Muribaculaceae) and reducing pathogenic bacteria (Escherichia-Shigella). Overexpression of TLR4 and MyD88 in the Caco-2 cell line abrogated the protective effects of ISL, as evidenced by increased expression of inflammatory cytokines and downregulated expression of tight junction proteins. Overall, these findings suggest ISL may protects against DSS-induced UC by suppressing the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, enhancing intestinal barrier function, and modulating GM, supporting its potential as a therapeutic supplement for UC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46594-8.Keywords: Ulcerative colitis, Isoliquiritigenin, Inflammatory, Intestinal barrier, Gut microbiotaSubject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Medical research, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a long-term inflammatory disorder that primarily targets the colon and rectum, with damage restricted to the intestinal mucosa and submucosa1. The condition commonly manifests with symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, and bloody stools2. Current treatments, such as salicylates, hormones, immunosuppressants, and biological therapies3, are often associated with side effects like resistance, intolerance, dependence, and opportunistic infections. Consequently, 10–20% of patients with drug-refractory UC require surgical resection of the colorectum4. Therefore, further exploration and selection of natural compounds with fewer side effects as an alternative or complementary treatment remains a hot issue for clinicians and researchers today.

The etiology and pathogenesis of UC remain complex and unresolved. Growing evidence indicates that continuous inflammation-induced intestinal mucosal barrier damage plays a pivotal role in UC development5. Research has shown that abnormal activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis is commonly found in the intestinal mucosa of individuals with UC6–8.TLR4 initiates the activation of NF-κB through the MyD88-dependent signaling axis, which, in turn, drives the production and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines uch as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, ultimately exacerbating mucosal inflammation9–12. Thus, reducing intestinal inflammation and preserving mucosal barrier integrity is vital for treating UC, with the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling axis emerging as a promising target for therapeutic intervention.

Isoliquiritigenin (ISL) is a flavonoid derived from the rhizomes of Glycyrrhiza glabra, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb, with known anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antitumor properties, including the inhibition of cell proliferation and migration13. ISL is present in herbal formulas used as adjunctive treatments for UC14,15. Animal studies have also demonstrated that ISL can alleviate intestinal inflammation in UC by reducing neutrophil and macrophage infiltration in colonic tissues16,17. However, the specific mechanisms by which ISL acts on UC remain uncertain. Thus, this study evaluated the therapeutic effects of ISL on DSS-induced UC in mice, focusing on its potential mechanisms, including the regulation of the intestinal mucosal barrier, inhibition of inflammatory factor expression, and improvement of intestinal microbiota dysbiosis. These findings provide both theoretical and experimental evidence to support the clinical use of ISL in treating UC.

Materials and methods

Experimental reagents

Dextran sulfate sodium salt (DSS) was acquired from MP Biomedicals (0216011080, USA). Isoliquiritigenin and 5-ASA were procured from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd (I111284, A129982, China). TLR4 antibody was supplied by ABclonal Biotechnology Co.Ltd.(A5258, China). MyD88 antibody and Claudin antibody were provided by Santa Cruz Biotechnology (SC-74532, SC-166338, USA). NF-κB was procured from Hangzhou Huaan Biotechnology Co.Ltd.(ET1603-12, China). P-NF-κB was sourced from Cell Signaling Technology (CST#3033, USA). ZO-1 and Occludin antibodies were acquired from Proteintech Group (21773-1-AP, 13409-1-AP, China). Claudin antibody (Cat# EPR25359-48) was purchased from Abcam. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was obtained from Nanjing SenBeiJia Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (BC-SE-FBS08, China). The 1600 culture medium (XP-1040-N), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, XP-1010-N), and trypsin (XP-2010-N) were purchased from XAPUNU Biological Technology Co., Ltd(China). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was sourced from Solarbio (#D8370, China). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, CAS No. 93572-42-0) derived from *Escherichia coli* 0111: B4 was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The overexpression plasmids for TLR4 (Cat# CH804693) and MyD88 (Cat# CH865243), along with the transfection reagent (Cat# FH880808), were obtained from Vigene Biosciences. The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Cat# CK04) was purchased from Dojindo, Japan. The RNA extraction kit (Cat# WX624930) was acquired from ABclonal Technology (Wuhan, China). The reverse transcription kit (Cat# AG11706) and the 2× SYBR Green qPCR Premix kit (Cat# AG11718) were sourced from Accurate Biology. The BCA protein assay kit (Cat# 23227) was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Experimental animals

Forty-two male C57BL/6 mice, 8 weeks old and weighing 21 ± 2 g, were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China; production license: SCXK(SU)2023-0009) and housed at the Animal Center of Zhejiang University of Technology (Use License: SYXK(Zhe)2022-0007). The mice were kept in a controlled environment with 45–55% humidity, a temperature of 24 ± 2 °C, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All animal experiments were granted by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Physical Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University of Technology (Approval No. ZH20230705003). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Grouping and modeling of animals

After a 7-day acclimatization period, the mice were randomly categorized into six groups: Control, DSS model, low-dose ISL (25 mg/kg), medium-dose ISL (50 mg/kg), high-dose ISL (100 mg/kg), and positive control treated with 5-ASA (100 mg/kg), with 7 mice in each group. The drug doses were selected based on previously reported doses in the literature and the results of our preliminary experiments. To induce the UC model, the DSS, ISLL, ISLM, ISLH, and 5-ASA groups received 3% DSS ad libitum for 7 days, while the control group was given distilled water ad libitum. From the third day of modeling, mice in the ISLL, ISLM, ISLH, and 5-ASA groups received the respective drug dosages via gavage for 7 consecutive days. During the experiment, the Disease Activity Index (DAI) was evaluated based on body weight loss (scored as 1 for 1–5%, 2 for 5–10%, 3 for 10–15%, and 4 for more than 15%), stool consistency (0 for normal, 1 for loose stools, and 3 for watery stools), and the presence of occult or visible blood in the feces (0 = normal; 1 = occult blood+; 3 = bloody stools). At the conclusion of the study, colorectal tissue and fecal samples were collected from all mice following euthanasia by cervical dislocation and necropsy.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Caco-2 is a human epithelial colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line (Meisen Cell Technology Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China), cultured in 1640 medium supplemented with 20% FBS and maintained in an incubator at 37 °C with 95% air and 5% CO2. LPS was dissolved in PBS to prepare a 1 mg/mL solution, while ISL was dissolved in DMSO at a final concentration of < 0.1%, which exhibited no significant effect on Caco-2 cell viability.

Caco-2 cells (2 × 10⁴ cells per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and subsequently treated with varying concentrations of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 0–100 µg/mL) or isoliquiritigenin (0–200 µM) for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were incubated with Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent for 1 h, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Cell transfection

Caco-2 cells were seeded into 6-well plates. Transfection was initiated when the cells reached 20%-30% confluence. The transfection reagent was prepared according to the specified ratio. Specifically, overexpression plasmids for TLR4 and MyD88, as well as the transfection reagent, were diluted in 50 µl of DMEM medium following the manufacturer’s instructions. The diluted plasmids and transfection reagent were mixed and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The mixture was then added to each well, and transfection was carried out for 4 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced, and the cells were treated with LPS and ISL for 24 h before subsequent experiments.

Grouping and modeling of cells

Prior to the experiment, the optimal concentrations of LPS and ISL were preliminarily determined. According to the CCK-8 assay, the experimental concentration of LPS was determined to be 10 µg/mL, and the experimental concentration of ISL was determined to be 1 µM (Supplementary Fig. S1).

The involvement of TLR4 and MyD88 in this protective mechanism was explored through two separate cellular experiments. (1) Caco-2 cells were randomly assigned into five groups: blank control, LPS+TLR4 empty plasmid, LPS+TLR4 plasmid, ISL + LPS, and ISL + LPS+TLR4 plasmid. (2) Caco-2 cells were randomly divided into five groups: blank control, LPS+MyD88 empty plasmid, LPS+MyD88 plasmid, ISL + LPS, and ISL + LPS+MyD88 plasmid.

HE staining of colon tissue

The colon tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and paraffin-embedded, sectioned and stained with HE. Microscopic images of the stained tissues were captured and evaluated using the Histochemistry Score (HI). The HI score included three parameters: the degree of inflammation (1 = mild; 2 = severe), lesion depth (1 = mucosal muscular layer; 2 = muscular layer; 3 = plasma layer), and crypt disruption (1 = 1/3 crypt disruption; 2 = 2/3 crypt disruption; 3 = intact surface epithelium only; 4 = complete epithelial disruption).

Real-time PCR (RT-PCR) for the assay of inflammatory factors

Colon tissue samples (20–25 mg) were homogenized utilizing a tissue homogenizer, and total RNA was extracted from the homogenate utilizing an RNA extraction kit (ACCURATA, Hunan, China). After drug administration and stimulation, the supernatant was removed, and total RNA was extracted from Caco-2 cells using an RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ABclonal, Wuhan, China).

The isolated RNA was subsequently converted into cDNA with a reverse transcription kit (ACCURATA, Hunan, China). The SYBR Green kit (ACCURATA, Hunan, China) was used for sample preparation, and quantitative PCR was carried out on an ABI 7500 system. The reaction conditions were set as follows: an initial step at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 5 s, repeated for 40 cycles. Afterward, the relative mRNA expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in colonic tissues were calculated utilizing the 2-ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences were: TNF-α(forward, CTCATGCACCACCATCAAGG; reverse, ACCTGACCACTCTCCCTTTG); IL-1β(forward, CTTTCCCGTGGACCTTCCAG; reverse, AATGGGAACGTCACACACCA); IL-6 (forward, TGCCTTCTTGGGACTGATGC; reverse, TGAAGTCTCCTCTCCGGACT); GAPDH (forward, ACGGCAAGTTCAACGGCACAG; reverse, GAAGACGCCAGTAGACTCCACGAC).

Immunohistochemical analysis of tight junction protein expression (ZO-1, Claudin-1, Occludin) in colon tissue

Paraffin-embedded colon sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to 3% H₂O₂ to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After PBS washing, antigen retrieval was conducted using high-pressure thermal methods. The sections were then treated with non-immune goat serum for blocking and incubated overnight with primary antibodies against ZO-1, Claudin-1, and Occludin. Sections were incubated with secondary antibodies from an immunohistochemical kit, followed by staining with DAB and counterstaining with hematoxylin. Finally, sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted in neutral resin. Staining results were visualized under a microscope, and images were captured for analysis.

Western blot analysis of TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway proteins and colonic barrier protein expression

Colon tissues were homogenized, and the supernatant was obtained. Caco-2 cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer at 4 °C to extract total protein from each group. The lysate was transferred to EP tubes and centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min. Protein concentration was evaluated utilizing the BCA protein assay kit. Proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes through the wet transfer technique. After blocking the membranes with skimmed milk for 1 h at room temperature, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting TLR4, MyD88, NF-κB P65, P-NF-κB P65, ZO-1, Claudin-1, and Occludin. The membranes were then washed with TBST the following day and incubated with enzyme-conjugated secondary antibodies on a shaker at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and analyzed utilizing ImageJ software for quantification.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing

After euthanizing the mice, genomic DNA was isolated from their colon contents. The extracted DNA underwent quality assessment via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, followed by quantification of its concentration and purity. The DNA was then used as a template for PCR amplification targeting the V3-V4 variable region of the 16 S rRNA gene. After amplification, the PCR products were purified, and library construction was performed, followed by sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform. Raw sequencing data were quality-checked using fastp software and assembled with FLASH software. After assembly, the sequences were clustered, and chimeras were eliminated using UPARSE with a 97% similarity threshold to define operational taxonomic units (OTUs). Taxonomic classification of representative OTU sequences at the phylum and genus levels was conducted with the RDP classifier referencing the Silva 16 S rRNA database (v138) at a 97% similarity threshold.Alpha diversity, beta diversity, and Spearman correlation analyses were conducted utilizing the online platform of Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com), employing tools such as QIIME, R software, Mothur, and the Python LEfSe package.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data for each group were analyzed utilizing one-way ANOVA and multi-factor ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 9.5. Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), with statistical significance set at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Isoliquiritigenin ameliorates general symptoms in UC mice

The control mice exhibited a steady increase in body weight during the experimental period, remained active, and produced well-formed feces. In contrast, after 3 days of exposure to 3% DSS, mice in the DSS group began losing weight, became lethargic, and developed loose stools, resulting in progressively higher DAI scores. On the 4th day, the symptoms of UC in the DSS group worsened, with mice developing bloody stools. In the absence of pharmacological intervention, UC symptoms in the DSS group continued to progress, characterized by scant bloody stools, significant weight loss, and elevated DAI scores. After three days of treatment with ISL and 5-ASA, there was a marked reduction in weight loss, a gradual decrease in bloody stools, and a transition of feces from watery diarrhea to loosely formed stools. DAI scores also significantly declined (Fig. 1A,B). At the conclusion of the experiment, the colon lengths of mice from each group were measured. The DSS group exhibited shorter colons versus the control group (P < 0.01), while treatment with ISL and 5-ASA markedly reduced colon shortening compared to the DSS group (Fig. 1C,D).

Isoliquiritigenin alleviates histopathologic alternations in the colon of UC mice

HE staining revealed that the colon tissues of control mice displayed intact epithelial cells and glands, with well-organized cell structures and no evidence of inflammatory cell infiltration. However, the DSS group showed significant damage to the glandular structure of the colon, with destroyed or absent crypts and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, resulting in a disorganized tissue structure. After treatment with ISL and 5-ASA, a remarkable decrease in inflammatory infiltration was observed, along with the restoration of the glandular structure in the colon (Fig. 2A). Compared with the DSS model group, the ISL intervention significantly reduced histological injury (HI) ratings (P < 0.01), with the high-dose ISL group (ISLH) showing improvements comparable to the positive control group treated with 5-ASA (Fig. 2B).

Subsequently, TLR4 and MyD88 genes were overexpressed in Caco-2 cells. These findings suggest that ISL alleviates ulcerative colitis by suppressing the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway.

Effects of Isoliquiritigenin on the species diversity of gut microbiota (GM) in UC mice

We analyzed the influence of ISL on gut microbial diversity in UC mice using α-diversity and β-diversity metrics. The Shannon index, which reflects species richness and the evenness of species distribution, showed that higher values indicate greater richness and evenness. Conversely, the Simpson index reflects the likelihood that two consecutive individuals sampled from a community belong to the same species, with higher values suggesting lower species richness. These results highlighted that the Shannon index in the DSS group was significantly decreased (P < 0.05), while the Simpson index was significantly increased (P < 0.01). After ISL intervention, the Shannon index elevated, while the Simpson index diminished versus the DSS group. The most pronounced effects were noted in the high-dose ISL group (P < 0.01, P < 0.05), with values approaching those of the Control group (Fig. 5A,B). Additionally, the flat trend of the Shannon and Simpson dilution curves indicated that the sample size was adequate (Fig. 5C, D). Subsequent analyses, including PCoA (Fig. 5E), NMDS (Fig. 5F), and PLS-DA (Fig. 5G), highlighted significant variations in the intestinal microbiota composition between the DSS group and both the Control and ISL-treated groups. Notably, the ISL-treated mice showed microbiota composition patterns more closely resembling the Control group, showing a clear dose-dependent response. The high-dose ISL intervention had a more pronounced effect on increasing intestinal microbial diversity in mice.

Discussion

ISL, a flavonoid derived from licorice root, exhibits notable anticancer, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties18. Animal studies have demonstrated that ISL can reduce inflammation associated with obesity by inhibiting innate immune sensors such as TLR4 and NLRP3 inflammasomes19. ISL may also provide neuroprotective effects by mitigating brain inflammation and reducing neuronal damage20. Previous research has shown that ISL can alleviate UC symptoms and reduce inflammation16,17. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms of ISL in UC treatment remained unexplored. In this study, ISL treatment significantly reduced DSS-induced colitis symptoms, including weight loss, elevated DAI scores, and colonic shortening. Histopathological analysis revealed that ISL treatment reduced colonic tissue damage, including glandular structural disruption, inflammatory cell infiltration, and crypt destruction, compared to the DSS group. The results highlight that ISL has potential therapeutic effects in UC. Furthermore, this study offers insights into the potential molecular mechanisms through which ISL ameliorates UC.

A persistent inflammatory response in the intestinal mucosa is the primary feature of UC21. Common therapeutic drugs for UC used in clinical practice primarily target by neutralizing and inhibiting inflammatory factors22. Therefore, targeting key inflammatory regulators is crucial for effective UC treatment. NF-κB, a major inflammatory regulator, is closely linked to the transcription and expression of inflammatory mediators23. The TLR4 receptor, a critical member of the TLR family, is essential for initiating the innate immune response. Studies have demonstrated that TLR4 expression is upregulated in UC, activating NF-κB through the MyD88-dependent pathway, triggering an inflammatory cascade and exacerbating tissue damage24. Furthermore, studies have indicated that natural compounds like alkaloids from medicinal roots and Crocus sativus can alleviate inflammation associated with ulcerative colitis by regulating the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway25,26. Western blot analysis revealed an upregulation of TLR4/MyD88/ P-NF-κB proteins in the DSS-induced UC model. ISL treatment led to a substantial and dose-dependent suppression of these pathway proteins. In the LPS-treated UC cell model, overexpression of TLR4 and MyD88 was demonstrated to abrogate the protective effects of ISL. Therefore, ISL alleviates UC by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway.

Beyond its role in modulating inflammation, ISL offers notable advantages in protecting intestinal barrier function. Intestinal epithelial tight junctions are essential for safeguarding the barrier and blocking the entry of harmful substances and pathogens27. Disruption or loss of tight junctions compromises the intestinal barrier, increasing mucosal permeability and triggering inflammation and tissue damage. This dysfunction is an essential factor in UC development, contributing to symptoms such as diarrhea and bleeding in patients28,29. Research has demonstrated that tight junction protein expressions are significantly reduced in UC patients, resulting in impaired intestinal barrier function and increased mucosal permeability30. Moreover, inflammatory factors have been shown to inhibit the intestinal epithelial tight junction protein expression (ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudins), further impairing barrier function and elevating intestinal permeability. This disruption exacerbates the inflammatory response, creating a vicious cycle31. Immunohistochemistry and Western blot analyses confirmed that ISL treatment restored the expression of intestinal tight junction proteins in DSS-induced UC mice. Notably, in the high-dose group, the restoration of tight junction protein expression was comparable to the 5-ASA-treated positive control group. These results indicate that ISL restores intestinal barrier integrity in UC mice, thereby preventing further deterioration caused by inflammatory factors and responses.

In recent years, numerous studies have demonstrated that imbalances in the GM play a key role in the pathophysiology of UC and are closely linked to disease severity, remission phases, and acute flare-ups32,33. Currently, it remains unclear whether dysbiosis is the cause or consequence of intestinal inflammation, but evidence suggests that GM plays a crucial role in regulating the host immune system and intestinal inflammation34. Alterations in microbial diversity and disruptions in the balance between beneficial and harmful bacteria can impair intestinal integrity and increase the production of pro-inflammatory metabolites, thereby exacerbating intestinal barrier dysfunction and contributing to the progression of UC35,36. In this study, DSS-induced UC mice displayed a marked decline in microbial diversity, marked by a reduction in obligate anaerobes and an elevation in facultative anaerobes, such as Escherichia-Shigella and Enterococcus. In healthy individuals, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota are the predominant phyla in the gut, accounting for the majority of the microbial population. Firmicutes are primarily linked to dietary fiber decomposition and short-chain fatty acid production, while Bacteroidota aids in energy absorption and storage. The Firmicutes/Bacteroidota (B/F) ratio is often used as an indicator of microbial balance37. In our study, the B/F ratio was notably reduced in DSS-induced UC mice, but ISL treatment effectively restored it, showing a significant increase in the B/F ratio. These results align with clinical studies that report a reduction in Firmicutes and an increase in Proteobacteria in the intestines of UC patients38. These findings indicate that ISL can enhance gut microbial composition and restore microbial diversity in DSS-induced UC mice.

norank_f__Muribaculaceae is a common symbiotic bacterium that decomposes dietary fiber, increases short-chain fatty acid content39, and supports the restoration of normal intestinal function40. ISL treatment significantly increased the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as norank_f__Muribaculaceae and inhibited the overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria like Escherichia-Shigella, Bacteroides, and Enterococcus. This regulatory effect may help protect the intestinal mechanical barrier from damage by pathogenic bacteria and reduce inflammation by strengthening the biological barrier function of the gut41. Additionally, an interesting phenomenon was observed in this study regarding Lactobacillus, a traditional probiotic commonly used alongside conventional UC treatments or in combination with other probiotics to alleviate intestinal inflammation in UC patients42,43. However, in our study, Lactobacillus was the predominant bacterial group in the DSS group, but ISL treatment diminished its growth advantage, a finding inconsistent with previous studies44. This discrepancy may be linked to the gut’s self-regulatory response under stress. In the early stages of UC, the body may stimulate the growth of beneficial bacteria like Lactobacillus to counter the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria. Therefore, future studies could extend the DSS modeling period and monitor changes in Lactobacillus abundance over time to better understand the involvement of Lactobacillus in UC pathology.

The modulation of GM has been recognized as a critical factor in both the progression and remission of UC, especially by reducing the intestinal inflammatory response and enhancing barrier function, thereby improving the disease outcomes45. In this study, Spearman’s correlation analysis further confirmed the relationship between specific microbial species and UC. We observed that beneficial bacteria, such as Muribaculaceae, had a negative association with pro-inflammatory markers and a positive link with colonic tight junction proteins, highlighting that beneficial bacteria may facilitate intestinal barrier repair by reducing the release of inflammatory mediators. Conversely, harmful bacteria like Escherichia-Shigella were positively linked to pro-inflammatory markers and negatively associated with tight junction proteins, indicating that these pathogenic bacteria play a crucial role in sustaining inflammation and disrupting barrier function. These findings suggest that ISL exerts its protective effects against UC by modulating the GM, increasing beneficial bacterial populations, and suppressing pathogenic bacteria.

Although this study has yielded promising results, several limitations remain. Firstly, a single UC model was used in this study. The efficacy of ISL will be verified in another UC model in the future. Secondly, A preliminary validation was conducted in a cellular model by overexpressing TLR4 and MyD88 to confirm that ILS alleviates ulcerative colitis (UC) through the inhibition of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway. To further validate the precise role of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway in the mechanism of action of ISL, agonists or inhibitors were not employed in the animal model. Future research should focus on introducing specific pathway modulators to elucidate the molecular mechanisms involved in UC. Moreover, this study did not involve a direct assessment of the bioavailability, metabolic stability, or colonic targeting efficiency of ISL. The optimal therapeutic dosage, direct molecular targets and its potential long-term toxicity require further investigation. Future studies should incorporate detailed pharmacokinetic analysis and formulation development to further enhance the therapeutic potential of ISL and promote its clinical translation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings indicate that ISL possesses significant therapeutic potential against UC, as evidenced in the DSS-induced model where a high dose (100 mg/kg) was as effective as 5-ASA. The collective results suggest that ISL attenuates the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, while concurrently enhancing intestinal barrier function and beneficially modulating the gut microbiota. Future studies should focus on elucidating the precise molecular targets of ISL and assessing its therapeutic value across diverse models of UC.
