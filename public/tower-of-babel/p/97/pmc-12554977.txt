Tear fluid proteomics: a comparative study of DIA and DDA mass spectrometry
Authors: Saleh Ahmed, Jeremy Altman, Garrett Jones, Drew Mayernik, Eliza Williams, Amr Mahmoud, Tae Jin Lee, Wenbo Zhi, Shruti Sharma, Ashok Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Tear fluid is a biological fluid that plays critical roles in maintaining ocular surface homeostasis and lubrication, as well as providing protection against environmental pathogens [1,2]. Despite its small volume (3–10 μL), basal tear fluid has a highly complex composition of electrolytes, mucins, metabolites, and a diverse array of proteins and lipids [3]. While the majority of the tear fluid composition originates from the secretion of acinar cells within the lacrimal gland, other sources include meibomian glands, goblet cells, and epithelial cells in the cornea and conjunctiva [4]. Additionally, tear fluid contains components derived from blood filtrate that reflect systemic physiological and pathological states [5]. These characteristics make tear fluid a valuable medium for studying systemic health and serving as a proximal biomarker for ocular surface conditions. Consequently, tear fluid has been studied for the identification of protein biomarkers associated with a wide range of diseases such as Alzheimer's disease [6], Parkinson’s disease [7,8], Huntington’s disease [9], multiple sclerosis [[10], [11], [12]], diabetic retinopathy [13,14], cancer [15,16], glaucoma [17,18], age-related macular degeneration [19,20] and dry eye disease [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. These studies underscore the diagnostic potential of tear proteins in both ocular and systemic conditions.

However, the tear proteome presents unique analytical challenges due to its limited volume, high inter-individual variability, and wide dynamic range in protein concentrations.

Mass spectrometry (MS) is a powerful technique widely used in proteomics to analyze complex biological samples, such as tear fluid. Global tear proteome analysis primarily relies on liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using data-dependent acquisition (DDA) and data-independent acquisition (DIA) approaches. The most commonly employed MS approach is DDA, which selects the most abundant precursor ions detected in the MS1 survey scan for subsequent fragmentation and analysis. Although DDA is highly effective, its inherent sampling bias toward high-abundance proteins presents a challenge for the reproducible quantification of low-abundance peptides, as is often the case in tear fluid [[25], [26], [27]]. The implementation of dynamic exclusion settings may mitigate the problem to some extent, but it does not fully resolve the fundamental limitations of precursor ion selection in DDA.

Recent technological advancements have greatly enhanced DIA MS, making it a robust alternative to DDA. DIA methods allow for the complete isolation and fragmentation of all generated precursor ions by cycling through predefined m/z windows across the entire survey scan range, thereby improving reproducibility in protein identification [25,[28], [29], [30], [31]]. This comprehensive approach is especially valuable in studies with large sample sizes, where missing data from DDA can severely compromise the reproducibility and reliability of results, particularly in complex biological samples.

Although DIA holds great promise, comprehensive comparisons with DDA in tear proteomics remain limited. To address this gap, we conducted a direct comparison of DIA and DDA acquisition strategies to evaluate their effectiveness in profiling the tear fluid proteome. We assessed protein identifications, data completeness, quantification accuracy, and reproducibility to better understand the strengths and limitations of each approach. These insights are critical for advancing tear proteomics and its applications in biomarker discovery. Importantly, this study establishes a reliable and reproducible workflow to support future investigations in tear fluid proteomics.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Samples were obtained from 15 healthy individuals (8 males and 7 females), aged between 23 and 54 years (Table 1). Participants were screened for any history of ocular disease, acute illness, chronic autoimmune disorders, current contact lens use, recent ocular surgeries (within the past two months), or the use of topical medications within the past 24 h. Protein digestion and sample preparation

The in-strip protein digestion method was used for protein extraction and digestion, as previously described [32]. Schirmer strips were lyophilized and cut into 5 mm × 2.5 mm pieces. Proteins were denatured using 120 µL of 8 M urea in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol at 60 °C for 30 min, and alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer was added to dilute the urea. The pH of each sample was adjusted to 8 using pH indicator strips (range 0–14; #13640516, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mass spectrometry-grade trypsin (#90057, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added at a 1:20 trypsin-to-protein (w/w) ratio, and digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C.

Following digestion, peptide concentrations were quantified using the Pierce Quantitative Colorimetric Peptide Assay (#23275, Thermo Fisher Scientific), yielding final concentrations between 200–250 µg/mL. An aliquot containing 125 µg of peptide from each sample was purified using C18 spin columns, followed by lyophilization. Purified peptides were reconstituted in 80 µL of equilibration buffer (2 % acetonitrile, 0.1 % formic acid).

To ensure a fair comparison between methods and eliminate sample-to-sample variability, a single pooled sample was prepared for LC-MS/MS analysis by combining 20 µL from each of the 15 purified samples. The final concentration of the pooled peptide sample was standardized to 0.2 µg/µL for consistent analysis.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis using DDA and DIA

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using an Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid mass spectrometer coupled with an Ultimate 3000 nano-UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Both DDA and DIA methods were evaluated for protein identification, reproducibility, and data completeness. For analysis, 5 µL (∼1 µg) of pooled, reconstituted peptides were loaded onto a PepMap100 C18 trap column (5 µm, 0.3 × 5.0 mm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and washed at a flow rate of 20 µL/min.

Peptide separation was performed using a PepMap100 RSLC C18 analytical column (2.0 µm, 75 µm × 150 mm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a multistep gradient ranging from 2 % to 40 % acetonitrile in 0.1 % formic acid. The gradient was applied over 150 min for DDA and 100 min for DIA. The column was maintained at 40 °C with a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1 % formic acid in water, while mobile phase B contained 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile. LC-MS/MS was conducted in positive ion mode using a nano-electrospray ionization source, with a spray voltage of 2000 V and an ion transfer tube temperature of 300 °C.

DDA acquisition

An Orbitrap MS was used to perform full MS scans at a resolution of 120,000, with a maximum injection time of 50 ms, covering an m/z range of 400 to 1600. Precursor peptide ions were fragmented using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy of 30 % and an isolation window of 1.6 m/z. Only peptide ions with charge states ranging from + 1 to + 7 were selected for fragmentation. MS/MS analysis was performed using an ion trap mass spectrometer (ITMS) analyzer, with a dynamic exclusion time of 15 s, a repeat count of 1, advanced peak determination (APD) enabled, and a cycle time of 3 s.

DIA acquisition

In DIA, the precursor mass scan was performed at a resolution of 60,000, with a maximum injection time of 58 ms and a standard AGC target mode, covering the m/z range of 400–1200. This was followed by 40 MS2 scans spanning the range of 300–1500 m/z. Precursor ion fragmentation was performed using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) at a normalized collision energy of 30 %. The isolation window was set to 20 m/z, and the cycle time was fixed at 3 s using the loop control function to ensure consistent acquisition duration, even when the ion flux drops and the AGC target cannot be promptly reached.

Assessment of quantification accuracy using serial dilutions

To assess quantification accuracy, an E. coli digest (Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA) was mixed with tear peptides to create a complex biological matrix. Equal concentrations (0.2 µg/µL) of E. coli digest and tear peptides were combined and subjected to serial dilutions (2-, 4-, and 8-fold), using E. coli digest as the diluent. Triplicates of 5 µL (∼1 µg) peptide mixtures from each dilution were analyzed using the same LC gradient under both DDA and DIA modes.

Mass spectrometric data processing

Mass spectrometric raw data from the DDA approach were processed using Proteome Discoverer 2.5 (PD 2.5) against curated protein databases. For human tear fluid samples, the UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot human protein database (downloaded on 07/10/2024, containing 20,412 entries) was used. For mixed samples of human tear fluid and E. coli peptides, the human and E. coli (downloaded on 07/10/2024, containing 4,401 entries) databases were combined. Search parameters included a precursor mass tolerance of 10 ppm, product ion mass tolerance of 0.6 Da, fixed carbamidomethylation (+57.021 Da) of cysteine, variable oxidation (+15.995 Da) of methionine, a maximum of one missed cleavage, and a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1 % for both peptides and proteins. The Percolator node was used to validate the proteomic data.

For DIA analysis, raw MS data were processed using DIA-NN (version 1.9) [33] with default parameters. An in silico DIA-NN–predicted spectral library was generated from the human UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot 2024 database (20,412 entries). For mixed samples, a combined spectral library was created using the respective human and E. coli UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot protein databases. DIA-NN parameters included Trypsin/P as the protease, allowance for one missed cleavage, N-terminal methionine excision, carbamidomethylation of cysteine, gene-level protein inference, and the Quant UMS (high precision) quantification approach. Additionally, the options “No shared spectra,” “Heuristic protein inference,” and “MBR” were enabled.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed using Microsoft Excel (Office 365), GraphPad Prism (version 10), and R (version 4.3.1). Missing values in the dataset were assigned a value of “0,” except when calculating the coefficient of variation (CV), in which case missing values were excluded rather than imputed. Assigning missing values as “0” can lead to artificially inflated CVs, while imputing with the mean can underestimate variability; neither approach accurately reflects the true data distribution [25]. CVs were calculated at both the protein and peptide levels using peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) for DDA and peak intensities for DIA.

Correlation analyses were conducted at both the protein and peptide levels to assess consistency between DIA and DDA acquisition strategies. To achieve this, PSM counts obtained from DDA were log2-transformed to standardize the distribution and reduce variance. Similarly, peak intensities acquired from DIA were log2-transformed after dividing raw intensity values by 100,000 to scale them appropriately. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated to evaluate the linear relationship between DIA-based peak intensities and DDA-based PSM counts. Statistical significance was assessed via a two-tailed t-test, with p-values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Spike-in (E. coli digest) normalization was applied to both DIA and DDA datasets to evaluate quantification accuracy. Following normalization, a log2 transformation was performed to standardize the data for downstream analysis. Kendall’s rank correlation coefficient (Tau) was calculated to evaluate monotonic trends in protein abundance across serial dilution conditions. Tau values range from − 1, indicating a perfect inverse relationship, to + 1, signifying a perfect direct correlation. Values close to 0 indicate the absence of a monotonic trend. A two-tailed hypothesis test was used to determine statistical significance, with p-values less than 0.05 considered significant.

Results

DIA identified a total of 701 unique proteins and 2,444 peptides, outperforming DDA, which detected 396 proteins and 1,447 peptides (Figs. 2A, B). On average, DIA identified 641 proteins and 2,236 peptides per replicate, compared to 235 proteins and 989 peptides with DDA. Evaluation of quantification accuracy

We assessed the quantification performance of DDA and DIA workflows by analyzing changes in protein abundance across serial dilutions of pooled human tear fluid samples, using E. coli digest as a diluent to mimic a complex biological matrix. Each dilution was analyzed in triplicate using LC-MS/MS in both DDA and DIA modes. For comparison, only proteins consistently detected in all three replicates of the undiluted (base) pooled sample were included (n = 96 proteins). The filtered dataset is provided in Supplementary File S1.

Kendall’s Tau was used to assess the monotonic trend in protein abundances across the dilution series. The DIA dataset exhibited a stronger negative correlation (Tau = − 0.7636, p < 0.001) compared to DDA (Tau = − 0.6069, p < 0.001), indicating that DIA better preserved the expected dilution-dependent trend. Overall, the DIA workflow detected a greater number of proteins at lower dilution levels compared to DDA. In addition, DIA data showed narrower dispersion across the dilution series, indicating more consistent quantification, particularly for low-abundance proteins (Fig. 6). Discussion

Human tear fluid is a complex biological matrix composed of proteins spanning a wide dynamic range, from highly abundant to extremely low concentrations [34,35]. Nearly 90 % of the total tear protein content is contributed by just 20 highly abundant proteins [36], posing a significant analytical challenge, as signals from low-abundance proteins are often masked by their high-abundance counterparts, making consistent detection difficult.

DDA has been the predominant method used in tear proteomics due to its well-established workflows and straightforward data analysis. However, DDA is inherently biased toward the most abundant ions, making it less effective at consistently identifying low-abundance proteins, which are often of high biological interest [37]. Comprehensive characterization of the tear proteome is crucial for understanding the role of tear fluid in ocular health and advancing biomarker research.

DIA has emerged as a powerful alternative, offering unbiased, reproducible, and high-throughput quantification [25,26]. Unlike DDA, DIA systematically fragments all ions within defined m/z windows, capturing MS2 spectra from virtually all detectable peptides. While DIA results in more complex MS2 data, advances in computational tools such as DIA-NN [33], Spectronaut [38], EncyclopeDIA [39], OpenSWATH [40], MaxDIA [41], and DIA-Umpire [42], combined with high-resolution instrumentation, have dramatically improved sequence identification and quantitative reproducibility. DIA’s comprehensive fragmentation approach enables deeper proteome coverage and more consistent detection of low-abundance proteins, which is especially critical in tear fluid, where highly abundant proteins like lactotransferrin and lysozyme can mask potential biomarkers.

In this study, we evaluated a DIA-based LC-MS/MS workflow for tear proteome profiling, benchmarking it against a conventional DDA approach. DIA-NN was selected for data processing due to its high-throughput capabilities, robust FDR control, and superior performance in previous benchmarking studies [43,44].

Our DIA workflow identified 701 unique proteins per sample, nearly three times more than DDA. DIA also yielded more than twice the number of unique peptides, indicating substantially greater proteome coverage. These results not only outperform traditional DDA workflows [8,13,19,23,[45], [46], [47]], but also compare favorably with recent DIA studies, which report 595–669 unique proteins per sample [48,49].

Reproducibility is a key requirement for biomarker discovery and clinical application [50]. Our DIA workflow demonstrated superior reproducibility, with lower coefficients of variation for both proteins and peptides compared to DDA. These findings are consistent with prior studies reporting DIA’s lower technical variability, attributed to its deterministic acquisition strategy that avoids the stochastic sampling inherent to DDA [25,30,31,51]. This level of consistency is particularly valuable in tear proteomics, where sample volume is limited and reproducibility is challenging to achieve [52].

Data completeness, the consistent detection of proteins across replicates, is another area in which DIA excels. DIA-MS acquisition is widely recognized for achieving higher data completeness than other MS methods by reducing the incidence of missing protein intensity values within the data matrix [53,54]. In our study, approximately 80 % of proteins were detected across all replicates using the DIA workflow, compared to 40 % with DDA. Higher data completeness minimizes the need for imputation and improves statistical power in downstream analyses.

Using an E. coli digest as a diluent to simulate a complex biological matrix, we evaluated the performance of DDA and DIA across a range of protein concentrations, focusing on 96 proteins consistently detected in all three replicates of the base sample. DIA not only detected more proteins at lower dilutions but also exhibited reduced variability in protein abundance measurements across the dilution series.

The superior performance of DIA over DDA has been consistently demonstrated across various biological fluids. For example, in plasma proteomics, DIA identified an average of 838 proteins, compared to 236 by DDA [55]. Similarly, in cerebrospinal fluid, DIA yielded 1,574 identified proteins, more than double the 648 identified by DDA [56]. In urine, DIA not only doubled the number of identified peptides and proteins per sample but also achieved significantly lower coefficients of variation (DIA ≈ 8 % vs. DDA ≈ 16 %) [30].

Despite its advantages, DIA’s complexity introduces certain limitations. The dense MS2 spectra increase the risk of false positives [57]. DIA-NN addresses this challenge by incorporating machine learning to enhance peptide identification, manage spectral interferences, and control the false discovery rate [33]. While tools like DIA-NN were instrumental in our study, the continued evolution of DIA data analysis highlights the need for further algorithmic improvements, particularly in the deconvolution of co-eluting peptides, to further reduce false discoveries and improve quantification accuracy.

Moreover, reliance on in silico predicted libraries in DIA can lead to incomplete protein coverage. Developing more comprehensive, condition-specific spectral libraries from experimentally derived tear fluid samples, and validating them across larger cohorts, could enhance the robustness of protein identification. Normalization also remains a challenge in tear proteomics due to variability in protein recovery [58,59]. Incorporating external reference standards or implementing advanced normalization strategies may further improve quantitative accuracy.

Addressing these limitations will enhance the utility of DIA for tear fluid analysis. Future research investigating specific variations in the tear proteome associated with ocular and systemic diseases may enable the identification of unique proteomic signatures linked to disease states, ultimately contributing to the development of personalized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This study highlights the clear advantages of DIA over DDA for tear fluid proteome analysis. DIA offers deeper proteome coverage, superior reproducibility, and greater data completeness, key attributes for robust biomarker discovery and clinical application. By addressing the analytical challenges posed by the dynamic protein range in tear fluid, DIA emerges as a powerful and scalable platform for high-quality proteomics. Future efforts should aim to optimize DIA workflows, improving normalization strategies, and developing standardized protocols to accelerate its adoption in tear fluid research and beyond.
