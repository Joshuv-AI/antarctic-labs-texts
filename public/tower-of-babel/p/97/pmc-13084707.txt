Bedroom Air Filtration and Home Blood Pressures of Older Adults Living in a Residential Facility
Authors: Robert D. Brook, Yao Song, Robert L. Bard, Lu Wang, Zachary M. Klaver, Rosemary A. Ziemba, Geoffrey D. Barnes, Alec J. Brook, Linda Keilman, Masako Morishita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPortable air cleaners (PACs) can reduce blood pressure (BP) by decreasing fine particulate matter (PM2.5) air pollution exposure. However, the effectiveness of a single PAC in a bedroom and the impact on self‐measured home BPs over 4 weeks have not been evaluated. We conducted a randomized‐order double‐blind cross‐over trial of active PAC (HEPA filter in‐place) versus sham (no filter) intervention in the bedroom of older adults living in a senior residential facility from 1/11/2021‐11/4/2024. Each study limb lasted 4 weeks with a 2‐week washout period. Participants measured triplicate home BPs in the mornings and evenings during weeks 1 and 4. Bedroom PM2.5 concentrations were continuously monitored, and 24‐h personal exposures were determined during week 4. Results from 33 participants (74.9 ± 7.7 years old) completing the trial were analyzed. Active PAC intervention reduced bedroom PM2.5 concentrations (1.3±1.5 vs. 8.3±12.8 µg/m3, p < 0.001) and 24‐h personal exposures (5.1 ± 4.6 vs. 12.4±15.6 µg/m3, p < 0.001) versus sham. Average home systolic BP levels did not differ during active PAC versus sham intervention in the full study cohort (127.0 ± 16.2 vs. 126.5 ± 16.0 mm Hg, p = 0.59). However, having a larger body mass index plus higher baseline systolic BP, older age plus higher baseline systolic BP, and older age plus larger body mass index showed trends for modifying the benefits by active PAC. We showed for the first time that air filtration in the bedroom alone can successfully reduce PM2.5 exposures. While systolic BPs were not lowered over 4 weeks in the entire cohort, some individuals may have benefited.

Introduction

Fine particulate matter <2.5 µm in diameter (PM2.5) air pollution is a leading cause of global cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1]. One important pathway is PM2.5‐induced elevations in arterial blood pressure (BP) and the chronic promotion of hypertension. Numerous studies have elucidated a variety of underlying mechanisms including systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, autonomic imbalance, activation of the hypothalamic pituitary adrenal axis, vascular dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and renal disease [2, 3]. From a global public health perspective, this pro‐hypertensive response is highly relevant. PM2.5 levels above World Health Organization guidelines account for an increase in the worldwide systolic BP average by roughly 2.4 mm Hg [4].

Several personal‐level actions have been promoted as supplements to air quality regulations to help protect the public from the adverse health effects of PM2.5 [5]. A growing body of research has shown that the use of indoor portable air cleaners (PACs) with high efficiency particulate arresting (HEPA) filters can decrease exposure to PM2.5 by 50–70% and thereby improve health outcomes. In this regard, the latest meta‐analysis of 17 trials (880 participants) showed that PAC usage lowers systolic BP by 2–4 mm Hg [6]. We previously demonstrated that PACs positioned in the bedroom and main living space of adults in a senior facility in Detroit Michigan led to a reduction of systolic BP by 3.2 mm Hg over 3 days [7, 8].

However, several key issues pertaining to the clinical utility of air filtration as an approach to lower BP remained unresolved. First, the largest proportion of 24‐h personal PM2.5 exposure reduction in our prior trial occurred during nighttime [7, 8]. This suggests that a single PAC in the bedroom may be an effective but less burdensome and expensive strategy. Second, the BP‐lowering response from air filtration has largely been studied over a few days [6, 7]. The persistence of benefits of longer time periods, even up to 4‐weeks, has rarely been assessed. Third, the impact on self‐measured home BP averages – more reliable indicators of day‐to‐day BP levels and superior predictors of cardiovascular risk – has yet to be documented [6, 9, 10]. We therefore designed the Reducing Air Pollution In the Detroit‐area Study 2 (RAPIDS‐2) to address these key clinically relevant questions. We conducted the trial in a cohort of lower‐income older adults which represents a population that is both susceptible (i.e., at greater risk for adverse health effects) and vulnerable (i.e., faces higher levels of exposures) to PM2.5 exposures [11, 12].

Methods

Statement of Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Michigan and nationally registered (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04103346). All participants signed an informed consent document prior entering the study.

Trial Overview

The original trial began 2/24/2020 but was halted after 3/30/2020 due to COVID‐19 pandemic mandates. The trial was then redesigned to minimize in‐person contact between research staff and participants and ensure that all research activities were conducted in compliance with COVID‐19 mandates (e.g., safe distances, staff and participants wearing facemasks). This necessitated a simplification of design to feasibly meet study enrollment and protocol timelines given new barriers to completing the protocol. Several secondary endpoints (e.g., blood biomarkers, arterial compliance), supplementary assessments of BP changes (i.e., BP measured by research staff using automated professional devices and 24‐h ambulatory BP monitoring) and a third study limb (PACs positioned in two rooms) were removed. The simplified trial was launched on 1/11/2021 and continued until completion on 11/4/2024. During much of the trial period, participants were required by government mandates and residence policies to wear facemasks when inside the building expect for while in their own apartment.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults living full‐time (with no planned travel) in a low‐income older‐adult residential facility (containing 150 individual one‐bedroom apartments) located within 200 m of a 4‐lane major highway in Ypsilanti, Michigan. Information fliers to participate in the study were left at the doorway of all 150 apartments (for both the original and redesigned studies) and in common areas of the facility. Exclusion criteria included current smoking by self‐report, anyone smoking inside participants’ apartments (i.e., secondhand smoke exposure), use of continuous positive airway pressure machines or oxygen during sleep, cancer undergoing treatment, a cardiovascular disease event within the past 3 months, class 3 or worse angina or heart failure, lung disease requiring oxygen, documented uncontrolled hypertension (≥160/100 mm Hg) during past 3 months, dialysis, or a change in BP‐lowering or diabetes medications within the past month.

Study Protocol

Participants meeting all study entry criteria were randomized into a double‐blind, randomized order, cross‐over trial comparing active versus sham PAC intervention. Each study limb lasted 4 weeks with a 2‐week washout period before beginning the second limb. Prior to study limbs, a research coordinator educated participants about the protocol and positioned the randomized blinded PAC and indoor PM2.5 monitor on the opposite sides of the bedroom. On day 1 (Monday morning) of each limb, participants were instructed to turn on the PAC and indoor PM2.5 monitor and keep them both continuously running throughout each 4‐week study limb. During week‐4 of each limb, participants were provided with a small battery‐powered monitor to measure personal PM2.5 concentrations. At the end of each study limb, a study coordinator collected all equipment, including the home BP and PM2.5 monitors for data collection. After the 2‐week washout period, the identical trial procedure was followed in the second limb except the opposite randomized blinded PAC intervention was provided to the participants. Participants were instructed not to make any significant lifestyle (e.g., diet, exercise) or medication changes (e.g., new anti‐hypertensive) which might alter BP during the trial. They were instructed to not travel or sleep away from their residence during this time period as well. Participants asked to notify the research staff if any such changes occurred or were planned.

PACs and PM2.5 Monitoring

Each PAC was a Holmes console model for large rooms (HAP8650B‐NU‐1; https://www.holmesproducts.com/air‐purifiers/). For the active intervention a true‐HEPA filter was installed in the device, removing >99.97 of airborne particles at 0.3 µm. During sham intervention the HEPA filter was previously removed. There was no discernible difference (visual, weight, noise level) between active versus sham PAC. PACs were also sealed to prevent tampering or opening. PACs were positioned 1–2 feet away from a wall, not underneath any furniture, and on the opposite side of the bedroom from the PM2.5 monitor. The PAC fan speed was set to run continuously at a mid‐level; however, a few participants were allowed to lower the fan speed to reduce noise to maintain study retention.

Indoor and outdoor PM2.5 monitors were a PurpleAir Flex air quality monitors (https://www2.purpleair.com/products/purpleair‐flex). Indoor monitors were positioned on a mount 1–2 feet away from a wall and 5–6 feet above floor level in participants’ bedrooms. Outdoor monitors were positioned on the roof of the facility. Semi‐continuous (5‐min average) time‐stamped PM2.5 levels were recorded on the SD card of the devices during the trial. The data were downloaded for analysis after each limb. The average PM2.5 concentration over each entire 4‐week long limb served as the indoor and outdoor exposure outcomes for that limb.

Personal PM2.5 monitoring was conducted for a 24‐h period from Thursday to Friday morning during week 4 of both study limbs using a small battery‐powered particulate monitor (Thermo Scientific pDR‐1500) (https://www.thermofisher.com/order/catalog/product/PDR1500). Participants were instructed to keep the monitor on (or near) their person throughout the daytime and on a nightstand next to their bed while sleeping. The filter (37 mm PTFE) from each monitor was collected by research staff, and PM2.5 mass (over the 24‐h period) was measured using gravimetric analysis as previously described [7, 8].

Home BP Monitoring

Prior to each study limb, participants were educated by a research coordinator and provided with instructions for proper home BP monitoring in accordance with clinical guidelines [10]. Home BP was measured twice daily, Tuesday through Friday during weeks 1 and 4 of each study limb. The morning measurement session was conducted immediately after waking‐up, after using the bathroom and prior to any caffeine or breakfast. The evening session was conducted immediately before going to bed. Both sessions were conducted prior to taking any BP medications. The monitor was an Omron series‐10 (https://omronhealthcare.com/products/10‐series‐upper‐arm‐blood‐pressure‐monitor‐bp7465) device. Participants were instructed to rest alone for 5 min in a quiet room (e.g., no television) seated in a chair with legs uncrossed and feet flat on the floor. Participants were instructed to fit the BP cuff around their bare upper left arm and to rest their forearm on a table (i.e., upper arm positioned at approximately heart level). Participants next turned on the BP monitor, and the device thereafter automatically measured three sequential BP readings at 1‐min intervals. The monitor recorded into memory (date and time stamped) each BP reading as well as the triplicate average for each session. Participants were also instructed to keep a log for back‐up of the average BP levels for each session.

Safety

Discontinuation of study participation was planned if any week‐long average of home BP levels was ≥160 mm Hg systolic and/or ≥100 mm Hg diastolic. Participants were asked to notify the research staff if their healthcare provider recommended starting a new anti‐hypertensive medication or increasing the dose of existing medication during the trial period. This would also require discontinuation from the trial until BP levels and medications were stable for at least 3 months.

Statistical Methods

Analyses included participants who completed both 4‑week study limbs with valid home BP and PM2.5 measurements; participants with protocol non‐completion or implausible extreme values consistent with measurement error were excluded prior to analysis. Baseline participant characteristics were summarized using mean ± standard deviation (SD), with minimum–maximum when appropriate, for continuous variables and number (%) for categorical variables.

Our original sample size calculation for our primary study outcome (comparison of average systolic BP levels between study limbs) was based on 42–44 participants completing the trial (anticipating n = 50 enrolled with 6–8 dropouts). Within the range of the SD of systolic BP measurements from 6.2 to 9.8 mm Hg, the statistical power we can achieve ranges between 72.1% and 97.5% to detect a 3.0 mm Hg change in mean systolic BP. With an expected SD of 8.2 mm Hg, the study had 90.3% power to detect a primary outcome of 3.0 mm Hg.

For systolic BP, we identified outlying data, defined as values ≥2 SD from mean values, with SD calculated after removing the single outlying value. For personal PM2.5, outlying values were detected after manual review of the data suggestive of outlying (≥2 SD) or implausible values (e.g., negative results). Results from participants in one or both study limbs with multiple outlying data points, supporting repetitive inaccurate measurement or capture of BP or PM2.5 results determined by investigator assessment, were removed from analyses. All remaining available data (BP recordings, indoor and 24‐h personal PM2.5 levels) from each participant were included in the analyses.

PM2.5 exposure and BP outcomes were compared between active and sham PAC using paired t‑tests without covariate adjustment per original analysis plan due to the cross‐over study design. For adjusted comparisons of systolic BP between interventions, we fit three separate linear regression models to assess whether the magnitude of systolic BP benefit during active PAC (i.e., ΔSBP) differed for participants with higher age, body mass index (BMI), or a proxy for baseline systolic BP (denoted SBP0); SBP0 was defined as the first available non‐missing day of BP measurement (average of morning and evening sessions) within each time stratum during the sham PAC period. Within each time stratum, we created binary “high” indicators (Agehigh, BMIhigh, SBP0high) using a split at the top tertile (top third vs. lower two‐thirds). The outcome was the within‐subject, week‐specific, time‐specific contrast in weekly mean systolic BP between sham and active PAC in Equation (1): (1) ΔSBP≡SBP¯sham−SBP¯active, where SBP∗ denotes the SBP measures at limb “*”, and the bar (∗¯) denotes the weekly mean value. Thus, ΔSBP >0 indicates lower systolic BP under active PAC (i.e., intervention benefit). Each “indicator model” used ΔSBP as the outcome and included week (1 and 4), time (AM and PM), sex, race, mean‐centered continuous variables (Agec, BMIc, SBP0c), clinical covariates (coronary artery disease, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, aspirin use), and personal and outdoor PM2.5; the model then added one binary indicator (Agehigh or BMIhigh or SBP0high). Robust standard errors were used for inference, and adjusted means were obtained via estimated marginal means.

We fit 3 additional models assessing effect modification that added one 2‐way interaction among centered continuous susceptibility factors: BMIc×SBP0c, Agec×SBP0c, and Agec×BMIc, adjusting for the same covariates as above. Adjusted ΔSBP at the 15th/50th/85th percentiles of the interacting variables was estimated using marginal means and presented with one‐sided 95% confidence intervals (CI). Hypothesis testing was constructed to examine if there is a significant systolic BP reduction with active filtration (equation 2). Under this setup, a one‐sided 95% CI reports the lower confidence bound; evidence of benefit at α = 0.05 is concluded when the lower bound of the CI is above zero. (2) H0:EΔSBP≤0.v.s.H1:EΔSBP.

The interaction models were fit using the entire study population with 3 × 3 panels displaying model‐based adjusted treatment differences evaluated at specified moderator values (15th/50th/85th percentiles), holding other covariates constant.

Results

Study Enrollment

Fifty‐two participants were enrolled in the study. The first six participants exited the trial due to the COVID‐19 research pause. After re‐launch, 46 participants were enrolled with 11 drop‐outs due to: “changed‐mind” (n = 4), “blowing cold air” (n = 1), “noise” (n = 4), “concern about elevated BP” (n = 1), “unable to measure BP” (n = 1). Annual enrolment rates were as follows: 2021 (n = 14); 2022 (n = 10); 2023 (n = 4); 2024 (n = 7). Results from two participants from both limbs were excluded (outlying BP data). Data from one participant during the active study limb (both weeks, outlying PM2.5 data) and from one other participant during the active study limb (week‐1, outlying BP data) were excluded. The excluded outlying data were strongly suggestive of invalid PM2.5 recordings (e.g., monitor malfunction, presence of active or secondhand smoking) or erroneous home BP readings (e.g., errors in measurement). The final sample size for analysis was 33 participants (control limb) and 32 participants (active limb). No patients exited the trial due to reported lifestyle or medication changes, travel, or meeting any trial safety parameters. A CONSORT diagram providing an overview of trial enrollment is provided in the online supplement (Figure S1).

Participants were older adults with a mean baseline systolic BP in the elevated stage and the majority having a history of hypertension treated with medications (Table 1). Two thirds were women, and the mean BMI was in the overweight range.

Predictors of BP‐lowering

Adjusted models evaluating ΔSBP (i.e., intervention benefit) showed trends for lower average BP levels during active PAC (i.e., higher ΔSBP values) in participants in the top tertiles of age (3.8 mm Hg, p = 0.07) and baseline systolic BP levels (1.8 mm Hg, p = 0.21). In an adjusted generalized additive model, ΔSBP levels non‐significantly trended higher as BMI increased. Adjusted models with 2‐way interaction terms evaluating for effect modification demonstrated that having a larger BMI plus higher baseline systolic BP (Figure 1), older age plus higher baseline systolic BP (Figure 2), and older age plus larger body mass index (Figure 3) showed trends for modifying the benefits by active PAC.

Discussion

We demonstrated for the first time that air filtration using a single PAC in the bedroom of older adults residing in a low‐income senior facility can significantly lower indoor PM2.5 concentrations and reduce average personal‐level exposures over a full 24‐h period. The latter was reduced by 7.3 µg/m3 (59%), which was similarly effective as positioning PACs with HEPA filters in both the bedroom and main living space of a residence (52%) [7, 8]. While home BP levels were not decreased in the overall cohort of older adults, a second novel study finding is that some individuals with two susceptibility factors may have benefited from active air filtration over 4 weeks.

Obese participants experienced a more robust systolic BP‐lowering from active PAC intervention in our prior trial [7]. In a recent study by Brugge et al. enrolling younger adults untreated for hypertension living in Eastern Massachusetts, lower systolic BPs were reported in the active PAC limb among individuals with higher baseline levels (>120 mm Hg) [13]. Our current study showed similar results with trends for greater BP‐lowering in participants with higher BMIs and baseline systolic BPs and, additionally, in older adults. Individuals who have higher values for any two out of these three susceptibility factors showed lower systolic BPs over 4 weeks during active PAC intervention. The fact that only certain sensitive individuals benefited is not surprising. Many interventions for high BP yield greater benefits in more hypertensive individuals as well as among select individuals (e.g., sodium reduction in sodium sensitive or obese patients, medication responsiveness based upon renin‐sodium profile) [10]. While our encouraging findings have a sound physiological basis and align with prior results [2, 3], secondary analyses must be interpreted with caution. We believe follow‐up trials specifically designed to test for benefits in key clinical groups are important.

The strengths of our study include the first‐time evaluation of repeated self‐measured home BP readings as the primary outcome—superior predictors of average BP levels and cardiovascular risk [6, 9, 10]. We also employed a longer duration of PAC intervention than most prior trials allowing for an assessment of the persistence of BP‐lowering benefits over 4 weeks [6]. Last, we assessed the effectiveness of air filtration in the bedroom alone, whereas many previous studies (especially in the United States) positioned two PACs in participants’ residences [6, 7, 14]. Personal 24‐h PM2.5 exposures were decreased by a similar degree to our prior studies despite using a 50% less expensive and burdensome intervention [7, 8, 14]. While participants were free to go about their daily routines, average 24‐h exposures were nonetheless reduced by 59% despite the fact that air filtration only occurred in their bedrooms. This is likely because most adults in the United States, especially older individuals, spend the majority of time inside their own residence (and roughly 8–9 h per day sleeping presumably in their bedroom) [15]. The success afforded by a single bedroom PAC may obviate the need for purchasing and deploying multiple units across a household. This has major cost‐effectiveness implications in light of the millions of people who are potential candidates for PAC intervention to help reduce exposures to PM2.5 5.

A minor study limitation is that we did not capture a true baseline BP value (e.g., run‐in period of home BP monitoring) prior to beginning the two study limbs, as our prior study results showed no carry‐over effect of intervention on BP after a 2‐week washout [7]. We therefore could only use a proxy of baseline BP (SBP0) in secondary exploration analyses. Nevertheless, we do not believe these findings would be different if a true baseline BP level was used.

It is uncertain why BP levels were not decreased by the active PAC intervention in the entire cohort as anticipated. The magnitude of PM2.5 exposure reduction (on both relative and absolute scales) is clearly not responsible. It exceeded those in the recent trial by Brugge et al. and was on par with many prior studies including our own [6, 7, 8, 13, 14]. The longer trial duration is not likely responsible (i.e., a real blunting of the BP‐lowering benefits over time exists). Several studies published after our prior trial have reported more sustained BP‐lowering responses from PAC intervention, including that by Brugge et al. conducted over one month [6, 13]. We also did not observe any decrease in BP during the first week of intervention. It is possible that the greater than expected drop‐out rate reduced our statistical power as we anticipated 42–44 participants completing the trial. However, this is not likely a full explanation as we observed no trends toward the expected results. Finally, participant characteristics (i.e., older adults, a high percentage of hypertensive individuals on medications) are also not likely responsible as they closely matched our prior study [7].

It is more probable that limitations of our redesigned protocol stemming from the COVID‐19 pandemic were responsible. First, we were unable to also assess BP by 24‐h ambulatory monitoring and by research quality standardized in‐person measurement as originally planned. This prohibits a direct comparison with previous studies [7]. We also cannot rule out the possibility that real benefits were obfuscated and may have been observed if BP were also determined by these more reliable techniques [6, 7]. One might posit that the elimination of the white coat effect by using home BP readings could be involved. For example, PM2.5‐induced neuro‐physiological stress might in theory increase the white coat effect more than basal BP levels [2, 3]. However, this is also unlikely given that in our prior trial we evaluated the average of the last five of six BP readings taken after 5‐min of rest using a fully‐automated device well‐known to mitigate the white coat effect [7]. While participants were not blinded to home BP readings, this also should not have influenced the results as all individuals were unaware of which study limb they were undergoing. Ultimately, we believe the most plausible explanation for the negative results is that day‐to‐day and inter‐patient home BP levels were more highly‐variable than anticipated (observed SD of 16.0 and 16.2 mm Hg vs. expected of 8.2 mm Hg), most probably driven by sub‐optimal measurement. Many characteristics of our study cohort (low‐income, lower‐education, older adults, high number of comorbidities) are associated with reduced home BP measurement quality [10]. However, COVID‐19 restrictions likely further exacerbated this issue. The effectiveness of educational sessions were hampered (e.g., staff wearing facemasks, inter‐personal distance, reduced time) as were quality control protocols (e.g., no follow‐up visits for troubleshooting or validation of techniques). Altogether, these factors likely led to a lack of precision and accuracy in the primary study outcome, thereby biasing the results toward the null.

Lastly, the universal wearing of facemasks while inside the residential building due to COVID‐19 mandates may have also contributed to the overall negative BP results. Due to their age and health concerns, many participants continued to wear facemasks even after mandates were lifted. However, the extent to which participants wore facemasks inside their own apartment (e.g., living with a spouse) was unfortunately not documented. Facemasks decrease the inhalation of PM2.5 by 50%–95% depending on their quality (e.g., N95 respirators, surgical masks) and fit [16]. The ultimate decrease in PM2.5 mass inhaled over a 24‐h period further depends on adherence. This unavoidable pitfall very likely blunted real differences in PM2.5 actually inhaled by participants between study limbs.

Conclusion

We showed for the first time that a single PAC positioned in the bedroom of older adults residing in a low‐income residential facility located near a major roadway can effectively lower both indoor and 24‐h personal PM2.5 exposures. Home BP levels were not reduced in the overall cohort; however, certain susceptible individuals may have benefited. Further studies evaluating the clinical usefulness of air filtration as a strategy to combat the twin global public health epidemics of hypertension and air pollution are warranted.
