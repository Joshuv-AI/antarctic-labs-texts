Differences in bacterial community composition and diversity in the rhizosphere and surrounding environment of wild soybean (Glycine soja) under different salt stress conditions
Authors: Junpeng Liu, Haiting Ding, Wenjuan Li, Bo Zhou, Liping Zhao, Jun Wang, Shuai Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Yellow River Delta region is densely covered with saline-alkali land. Wild soybean (Glycine soja Siebold & Zucc.), as a typical plant in this area, exhibits notable salt tolerance. Consequently, understanding the composition and function of its rhizosphere microbiota is therefore crucial for deciphering the mechanisms underlying its environmental adaptation.MethodThis study focused on wild soybeans from the Yellow River Delta region as the research subject. Salt stress treatments of varying concentrations were established, and Illumina-based high-throughput 16 S rRNA gene sequencing was employed to investigate the effects of salt stress on the composition and diversity of bacterial communities in both the rhizosphere and surrounding aquatic environments of wild soybeans.ResultsThe findings revealed that salt stress significantly decreased the diversity of bacterial communities in both the rhizosphere and aquatic environments and altered their bacterial community structures. At the phylum level, Proteobacteria predominated across all samples, with a notable upward trend in relative abundance as salinity increased. In the rhizosphere of wild soybeans, Cyanobacteria accumulated progressively with increasing salinity. At the genus level, the relative abundances of Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter significantly increased (p < 0.05) in response to elevated salinity levels. The roots of wild soybeans harbor Pseudomonas, Shewanella, and Rhizobium, which contribute to enhancing salt stress resistance. Research has found that under salt stress conditions, the dominant bacterial communities in the rhizosphere and surrounding environments of wild soybeans may shift toward more salt-tolerant species. These bacteria likely play a critical role in facilitating the adaptation of wild soybeans to saline environments. This study not only deepens our understanding of microbial-mediated salt tolerance mechanisms but also provides valuable technical insights for utilizing saline-alkali lands and breeding stress-tolerant crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04383-9.Keywords: High-throughput sequencing, Salt stress, Glycine max, Bacterial community, Yellow river delta

Introduction

About 7% of the world ‘s land surface is affected by salinization. Soil salinization is one of the main abiotic stress factors affecting agricultural development, threatens global food security by impairing crop productivity and reducing arable land area [1]. This challenge is particularly serious in China, and a large area of saline-alkali land poses a major threat to the sustainable development of ecology and agriculture [2]. Therefore, formulating a strategy for the rational development and utilization of saline-alkali land is of vital importance for ensuring food security [3]. Beyond conventional crop breeding, leveraging plant-microbiome interactions presents a promising avenue for enhancing crop resilience to salinity stress [4]. The rhizosphere is a crucial interface between plant roots and soil, featuring a complex microbial community and playing a significant role in promoting nutrient acquisition and stress relief for plants [5–7]. Some rhizosphere growth-promoting bacteria (PGPR) have been proven to mitigate the impact of salt on plant growth and promote plants’ adaptation to salinity [8]– [9]. For example, Yasmin et al. found that Pseudomonas seudoalcaligenes and Bacillus subtilis can significantly improve the growth of soybeans under salt stress [10]. Pseudomonas can improve the growth of wild soybeans under salt stress [11]. Studies have shown that the growth of plants at higher salinity levels is associated with denser and more complex root microbial communities [12]. Unraveling the adaptive strategies of the rhizosphere microbiome under salt stress can potentially formulate strategies for the utilization of microbial resources. Cultivated soybeans (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) are important economic crops, as one of the sources of oil and protein consumed by humans and livestock, as well as raw materials for biofuel production. Cultivated soybeans contributes the main protein share compared to other crops [13]– [14]. Cultivated soybean is generally considered to be more sensitive to salinity stress than other crops [15]. This limitation underscores the urgent need to explore resources from its wild relatives for crop improvement [16]. Wild soybean (Glycine soja Siebold & Zucc.), an annual twining herb belonging to the genus Glycine in the Fabaceae family, serves as a close relative and ancestor of cultivated soybean. It exhibits valuable traits, including robust stress resistance, high protein content, and favorable yield characteristics, making it an important resource for soybean improvement [17]– [18]. The Yellow River Delta region in China is among the few areas that harbor extensive populations of wild soybeans. The soil in this area is severely salinized and alkalized, with a saline-alkali land area of approximately 440,000 hectares. It enjoys superior geographical conditions, concentrated distribution, and huge potential for agricultural development [19]. Most previous studies have expounded on aspects such as the salt tolerance mechanisms and physiological characteristics of wild soybeans under saline-alkali stress conditions [20]– [21]. However, research on its rhizosphere microbiota (a key component of plant adaptation) is relatively scarce. Furthermore, many studies are conducted in complex soil matrices, making it difficult to disentangle the direct effects of salinity on the microbiome from other soil factors. Consequently, in this study, wild soybeans from the Yellow River Delta region were utilized as experimental materials to conduct hydroponic experiments under different concentrations of salt stress. High-throughput sequencing was employed to investigate the response and short-term dynamics of bacterial communities inhabiting the rhizosphere and environment (the water environment in which wild soybeans grow during the cultivation process) of wild soybean subjected to varying salt stress conditions. Understanding the adaptive responses of the rhizosphere microbiome to salt stress holds promise for developing strategies to harness microbial resources to improve plant growth in saline environments. This knowledge may provide a theoretical reference for the future development and utilization of salt-tolerant microorganisms associated with wild soybeans. It has significant theoretical and practical value for developing saline-alkali land and crop improvement.

Materials and methods

Salt stress wild soybean test

The hydroponic cultivation system utilizing Hoagland’s nutrient solution was selected for this study. It enables precise and uniform control over salt (NaCl) concentration and ionic composition in the root environment. This is crucial for us to study the response of microbial communities under different salt stress conditions, ensuring comparability between treatments and repeatability of results. It eliminates the inherent heterogeneity and confounding factors associated with natural soil matrices (e.g., variable native microbiota, organic matter). Thus, the direct effects of salt stress and plants on bacterial communities can be more clearly analyzed. Hydroponic permits the relatively clean and standardized separation and collection of microbial communities associated with the rhizosphere and environment. The Hoagland nutrient solution provides a completely controllable and uniform supply of mineral nutrients, ensuring that the basic nutrients of all treatments (different salt concentrations) are consistent.

The test material consisted of wild soybeans originating from the coastal saline-alkali land in Binzhou City, Shandong Province, China (37°16′N–38°16′N, 118°20′E–119°20′E). The wild soybean seeds collected in this experiment are preserved in the Herbarium of the School of Biology and Pharmaceutical Engineering at Shandong Aviation University, with the accession number SDUYDZ-001. Uniform, plump seeds free of insect damage were carefully selected and surface-sterilized in a 2% sodium hypochlorite solution for 30 min, followed by thorough rinsing with distilled water. Wild soybean seeds were sown evenly in seedling trays measuring 30 cm × 25 cm × 5 cm and irrigated with water until germination. After the second pair of true leaves had fully expanded, healthy seedlings exhibiting uniform growth were selected and transplanted into a full-strength Hoagland nutrient solution for hydroponic cultivation. Four treatments were established as follows: a control group (CK) consisting exclusively of Hoagland nutrient solution, a low-salt treatment group (LT) supplemented with 100 mmol/L NaCl, a medium-salt treatment group (MT) supplemented with 200 mmol/L NaCl, and a high-salt treatment group (HT) supplemented with 300 mmol/L NaCl [11, 22]. Each treatment was replicated three times, yielding 12 hydroponic boxes, each containing 60 wild soybean seedlings. The hydroponic boxes were positioned in a controlled-temperature climate chamber maintained at 25 °C, with a photoperiod of 16 h of Light, 8 h of darkness, and a Light intensity of 6000 lx (approximately equivalent to a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 108 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). The cultivation process lasted for 21 days. To ensure the stability of the nutrient solution and NaCl concentrations, the nutrient solution with different salt concentrations was reconfigured every 7 days, and the nutrient solution of each group was replaced.

Sample processing and high-throughput 16sRNA amplicon sequencing

After the cultivation of wild soybeans was completed, root samples and water samples were collected from the wild soybean plants. During the sampling procedure, disposable gloves were worn to maintain sterility, and a fresh pair of gloves was donned immediately after each sample collection to avoid cross-contamination between samples. According to the method of by Lakshmanan et al. and Vlasselaer et al. [23]– [24], plant roots were sampled and treated to isolate rhizosphere microorganisms. A total of eight samples were collected per group, which were designated as wild soybean rhizosphere samples (sample identifiers: RootCK1-8, RootLT1-8, RootMT1-8, RootHT1-8), resulting in a total of 32 samples. Following the collection of the root samples, the water from the hydroponic tanks was regarded as the bacterial enrichment Liquid within the rhizosphere microenvironment of wild soybeans. Under sterile conditions, the air pressure of the vacuum filtration system was maintained at 0.02 MPa, and 0.22 μm filter membranes were utilized to filter water from the four experimental groups. The filter membranes were subsequently collected and transferred into sterilized self-sealing bags with clearly labeled identifiers. Eight samples were obtained for each group, designated as water environment samples (sample numbers: WaterCK1-8, WaterLT1-8, WaterMT1-8, WaterHT1-8), resulting in a total of 32 samples. Upon completion of sample collection for each type, all samples were uniformly placed in clean large self-sealing bags, marked with clearly labeled tags, and stored in a −80 °C freezer for long-term preservation.

By extracting DNA from various samples, genomic DNA of the rhizosphere and water environment flora associated with wild soybeans was obtained for 16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis. Total genomic DNA was isolated from wild soybean rhizosphere samples and water environment samples using the Powersoil DNA Kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The integrity and quality of the extracted DNA were evaluated via 1.8% agarose gel electrophoresis. The concentration and purity of DNA were determined by NanoDrop 2000 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA) at 260 nm and 280 nm wavelengths. The hypervariable V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). Both forward and reverse primers were tagged with sample-specific Illumina index sequences for deep sequencing. PCR was conducted in a total reaction volume of 10 µL, comprising 5–50 ng of DNA template, 0.3 µL of each primer (10 µM), 5 µL of KOD FX Neo buffer, 2 µL of dNTPs (2 mM each), 0.2 µL of KOD FX Neo polymerase, and ddH2O to adjust the final volume to 10 µL. The PCR cycling conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 20 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 40 s, concluding with a final extension step at 72 °C for 7 min. The amplified products were purified using the Omega DNA Purification Kit and quantified using the Qsep-400 system. The amplicon library’s paired-end sequencing (2 × 250) was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Beijing Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Sequencing process and data analysis

The initial filtering of the raw data was performed using Trimmomatic (version 0.33), based on the quality scores of single nucleotide [25]. Primer sequences were identified and trimmed using Cutadapt (version 1.9.1) [26]. Subsequently, the denoising algorithm DADA2 was applied to the feature classification to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), with the filtering out of ASVs present in fewer than two samples across all datasets [27]. Alpha diversity indices (Chao1 and Shannon index) were calculated using QIIME2 software. Chao1 and Shannon index were used to evaluate the richness and diversity of bacterial communities in the samples, respectively. A petal plot was constructed to identify differences in amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) between the two sample groups using the R VennDiagram package. Dilution curves of the Shannon diversity index were generated with R language tools, based on the Shannon index (a metric reflecting microbial diversity within samples) at varying sequencing depths for each sample, to assess microbial diversity across different sequencing quantities. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), based on weighted UniFrac distances, was employed to visualize variations in bacterial community structure, and the PCoA plot was generated using the R vegan package [28]. In addition, a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed to provide statistical insights into the PCoA results. Subsequently, ANOVA was utilized to quantify the extent of differences among sample groups.

Results

Sequencing quality and bacterial ASVs analysis

We acquired 16 S rRNA V3-V4 amplicon sequences of bacterial communities from a total of 64 samples, including 32 rhizosphere samples from wild soybeans and 32 samples from the surrounding water environment. Sequencing generated 5,121,157 paired-end reads, which were subsequently subjected to quality control and assembled into 4,459,397 clean reads. Each sample yielded a minimum of 66,756 clean reads, averaging 69,678 clean reads per sample. These high-quality sequences were clustered into 12,579 Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs), with individual samples containing between 167 and 2,458 ASVs (Fig. 1a). A total of 8,735 ASVs were detected in the rhizosphere samples of 32 wild soybean plants. In comparison, 11,344 ASVs were identified in 32 water environment samples. The petal plot illustrates the distribution of shared and unique ASVs among different groups of wild soybean rhizosphere and water environments. Among the eight groups analyzed, 60 ASVs (0.51%) were found to be shared across all groups (Fig. 1b).

Bacterial diversity

Compared with wild soybean rhizosphere samples and water environment samples, bacterial communities’ richness index (Chao1) exhibited no significant variation across most groups, suggesting no marked difference in bacterial richness between wild soybean rhizosphere samples and water environment samples (Fig. 2a).The diversity index (Shannon index) of bacterial communities under the same salinity treatment across different groups demonstrated significant differences exclusively between rhizosphere samples (RootLT) and water environment samples (WaterLT) in the low-salinity treatment group. The control group (WaterCK) exhibited significant differences among water environment samples compared to all three treatment groups. In the rhizosphere samples of wild soybeans, the high-salinity treatment group (RootHT) showed significant differences relative to the control group (RootCK), the low-salinity treatment group (RootLT), and the medium-salinity treatment group (RootMT) (Fig. 2b). This suggests that at the same salinity, the bacterial community diversity of wild soybean rhizosphere and water environment samples is roughly the same, with no statistically significant differences observed. Under varying salinity treatments for samples within the same category, the diversity of bacterial communities exhibits substantial variation. Comparable conclusions can also be drawn from the Shannon index curve (Fig. 3). As salinity increases, the alpha diversity of bacterial communities in the rhizosphere and surrounding environments of wild soybeans tends to decline.

The PERMANOVA analysis further confirmed that beta diversity among the different sample groups was significantly distinct (Fig. 4b). R2 = 0.763 indicates that the model explains 76.3% of the variance. The p-value of 0.001 suggests that the differences in community composition among treatment groups are statistically significant, with inter-group variations being substantially greater than intra-group variations. This implies that salinity treatment has a pronounced effect on the bacterial community structure in the rhizosphere and surrounding environment of wild soybeans; as salinity increases (RootLT < RootMT < RootHT), the weighted UniFrac distance gradually decreases, indicating a reduction in the dissimilarity of rhizosphere bacterial community structures. Notably, RootHT exhibits the most substantial impact, with the minor community composition difference, suggesting that high-salt environments may exert intense selective pressure on rhizosphere bacterial communities, potentially inhibiting bacterial diversity or structural differentiation. This suggests that salt stress markedly alters the microbial community structure in the rhizosphere of wild soybeans, with the impact becoming more pronounced as salinity increases. However, as salinity rises, the weighted UniFrac value of the environmental group initially decreases and subsequently increases. Similarly, the beta diversity within the group exhibits a trend of first decreasing and then increasing, indicating that low-salt treatment exerts less disturbance on the microbial community in the surrounding environment. Moreover, bacterial communities in aquatic environments may be more sensitive to salinity changes, with increasing salinity exacerbating differences in community structure. The bacterial communities in the rhizosphere and environment exhibit distinct differences in their response patterns to salinity stress. The community composition of environmental samples demonstrates the most significant variation under medium-salinity treatment, whereas the rhizosphere community composition shows minimal variation under high-salinity treatment. Additionally, beta diversity within the RootLT and WaterMT groups exhibits a slight increase, potentially indicating fluctuations in community adaptability induced by the salinity gradient.

Discussion

In the natural environment, plant microbiome is derived from a variety of sources, including soil, air, neighboring plants, seeds and insects [29]. Soil microbiota, as the main reservoir for plants to recruit their rhizosphere microbiome, can migrate to the rhizosphere through passive diffusion or active recruitment by plants, becoming an indispensable part of the rhizosphere microbiome of plants [30]– [31]. The phyllosphere provides an open ecological niche for air-derived microbial colonization, such as wind, rain, and insect activity [32]. Microorganisms can not only spread horizontally from the surrounding environment but also vertically from parent plants to host plants through seeds and other means [33].The rhizosphere, serving as a critical interface for material exchange and energy transfer between plants and the environment, plays an essential role in the physiological and biochemical processes of plants, including growth and development, metabolism, and other related functions. In this context, rhizosphere microorganisms are pivotal, significantly contributing to helping plants withstand adverse stress conditions [34]– [35]. Plants recruit and establish rhizosphere microbial communities from the environment via root exudates and immune responses, thereby engaging in various physiological and biochemical processes. This facilitates nutrient uptake, enhances plant growth, and strengthens the plant’s tolerance to both abiotic and biotic stresses [36]. Rhizosphere beneficial microorganisms, including rhizosphere-promoting bacteria and rhizobia, interact with host plants to promote plant growth and health. These interactions enhance plant tolerance to adverse environmental conditions, such as drought and salinity stress [37]– [38]. Salinity exerts a significant influence on the abundance, diversity, composition, and function of rhizosphere microbial communities, thereby emphasizing the heightened sensitivity of bacteria to abiotic stress and resulting in more pronounced ecological consequences [39].

Changes of bacterial community diversity

Salt stress markedly influenced the diversity of bacterial communities in both the wild soybean rhizosphere and surrounding environments (the water environment in which wild soybeans grow during the cultivation process), with significantly distinct response patterns observed between the two. In the rhizosphere, regarding richness (Chao1 index), a significant difference was only detected between the low-salt (RootLT) and high-salt (RootHT) groups. By contrast, diversity (Shannon index) exhibited significant differences between the high-salt (RootHT) group and the control (RootCK), as well as between the low-salt (RootLT) and medium-salt (RootMT) groups. These findings indicate that variations in salt concentration substantially affect the diversity of bacterial communities in the rhizosphere of wild soybeans.PCoA further demonstrated that the rhizosphere bacterial communities under high-salt stress (RootHT) were distinctly segregated from those of the other treatment groups. This suggests that the high-salt environment exerts a pronounced screening effect on the structure of rhizosphere microbial communities, potentially reducing intracommunity heterogeneity. Conversely, environmental bacterial communities (Water group) exhibited heightened sensitivity to salinity fluctuations. Specifically, the Shannon index of the control group (WaterCK) was significantly different from all salt treatment groups (WaterLT, WaterMT, WaterHT). The PCoA results revealed that WaterCK was entirely separated from the three treatment groups, indicating that environmental microbial communities are directly subjected to salt stress and experience more extensive community reorganization. In contrast, rhizosphere microbial communities may preserve relative stability through plant-mediated regulatory mechanisms. With the increase in salinity, the alpha diversity of bacterial communities in both the rhizosphere and water environments of wild soybeans decreased. This is consistent with previous studies that reported higher bacterial α-diversity in coastal ecosystems during periods of low salinity, as salt stress diminishes the richness of bacterial communities [40]– [41]. Simultaneously, the beta diversity of rhizosphere and environmental bacterial communities associated with wild soybeans exhibited significant differences across the three salinity levels, thereby verifying that salinity exerts a substantial influence on the structural composition of bacterial communities [42]– [43]. This observed reduction in richness aligns with previous reports indicating inhibited rhizosphere microbial growth under saline-alkali conditions [44]– [45]. The increase in salinity was found to significantly alter the bacterial community structure in both the rhizosphere and the surrounding water environment of wild soybeans. This effect becomes increasingly pronounced with higher salinity gradients, resulting in a marked reduction in the richness and diversity of bacterial communities in both habitats. Based on these observations, it can be hypothesized that the environmental group (Water group) may exhibit greater susceptibility to salinity changes compared to the rhizosphere group (Root group).

Root secretions from root tissues have multiple functions to influence abiotic and biological processes in the soil, including altering physicochemical properties and recruiting beneficial microorganisms to resist external stress [46]. When plants are subjected to various stresses, the secretion of root exudates regulated by the overexpression or mutation of stress-related genes will have a special signaling effect on the rhizospheres. These special signals can enrich and maintain the specific beneficial microorganisms [47]– [48]. Many studies have demonstrated the influence of signals from root secretions on the interaction between plants and rhizosphere microorganisms, such as small signal molecules, antibacterial agents or plant hormones [49–51]. We propose that the relative stability of the rhizosphere community is underpinned by the selective recruitment of beneficial, salt-tolerant taxa mediated by root exudates.Under salt stress, plants like wild soybean often alter the quantity and composition of their root exudates, releasing specific compounds (e.g., organic acids, amino acids) that serve as chemoattractants and selective carbon sources [52]. This creates a nutrient-rich ecological niche, which is conducive to the utilization of these compounds by salt-tolerant bacteria with metabolic capabilities, including those beneficial to plants. The plant selectively feeds these microbes, which in turn can enhance plant tolerance through various mechanisms, creating a mutually beneficial feedback loop that structures the community. Secondly, the recruited microbes themselves contribute to stability through microbial interactions and niche construction. For instance, Pseudomonas can produce a large amount of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) under stress conditions. This EPS can form a protective biofilm on root surfaces, physically buffering the local microbial microhabitat from abrupt changes in ionic strength and creating a hydrated zone that mitigates osmotic stress. This biofilms not only protect the producing bacteria but also can facilitate the establishment of other community members, thereby enhancing the overall stability and coherence of the rhizosphere consortium against external pressures [53]. It is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that salt-adapted plants can selectively recruit specialized microbial consortia, thereby promoting the enrichment and proliferation of salt-adapted microbial taxa [54]– [55]. In contrast, the bacterial community in water environment is exposed to the direct, unmitigated impact of salinity.

Changes in bacterial community composition

At the phylum level, Proteobacteria consistently dominated across all control and treatment groups, with a relative abundance exceeding 50%. Its abundance increased in response to rising salinity levels, as observed in both rhizosphere samples of wild soybeans and environmental samples. For instance, in the rhizosphere, its relative abundance increased from 85.36% in RootCK to 91.19% in RootHT, while in the environment, it rose from 58.60% in WaterCK to 88.44% in WaterHT. This trend indicates that Proteobacteria demonstrates strong adaptability to high-salt environments. Proteobacteria is well-known for its relatively high salt tolerance. This phylum contains a large number of genera with various salt tolerance strategies. For instance, the lipopolysaccharide component on the outer membrane helps maintain the stability of the cell membrane under high pressure. Moreover, many of these bacteria have the ability to efficiently synthesize and accumulate compatible solutes, which can balance the osmotic pressure inside and outside the cell without interfering with cell metabolism [44, 56]. Furthermore, many subgroups within Proteobacteria, such as rhizobia, possess nitrogen-fixation capabilities and can thrive in diverse and complex environments. Consequently, Proteobacteria is the predominant microbial phylum in saline-alkali soils [57]. Vaishnav et al. found that the accumulation of proline and the increase of antioxidant activity in soybeans inoculated with Proteobacteria contributed to the improvement of salt tolerance, and the biomass, water content and chlorophyll of plants would all increase [58]. In contrast, the abundance of Bacteroidota decreased with increasing salinity in both rhizosphere and environmental samples of wild soybeans, potentially attributable to the inhibition of its metabolic activity under salt stress. As a dominant phylum, Bacteroidota is typically associated with high salinity in aquatic environments and can mitigate salt absorption through specialized membrane or cell wall structures, thereby demonstrating its adaptability to salt stres [59]– [60]. Furthermore, specific genera within the phylum Bacteroidota are capable of secreting substantial amounts of extracellular polysaccharides, which may enhance the resilience of microbial communities to extreme salinity conditions and improve nutrient accessibility [61]. Actinobacteriota is a dominant phylum in secondary saline-alkali soil [62]. Actinobacteriota are known to participate in carbon fixation within specific ecological niches [63]. The abundance of Actinobacteriota exhibited a trend of initially decreasing and subsequently increasing with the rise in salinity. Its relative abundance was significantly elevated across all three salt stress treatment groups. It is hypothesized that salt stress initially inhibits the growth of this microbial community; however, as salinity increases further, Actinobacteriota demonstrates relatively reduced sensitivity to salinity fluctuations, thereby maintaining community stability. This observation highlights their remarkable growth rate and adaptability in high-salinity environments, which aligns with prior research indicating a positive correlation between salinity levels and the diversity of Actinobacteriota communities [64]. Actinobacteriota are renowned for their metabolic versatility and ability to utilize a wide range of complex, recalcitrant organic compounds (e.g., chitin, cellulose) that may accumulate under stress or be released in root exudates [65]. This strategy (slow-growing, resource-efficient) allows them to thrive in nutrient-scarce. Actinobacteriota can produce spores under salt stress, allowing them to lie dormant during periods of acute stress and rapidly germinate and recolonize the niche once conditions become more stable or when they adapt physiologically [66]. Additionally, certain members of the phylum Actinobacteriota possess the capability to degrade complex compounds, including polysaccharides and phenolic compounds. This enables them to obtain nutrients and thrive in adverse or nutrient-depleted environments [67]. In our study, Actinobacteriota and Bacteroidota appear to play crucial roles in various nutrient cycling and biochemical processes under salt stress, a finding that aligns with prior research [68]– [69]. It is worth noting that, within bacterial communities in water environments, the relative abundance of Firmicutes exhibited a marked decrease with increasing salt concentration, declining from 12.63% in the control group (WaterCK) to 1.21% in the high-salt treatment group (WaterHT). This trend highlights the sensitivity of Firmicutes to salinity. This is related to the salt-tolerant strategies of many members of this phylum. Some thick-walled bacteria (such as Bacillus) enter dormancy by forming endogenous spores to survive extreme environments [70]. As a dominant phylum in environmental bacterial communities, Firmicutes may play a crucial role in assisting host plants in adapting to diverse environments, given its prevalence in the microbial communities of other halophytic species [71]. The observation that Myxococcota is no longer a dominant phylum in environmental bacterial communities under salt stress is consistent with previous studies and further substantiates their conclusions [72]. In the rhizosphere bacterial communities of wild soybeans, Cyanobacteria were the predominant phylum in both the low-salt and medium-salt treatment groups.It is because they have the ability of auxin biosynthesis, siderophore-mediated iron mobilization and photosynthetic nitrogen fixation [73]. Cyanobacteria exhibit strong salt tolerance and possess the potential to alleviate the adverse effects of salt stress [74]. Overall, the predominant phyla identified in the rhizosphere and surrounding environments under salt stress conditions in this study were largely consistent with those reported in previous studies [75]– [76].

At the genus level, the relative abundance of Pseudomonas was observed to significantly increase in both the rhizosphere and water environments of wild soybeans as salinity levels increased. Notably, in the high-salt treatment group, its relative abundance reached the highest level. Under salt stress conditions, Pseudomonas exhibited significant enrichment within the microbial communities associated with the rhizosphere and surrounding water environments of wild soybeans. Studies have shown that Pseudomonas maintains intracellular ionic homeostasis by highly expressing potassium uptake systems and polyamine transporters [77]. Meanwhile, it can efficiently synthesize EPS, forming a protective biofilm around the roots, physically buffering the impact of salt and creating a beneficial microenvironment for the root system [78]. A study demonstrated that Pseudomonas isolates XN05-1 and YE17 substantially enhanced the growth of both roots and shoots of wild soybeans under salt stress conditions. The plant growth promotion mediated by these strains was predominantly triggered in response to salt stress. Additionally, it was revealed that wild soybean roots secrete key metabolites such as purines, which not only enhance the motility and colonization capacity of Pseudomonas but also attract beneficial Pseudomonas strains to the rhizosphere, thereby facilitating their enrichment and enabling plants to better resist salt stress [11]. This study further validated the accuracy of its conclusions. Previous research on Pseudomonas has demonstrated its ability to significantly enhance stress enzyme activity and proline content in plants, while simultaneously reducing Na+ concentrations and increasing K+ concentrations in both roots and leaves, thereby facilitating plant growth [79]. Several strains within this genus are capable of growing normally at high salt concentrations of up to 5%, producing indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and protease, which promote plant growth under saline stress conditions [80]. Additionally, experimental evidence has confirmed that certain Pseudomonas species can synthesize 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase, thereby reducing ethylene levels in plants and mitigating the inhibitory effects of salt stress on plant growth [81]. In the bacterial community of the water environment, Acinetobacter exhibited marked differences in relative abundance between the control group and the various salt treatment groups. However, no significant variations were detected across different salt concentration levels. Acinetobacter is recognized for its metabolic versatility and robust salt tolerance [82]. They can utilize multiple carbon sources, which is a key advantage in the rhizosphere where salt stress leads to carbon source limitation. Its stable presence indicates that Acinetobacter may act as a “universal” rhizosphere settler, capable of tolerating a high degree of salt stress and maintaining its ecological niche. Studies indicate that Acinetobacter promotes soybean growth under salt stress through IAA production [83]. The relative abundance of Rhizobium exhibited a significant increase with rising salinity levels. In the rhizosphere of wild soybeans, the relative abundance of Rhizobium demonstrated significant differences between the control group and various salt treatment groups; however, no significant variations in its relative abundance were observed across different salt concentrations. Additionally, no substantial differences were detected in the structure of the environmental bacterial community. This genus is predominantly found in the rhizosphere of wild soybeans, where leguminous plants supply essential nutrients to Rhizobium, while Rhizobium converts atmospheric N2 into a reduced form that can be utilized by the host plant for nutritional purposes [84]. Studies have demonstrated that the symbiotic relationship between Rhizobium and plants can enhance the host’s resistance to salt stress by increasing the activity of antioxidant enzymes and improving osmotic adjustment capacity [85]. Moreover, specific strains of Rhizobium have been shown to decrease the levels of abscisic acid and salicylic acid in plants while promoting the accumulation of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) in leaves [86]. In the rhizosphere of wild soybeans, the relative abundance of Sphingobium remained relatively stable across different sample groups. No significant differences were observed among the control group, low-salt treatment group, and medium-salt treatment group. However, the high-salt treatment group exhibited significant deviations from the other three groups, indicating its heightened sensitivity to elevated salinity levels. Its cell membrane is rich in sphingolipids, which may lead to insufficient membrane fluidity and functional impairment in a hypertonic environment, thereby affecting nutrient absorption and growth. As a plant growth-promoting bacterium (PGPB), Sphingobium positively influences plant growth through the production of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), siderophores, and carbohydrate utilization capabilities. Furthermore, the abundance of Shewanella was markedly elevated in the high-salt treatment group, potentially attributable to its inherent salt tolerance [87]. Under high-salt conditions, Shewanella enhances the secretion of extracellular polymeric substances and accumulates compatible solutes to maintain osmotic balance, thereby effectively mitigating high-salt stress [88]. Studies have confirmed that specific strains of Shewanella enhance plant tolerance to adverse stress conditions by modulating root growth, regulating the expression of plant hormones, and promoting the expression of stress-resistant genes [89].

Due to the influence of plant root activities, the bacterial community structure in the rhizosphere of wild soybeans differs significantly from that in the surrounding environment. Nevertheless, the presence of these dominant bacteria in this study suggests that under salt stress conditions, microbial distribution exhibits certain similarities. Among them, the relative abundance of salt-tolerant bacteria will further increase, and more dominant bacteria will transform into salt-tolerant bacteria. This finding supports the hypothesis that similar ecological pressures select for comparable microbial assemblages. Under salt stress, the dominant bacteria in the rhizosphere of wild soybeans and their surrounding environment may transition into salt-tolerant species, thereby possessing the capacity to modify the rhizosphere microenvironment to enhance its suitability for plant growth [90]– [91]. Specifically, salt-tolerant rhizosphere beneficial microorganisms typically enhance plant growth by producing hormone-like substances, improving nutrient absorption, and inducing enhanced resistance in plants [92]– [93].

Conclusion

The diversity of bacterial communities in both the wild soybeans rhizosphere and the surrounding environment (the water environment in which wild soybeans grow during the cultivation process) decreased as salinity increased. Bacterial communities in the surrounding environment exhibited greater sensitivity to salinity changes, with substantial reorganization of their community structure. In contrast, rhizosphere bacterial communities may maintain relative stability under salt stress due to plant-mediated regulation. Regarding bacterial community composition, significant variations were observed at both the phylum and genus levels. These findings indicate that dominant bacteria in the rhizosphere and surrounding environment of wild soybeans gradually shifted toward salt-tolerant taxa. Such salt-tolerant bacteria likely enhance the salt stress resistance of wild soybeans via various mechanisms, thereby playing a critical role in their adaptation to saline environments.
