Diabetes and socioeconomic status as key risk factors for colorectal cancer in the pashtun population: a case–control study
Authors: Husna Shinwari, Muhammad Osama, Aishah Binte Nawaz, Maaz Khan, Muhammad Imran Marwat, Muhammad Altaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is a major global health burden, with rising incidence in South Asia and significant variation across ethnic and socioeconomic groups. Despite this growing concern, CRC risk factors remain understudied in the Pashtun population of Pakistan. Given the potential influence of metabolic and socioeconomic determinants, this study aimed to identify key CRC risk factors within this underserved community.MethodsA case–control study was performed on Pashtun individuals registered for colonoscopy or surgery in tertiary care hospitals in Peshawar from April 2023 till March 2025. A sample of 51 cases and 51 controls was selected through simple random sampling. Data on CRC risk factors, including diabetes mellitus (DM), smoking, lifestyle, and family history, were collected via a validated questionnaire. Statistical analysis included chi-squared tests; multiple regression analysis, and correlation analysis, were used.ResultsThe study included 51 cases and 51 controls to explore risk factors for colorectal cancer (CRC). Results indicated that a higher monthly income was linked to a reduced risk of CRC (p = 0.03). Notably, the presence of diabetes mellitus (DM) prior to CRC diagnosis was significantly associated with an increased risk (p = 0.002), a finding further supported by multiple regression analysis. Correlation analysis found weak and non-significant links between CRC and other factors, such as exercise frequency, regular walking distance, HbA1c levels, smoking status, and family history of cancer.ConclusionThese findings highlight the significant role of socioeconomic status and diabetes in CRC development among the Pashtun population. While certain factors like smoking and family history did not show significant associations, the study underscores the need for targeted preventive strategies to reduce the CRC burden. Further research is necessary to elucidate these relationships comprehensively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04759-9.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a significant global health concern, ranking as the second most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. Its distribution varies across regions, ages, genders, and racial groups [2]. In the US alone, an estimated 153,020 individuals were diagnosed with CRC in 2023, resulting in 52,550 deaths, with 19,550 cases and 3,750 deaths occurring in individuals under 50 years old [3]. Eastern and Central Europe report high mortality rates, while in Western Africa, males have a higher incidence than females [4]. Asia holds the highest rates of CRC incidence and mortality globally [5]. In India, approximately 95,000 men and 68,000 women die from colorectal cancer (CRC) annually, corresponding to mortality rates of 7.2 per 100,000 in men and 5.1 per 100,000 in women, respectively [6]. In Pakistan, CRC prevalence is 5% suggesting that approximately 11 million individuals are estimated to be affected by colorectal cancer (CRC), out of population of 220 million [7].

Age, chronic diseases, and lifestyle contribute to CRC risk [8]. Regular exercise reduces overall CRC risk, while male sex and metabolic syndrome were independent risk factors. Factors like obesity, DM, and lifestyle habits impact CRC development. Obesity increases the risk of CRC through chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and alterations in gut microbiota. Diabetes mellitus contributes to CRC development by promoting hyperinsulinemia and increased levels of insulin-like growth factor (IGF), which stimulate cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis. Unhealthy lifestyle habits, such as a diet high in processed meats and low in fiber, physical inactivity, and smoking, further contribute to colorectal neoplasia by inducing genetic mutations, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation [9]. According to a population-based national study conducted in the US, Early-onset CRC is linked to smoking, family history of non-CRC malignancy, and HIV [10]. Late-onset CRC is associated with obesity, Type 1 diabetes, and alcohol consumption [11]. According to a study in Morocco, CRC cases are older, less educated, and have more family history than controls [12]. Korean study shows waist circumference and BMI strongly correlate with CRC risk regardless of age or sex [13]. National Institutes of Health-American Association of Retired Persons (NIH-AARP) study found higher CRC risk in those with family history, living in poor neighborhoods [14].

Given the unique cultural, genetic, and socioeconomic characteristics of the Pashtun population, an ethnic group often underrepresented in cancer research, this study focuses on identifying colorectal cancer (CRC) risk factors within this community. Conducted in Peshawar, where Pashtuns form the majority, it addresses a critical regional gap in the literature. Although diabetes mellitus and socioeconomic status were primary areas of interest, the study also explored additional relevant factors such as family history, physical activity, and lifestyle behaviors due to their established links to CRC. This broader scope provides a more comprehensive understanding of CRC risk in this population and supports the development of culturally and contextually appropriate prevention strategies in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Aims

This study aimed to identify key risk factors for colorectal cancer in the Pashtun population, with a focus on diabetes mellitus and socioeconomic status. It also sought to evaluate the association of lifestyle and demographic factors with CRC risk.

Study design and settings

The study was reported in line with STROBE guidelines for observational research. The study was a case–control design conducted on individuals of Pashtun origin who were registered for screening colonoscopy or surgery at tertiary care hospitals in the district of Peshawar, Pakistan. The study took place between April 2023 till March 2025.

Screening program for CRC in Pashtuns

In the Pashtun population as well as in other parts of Pakistan, the colorectal cancer (CRC) screening program aligns broadly with international guidelines, recommending initiation of screening at the age of 45 and repeating it every 10 years. However, in practice, there is no formally enforced or systematically implemented CRC screening program in the region. Instead, opportunistic screening is the norm. When individuals within the eligible age group present to outpatient departments (OPDs), often for unrelated concerns, healthcare providers take that opportunity to assess CRC risk factors and counsel patients about screening options. If the patient consents, they are scheduled for appropriate procedures, such as colonoscopy or alternative non-invasive methods, based on availability and patient preference. This opportunistic, contact-based approach reflects the current implementation context in the Pashtun region, where formal screening infrastructure and outreach efforts are limited [1, 2].

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using Open-Epi, Version 3, with a confidence interval (CI) of 95%, power of 90%, a case-to-control ratio of 1:1, and an odds ratio (OR) of 5, based on a similar study conducted in Africa [3]. The calculated sample size was 51 cases and 51 controls, resulting in a total sample size of 102 participants. The sampling technique used was simple random sampling. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Matching

Matching of cases and controls was carefully implemented by ensuring equal gender distribution and similar mean age between the two groups, thereby controlling for these key demographic factors and minimizing potential confounding variables.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Cases were defined as adult Pashtun individuals with a histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of colorectal cancer (CRC), recruited from patients undergoing colonoscopy or surgery at the selected tertiary care hospitals (Hayatabad Medical Complex, Khyber Teaching Hospital, Lady Reading Hospital) during the study period. Controls were age- and sex-matched Pashtun individuals without a diagnosis of CRC, selected from the same hospitals during routine visits or screening procedures. Participants were included after providing informed consent. Those with a previous history of CRC or any other malignancy, those from non-Pashtun ethnic backgrounds, or those unable or unwilling to consent were excluded from the study Fig. 1.

Variables

The variables investigated included age (in years), gender (male/female), and Pashtun ethnicity (self-reported). Residence was classified as urban or rural. BMI was calculated and categorized as underweight, normal, overweight, or obese. Education level was recorded as no formal, primary, or secondary. Occupation and monthly income were self-reported and categorized. CRC presence was histopathologically confirmed in cases; controls had no CRC. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was recorded as present or absent, with timing (before CRC) noted. HbA1c was recorded. Smoking status was categorized as current, former, or never. Other addictions (e.g., alcohol, drugs) were noted. Physical activity was assessed by regular walking distance and exercise frequency. Family history of any cancer and specifically CRC in first-degree relatives was recorded as binary (yes/no). Most variables were binary or categorical; continuous ones (e.g., age, HbA1c) were used as such in analysis.

Data collection procedure

Data was collected through interviews using a structured, validated questionnaire. The questionnaire covered demographic details, socioeconomic status, education, employment, smoking, physical activity, diabetes mellitus, and family history of cancer. Each response was categorized for analysis (e.g., yes/no for diabetes, smoking; income and distance walked in ranges). The main outcome was the presence or absence of colorectal cancer (CRC), and the variables studied in relation to it included diabetes, smoking, physical activity, education level, occupation, income, and family history of CRC. Scoring was done using binary and categorical formats for statistical comparison.

Data analysis

Baseline characteristics of the cases and controls were compared using the Chi-squared test for categorical variables and a student t-test for continuous variables. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to assess the strength of associations between various risk factors and CRC. Binary logistic regression, multiple regression analysis, and correlation analysis were employed to identify independent risk factors for CRC. Data analysis was performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp, Armonk NY, USA).

Results

The study included 51 cases and 51 controls to evaluate risk factors for colorectal cancer (CRC). Overall, the mean age and gender distribution were similar between groups. Socioeconomic status appeared to influence CRC risk, with higher monthly income associated with a lower likelihood of disease. Diabetes mellitus diagnosed prior to CRC and family history of CRC was more prevalent in cases (p < 0.05), highlighting their role as risk factors. Discussion

The study analyzed various factors to determine their association with colorectal cancer (CRC) risk. Socioeconomic factors, including monthly income, showed associations with CRC, while diabetes mellitus (DM) was more prevalent in cases than controls. However, multivariate analysis did not identify any significant independent predictors of CRC.

Younger people are becoming more frequently diagnosed with colon cancer [15] with prevalence in age < 50 years [16]. Age increased the likelihood of having early-onset CRC (EOCRC) by 5% per year [17]. The chance of developing CRC with early onset may be increased by unhealthy eating habits, inactivity, and the excess body weight that results from metabolic syndrome [11, 18]. This shift is thought to be driven by modern lifestyle changes which contribute to metabolic syndrome, a condition characterized by insulin resistance and chronic inflammation, both of which are implicated in CRC pathogenesis. An increased risk of CRC is associated with a family history of CRC in First Degree Relatives [19]. Although the cause of EO-CRC is mostly unclear, its traits are distinct from those of CRC seen in older patients in many significant aspects [20].

There is a shift of CRC towards younger. Various studies state that significant variables for CRC include age > 70 Years [21], but in our study, the mean age for cases is 48.24 ± 17.848 which suggests early onset in Pashtuns similar to a cohort study in our country with a mean age of 49.9 + 14.9 years [22]. The same is noticed in Canada the incidence of CA is decreasing in persons over the age of 50 [21]. The decline is mainly attributed to widespread CRC screening programs like fecal occult blood tests (FOBT), fecal immunochemical tests (FIT), and colonoscopies, enabling early detection and removal of precancerous polyps. Increased public awareness of CRC risk factors, healthier diets, reduced smoking rates among older adults, and improved access to preventive care in developed countries also contribute to this trend. The prevalence is 5.9% in 20–29 years of age and 9.5% in 30–39 age groups [11]. Age increased the likelihood of having EOCRC by 5% per year (OR 1.05, 95% CI [1.03, 1.07]) [23].

Males have a 1.5-fold higher risk of CRC than females across all ages and regions [24]. Chinese studies indicate a higher incidence of early-onset CRC in males [25]. Obesity, diabetes mellitus (DM), and lifestyle habits like smoking and alcohol use contribute to CRC risk, with different effects observed between genders [18, 21, 23].

The gender more prone to CRC is becoming a controversial thing as various ethnicities show different results. In our study, Gender showed insignificant association with CRC; contrary to previous work where CRC is considered to be common in males7. In a case–control study of US of study population between age 18 to 49, the risk of acquiring Early Onset Colorectal Carcinoma (EOCRC) was 2.2 times higher in men than in women (OR 2.21, 95% CI [1.68, 2.91]) [23]. Whereas, according to the Shaukat Khanum Memorial Cancer Hospital (SKMCH) study, females are 2 × more likely to get CRC [22]. In Chinese Youth, 94% (95% CI: 1.12–3.36) higher incidence of EO-CRC was shown to be related to male sex [26].

There is a correlation between high BMI and CRC [22]. For individuals with a BMI ≥ 25, each 1-unit increase in BMI is associated with a 7% higher risk of developing CRC, equivalent to a 1.07 times increased risk per unit (HR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.14), highlighting BMI as a significant, modifiable risk facto [12], especially in early-onset cases [25]. An elevated risk of CRC was strongly correlated with both waist circumference and body mass index. After mutual adjustment, only waist circumference (HR = 1.275, 95% CI: 1.205–1.349) was substantially linked to an elevated risk of CRC [13]. Obese men were found to have a 50% higher risk of colon cancer and a 20% higher risk of rectal cancer (the corresponding figures for women are 20% and 10%) [24]. In comparison to patients who are normal weight or overweight, patients who were underweight or obese had a higher risk of dying from CRC [27].

Overweight and obese individuals had a 31% decreased risk of getting EOCRC compared to those with a normal [27] Obesity was more common in CRC cases than controls, defined as individuals without a diagnosis of CRC, who were matched to cases of CRC based on demographic and lifestyle factors [25]. In Chinese, BMI was independently linked to a 7% higher chance of developing an early-onset colorectal tumor (95% CI: 1.01–1.14) [28]. However, in our study, BMI showed an insignificant association with CRC with a p-value of 0.394. The reason probably might be there is ultimate weight loss in the course of the disease. Similar to the results of SKMCH where the BMI showed no significant association with CRC with univariate regression yielding OR 1.40 (95% CI: 0.78–2.37, p = 0.27) and OR 1.10 (95%, CI 0.60–2.10; p = 0.82) on multivariate, indicating BMI's limited impact in their analysis [22]. Some studies find increased BMI and body weight as a protective factor in the case of EOCRC [25].

Diabetes Mellitus increases colorectal cancer (CRC) risk by 1.3fold, with an 8.8% rise in diabetes prevalence at CRC diagnosis, likely due to chronic hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and associated inflammation [28]. Genetic predisposition to type 2 diabetes and elevated HbA1c levels heighten CRC risk [29]. Men face higher CRC risk from elevated HbA1c levels. T2DM patients in Korea had a CRC incidence of 2.3 per 1,000 person-years [21]. Chinese research found a 1.18 adjusted Hazard Ratio for CRC among diabetics [30]. Women with T2DM exhibit a higher risk of proximal colon cancer in the Netherlands [31].

In a UK study, diabetes prevalence was higher in cases compared to controls (9.3% vs. 3.3%; both p < 0.001) [28]. However, a US study found no association between diabetes and early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC)18(26). Elevated fasting insulin levels were linked to increased colorectal cancer risk (OR per 1-SD = 1.65) while fasting and 2-h glucose concentrations did not significantly impact risk [32].

In contrast to women, higher HbA1c levels were associated with an increased risk of rectal cancer in men (OR per 1-SD = 1.21) [18]. However, in our study, we found no significant association between HbA1c levels and rectal cancer risk (p = 0.236). A cohort study in the Netherlands revealed that women with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) had a notably higher risk of proximal colon cancer (HR = 1.80) [31]. In our study, pre-existing diabetes mellitus was insignificantly associated with colorectal cancer (p = 0.089, OR = 7.942), similar to findings from the SKMCH study where no association was observed (p = 0.85) [22].

If a first-degree relative has had colorectal cancer, the risk is more than twice as high as it is for the general population [19]. Thirty percent or more of CRC patients had a family history of the tumor [24]. In the Chinese population, EO-CRC was five times more likely to develop with a positive family history of CRC [25]. When a family history of CRC is recorded, it is possible to hypothesize that low-penetrance genes interacting with dietary and lifestyle factors contribute differently to people of different ages [19]. The probability increased to 1.36 in families with four impacted FDRs from 1.10 in those with one FDR who had discordant cancer [33].

In our study, participants with a first-degree relative (FDR) diagnosed with cancer comprised 60% of cases and 40% of controls. However, the presence of cancer diagnoses in FDRs showed no significant association with colorectal cancer (CRC) development (p = 0.999). Similarly, CRC diagnosis in FDRs exhibited no significant association with CRC development (p = 0.999) This aligns with findings from the SKMCH study where a family history of cancer was not significant (p = 0.321, multivariate analysis OR 1.22, CI 0.66–2.25, p 0.52) [22]. Conversely, a systematic review of 63 studies involving over 9 million patients demonstrated that participants with an FDR with CRC were more likely to develop CRC (RR = 1.76, 95% CI = 1.57–1.97, p < 0.001) compared to those without family history [19]. Additionally, some studies indicated a significant association between a history of CRC in FDRs, genetic risk scores, and CRC incidences [34].

Smoking is a risk factor for the development of CRC [19] Nicotine is considered to be a carcinogen as it forms intestinal polyps [7] Women had a larger smoking effect than males, but not a larger drinking effect although both increases risk of CRC, but their effects differ. Smoking introduces carcinogens that promote DNA damage and polyp formation, with women experiencing a stronger association between smoking and CRC risk compared to men. Alcohol contributes to CRC risk by impairing DNA repair, inducing oxidative stress, and enhancing carcinogen activation, with heavy consumption showing the greatest impact. These findings suggest distinct mechanisms through which smoking and alcohol independently elevate CRC risk [35]. According to research, regular smoking carries a 1.18 relative risk of CRC. Rectal cancer was found to be more likely to develop among smokers [36].

Some Studies find neither current nor prior smoking to be associated with EOCRC [27] Likewise in our study, 64.3% of the smokers had CRC. Smoking had an insignificant association with p-value of 0.360. The same results were obtained in the SKMCH study with OR 1.10; CI (0.60–2.10); p-value of 0.82 [22]. While in the South Korean young population, current smoking (OR = 1.60, 95%CI: 1.07–2.41, p = 0.023) was an independent, significant risk factor [37].

Alcohol use is a risk factor for the development of CRC [19]. Drinkers who consume two to three alcoholic beverages per day have a 20% higher risk of developing CRC, and those who consume more than three drinks have a 40% higher risk [36]. Frequently, heavy drinkers (45 g/day) face a risk that is up to 1.41 times greater than non-drinkers. The relationship between alcohol consumption and the risk of CRC varied according to an individual's level of obesity. Only obese patients had a higher risk of CRC linked to alcohol consumption, which raises the possibility that alcohol and obesity interact synergistically [38].

Light to moderate alcohol consumption (up to 2 drinks/day) may offer protection against colorectal cancer (CRC), while heavy intake (more than 3 drinks/day) may elevate risk [39]. In our study, 63.2% of individuals with other addictions had CRC, and the evidence linking these addictions to CRC was strong (p = 0.008, OR 0.002, 95% CI 0.000–0.212), similar to findings from the SKMCH study (p = 0.325) [22].This could be attributed to the low prevalence of alcohol consumption among Pakistani individuals.

A sedentary lifestyle can make you more prone to have CRC. Regular physical activity, however, has been shown to reduce CRC risk by improving metabolic function, reducing inflammation, and promoting healthy body weight [7]. There is a link between colorectal cancer and no/low physical activity [40]. A 25% decreased risk of CRC is associated with frequent physical activity. The risk of having CRC is up to 50% higher among the most sedentary people [24].

An Iranian study links low physical activity levels with colorectal cancer (OR no activity: 36.09, 95% CI: 10.94–119; OR low activity: 2.96, 95% CI: 1.43–6.13) [40]. Similarly, Chinese research identifies no regular exercise as a significant risk (OR = 3.228, 95% CI: 1.230–8.469, P = 0.017) [37]. In our study, both distances of walking and exercise frequency showed no significant association (p = 0.203 and 340, respectively). Conversely, in the SKMCH study, daily walking and exercise were protective (OR 0.41, CI 0.20–0.85, p = 0.02; OR 0.37, CI 0.14–0.95, p = 0.04)22(22). Additionally, physical activity may reduce CRC risk in high-risk groups like obese individuals [41].

A higher lifestyle risk score, while specific numeric values for the total score are not directly detailed, (combining factors such as diet, physical activity, smoking, and alcohol consumption) was linked to an increased risk of colorectal cancer (OR, 5.82; 95% CI, 4.02 to 8.44 for the highest vs. lowest) [37].

Socioeconomic status is one of many determinants, with poor socioeconomic level being linked to a higher risk of colorectal cancer development [15]. Socioeconomic status influences CRC risk through indicators like limited access to healthcare, lower awareness of screening, and unhealthy lifestyle choices associated with poverty [13], as is seen in our study.

The study's limitations include a relatively small sample size, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Additionally, reliance on self-reported data and potential recall bias could influence the accuracy of risk factor assessment. To address the study's limitations, several measures were implemented to enhance its rigor and reliability. These included careful matching of cases and controls based on key demographic factors, using a validated questionnaire to collect data, and applying appropriate statistical methods to control for potential confounding variables. Additionally, efforts were made to minimize bias by conducting the study in a single, well-defined geographic area comprising of Pashtun population.

Conclusion

The authors should exercise caution when interpreting the presence of diabetes mellitus prior to colorectal cancer (CRC) diagnosis, as both conditions are chronic in nature and characterized by extended latency periods, which may complicate causal inferences. The recommendations proposed are already being implemented within the current program; therefore, the authors are encouraged to suggest alternative strategies that may offer additional value or improved outcomes.

In addition to ongoing public health efforts, we recommend the integration of diabetes screening and management into colorectal cancer risk assessment protocols, particularly in primary care settings. Leveraging electronic medical records (EMRs) to flag high-risk individuals (e.g., those with longstanding diabetes or low socioeconomic status) may facilitate targeted CRC screening. Furthermore, culturally tailored education programs and mobile screening units in underserved Pashtun areas could bridge gaps in access and awareness. Future initiatives should also explore the feasibility of implementing low-cost, non-invasive CRC screening tools (e.g., fecal immunochemical testing) to enhance early detection in resource-limited settings.
