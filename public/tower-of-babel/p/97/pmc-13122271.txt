Outcomes of Inverted Tympanomeatal Flap Elevation for Large Anterior Tympanic Membrane Perforations in Narrow External Auditory Canals
Authors: Abdullah Belada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectiveAnterior tympanic membrane perforations—particularly in patients with a narrow external auditory canal—remain technically challenging due to limited visualization and inadequate anterior support. The inverted tympanomeatal flap technique has been proposed to address these issues, but evidence is limited. This study aimed to evaluate its anatomical and functional outcomes in large anterior perforations treated with endoscopic transcanal tympanoplasty.MethodsThis retrospective single‐surgeon study included 70 patients with large anterior perforations and ≥ 20% anterior quadrant non‐visualization. All underwent standardized audiometry and at least 6 months of follow‐up. Outcomes were graft success, complications, and hearing improvement. Analyses included audiometric comparisons, logistic regression, and ROC curves to identify predictors of graft failure.ResultsThe graft success rate was 95.7% (67/70). Mean ABG improved from 23.44 ± 10.95 dB preoperatively to 13.52 ± 8.86 dB postoperatively (p < 0.001), with a mean hearing gain of 9.91 ± 8.61 dB (Cohen's d = 1.00). Larger perforation size (p = 0.046) and severe external auditory canal narrowing (p = 0.041) independently predicted graft failure. ROC analysis identified > 72% perforation size (AUC = 0.78) and > 40% canal narrowing (AUC = 0.71) as risk thresholds. No cases of chorda tympani injury, canal trauma, or iatrogenic cholesteatoma occurred.ConclusionIn conclusion, inverted tympanomeatal flap elevation showed favorable graft success and hearing improvement in challenging anterior perforations with narrow canals. However, due to the retrospective single‐surgeon design and absence of a control group, these results should be interpreted cautiously. Larger prospective studies are needed to validate its effectiveness.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: anterior tympanic membrane perforation, endoscopic ear surgery, external auditory canal narrowing, tympanomeatal flap, tympanoplasty

Introduction

Tympanoplasty aims to restore a structurally intact and acoustically functional tympanic membrane while preserving the ventilation and physiological integrity of the middle ear cavity [1, 2]. Although various techniques have been developed over the past decades, anterior tympanic membrane perforations continue to represent one of the most challenging subtypes encountered in middle ear surgery. Their inherent anatomical limitations—particularly the steep angle of the anterior canal wall, restricted visualization, and insufficient medial support for graft placement—are well known to reduce surgical success and contribute to higher rates of graft lateralization, reperforation, and inadequate graft nourishment [3, 4].

Traditional tympanoplasty techniques, including the underlay and overlay approaches, have distinct advantages and shortcomings when applied to anterior defects. The underlay method avoids anterior blunting but often provides limited access to the anterior annulus and may predispose to adhesions or residual perforation due to insufficient anterior support [5, 6]. Conversely, the overlay approach improves anterior exposure but carries a risk of graft lateralization, epithelial ingrowth, and elevation‐related complications [7, 8]. In response to these limitations, several modified techniques—such as the anterior pull‐through method, loop overlay, loop underlay, mediolateral grafting, three‐point fixation, and window‐shade tympanoplasty—have been proposed to enhance graft stability in anterior perforations [9, 10]. However, many of these approaches require a retroauricular incision, increased soft‐tissue dissection, or are technically demanding in patients with a narrow external auditory canal (EAC), thereby limiting their applicability in endoscopic ear surgery [11, 12].

With the advancement of endoscopic otologic surgery, minimally invasive approaches have gained prominence, offering a wide‐angle field of view and improved visualization of hidden recesses. Despite these developments, the presence of a narrow EAC remains a major obstacle in achieving reliable access to the anterior annulus. In such cases, manipulation of the tympanomeatal flap becomes increasingly difficult, and the risk of flap trauma, inadequate graft placement, and reduced anterior support is heightened [13, 14]. These anatomic constraints underscore the need for techniques that permit safer elevation of the anterior annulus while maintaining a stable surgical field and minimizing disruption to the canal skin.

Recently, techniques involving medial‐to‐lateral or “inverted” elevation of the tympanomeatal flap have generated interest. Unlike the classic lateral‐to‐medial elevation, this approach allows the surgeon to release the anterior annulus more effectively, reduce tension on the flap, and access the anterior canal wall without excessive manipulation [15, 16]. The method may also improve graft stability by facilitating accurate placement over the malleus and providing continuous support to the anterior perforation margin. Despite these theoretical advantages, evidence on the effectiveness of inverted tympanomeatal flap elevation in patients with both large anterior perforations and a narrow EAC remains scarce, and current literature does not adequately address its outcomes in this anatomically constrained subgroup [17, 18].

The present study aims to evaluate the surgical performance of the inverted tympanomeatal flap elevation technique in patients with large anterior perforations and narrow EACs. Specifically, it investigates graft success rates, postoperative hearing outcomes, and complication profiles to help clarify the role of this technique in overcoming the limitations traditionally associated with anterior perforation repair [19, 20].

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This study was designed as a retrospective clinical analysis conducted at a tertiary referral center. All tympanoplasty procedures included in this investigation were performed by the same surgeon between January 2018 and September 2025. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Duzce University Non‐Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Date: 06.10.2025, Decision No: 2025‐277). All clinical and surgical procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient confidentiality was maintained throughout the study by anonymizing all identifiable information during data collection and analysis.

Patient Selection and Eligibility Criteria

Patients were identified through the institutional electronic medical record system and operative logs. Individuals with chronic tympanic membrane perforation localized primarily in the anterior quadrant, accompanied by a narrow EAC—as defined by less than 20% anterior perforation visibility on routine endoscopic evaluation—were considered eligible. The degree of EAC narrowing was determined based on the percentage of the anterior tympanic membrane obscured by the anterior canal wall during 0° endoscopic visualization. Patients were stratified into three groups according to this percentage: mild (20%–30% obscuration), moderate (30%–40%), and severe (> 40%). Grading was performed intraoperatively by the primary surgeon based on standardized endoscopic assessment. Computed tomography–based morphometric measurements were not routinely used for grading. All included patients had undergone endoscopic transcanal tympanoplasty without any additional retroauricular approach. Patients were excluded if they presented with active otorrhea at the time of surgery, cholesteatoma, ossicular chain discontinuity or fixation, tympanosclerosis involving the umbo or annulus, a history of previous middle ear surgery, marginal perforations extending posteriorly beyond the annulus, granulation tissue obscuring the anterior canal wall, or inadequate postoperative follow‐up of less than 6 months.

Preoperative Evaluation

All patients underwent a standardized preoperative assessment consisting of a detailed otoscopic and endoscopic examination, performed using a 0° or 30° rigid endoscope. Perforation location, the percentage of perforation area, and the morphology of the EAC were documented. Pure‐tone audiometry was obtained using calibrated equipment in a sound‐treated booth, measuring air‐ and bone‐conduction thresholds at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 4 kHz. Temporal bone computed tomography was performed when clinically indicated to assess the attic, antrum, canal wall anatomy, and to exclude hidden pathology or ossicular abnormalities.

Surgical Technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia using a transcanal endoscopic approach. After preparation and draping, a 0° and 30° endoscope were used throughout the procedure depending on visualization requirements. Tragal cartilage was harvested through a small incision at the medial aspect of the tragus. The cartilage was excised together with bilateral perichondrium, and the perichondria were carefully elevated to obtain two separate sheets while preserving the structural integrity of the cartilage.

The perforation margins were freshened circumferentially by removing the squamous epithelial layer. The tympanomeatal flap was elevated from medial to lateral, starting from the umbo and extending circumferentially along the annulus. Special care was taken to elevate the anterior annular region up to the level of the anterior canal wall bend, particularly in patients with narrow EACs. During flap elevation, the mucosal layer over the promontory and the fibrous annulus was preserved to avoid injury. The ossicular chain was routinely inspected to confirm integrity and mobility. No canalplasty or anterior canal wall drilling was performed in any patient.

Following flap elevation, the middle ear was packed with a small amount of absorbable gelatin sponge to support the graft. The tragal cartilage was shaped to contour the manubrium of the malleus and was positioned over the malleus handle using the over‐underlay technique, with cartilage placed over the manubrium and the perichondrial layer positioned beneath the elevated flap. This configuration was intentionally selected to enhance anterior graft support, improve mechanical stability in large anterior perforations, and reduce the risk of graft medialization or lateralization, particularly in anatomically narrow ear canals. One sheet of perichondrium was placed anteriorly, over the bony canal and cartilage, ensuring continuity and coverage of the anterior perforation region. The second sheet was positioned posteriorly in a similar fashion. The tympanomeatal flap was then carefully repositioned without folding or tension. The EAC was filled with small pieces of gelatin sponge, and the tragal incision was closed with absorbable sutures. Antibiotic‐soaked cotton was placed at the canal entrance.

Postoperative Care and Follow‐Up

Patients were monitored in the recovery unit and discharged on the same day or the following morning, depending on clinical status. Postoperative care included standard analgesic and antibiotic therapy as clinically indicated. Follow‐up visits were scheduled at 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively, with additional visits thereafter based on clinical need. During each visit, endoscopic evaluation of the tympanic membrane was performed to assess graft integrity, epithelialization, and possible complications. Pure‐tone audiometry was repeated at the 6‐month follow‐up to assess postoperative hearing status.

Data Collection and Outcome Measures

Demographic variables, including age and sex, as well as clinical variables such as perforation size, extent of EAC narrowing, side of surgery, operative duration, and follow‐up duration, were recorded. Audiometric data included air‐ and bone‐conduction thresholds at all measured frequencies and the calculated air–bone gap (ABG). Surgical outcomes included graft success, defined as complete closure of the tympanic membrane at the final follow‐up, and graft failure, including persistent perforation, reperforation, or infection‐related graft breakdown. Additional complications such as chorda tympani injury, taste disturbance, external canal trauma, and iatrogenic cholesteatoma were also documented when present.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical procedures were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to analysis, the distributional characteristics of continuous variables were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, Q–Q plots, and histogram evaluation. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables, median (interquartile range) for non‐normally distributed variables, and number (percentage) for categorical variables.

Audiological parameters, including frequency‐specific air‐conduction thresholds at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 4 kHz and mean ABG, were compared between preoperative and postoperative assessments using the paired‐samples t‐test if normality assumptions were satisfied. When non‐normal distribution was observed, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was employed. Hearing gain was calculated as the numerical difference between postoperative and preoperative ABG values. In addition, the magnitude of postoperative improvement in ABG was quantified using Cohen's d, calculated from pooled SDs, to determine effect size and clinical relevance.

Postoperative tympanic membrane integrity was classified dichotomously as “graft success” or “graft failure.” Categorical variables were compared between outcome groups using Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test when expected cell counts were < 5. Continuous variables, such as age, perforation size, operative duration, and audiometric thresholds, were compared across outcome categories using the independent‐samples t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the normality of distribution.

To determine independent predictors of graft failure, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed. Variables demonstrating clinical relevance and/or p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into the model. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Model fit was examined using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test, and variance inflation factors were evaluated to exclude multicollinearity.

A subgroup analysis was performed based on the degree of EAC narrowing. Patients were stratified into mild, moderate, and severe narrowing groups according to predefined anatomical criteria. These criteria were based on intraoperative endoscopic assessment of anterior tympanic membrane visibility as described above. Audiological changes, hearing gain, and graft outcomes were compared across these subgroups using one‐way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, with post hoc pairwise comparisons (Tukey or Dunn tests) administered where appropriate.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted to evaluate the discriminative ability of key variables, such as perforation size and severity of EAC narrowing, for predicting graft failure and suboptimal hearing gain. The area under the curve (AUC), optimal cut‐off values (Youden index), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. AUC values were interpreted as follows: 0.5–0.6 poor, 0.6–0.7 fair, 0.7–0.8 acceptable, 0.8–0.9 excellent, and > 0.9 outstanding discriminative performance.

A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power version 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). For paired audiometric comparisons, assuming an effect size of 0.80 (large effect, based on preliminary clinical data), α = 0.05, and power (1 − β) = 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 26 patients. For the logistic regression model, with an anticipated medium effect size (OR 2.5–3.0) and up to five predictor variables, the minimum sample requirement was estimated at 56 participants [5]. Therefore, the final sample of 70 patients exceeded the required power thresholds for all primary and secondary analyses. A two‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Discussion

This study evaluated the outcomes of an inverted tympanomeatal flap elevation technique in patients with large anterior tympanic membrane perforations and narrow EACs, a group that is traditionally considered challenging in middle ear surgery [21, 22]. Overall, the technique was associated with a high graft success rate of 95.7% and a statistically and clinically meaningful improvement in hearing thresholds, with a large effect size for ABG reduction. These findings suggest that inverted flap elevation may represent a useful option in anatomically constrained cases; however, they should be interpreted cautiously in light of the study's retrospective design and lack of a control group.

The graft success rate observed in this series is comparable to or slightly higher than many reports of endoscopic or microscopic tympanoplasty for anterior perforations, where closure rates typically range between 80% and 95% depending on perforation size, technique, and patient selection [20, 23]. The absence of chorda tympani injury, canal wall trauma, or iatrogenic cholesteatoma is also reassuring, especially considering that flap manipulation in the anterior quadrant is a recognized risk factor for these complications [24]. It is plausible that elevating the tympanomeatal flap from medial to lateral, rather than the conventional lateral‐to‐medial direction, reduces torsion and excessive traction on the flap, thereby improving safety in narrow anatomical corridors.

From an audiological standpoint, the mean ABG decreased from 23.44 dB preoperatively to 13.52 dB postoperatively, with a mean hearing gain of approximately 10 dB. The corresponding Cohen's d of 1.00 indicates a large effect size, consistent with clinically meaningful improvement in conductive hearing [25]. Frequency‐specific air‐conduction thresholds improved significantly at all tested frequencies, suggesting that the technique not only achieves anatomical closure but also restores effective sound transmission across a broad frequency range. While these gains are in line with other cartilage‐based tympanoplasty techniques, especially in large or marginal perforations [26], direct comparisons cannot be drawn due to methodological differences and the absence of a parallel control group in the present study.

One of the most clinically relevant findings is the association between anatomical factors and graft outcomes. Larger perforation size and more severe EAC narrowing were more frequently observed in patients with graft failure, and both parameters emerged as significant predictors in multivariable logistic regression. Perforation size was associated with a modest increase in the odds of failure per percentage point, and severe EAC narrowing conferred higher odds of an unsuccessful outcome. These associations are intuitive: extensive anterior defects and limited working space are known to compromise graft support, visualization, and stability [27, 28]. The subgroup analysis further supports this interpretation, as patients with severe EAC narrowing had higher preoperative ABG values and lower graft success rates compared with those with mild or moderate narrowing.

The ROC analysis complements these observations by suggesting potential threshold values for risk stratification. A perforation size greater than approximately 72% demonstrated acceptable discrimination for predicting graft failure, with an AUC of 0.78. Similarly, severe EAC narrowing showed fair discrimination with an AUC of 0.71. Although these cut‐off values should not be viewed as rigid clinical rules, they may help clinicians identify patients who could benefit from more meticulous graft support, closer follow‐up, or alternative reconstructive strategies. Prospective studies are needed to validate these thresholds in larger and more diverse populations.

The technical aspects of the inverted tympanomeatal flap approach may help explain the observed outcomes. Elevating the flap from medial to lateral enables controlled release of the anterior annulus and canal skin, which can improve visualization of the anterior perforation margin without excessive drilling or canal wall manipulation. This may be particularly advantageous in patients with a narrow EAC, in whom standard flap elevation and anterior canal work can be technically demanding [21, 29]. Additionally, positioning cartilage over the malleus handle with anterior and posterior perichondrial support likely provides a stable scaffold that conforms to the annulus and canal wall, supporting graft integration and reducing the risk of lateralization or anterior blunting [30]. These mechanistic considerations, while speculative, are consistent with the favorable anatomical and functional results observed in this series and with prior reports of cartilage‐based techniques in challenging perforations [31, 32].

At the same time, the results should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority over established methods such as anterior pull‐through, loop overlay, or mediolateral graft techniques, as no direct comparisons were performed. It is also important to note that all operations were performed by a single surgeon with specific experience in endoscopic ear surgery. The learning curve and reproducibility of this technique in other hands, particularly in centers with different caseloads or resources, remain unknown. Thus, while the findings support the feasibility and potential utility of inverted flap elevation in selected patients, they should be seen as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive.

From a broader clinical perspective, the high closure rate, low complication profile, and meaningful hearing improvement observed in this cohort suggest that inverted tympanomeatal flap elevation can be safely considered in patients with anterior perforations and narrow EACs for whom conventional approaches are expected to be technically challenging. The technique may be especially useful when a purely endoscopic, transcanal route is preferred to avoid retroauricular incisions and more extensive soft tissue dissection. However, careful patient selection, meticulous flap handling, and strict attention to graft support remain essential, as even small technical errors in this anatomic region can compromise outcomes.

This study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design is inherently prone to selection bias and relies on the accuracy and completeness of existing clinical records. Patients with incomplete data or insufficient follow‐up were excluded, which may have led to a slight overestimation of graft success if those lost to follow‐up had poorer outcomes. Second, there was no control group treated with conventional underlay, overlay, or other modified techniques, precluding any direct comparison of efficacy or safety. Consequently, the results can only demonstrate that the inverted flap technique is feasible and appears effective in this cohort, not that it is superior to alternative methods.

Third, all surgeries were performed by a single surgeon at a single tertiary center, which, while ensuring technical consistency, limits the generalizability of the findings. The outcomes may reflect the surgeon's individual expertise in endoscopic ear surgery and may not be fully reproducible in different settings or during the early stages of the learning curve. Fourth, the number of graft failures was relatively small (n = 3), which reduces the statistical robustness of the multivariable logistic regression model and increases the risk of overfitting. The identified predictors of failure—perforation size and severe EAC narrowing—should therefore be interpreted with caution and validated in larger samples.

Fifth, the assessment of EAC narrowing was based on endoscopic visualization criteria rather than standardized morphometric measurements. Although this approach reflects real‐world clinical practice, it introduces a degree of subjectivity and may vary between observers. More objective methods, such as three‐dimensional imaging or quantitative canal measurements, could provide more precise stratification in future studies. Sixth, audiological evaluation was primarily based on pure‐tone audiometry and ABG analysis; additional measures such as speech discrimination scores, tympanometry, or patient‐reported outcome measures (e.g., hearing‐related quality‐of‐life questionnaires) were not systematically collected. As a result, the broader functional impact and patient satisfaction associated with this technique remain incompletely characterized.

Finally, the follow‐up duration, although on average longer than 2 years, was heterogeneous across patients. While this period is generally sufficient to detect early and mid‐term graft failures and complications, late events such as delayed cholesteatoma formation or very late reperforations cannot be entirely excluded. Prospective, multicenter studies with standardized follow‐up protocols, larger cohorts, and comparative designs are needed to confirm these findings, refine risk stratification, and better define the long‐term role of inverted tympanomeatal flap elevation in the management of anterior tympanic membrane perforations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the inverted tympanomeatal flap elevation technique, performed via a transcanal endoscopic approach, demonstrated a high graft success rate, meaningful improvement in conductive hearing, and a low complication profile in patients with large anterior tympanic membrane perforations and narrow EACs. Anatomical variables—most notably perforation size and the degree of EAC narrowing—were found to influence graft outcomes and may serve as useful parameters for preoperative risk stratification. Although these findings support the feasibility and potential utility of this technique in anatomically challenging cases, the lack of a control group, the small number of graft failures, and the single‐surgeon design necessitate cautious interpretation. Further prospective, controlled, and multicenter studies are warranted to validate these observations and to more clearly define the comparative effectiveness of inverted tympanomeatal flap elevation relative to other established techniques for anterior perforation repair.
