A frameshift variant in FAM129C contributes to achalasia through B cell responses against the GABAA receptor
Authors: Xiao-Qing Li, Xin-Yue Li, Wei-Feng Chen, Zi-Ye Xu, Zu-Qiang Liu, Yun Wang, Ji-Yuan Zhang, Ya-Yun Gu, Lu Yao, Yan-Fang Tan, Xiang-Jun Chen, Bo Deng, Ke-Hao Wang, Jia-Qi Xu, Meng-Jiang He, Zi-Han Geng, Ke-Yang Fan, Zhao-Chao Zhang, Li Wang, An-Yi Xiang, Hai-Ting Pan, Zhi-Bin Hu, Yun-Li Xie, Cheng Wang, Ping-Hong Zhou, Quan-Lin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Achalasia is a rare esophageal motility disorder of poorly understood etiology. Here, we perform a large trio-based whole-genome sequencing study of achalasia and identify a recessively inherited frameshift variant in FAM129C (p.Ala454fs). A CRISPR/Cas9-engineered Fam129c-mutant mouse model recapitulating key features of achalasia was established, including growth retardation, elevated lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure, and selective loss of inhibitory neurons. Multi-omic analyses revealed substantial B cell expansion and activation within the LES, accompanied by enhanced humoral immune responses. Time-course experiments demonstrated that B cell accumulation preceded overt neuronal loss, while B cell depletion via anti-CD20 antibodies or intravenous immunoglobulin treatment partially rescued the phenotypes. Further protein profiling and cell-based assays suggested that the GABAA receptor may represent one potential neuronal antigen targeted by circulating autoantibodies. Together, these findings identify FAM129C as a genetic contributor to achalasia and support a neuroimmune mechanism in which B cell activation and autoantibody-mediated responses contribute to inhibitory neuronal injury. These results provide important insights into achalasia pathogenesis and highlight the potential of immunomodulatory strategies for disease intervention in the early stage.

Introduction

Achalasia is a rare primary esophageal motility disorder, characterized by elevated lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure and impaired esophageal emptying1,2. The key pathophysiological feature of achalasia lies in the abnormality of LES myenteric plexus3. Histological staining of LES specimens from patients has revealed a marked reduction in inhibitory intermuscular neurons, particularly those expressing neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS)3–6. Despite decades of investigation, the molecular mechanisms underlying the neuronal degeneration remain poorly understood, largely due to the lack of robust and reproducible animal models. Most treatment options for achalasia are primarily aimed at relieving LES tension mechanically and associated with limited clinical efficacy and high recurrence rate7–12.

To date, three major hypotheses have been proposed to explain the loss of esophageal myenteric neurons in achalasia: genetic predisposition, viral infection, and immune dysregulation13–15. Among these, genetic predisposition is generally considered the foundational factor, providing the biological basis upon which environmental and immunological triggers may act16–20. Viral infections may further exacerbate aberrant immune responses in genetically susceptible individuals, ultimately leading to neuronal injury21. Studies have shown that achalasia phenotypes are rarely observed in populations without genetic mutations, even in those with viral infections or immune abnormal activation17,22–24. Nevertheless, the interplay and temporal sequence among these mechanisms remain unclear. Specifically, whether genetic defects initiate neuronal vulnerability and trigger secondary immune activation, or whether immune dysregulation itself acts as the primary pathogenic driver remains unclear. Direct evidence linking specific genetic mutations to downstream pathogenic cascades is still relatively limited.

Previous studies investigating genetic susceptibility in achalasia have largely focused on variants in neuron-related genes (such as NOS1 and SPRY2) or loci within the HLA region25–32. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) provides as an unbiased strategy for identifying disease-associated variants and has revealed several achalasia-related loci involving both neuronal and immune pathways33. These findings suggested that neurodegeneration and immune dysregulation may interact in the pathogenesis of achalasia. However, the functional validation of candidate genes and clarification of their mechanistic roles remain insufficient.

To address this gap, we conducted a large familial trio-based WGS study of achalasia, including 22 probands and their unaffected parents. This analysis identified a potential pathogenic frameshift variant in FAM129C (p.Ala454fs) which exhibited a classic autosomal recessive inheritance pattern and was further validated in sporadic cases. To investigate its functional consequences, we generated Fam129c mutant mice (Fam129c-mut) using CRSPR/Cas9 technology. These mice exhibited achalasia-like phenotypes at anatomical, histological, and functional levels. Further mechanistic explorations revealed that the mutation promoted the production of anti-GABAA receptor autoantibodies through activation of B cells within the LES tissues, ultimately contributing to the development of achalasia.

Results

Identification of a FAM129C frameshift variant associated with achalasia

To delineate the genetic determinants of achalasia, WGS was performed among proband-parent trio families (Fig. 1A). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the enrolled cohort are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. A total of 22 probands with primary achalasia from 22 trio families were included in the genetic analysis, comprising 14 females and 8 males (female-to-male ratio 1.75:1). The mean age at sampling was 22.27 ± 8.74 years, and the estimated age at symptom onset was 19.11 ± 7.56 years, with an average disease duration of 3.13 ± 2.85 years. The mean preoperative Eckardt score was 6.27 ± 2.05. According to high-resolution esophageal manometry based on the Chicago Classification, 6 patients (27.3%) were classified as type I achalasia and 16 patients (72.7%) as type II achalasia, with no cases of type III achalasia identified. Loss-of-function variants under recessive or de novo inheritance models that produced truncated proteins were further evaluated. Ultimately, three candidate pathogenic variants were identified, including two recessive variants (GMPPA p.Leu89fs and FAM129C p.Ala454fs) and one de novo variant (KIF26B p.Arg389*) (Supplementary Table 2). Among them, FAM129C, a gene encoding B cell-specific plasma-membrane protein emerged as a focal point, since the possible effects of this variant on the functions of B cells and the pathogenesis of achalasia remain unexplored. Sanger sequencing confirmed a homozygous frameshift variant in FAM129C (CTGGCAGCACCGTT → T, chr19:17653035) in one trio family with heterozygous carrier parents (Fig. 1B). This homozygous variant was also identified in one carrier among 100 Chinese sporadic achalasia samples33, and a case-control analysis against East Asian controls from gnomAD (n = 4980) revealed a significant association (1/100 cases; 1/4980 controls; p = 0.039; OR = 50.04)34. The variant is located in chr 19, and it introduces a 13 bp non-trinucleotide frameshift (Fig. 1C). To further assess the impact of this variant on FAM129C protein structure, we performed AlphaFold3-based structural modeling for both the WT and p.Ala454fs variant FAM129C proteins (Fig. 1D). Structural comparison shows that the p.Ala454fs variant leads to a clear loss of the entire C-terminal portion of the protein. In the aligned models, this missing region is highlighted in red, illustrating that the remaining N-terminal region aligns well between the two forms and the mutant structure lacks a substantial segment present in the wild type. These prediction results confirm that the variant results in a pronounced deletion of this portion of the protein. Both RT-qPCR and Western blot based on LES tissues of WT and Fam129c-mut mice demonstrated that the frameshift mutation could significantly reduce the mRNA and protein expression of FAM129C (Fig. 1E, F). In addition, FAM129C protein cannot be detected in the LES tissues of Fam129c-mut mice, which further validates the substantial structure change caused by the frameshift mutation (Fig. 1G). As for the cellular localization of FAM129C, mIHC showed that FAM129C robustly colocalized with CD20 + B cells as reported in previous studies35–37, while no detectable overlap was observed with α-SMA+ smooth muscle cells, Tuj1+ neurons, VWF+ endothelial cells, CD8 + T cells, or CD68+ myeloid cells in WT mice (Fig. 1H). In addition, we validated the specificity of the antibody against FAM129C using an in vitro siRNA-mediated knockdown model in WIL2-S cell line (Supplementary Fig. 1). Taken together, we identified FAM129C as a novel B cell-associated gene associated with achalasia, providing a critical framework for exploring genetic-immunological crosstalk in achalasia.

Fam129c-mut mice exhibit achalasia-like phenotypes

To understand whether the variant in FAM129C contributes to achalasia, the Fam129c frameshift mutation mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, and the mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 2). Compared with WT mice, Fam129c-mut mice appeared to be visibly smaller in body size (Fig. 2A) and displayed significantly lower body weight gain (Fig. 2B). With the feeding of equal amounts of food (m0 = 100 g), Fam129c-mut mice consumed less food over 24 h than WT mice (Fig. 2C). After oral gavage of the semi-solid charcoal paste, we observed overt food retention in the lower esophagus of Fam129c-mut mice but not in WT controls (Fig. 2D). Quantitative analysis showed a significantly higher 10 second esophageal retention rate in Fam129c-mut mice, indicating delayed esophageal emptying (Fig. 2E left). Both the external diameter and perimeter of the LES in Fam129c-mut mice significantly decreased (Fig. 2E middle and right). Additionally, the circular and longitudinal muscle layers of the LES in Fam129c-mut mice were thickened, resulting in a decrease of the inner diameter and relative stenosis of the LES (Fig. 2F, G). Functionally, Fam129c-mut mice demonstrated impaired esophageal motility. It was revealed by esophageal manometry that the LES pressure in Fam129c-mut mice was elevated (Fig. 2H), and we conducted EFS on LES strips that were pre-contracted with carbochol. WT mice exhibited clear relaxation waves after EFS, while these were absent in Fam129c-mut mice (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, analysis of tension-time graphs demonstrated significantly reduced relaxation responses in Fam129-mut mice versus WT controls, as indicated by smaller areas under the curve (Fig. 2J, K).

Histologically, whole-mount mIHC staining of pan-neurons (Tuj1), inhibitory neurons (nNOS and VIP) and excitatory neurons (CHAT) was performed in LES tissues (Fig. 2L). It was observed that Fam129c-mut mice had a significantly lower number of nNOS- and VIP-positive inhibitory neurons compared to WT controls (Fig. 2M), while the CHAT-positive excitatory neurons were comparable between the two groups (Fig. 2M). These findings indicated a selective loss of inhibitory neurons, which was a characteristic feature of achalasia pathology.

Collectively, we constructed the Fam129c-mut mouse model in this study, which recapitulated the key clinical and histological features of human achalasia, providing a basis for following mechanistic studies and preclinical evaluation of targeted therapies.

FAM129C mutation promotes achalasia-like phenotypes through a B cell-dependent pathway

Given the achalasia-like phenotypes in Fam129c-mut mice, the underlying immunological mechanisms were explored subsequently. Bulk RNA-seq analysis of LES showed that the expression of Cd79a, Cd19 and other immunoglobulin-related proteins exhibited an increasing trend in Fam129c-mut mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 3A). To investigate the temporal relationship between B cell infiltration and neuronal dysfunction, we performed a time-course analysis in Fam129c-mut and WT mice at 1, 4, and 8 weeks of age (Supplementary Fig. 3). At 1 week, CD20 immunofluorescence staining revealed the presence of B cells in the LES of Fam129c-mut mice, whereas nNOS-positive inhibitory neurons appeared preserved. Functional assessment was not feasible at this stage due to the small size of the LES. At 4 weeks, B cell infiltration persisted, and EFS recordings demonstrated a mildly attenuated relaxation response in Fam129c-mut mice, despite the absence of marked structural loss of inhibitory neurons. By 8 weeks, progressive accumulation of B cells was accompanied by a substantial reduction in nNOS-positive neurons, together with near-complete loss of LES relaxation in response to EFS. This temporal pattern indicates that immune infiltration emerges early and precedes, or coincides with, progressive inhibitory neuronal dysfunction.

To further determine whether B cells play a causal role in this process, we performed B cell depletion experiments using anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies in 8-week-old mice, corresponding to the early symptomatic stage (Fig. 3C). B cell depletion resulted in a significant increase in nNOS-positive inhibitory neurons in the LES of Fam129c-mut mice (Fig. 3D–E) and partially restored LES relaxation responses in EFS assays (Fig. 3F). These findings indicate that the pathogenic effects of the Fam129c mutation are mediated, at least in part, through a B cell–dependent mechanism. To validate the specificity of the B cell-mediated mechanism, we performed longitudinal follow-up analyses after anti-CD20 treatment. Mice were evaluated at 3, 4, and 6 weeks after antibody administration, corresponding to progressive B cell repopulation. Notably, gradual repopulation of B cells in the LES was accompanied by reappearance of inhibitory neuronal loss (Fig. 3G) and progressive deterioration of LES relaxation (Fig. 3H), indicating that restoration of the disease phenotype parallels B cell recovery.

Finally, we investigated whether B cell depletion could reverse disease at later stages. In contrast to the early intervention at 8 weeks, anti-CD20 treatment in aged mice (~1 year old) failed to improve the achalasia-like phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 4). This observation suggests that once substantial neuronal degeneration and structural remodeling have occurred, the disease becomes largely irreversible, consistent with the progressive neurodegeneration in advanced achalasia.

Collectively, these findings indicate that B cell infiltration represents an early pathogenic event that precedes and contributes to progressive inhibitory neuronal dysfunction, and that immune-mediated mechanisms are particularly critical during the early stage of disease development.

Fam129-mut mice displayed expanded B cells in the LES and increased immunoglobulin production

To better characterize the changes of B cells in the LES tissues, scRNA-seq was performed on LES tissues of Fam129c-mut and WT mice. After dimensionality reduction, unsupervised clustering, and annotation, 14 major cell types were identified (Fig. 4A), each characterized by representative marker genes consistent with previously reported literature38,39 (Fig. 4B). Among them, B cells showed significant expansion in Fam129c-mut mice compared to WT controls, while the proportions of plasma cells were comparable between two groups (Fig. 4C, D). We also found that Fam129c was specifically expressed in B cells (Fig. 4E), which is consistent with immunofluorescence results in Fig. 1H. To validate the single-cell transcriptomic findings, we performed flow cytometric analyses of LES tissues from Fam129c-mut and WT mice. Consistent with the scRNA-seq results, CD19 + B cells were significantly expanded in the LES of Fam129c-mut mice compared with WT controls (Fig. 4F–G). In contrast, the frequencies of CD138+ plasma cells and CD138 + CD27 + + CD38 + + plasma cells did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 4H, I, and Supplementary Fig. 5B, C). These findings indicate that B cells, rather than plasma cells, represent the predominant expansion population in the LES. Consistent with this interpretation, clinical evaluation of the individual carrying the FAM129C frameshift mutation revealed no evidence of plasma cell dyscrasia at baseline, and no related manifestations were observed during a 10-year follow-up period (Supplementary Table 3), making plasma cell dyscrasia an unlikely confounding factor in this study. To further characterize functional alterations within the expanded B cell compartment, we performed GO enrichment analysis for differentially expressed genes in B cells and plasma cells isolated from the LES of Fam129c-mut and WT mice. As shown in Fig. 4J, B cells from Fam129c-mut mice displayed significant enrichment of pathways related to immunoglobulin-mediated immune response, B cell activation, and B cell proliferation, indicating enhanced B cell activity within the LES microenvironment. Consistently, GO analysis of plasma cells revealed enrichment of pathways associated with immunoglobulin production and humoral immune responses (Fig.4K). Notably, although plasma cell frequency was not significantly increased, these transcriptional signatures suggest that plasma cells in the mutant group are functionally activated and exhibit enhanced antibody-secreting capacity. Together, these findings support a model in which local expansion and activation of B cells in the LES promote increased immunoglobulin production, rather than numerical accumulation of plasma cells, thereby contributing to antibody-mediated pathogenic mechanisms. Identifying GABAA receptor autoantibody as a candidate contributor to inhibitory neuron dysfunction in Fam129c-mut mice

To explore the direct impairing effects of immunoglobulins from Fam129-mut mice on inhibitory neurons, we administered IVIG or PBS (vehicle) once weekly into WT and Fam129c-mut mice according to the schematic diagram in Fig. 5A. In vitro, with the treatment of PBS, primary enteric neurons cultured with serum of Fam129c-mut mice displayed obvious morphological alterations compared with neurons treated with WT serum (Fig. 5B). However, this difference disappeared after IVIG treatment, indicating the cytotoxic effects of autoantibodies on enteric neurons (Fig. 5B). Consistent with these findings, in vivo analyses showed that IVIG administration partially restored the abundance of nNOS-positive inhibitory neurons in the LES of Fam129c-mut mice (Fig. 5C, D). Functional assessment using EFS further demonstrated that the impaired LES relaxation responses in Fam129c-mut mice were also partially rescued after IVIG treatment (Fig. 5E, F). Results of TBA further confirmed that the presence of serum auto-antibodies in both FAM129C-mut human and Fam129c-mut mice, whereas no immunoreactivity was detected in corresponding WT controls (Fig. 5G). Cell type co-localization analysis revealed that these tissue-binding signals were restricted to Tuj1+ neurons and showed no detectable overlap with α-SMA+ smooth muscle cells or CD20 + B cells (Fig. 5H), indicating neuron-specific antigen-antibody reactivity within the LES.

To explore the candidate antigen targets, we screened several upregulated neuron-related antigens in the LES tissues of Fam129c-mut mice using protein profiling. According to the results, the inhibitory γ-aminobutyric acid-type A receptor (GABAA receptor) was the most significantly elevated neuron-related protein in Fam129c-mut mice (Fig. 5I), followed by MOG, AQP4, and GAD. CBA experiments further demonstrated that serum from FAM129C-mut human and Fam129c-mut mice selectively reacted with GABRA1-overexpressing HEK293T cells, comparable to positive-control serum from autoimmune encephalitis patients with established anti-GABRA1 antibodies (Fig. 5J–L). No immunoreactivity was observed in MOG-, AQP4-, or GAD2-overexpressing cells (Supplementary Fig. 6). Altogether, these results suggest that humoral immune responses mediated by B cell–derived antibodies contribute to inhibitory neuronal dysfunction and LES motility impairment in Fam129c-mut mice, with GABAA receptor representing one potential neuronal target, and that immunoglobulin-targeting intervention with IVIG can partially rescue these pathological features.

Discussion

Achalasia is a rare esophageal motility disorder characterized by progressive impairment of inhibitory myenteric neurons within the LES40–42. Despite its clinical significance, the mechanisms initiating the disease remain largely unclear. Genetic predisposition has long been suggested as an important factor in achalasia development7,13–15,43–47. However, specific causative mutations have remained elusive due to limited sample sizes and the lack of reliable animal models for mechanistic investigations. In the present study, through trio-based whole-genome sequencing combined with functional validation in a genetically engineered mouse model, we identify FAM129C as a previously unrecognized genetic contributor to achalasia. Our findings further demonstrate that a frameshift mutation in FAM129C promotes abnormal B cell expansion and humoral immune responses within the LES, which are associated with selective loss of inhibitory neurons and impaired LES relaxation. Together, these results provide genetic and mechanistic evidence linking immune dysregulation to the development of achalasia.

Previous genetic studies of achalasia have largely focused on neuronal genes or immune-associated loci within the HLA region. However, direct connections between genetic defects and downstream pathogenic mechanisms remained limited. Our identification of a frameshift variant in FAM129C, also known as B cell-specific plasma-membrane protein 1 (BCNP1), a recently described protein involved in BCR signal transduction35,36,48, expands the genetic landscape of achalasia and suggests that immune pathways may play an important role in disease initiation. Importantly, CRISPR-engineered Fam129c-mut mice recapitulated key clinical and pathological features of achalasia, including impaired esophageal emptying, elevated LES pressure, and selective loss of inhibitory neurons. These findings establish a functional link between FAM129C mutation and the core features of achalasia.

Transcriptomic profiling revealed enrichment of pathways related to B cell activation and immunoglobulin-mediated immune responses were significantly upregulated in Fam129c-mut mice. To thoroughly clarify the role of B cells in achalasia pathogenesis, we performed temporal analysis of immune infiltration and disease phenotypes, B cell depletion and repopulation experiments, and stage-specific therapeutic interventions. Achalasia is widely recognized as a disorder characterized by progressive inhibitory neuronal loss and fibrotic remodeling, particularly in advanced Type I disease1. Our findings should therefore be interpreted within a stage-dependent framework rather than as a binary distinction between immunological and neurodegenerative mechanisms. In our time-course analysis, CD20 + B cell expansion in the LES was detectable as early as 1 week of age, preceding overt nNOS+ neuron damage and measurable impairment of LES relaxation. Anti-CD20–mediated B cell depletion partially preserved inhibitory neurons and improved LES function, whereas B cell repopulation was accompanied by recurrence of neuronal injury and motility dysfunction. However, in aged mice, B cell depletion no longer effectively reversed the achalasia-like phenotypes, suggesting that progressive structural damage eventually becomes less reversible. Together, these findings support a model in which immune dysregulation operates early under a genetic background, potentially initiating or accelerating subsequent neurodegeneration and fibrosis. The partial recoverability observed with IVIG or anti-CD20 treatment is therefore most consistent with an immune-modifiable early phase of disease, rather than with established end-stage neurodegenerative remodeling. One thing to be clarified is that our current analyses primarily rely on nNOS+ neuronal quantification and LES functional assessment, which do not fully capture the earliest neuromuscular synaptic alterations. Future studies integrating ultrastructural imaging and high-resolution neurophysiological approaches will be important to characterize the dynamic changes of neuromuscular synapses during the initiation and progression of achalasia.

In contrast to the substantial expansion of B cells within LES tissues, both scRNA-seq and flow cytometry revealed that the abundance of plasma cells did not significantly increase. Although plasma cells represent the terminal antibody-secreting effector cells, their differentiation and long-term maintenance typically depend on specialized survival niches. The absence of a significant increase in plasma cell abundance in the LES, despite marked B cell infiltration, suggests that the LES is unlikely to function as a major plasma cell maintenance niche. Instead, the LES may represent a target tissue where infiltrating B cells and circulating autoantibodies contribute to neuronal injury and impaired LES relaxation.

Although anti-neuronal antibodies have been detected in a subset of achalasia patients49–51, direct evidence supporting a functional role of humoral immunity in disease pathogenesis remains limited. In the present study, several lines of evidence suggest that antibody-mediated immune responses may contribute to neuronal injury in achalasia. First, therapeutic modulation of humoral immunity influenced disease phenotypes in our model. IVIG treatment partially ameliorated neuropathic changes and improved LES function in Fam129c-mut mice, supporting the possibility that antibody-mediated processes participate in disease development. Consistent with this observation, in vitro assays demonstrated that serum from Fam129c-mut mice impaired the growth and morphology of primary enteric neurons, suggesting the presence of circulating neurotoxic factors. Further analyses provided evidence implicating humoral immune responses. TBA revealed neuron-specific immunoreactivity of serum from Fam129c-mut mice and from a human carrier of the FAM129C mutation, indicating that circulating antibodies recognize antigens within enteric neurons. Complementary CBA demonstrated immunoreactivity with GABRA1-expressing cells, whereas no signal was detected in cells overexpressing several other candidate neuron-related antigens identified by protein profiling. Notably, GABAergic signaling has been reported to be implicated in the regulation of inhibitory enteric neuronal circuits, including neuronal populations involved in LES relaxation such as VIP-positive neurons52. Although we have to recognize that our findings regarding candidate neuronal antigens should be interpreted with caution and do not establish GABAA receptor antibodies as the exclusive pathogenic factor given the complexity of antibody-mediated mechanisms and the possibility that multiple neuronal antigens may be involved, these findings support a model in which B cell activation and downstream humoral immune responses under genetic susceptibility may contribute to selective injury of inhibitory enteric neurons in achalasia. In this framework, antibody-mediated mechanisms may represent one component of broader neuroimmune interactions underlying the initiation of achalasia.

By integrating trio-based WGS, CRISPR-engineered mouse model, and multi-omics analyses, the present study bridges three previously disconnected aspects of achalasia: genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation, and neuronal injury. This triad has long been controversial but has not been systematically investigated. Our work provides mechanistic insight linking these elements into a coherent disease model. We established a novel mouse model that recapitulates multiple aspects of achalasia based on the largest familial WGS screening to date. In addition, our results suggest that abnormal expansion and activation of B cells may promote humoral immune responses that contribute to achalasia-like phenotypes in mutant mice, and GABAA receptor was identified as a candidate neuron-related target antigen of circulating autoantibodies. Overall, although more detailed studies are needed to determine the precise mechanisms, safety, and clinical applicability, our findings highlight the potential of immunomodulatory strategies, such as IVIG or B cell–targeted therapies, as etiological treatments for achalasia patients with relatively early-stage disease. Furthermore, this work provides a generalizable research framework for investigating rare and complex disorders with poorly understood etiologies.

However, some limitations existed in this study. Firstly, although the FAM129C p.Ala454fs frameshift mutation was identified across achalasia patients, due to the complexity of human body, the gene-edited mouse model cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of human disease. For example, due to the short length and narrow diameter of the murine esophagus, high-resolution multi-sensor manometry—routinely applied in clinical practice to differentiate subtypes of esophageal motility disorders—cannot be reliably implemented in this model. Thus, precise subtype classification according to the Chicago criteria is not feasible. This limitation is shared by previously published studies, which were likewise restricted to single-channel recordings25,31,53. Future investigations employing larger animal models that permit multi-channel manometry may enable more physiologically refined characterization of esophageal motility patterns. Secondly, this mutation appears relatively infrequent in sporadic achalasia cases, suggesting that the mechanisms described here may apply only to a subset of patients. However, we validated the significant association between this mutation and the risk of achalasia using genome data of another independent sporadic achalasia cohort and case-control analysis. Thirdly, we focused on local immune events within the LES and did not investigate mechanisms regarding the systemic origin of the expanded B cell population (such as bone marrow). Finally, whether gene-editing approaches can reverse achalasia remains unexplored. If feasible, this could offer a new insight to precision therapies targeting not only FAM129C, but also other causative genes.

In conclusion, our study identified FAM129C as a novel genetic susceptibility gene for achalasia, and Fam129c-mut mice exhibited multiple features that closely resembled human achalasia. Additionally, our findings suggest a previously unrecognized immune mechanism in which aberrant B cell activation and humoral immune responses may contribute to inhibitory neuronal injury. These results link B cell dysregulation with neurodegenerative processes in achalasia and highlight the potential of immune-targeted interventions to shift treatment strategies from symptomatic relief toward addressing underlying disease mechanisms (Fig. 6). Methods

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS)

Peripheral blood from 22 proband-parent achalasia trio families was collected from Endoscopy Center, Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Approval No. B2021-865R2), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All included individuals provided written informed consent to publish information including age, sex, and diagnosis, etc. Genomic DNA was extracted and sent for WGS (Illumina HiSeq/NovaSeq, 30× coverage). Reads were aligned to GRCh37 using BWA-MEM, with duplicates removed via Picard. Variant calling and joint genotyping were performed using GATK (v3.8)54. A machine learning-based quality control model (61 genomic features) filtered variants. Rare (<1% frequency) homozygous or de novo loss-of-function (LoF) variants were included, annotated against gnomAD55 and GENCODE v19 via VEP56. LoF variants included nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site (±2 bp) mutations.

Fam129c mutant mouse model generation

All mouse models used in this study were generated on a C57BL/6 background purchased from Phenotek Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd, stock no. PNK0005. Female mice were used for all experiments. According to the results of WGS, the sgRNA sequence in line with the human mutation was designed, which was successfully verified by BLAST. The sgRNA sequence with a high quality score was finally selected in this study: 5’-ACCAGGTCGACCGTTTTGGG-3’. Based on the CRISPR-Cas9 technology, Fam129c-mut mice with a TGGCAAAACCCTT deletion were successfully obtained, and the mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 2).

All mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free animal facility under controlled conditions (12 h light/12 h dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, 50–60% humidity) with ad libitum access to food and water. Experimental and control animals were co-housed to minimize environmental variation. Mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. Mice of 16-week-age were used for experiments unless otherwise specified. All animal operation procedures were in strict agreement with the ethical guidelines approved by the Scientific Research Department of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University.

Protein structural prediction

The predicted three-dimensional structures of wild-type and mutant FAM129C proteins were generated using Alphafold357. The amino acid sequences of the full-length WT FAM129C and the p.Ala454fs mutant protein were submitted separately for structure prediction. Structural models were visualized and aligned using PyMOL (v3.0.3) to assess conformational differences between WT and mutant proteins. The truncated region resulting from the frameshift mutation was identified based on sequence alignment and structural comparison.

Cell type co-localization of FAM129C

LES and spleen tissues were harvested from WT mice, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Given the high abundance of immune cells in the spleen, co-localization of FAM129C with immune cells was analyzed in spleen tissues, whereas co-localization with other cell types was examined in LES tissues. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed using antibodies against FAM129C (1:50, ABIN2170578, ANTIBODIES ONLINE), CD20 (1:50, ab64088, Abcam), α-SMA (1:250, ab124964, Abcam), VWF (1:100, ab287962, Abcam), Tuj1 (1:500, ab18207, Abcam), CD8 (1:200, ab316778, Abcam), and CD68 (1:100, ab283654, Abcam).

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from mouse LES tissues or WIL2-S cells (purchased from ProCell CL-1039) using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit, and quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR system. Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to expression of a control gene (β-actin) for each experiment. Primer sequences of human FAM129C were 5’-AGGAGGGAGCAGATGAGGAA-3’ (forward) and 5’-AGAAGGCGGTGGGCAGAGT-3’ (reverse); human β-actin were 5’-AGAGCTACGAGCTGCCTGAC-3’ (forward) and 5’-AGCACTGTGTTGGCGTACAG-3’ (reverse); mouse FAM129C were 5’-TGCCTGTGTCAGAAGGTGGA-3’ (forward) and 5’-CCCTGGATGAGAAGGGTCC-3’ (reverse); mouse β-actin were 5’-CGGTTCCGATGCCCTGAGGCTCTT-3’ (forward) and 5’-CGTCACACTTCATGATGGAATTGA −3’ (reverse). All these primers were purchased from Tsingke Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.

Western Blot analysis

Protein lysates from mouse LES tissues or WIL2-S cells were prepared in RIPA buffer (Beyotime) with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime), and the protein lysates were boiled in SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Yamaybio Inc) for 15 min at 95°C. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE gels (Yamaybio Inc) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Beyotime). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated with primary antibodies against FAM129C (1:500, ABIN2170578, ANTIBODIES ONLINE) and β-tubulin (1:6000, 10094-1-AP, Proteintech) at 4°C overnight, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody incubation at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the protein bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (Biosharp).

RNA interference

siRNAs against FAM129C were synthesized by Genomeditech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. The negative control siRNA was obtained from Qiagen (AllStars Negative Control siRNA, 1027281). All siRNA transfections were performed with Lipo8000 (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were harvested for mRNA and protein analyses by real-time quantitative PCR and Western blot analysis. The siRNA sequences are listed as follows: siFAM129C#1: 5’-AAUUCUCUGUCAAUCCUCUGCTT-3’; siFAM129C#2: 5’-AGUUCUCCUUGUGACUGAACCTT-3’.

Assessment of achalasia-like phenotypes of mouse model

General phenotypic assessment

The overall appearance, body weight gain per week, and food intake per 24 hours of age-matched Fam129c-mut and WT mice were recorded.

Esophageal emptying assay

Esophageal emptying was evaluated using an oral gavage assay with a semi-solid charcoal paste. The paste was prepared by dissolving 10 g sodium carboxymethylcellulose, 16 g milk powder, 2 g activated charcoal powder, and 8 g sugar–starch mixture in 300 mL distilled water. Mice were gavaged with the prepared paste, and the animals were sacrificed 10 seconds after administration. The esophagogastric junction (EGJ) and LES were exposed and photographed for gross anatomical comparison. After measuring external diameter and perimeter of the LES, the esophagus was immediately dissected, and the retained paste was collected and weighed. The esophageal retention rate was calculated as the ratio of retained paste weight in the esophagus to the total administered weight.

Histological analysis of LES morphology

LES tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess tissue morphology. Images were captured using a light microscope. The thickness of circular and longitudinal muscle layers and the inner diameter of the LES lumen were measured using ImageJ software (v1.49).

Esophageal motility assessment

Given the short and narrow anatomy of the mouse esophagus, esophageal pressure was measured using a single-channel catheter, consistent with previous reports25,31,53. Mice were anesthetized, and a pressure catheter specifically designed for small animals was inserted into the esophagus through a gastric fundus incision. The catheter was positioned across the LES under guidance of a silk thread. Swallowing was induced by mechanical stimulation, and esophageal motility was recorded for 10 minutes.

LES strips were isolated from euthanized mice and mounted in an organ bath system connected to tension sensors to measure muscle contractility. Muscle strips were first precontracted with carbachol (CCh) to establish a baseline contraction. Electrical field stimulation (EFS) was then applied to induce LES relaxation using the following parameters: stimulation duration 15 s, frequency 20 Hz, pulse duration 400 ms, and voltage 15 V. Changes in muscle tension were recorded continuously, and relaxation responses were analyzed from the tension–time curves.

Whole-mount staining of LES tissues

LES tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and the tissues were then paraffin-embedded and sectioned. Antigen retrieval was performed by citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Subsequently, sections were permeabilized (0.3% Triton X-100), blocked (5% BSA), and incubated overnight with primary antibodies: β-III tubulin (Tuj1, 1:500, ab18207, Abcam), neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS, 1:50, ab1376, Abcam), choline acetyltransferase (CHAT, 1:250, AB143, Sigma-Aldrich), and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP, 1:200, ab272726, Abcam). Thereafter, fluorescent secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488/594/647) and DAPI counterstaining were applied. A confocal microscope (Leica SP8) was employed to obtain the images. Numbers of Tuj1-, nNOS-, VIP-, and CHAT-positive cells were counted per field using ImageJ, with 5 animals in each group.

B cell time-course analysis, and depletion and repopulations experiments

Time-course analysis of B cell infiltration and neuronal injury

To investigate the temporal relationship between immune infiltration and neuronal dysfunction, Fam129c-mut mice and WT littermates were analyzed at 1, 4, and 8 weeks of age. LES tissues were harvested for immunofluorescence staining and functional analyses. Sections were stained with antibodies against CD20 (1:50, ab64088, Abcam) and nNOS (1:50, ab1376, Abcam), followed by DAPI nuclear counterstaining. LES relaxation function was assessed using EFS to evaluate relaxation responses. Due to the small size and fragility of LES tissues in 1-week-old mice, stable EFS recordings could not be obtained at this time point.

Anti-CD20–mediated B cell depletion

For B cell depletion experiments, Fam129c-mut and WT mice were randomly assigned to two groups. Mice in the treatment group received 250 μg anti-mouse CD20 monoclonal antibody (BE0356, BioXcell) via a single intraperitoneal injection to induce B cell depletion. Control mice received 250 μg anti-human CD20 monoclonal antibody (BE0276, BioXcell) administered intraperitoneally as an isotype control. Fourteen days after treatment, mice were euthanized and LES dysfunction and myenteric neuronal injury were evaluated using the methods described above. To examine stage-dependent effects of B cell depletion, anti-CD20 injections were administered at 8 weeks of age (early stage) or ~1 year of age (advanced stage).

B cell repopulation analysis

To assess B cell repopulation following depletion, mice treated with anti-CD20 antibodies were monitored longitudinally at 3, 4, and 6 weeks after treatment corresponding to suspected B cell repopulation of 1, 2, and 4 weeks. B cell repopulation in the LES was evaluated by CD20 immunostaining. Concurrently, myenteric inhibitory neuron loss and LES relaxation dysfunction were assessed using the methods described above.

Bulk RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from LES tissues of three WT and three Fam129c-mut mice by TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the instructions of the manufacturer. Total RNA was qualified and quantified by a Nano Drop and Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples with the RNA concentration >10 ng/μL and RNA integrity/quality numbers >7 would be selected for the following RNA-seq library preparations. RNA sequencing was performed by Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Lianchuan Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Differentially expressed genes were identified using the R package edgeR (v3.42.4). Additionally, the online Metascape website tool (https://metascape.org/) was employed to conduct the enrichment analysis of bulk RNA-seq data.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-cell BCR sequencing (scBCR-seq)

LES tissues from two WT and two Fam129c-mut mice were collected, respectively. For each genotype, LES tissues from two mice were mixed and dissociated into single-cell suspensions by enzymatic digestion, followed by erythrocyte and dead cell removal. Libraries were generated by the 10X Genomics Chromium Single Cell 5’ Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Bohao Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and the data were processed with Cell Ranger (v3.0.1). After quality control, the standard pipeline of the Seurat R package (v5.1.0) was applied in the analysis of the scRNA-seq data, followed by the application of principal component analysis in the dimension reduction based on the 2000 highly variable genes. Subsequently, the “RunUMAP” function was applied in the uniform manifold approximation and projection analysis. Besides, the “FindClusters” and “FindAllMarkers” functions were employed for cell clustering and marker identification. Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses of B cells and plasma cells of Fam129c-mut mice were performed based on the clusterProfiler R package (v4.8.3).

ScBCR-seq was conducted on the same cells as scRNA-seq. Raw BCR data were processed with Cell Ranger (v3.0.1) against the human VDJ reference genome. BCR amino acid sequences were artificially translated from their nucleic acid sequences, with the filtration of incomplete or ambiguous data. Only cells with both heavy chain (IGH) and light chain (IGK/IGL) were included, resulting in 2,455 Fam129c-mut and 4,084 WT B cells. Each unique IGH(s)–IGK/IGL(s) pair was defined as a clonotype. Regarding the BCR data, clonotypes were defined based on CDR3 amino acid sequences with receptor_arms = ‘any’, metric = ‘alignment’ and default cutoff of ten. Cells harboring the clonotype that presented in at least two cells were considered to be clonal, and the number of cells with such pairs could indicate the degree of clonality of the clonotype. Besides, the diversity of BCR clone and immunoglobulin abundance between Fam129c-mut and WT mice were compared.

Flow cytometry analysis of B cells and plasma cells

To quantify the proportions of B cells and plasma cells in LES tissues between WT and Fam129c-mut mice, fresh tissues were harvested and minced into ~1 mm3 pieces, and the tissue fragments were washed several times with PBS. LES tissue fragments were enzymatically digested into single-cell suspensions using collagenase solution (100 U/mL) prepared in DMEM medium for flow cytometry sorting using BD FACSAria II instrument (BD Biosciences). Dissociated samples were filtered through the 70μm strainer, and re-suspended in the red blood cell lysis buffer (Beyotime). To minimize non-specific antibody binding, viable cells were pre-incubated with 2% TruStain FcX™ (anti-mouse CD16/32, BioLegend, #101319) on ice for 10 min. Flow cytometry antibodies used in this study were as follows: PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD45 (157611, BioLegend), PE anti-mouse CD3 (E-AB-F1013D, Elabscience), APC anti-mouse CD19 (115511, BioLegend), PE-Cy7 anti-mouse CD27 (25-0271-82, Thermofisher), FITC anti-mouse CD38 (11-0381-82, Thermofisher), and BV421 anti-mouse CD138 (566289, BD Pharmingen). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1). The gating strategy is shown in Supplementary Fig. 5A.

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) treatment

WT and Fam129c-mut mice of 8-week-age were divided into two groups respectively, with one group receiving 2 g/kg of extrinsic mouse IVIG and the other group receiving an equivalent volume of PBS as a control. Tail vein injections were administered weekly for four consecutive weeks.

Primary enteric neuron extraction and cell co-culture

Primary enteric neurons were isolated from female fetal mice via microdissection of intestinal tubes under sterile conditions. Tissues were digested enzymatically, neutralized with DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and filtered to obtain single-cell suspensions. Subsequently, cells were centrifuged (200 × g, 5 min) and resuspended in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, followed by seeding into 24-well plates at 2.4 × 105 cells/cm². After 24 h, the medium was replaced with Neural basal medium, which contained N2, B27, glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

Blood was collected from Fam129c-mut and WT mice via orbital blood sampling. Serum was separated by centrifugation (1000 × g, 10 min) and added (1 mL) into neuron cultures. The neuronal viability and morphology of the samples were observed by a light microscope.

Proteome profiling

LES tissue proteins were extracted and quantified by a BCA assay (NanoDrop). After the tryptic digestion (1:25 enzyme-to-protein ratio, 37 °C, 17 h), peptides were desalted, dried under vacuum, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. Samples were analyzed via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) on a Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with an EASY nLC1200 HPLC. Raw mass spectrometry data were processed using the Firmiana platform (v2.0), which was employed for spectral library construction and label-free quantification, enabling the identification of differentially expressed proteins between the Fam129c-mut and WT groups.

Tissue based assay (TBA)

For TBA, LES tissue sections of WT mice were incubated with serum from WT human, FAM129C-mut human, WT mice and Fam129c-mut mice, followed by the addition of fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies. Antibodies that were bound to specific cells or structures would eventually fluoresce, providing a full antigen profile present in the tissue. For cell type co-localization TBA, LES tissue sections of WT mice were incubated with serum from Fam129c-mut mice. Co-immunostaining was performed using the following primary antibodies: anti-Tuj1 (1:500, ab18207, Abcam), anti-α-SMA (1:250, ab124964, Abcam), and anti-CD20 (1:50, ab64088, Abcam).

Cell based assay (CBA)

HEK293T cells (obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Type Culture Collection) were transfected with empty vector or plasmids encoding GABRA1, MOG, AQP4, and GAD2 purchased from Genomeditech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd, respectively. Cells were incubated with serum from FAM129C-mut human or Fam129c-mut mice. Besides, serum from one autoimmune encephalitis patient with confirmed anti-GABRA1 antibodies was included as a positive control. If the primary antibodies present in the serum could bind to the overexpressed antigen, a fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody would attach to the primary antibody, which allowed the detection of fluorescence under the microscope.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

All statistics of wet experiment data were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v10.1.2). The specific statistical methods and reproducibility of each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Bioinformatics data were analyzed using R software (v4.3.1). Cells and animals were assigned to experimental groups using randomization procedures where applicable. Investigators were blinded to group allocation during experimental procedures and outcome assessment.
