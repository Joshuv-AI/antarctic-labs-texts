Tracing the signatures of ozone recovery in the Arctic ozone
Authors: Sathyanath Anjali, Jayanarayanan Kuttippurath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe examine the long-term trends in Arctic stratospheric ozone using satellite, ozonesonde and ground-based measurements for the period 1988–2024. Our analysis reveals statistically significant positive trends (up to 0.915 ± 0.251%/dec) in the upper stratospheric ozone (5–1 hPa) with the Stratospheric Water and Ozone Satellite Homogenized (SWOOSH) and the merged satellite (SAGE-CCI-OMPS) data. The assessment of total column ozone (TCO) also shows significant positive trends in the merged satellite (8.51 ± 6.03 DU/dec) and ground-based (6.51 ± 3.88 DU/dec) measurements in 2000–2024. In addition, the combined ground-based data reveal significant positive trends in the southern Arctic stations (Lerwick, Scoresbysund, Sodankylä, Churchill, Andøya and Oslo) in autumn (9.11 ± 2.38 DU/dec) and spring (6.24 ± 4.71 DU/dec). The analysis for different post-2000 periods finds a high impact of the unprecedented ozone depletion event of 2020 on the estimated trends. However, the selected four stations still show positive trends in 2000–2024. Introduction

The ozone layer serves as a protective shield in the Earth’s atmosphere, preventing harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun reaching the surface. Ozone is continuously produced in the stratosphere by the action of solar ultraviolet (UV) radiation on molecular oxygen, and destroyed by different chemical processes1. Catalytic reactions involving different oxides are the main cause of ozone depletion in the stratosphere2. The concentration of ozone reaches its maximum in the stratosphere at altitudes between 15 and 25 km3. Generally, 90% of ozone is in the stratosphere, in which 20–30% are in the upper stratosphere, depending on altitude and latitude. Ozone is transported from areas of net production to regions of net loss, and its distribution is governed by the balance between its production and loss4. This transport is influenced by changing wind patterns in the stratosphere; leading to its daily fluctuations, seasonal cycles, and interannual variability2.

The amount of ozone in the atmosphere varies because of both natural and human activity, and determining its future change requires an understanding of its past trends5. The initial signs of ozone depletion over Antarctica become noticeable in the late 1970 s, primarily due to elevated levels of stratospheric chlorine6. The ozone loss in the polar stratosphere is caused beyond doubt by the burden of chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), which is largely due to anthropogenic activities7. After the discovery of the Antarctic ozone hole, the Montreal Protocol was signed in 1987 to regulate the production of chlorine- and bromine-containing ozone-depleting substances (ODS) and thus to save the ozone layer. Before the peak recorded in ODS in the stratosphere, Mount Pinatubo underwent a significant volcanic eruption in 1991. The lowest annual total column ozone (TCO) in the northern hemisphere (NH) was recorded in 1992 because of the huge ozone depletion associated with the heterogeneous chemistry on these volcanic aerosols8. Subsequently, the concentration of chlorine in the polar stratosphere attained its maximum in the early 2000 s due to the emission of ODS9,10. The Montreal Protocol and its subsequent amendments helped to reduce the stratospheric concentration of ODSs and thus, they started to decline gradually after the year 200011.

The polar ozone depletion is associated with heterogeneous chlorine chemistry, which takes place at low temperatures on the surfaces of polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs)12. Despite the first signs of stratospheric chemical ozone losses in the Arctic being reported during the early 1990s13evaluation of photochemical destruction in this region is more complex than in the Southern Hemisphere (SH) due to the high activity of planetary waves there14. The analysis using various methods consistently suggests that the most significant ozone losses occur during the coldest winters (e.g. 1995, 2011 and 2020), and minimal destruction during very warm winters15,16. The cold winters cause significant chemical ozone loss because of the undisturbed and prolonged vortex, large areas of PSCs, and high concentrations of halogens17,18.

There is a possibility of high ozone loss in the Arctic, even if the temperature is milder than those in the Antarctica19. Normally most Arctic winters have ozone loss in the range from 0.5 to 1.5 ppmv, but 1.5–2.2 ppmv in the very cold winters at 475 K20,21. The typical TCO depletion varies between 30 and 50 DU, about 60 DU in the moderately cold winters and up to 130 DU in extreme cases22. A substantial loss in the Arctic ozone is expected to occur in very cold winters, as the temperature will be well below its seasonal average in those winters23,24. Therefore, the interannual variability of springtime Arctic ozone is driven by the dynamical effects on transport and chemistry19. There was exceptionally low ozone in the winter of 2011, between 15 and 23 km19. Other than this, there were substantial losses of ozone in some winters, such as 1996, 2000, and 200525. In 2020, exceptional ozone loss was observed within the Arctic vortex for almost three weeks21,26.

The TCO trends observed in the Arctic during March remain small and nonsignificant compared to its year-to-year variability23. However, a recent study conducted using the measurements from three northern high-latitude stations reports a significant positive trend27. Similarly, recent studies in the Northern Hemisphere (NH) stratosphere also show positive but nonsignificant trends in TCO28. Therefore, we conduct a thorough analysis of TCO and ozone profiles from multiple satellite and ground-based measurements to investigate the trends in Arctic ozone for the period 1988–2024. We have applied the multiple linear regression (MLR) method to estimate the trends and contributions of different drivers of ozone change in the Arctic.

Results

Ozone distribution and variability in the Arctic

In general, the ozone concentration is higher (20–23 DU/km) in the lower stratosphere (100–25 hPa) in winter (December–January–February, DJF). The lowest ozone values, around 0.5 ppmv, are observed at altitudes of 18–20 km, while the values are around 2 ppmv at higher altitudes above 30 hPa level (Supplementary Fig. S1). Furthermore, a large region has very small ozone values in March–April 202029 as the ozone loss was severe in this winter26. In the upper stratosphere, seasonal ozone variability is primarily influenced by temperature-dependent photochemical reactions, but atmospheric dynamics in the lower stratosphere is also evident in the ozone distribution.

The contribution of selected proxies to the changes in ozone deduced using the Stratospheric Water and Ozone Satellite Homogenized (SWOOSH) data for the Arctic winter (DJF: December, January and February) is shown in Fig. 1. One of the most significant routes for ozone transport from the tropics to the Arctic is the Brewer-Dobson circulation (BDC)30. Therefore, winds and temperature are the highest contributing factors to ozone change in the stratosphere31. The lower stratospheric (100–20 hPa) ozone changes are primarily governed by stratospheric temperature (T) at 50 hPa (55–74%) and Eddy Heat Flux (EHF) averaged over 45° N–75° N at 100 hPa (9–14%). Also, the Quasi-Biennial Oscillation (QBO) (6–28%), Solar Flux (SF) (7–28%) and T (8–64%) have high contributions in the middle stratosphere (20–5 hPa). In the upper stratosphere, T (4–51%), aerosols (5–23%) and SF (1–28%) are the major contributors to the ozone change. Our analysis has a comparable result with the previous studies, as they also show EHF, QBO and tropopause pressure as the major contributors to the ozone change in the Arctic32,33. Similarly, Weber et al.34 report that BDC is linked to the Arctic ozone loss during the cold winters, as also shown in our analysis. Some predictors (e.g. ENSO and QBO) impact ozone with lags of several months, as shown in some previous studies35. Therefore, we have also done the MLR analysis with the lags, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S2 with a 6-month lag to ENSO and QBO. However, the results do not indicate any significant difference.

Vertical trends in the Arctic ozone

Figure 2 shows the trends in annual and seasonal ozone profiles for the pre- and post-2000 periods deduced from the SWOOSH, SAGE-CCI-OMPS (hereafter CCI) and merged ground-based data. The statistically significant trend value that satisfies the 95% confidence interval criterion is marked with a circle at those altitude levels. The analysis shows significant negative trends in the upper stratosphere (5–1 hPa) in SWOOSH (−1.783 ± 1.335%/dec) and CCI (−1.853 ± 0.858%/dec) data in the pre-2000 period (upper panels). The middle (25–5 hPa) and lower (100–25 hPa) stratosphere show nonsignificant negative trends. There are seasonal differences in the estimated trends (bottom panels). For instance, SWOOSH shows significant negative trends above 3 hPa in the upper stratosphere in the pre-2000 period in JJA (June, July and August) (−1.39 ± 1.082%/dec) and MAM (March, April and May) (−2.34 ± 1.251%/dec) seasons. However, DJF and SON (September, October and November) have nonsignificant trends. The CCI data also show significant negative trends in the upper stratosphere for the pre-2000 period in JJA (−1.981 ± 0.728%/dec) and MAM (−1.119 ± 0.883%/dec), but SON (−0.811 ± 0.791%/dec) has a significant negative trend in the middle stratosphere. However, DJF shows nonsignificant positive trends at these altitudes. In brief, the analysis shows a negative trend in the pre-2000 period due to the high halogen loading in the stratosphere7,36,37.

For the post-2000 period, the estimates show a significant positive trends in the SWOOSH (0.915 ± 0.251%/dec) and CCI (0.843 ± 0.351%/dec) data in the upper stratosphere (5–1 hPa). The seasonal analysis of winter months (DJF) with the SWOOSH data shows a significant positive trend in the upper (5–1 hPa) stratosphere. Furthermore, SWOOSH data exhibit significant positive trends in the upper stratosphere (5–1 hPa) in JJA (0.731 ± 0.421%/dec), DJF (0.573 ± 0.257%/dec), MAM (0.556 ± 0.286%/dec) and SON (0.925 ± 0.264%/dec). Similarly, CCI data exhibit significant positive trends in the upper stratosphere (5–1 hPa) for JJA (1.04 ± 0.296%/dec), MAM (0.578 ± 0.212%/dec) and SON (1.003 ± 0.241%/dec) seasons during the post-2000 period. The analysis also yields significant positive trends with CCI in SON (0.467 ± 0.371%/dec) and MAM (0.402 ± 0.308%/dec) in the middle stratosphere (25–5 hPa) during post-2000. Consistent with the findings of Chipperfield et al.31 and Ball et al.38, no recovery trend is observed in the lower stratosphere. However, significant positive trends are evident in the middle stratosphere in the SON and MAM seasons.

In addition to the merged satellite, we also examined the high resolution ozonesonde measurements from different stations (Alert, Ny-Ålesund, Scoresbysund, Sodankylä, Eureka, Lerwick, Resolute and Churchill) in the Arctic. The annual-mean trend in the combined multi-station ozonesonde profiles shows significant negative values in the middle stratosphere (−1.116 ± 1.011%/dec) for the pre-2000 period. Seasonal analysis of the profiles also shows a nonsignificant negative trend in the lower stratosphere (100–25 hPa) in this period. In contrast, the post-2000 period shows a nonsignificant positive trend in the annual averaged profiles at all altitudes. Additionally, nonsignificant positive ozone trends are observed in the ozonesonde data during DJF and MAM in the middle stratosphere (25–5 hPa). Note that, summer months are dynamically less active, which makes the statistical analysis less challenging in JJA compared to other seasons.

Furthermore, we have analysed two more satellite datasets for checking the consistency and robustness of the derived trends, which are available for the post-2004 period. Figure 3 shows the trend calculated with the Merged Gridded Dataset of Ozone Profiles (MEGRIDOP) and Aura-MLS (Microwave Limb Sounder) data for 2005–2024. There is a significant positive trend in the upper stratosphere in MEGRIDOP (1.144 ± 0.487%/dec) and Aura-MLS (1.242 ± 0.493%/dec), consistent with the results of SWOOSH and CCI results for the post-2000 period. Similarly, MLS shows a significant positive trend in JJA (1.667 ± 0.523%/dec), DJF (0.693 ± 0.411%/dec), SON (0.94 ± 0.286%/dec), whereas MAM has a nonsignificant trend in the upper stratosphere, above 3 hPa. Nevertheless, MEGRIDOP ozone shows significant positive trends in JJA (1.376 ± 0.336%/dec), MAM (0.48 ± 0.243%/dec) and SON (0.532 ± 0.238%/dec) in the upper stratosphere above 3 hPa, whereas DJF shows a nonsignificant trend. The trends estimated using SWOOSH and MLS ozone for the pre- and post-2000 period at different altitude levels are also listed in Table S1. The seasonal analysis conducted by Bahramvash et al.32 with the sonde measurements from Arctic stations for the period 2005–2017 yields a comparable result to our study. The trend analysis over the period revels a notable change across most altitudes at Summit and Ny-Ålesund during summer and fall seasons. Previous studies10,39,40 analysed ozone trends at 60° S–60° N, excluding polar regions, while Sofieva et al.41 reported a significant positive trend in the NH middle stratosphere (25–30 km), as for our analysis in the upper stratosphere. This significant positive trend in the upper stratosphere (3–1 hPa) is consistent with the trends shown in the previous studies10,39,42,43. Also, the analysis performed with different station (Alert, Ny-Ålesund, Eureka, Resolute, Scoresbysund, and Sodankylä) measurements in 1999–2019 shows a negative trend in the lower stratosphere, indicating that the recovery signals are yet to be found there44.

Ozone vertical trends for the period 2005–2024. Annual averaged ozone vertical trends estimated using (a) MEGRIDOP and (b) AURA-MLS ozone for the 2005–2024 period. Seasonal (DJF, JJA, MAM and SON) trends estimated using (c) MEGRIDOP and (d) AURA-MLS ozone for the 2005–2024 period.

In addition to the zonally-averaged analysis, we examined the longitude-resolved ozone trends in the Arctic using the MEGRIDOP data for the period 2005–2024 (Supplementary Fig. S3), which provide gridded ozone in both altitude and longitude. The analysis with annual data shows positive trends at most longitudes, except in the lower and middle stratosphere around 100° E. However, there are large Changes in seasonal trends. For instance, the DJF trends are mostly negative at most altitudes, except in the upper stratosphere at 100° W. A similar trend pattern is found in MAM, but all trends are negative beyond 50° E and 100° W. The trends in the western longitudes from 50° W onwards are negative at all altitudes in SON. However, the trends in JJA have a completely different pattern, as they show positive values in the lower and upper stratosphere, but negative values in the middle stratosphere across the longitudes. This analysis shows that there is large spatial heterogeneity in the derived trends, as also shown by the station measurements at different regions in the Arctic. In agreement with our findings, Sofieva et al.39 report a positive trend in the northern hemispheric polar middle stratosphere (25–30 km), and Arosio et al.45 also observe a positive trend in the upper stratosphere (34–48 km) around 180° W and 20° W.

Trends in the total column ozone

The TCO trends estimated from the merged ground-based station measurements and MSAT (merged satellite data) for the pre-and post-2000 periods are listed in Table 1. The merged station measurements show a significant negative trend for the annual (−19.41 ± 15.1 DU/dec), JJA (−13.73 ± 12.29 DU/dec), DJF (−30.99 ± 27.77 DU/dec) and SON (−23.43 ± 11.45 DU/dec) data for the pre-2000 period. Similarly, the MSAT trends show a significant negative value in the annual (−19.34 ± 17.24 DU/dec) data for the pre-2000 period. DJF is the winter season in which the peak ozone loss takes place. In addition, the pre-2000 period had the volcanic aerosol-induced ozone loss in the early 1990 s, and the high ozone loss years of 1995, 1996, and 2000. These are the reasons for the high ozone loss during the period. Although our analysis overestimates the results reported by Weber et al.34 it shows a comparable trend in the polar cap regions for March (−2.6 ± 5.5%/dec).

The impact of various meteorological parameters on ozone variations is shown by the fitted variables from the multiple linear regression (MLR) analysis on TCO using MSAT (Supplementary Fig. S4) and Oslo (Supplementary Fig. S5) data, covering the years 1988–2024. The analysis shows that Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) (9–22%), SF (2–23%), qbo30 (9–12%) and T (15–16%) are the primary contributors to the ozone variability across both datasets, consistent with our understanding of the ozone chemistry and dynamics in the Arctic46. According to Bernet et al.27 temperature and tropospheric pressure explain ozone variation at Oslo, which also supports our results.

Discussion

As there are signs of ozone recovery in the upper stratosphere and in TCO, we estimated ozone trends at six individual stations in the Arctic (Ny-Ålesund, Lerwick, Scoresbysund, Sodankylä, Andøya and Oslo) and their average for different periods; 2000–2024, 2000–2023, 2000–2022, 2000–2021, 2000–2020 and 2000–2019. This is also to examine the effects of extreme low ozone episode in 2020 and very high TCO in 2024 on the long-term trends to diagnose the robustness of estimated trends, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The analysis indicates that the trend values decreased by 0.2 to 1.5 DU/dec in 2000–2020 compared to that in 2000–2019. This suggests a decrease in trends with the 2020 data, reflecting the impact of extreme low ozone year. The recent study by Bernet et al.2 aligns well with our findings. However, they report significant positive trends at Andøya (2.9 ± 2.5 DU/dec) and Ny-Ålesund (5.1 ± 3.6 DU/dec) for the 2000–2020 period, which may be attributed to the differences in proxy variables used in their analysis. Pazmiño et al.2 also obtained a nonsignificant positive trend in the NH high-latitude ozone. They suggested that the observed positive trends in the NH are lower than expected due to the influence 2020 data, which is in agreement with our analysis. Therefore, the long-term trend analysis shows significant positive trends at Oslo (7.93 ± 3.36 DU/dec), Lerwick (4.88 ± 4.39 DU/dec), and in the combined station data (6.44 ± 3.87 DU/dec) for the 2000–2024 period. Furthermore, all stations show positive trends during the same period. This suggests that ozone has begun to recover following its record low in 2020. Similarly, the record-high ozone observed in the Northern Hemisphere4 can also influence the positive ozone trends, though a significant rise in trends is not evident due to the previous year (e.g. 2020) low values.

Trends were calculated for two sets of combined station data to examine the regional differences in seasonal trends. The north and south Arctic regions are delineated by a magenta dotted line in the figure. The analysis reveals nonsignificant positive trends in MAM (3.06 ± 4.93 DU/dec) and DJF (1.72 ± 7.90 DU/dec), and a significant positive trend in SON (7.44 ± 2.49 DU/dec) during 2000–2023 for the combined TCO data from Alert, Ny-Åalesund, Thule and Eureka (hereafter referred to northern Arctic). However, JJA (−0.69 ± 3.03 DU/dec) has a nonsignificant negative trend in the same period. Similarly, the combined station data from Lerwick, Scoresbysund, Sodankylä, Churchill and Oslo (hereafter referred to southern Arctic) show significant positive trends in SON (9.10 ± 2.38 DU/dec) and MAM (6.24 ± 4.71 DU/dec), and nonsignificant positive trends for other seasons during the 2000–2024 period. These results show spatial variability in trends, which could be a reason for the smaller values found with the MSAT data. The regional or station-based analysis agrees with the spatial TCO trends, as it shows significant positive values in the southern Arctic stations. However, northern Arctic stations show a negative trend in the JJA season. The estimated Linear trend analysis with MSAT for the period 1988–2024 is shown in Supplementary Fig. S6. Their seasonal analyses reveal positive trends during SON in both northern (5.07 ± 3.09 DU/dec) and southern (6.01 ± 5.39 DU/dec) stations, consistent with our earlier results. Note that the measurements used in this study have their inherited uncertainties, such as those arise from retrieval errors, a priori data, bias correction, and data merging. In addition, the MLR analysis does not account for nonlinear interactions between and among different processes.

In summary, we use ground-based, ozonesonde, satellite, and merged-satellite data to examine the ozone trends in total column and vertical profiles for the past four decades (1988–2024). The SWOOSH measurements show significant positive trends in the upper stratosphere (3–1 hPa) in the annual (0.915 ± 0.289%/dec), DJF (0.573 ± 0.257%/dec), JJA (0.731 ± 0.421%/dec), SON (0.925 ± 0.264%/dec) and MAM (0.556 ± 0.286%/dec) data for the post-2000 period. The CCI (0.843 ± 0.351%/dec) data confirm these positive trends in the upper stratosphere for the same period. Similarly, the MLS (1.242 ± 0.493%/dec) and MEGRIDOP (1.144 ± 0.487%/dec) data during 2005–2024 also yield significant positive trends in the upper stratosphere (3–1 hPa). For TCO, the MSAT data display a nonsignificant positive trend (5.35 ± 5.42 DU/dec) for the post-2000 period. However, the merged-station (6.88 ± 3.74 DU/dec), and Lerwick (4.87 ± 4.39 DU/dec) and Oslo (6.85 ± 3.51 DU/dec) measurements exhibit significant positive trends, and Sodankylä (1.33 ± 3.94 DU/dec) and Scoresbysund (2.236 ± 5.927 DU/dec) show nonsignificant positive trends for the 2000–2024 period. Note that we have used different satellite, ground-based, and ozonesonde measurements of varying resolution, coverage, units, and observation periods for this study to avoid biases in using a single station or satellite data, and thus to draw robust conclusions. Therefore, these results suggest a turnaround of ozone column data in the Arctic, although profiles are yet to show any recovery signs in the lower stratospheric ozone.

Methods

Ground-based measurements

The World Ozone and Ultraviolet Radiation Data Centre (WOUDC) and the Network for the Detection of Atmospheric Composition Change (NDACC) are global initiatives dedicated to monitoring the atmospheric composition, with a focus on ozone and related parameters. WOUDC gathers and validates the data from different ground-based measurements, such as Dobson, Brewer and Système d’Analyse par Observation Zénithale (SAOZ) instruments48. Dobson spectrophotometer is the first device created and employed to measure TCO in the atmosphere using sun irradiance data49. Our analysis uses TCO observations from Dobson spectrophotometer at the Oslo (58.98° N, 10.72° E), Churchill (58.77° N, 94.17° W), Resolute (74.7 ° N, 95.0 ° W), Barrow (71.32° N, 156.68 ° W) and Lerwick (60.13° N, 1.1° W) stations for the period 1978–1991, 1964–1989, 1957–1990, 1973–2020 and 1952–2024, respectively. TCO is estimated by directly observing the sun or zenith of the sky50. When the solar altitude is greater than 15°, a well-maintained and calibrated Dobson spectrophotometer can measure TCO ozone with an estimated accuracy of 1% for direct Sun and 2–3% for zenith observations51. Brewer spectrophotometer is developed as an accurate tool for measuring total ozone52. It measures spectral Ultra Violet (UV) irradiance at 0.5 nm intervals in 286.5–363 nm. We use the ozone measurements from the stations Oslo, Resolute, Churchill, Eureka (80.05° N, 86.42° W) and Andøya (69.3° N, 16.0° E), for the periods 1990–2022, 1987–2019, 1986–2019, 2001–2022 and 2000–2020, respectively. A Brewer MKIII (B104) double-monochromator is installed at Andøya, and a Brewer MKV single-monochromator at Oslo. They make daily measurements from direct sun in the UV cloud-free conditions. This method yields the most precise Brewer TCO data and can maintain a precision of 1%53.

SAOZ UV-Visible spectrometer ozone measurements from Ny-Ålesund (78.9° N, 11.9° E), Thule (76.53° N, 68.74° W), Scoresbysund (70.48° N, 21.97° W) and Sodankylä (67.37° N, 26.65° E) are taken for the analysis. SAOZ is a passive remote-sensing instrument that measures the sunlight scattered from zenith sky in 300–650 nm. This is a UV-Visible spectrometer that observes sunlight scattered by the atmosphere at zenith to retrieve O3 and NO2 column measurements during sunrise and sunset, regardless of weather conditions54. The previous assessments show an accuracy of 5.9% for the ozone measurements from SAOZ. Further details of the stations and measurement period are given in Table 2. In addition, the ground-based ultraviolet radiometer (GUV) TCO measurements from Andøya are also considered. The GUV instrument measures ozone using five UV channels in 305–380 nm27. Accurate calibrations are essential for GUV ozone retrievals. Svendby et al.55 have shown that the standard deviation between Brewer and GUV in Andøya is within 2.5%, as deduced from the measurements in 1995–2019. The accuracy of ozone measurements in Andøya varies about 1–2% and 5–10% for the Brewer and GUV instruments, respectively.

Combined TCO measurements from Ny-Ålesund, Scoresbysund, Sodankylä, Lerwick, Alert, Barrow, Churchill, Resolute, Andoya, Thule and Oslo are used to analyse the regional Arctic ozone trends. Additionally, ozone profile data from Electrochemical Concentration Cell (ECC) ozonesondes are collected from the stations Alert (82.5° N, 62.33° W), Ny-Ålesund, Scoresbysund, Sodankylä, Eureka (80.05° N, 86.42° W), Lerwick (60.13° N, 1.1° W), Resolute (74.7° N, 95.0° W), and Churchill (58.73° N, 93.82° W). These data are then combined to create a long-term dataset spanning from 1980 to 2024. The ECC sondes measure ozone mixing ratios at different altitudes and record vertical profiles of ozone, with the current measured being directly proportional to the ozone absorption rate in the cathode chamber56. These sondes have a precision of 3–5% and an overall uncertainty of ± 10% up to 30 km altitude57,58.

Satellite observations

The SWOOSH ozone data are made from merging the measurements of satellite instruments of Stratospheric Aerosol and Gas Experiment II/III (SAGE II/III), Halogen Occultation Experiment (HALOE), Upper Atmospheric Research Satellite (UARS) Microwave Limb Sounder (MLS) and Aura-MLS59. The combined data provide zonally averaged monthly mean ozone mixing ratios at 31 pressure levels from 316 to 1 hPa. These data are available from 1984 onwards on 2.5°, 5°, and 10° zonal mean grids. To ensure consistency across the datasets, measurements have been homogenised by applying correlations derived from overlapping observation periods60. We have used 10° latitudinal band monthly averaged data in the polar cap (55° N–90° N) region. Therefore, short periods of missing data do not affect the trend analysis.

The SAGE-CCI-OMPS is a merged ozone profile dataset developed as a part of the European Space Agency (ESA) Ozone Climate Change Initiative (CCI). It is derived using measurements from seven limb and occultation satellite instruments; Michelson Interferometer for Passive Atmospheric Sounding (MIPAS), Scanning Imaging Spectrometer for Atmospheric Cartography (SCIAMACHY), Global Ozone Monitoring by Occultation of Stars (GOMOS), Optical Spectrograph and Infrared Imaging System (OSIRIS), Atmospheric Chemistry Experiment Fourier Transform Spectrometer (ACE-FTS), Ozone Monitor Profiling Suite Limb Profiler (OMPS-LP) and Stratospheric Aerosol and Gases Experiment II (SAGE II)61. The GOMOS, MIPAS, SCIAMACHY, OSIRIS, and ACE-FTS measurements from 2001 to 2016 are combined with those from SAGE II (1984–2005) and OMPS-LP (2012–2024) in this merged dataset. It has monthly zonal mean ozone profiles in 10° latitude bins for an altitude range of 10–50 km. These data are available for the period from late 1984 to 2024, and the merging is done using the deseasonalised ozone anomalies43.

In addition, we have used MEGRIDOP and Aura-MLS data. MEGRIDOP offers a monthly mean gridded ozone profile dataset in 10° latitude × 20° longitude at 10–50 km41,43 for the period 2001–2024. The information is taken from six Limb and occultation satellite instruments; OMPS, OSIRIS, SCIAMACHY, MIPAS, Aura-MLS and GOMOS. The Aura MLS instrument was placed into the orbit on 15 July 2004, and is the improved version of UARS-MLS with better vertical resolution and latitudinal coverage (82° S to 82° N). These are available in a horizontal resolution of about 300 km, covering an altitude range of 5–120 km. The ozone profiles are considered reliable between the pressure levels of 261 and 0.02 hPa62 and the estimated accuracy of ozone is 140–40 ppbv63 or + 5%.

The merged satellite data of the Total Ozone mapping spectrometer (TOMS) and Ozone Monitoring Instrument (OMI) are also used for the trend analysis. These data are made using the Nimbus7 (N7), Meteor 3 (MT3), Earth probe (EP) TOMS and OMI measurements, and are available from 1985 to 2024 at a spatial resolution of 1°× 1°. According to Kroon et al.64 there is a 1–2% bias in the ozone products from the various TOMS platforms, and the uncertainty of the TOMS ozone column data is 3.3%. For sun zenith angles below 84°, the OMI TCO uncertainty varies between 2 and 5%65.

In addition to satellite and ground-based measurements, we have used a reanalysis as these data are continuous with a long record (1979 – to date). ERA5 is the most recent (fifth-generation) reanalysis of the atmosphere dataset for the entire world produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF)66. The ERA5 reanalysis provides several meteorological and chemical parameters on 0.25°× 0.25° spatial resolution. ERA5 models the atmosphere using 137 levels, extending from the surface up to 0.01 hPa, at a horizontal resolution of 31 km67.

MLR analysis

We selected the Arctic region from 55° N to 90° N for this analysis, as the polar vortex can occur anywhere within these latitudes. However, we have not used the vortex criteria for this analysis, as we could not find a significant difference between with and without vortex-based analysis. In addition, vortex segregated data are relatively few and our finding is consistent with previous studies68,69. The trend is estimated for different seasons; DJF (winter), MAM (spring), JJA (Summer), and SON (autumn), and the annual average for the period 1988–2024. In the polar regions, the ODS peaked in 2000 and they started to decrease in the atmosphere thereafter70 and therefore we have taken the turnaround year as 2000. The trend in yearly average ozone is assessed through the MLR technique, an approach similar to that of Kuttippurath et al.71. The independent variables used in MLR are QBO at 10 and 30 hPa, SF, AOD, El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO), Arctic Oscillation (AO), T at 50 hPa and the EHF averaged over 45° N–75° N at 100 hPa. Therefore, the ozone change computed by MLR can be written as;

O3(t) = Constant + C1(t1) + C2(t2) + C3 Aerosol(t) + C4 Heat Flux(t) + C5 Solar Flux(t) + C6 ENSO(t) + C7 QBO 10(t) + C8 QBO 30(t) + C9 T(t) + R(t).

Here, C3–C9 are the regression coefficients of respective proxies. C1 represents the linear trend, C2 represents the change in trend, t1 is the number of years from 1988 to 2024 and t2 is the number of years from 2000 to 2024. Here, C1(t1) + C2(t2) represents the piecewise linear trend (PWLT) term, and ‘R’ represents the residual from the regression analysis72. QBO represents the quasi-periodic oscillation of the equatorial zonal winds and its impact varies at different latitudes73. Here, we use QBO10 and QBO30 that represent the Singapore zonal winds at 10 and 30 hPa, respectively. To include the effects of aerosols from volcanic eruptions (e.g. 1982 and 1991–92) on ozone Change, we have taken the monthly time-averaged single-level AOD from the Modern-Era Retrospective analysis for Research and Applications Version 2 (MERRA-2) and averaged it for the latitudinal range 55° N–90° N. The effect of Brewer-Dobson circulation (BDC) in the stratosphere is taken under consideration using the EHF calculated with the ERA5 monthly temperature and meridional winds at the 100 hPa over the latitudes 45° N – 75° N14. The effect of 11-year solar cycle on ozone is added using the monthly mean SF data at 10.7 cm74. PSCs and stratospheric temperatures are closely related to chemical ozone loss at high latitudes19. Thus, we have included the stratospheric temperature (T) at 50 hPa. To check the significance of trend values (ω), the standard deviation at each altitude level is calculated according to Press et al.75. Here, we consider the trend value to be significant when it satisfies the 95% confidence interval (95 CI) criterion. Also, the dominance analysis76,77 is applied to verify the relative importance of variables and their contribution to ozone change in the regression.

The proxies for the MLR analysis are selected through cross‑correlation checks to minimize multicollinearity. Additionally, we used the standardised and deseasonalised anomalies for the trend detection. For the seasonal analysis, values of all predictors in the respective seasons are used to ensure their comparability. The same set of predictors is applied across all seasons in the MLR analysis to maintain consistency and to enable direct comparison of derived trends. The selected predictors (e.g. QBO, solar cycle, ENSO, volcanic aerosols, and temperature) represent the key processes influencing ozone variability and trends in the stratosphere. Although the relative importance of individual predictors may vary seasonally, using a consistent predictor set minimises the methodological biases and ensures robust trend estimates. This approach is also consistent with previous ozone trend studies11,78 which have adopted a similar methodology for seasonal analyses.
