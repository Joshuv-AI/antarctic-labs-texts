A two-hit ecological framework linking social context to caries-associated microbiome shifts in children
Authors: KeXin Xue, Shengmei Lei, Xiaojing Cheng, Wenfei Xu, Zhehao Lin, Yutong Zhou, Xiaokai Mao, Xin Ge, Haihua Zhu, Fudong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Background Dental caries arises from an ecological imbalance within a complex community. How chronic social context relates to ecological heterogeneity and dysbiosis-associated microbial shifts in school-age children remains unclear.ObjectiveTo investigate the associations of left-behind status and caries burden with the salivary microbiome and to explore a two-hit ecological framework linking social context to caries-associated microbial shifts.DesignIn this cross-sectional study, 127 rural children were classified using a 2 × 2 framework based on left-behind status and caries burden. Saliva samples underwent shotgun metagenomic sequencing. Ecological analyses and covariate-adjusted multivariable models were performed.ResultsAlpha diversity did not differ across groups. Global community centroids were similar, whereas within-group dispersion was higher in left-behind children, suggesting greater ecological heterogeneity. After covariate adjustment, no genus-level associations remained significant, whereas several KEGG level 3 pathways related to translation and carbohydrate utilization were positively associated with dmft. Stratified analyses showed concordant caries-related enrichment of Streptococcus, Veillonella, and carbohydrate-utilization pathways across social strata. Ecological subtyping identified Neisseria- and Veillonella-anchored community types.ConclusionThe findings are consistent with a two-hit ecological framework in which social context is associated with greater ecological heterogeneity and cariogenic pressure is associated with reproducible functional shifts. Given the cross-sectional design, this framework should be considered hypothesis-generating.Keywords: Dental caries, left-behind children, salivary microbiome, social determinants, metagenomics, shotgun metagenomics, ecological heterogeneity

Introduction

Dental caries is a chronic, microbe-driven and multifactorial disease that remains one of the most prevalent oral health problems among children worldwide [1]. In 2021, an estimated 514 million children were affected, with major impacts on pain and tooth loss [2]. The burden is a major public health challenge in China. The Fourth National Oral Health Survey reported a prevalence of 70.9% for caries in the primary teeth of 5-year-old children [3,4]. Among many contributing factors, the family and the social environment are critical determinants that shape health behaviors and outcomes during early life [5,6].

Rapid urbanization in China has led to the emergence of a large group of rural left-behind children (LBC) [7]. The long-term absence of migrant worker parents can create challenges in guardianship, diet quality and emotional support [8,9]. These conditions place LBC at a raised risk for adverse health outcomes. Evidence points to a heavier burden of dental caries in this group, while the biological mechanisms behind this vulnerability remain unclear.

Current concepts view caries as a transition of the oral microbiome from a stable state to dysbiosis, often triggered by behavioral or environmental stressors [10,11]. Prior studies often confounded social status with clinical disease [12]. As a result, it is difficult to determine whether observed microbial differences are independently associated with the social environment or simply reflect disease status. Clarifying the independent contributions of these two factors and any interaction between them remains an important unanswered question.

To answer this question, we combined an original 2 × 2 stratified ecological framework with covariate-adjusted multivariable analyses. Participants were stratified by both social status and clinical status into four groups: LBC with higher caries burden (LBC-HC), LBC with lower caries burden (LBC-LC), non-LBC with higher caries burden (NLBC-HC) and non-LBC with lower caries burden (NLBC-LC). Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was applied to characterize taxonomic and functional profiles [13]. The stratified design was used to compare ecological patterns across social and clinical strata, while covariate-adjusted models were used to estimate independent associations with microbial features.

We examined three related questions. First, caries status may be associated with a shared microbial signature across social groups, including shifts in cariogenic taxa and related functional pathways [10]. Second, LBC status may show an independent association with oral community structure that may reflect behavioral or psychosocial exposures [14]. Third, the observed patterns may be interpretable within a two-hit ecological framework, in which social context contributes to ecological heterogeneity and caries burden is associated with dysbiosis-related functional change.

By integrating social determinants with clinical disease in a single analytic framework, this work aims to clarify how environment and disease intersect in pediatric oral ecology. The goal is to inform prevention strategies that go beyond hygiene instruction and also involve family engagement and psychosocial support.

Methods

Participants and factorial design

This cross-sectional study enrolled 127 school-age children from rural communities in southern China (age 6–12 years) within a community program on social and biological factors of caries [15]. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethical Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Stomatology, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Approval Number: 2024-12). Parents or legal guardians provided written informed consent.

Inclusion criteria were permanent residence in the study area, age 6–12 years, no systemic or infectious disease, no visible enamel or dentin developmental defect, no orthodontic appliance and no antibiotic or probiotic use within the prior three months. Exclusion criteria were acute infection, chronic systemic condition, or inability to provide saliva. Left-behind status followed national census criteria and referred to living apart from one or both parents for at least six consecutive months due to parental migration [16]. This definition did not require both parents to be absent. Within the left-behind group, parental migration could involve the father only, the mother only, or both parents. Caregiver structure was recorded in the questionnaire, but was not modeled as a primary stratification factor because of the limited sample size. Structured questionnaires were also used to collect demographic and behavioral metadata, including dietary sugar intake and toothbrushing frequency, for subsequent covariate-adjusted analyses.

A 2 × 2 factorial design evaluated associations of social status and caries status. Factor 1 was social status with two levels: left-behind children (LBC) and non-left-behind children (NLBC). Factor 2 was caries status with two levels: higher caries burden (HC) and lower caries burden (LC). Caries experience used the dmft index [17]. Higher caries burden was dmft ≥ 7 and low caries burden was dmft ≤ 4, based on cohort distribution and national references. These thresholds were used to contrast children at relatively distinct ends of the caries-burden spectrum within this high-prevalence cohort, thereby facilitating ecological stratification rather than defining disease presence versus absence.

The four groups were LBC-HC (n = 18), LBC-LC (n = 17), NLBC-HC (n = 16) and NLBC-LC (n = 16). Age and sex distributions were comparable across groups. An overview of enrollment, group allocation and the analysis workflow is shown in Figure 1 ( Table 1).

Clinical assessment and questionnaire

Dentists trained according to the World Health Organization criteria recorded DMFT or dmft. Lesion severity was assessed using ICDAS II [18].

A two-day calibration with chairside practice achieved weighted kappa values of ≥0.80 for ICDAS II and ≥0.85 for DMFT or dmft [19]. Field teams were blinded to social status. Caries experience was assessed using the dmft index and ICDAS-based lesion severity. Lesion activity status (active versus inactive) was not available for the present analyses. Caregivers and children completed a structured questionnaire that covered demographics, parental migration, diet, oral hygiene, fluoride exposure, dental service use and psychosocial environment. Items were piloted for clarity. Responses were collected on encrypted tablets with range checks. Paper forms were double-entered with discrepancy resolution. Questionnaire-derived variables used in covariate-adjusted analyses included dietary sugar intake frequency and toothbrushing frequency. For modeling, sugar intake was categorized into low, moderate and high frequency levels and toothbrushing frequency was simplified into twice or more per day versus less than twice per day.

Diet reflected the frequency of sugar-sweetened beverages and between-meal snacks in the prior week. Oral hygiene reflected the frequency of tooth-brushing and the use of fluoride toothpaste. Care seeking reflected dental visits in the past year and reasons for visits. Communication reflected the frequency of parent–child contact in person or by phone in the past month. These questionnaire data were used both for descriptive characterization and, where specified, as covariates in multivariable models.

Sampling, sequencing and bioinformatic processing

Unstimulated saliva was collected in the morning at schools. Children refrained from eating, drinking, or toothbrushing for at least two hours. About 2 mL was collected into sterile tubes, kept on ice, transferred within four hours and stored at −80 ◦C [20]. DNA extraction used mechanical lysis with column purification that included bead beating. Each batch included a blank and a mock community. DNA concentration and purity were measured by fluorometry and absorbance ratios. Extraction order was randomized across groups. Shotgun libraries were prepared with a standard kit with a fragment size near 350 bp. Indexed libraries were pooled equimolarly and sequenced on an Illumina platform in paired-end mode (2 × 150 bp). The target depth was at least 5 Gbp per sample. Library indices were balanced across the four groups and technicians were blinded to group labels. Each run included negative and mock controls. Raw data were converted to FASTQ and stored on a secure server. Raw reads underwent adapter and low-quality trimming, and host sequences were removed by mapping to the human reference genome [21]. Only high-quality non-host reads were used for profiling. Taxonomic profiling was performed at the species level based on marker genes. Functional profiling was summarized to KEGG Orthologs and level 3 pathways [22]. For downstream association analyses, features that were rare in the cohort or had very low mean relative abundance were excluded according to predefined prevalence and abundance filters. All processing steps followed a scripted workflow.

Statistical analysis

The primary ecological outcome was beta diversity at the species level. Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was visualized by principal coordinates analysis. Group differences were tested by permutational multivariate analysis of variance with 9,999 permutations using,

Dispersion homogeneity was evaluated by a standard test of within-group distances [23]. Alpha diversity by the Simpson index was summarized descriptively. These analyses were used to visualize ecological patterns across caries severity and social status within the original stratified framework.

Secondary outcomes were differentially abundant taxa and KEGG level 3 pathways. Species relative abundances were analyzed under compositional constraints using centered log-ratio values [24]. Two-level contrasts applied rank-based tests. Factorial comparisons used two-way models with caries status, LBC status and their interaction. Multiple testing was controlled by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure with q < 0.10 taken as significant [25]. Effect directions and standardized differences were reported.

To adjust for potential confounding variables and estimate independent associations, we additionally performed covariate-adjusted multivariable association modeling using MaAsLin2. Linear models were fitted with genus-level taxonomic profiles and KEGG pathway abundances as outcomes. The models included dmft and left-behind status (LBC) as primary explanatory variables, while age, sex, dietary sugar intake frequency and toothbrushing frequency were included as covariates. This multivariable approach allowed estimation of independent microbial associations while accounting for behavioral and demographic factors known to influence oral microbial ecology. Exploratory analyses included community subtyping, co-occurrence networks, constrained ordination and a neutral community model. These analyses were treated as hypothesis-generating, and results that did not survive multiple-testing correction were interpreted cautiously. Further details on quality control, software implementations, model specifications and parameter choices are provided in Appendix A.

Results

Participant characteristics and clinical findings

A total of 127 school-age children were enrolled (age 6–12 years), including 70 left-behind children (LBC) and 57 non-left-behind children (NLBC). The overall prevalence of dental caries was 95.6%. Severe lesions with dentin exposure were present in 35.1% of participants. These severe lesions were considered in interpreting caries burden across the stratified groups.

For the metagenomic sub-study, participants were allocated under the 2 × 2 design into four groups: LBC with higher caries burden (LBC-HC, n = 18), LBC with lower caries burden (LBC-LC, n = 17), non-LBC with higher caries burden (NLBC-HC, n = 16) and non-LBC with lower caries burden (NLBC-LC, n = 16). Age and sex distributions were comparable across groups (p > 0.05). Demographic and clinical features are summarized in Table 2. The same 67 samples were also used for covariate-adjusted multivariable analyses. In these models, dmft was analyzed as a continuous variable, and age, sex, sugar intake frequency, toothbrushing frequency, and left-behind status were incorporated as explanatory variables.

Covariate-adjusted associations

To address potential confounding in this observational study, we performed covariate-adjusted multivariable association analyses in the same 67 samples included in the stratified metagenomic sub-study. The models included dmft, left-behind status, age, sex, dietary sugar intake frequency and toothbrushing frequency as explanatory variables.

At the genus level, no associations remained significant after false discovery rate correction. This finding suggests that, after adjustment for demographic and behavioral covariates, taxonomic differences were not independently associated with dmft or left-behind status in this dataset. In contrast, KEGG level 3 functional profiling showed significant associations with dmft after covariate adjustment (Figure 2). Pathways related to translational activity and carbohydrate utilization, including Ribosome, RNA polymerase, Phosphotransferase system (PTS), Galactose metabolism and Protein export, were positively associated with higher dmft values. No KEGG level 3 pathways remained independently associated with left-behind status after correction.

Covariate-adjusted associations between caries burden and KEGG level 3 functional pathways. Multivariable association analysis was performed using MaAsLin2 in the same 67 samples included in the stratified metagenomic sub-study, adjusting for left-behind status, age, sex, dietary sugar intake frequency and toothbrushing frequency. Significant associations were detected for dmft, whereas no KEGG level 3 pathways remained independently associated with left-behind status after false discovery rate correction. Positive coefficients indicate higher pathway abundance with increasing caries burden.

Taken together, these covariate-adjusted analyses suggest that caries severity was more consistently reflected in microbial functional shifts than in robust taxonomic reconfiguration, whereas the independent signal of left-behind status was comparatively modest.

Similar community centroids with heterogeneous dispersion

We next examined between-sample ecological structure in the original stratified 2 × 2 framework. Principal coordinates analysis based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity showed extensive overlap across the four groups without clear separation (Figure 3a). A two-way PERMANOVA confirmed the visual impression. The main effects of caries status and LBC status were not significant (p = 0.141 and p = 0.671), and the variance explained by these factors was small (R 2 = 0.0324 for caries; R 2 = 0.01529 for LBC). These results indicate that the global community centroid was similar across social and disease strata.

Global community centroids were similar, whereas within-group dispersion differed across groups.

In contrast, dispersion analysis showed heterogeneous within-group distances, with left behind higher caries burden children having the most dispersed communities (Figure 3b). This pattern suggests that average community composition remained similar, whereas within-group heterogeneity differed across strata. Increased dispersion was interpreted as a statistical ecological indicator consistent with altered community heterogeneity, rather than as a direct measure of functional or clinical instability.

Supplementary analyses of alternative distance metrics and taxonomic levels gave consistent results. Alpha diversity measured by the Simpson index did not differ significantly between groups (Figures B1–B4).

Caries-related taxonomic and functional patterns across social strata

Because global community centroids were similar across groups, we next examined whether caries status showed directionally consistent patterns within social strata in the original stratified 2 × 2 framework. Within left-behind children and within non-left-behind children, groups with higher caries burden were contrasted with those with lower caries burden. Effect directions were broadly concordant across strata, and the interaction between caries status and social status was not significant.

These stratified comparisons were consistent with a caries-related ecological signal at the taxonomic and functional levels, despite limited separation in global community structure.

Groups with higher caries burden were enriched for acidogenic and aciduric Streptococcus species and related fermentative taxa, whereas those with lower caries burden showed higher relative abundance of health-associated commensals such as Neisseria and Rothia. At the functional level, higher-burden groups showed higher activity in pathways related to carbohydrate uptake and fermentation, cell wall biosynthesis and translation, while lower-burden groups showed higher activity in pathways linked to metabolic breadth and environmental adaptation. Spearman correlations between caries status and genera or pathways were directionally consistent with these patterns. Social-status contrasts within caries strata

Given the caries-related patterns described above, we next examined whether social status was associated with taxonomic and functional variation within strata of caries burden. We contrasted LBC and NLBC within the higher-burden stratum and within the lower-burden stratum. The observed differences were modest and context-dependent, with effect sizes generally smaller than those seen for caries-related contrasts.

Within the stratum with higher caries burden, left-behind children tended to show higher abundance of aciduric and anaerobic taxa and higher activity in pathways related to carbohydrate utilization, central growth functions and cell wall biosynthesis. Within the stratum with lower caries burden, social-status differences were smaller and involved a more limited set of taxa and pathways, including modules related to stress responses and amino acid and energy metabolism.

Overall, these stratified comparisons suggest that social-status-related signals were weaker and more context-dependent than caries-related signals, and may reflect modulation of salivary community organization rather than a redefinition of community identity. Exploratory network analysis of interaction patterns

At the overall community level, the Caries×LBC interaction was not significant (Section 3.3). To explore whether interaction-related signals might also appear at the systems level, we constructed species-level co-occurrence modules using a WGCNA workflow on prevalence- and abundance-filtered features.

Weighted co-occurrence networks identified several modules of co-varying taxa (Figure 6a). Module eigengenes showed trend-level relationships with the interaction between social status and caries status, although none remained significant after false discovery rate correction (Figure 6b). Modules enriched for acidogenic and Veillonella-related taxa tended to be more strongly represented in left-behind children with high caries burden. Module graphs showed dense intra-module connectivity and cohesive structure (Figure 6c–e).

Nevertheless, their directional consistency with the stratified caries-related patterns suggests that larger or longitudinal cohorts may help clarify whether social context is associated with context-dependent reorganization of caries-associated microbial consortia.

Ecological subtyping identified Neisseria- and Veillonella-anchored types

We derived data-driven community subtypes from key species abundances and projected samples into a low-dimensional space. Two centroids emerged that were anchored by Neisseria species and Veillonella species, respectively (Figure 7). Ellipses indicate cluster dispersion around the two centroids, and group colors map the four strata defined by social status and caries burden. Samples closer to the Veillonella-anchored centroid tended to occur more often in high-caries strata, whereas samples closer to the Neisseria-anchored centroid tended to occur more often in low-caries strata. This pattern was directionally consistent with the stratified caries-related contrasts, in which fermentative and acid-associated taxa increased with greater caries burden, whereas health-associated commensals such as Neisseria were relatively more abundant in groups with lower caries burden. Differences between LBC and NLBC within the same caries stratum were modest, suggesting that social context was not strongly linked to subtype identity but may relate more to broader ecological heterogeneity.

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

This study integrated social context and clinical status within a stratified ecological framework and covariate-adjusted analytic design. In covariate-adjusted analyses, caries burden was more consistently associated with microbial functional shifts than with robust taxonomic reconfiguration, whereas the independent signal of left-behind status was comparatively modest. Com- munity centroids were similar across groups, while within-group dispersion differed. Within the original stratified framework, caries-related taxonomic and functional patterns were directionally concordant across social strata, at species, genus and KEGG level 3 layers [10,26]. By contrast, social-status-related differences were weaker and more context-dependent within caries strata. Network analyses suggested trend-level module associations with caries-related traits that did not survive multiple testing (Section 3.6) [27]. These network findings were therefore interpreted as exploratory. Data-driven subtyping identified two centroids anchored by Neisseria and Veillonella, with directional alignment to caries burden but only modest differences by social status [28,29].

Conceptual framework: a two-hit ecological model

Taken together, these findings support a two-hit ecological framework. The first hit may reflect chronic social context associated with greater ecological heterogeneity without clearly redefining community identity [30,31]. The second hit may reflect cariogenic pressure associated with functional shifts in a more vulnerable ecological background. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies in school-age children to provide multi-layered ecological evidence consistent with a two-hit framework across community structure, function, networks and subtyping. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistic evidence, and longitudinal studies will be required to test temporal ordering and causal relationships.

First hit and ecological heterogeneity

Evidence relevant to the proposed first hit comes from the observation of similar beta-diversity centroids together with heterogeneous within-group dispersion across social strata. Greater dispersion can be interpreted as a statistical ecological indicator of altered community heterogeneity, although it does not directly demonstrate reduced functional stability or resilience [30]. A neutral-model fit with relatively high dispersal is consistent with a setting in which stochastic forces may contribute to centroid similarity while ecological variation remains heterogeneous across social strata (Figure S4) [32]. Within this interpretation, social context may be associated with a more heterogeneous ecological background rather than with the emergence of a distinct community type.

Second hit and caries-associated ecological selection

Within this more heterogeneous ecological background, cariogenic pressure was associated with concordant signals across both social strata, including enrichment of acidogenic and aciduric taxa such as Streptococcus and Veillonella, together with upregulation of translation-, cell-wall-biosynthesis- and carbohydrate-uptake-related pathways, including ribosome, peptidogly can biosynthesis, teichoic-acid biosynthesis and the phosphotransferase system [5,10]. These findings are consistent with ecological selection under low pH and frequent sugar exposure and support the interpretation that greater caries burden is linked to a reproducible functional shift in the salivary microbiome, although they do not by themselves establish a deterministic state transition.

Synergy and risk amplification suggested by networks

Module–trait correlations and membership–significance relationships showed the clearest trends for caries-related traits, whereas the interaction term displayed broader but non-significant associations after multiple-testing correction. Although not statistically robust after correction, these patterns were directionally consistent with the possibility that social context may be associated with greater susceptibility to caries-associated reorganization of microbial consortia. These signals should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and they motivate validation in larger or longitudinal cohorts [27,33].

Ecological subtypes and the possibility of alternative community configurations

Subtyping revealed two centroids anchored by Neisseria and Veillonella (Figure 7; Section 3.7) [28,34]. This subtype structure suggests that caries-related ecological change may involve more than one community configuration, rather than following a simple linear continuum. Social context did not appear to determine subtype identity directly, but may instead contribute to the broader ecological conditions under which communities shift toward Veillonella-associated configurations. These observations support a descriptive ecological interpretation of the two-hit framework, but they should not be taken as direct evidence of resilience loss or discrete state transitions [10].

Limitations and future work

The cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Accordingly, the present data cannot establish temporal ordering, resilience loss, or state transitions. Saliva may underrepresent lesion-proximal processes. In addition, dmft is a cumulative index and does not distinguish active from inactive lesions, which may have introduced clinical heterogeneity into the microbiome associations. Stratum-specific sample sizes reduced power for interaction and network tests, and several network trends did not survive correction. Annotation uncertainty motivates caution at the species level. Coarsely measured factors can also confound associations. Although we incorporated age, sex, sugar intake frequency and toothbrushing frequency into the covariate-adjusted models, residual confounding remains possible. More detailed clinical phenotyping and metadata reporting are increasingly recognized as important for robust interpretation in oral microbiome studies [35].

Furthermore, left-behind status is a heterogeneous construct spanning caregiver contact frequency, economic support, supervision routines and perceived emotional support, which were not resolved in this study. Caregiver structure and parental migration patterns were not modeled in sufficient detail to separate these components. Future work requires finer-grained instruments to test how each contributes to ecological heterogeneity and caries-associated microbial change.

Future work should adopt longitudinal sampling across saliva and plaque, integrate metatranscriptomics and metabolomics, collect higher-resolution psychosocial measures and power interaction tests based on observed effect sizes [36]. High-quality genomes and strain tracking can test whether specific lineages within Streptococcus, Veillonella and Gemella drive observed signals [37]. More detailed characterization of parental migration, caregiving arrangements and fluoride exposure would also strengthen future causal inference. Interventions that reduce sugar exposure during school hours and help stabilize daily routines could be evaluated as quasi-experiments.

Public health implications

Prevention may benefit from two coordinated tracks. First, reduce the second hit through sugar exposure control, sustained fluoride use and timely restorative and preventive care; support school snack policies and routine varnish application where feasible [38]. Second, buffer the first hit by stabilizing daily routines and reducing psychosocial stress through caregiver communication reinforcement, structured snack guidance at school and community-based support for supervision [39]. Although the proposed two-hit framework remains hypothesis-generating, it offers a practical way to link social context with microbial and behavioral risk management within existing oral health programs.

Conclusion

This study integrated social context and clinical status in a 2 × 2 design and provided multi-layered ecological observations across community structure, function, networks and subtyping. Community centroids were similar, whereas within-group dispersion was higher in left-behind children, a pattern consistent with greater ecological heterogeneity rather than a direct measure of reduced ecosystem stability. After covariate adjustment, caries burden was more consistently associated with functional pathway shifts than with robust taxonomic reconfiguration. Within the original stratified framework, caries-related taxonomic and functional patterns were directionally concordant across social strata, including enrichment of acidogenic and aciduric taxa and upregulation of growth- and carbohydrate-uptake-related pathways. Network analyses suggested trend-level associations that did not survive correction, and data-driven subtyping identified two centroids anchored by Neisseria and Veillonella. Taken together, these findings are consistent with a two-hit ecological framework. The first hit may reflect social context associated with greater ecological heterogeneity without clearly redefining community identity. The second hit may reflect cariogenic pressure associated with reproducible functional shifts toward dysbiosis-related ecological change. Because of the cross-sectional design, this framework should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistic evidence. Future longitudinal and multi-omic studies with finer-grained social measures can test temporal ordering and refine targets for intervention.

Appendix A. Supplementary methods

Appendix A.1. Sequence quality control and profiling

Adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed with fastp 0.23.2. Parameters were minimum read length 50 bp, minimum mean quality score 20, and removal of reads with >5% ambiguous bases. Human reads were filtered by mapping against GRCh38 with Bowtie2 2.5.1 using default alignment settings.

Taxonomic profiling used MetaPhlAn 4.0 marker-gene models at the species level. Functional profiling used HUMAnN 3.6 with UniRef90, regrouped to KEGG Orthologs and summarized to KEGG level 3 pathways. Rarefaction and read-retention summaries were inspected to confirm adequate depth.

All steps were executed in a Snakemake workflow with fixed versions and documented parameters. Quality reports were compiled with MultiQC. Meta-data including sequencing depth, GC content, extraction batch and run were retained for covariate checks.

Appendix A.2. Diversity analyses

Alpha diversity used the Simpson index on species-level profiles. Beta diversity used Bray–Curtis dissimilarity (vegan 2.6) with principal coordinates analysis for visualization. PERMANOVA used adonis2 with 9,999 permutations for the model

Dispersion homogeneity was tested with betadisper and permutational tests. Within-group dispersion was summarized as the distance from each sample to its group centroid.

Appendix A.3. Differential abundance

Species and genera were filtered to mean relative abundance >1 × 10−5 and prevalence ≥ 10%. Relative abundances were transformed with centered log ratio after adding a pseudocount 1 × 10−6. Two-group contrasts used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Factorial comparisons used two-way models with terms for caries, LBC, and their interaction where appropriate. Multiple testing used Benjamini–Hochberg control with significance at q < 0.10. Effect directions and standardized differences were reported; sensitivity models adjusted for age, sex and sequencing depth.

Appendix A.4. Functional and pathway analyses

HUMAnN outputs were regrouped to KEGG Orthologs and summarized to KEGG level 3 pathways. Differential analyses followed the same framework as taxonomic tests with BH correction at q < 0.10. Enrichment summaries highlighted translation, cell-wall biosynthesis and carbohydrate-uptake modules when applicable. Spearman correlations between representative taxa and pathways were computed with BH-adjusted q-values to support directionality.

Appendix A.5. Network analyses

Co-occurrence modules were derived with a WGCNA workflow at the species level. Counts were CLR-transformed. An unsigned adjacency was computed with a soft-threshold power chosen by scale-free topology fit. Topological overlap matrices were clustered and modules defined by dynamic tree cut with minimum module size 20. Module eigengenes were correlated with caries, LBC and the interaction term. p-values were adjusted across modules with BH; trend-level results that did not survive correction were labeled exploratory. Membership–significance relationships were summarized for selected modules.

A sensitivity co-occurrence network used SPIEC-EASI (MB method) with stability selection. Edges were retained under conservative sparsity to mitigate compositional artifacts. Global properties including density, transitivity and modularity were compared by permutation.

Appendix A.6. Ecological subtyping

Community subtypes were derived from species-level embeddings by k-means with k = 2. Distances used Euclidean on CLR-transformed features. Subtype stability was assessed by bootstrap resampling. Associations between subtype and strata were summarized by contingency tables with Fisher's exact test. Centroids and dispersion ellipses provided visualization.

Appendix A.7. Statistical framework and thresholds

Analyses were conducted in R 4.3.2 and Python 3.10. Two-sided tests were used. Unless otherwise specified, multiple testing used BH with significance at q < 0.10. For exploratory network trends that did not pass correction, results were labeled as hypothesis-generating.
