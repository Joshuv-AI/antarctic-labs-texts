First Responders Have Lower COVID-19 Vaccination Rates Than Other Healthcare Providers
Authors: Mirela Anghelina, Valerie Sircelj, Jennifer A. Frey, John Rider, Daniel J. Bachmann, Soledad Fernandez, Ashish R. Panchal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesCOVID-19 vaccination status varies widely among first responders. We sought to understand whether similar vaccination rates exist for first responders and healthcare providers (nurses/physicians) and if this rate changed over time.MethodsThis is a secondary analysis of a prospective longitudinal study of first responders and healthcare providers recruited starting in January 2021 and followed until April 2024. Following enrollment, participants completed surveys concerning personal demographics (age, sex, race, professional role [first responder, healthcare worker], use of personal protective equipment (PPE), and number of household members under age 18), and medical history (COVID-19 vaccination status, previous COVID-19 infection, and history of comorbidities). Participant vaccination status was confirmed at least every 6 months. Descriptive statistics were calculated, followed by logistic regression models (OR, 95% CI) to describe the associations between vaccination, demographics, and work characteristics. The cumulative incidence was visually described using a Kaplan-Meier plot.ResultsAcross 190 study participants, 61.1% were first responders, and 38.9% were healthcare providers. First responders were older, predominantly male, and reported more prior exposure to COVID-19. Although the odds of vaccination varied greatly within healthcare providers (OR 8.26, 95% CI 2.42-28.18), their odds of vaccination were higher than those of first responders (referent). Kaplan-Meier plots demonstrated that vaccination rates for both groups plateaued after 1 year.ConclusionsThis study noted differential rates of vaccination between first responders and healthcare professionals and that these rates plateaued, demonstrating fixed perspectives on vaccination. Focused efforts to understand diverse perspectives on vaccination concerns may enhance the protection and maintenance of these workforces.

The Bottom LineConcerns about COVID-19 vaccinations persist among first responders. Overall, their vaccination rates remain lower than (74.1%) those of healthcare professionals (95.9%), and most make their decision early and rarely change it after 12 months. Addressing clinicians’ legitimate concerns is essential to protect and stabilize the Emergency Medical Services workforce during a pandemic.

The Bottom Line

Concerns about COVID-19 vaccinations persist among first responders. Overall, their vaccination rates remain lower than (74.1%) those of healthcare professionals (95.9%), and most make their decision early and rarely change it after 12 months. Addressing clinicians’ legitimate concerns is essential to protect and stabilize the Emergency Medical Services workforce during a pandemic.

Introduction

Background

Vaccines are important tools to build strong protection against serious infectious diseases, including the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) that causes severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2).1,2 In March 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared a global pandemic and subsequently began a large-scale vaccine dissemination strategy with plans for global vaccination by 2022.3,4 Despite widespread availability of the vaccine in the USA after December 2020, only 79.1% of adults had completed the primary series of COVID-19 vaccination at the end of 2022. This is in the face of goals of >90% vaccinations to achieve herd immunity due to the high transmissibility of COVID-19 and low effectiveness of vaccinations in preventing infections.5,6 Further, variability in vaccination rates are also observed in the healthcare population even with many healthcare organizations mandating COVID-19 vaccinations.7, 8, 9 Vaccinations in these populations is particularly critical recognizing the high risk of infectious spread both within this population and the patients they treat as well as the potential for maintaining a healthy and available workforce.10,11

Importance

In the frame of pandemic preparedness, the large variability in first responder and healthcare worker vaccine uptake is a large source of potential concern. Though the percentage of vaccination nearly reached 80% in the general population, first responders achieved only 70%, as noted in large national cohorts.7 Even prior to the availability of COVID-19 vaccinations, evaluations of the acceptability of vaccinations in Emergency Medical Services (EMS) clinicians demonstrated low possible uptake rates.12,13 The large vaccine and booster hesitancy that was expressed during COVID-19 defined challenges with vaccine uptake, but much of this was thought to be mitigated by organizational policies both at the hospital and EMS agency level. It is unclear whether this contrast in vaccine hesitancy and update continued through the pandemic between first responders and other healthcare providers in the face of continued workplace exposures and safety policies. Additionally, questions remain whether first responders and healthcare providers continued to receive vaccinations slowly over time as the pandemic continued.

Goals of This Investigation

The aim of this study was to assess the differences between first responders and healthcare providers in COVID-19 vaccine uptake, as well as the associated factors that influence the vaccination decision throughout the pandemic. Leveraging a longitudinal sample of EMS and hospital-based healthcare providers, we assessed whether vaccination rates were comparable and examined trends in vaccine uptake over time.14,15

Methods

Study Design

This is a secondary analysis of a prospective longitudinal cohort study of first responders and healthcare providers in an urban Midwest area of the USA. This study is part of a greater effort of a U54 grant [#U54CA260582] led by the Center for Serological Testing to Improve Outcomes from Pandemic COVID-19 (STOP-COVID), which has an overarching goal of understanding the prevalence and transmission of COVID-19. The study's funders had no role in the study protocol design, data collection, data management, analysis, data interpretation, or manuscript writing. The Ohio State University Institutional Review Board approved the protocol (IRB#2020H0531), and informed consent was obtained from all participants in this study.

Setting and Participants

First responders and healthcare providers were recruited to be part of a longitudinal study to understand COVID-19 infection and its changing impact on immunity.16, 17, 18, 19 Participants were over 18 years old and first responders (eg, police, fire, emergency medical services) or healthcare providers (eg, nurses, physicians) who were willing to have COVID-19 clinical tests and blood collection minimally twice a year and were willing to participate in brief recurring surveys about demographics, health risks, and exposures. Recruitment to the study was ongoing and utilized fliers distributed at their respective agencies (ie, fire department, police department, hospital/clinical units) and in-person recruitment events from January 2021 to February 2023. Entry into the study was strictly voluntary with no incentives or compensation. All study visits and surveys were completed by April 2024, and all participants were followed for the duration of the study.

Measurements

Following enrollment, surveys were completed by participants, including demographic information and medical history, including COVID-19 vaccination status. Demographic information included age, sex (male, female), and race (White, non-White). Medical history included participants’ status with known past COVID-19 infections, comorbidity as defined by the Charlson Comorbidity Index and presented as a dichotomous variable (comorbidity yes/no) due to the healthy population, and smoking status.20

Additionally, data were collected regarding whether subjects had children due to the enhanced potential for infectious exposures in the home, and the portion of time that personal protective equipment (PPE) was used in public when not at work. PPE use was defined as the proportion of the time a person uses a face mask in public when not at work. PPE use was categorized, based on the distribution of PPE use in the population, as wearing a face mask less than 90% of the time in public or masking 90% or more of the time in public.

Vaccination status was collected and confirmed at least every 6 months; being fully vaccinated was defined as receipt of the first/second dose of the COVID-19 vaccine without a booster based on the type of vaccine received.

Data Collection Procedures

Study participants completed COVID-19 clinical tests (nasopharyngeal or anterior nares swabs) and blood collection at least twice a year and completed brief recurring surveys about demographics, health risks, and exposures. Following enrollment in the study, participants were contacted weekly and surveyed regarding symptoms of COVID-19 infection. If a participant was found to have symptoms of COVID-19 infection, the research team conducted a symptomatic visit where COVID-19 clinical test samples (nasopharyngeal or anterior nares swabs) and blood were collected. After a positive COVID-19 test, the participant was then considered having prior COVID-19 infection.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics of all measures, such as frequencies, medians, and ranges, were provided for the sample and for the 2 subgroups of patients based on whether they are first responders or healthcare providers. Comparisons were made between these groups using t test, chi-square, or Fisher’s exact test depending on the outcome. Univariable logistic regression models were fit using complete case analysis to evaluate the associations between patients’ characteristics and odds of vaccination. The cumulative incidence was visually described using a Kaplan-Meier plot, and the log-rank test was used to assess differences between the 2 groups. Stata 18 (College Station, Texas) was used for all analyses. All p values were two sided, and the statistical significance level was set at.05.

Ethics Approval

The local Institutional Review Board approved the protocol, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in this study.

Results

A flow diagram of the population is provided in Figure 1. The enrolled study population was 294 which included first responders, healthcare providers, and household contacts. Of these, 65 participants dropped out of the longitudinal study (retention rate 78%) for a total population, followed longitudinally, of 229. We excluded household contacts (n = 30) and those with missing primary outcome data (n = 9). Vaccination Rates Over Time

Cumulative incidence of vaccination among first responders and healthcare providers was assessed using Kaplan-Meier method, as shown in Figure 2. Analysis reveals that both groups have the same longitudinal vaccination pattern, with more than 50% of the population getting vaccinated in the first 3 months of 2021 and a much slower rate of vaccination after that. However, 75% of healthcare providers received vaccinations in the first 41 days, while the percentage of vaccinated first responders never reaches 75% over the analyzed time period (p <.001). Although the proportion of the participants that are vaccinated decreases for both populations at each booster schedule, healthcare providers continue to be represented in a larger proportion than first responders for boosters 1, 2, and 3. Limitations

This sample of first responders and healthcare providers may not be representative of the entire US healthcare workforce and may reflect that healthcare providers in the local health system had expectations for vaccination. However, the study was conducted in a metropolitan area and was not limited to any specific type of hospital or EMS/fire agency. Selection bias is possible since this is a longitudinal study where participants self-selected to engage in research and were followed over time. The sex distribution in our study population was not balanced between the 2 groups: more first responders were male, while more healthcare providers were female. However, it is similar to the sex distribution of these 2 health occupations in the USA.21 Due to the very low frequency of 3 participants in the group of healthcare providers without vaccination, the standard deviation and confidence interval of the odds ratio of healthcare providers vs first responders are large, so there is uncertainty about the strength of the relationship. However, we consider that the specific OR is worth reporting since it is consistently significant. Finally, in this study, we did not evaluate the potential positive clinical and workforce impact of vaccinations of first responders or healthcare workers. However, prospective multicenter evaluations have demonstrated decreased morbidity and absenteeism in healthcare personnel, suggesting the potential impact of enhanced vaccinations in these populations.10,11

Discussion

In this evaluation, we note contrasting COVID-19 vaccination rates between first responders and healthcare providers over the time period of the study. Though both populations face high risks of COVID-19 exposure throughout the pandemic, in our longitudinal cohort, we observed an early uptake of COVID-19 vaccination followed by a plateau in both populations. This suggests that the events and experiences around the pandemic did not drive clinicians to get vaccinated after making their initial decision. In contrast, those who were concerned about risk and were fully vaccinated continued to protect themselves with consistent use of PPE.

These findings have important implications for pandemic preparedness and vaccination strategies for first responders and healthcare providers in the USA. For first responders, enhancing vaccination is critical since they play a key role in the healthcare continuum linking patients at home with the hospital system.7,22 Multiple concerns around vaccinations within this population have been observed previously, making a high rate of vaccine protection (even for influenza and other diseases) difficult to achieve.23,24 In contrast, healthcare providers benefited from well-established hospital policies focused on clinician wellness to maintain a healthy hospital workforce. These structured hospital-based practices helped ensure high vaccination rates. This paradox between first responders and healthcare providers suggests a need to optimize vaccine uptake in this high-risk first responder group while improving messaging efforts through agency-based systems. However, opposition still exists regarding vaccination requirements for first responders stemming from legitimate concerns of medical, employer, and media mistrust, recognized low-risk infections in young healthy populations, and a lack of clarity on the rapid development of vaccines.25, 26, 27 This suggests the need to better understand the challenges around vaccination programs in this population.

The focus on this cohort of patients was based on their unique higher risk profile for COVID-19 exposure and the regulated environment in which they work. PPE use was required during the period of the COVID-19 pandemic for most, if not all, members of this cohort. Despite these contextual similarities, these groups have important variations in perspectives regarding COVID-19 vaccination that have not been thoroughly explored. This analysis provides an important perspective regarding the lower rate of first responder vaccination compared with the healthcare worker subgroup. It further correlates PPE use with vaccination uptake. Interestingly, this analysis demonstrates that a ceiling effect on first responder vaccinations exists due to legitimate concerns regarding vaccines. In the end, those who do not want vaccinations tend not to change their mind even with many of their peers being vaccinated. Within a year, those who wanted a vaccination, or were undecided, received their vaccinations. A better understanding of how to message the importance of vaccinations to this population will be needed for future pandemic preparedness.

Understanding the drivers of first responders in comparison to healthcare providers may begin to provide evidence for the next steps in pandemic preparedness strategies. We found that the odds of vaccination for first responders were much lower than those of the healthcare providers. While there was no difference between the 2 groups in PPE use in public, the odds of vaccination were higher among those who reported wearing PPE 90% of the time or more when in public and outside of work. All the other factors we explored do not seem to be correlated with the vaccination decision. Surprisingly, the presence of comorbidities does not change the rate of vaccination. Although around half of the participants of the 2 groups reported 1 or more comorbidities, the enrolled population is relatively young and may not suffer severe comorbidities. Previous history of COVID-19 infection was shown to increase vaccination rates in other studies of the US population; however, in our study, it did not change the vaccine acceptance.28

Other work has identified 3 key factors that influence vaccination decisions: perceived risk of COVID-19, medical mistrust, and vaccine confidence as drivers of hesitancy.7 Perceived risk of infection and the ramifications vary for frontline clinicians, with some expressing significant concerns while others have none.25 In large EMS populations, as perceived individual risk increased, odds of vaccination dramatically increased by twofold, though in general, EMS clinicians felt that COVID-19 was of low risk.7 In contrast, medical mistrust impacted vaccine decisions in EMS clinicians greatly, where high levels of medical mistrust were associated with decreased vaccination rates.7,25,29 However, stronger than both of these associations was that of confidence in the COVID-19 vaccine itself. When clinicians were supporters of the vaccine and received vaccinations, the odds of vaccination were high.7,25 Of note, 1 driver that was not evaluated in this study is the impact of the diverse workplace settings and possible required vaccinations by employers. As noted above, a key driver of vaccine decision-making is trust and pressure from employers, and possible negative consequences from not being vaccinated, was thought to break trust and had an impact on clinician decision-making.7,25,26

High-risk populations of first responders and healthcare providers demonstrated differential rates of COVID-19 vaccinations. The COVID-19 vaccination choices were made early and appear to stay consistent through the pandemic, with very few choosing to be vaccinated after the first year of vaccine availability. Thus, the first responder population has potentially firmly held beliefs regarding vaccinations. As part of future pandemic preparedness efforts, a focused effort to enhance confidence in vaccinations is essential for the protection and maintenance of these critical workforces, and strategies need to be developed to address the low rates in first responders.
