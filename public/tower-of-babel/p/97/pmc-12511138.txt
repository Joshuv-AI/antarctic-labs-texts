Classification of intracranial aneurysm remnants after clipping in the era of three-dimensional digital subtraction angiography
Authors: Serge Marbacher, Lukas Andereggen
Published: 2025

We read with great interest the study by Thorsteinsdottir et al. “Detection of remnants in clipped unruptured intracranial aneurysms by intraoperative CT-angiography and postoperative DSA: clinical relevance and follow-up” [17]. In a subgroup of 74 patients from a cohort of 270, the authors evaluated the surgical outcome of intracranial aneurysm (IA) clipping using intraoperative CT angiography (iCTA) in addition to microvascular Doppler sonography, microscope-integrated near-infrared indocyanine green video angiography (ICG-VA), and postoperative three-dimensional digital subtraction angiography (3D-DSA). They demonstrated that the additional yield for surgical relevant information provided by iCTA (4.1%, 3/74) was nearly equivalent to that of the established vascular monitoring techniques with Doppler sonography and ICG-VA (4.5%, 12/270). Compared to postoperative 3D-DSA, the sensitivity of iCTA for detecting remnants of any size was 15.8%, and 75% for larger remnants.

The authors should be commended for their efforts to enhance the safety and efficacy of IA surgery. Provided that spatial resolution can be improved in future devices, iCTA has the potential to become a valuable non-invasive and resource-efficient intraoperative tool for assessing the surgical outcome and for clip repositioning if necessary. This study also represents an important contribution to the existing literature, as to date, only a limited number of authors have reported 3D-DSA-based data on IA remnant rates and recurrences [9, 13]. The introduction of 3D-DSA has enabled a more accurate differentiation between completely and incompletely occluded aneurysms, an important distinction that was not consistently achievable using conventional 2D-DSA.

2D-DSA has traditionally been regarded as the gold standard for assessing IA remnants following microsurgical clipping. However, comparative studies have demonstrated that 2D-DSA fails to detect approximately 50% of all remnants and up to 80% of small remnants when compared to 3D-DSA [1, 6, 10, 12, 14, 16]. As a result, it remained uncertain whether a documented recurrence stemmed from a previously undetected remnant. This diagnostic uncertainty is of clinical significance, as the risk of recurrence following complete occlusion is exceedingly low (0%—2%), whereas IAs with residual filling exhibit significantly higher recurrence rates, with reported remnant growth in 7%—42of cases [2–5, 7, 14, 18]. Thorsteinsdottir et al. clearly illustrate this distinction, reporting recurrence rates of 1.5% in completely occluded aneurysms compared to 16.7% in those with remnants [17].

Beyond the critical distinction between complete and incomplete occlusion, 3D-DSA enables the possibility to precisely measure the residual IA filling in all dimensions, allowing for quantification of the remnant based on its maximum dimension (Fig. 1). In the present study, the authors utilize this quantification of remnant size to guide clinical decisions and follow-up planning. Complementing the size-based quantification, the 2D-DSA classification system developed by Sindou and colleagues is also applied [14]. However, the 2D-DSA-based classification of IA remnants has inherent technical limitations that adversely compromise both its clinical utility and reproducibility. Two-dimensional imaging fails to represent the irregular topography and the subtle spatial relationship between the clip, residual aneurysm tissue, and the parent vessel. This can lead to misinterpretation of remnant size and morphology. Overlapping vascular structures and clip-induced artifacts can obscure small remnants or hinder accurate delineation of the true extent of residual aneurysm tissue. A further limitation lies in the reliance on arbitrary dimensional thresholds and comparison with preoperative angiographic image measurements [11]. For instance, the widely used Sindou classification quantifies remnants by comparing their size to the preoperative IA, such as defining residual filling as greater or less than 50% of the initial neck or 75% of the aneurysm sac. Moreover, despite calibration efforts, size measurements in 2D-DSA remain inherently limited by projection-related distortions. Vessels not oriented orthogonally to the imaging axis may appear foreshortened or narrowed, and magnification effects vary with the distance between the object and the detector. Accurate comparisons would thus require identical patient positioning and consistent detector-to-object distance during pre- and postoperative imaging, as well as unchanged local anatomy post-clipping, conditions that are rarely met in clinical practice.We recently investigated the value of 3D-DSA for the detection and classification of IA remnants after clipping (Fig. 2). Our findings demonstrated that even the smallest remnants, regardless of the complexity of the local angioarchitecture or the number of applied clips, can be visualized with high precision [14, 15]. Moreover, our method of quantitative grading of 3D-DSA remnants proved to be both simple and practical, exhibiting strong inter- and intra-rater agreement [8]. Given the increasingly widespread use of 3D-DSA for postoperative assessment, as highlighted by the work of Thorsteinsdottir and colleagues, it will soon be possible to validate the preliminary advantages of a 3D-DSA-based classification system.
