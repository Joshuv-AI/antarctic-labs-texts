Cell-based drug delivery for combating bacteria: focus on refractory infections
Authors: Weijing Cheng, Nan Ding, Ling Wu, Tingrui Zhang, Yu Liao, Zongguang Tai, Jing Tian, Zhuo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The escalating crisis of antimicrobial resistance and biofilm-associated infections has fundamentally compromised conventional anti-infective therapy. Current antimicrobial agents for refractory infections face critical limitations, including systemic toxicity, inadequate tissue penetration, and the rapid emergence of multidrug resistance (MDR). As a promising strategy, cell-based drug delivery systems (CDDS) leverage living cells or their bioactive derivatives as sophisticated carriers to overcome these barriers. By harnessing innate biological functions, such as prolonged circulation, immune evasion, and active targeting, CDDS enhances therapeutic efficacy in preclinical models against refractory infections. This review systematically elaborates on CDDS platforms engineered from erythrocytes, immune cells, platelets, and stem cells, highlighting their unique mechanisms and advantages in anti-infective drug delivery. Furthermore, we critically evaluate the translational progress and future potential of CDDS in combating refractory infections, particularly those involving multidrug-resistant bacteria and biofilm-associated pathogens.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Representing the second most substantial contributor to the worldwide burden of disease, bacterial infections currently remain a major ongoing challenge to global public health security. The current antimicrobial efficacy is seriously threatened by the development of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), especially multidrug resistance (MDR) [1–3]. Bacterial resistance evolves through three primary adaptive mechanisms [4]: plasmid-mediated acquisition of resistance genes [5], overexpression of broad-spectrum multidrug efflux pumps [6], and enzymatic drug inactivation via hydrolysis (e.g., β-lactamases) or chemical modifications, such as acetylation, phosphorylation, and adenylylation [7].

The rising threat of multidrug-resistant organisms and biofilm-related infections has prompted an urgent call for novel therapeutic interventions [8]. To address the shortcomings of traditional antibiotics, sophisticated drug delivery systems, particularly nanoscale drug delivery systems (NDDS), have been devised [9]. Nanoscale materials like liposomes, polymers, and inorganic particles prolong circulation, facilitate the speed up of extravasation, and inhibit macrophage phagocytosis [10]. Examples include liposomal amphotericin B (AmBisome®), which improves medication stability while decreasing nephrotoxicity [11], and PEGylation strategies that promote particle penetration in pulmonary mucus. Active targeting approaches with ligands like mannose, folate receptor aptamers, or antibodies enhance site-specific accumulation at infection foci [12]. Nonetheless, clinical translation faces ongoing challenges. The circulation half-life of nanoparticles is markedly reduced due to their swift sequestration by the mononuclear phagocyte system [13]. The dense extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) matrix of bacterial biofilms physically impedes nanoparticle penetration [14]. Moreover, slow metabolic clearance of inorganic nanoparticles might result in long-term organ toxicity, immunological dysfunction, and neurotoxicity [15].

Among these challenges, the dense EPS matrix of biofilms is particularly problematic, as it physically impedes nanoparticle penetration [16]. Biofilm-associated infections are not a monolithic entity; they can be classified along several axes that influence therapeutic outcomes. First, by location: surface-associated biofilms (e.g., on medical devices, prosthetic joints, or necrotic tissue) versus intracellular biofilm-like niches (e.g., within phagocytes or epithelial cells, as seen in chronic salmonellosis or tuberculosis). Second, by disease stage: acute biofilms versus chronic biofilms. Acute biofilms are typically more susceptible to immune intervention and antibiotic penetration. Chronic biofilms are characterized by increased matrix deposition, metabolic dormancy of persister cells, and heightened tolerance to conventional therapy [17]. Third, by microbial composition: mono-species biofilms (e.g., Pseudomonas aeruginosa in cystic fibrosis) versus polymicrobial biofilms (e.g., diabetic foot ulcers containing multiple bacterial and fungal species) [18, 19]. Each of these biofilm contexts presents distinct physiological barriers and therapeutic challenges.

To address these challenges, researchers have turned to biomimetic strategies. Inspired by the concept of biomimicry, cell-based drug delivery systems (CDDS) have received a lot of interest in recent years due to their outstanding ability to reproduce natural biological processes [20]. CDDS mimics endogenous delivery pathways by combining the biological features of living cells, resulting in antimicrobial platforms with inherent biocompatibility, precision targeting capabilities, and physiological adaptability [21]. CDDS provide notable advantages over conventional drug delivery methods, including longer circulation, increased targeted specificity, greater biocompatibility, and barrier-penetrating capacity [22]. CDDS architectures are based on three fundamental paradigms: (1) Whole-cell carriers exploit inherent biological properties for targeted drug transportation; (2) Cell membrane- encapsulated nanoparticles achieve precise cellular targeting via membrane-bound protein recognition; (3) Extracellular vesicles (EVs) function as endogenous nanocarriers with intrinsic low immunogenicity (Fig. 1). Functionally, CDDS achieve dynamic performance by preserving or engineering membrane surface molecules, enabling intelligent behaviors such as immune evasion, targeted recognition, and microenvironmental responsiveness [20]. CDDS use ligand-receptor interactions and stimuli-sensitive mechanisms to precisely localize to infection sites, penetrate EPS biofilm matrices and host cell membranes, and significantly improve drug delivery to intracellular pathogens and deep biofilm layers [23].

However, several factors account for the restricted application of CDDS in anti-infective therapy despite these promising preclinical advantages. Manufacturing complexity poses substantial challenges for large-scale production and clinical translation, as cell-based carriers require complex processes including cell sourcing, expansion, drug loading, and quality control that are difficult to standardize [24]. Safety concerns associated with live cell carriers also require rigorous preclinical evaluation. Excessively activated macrophages can release pro-inflammatory cytokines that may aggravate tissue damage, and stem cell-based carriers carry potential tumorigenicity risks [25]. The short functional lifespan of certain cell types further limits their therapeutic window. Neutrophils have a circulating half-life of only 6–8 h under physiological conditions [26]. Batch-to-batch variability remains another significant hurdle, particularly for cell-derived platforms such as extracellular vesicles, where donor cell state, isolation methods, and storage conditions can substantially affect product consistency [27]. These challenges collectively explain why, despite the growing number of preclinical studies, CDDS have not yet been widely adopted in anti-infective therapy.

Despite potential breakthroughs in CDDS for oncological and inflammatory diseases, their application in infectious diseases is still at an early stage. A growing number of preclinical studies have been reported, but significant knowledge gaps remain. These include in vivo behavior, long-term safety, and platform standardization, particularly for refractory infections caused by MDR bacteria and biofilms. To address these knowledge gaps, this review adopts a systematic approach. We first discuss the advantages of CDDS and benchmark them against conventional NDDS. We then evaluate drug loading strategies for cell-based and cell-derived delivery systems, followed by a comprehensive review of the applications of various CDDS platforms. We highlight emerging cell sources that have proven therapeutic potential in non-infectious situations and could be repurposed for antimicrobial applications. Finally, we identify evidence gaps for each platform in specific biofilm contexts and discuss potential mechanisms by which CDDS may influence antimicrobial resistance evolution. By integrating contemporary breakthroughs in biomimetic nanotechnology and host-pathogen interactions, this work provides a timely and authoritative perspective on CDDS as promising emerging tools against antimicrobial resistance, although their clinical translation remains in early stages.

Advantages of CDDS in bacterial infections

Engineered drug carriers have been constructed based on the structural traits, dynamic behaviors, and molecular mechanisms of living organisms, giving them biological intelligence and functional adaptability that surpasses traditional drug delivery systems. Among these, CDDS demonstrate significant advantages in enhancing in vivo drug delivery efficiency through their unique capacity to reconstruct biological properties [20]. The core of CDDS lies in leveraging the intrinsic characteristics of natural cells to achieve more precise and sustained drug delivery [28] (Fig. 2).

Immune evasion for prolonged circulation in chronic infections

For patients with chronic infections—such as prosthetic joint infections, chronic osteomyelitis, or device-related infections—current antibiotic therapy often requires prolonged intravenous administration, frequently necessitating hospitalization for weeks or months. This not only compromises patient quality of life but also drives healthcare costs and adherence challenges. CDDS leverage their inherent biocompatibility and low immunogenicity to achieve sustained drug exposure, reducing the need for frequent dosing. These carriers, derived from autologous or allogeneic cells, naturally possess host cell biological traits and are recognized as self by the immune system, effectively avoiding immune clearance [29]. Coating drugs with erythrocyte membranes can significantly prolong their circulation duration in vivo and improve anti-infective efficacy because erythrocytes have a circulation lifespan of up to 120 days and are low in immunogenicity [30]. Similarly, EVs, including exosomes and microvesicles, contain native membrane proteins and lipid bilayers that enable them to evade immune surveillance via host cell recognition mechanisms, effectively extending the drug’s half-life in vivo [31]. Quantitative evidence from other CDDS platforms further supports this immune evasion capability: CD47-mimetic peptide-functionalized nanoparticles reduced macrophage internalization by 3.5-fold, and macrophage membrane coating reduced phagocytosis by 3-fold [32, 33].

Active targeting for deep-seated and intracellular infections

The inherent targeting capability of CDDS provides unique advantages in anti-infective therapy. Immune cells, such as macrophages and neutrophils, possess specific chemotaxis abilities that recognize infection sites and selectively accumulate at inflamed tissues [34]. This intrinsic targeting capability contrasts with conventional NDDS, which typically require conjugation of exogenous ligands (e.g., antibodies, aptamers) to achieve active targeting. CDDS mimics these chemotaxis and phagocytic activities, enabling targeted antibacterial treatment. The clinical significance of this capability becomes evident when considering two particularly challenging scenarios: deep-seated infections (e.g., osteomyelitis, endocarditis) where systemic antibiotics struggle to achieve therapeutic concentrations due to poor vascularization, and intracellular pathogens (e.g., Salmonella, Mycobacterium tuberculosis) that evade extracellular antibiotics by residing within host cells. In both cases, the ability of CDDS to actively home to infection sites offers a potential solution where conventional delivery falls short. Li et al. found that collagen-based nanoparticles coated with macrophage membranes (Ce6/Col/MM) exhibited 5.7-fold higher association with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) compared to free photosensitizer, and 4.9-fold higher compared to uncoated nanoparticles. Under light irradiation, this system reduced bacterial viability to 6.7 ± 0.6% in an MRSA-infected wound model, demonstrating potent targeted antibacterial efficacy [35]. Besides, Wang et al. found that stimulation by specific pathogens can upregulate recognition receptor expression, further strengthening CDDS’s targeting capabilities [36]. CDDS’s intrinsic targeting significantly reduces systemic drug distribution and side effects, enhancing therapeutic safety and specificity [37].

Controlled release to reduce dosing frequency and toxicity

Conventional antibiotics face two interrelated pharmacokinetic limitations: many are rapidly degraded by enzymes or cleared by the kidneys, requiring frequent dosing to maintain therapeutic levels; conversely, those with narrow therapeutic windows (e.g., aminoglycosides, vancomycin) pose significant risks of off-target toxicity when administered systemically at high doses. CDDS offer a dual solution to these limitations by creating a protective barrier that shields drugs from premature degradation and by enabling controlled release in response to specific microenvironmental cues [38]. For instance, exosomes and erythrocyte membrane carriers can successfully shield encapsulated medications because of their intrinsic stability and biocompatibility [39]. Moreover, to initiate drug release, CDDS can be designed to react to particular microenvironmental cues, such as an acidic pH, enzyme activity, or temperature changes in lesion sites. Lysosomes and exosomes can release antimicrobial agents in infected regions, and surface ligands on exosomes can be modified for precise environment-responsive drug release [40]. This controlled release mechanism can increase local drug concentration, enhance therapeutic efficacy, and reduce systemic toxicity.

Multifunctionality against MDR, biofilms, and tissue damage

CDDS can be modified through genetic engineering and surface modification to carry various antimicrobial agents, such as small-molecule antibiotics, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), and gene therapeutics, thereby enhancing efficacy, targeting, and bioavailability [41]. The value of such multifunctionality is particularly pronounced in three refractory infection scenarios where single-modality therapies consistently fail: (1) multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial infections, which often require combination therapy but conventional drug combinations may have antagonistic pharmacokinetics or additive toxicity; (2) biofilm-associated infections, where the physical barrier of the extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix and metabolically dormant “persister” cells render antibiotics ineffective; For biofilm-associated infections, CDDS offer enhanced penetration and bacterial clearance. Macrophages demonstrated approximately 3-fold higher bacterial clearance from biofilms compared to conventional biomaterials, achieving up to 70% reduction in bacterial numbers [42]; and (3) post-infectious tissue damage (e.g., lung fibrosis after pneumonia, bone destruction after osteomyelitis), which antibiotics alone cannot address. Modification with chemokines, AMPs, or specific targeting ligands can improve CDDS localization and antimicrobial efficacy [43]. AMPs have extensive antimicrobial properties against bacteria, viruses, and fungi, and may have anticancer effects [44]. Genetically modified bacteria can deliver AMPs (such as co-delivery of GLP-1), exerting therapeutic effects by reducing pathogen niches, eliminating resistant strains, and remodeling gut microbiota, thereby achieving microbiome reconstruction and multifaceted antibacterial action [45]. Genetically engineered bacteriophages can produce biofilm-degrading enzymes (e.g., lactonase) or quorum-quenching enzymes to dismantle the structural integrity of biofilms, thereby addressing resistant bacteria [46]. Studies have shown that stem cells can precisely transport active antibiotics (such as ciprofloxacin) to the infection site, and promote tissue remodeling and wound healing by secreting growth factors and cytokines (e.g., recruiting regulatory T cells and supporting fibroblast proliferation) [47, 48]. Although stem cell-based bionic drug delivery systems have not yet been applied in the field of anti-infection, the tissue repair and immune modulation properties of stem cells show great potential for drug delivery in the future anti-infection field.

Nevertheless, CDDS face their own translational hurdles. As summarized in Sect. "Unlocking the clinical potential of CDDS for infectious diseases", no CDDS platform has yet received regulatory approval for anti-infective therapy. Thus, CDDS and conventional NDDS are best viewed as complementary approaches, each suited to different clinical scenarios.

Drug loading strategies based on cell and cell-derived delivery systems

Cells and their derivatives (such as cell membranes and extracellular vesicles) serve as drug carriers by leveraging functional groups and surface properties provided by biomolecules (proteins, lipids, polysaccharides). These properties allow therapeutics or nanoparticle carriers to adhere to the cell surface or be internalized, forming drug-cell or particle-cell complexes that enable cell-mediated drug delivery. Selecting appropriate drug-loading strategies for various cells and their derivatives ensures stronger binding between the drug/particles and carriers, prolonged circulation time, and reduced immunogenicity [52]. While the drug loading strategies described below are generally applicable to CDDS across various disease models, this section focuses on their specific applications and considerations in anti-infective therapy.

Intracellular encapsulation

The direct loading of drugs into cells is a commonly employed method to enable cell-mediated drug targeting. Encapsulating drugs within cells takes advantage of the cell’s natural barrier to protect the drug from degradation and enables delivery to target sites via the cell’s circulatory pathways. Drugs can be effectively encapsulated into cells using co-incubation, electroporation, chemical transfection, and hypotonic swelling (Fig. 3).

Co-incubation

Co-incubation serves as a straightforward and effective drug-loading technique, which entails incubating cells together with therapeutic agents under defined conditions. It facilitates drug absorption through endocytosis, passive diffusion, or membrane transport proteins. Tao et al. demonstrated that at a drug concentration of 100 µmol/L, macrophages nearly fully internalized the loaded nanoparticles within 8 h. Under physiological conditions, approximately 80% of the drug was released within 4 days, highlighting the potential of macrophages as drug delivery vehicles [53]. Co-incubation or endocytosis is an efficient cell-loading method, but it has low drug loading efficiency and potential cytotoxic effects [52]. Endocytosis, on the other hand, can lead to drug inactivation due to lysosomal escape, requiring enhancement of lysosomal escape [21]. Additionally, endocytosis efficiency is influenced by various factors such as cell type, drug properties, and cell membrane permeability [54]. Nanocarriers can address these issues by linking them to the cell surface via covalent or non-covalent bonds. Despite these challenges, the co-incubation strategy has been successfully applied in anti-infective contexts. Wu et al. demonstrated that M2 macrophages internalized vancomycin-PAMAM nanoparticles (VP@M2) at a loading of 33.75 pg per cell, achieving a minimum inhibitory concentration of 0.469 µg/mL against MRSA—four-fold lower than free vancomycin [55].

Electroporation

As a widely used physical loading technique, electroporation utilizes short, high-voltage pulses to generate nanoscale, transient openings in the cell membrane, which directly facilitates the inward transport of various cargo molecules—from small chemical drugs to large nucleic acids and proteins [56]. This permeability change is reversible, and cell membrane integrity is rapidly restored after removing the electric field, preventing permanent cell damage [57]. This method is commonly used for loading macromolecules and can deliver CRISPR gene-editing plasmids, mRNA, and other substantial molecules into primary cells. Tao et al. found that electroporation markedly enhanced the intracellular uptake of gentamicin sulfate-loaded liposomes (GS-Lipo) while preserving liposomal integrity and cell viability This approach considerably increased intracellular drug accumulation [58], which can apply to various cell types, including primary cells, stem cells, and neurons. However, electroporation has drawbacks, such as potential membrane disruption and structural damage [59], making it crucial to optimize voltage to preserve cell integrity.

Chemical transfection

Chemical transfection is a carrier-mediated technique that employs cationic liposomes or polymers to deliver therapeutic or nucleic acid cargo into cells [60]. The most commonly used method is cationic liposome transfection, where positively charged head groups on liposomes electrostatically bind negatively charged nucleic acids (DNA, RNA, antisense oligonucleotides), forming stable DNA-liposome complexes [61]. These complexes adsorb onto negatively charged cell membranes and enter cells via membrane fusion or endocytosis. PEI, with its high buffering capacity and protonatable amines, facilitates endosomal escape and releases nucleic acids or drugs into the cytoplasm under low pH conditions [62]. PEI-DNA complexes enter cells through endocytosis and release DNA in lysosomes. Then, the DNA is liberated into the cytoplasm through osmotic swelling and lysosomal rupture. Photoreactive gold-coated SPIO core-shell magnetic nanoparticles (SPIO@AuNPs) have been transfected into adipose-derived MSCs, endowing them with MRI contrast ability and disease-targeting homing capability. This strategy improves drug targeting and enhances in vivo visualization [63]. Notably, chemical transfection has been extended to antimicrobial gene delivery. Lentivirus-mediated transfer of the PR-39 antimicrobial peptide gene into rabbit bone mesenchymal stem cells achieved a transfection efficiency of 74.09% and demonstrated 98.43% inhibition against Staphylococcus aureus. PEI has also been used to deliver a human β-defensin 3 expression plasmid into HEK 293 cells, confirming both gene expression and antimicrobial activity [64].

Hypoosmotic swelling method

The hypoosmotic swelling method is the most commonly used drug-loading technique for red blood cells (RBCs) [65]. The principle involves the swelling of cells in a hypoosmotic solution, which generates transitory pores on the cell membrane, facilitating the entry of extracellular medicines into RBCs and subsequently restoring normal RBC morphology in a hyperosmotic solution, thus encapsulating the medications. This technique generally has three phases: swelling, resealing, and annealing [66]. Initially, RBCs are placed in a hypotonic solution, causing them to swell and form transient pores that allow drugs to diffuse inward. Subsequently, transferring the cells to a hypertonic solution closes these pores and reseals the membranes, thereby trapping the therapeutic agents inside. After centrifugation and washing to eliminate unencapsulated drugs, the loaded RBCs are reinfused into the bloodstream, enabling a gradual release of the molecules and maintaining stable, prolonged drug levels in circulation [67, 68]. Magnani et al. have successfully applied hypoosmotic dialysis to deliver a range of therapeutics, including dexamethasone phosphate, phenylalanine ammonia lyase, and ferucarbotran, for managing Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, and phenylketonuria [69]. Beyond the standard encapsulation techniques, ultrasound methods and biological bridge approaches can also be used. Ultrasound temporarily disrupts cell membranes, allowing drug entry, and is often applied for RBC drug loading [70]. The biological bridge method uses specific biomolecules (e.g., proteins) as bridges to facilitate drug entry into cells, enhancing delivery efficiency [71]. Additionally, stem and tumor cells can achieve efficient drug delivery via intracellular encapsulation. These techniques offer a versatile toolkit for intracellular drug loading, thereby advancing the development of cell-based therapeutic strategies.

Cell loading

Acting as a selectively permeable barrier, the cell membrane is a phospholipid bilayer that incorporates both proteins and carbohydrates. Its external face presents an array of structures-such as ligands, receptors, and functional groups-and is characterized by a net negative charge, largely conferred by the polar heads of phospholipids [72]. In contrast, the membrane interior is hydrophobic. This distinct physicochemical gradient facilitates the attachment of therapeutic agents to the cell surface through several mechanisms. These encompass specific receptor-ligand binding, stable covalent bond formation, and non-covalent associations driven by electrostatic or hydrophobic forces [73]. Covalent modifications involve chemical, metabolic, or enzymatic reactions of amino, thiol, and hydroxyl groups, enabling stable binding of drugs to the cell membrane via robust chemical bonds. Non-covalent modifications include electrostatic interactions, biotin-avidin binding, specific receptor-ligand recognition, membrane fusion, fatty acid chain insertion, and supramolecular host-guest interactions, enabling drug conjugation via physical or biological interactions. Genetic engineering involves gene transfection to induce cells to express therapeutic molecules or drug-binding sites directly (Fig. 4).

Covalent modifications

Covalent linkage employs chemical reactions to connect drug molecules with specific membrane functional groups (e.g., amino, carboxyl, or thiol groups), through which stable, irreversible conjugation is achieved [74]. This method offers robust binding affinity, significantly enhancing drug retention on the cell surface, thereby improving the drug delivery system’s structural stability and in vivo residence time. In practice, covalent conjugation usually relies on naturally occurring reactive groups on the cell surface, such as amines and thiols, which selectively react with active groups on the nanocarrier [75]. For instance, researchers have conjugated the antibiotic vancomycin to cell membranes by reacting its carboxyl groups with surface-exposed amine functionalities, forming stable amide linkages. This covalent attachment enables targeted recognition and eradication of Gram-positive bacteria. The modified membrane retained the macrophage membrane’s natural immune evasion and targeting properties. At the same time, vancomycin’s strong affinity for bacterial peptidoglycan enabled specific binding and killing of Gram-positive pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus [76]. Covalent conjugation demonstrates application potential in cell modification and targeted delivery due to its high stability and structural controllability.

Currently, bioorthogonal chemistry enables precise conjugation of drugs or functional molecules to the cell surface by introducing uniquely reactive groups (e.g., azides, N3) onto the membrane or nanocarrier, followed by bioorthogonal reactions such as click chemistry [77]. These reactions typically proceed rapidly, with high selectivity under mild conditions, allowing efficient conjugation while preserving cellular physiological activity and membrane integrity [78]. The core mechanism involves metabolic labeling or chemical modification to incorporate bioorthogonal groups on the cell membrane, which then react rapidly and selectively with complementary groups on drugs or carriers. For example, the cycloaddition between azide (N3) and alkyne groups (e.g., Huisgen 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition) is one of the earliest and most widely used forms of bioorthogonal conjugation, enabling stable drug attachment to cell surfaces. In addition to the N3/alkyne system, commonly used bioorthogonal groups include dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO), trans-cyclooctene, and tetrazine [75]. In the design of anti-infective drug delivery systems, one study incubated mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) with peracetylated N-acetylgalactosamine (Ac4GalNAz), successfully introducing azide groups onto the cell membrane to generate N3-MSCs. These modified cells were then efficiently conjugated with pre-assembled DBCO-PEG-TK-PCL micelles (TK-M/Lu) loaded with the anti-inflammatory agent luteolin. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the micelles were uniformly attached to the N3-MSC surface, forming stable cell–drug complexes with promising directional delivery ability and inflammation-targeting potential [79]. In another study targeting drug-resistant bacteria, researchers introduced a norbornene structure into the peptidoglycan of MRSA (Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) via metabolic labeling. Vancomycin modified with tetrazine (Van-Tz) was then introduced, and via near-infrared activation of Nd3+-doped upconversion nanocrystals, the precursor Van-dHTz was catalytically converted into active Van-Tz, which subsequently underwent an IEDDA reaction with norbornene to achieve selective antibiotic conjugation on the bacterial surface [80].

Non-covalent modifications

Non-covalent interactions represent a primary strategy for drug-membrane association, encompassing hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, hydrophobic effects, and electrostatic interactions [73]. Although these interactions are weaker than covalent bonds, they offer mild reaction conditions and strong reversibility, which help preserve membrane integrity and bioactivity while allowing controlled drug release under specific conditions [81]. Non-covalent coupling offers advantages such as operational simplicity, minimal disruption to cell membranes, and controllable drug release, making it a practical approach for membrane surface functionalization [82]. Through the rational design of intermolecular interactions, steady and reversible drug loading can be accomplished while preserving membrane bioactivity, establishing a basis for efficient and safe biomimetic delivery systems.

Electrostatic interactions

Electrostatic attraction serves as a fundamental non-covalent coupling strategy that exploits the natural adsorption between positively charged molecules and the anionic constituents present on the cell membrane surface [83]. Positively charged nanocarriers can associate with anionic molecules, such as glycosaminoglycans or phospholipids on the membrane, facilitating fast and stable drug attachment [84]. Meanwhile, adjusting the surface charge density of nanoparticles allows for the modulation of electrostatic interaction strength, providing tunability to the delivery mechanism. Although electrostatic adsorption has been extensively utilized in tumor-targeted drug delivery—particularly for the non-covalent immobilization of therapeutics via charged functional groups on cell membranes—its application in anti-infective therapy remains limited [85].

Hydrophobic interactions

Hydrophobic interactions also play a crucial role in non-covalent coupling. Given the hydrophobic nature of the outer membrane layer, nanoparticles or drug molecules exposing hydrophobic groups can spontaneously adsorb onto the membrane. For example, as a result of hydrophobic interactions, more hydrophobic nanoparticles are more readily encapsulated by the membrane [86]. The distinctly hydrophobic nature of the membrane drives the spontaneous association and adsorption of non-polar molecules or domains through strong hydrophobic interactions. In addition, nanocomposite systems based on polyphenol-protein interactions have been developed to enhance drug-loading efficiency on cell membranes. For instance, tannic acid nanoparticles encapsulating perforin and granzyme B can interact electrostatically and hydrophobically with platelet membranes via their polyphenol structure to form stable platelet-drug conjugates (PDC). In comparison to native platelets, engineered PDCs exhibit rougher membrane surfaces, indicating successful drug binding and environment-responsive release. Although this system has been primarily explored for cancer therapy, its design principles offer valuable insights for developing anti-infective strategies [87].

Ligand-receptor binding

Ligand-receptor binding comprises another major category of biologically significant interactions. Ligand-receptor interactions represent a particular and widely occurring biological recognition mechanism that enables precise drug conjugation and targeted delivery at the cell membrane surface [88]. This strategy relies on the non-covalent binding between naturally expressed membrane receptors and synthetically designed ligands, often achieving stable and efficient conjugation without chemical modifications, and offering high selectivity and biocompatibility [89]. Common ligands include monoclonal antibodies, single-chain variable fragments (scFv), nucleic acid aptamers, and functional peptides, which can target specific receptors on cell membranes [90]. As an example, the formyl peptide receptor (FPR) on the surface of neutrophil membranes can bind with high affinity to artificially designed peptide ligands [91]. In one study, a neutrophil-targeting strategy was developed by functionalizing nanoparticles with the peptide cinnamoyl-F-(D)L-F-(D)L-F (CFLFLF), which specifically recognizes the formyl peptide receptor (FPR) on neutrophil surfaces. After intravenous injection, the functionalized nanoparticles achieved efficient binding to circulating neutrophils through this FPR-mediated adhesion mechanism, enabling effective cell-mediated delivery. This neutrophil-hitchhiking mechanism facilitates enhanced nanoparticle delivery to inflammatory sites in the ischemic brain [92]. Similarly, ligand-receptor interaction strategies have been extensively utilized to achieve stable attachment of nanoparticles onto cell surfaces. These systems encompass a variety of binding pairs, including antibody-antigen interactions, the high-affinity binding between avidin (and its analogs such as streptavidin and neutravidin) and biotin, as well as lectin-carbohydrate recognitions within the glycocalyx. Notably, the avidin-biotin interaction exhibits exceptional strength, representing one of nature’s most powerful noncovalent binding mechanisms [75].

Biotin-Avidin system

Biotin, a tiny water-soluble vitamin, is highly biocompatible and chemically modifiable, making it ideal for conjugation with medicines, nanocarriers, or probes. Its corresponding binding proteins-avidin (derived from egg white) and streptavidin (secreted by Streptomyces)-are tetrameric, with each subunit capable of binding one biotin molecule, forming highly stable complexes [93]. The core of the biotin–avidin system lies in the extreme affinity between biotin (vitamin B7) and avidin or streptavidin, with dissociation constants (Kd) as low as 10–15 mol/L, enabling efficient and stable anchoring of drugs or functional molecules onto cell membranes [94]. The binding is nearly irreversible and remains stable even under extreme pH, denaturants, or enzymatic degradation, providing excellent structural stability and sustained drug loading for nanodelivery systems. Compared to avidin, streptavidin lacks glycosylation, resulting in lower nonspecific adsorption and making it more suitable for precise functionalization of cell surfaces in complex biological environments [95].

A significant advantage of this system is its “bridging capability,” whereby a single avidin/streptavidin molecule can simultaneously link multiple biotinylated molecules, enabling synergistic loading of multifunctional modules [96]. For instance, employing avidin as a molecular anchor enables the concurrent assembly of therapeutic agents, targeting ligands, and imaging probes on the cellular surface, thereby conferring biomimetic delivery systems with integrated multifunctionality and augmented therapeutic capabilities. Although explicit reports on applying the biotin-avidin system to immobilize antibiotics on cell membranes in anti-infection are currently lacking, related studies provide a technical basis for this strategy [93]. This approach offers a novel concept for constructing cell biomimetic systems, allowing efficient conjugation of biotinylated antibiotics on their surface to establish targeted and effective antibacterial delivery platforms [97].

Genetic engineering

In cell-based biomimetic nanodelivery systems, besides covalent and non-covalent modifications, genetic engineering is an important strategy to achieve cell-drug conjugation [98]. Unlike previous approaches, which rely on the cell membrane’s existing chemical or physical properties, genetic engineering genetically alters cells to express specific targeting molecules, receptors, or functional proteins, allowing for targeted drug binding or regulated release [99]. Researchers frequently utilize viral or non-viral vectors to introduce target genes into cells. This leads to membrane surface expression of proteins with specific functions, such as drug-recognizing receptors, antibody fragments, or engineered binding peptides [100]. These expressed products can stably conjugate with drugs or their carriers via ligand-receptor recognition or affinity interactions, achieving precise delivery. Moreover, genetic engineering can enhance cellular responsiveness to the infection microenvironment. By introducing regulatory elements such as promoters activated by inflammatory factors, expression of specific functional molecules can be induced “on-demand” in lesion areas, improving drug delivery’s temporal and spatial precision. Gene therapy in vivo faces dual challenges: firstly, the risks of gene silencing and off-target effects that can provoke severe adverse reactions; and secondly, the intrinsic genetic instability of engineered cells coupled with their low nucleic acid uptake efficiency, which collectively elevate the potential for mutations and aberrant gene expression. Further, the selection of gene vectors must ensure low toxicity to guarantee safety. Finally, the complexity and challenges of gene technology limit the applicability of genetic engineering to short-lived cells like neutrophils.

Cell Membrane-Coated Nanoparticles (CM-NPs)

Since the pioneering work by Hu et al. in 2011, which first employed red blood cell (RBC) membranes to coat polymeric nanoparticles, subsequent research has broadened the scope to include membranes from immune cells, platelets, stem cells, and cancer cells, significantly diversifying the functional capabilities of cell membrane-coated nanoparticles (CM-NPs) [101] (Fig. 5). The fabrication of CM-NPs generally comprises three key steps: preparation of the nanoparticle core, harvesting of cell membranes, and fusion of membrane vesicles with the synthetic cores. Each step is critical for ensuring the functionality and structural integrity of the final biomimetic system. Membrane extraction typically employs hypotonic lysis, multiple freeze-thaw cycles, or sonication to disrupt cellular structure, followed by differential centrifugation or density gradient purification to remove cytosolic components and obtain purified membrane vesicles [24]. Coating nanoparticles with cell membranes is primarily driven by physical methods such as membrane extrusion, ultrasonic disruption, and electroporation, which enable effective membrane fusion and vesicle formation [102]. Nanoparticles coated with human platelet membranes (PNP) serve as biomimetic decoys that specifically neutralize toxins produced by Staphylococcus aureus while preserving host cell defensive functions, effectively blocking cytotoxicity and preventing fatal systemic infections [103]. A macrophage membrane-coated allicin-loaded nanocomposite (Allicin@MSN@CM) was developed using cell membrane modification technology. This system leverages the inflammation-homing capability of macrophage membranes to facilitate active accumulation of nanomaterials in vivo, significantly enhancing bacterial eradication and alleviating pulmonary inflammation [104]. Recently, hybrid membrane-coated nanoparticles (WBC-OCM-NPs) combining membranes from different cell types have emerged, further expanding CM-NPs’ application scope. These composite systems harness the complementary benefits of multiple membrane types, thereby broadening their functional repertoire and operational adaptability. For example, Zhang et al. developed a nanocarrier coated with neutrophil and platelet membranes, loading antibiotic carriers (e.g., framework nucleic acid carriers) into a hybrid membrane system. The neutrophil membrane imparts inflammatory chemotaxis to target infected tissues, while the platelet membrane provides adhesion to bacterial toxins and pathogens. This system triggers drug release upon bacterial toxin induction, while nucleic acids exert immunomodulatory effects [105].

EVs delivery

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanosized extracellular vesicles secreted by living cells and delimited by a phospholipid bilayer, facilitating the transfer of proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and other bioactive molecules between cells [106]. They participate in intercellular communication, immune regulation, and pathological processes through mechanisms such as membrane fusion, ligand–receptor binding, and endocytic uptake [107]. Based on their biogenesis routes and physical size, EVs are categorized into four principal types: exosomes, microvesicles, apoptotic bodies, and oncosomes. Of these, exosomes have attracted considerable attention as drug carriers due to their notable stability, high biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and remarkable ability to cross the blood–brain barrier, making them promising candidates for therapeutic applications [27]. EVs inherently possess a complex surface structure composed of membrane proteins and lipids, which protect the internal cargo during circulation and confer particular targeting abilities, allowing selective binding to specific recipient cells to complete delivery [108]. Thereby, compared to synthetic nanocarriers, EVs are closer to the ideal standard of an “endogenous delivery system.“EV delivery techniques are classified as endogenous or exogenous based on their medication loading approaches [109].

Endogenous loading

Endogenous loading is achieved by manipulating the parent cells of EVs to incorporate drugs into EVs during secretion; it typically exhibits low loading efficiency and mainly includes two methods: direct incubation and transduction/transfection. Direct incubation involves incubating the drug with parent cells so that the secreted EVs naturally contain the drug, which is suitable for small-molecule chemotherapeutics with low cytotoxicity. Transduction, also known as transfection, is the process of delivering target genes (such as miRNA, siRNA, or fusion protein-encoding plasmids) into EV-producing cells, allowing for the normal secretion of EVs with therapeutic compounds encased inside vesicles. Transduction/transfection is an efficient strategy for loading large biomolecules such as nucleic acids and proteins (Fig. 6).

Exogenous loading

Compared to endogenous loading, exogenous loading, which directly incorporates drugs into EVs, is more common and generally achieves higher loading efficiency. The primary methods include co-incubation, electroporation, and sonication. Hydrophobic small molecules can passively integrate into the exosomal lipid bilayer via co-incubation, and the drug concentration plays a decisive role in loading efficiency. However, a lack of targeting may result in low EV yield and limited drug loading capacity. Yang et al. utilized exosomes secreted by mouse-derived RAW264.7 macrophages and directly co-incubated them in vitro with the antibiotic linezolid to successfully construct a linezolid-exosome nanoformulation [110]. Electroporation applies a high-intensity electric field to EVs, creating transient pores in their membranes to facilitate drug entry, suitable for nucleic acids, chemical drugs, and others [111]. Several studies have demonstrated that siRNA can be efficiently loaded into exosomes via electroporation and delivered to specific target organs. Banizs et al. loaded luciferase siRNA into endothelial cell-derived exosomes via electroporation (exosome/siRNA Luc complex) to intervene in primary endothelial cells transfected with the luciferase gene. The observed approximately 40% reduction in luciferase expression within endothelial cells demonstrated the efficacy of exosome-mediated siRNA delivery, validating both the successful loading of siRNA via electroporation and its functional delivery to target cells [112]. The application of ultrasonic energy through sonication disrupts the bilayer architecture of EVs, thereby facilitating the integration of therapeutic compounds into these natural carriers. This acoustic method further enables spatiotemporally controlled drug delivery and release through mechanisms including ultrasound-triggered vesicle aggregation, radiation-mediated guidance, and transient membrane permeabilization. Haney et al. loaded the antioxidant enzyme catalase into exosomes via sonication by mixing catalase with exosomes and applying six cycles of sonication with 2-minute cooling on ice between cycles using a Qsonica device, demonstrating potential in Parkinson’s disease therapy [113]. Other loading strategies include incubation at room temperature, saponin permeabilization, freeze-thaw cycles, and extrusion [106]. Fuhrmann et al. employed saponin permeabilization to load porphyrin into exosomes, significantly enhancing cellular uptake and phototoxicity of porphyrin in vitro [114]. Loading efficiencies vary among methods; researchers typically select the optimal loading strategy based on the drug’s physicochemical properties, target tissue requirements, and delivery efficiency. Despite EVs’ unique advantages as drug delivery platforms, clinical application still faces challenges such as large-scale extraction, purification, standardization, uncertain loading efficiency, and difficulty controlling functional consistency. Current research attempts to engineer EVs by surface conjugating specific ligands and artificial regulation of membrane protein expression to improve targeting and drug loading capacity.

Among the strategies discussed above, co-incubation and hypoosmotic swelling are particularly suitable for anti-infective applications due to their simplicity and preservation of cell viability, while electroporation and chemical transfection offer higher loading efficiency for nucleic acid-based antimicrobial agents such as siRNA and antimicrobial peptide-encoding plasmids. For cell surface loading, covalent modifications enable precise attachment of antibiotics to bacterial surfaces for targeted killing. Vancomycin-modified mesoporous silica nanoparticles achieved selective recognition and eradication of gram-positive bacteria through hydrogen bonding interactions [76], and bioorthogonal chemistry has been used to conjugate vancomycin to drug-resistant bacteria via near-infrared light activation, reducing the MIC by 6–7 fold [80]. Non-covalent approaches (e.g., electrostatic interactions, ligand-receptor binding) offer reversibility and mild conditions that preserve cell function [87, 92]. The choice of loading strategy should be guided by the specific pathogen, the type of therapeutic agent, the desired release profile, and the target cell type.

Applications of Cell-Based Biomimetic Drug Delivery Systems in Anti-Infective Therapy

Red blood cells

Red blood cells (RBCs) are the most numerous cells in blood and serve as oxygen carriers [115]. Their reversible deformability, high drug-loading capacity, and well-established handling methods make them ideal drug delivery platforms [116]. While the general principles of CDDS have been discussed in Sect. "Advantages of CDDS in bacterial infections", erythrocytes possess several distinctive physical characteristics that merit specific attention. Their high deformability facilitates passage through the microcirculation, prolonging circulation time and enhancing drug delivery efficiency; their long lifespan (up to 120 days) endows them with potential as long-term drug reservoirs; the absence of a nucleus and DNA eliminates tumorigenic risks, while the membrane structure provides flexibility and stability. As noted in Sect. "Immune evasion for prolonged circulation in chronic infections", CD47 expression on RBC membranes enables immune evasion [49, 50, 117].

The expression of complement receptors and pattern recognition receptors (such as TLR9) on the RBC membrane endows them with certain immune functions, enabling binding to antigen-antibody complexes and their transport to the reticuloendothelial system (RES) organs for clearance, thereby participating in immune modulation during infection [118]. Also, RBCs can retain partial physiological functions after drug loading via membrane encapsulation or surface modification, providing a biological basis for precise delivery. Infectious lesions are frequently characterized by disrupted capillary structures, increased tissue permeability, and local hypoxia, which create an ideal microenvironment for RBC-based nanodrug delivery systems [119]. On the one hand, vascular barrier disruption facilitates nanoparticle penetration and retention at the lesion site; on the other hand, the oxygen-carrying capacity of RBCs helps alleviate hypoxia in the lesion area, thereby enhancing local therapeutic efficacy. Researchers have further enhanced oxygen delivery under hypoxic conditions by modifying hemoglobin structures or engineering RBCs, which may improve the overall effectiveness of anti-infective therapies [120].

Based on these biological features, RBC-DDS have demonstrated broad application prospects in various infectious disease models, particularly in pulmonary and central nervous system infections. One study developed an RBC-MPSS-CSNPs system by adsorbing chitosan nanoparticles onto the RBC surface, achieving targeted delivery to lung tissues and effectively alleviating pulmonary edema and inflammation [121]. Additionally, “nano-RBCs” formed by encapsulating nanoparticles can mimic senescent RBCs, triggering macrophage phagocytosis and promoting drug accumulation at inflammatory sites, offering new strategies for treating complex infections, including meningitis and sepsis.

In recent years, relevant studies have been roughly classified into three types: (1) Antibiotic delivery: In a murine model of Klebsiella pneumoniae-induced sepsis, Liu et al. developed a bioinspired delivery system using red blood cell membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles to encapsulate ciprofloxacin, demonstrating potent therapeutic efficacy against Klebsiella pneumoniae-induced sepsis [122], These findings await validation in larger animal models and ultimately in human trials; Wu et al. designed RBC-biomimetic nanoparticles loaded with tedizolid phosphate for treating Gram-positive bacterial infections [123]; (2) Toxin neutralization and immune modulation: Escajadillo, Koo, and others developed a defensive strategy that focuses on “detoxification” rather than bactericidal activity by exploiting the natural affinity of RBC membranes for bacterial exotoxins [124–126]; Whether this detoxification strategy confers long-term protection in chronic or recurrent infections remains to be determined. (3) Stimuli-responsive release and multimodal therapies: For example, Zhuge et al. developed a toxin-responsive bubble platform enabling spatiotemporally precise drug release in photodynamic therapy (PDT) [127, 128]. Although these strategies vary in drug types, targeting mechanisms, and functional implementations, they share several common advantages: First, the use of RBC membranes enhances system stability and prolongs circulation time in the bloodstream; Second, they improve the targeted accumulation of drugs at infection sites; Lastly, surface functionalization confers responsiveness to specific pathogens or inflammatory signals. For instance, He et al. created an erythroliposome system by combining synthetic lipids with RBC membranes, which significantly improved toxin adsorption and clearance [129]; Chen et al. emphasized the recyclability and environmental friendliness of carrier materials, offering new perspectives for developing reusable antimicrobial systems [130]. It should be noted, however, that the study by Liu et al. [110] focused on an acute sepsis model with planktonic bacteria and did not specifically address biofilm-associated infections. Evidence for the anti-biofilm efficacy of RBC-based systems remains limited, particularly for chronic or polymicrobial biofilm contexts.

Despite their promising outlook, RBC-DDS still faces several challenges. For instance, prolonged circulation in complex mechanical environments may damage RBC membrane integrity and ATP metabolism, leading to energy depletion, cytoskeletal disruption, and eventual hemolysis. Additionally, drug loading or membrane modification processes may result in the loss of key immune-regulatory molecules such as CD47, compromising immune evasion and increasing the risk of immune recognition and clearance. Therefore, maintaining the native functions of RBCs while achieving efficient drug loading remains a significant challenge in the design of future RBC-DDS.

Monocytes and macrophages

Monocytes are critical immune cells in the blood, accounting for approximately 2% of the white blood cell population. They subsequently migrate into tissues and differentiate into macrophages or dendritic cells, which are widely distributed in the liver (Kupffer cells), lungs (alveolar macrophages), spleen, lymph nodes, and other barrier tissues. Under homeostatic conditions, macrophages participate in apoptotic cell clearance, tissue repair, and immune surveillance. During infection or inflammation, the monocyte-macrophage system is rapidly activated to perform multiple functions, including pathogen phagocytosis, cytokine release, and antigen presentation [131].

Monocytes and macrophages possess excellent biocompatibility and engineering potential, and naturally participate in pathogen clearance and tissue chemotaxis, making them key components in the development of CDDS. For instance, during bacterial infections, lesions release chemokines such as MCP-1 (CCL2) and IL-8, which bind to receptors like CCR2 to induce directed migration of monocytes to the infected sites [132, 133]. Furthermore, monocytes and macrophages have outstanding endocytic and phagocytic capacities, allowing for the transport of different medicines, nanoparticles, and nucleic acids [134]. Their intracellular multivesicular body (MVB) systems and lysosomal pathways facilitate controlled drug release [135]. Various drug delivery platforms based on macrophages or monocytes have been developed for anti-infective therapy [136, 137]. M1/M2 phenotype modulation strategies enable immune activation during early infection and immunosuppression during later stages, thereby alleviating tissue damage caused by drug-resistant bacteria [138].

Macrophage membranes exhibit significant immunomodulatory capabilities, characterized by the surface expression of several receptors, including CD11b, CD14, TLRs, and FcRs. This facilitates the identification of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and activation of inflammatory cascades. The use of macrophage membranes as biomimetic coatings has been applied in various systems. Li et al. created a system using macrophage membranes to encapsulate curcumin-loaded Platycodon polysaccharide nanoparticles(PNPs), which effectively targeted pneumonia sites, enhancing pulmonary accumulation and reducing inflammatory cytokine release, indicating promising therapeutic potential [139]. Macrophages can also mediate intercellular drug transport via EVs [139]. In certain studies, engineered macrophages have been induced to secrete drug-loaded exosomes, serving as natural carriers to deliver therapeutics to sites of infection or inflammation. This strategy avoids the potential immunogenic risks associated with live cells while preserving their targeting capabilities. In a recent study, exosomes derived from M2-type macrophages (M2-Exos) were loaded with meropenem (MEM) via electroporation to construct a MEM@Exos nanosystem, which employed CD11b/CD18 integrins on the M2-Exos surface to bind ICAM-1 on inflamed endothelial cells, achieving homotypic targeting. The study explicitly identified ‘pathogens nestling in phagocytic cells and biofilms’ as a key therapeutic challenge in pneumonia. In a murine acute lung injury (ALI) model, pulmonary accumulation of MEM@Exos was fourfold higher than free antibiotics, with retention exceeding 24 hours. In an in vitro intracellular infection model, this system penetrated macrophage membranes to eradicate intracellular drug-resistant bacteria (e.g., MRSA), achieving a dead-to-live intracellular bacterial ratio exceeding 8:1 within 15 hours. This study was the first to harness the homotypic targeting of M2-Exos to address intracellular bacterial infections, offering antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects [140]. However, evidence for their efficacy against surface-associated biofilms or polymicrobial infections remains limited. These promising preclinical results support further evaluation in larger animal models and, if successful, eventual clinical translation.

Monocyte membrane-coated nanoparticles (Me-ANPs) have effectively treated intracellular and complex infection models. For instance, in the case of *Staphylococcus aureus* infection, Shi et al. developed a monocyte-based system using aspirin-loaded gold nanocages (Aspirin@GNC), which leveraged the innate chemotactic and phagocytic properties of monocytes for active targeting of infection sites. In this system, the living cell carriers migrated to osteomyelitis lesions and utilized surface integrins (e.g., CD11b/CD18) to mediate adhesion to vascular endothelial cells, thereby enhancing local drug accumulation [141]. The encapsulated aspirin achieved spatiotemporally controlled release: during the acute phase of infection, it inhibited COX-2 to reduce proinflammatory cytokine expression (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α); At later stages, it activated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby stimulating osteoblast differentiation and culminating in a 2.3-fold enhancement in bone regeneration efficiency. In a murine osteomyelitis model, this system achieved a 95% bacterial clearance rate and 80% bone defect repair rate, validating the feasibility of live cell-based carriers in programmed anti-infective therapy. In a separate study, Li et al. developed a self-assembled antibacterial nanoparticle composed of triclosan and ciprofloxacin, which was subsequently cloaked with membranes derived from murine macrophages or human monocytes to form membrane-coated antimicrobial nanoparticles (Me-ANPs). These Me-ANPs retained Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and negatively charged surface properties, facilitating preferential uptake by Staphylococcus aureus-infected macrophages. In infection models, Me-ANPs demonstrated superior antibacterial efficacy against intracellular S. aureus, achieving markedly enhanced bacterial clearance compared to both non-coated nanoparticles and free ciprofloxacin. Furthermore, in a murine peritoneal dissemination model, Me-ANPs demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy [142]. Notably, this study represents an early proof-of-concept; further evaluation in clinically relevant models (e.g., chronic or relapsing infections) is warranted.

In recent years, macrophage-based drug delivery systems (Mφ-DDS) have predominantly advanced through three strategic approaches: (1) whole-cell-based delivery, (2) membrane-camouflaged nanoparticles, and (3) macrophage-mimicking nanoplatforms. Each approach presents distinct advantages in combating infectious diseases. For whole-cell-based delivery, Liu et al. engineered macrophages enriched with lipid droplets and labeled with aggregation-induced emission (AIE) fluorophores to enable visual tracking and effective treatment of bacterial sepsis [143]. This study underscores the potential of integrating cell metabolism modulation and real-time imaging for infection-responsive therapy [144]. Conversely, membrane-camouflaged techniques prioritize improved immune evasion and inflammation-targeting capacities. As an instance, Lin et al. developed engineered macrophage membrane-derived “nanodecoys” capable of modulating the microenvironment of periodontitis [145]. Cao et al. constructed macrophage membrane-coated non-viral gene vectors to overcome therapeutic resistance in MDR sepsis [146] (Fig. 7).

Wang and Li further demonstrated that preconditioning or pathogen-responsive modification of macrophage membranes significantly improved the targeting and delivery efficiency of membrane-coated nanoplatforms, highlighting the critical influence of membrane source and state [36]. Zeng et al. extended this approach to chronic infectious wounds, validating the dual role of membrane-based systems in regulating inflammation and tissue repair in diabetic ulcers [147]. The third strategy focuses on mimicking or even surpassing native macrophage functions. Fu et al. designed Fe3O4@PLGA biomimetic particles capable of pathogen-responsive catalytic activation, enabling selective eradication of MDR bacteria [148]. Liu et al. applied macrophage membrane-coated silicon nanowires for bloodstream clearance of ESKAPE pathogens [149]. Furthermore, Thamphiwatana and Wang developed nanoplatforms with cytokine-adsorbing or homotypic-recognition properties, achieving targeted intervention in cytokine storms and intracellular infections, respectively [140, 150]. Unlike earlier approaches, these systems do not rely on natural cells or membranes, but rather leverage functional mimicry to improve stability, programmability, and therapeutic precision.

Nevertheless, drug delivery platforms based on monocytes and macrophages face several limitations. Firstly, excessively activated macrophages can release substantial quantities of pro-inflammatory cytokines—including TNF-α and IL-1β—that may aggravate inflammatory tissue damage [25]. Secondly, macrophages tend to polarize toward the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype in chronic infections or tumor microenvironments, potentially compromising therapeutic efficacy. High drug loading may also impair macrophage functions, such as reduced phagocytic capacity and increased apoptosis rates. Consequently, in system design, it is crucial to balance drug loading with preservation of cellular functionality, to prevent delivery cells themselves from becoming sources of inflammation.

Neutrophils

Neutrophils, the dominant granulocyte population in the innate immune system, originate from granulocyte-monocyte progenitors (GMPs) in the bone marrow. Under physiological conditions, they exhibit a transient circulatory lifespan, typically ranging from 6 to 8 h [26]. However, under infectious or inflammatory stimulation, their lifespan becomes significantly extended, facilitating rapid accumulation at infection sites. There, they execute potent antimicrobial functions through phagocytosis, degranulation, and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, collectively establishing them as a primary defense barrier against bacterial and fungal pathogens. However, exhibit several unique characteristics beyond the general features, including their short half-life, rapid response, and NETosis mechanism. In recent years, owing to their strong chemotactic ability, phagocytic activity, and inflammation-homing properties, neutrophils have been employed in the design of cell-mediated and biomimetic nanodrug delivery systems, particularly for combating MDR bacterial infections and complex inflammatory microenvironments.

Neutrophils can interact with upregulated adhesion molecules, including ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 in the inflamed microenvironment via surface integrins like CD11b/CD18, facilitating transendothelial migration across inflamed vasculature and forming the biological basis for “Trojan horse” drug delivery strategies [151]. In this strategy, drugs are encapsulated in neutrophils via endocytosis, surface adsorption, or extracellular vesicle loading, and are delivered to the target lesion through neutrophils’ intrinsic migratory capacity. For instance, a neutrophil-based delivery platform engineered by Tan et al. displayed potent targeting specificity and anti-inflammatory efficacy in a septic model, thereby significantly suppressing the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and TNF-α [152].

The formation of NETs also offers a novel route for drug delivery. NETs, comprised of DNA and diverse antimicrobial proteins, can ensnare bacteria at infection sites. Using an in vitro MRSE biofilm model, Huang et al. exploited the oxidative responsiveness of NETs to construct a self-assembling nanofiber network in the infectious microenvironment, forming a biomimetic supramolecular structure that exhibited threefold greater antibacterial efficacy against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis (MRSE) compared to vancomycin. The translational potential of this supramolecular approach in vivo remains to be established. Also, elastase and myeloperoxidase (MPO) released during NET formation can be enzyme-responsive elements to enhance further drug delivery systems’ targeting specificity and release efficiency [153]. However, evidence for this strategy in polymicrobial biofilm contexts or chronic infection settings remains to be established.

Neutrophils have also been utilized as active transport platforms. For instance, in a rat model of bacterial meningitis, Zhou et al. developed a neutrophil-driven biomimetic nanozyme system capable of actively penetrating the blood-brain barrier, achieving precise accumulation at inflammatory sites in a bacterial meningitis model, significantly increasing intracerebral antibiotic concentrations and improving behavioral scores [154] (Fig. 8). While these results are encouraging, the translational potential of this system remains to be assessed in higher-order animal models and non-human primates before clinical consideration.

Inspired by the diverse biological roles of neutrophils, a spectrum of innovative drug delivery platforms has been engineered. In this study, Zhou et al. and Wang et al. constructed responsive delivery systems using live neutrophils, achieving precise drug release and enhanced immune responses in models of meningitis and drug-resistant bacterial infections [154, 155]. Lu et al. and Yu et al. employed neutrophil membrane camouflaging strategies to leverage their intrinsic inflammation-targeting and self-limiting release mechanisms, significantly enhancing drug accumulation in pulmonary infections and chronic inflammatory lesions [156, 157]. Zhou et al. further designed DNA nanorobots capable of modulating neutrophil recruitment and phenotype transition, enabling systemic immune intervention in sepsis treatment [158]. Zhang et al. developed “super neutrophils” and functional microrobots that mimic bactericidal mechanisms, achieving precise localization and controlled drug release in models of infection and cancer [51, 159]. From a materials strategy perspective, Peng et al. and Gao et al. co-delivered lipid nanoparticles and Resolvin D1 to activate or modulate neutrophil function, synergistically enhancing antibacterial and immunoregulatory efficacy [160, 161] (Fig. 9).

In comparison to other drug delivery vectors like red blood cells or macrophages, neutrophils exhibit enhanced drug delivery efficacy during the acute phase of infection, rendering them especially appropriate for rapid-onset antibacterial and anti-inflammatory treatments. However, their short lifespan and high reactivity present significant challenges: premature drug release may occur during activation before reaching the target site, compromising delivery efficiency; furthermore, reactive oxygen species and lysosomal enzymes released during degranulation may affect carrier stability and drug bioactivity. In addition, excessive neutrophil recruitment in chronic or persistent inflammation may lead to tissue damage or even trigger autoimmune responses. That means drug-loading strategies for neutrophil-based delivery systems require further optimization.

Platelets and others

Platelets are anucleate cellular elements that originate from the direct fragmentation of mature megakaryocytes in the bone marrow. Under physiological conditions, platelet counts in human circulation range from approximately 1.5 × 1011 to 4 × 1011/L, with a lifespan of about 7 to 10 days, after which they are primarily cleared by the spleen and liver [162]. Beyond their primary function in hemostasis and thrombosis, platelets actively engage in immunological and inflammatory responses, serving a vital role in host defense against infectious diseases.During infection, platelets can recognize inflammatory cells such as neutrophils and monocytes via surface molecules like P-selectin, forming aggregates that modulate the inflammatory microenvironment, recruit immune cells, and mediate pathogen clearance [163]. Moreover, platelets release various bioactive factors, including chemokines and proinflammatory cytokines, that enhance local immune responses at infection sites by activating innate and adaptive immune systems, thereby augmenting anti-infective efficacy [164].

During infection site formation, vascular endothelial damage induces platelet activation, aggregation, and degranulation, a feature that can be exploited as a precise trigger for controlled drug release [165]. In biomimetic nanodrug delivery systems, platelet membrane coating endows nanoparticles with excellent immune evasion capabilities and enables targeted delivery to pathogen-associated inflammatory sites. This biomimetic approach avoids the accelerated blood clearance phenomenon sometimes observed with PEGylated nanoparticles. Engineered platelets can be functionally modified on their surface to enhance binding to specific pathogens or inflammatory molecules, facilitating targeted delivery of anti-infective agents. Notably, platelet-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs), the most abundant exosomes in the bloodstream, retain functional properties of their parental cells [166]. Studies have confirmed that PEVs can preferentially accumulate at inflammatory foci and thereby potentiate localized anti-infective efficacy through targeted delivery of a broad spectrum of therapeutics including antibiotics, nucleic acids, and immunomodulators [167].

Platelet-based drug delivery strategies can be further integrated with innovative trigger systems to enhance their controlled release capabilities. For instance, leveraging the dense granule release upon platelet activation, researchers have developed multilayer polyelectrolyte capsule systems that enable rapid release of bioactive agents at infection sites, thereby increasing local drug concentrations. Hansen et al. developed an intelligent drug delivery system integrating platelets with multilayer polyelectrolyte capsules. Upon activation of platelets at sites of inflammation or tissue injury, dense granule release and contraction trigger the rupture of the attached capsules, leading to the rapid release of encapsulated bioactive agents (e.g., factor VIII). This mechanism significantly enhances local drug concentrations and therapeutic efficacy [168]. Additionally, hybrid delivery systems combining platelets with immune or stem cells can be constructed to exploit the natural homing ability of platelets to inflamed tissues, enabling precise drug delivery and sustained therapeutic effects at infection sites.

In recent years, platelet membranes (PMs) have become a research hotspot for anti-infective therapy due to their inflammation-targeting and immune-evasive properties. Wei et al. have shown that activated PM vesicles can efficiently accumulate in inflamed endothelial regions, enabling targeted drug release [169]. Kim et al. engineered PLGA nanoparticles coated with platelet membranes that neutralize Staphylococcus aureus toxins, significantly improving host survival and exhibiting strong potential for antitoxin therapy [105]. Multiple studies have further expanded the application formats of platelet membranes. Hu et al. co-utilized red blood cell and platelet membranes to coat Fe3O4 and cinnamaldehyde nanoparticles, enabling ferroptosis-based treatment of MRSA-induced pneumonia [170]. Through engineering a platelet-membrane-cloaked bimetallic MOF nanozyme, Shi et al. created an antibacterial platform exhibiting broad-spectrum activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens [171]. Li et al. developed an inhalable platelet membrane-PLGA microrobot for noninvasive pulmonary infection treatment, demonstrating effective lung deposition [172]. Huang et al. wrapped Ag-MOF nanostructures with platelet membranes, markedly enhancing therapeutic efficacy against MRSA infections [173]. Jin et al. designed a hybrid system of platelet membranes and bacteriophages, in which cyclic elongation peptide modification prolonged circulation time and enhanced antibacterial efficacy [174]. Earlier, Hu et al. demonstrated that platelet membrane-coated PNPs could effectively deliver vancomycin for systemic infection treatment [175].

Alternatively, a range of cell membranes—such as those isolated from dendritic cells, red blood cells, and gastric epithelial cells—along with bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) (Table 2), have been exploited to improve targeting precision and antibacterial performance in drug delivery systems (Fig. 10). For instance, Angsantikul et al. designed biomimetic nanoparticles cloaked with gastric epithelial cell membranes to achieve precision targeting and treatment of Helicobacter pylori infections. In another study, Wu et al. employed Escherichia coli-derived OMVs to deliver rifampicin for peritonitis therapy; both strategies leverage the homologous targeting affinity between membrane materials and the corresponding pathogens [176, 177]. Among these membrane camouflage approaches, platelet membranes stand out as particularly suitable for treating pulmonary and systemic infectious diseases due to their intrinsic vascular adhesion and inflammation‐homing characteristics.

Promising Cell Types for Future Development in Anti-Infective Drug Delivery

In addition to immune cells already employed in anti-infective therapy - such as red blood cells, monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils—numerous other cell types have not yet been applied in this field, but have demonstrated significant drug delivery potential in different disease contexts. Beyond the general CDDS advantages, the following cell types offer unique characteristics that merit attention for future anti-infective applications. The following are representative cell types and their potential directions for expansion.

T Lymphocytes

After originating from bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells, T Lymphocytes (T cells) migrate to the thymus where they undergo differentiation and maturation. These cells are broadly classified by surface marker expression and function into three main lineages: CD4+ helper T cells (Th), CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CTLs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs). They have a broad role in pathogen clearance and immune response regulation [178]. In human peripheral blood, T cells account for approximately 70%–80% of total lymphocytes and possess high antigen specificity and immune memory, providing a theoretical foundation for their use in targeted drug delivery systems [179].

T cells naturally possess targeted migratory capabilities. Their expression of chemokine receptors (e.g., CXCR3, CCR5) and integrins (e.g., LFA-1, VLA-4) enables them to transmigrate across vascular endothelium and actively home to inflamed tissues, infection sites, and the tumor microenvironment [180]. They can accurately identify pathogen-associated antigen-MHC complexes in infectious diseases, making them ideal drug carriers endowed with “biological navigation” capabilities [179]. In viral encephalitis or bacterial pneumonia models, activated T cells can cross the blood-brain or pulmonary epithelial barrier to achieve targeted delivery to deep-seated tissues [181].

T cells also possess the ability to coordinate and regulate immune responses. CD4⁺ T cells release cytokines including IL-2 and IFN-γ to orchestrate immune activation of macrophages and B cells, whereas CD8⁺ T cells mediate viral clearance through the specific recognition and lysis of infected host cells [182]. These functions can synergize with drugs in T cell-based drug delivery systems (T cell-DDS) to achieve dual therapeutic effects of “drug delivery plus immunomodulation.” According to recent literature, preliminary explorations have been conducted in the anti-infective field using T cells or their membrane components to construct biomimetic nanodelivery systems. The primary strategy involves extracting T cell membranes to coat nanoparticles or engineered bacteria, thereby mimicking T cell biological behavior to enhance targeting specificity and immune evasion [183]. It is important to note, however, that these T cell-based approaches remain largely at the proof-of-concept stage, with most studies conducted in vitro or in cancer models rather than in infectious disease settings.

Natural Killer Cells

Natural killer (NK) cells are crucial innate immune effector lymphocytes comprising 5–15% of blood lymphocytes. Derived from bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells, they mature in the medullary compartment before migrating to peripheral tissues for ongoing immune monitoring [184]. NK cells differ fundamentally from T cells in their innate capacity to directly detect and destroy infected or cancerous targets without antigen priming; this capability stems from coordinated signaling between activating receptors like NKG2D and NKp46, and inhibitory receptors such as KIR and NKG2A [185].

NK cells possess high biocompatibility and innate recognition capabilities, making them biologically safe and ideal candidates for constructing cell-mimetic drug delivery systems. Their intrinsic tissue migratory capacity, cytotoxicity, and immunoregulatory functions confer distinct advantages in targeting infectious foci or the tumor microenvironment [183]. NK cells can traverse vascular endothelium to reach lesion sites, especially during early inflammation or viral infection stages, where they rapidly accumulate in response to chemokines such as CXCL10 and CCL5 [186]. Moreover, surface molecules such as LFA-1 and CXCR3 endow NK cells with excellent transendothelial migration and targeting capabilities [187].

In live cell-based delivery strategies, anti-infective agents, small RNAs, or nanoparticles can be encapsulated within NK cells to achieve a dual effect of “delivery plus cytotoxicity.” NK membrane-coated nanoparticles (NK-MNPs), which utilize NK cell membranes as outer shells encapsulating nanocarriers, integrate the advantages of nanodrug delivery with the natural recognition properties of cell membranes. Membrane proteins such as NKG2D and DNAM-1 retained on NK membranes can recognize stress-induced molecules like MICA/B and PVR, enabling active recognition of infected cells or inflammatory regions [188]. Notably, the perforin and granzyme system naturally expressed by NK cells can contribute to pathogen clearance during the later stages of drug delivery, providing synergistic therapeutic effects. Additionally, NK cells are crucial in immune regulation [189].

During the early phase of infection, NK cells can secrete cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α to stimulate dendritic cells and macrophages, thereby enhancing anti-infective responses [190]. NK cells also contribute to immune homeostasis by eliminating overactivated immune cells, such as T cells or dendritic cells lacking sufficient MHC-I expression [191]. By means of surface engineering modifications, NK-DDS can operate as “immune bridges,” delivering regulatory factors in hyperinflammatory environments to achieve immune balance. Retained membrane cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, TNF-α) can facilitate active migration to inflammatory sites and modulate immune cell behavior, influencing infection progression.

Despite their promising potential, NK-DDS still face several challenges. First, viable NK cells’ in vitro expansion efficiency is relatively low, and clinical-scale expansion depends on IL-2/IL-15 systems, making the process complex and costly [192]. Second, the membrane extraction process must preserve key functional proteins; otherwise, targeting capabilities may be compromised. Certain drugs or modification processes may also impair NK cell functions, including their penetration, recognition, and effector capabilities. Current research efforts are focused on enhancing the stability and functionality of NK cell membrane-based nanosystems. Recent advances have demonstrated that membrane vesicles prepared via microfluidic technology exhibit significantly improved particle size uniformity (PDI < 0.1) and greater than 90% membrane protein retention, outperforming traditional extrusion methods [193]. In addition, CRISPR gene editing can precisely knock out complement-activating membrane proteins (e.g., C3b receptor), significantly prolonging nanoparticle circulation time in blood to up to 24 h [194]. With the advent of cryo-electron microscopy and single-cell sequencing technologies, the membrane proteomic characteristics of distinct NK cell subpopulations can be deeply profiled, offering theoretical and data support for designing precision biomimetic systems.

Stem cells

Stem cells possess two defining properties: the ability to self-renew and the potential to differentiate into multiple cell types. According to their developmental potential, they are classified as totipotent, pluripotent, or adult stem cells [195]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) occupy a prominent role in drug delivery systems among adult stem cells, owing to their broad tissue availability, excellent biocompatibility, minimal immunogenicity, and innate homing capacity. Their translational potential is further supported by accessibility from multiple sources—including bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and dental pulp. Furthermore, MSCs exhibit inherent chemotactic mobility, enabling targeted migration toward pathological lesions in response to chemotactic signals (e.g., SDF-1, MCP-1, and IL-8) released from tumor, injury, or infection sites [196]. These properties establish MSCs as ideal candidates for targeted drug delivery in inflammation-associated infections. Furthermore, MSCs surface-express key adhesion molecules (e.g., VLA-4, LFA-1) and integrins (e.g., α4β1, α5β1), which collectively enhance their binding to activated vascular endothelium and facilitate efficient transendothelial migration toward inflammatory foci [197].

Stem cell-based drug delivery platforms offer multiple advantages. Primarily, their elevated drug-loading capacity and amenability to engineering allow them to carry a wide range of therapeutics, including small-molecule antibiotics, anti-inflammatory drugs, proteins, peptides, siRNAs, and CRISPR components. Drugs can be loaded into or onto stem cells via endocytosis, spontaneous adsorption, covalent modification, or genetic engineering. Secondly, stem cells have intrinsic anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory characteristics; they release immunosuppressive factors like as IL-10, TGF-β, and PGE2, which help mitigate infection-induced cytokine storms and improve inflammation regulation [198]. The therapeutic role of stem cells in infection-related diseases extends beyond functioning as drug carriers. Their immunomodulatory functions have shown therapeutic effects in various infection models. Studies indicate that MSCs exert significant anti-inflammatory effects in animal models of acute lung injury (ALI) or sepsis. The underlying mechanism involves a dual action: suppression of the NF-κB signaling pathway coupled with activation of protective antioxidant pathways, notably HO-1/NRF2 [199]. Moreover, exosomes released by stem cells can directly act on macrophages, inducing their polarization to an M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby promoting tissue repair and restoration of immune homeostasis [200]. MSCs possess inherent immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory capabilities, making them promising candidates for treating sepsis, chronic infections, or post-infection tissue repair. Engineered stem cells can express antimicrobial peptides or be loaded with antibiotics, functioning as a “Trojan horse” to deliver therapeutics to infectious sites - particularly effective against biofilm-associated or drug-resistant bacterial infections [201].

Others

In addition to the mainstream cell types mentioned above, several unconventional cellular carriers have recently demonstrated remarkable potential in anti-infective drug delivery. Bacteria-derived carriers, such as attenuated Salmonella, have emerged as research hotspots due to their unique biological characteristics. These carriers naturally possess strong chemotaxis and membrane-penetrating capabilities. They can be genetically engineered into multifunctional “living antibiotic factories,” such as those expressing antimicrobial peptides (e.g., defensins), carrying CRISPR-Cas9 systems for targeted knockout of resistance genes, continuously secreting antibacterial peptides or enzymes at infection sites, or serving as nanoparticle drug delivery platforms for localized treatment of drug-resistant infections (e.g., VRE, CRE) or chronic pulmonary infections [202–204]. Certain engineered bacterial strains can recognize specific signaling molecules in the infected microenvironment (e.g., quorum-sensing molecules), enabling intelligent drug release regulation [205].

Advances in synthetic biology have driven artificial cellular carriers’ structural design and functional expansion. Hybrid carriers that combine natural cell membrane components with functional synthetic materials exemplify the design philosophy of “utilizing nature’s advantages to offset synthetic deficiencies [206].” Improvements in microfluidic technology have increased the accuracy in producing such carriers. More innovatively, some research teams have utilized DNA origami to construct programmable artificial cell mimics, with spatially organized surface targeting ligands for precise recognition and binding of multivalent pathogens [207]. With the ongoing advancement of technology, cell-based carriers are expected to be increasingly applied in anti-infective therapy, offering considerable potential for future clinical applications. In addition to the specific applications discussed above, CDDS may also influence the evolution of antimicrobial resistance through several distinct mechanisms.

Implications of CDDS for antimicrobial resistance evolution

Beyond their direct antibacterial efficacy, CDDS may influence the evolution of antimicrobial resistance through several mechanisms that differ fundamentally from conventional antibiotics. Conventional antibiotic therapy often involves intermittent dosing, creating fluctuating drug concentrations that expose bacteria to sub-inhibitory levels, a well-established driver of resistance selection. CDDS, particularly erythrocyte-based and membrane-coated nanoparticle systems, enable sustained drug release and prolonged circulation, maintaining therapeutic concentrations at infection sites while avoiding this sub-inhibitory window [30, 49]. In addition, CDDS can target intracellular bacterial reservoirs that conventional antibiotics cannot reach. Intracellular pathogens such as Salmonella and Mycobacterium tuberculosis persist within host cells, serving as hidden reservoirs for relapse and resistance. Macrophage-derived exosomes and membrane-coated systems can deliver antibiotics directly into these sanctuaries [140, 142]. Furthermore, CDDS that employ active motility (e.g., neutrophils) or enhanced deformability (e.g., RBCs) penetrate biofilms more effectively than conventional nanoparticles, potentially targeting the tolerant persister-like populations that survive intermittent dosing [50, 51].

Another distinct advantage of some CDDS platforms is their non-bactericidal mechanism of action. Unlike conventional antibiotics that kill bacteria directly—thereby imposing strong selective pressure for resistance—RBC membrane-coated nanosponges neutralize bacterial toxins without killing the pathogens themselves, effectively disarming them without interfering with essential growth processes [124–126, 208]. This anti-virulence approach is theoretically less likely to drive resistance evolution. Moreover, by improving targeted delivery and local drug accumulation, CDDS may achieve effective bacterial eradication with reduced antibiotic doses and shorter treatment durations, as suggested by studies demonstrating enhanced efficacy with lower drug amounts [122, 123]. This reduction in overall antibiotic exposure could lower selective pressure on both pathogenic bacteria and commensal microbiota. It is important to note, however, that these theoretical advantages require systematic experimental validation; direct evidence linking CDDS to reduced resistance evolution in clinical settings is currently lacking.

Safety considerations for living cell carriers in anti-infective therapy

While living cell carriers offer unique advantages in targeted drug delivery, their in vivo application raises important safety concerns that require careful evaluation. These risks vary with cell type and arise from their intrinsic biological activities, which may be amplified or dysregulated after administration.

For macrophages and monocytes, excessive activation can trigger massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, potentially leading to systemic inflammatory responses or even cytokine storm, a risk especially pronounced in sepsis or severe infection where the inflammatory microenvironment may further activate the delivered cells [25]. In addition, macrophages loaded with high drug doses may show reduced phagocytic activity or increased apoptosis, impairing their therapeutic efficacy. Neutrophils have a physiological circulating half-life of only 6–8 h, which significantly limits their therapeutic window, and their high reactivity may cause premature degranulation and release of reactive oxygen species and proteases before reaching the target site, potentially resulting in tissue damage; moreover, excessive neutrophil infiltration in chronic inflammation can exacerbate tissue injury and induce autoimmune responses [26].

T cell-based carriers carry risks of off-target activation and uncontrolled proliferation, where inappropriate activation of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells may lead to excessive cytokine secretion (cytokine release syndrome) or unintended cytotoxicity toward healthy tissues [182]. Furthermore, allogeneic T cells carry the risk of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), in which donor T cells attack host tissues [183]. Although NK cells are generally regarded as safer owing to their intrinsic self/nonself recognition, overactivation can induce excessive secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α, contributing to inflammatory tissue damage [190]. In addition, in vitro expansion of NK cells for clinical application usually requires IL-2 or IL-15, which may inadvertently activate other immune cells if not tightly controlled [192].

Stem cells also present unique safety challenges. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are generally considered low-risk due to their limited self-renewal capacity, yet long-term in vivo studies have reported sarcoma formation in immunocompromised mouse models [195]. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), with higher pluripotency, pose a greater teratoma risk if incompletely differentiated before administration. Furthermore, MSCs have been shown to promote tumor progression in certain cancer settings by suppressing antitumor immunity, raising safety concerns for patients with occult malignancies [196, 209].

Several strategies have been developed to mitigate these safety risks. Replacing live cells with cell membrane-coated nanoparticles or extracellular vesicles avoids risks related to cell persistence and proliferation while preserving targeting ability [24, 27]. Genetic introduction of safety switches — such as inducible caspase-9 or herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase — enables controlled ablation of transplanted cells in case of adverse events [194]. Optimizing cell sources, such as using autologous rather than allogeneic cells, reduces immunogenicity and rejection risks. Finally, rigorous preclinical evaluation in suitable animal models (including immunocompetent and long-term models) is essential before clinical translation.

Although living cell carriers present considerable therapeutic potential, their safety profiles must be thoroughly assessed in a cell-type-specific manner. The selection of cell type, source, and engineering strategy should be guided by a rigorous risk–benefit analysis. Where safety concerns cannot be adequately resolved, cell membrane-coated nanoparticles or extracellular vesicles serve as promising alternatives that retain many advantages of living cells without the associated biological risks.

Unlocking the clinical potential of CDDS for infectious diseases

Beyond the mechanisms discussed above, the clinical promise of CDDS must be evaluated against the current state of translational evidence. The growing crisis of antibiotic resistance has driven the development of innovative anti-infective strategies, among which CDDS have gained prominence as a promising approach. However, the translational maturity of these platforms varies substantially. Before discussing specific platforms, it is important to contextualize the translational readiness of CDDS in anti-infective therapy.

Based on a general assessment of the original studies cited in this review, we note that the majority have demonstrated efficacy in small-rodent models (mice, rats), while a smaller proportion remain at the in vitro proof-of-concept stage. Fewer than 5% have been evaluated in larger animal models (rabbits, pigs). Only a handful of platforms—primarily erythrocyte-based systems—have advanced to early-phase clinical trials (Phase I/II), mostly for non-infectious indications [69, 210]. For infectious diseases specifically, only two biomimetic platforms, the erythrocyte-mimetic nanosponge (CTI-005) and the macrophage-inspired nanosponge (CTI-111) from Cellics Therapeutics, have received IND approval and are entering Phase 1b/2a trials [150, 208]. No CDDS platform has yet received regulatory approval for anti-infective therapy in humans. Thus, while the preclinical evidence base is rapidly expanding, clinical translation remains in its infancy. Drug delivery platforms derived from erythrocytes stand as the pioneering biological vehicles to progress to clinical evaluation. These erythrocyte-based systems display considerable promise for managing hereditary, autoimmune, and cancerous diseases by providing enhanced pharmacokinetic characteristics for agents such as dexamethasone-21-phosphate, L-asparaginase, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, along with thymidine phosphorylase [69]. During 2013, a Phase II clinical study (NCT01171807) indicated that erythrocyte-facilitated transport of dexamethasone-21-phosphate produced positive therapeutic outcomes while avoiding corticosteroid-associated side reactions in individuals suffering from steroid-dependent ulcerative colitis, demonstrating a distinct therapeutic advantage for this chronic inflammatory condition [210].It is worth noting, however, that this study focused on an inflammatory condition rather than an infectious disease, highlighting the need for dedicated clinical trials of CDDS in anti-infective therapy.

As a promising strategy, cell membrane–based biomimetic nanocarriers merge natural membrane biology with material multifunctionality. Industrial development is increasingly visible, exemplified by Cellics Therapeutics’ erythrocyte-mimetic nanosponge (CTI-005). Designed to neutralize MRSA virulence factors, it has obtained FDA IND approval and will soon begin a Phase 1b/2a study [208]. The firm’s macrophage-inspired nanosponge (CTI-111), aimed at sepsis, has also garnered CARB-X funding, underscoring the translational momentum in this arena [150].

EVs, natural and engineered EVs can substantially enhance therapeutic efficacy through endogenous or exogenous loading strategies. Numerous clinical trials are investigating their applications in cancer, viral infections, neurodegenerative diseases, and cardiovascular disorders. For example, Ilias Biologics has developed ILB-202, a nanotherapeutic encapsulating the anti-inflammatory protein super-repressor srIκB, designed to prevent acute kidney injury associated with cardiac surgery [211]. The therapeutic candidate has obtained approval from the Australian Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) and has initiated a Phase I clinical trial. Concurrently, mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs)—harnessing their immunomodulatory properties—are being evaluated in clinical studies for applications in graft-versus-host disease and pulmonary inflammation.

CDDS offer advantages such as immune evasion, prolonged circulation, enhanced targeting, and biofilm penetration, making them particularly suited for chronic infections, biofilm-associated infections, and multidrug-resistant bacterial infections. In scenarios such as hospital-acquired infections, sepsis, and drug-resistant bacterial pneumonia, CDDS can markedly improve efficacy, reduce dosing frequency, and lower toxic side effects, potentially replacing or complementing conventional antibiotics.

Despite these advances, the clinical translation still faces multiple challenges, including immunogenicity risks, lack of standardized manufacturing processes, incomplete quality control systems, and absence of dedicated regulatory frameworks. In particular, the scarcity of GMP-compliant large-scale production platforms has limited the application of bacterial outer membrane vesicles and genetically engineered cellular carriers.

Summary and future outlook

CDDS have recently emerged as a promising strategy with growing preclinical evidence in anti-infective therapy. Unlike conventional NDDS, CDDS employ natural cells or their derivatives as carriers, capitalizing on their intrinsic biocompatibility, effective immune evasion, and innate targeting capacities to improve both the precision and efficacy of treatments. This review systematically outlines recent progress in the development of CDDS for combating infections, highlighting their advantages over NDDS in terms of in vivo stability, targeting accuracy, and overall therapeutic performance. It is important to recognize, however, that the vast majority of these findings stem from in vitro or small-animal studies, and clinical validation remains limited. By examining CDDS derived from a range of cellular sources—such as red blood cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and platelets—we elucidate the mechanisms through which these systems prolong drug half-life, enhance localized drug accumulation, and help overcome multidrug resistance. Moreover, this review prospectively discusses several cell types that, although not yet widely applied in infection treatment, have exhibited promising delivery performance in other disease models, providing theoretical and technical references for future strategy development.

Most current studies about CDDS are still limited to in vitro or small animal models, lacking systematic preclinical safety and efficacy assessments, which hinders clinical translation. Furthermore, there are no standardized protocols for critical technical aspects such as cell source extraction, membrane purification quality, preservation of membrane protein functionality, and membrane–nanocarrier fusion efficiency, resulting in poor reproducibility and limited industrial scalability. Under complex infectious conditions, such as polymicrobial co-infection, biofilm formation, or immunodeficiency, the in vivo behavior and drug delivery efficiency of CDDS remain inadequately studied. Live cell-based delivery systems may pose safety risks in vivo, such as premature drug release or off-target delivery. Current research primarily focuses on bacterial infections, while drug delivery strategies for viruses, fungi, and parasites remain underdeveloped, limiting the broad-spectrum anti-infective potential of CDDS. Future research should concentrate on the following areas to address the above challenges. Initially, enhance preclinical research by establishing comprehensive toxicological, safety, and pharmacokinetic evaluation systems to facilitate the transition of CDDS from animal studies to human clinical applications. Secondly, develop efficient, gentle, and controllable membrane extraction and membrane-carrier fusion technologies to improve product consistency and promote standardized operational protocols. Third, systematic validation in complex models such as polymicrobial infections, biofilm-associated infections, and immunocompromised hosts should be conducted to broaden the therapeutic applicability and depth of CDDS, as current evidence is heavily skewed toward mono-species, acute, surface-associated biofilm models. Fourth, intelligent systems responsive to infectious microenvironments should be designed to achieve precise targeting and controlled drug release. Fifth, synthetic biology and artificial intelligence should be integrated to optimize cellular engineering and system-level design, enhancing delivery efficiency and targeting specificity. Finally, extend the application of CDDS to infections caused by viruses, fungi, and emerging drug-resistant pathogens, advancing toward a broad-spectrum anti-infective platform.

In conclusion, although substantial challenges remain, the promising potential of CDDS to advance anti-infective therapy is clear. With sustained research efforts and technological innovation, this field holds promise for addressing current limitations in infection management. However, realizing this potential will require rigorous translational research, including validation in large-animal models and well-designed clinical trials, to bridge the current gap between preclinical promise and clinical reality.
