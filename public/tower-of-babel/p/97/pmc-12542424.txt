Association between end-of-third-year objective structured clinical examinations and accreditation council for graduate medical education milestones during intern year
Authors: Katherine Berg, Jillian Zavodnick, Dianna Cheney-Peters, Heather L Heiman, Sally A Santen, Yoon Soo Park, Anita Wilson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) administered in medical schools have increased in importance with the elimination of United States Medical Licensing Exam (USMLE) Step 2 Clinical Skills (CS).ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to understand the association between student performance on an end-of-third-year OSCE and performance in residency.MethodsThis study explored the correlations between OSCE performance and relevant Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) milestone 1.0 performance. This study included students’ scores from 139 Sidney Kimmel Medical College (SKMC) students who completed an end-of-third-year OSCE during 2015 and 2016 and completed an intern year in Internal Medicine (IM). Main outcomes were the relevant IM sub-competencies: Patient Care (PC1-information gathering and PC2-management plan) and Interpersonal and Communication Skills (ICS1-patient communication and ICS2-team communication).ResultsThe correlations between the OSCE Integrated clinical encounter (ICE) scores and PC1, PC2, and ICS1 were statistically significant; the correlation with ICS2 did not reach significance. The strongest correlation was between the OSCE ICE score and PC1 (r = 0.28 with 95% confidence interval of [0.12, 0.43]). There was not a statistically significant relationship between the OSCE Communication Skills scores and any of the Milestone 1.0 ratings - PC1, PC2, ICS1 or ICS2, though all correlations were positive. The magnitude of the correlations and 95% confidence intervals suggest the significant effects were small.ConclusionsThis study found a correlation between student OSCE performance and ACGME milestone 1.0 patient care and interpersonal and communication skills sub-competencies. OSCE scores could contribute additional information to the data currently used as an educational handoff in students’ transition to residency.Keywords: UME OSCE, GME milestones, Internal medicine

Introduction

Clinical skills are critical for physicians to master and comprise much of the medical school curriculum and assessments. Until 2020, the UMSLE Step 2 Clinical Skills (CS) was a national standardized clinical skills assessment required for medical licensure, with predictive ability for intern performance [1, 2]. This requirement was eliminated, due in part to concerns about high cost and high passing rate, and medical schools are now tasked with filling this crucial gap with their own assessment of clinical skills [3]. In addition to assessing these skills during clinical rotations, many schools use standardized patient Objective Structured Clinical Exams (OSCEs) for standardized clinical skills assessment [4].

The Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) Milestone competency framework describes skills necessary for independent practice. Skills are specialty-specific and grouped into one of six competency domains: patient care (PC), medical knowledge (MK), systems-based practice (SBP), interpersonal and communication skills (ICS), practice-based learning and improvement (PBLI), and professionalism (PROF) [5]. Individual skills are known as sub-competencies. Milestones are used to describe learners’ progression within each sub-competency from the level expected at entry into residency to competency needed for independent practice [5–7]. Program directors and clinical competency committees use milestones to assess resident performance periodically throughout residency [5]. Every 6 months, programs report milestones data to the ACGME. The milestones are under continuous revision, and the set in use prior to 2020 is referred to as “milestones 1.0.”

The Coalition for Physician Accountability’s Undergraduate Medical Education-Graduate Medical Education Review Committee (UGRC) recommends that medical educators work to create a more streamlined transition from undergraduate medical education (UME) to graduate medical education (GME). The committee cities the need to identify UME assessment data most predictive of GME performance [8]. However, the lack of a shared UME and GME mental model for learner competency as well as separate assessment frameworks make it difficult to determine which UME assessment data is predictive [8]. Additionally, the UME to GME information handover is often conceptualized as a competition for residency positions rather than an assessment of competency for those positions, which incentivizes medical schools to emphasize positive traits of their students and minimize deficiencies.

Program directors rely heavily on licensure exam scores to select applicants for residency [9, 10]. United States Medical Licensing Exam (USMLE) Step 1 and Step 2 CK have historically been available as numeric scores, though since 2022, only Step 2 CK is reported as a numeric score. With a first-time passing rate of 92% for US MD students, pass-fail reporting limits the utility of Step 1 as a criterion for residency selection [11]. Even though the Step 2 CK numeric score shows a weak correlation with residents’ performance in patient care (r = 0.18 in a multi-institutional study), it is not intended to function as the sole basis of residency selection, and the USMLE cautions program directors against such use [12, 13]. Additionally, this examination assesses mostly medical knowledge and only “basic patient centered skills that provide the foundation for the safe and competent practice of medicine;” it does not claim to be a sophisticated discriminator of clinical skills [14]. The information available to program directors for selection, including grades, USMLE scores, medical student performance examination may not provide a clear picture of graduates’ clinical skills to allow for improved and safer transition to residency.

Little is known about the relationship between end-of-third-year UME OSCEs and residency performance. While prior studies suggest correlation between OSCEs at the beginning of intern year and future performance on ACGME core competencies, this association has not been investigated across the UME and GME spectrum or in internal medicine (IM) residents [11–14]. In this study, we examined the correlation between UME OSCE scores and performance on relevant ACGME IM 1.0 sub-competencies.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study included all Sidney Kimmel Medical College (SKMC) students who completed an end-of-third-year OSCE (described below) in 2015 or 2016, as well as reported intern year 6-month milestone 1.0 data from an ACGME accredited IM residency program during the academic years 2016–2017 or 2017–2018 (n = 139). Students who go on to participate in ACGME-accredited residencies were assessed after 6 months by their respective residency programs using the relevant specialty-specific competencies. Students who did not have IM milestone ratings for the relevant time period, whether due to a leave of absence or participation in a residency program other than IM, were excluded. This resulted in the exclusion of 307 student records across 14 specialties. Students in the Internal Medicine residency program are the largest group within a single specialty and represent a variety of students. As such, the IM students did not perform statistically differently than students from any of the other specialty programs on the OSCE or in the USMLE Step 2 Clinical Skills performance.

End-of-Third-Year OSCE

All SKMC students complete a summative end-of-third-year OSCE. The end-of-third-year OSCE during the study time period was a 10-station skills assessment in which standardized patients portrayed cases and assessed student performance during a 15-minute encounter; students also had 10 min to complete a post-encounter activity to assess clinical reasoning. The OSCE included six cases developed by the Mid-Atlantic Consortium (MAC), a consortium consisting of 6 medical schools that develop and implement OSCE cases in the mid-Atlantic region. The other four cases were designed at SKMC and developed by faculty content expert consensus. The examination experience and assessment structure of the OSCE were designed to resemble the USMLE Step 2 CS examination [15]. The MAC and the medical school clinical skills faculty determined the case content, which was based on the objectives of the medical schools and major clerkships. All the OSCE cases were performed prior to the study period and underwent a systematic review of assessment data to improve validity and rating scales. Although the students during the study period were also assessed using the Step 2 CS exam, the SKMC OSCE has not undergone major changes since the elimination of this exam.

Standardized patients (SPs) scored students using a faculty-designed checklist of history items (rated as asked or did not ask) and physical examination maneuvers (rated as performed correctly, performed incorrectly, or not done) as well as interpersonal and communication skills (10 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale). SPs were trained and standardized in case portrayal and checklist completion according to standing protocols, with quality assurance performed by real time video review to assure an overall interrater agreement of greater than 70%. One of three clinical skills faculty scored student post-encounter notes using a standardized rubric; faculty scored and discussed ten patient notes with other faculty before proceeding to score the rest independently. The scores were analyzed across cases in domains including history, physical exam, patient encounter note, and interpersonal and communication skills. Percentage correct scores were calculated for each domain. Students must pass each domain to pass the exam. A passing score was required for promotion to the fourth year and graduation.

Similar to the USMLE Step 2 CS, the OSCE final scores were represented by two domains: the integrated clinical encounter (ICE) score, which was the average of the history taking, physical exam, and patient encounter note scores, and the interpersonal and communication skills (ICS) score [16]. When the cases were developed, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated as a measure of internal consistency for the overall OSCE assessment and the domain scores. Cronbach’s alpha for most of the cases ranged from 0.70 to 0.78. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.56, 0.48, and 0.89 for the history taking, physical examination, and communication skills domains, respectively. Most of the cases for the students remain unchanged from year to year, as such, we assume the reliability estimates were similar.

ACGME internal medicine milestones

The IM residency is a 3-year training program. Accredited programs report performance data to the ACGME from the standard set of 6 core competencies. We obtained 6-month milestone data for SKMC students who entered any IM residency program during 2017 or 2018 via the Stemmler funded Medical School Outcomes Milestone Study Group. The milestone set in use during this time was first version released by the ACGME, referred to as milestones 1.0. For this study we obtained ACGME milestone ratings for four sub-competencies: Patient Care 1 (PC1; gathers and synthesizes essential and accurate information to define each patient’s clinical problem(s)); Patient Care 2 (PC2; develops and achieves comprehensive management plan for each patient); Interpersonal and Communication Skills 1 (ICS1; communicates effectively with patients and caregivers); and Interpersonal and Communication Skills 2: (ICS 2; communicates effectively in interprofessional teams). Each sub-competency has milestones on a scale between level 1 and level 5 on 0.5-unit intervals with a preceding 0 to indicate that the learner has not achieved level 1. Level 4 is the recommended level for a resident to practice independently. We used these sub-competency scores to correlate with the OSCE ICE and ICS scores.

The Thomas Jefferson University Institutional Review Board (IRB) declared this study exempt from review. The process of obtaining this exemption included agreement from the IRB that consent from participants was not required.

Data analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics (i.e., mean and standard deviation) for each year’s OSCE domain percentage correct scores. The content of the OSCE was the same for both academic years. To account for any variation in the instructional methods received by students from year to year, OSCE domain scores were transformed within each year to a standard scale with a mean of 85 and standard deviation of 6 (the “standardized score”). The mean and standard deviation were chosen as a reflection of the typical normally distributed grading scale from 0 to 100% with 70% representing a passing score. After the transformation, we combined OSCE data from both years, thus increasing the sample size. Finally, we evaluated the relationship between the OSCE domain scores and the IM sub-competencies using the Spearman correlation coefficient. The Spearman correlation can range from – 1 to 1. To understand the strength of the relationship, we included the 95% confidence intervals for the Spearman correlation coefficient estimates. Effect sizes below 0.2 are considered a small effect, while values of 0.5 and greater than 0.8, suggest a medium or large effect, respectively [17]. We used SAS 9.4 TS Level 1M0 for data analysis.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a positive and statistically significant correlation between the UME OSCE data and ACGME milestone 1.0 ratings on specific relevant sub-competencies. The OSCE ICE domain scores are significantly correlated, with a very small effect size, with patient care (PC1 and PC2) sub-competencies. Interestingly, the OSCE ICE domain scores are also significantly correlated with one of the sub-competencies for interpersonal communication skills (ICS1), though the effect size is even smaller. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a relationship between end-of-third-year UME OSCE performance and future residency milestone performance.

The correlation between the OSCE ICE domain scores and ACGME patient care sub-competencies is unsurprising. The authors suspect that there are overlapping skills captured in the OSCE ICE domain that incorporates history-taking, physical exam, and note writing and in the PC1 skills of data gathering and problem identification and the PC2 management skills. However, the correlation with ICS1, communicating effectively with patients and caregivers, is more surprising, as the ICE domain does not directly attempt to capture patient communication skills. It may be that communication skills utilized in OSCE history-taking and note writing are beneficial in future patient communications in an unclear manner. Alternatively, the authors hypothesize that ICS1 suffers from a lack of direct observation in the resident clinical learning environment halo or straight-lining effect where raters assume that the skills not -observed are the same as those observed and score them the same. Conversely, ICS2 (communication with the medical team) garners more direct observation and is less subject to this bias; in other words, it has stronger construct validity and understandably does not correlate with the ICE domain OSCE scores, which assess a separate construct.

Contrary to the findings for OSCE ICE scores, the data did not show a significant correlation between OSCE communication skills scores and PC1, PC2, ICS1, and ICS2 performance ratings. Prior literature on the correlation between OSCE communication skills performance and communication skills as assessed in a real practice environment showed similar findings [15]. In that study, an OSCE assessed learners’ communication skills, and high-performing learners’ skills were then assessed in supervised clinical practice. There was no correlation of communication skills between these two settings, indicating OSCE performance was not predictive of performance in supervised practice. Our OSCE replicates this finding for a large population of medical school graduates, in US clinical practice, and after the introduction of ACGME milestones. This lack of correlation across settings may be due to differences in assessment of communication skills or variations in learner demonstration of these skills in a simulated versus clinical environment, or to differing constructs of these skills in these two environments.

Although there is limited prior research on the correlation of UME OSCEs with residency milestone performance, there is a body of literature on OSCEs used to assess incoming residents, which may be a close proxy for UME-acquired skills. These studies have shown correlation with later performance on communication skills, patient care, medical knowledge, practice-based learning and improvement, and overall performance, and have shown promise in assessing systems-based practice [18–21]. Individual clerkship OSCEs have been shown to correlate with step 2 CS, and low clerkship OSCE performance, in particular, has been shown to correlate weakly with low residency performance ratings, though this was assessed outside of the ACGME competency framework [22]. When it comes to high stakes assessments in UME, an early study showed that scores on a prototype step 2 CS significantly correlated with intern performance [23]. All residency performance data in these studies was measured in a pre-milestones assessment framework. This study adds to this body of literature by demonstrating a relationship between UME OSCE performance and future GME performance after the introduction of the milestone paradigm.

This study supports the use of end-of-third-year OSCEs in UME assessment, given this correlation with an individual’s future residency ACGME patient care milestones. OSCE data may also be used formatively by medical schools to provide support to students with lower scores in the OSCE ICE domain with the goal of potentially improving future patient care outcomes. This correlation also makes the OSCE a useful piece of data for a UME to GME handover process. According to recommendations resulting from a meeting of the American Medical Association’s Accelerating Change in Medical Education consortium, “the purpose of educational handover should be to provide medical school performance data to guide continued improvement in learner ability and performance.” [24] These recommendations further envision the operationalization of such a handover, including incorporating UME data into residency program reviews and maximizing standardization such as by using the competency framework. Prior groups have translated UME data to ACGME milestones for a UME to GME handover, but none have used UME OSCE data that has been shown to be associated with future ACGME milestone performance about learners [25, 26]. By incorporating UME OSCE data into the UME to GME transition, a day one learning plan could be created for incoming interns with low performing OSCE ICE scores to assist them in their growth.

A limitation of this study may be generalizability to non-IM specialties, as it included only learners who entered an IM residency. The IM sub-competencies closely aligned with the skills assessed in the end-of-third-year OSCE, but this may not be the case for other specialties. Another limitation is the use of a single medical school’s OSCE; while the OSCE in this study follows the overall format of the previous Step 2 CS exam, which is likely true of many medical schools, the assessment tools may vary between schools. A third limitation is the use of the milestones 1.0 framework, which was the framework used during the study period; this was replaced by the milestones 2.0 framework in 2020, and future research should evaluate these relationships under the new assessment framework, as well as with more recent data (outcomes for this dataset were collecting during the 2016–2017 and 2017–2018 academic years, a period before the elimination of Step 2 CS). Finally, limitations of the variables themselves may limit the study’s power to detect a correlation or the size of the effects seen. For example, the OSCE is a point-in-time assessment that assesses clinical reasoning and communication skills at a lower expected level of sophistication, whereas residency assessments are longitudinal in nature and may provide a deeper and more nuanced assessment of these skills. Alternately, early residency milestones are assessed starting only six months into residency, which may be too soon for program directors to reliably discriminate between high and low performers due to narrow performance range in early training.

This study demonstrates a correlation between our school’s end-of-third-year OSCE clinical skills assessment and resident achievement on ACGME patient care sub-competencies using the milestones 1.0 framework. The magnitude of these correlations is similar to the relationship between USMLE scores, commonly used for residency application selection, and GME performance [13]. Important areas for future research include evaluating data from multiple medical schools’ OSCEs, from residents from multiple specialties, and using the milestones 2.0 framework currently in use. If this association is consistent when examined in additional populations, OSCE scores could be a useful addition to the other data currently used to determine student suitability for residency programs. Similarly, medical schools and residency programs could use UME OSCE data in developing an educational handoff for learners.
