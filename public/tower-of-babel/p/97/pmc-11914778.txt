A set of diagnostic tests for detection of active Babesia duncani infection
Authors: Meenal Chand, Pratap Vydyam, Anasuya C Pal, Jose Thekkiniath, Dounia Darif, Zeng Li, Jae-Yeon Choi, Ruben Magni, Alessandra Luchini, Laura Tonnetti, Elizabeth J Horn, Danielle M Tufts, Choukri Ben Mamoun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:Human babesiosis is an emerging and potentially fatal tick-borne disease caused by intraerythrocytic parasites of the Babesia genus. Among these, Babesia duncani is particularly notable for causing severe and life-threatening illness in humans. Accurate diagnosis and effective disease management hinge on the detection of active B. duncani infections. While molecular assays are available to detect the parasite in blood, a reliable method for identifying biomarkers of active infection remains elusive.Methods:We developed the first B. duncani antigen capture assays, targeting two immunodominant antigens, BdV234 and BdV38. These assays were validated using established in vitro and in vivo B. duncani infection models, and following drug treatment.Results:The assays demonstrated no cross-reactivity with other species such as B. microti, B. divergens, Babesia MO1, or Plasmodium falciparum, and can detect as few as 115 infected erythrocytes/μl of blood. Screening of 1731 blood samples from various biorepositories, including samples previously identified as Lyme and/or B. microti-positive, as well as new specimens from wild mice, revealed no evidence of B. duncani infection or cross-reactivity.Conclusions:These assays hold significant promise for various applications, including point-of-care testing for the early detection of B. duncani in patients, field tests for screening reservoir hosts, and high-throughput screening of blood samples intended for transfusion.Keywords: Human babesiosis, Babesia duncani, ELISA, Infectious disease, Tick-borne diseases, Antigen Capture Assays

Introduction

In recent years, vector-borne infectious diseases have emerged as a significant global public health concern [1]. The expansion of the geographic range of the tick vectors has led to a notable increase in tick-borne diseases (TBDs), which now account for more than 75% of all reported vector-borne diseases in the United States annually [2–4]. Among these TBDs is human babesiosis, an emerging infectious disease caused by intraerythrocytic apicomplexan parasites of the Babesia genus [5].

To date, eight species of Babesia have been identified as human pathogens and documented to cause mild to severe babesiosis with some cases resulting in fatal outcomes [6]. The majority of clinical cases reported to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention are attributed to Babesia microti [7,8], whereas only a few cases of B. duncani babesiosis have been documented in the United States, primarily in Washington, Oregon, and California [9–14].

Currently, the true incidence of B. duncani babesiosis remains unknown. Accurate determination of the true prevalence of B. duncani babesiosis is challenging due to the absence of specific and reliable diagnostic tests that can determine active infection. Over the years, various diagnostic techniques including microscopy, serological tests like immunofluorescence assay (IFA), immunoblot and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and nucleic acid-based tests (NAT) such as nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR), reverse transcription-PCR (RT-PCR), and transcription-mediated amplification (TMA), have been employed to detect Babesia infections. Morphologically, B. duncani-infected red blood cells (RBCs) are indistinguishable from those infected with B. microti or other Babesia species and bear resemblance to the ring-stage forms of Plasmodium falciparum, the causative agent of human malaria. Therefore, microscopy cannot be reliably used to diagnose B. duncani active infection in a blood sample. Except for cases confirmed by both microscopy and PCR [9–14], most unverified cases attributed to this parasite have relied on serological assays, which cannot reliably distinguish between past and active infections. Nucleic acid-based detection techniques are indeed more specific and sensitive and have been effective in identifying positive B. microti blood samples. Nevertheless, in some instances, these techniques have been shown to detect residual parasite DNA or RNA even after the parasite has been eliminated [15,16]. Recent studies using microscopic examination of blood samples from B. microti-infected animals treated with drugs that cleared the infection demonstrated the presence of residual DNA after treatment with antibabesial drugs [16]. Studies using B. microti-infected blood revealed that antigen detection assays, which rely on the detection of highly abundant and often immunodominant secreted antigens of the parasite, exhibit a stronger correlation with active B. microti infection compared to NAT [17]. Therefore, efforts to develop similar assays for the detection of active infections by other Babesia species could be an invaluable resource to estimate the true incidence of babesiosis caused by each of the Babesia species that cause infection in humans.

In this study, we report the discovery of two highly reliable biomarkers of active B. duncani infection. Babesia duncani-specific antigen capture assays (BdACAs) that detect these antigens were developed and were shown to detect as low as 115 parasites/μl of blood. These assays can distinguish between active infections and past exposures, and accurately differentiate between B. microti and B. duncani.

Materials and methods

Babesia duncani infection in mice

Five to six weeks old female C3H/HeJ (The Jackson Laboratory) or Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID) (C.B-17/IcrHsd-Prkdcscid) mice from Inotiv were infected by injecting 1×106 or 1×107 B. duncani WA-1 strain or B. microti LabS1 strain, respectively, using blood collected from previously infected mice. Blood samples were collected at different time points following infection by retro-orbital bleeding or at the end of the study following cardiac puncture, and parasitemia was determined by light microscopy of Giemsa-stained blood smears.

Babesia duncani in vitro culture in human erythrocytes

Babesia duncani WA-1 parasites were cultured continuously in human erythrocytes in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM-F12) media [18], as described previously [19] and also included in the supplementary methods.

Immunodetection of BdV234 and BdV38

Polyclonal antibodies against BdV234 and BdV38 were raised in rabbits (Cocalico Biologicals, Inc., Pennsylvania), purified, and used for the immunodetection analysis as described in detail [20] and also included in the supplementary methods. Briefly, S (supernatant or plasma (PL)), H (hemolysate), and P (pellet) fractions collected in vitro (18% parasitemia) and in vivo (24% parasitemia) were mixed with 4X Laemmli sample buffer (3:1 ratio), boiled at 80°C for 5 minutes and separated on 4–20% mini-protean gels. Following gel transfer, the membranes were probed with anti-BdV234 and BdV38 primary antibodies (1:250 dilution) and goat-anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated immunoglobulin G Ab 1:5000 dilution.

Isolation of extracellular vesicles from plasma, culture supernatant, and hemolysate

Briefly, 1.5 ml of human RBC (hRBC) and mouse RBC (mRBC) samples (uninfected and B. duncani-infected plasma (Pl)/serum or supernatant [S] and hemolysate [H]) were sequentially centrifuged at 500 g for 30 minutes, followed by 16,000 g for 45 minutes. These samples were then centrifuged at 120,000 g for 14 hours at 4°C using a Sorvall MTX 150 micro-ultracentrifuge with an S52-ST swinging bucket rotor (Thermo Fisher) and ultracentrifuge pellet (Up/UHp) and ultracentrifugation supernatant (Us/UHs) fractions were collected [21]. Similarly, extracellular membrane vesicles containing protein cargo released from the parasite were isolated from S/Pl and H of B. duncani in vitro and in vivo culture by ExoQuick ULTRA EV isolation kit (EQULTRA-20A-1) following the user manual. Where indicated, parasite-derived vesicles were also isolated by the ultracentrifugation method, as mentioned previously [22].

BdACA using B. duncani in vitro culture supernatant and hemolysate fractions

Babesia duncani antigen capture sandwich ELISAs were performed as described earlier [16,17], with few modifications. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with 5 μg/ml of capture antibodies (BdV234 and BdV38) and incubated for 2 hours at room temperature. A blocking buffer consisting of 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Phosphate buffered saline (PBST (PBS + 0.05% Tween-20)) was added to each and the plates incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. The plates were then incubated overnight at 4°C with either (i) BdV38 and BdV234 recombinant protein (100 ng/well), (ii) a 1 in 10 dilution of B. duncani in vitro culture S, H, and whole blood samples, or (iii) Pl/serum (heat-inactivated for 30 minutes at 56°C), H and whole blood from uninfected or B. duncani-infected C3H/HeJ mice. Whole blood samples from the American Red Cross (ARC), Lyme Disease Biobank (LDB), and field-derived mouse samples were prepared as described above. The plates were then washed four times with PBST, and 5 μg/ml biotinylated detection Abs (BdV234 and BdV38) were added and the samples incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. Following this step, the plates were washed four times with PBST and incubated with HRP-conjugated streptavidin antibody in 1:5000 dilution at room temperature for 1 hour, followed by washing and incubation with 50 μl 3,3′,5,5′ tetramethyl-benzidine (TMB) substrate for 7 minutes in the dark. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 μl of 0.1N HCl, and the Optical Density at 450 nm was measured using a BioTek Synergy Mx plate reader.

Results

Identification of B. duncani-secreted antigens

To identify proteins exported by B. duncani, we used a Nanotrap-based proteomics approach [23,24] on hemolysate (H) and secreted (S) fractions collected from a culture of B. duncani-infected human erythrocytes (Figure 1a). Mass spectrometry analysis identified 27 proteins in the S fraction (Supplementary Table 1) and 39 proteins in the H fraction. Of these exported proteins, BdV234 and BdV38 were selected based on their peptide abundance, high expression levels of their encoding genes as determined by RNA sequencing [24], and predicted antigenicity profiles (Supplementary Table 1). Recombinant proteins were purified and used to generate specific polyclonal antibodies, which were utilized to monitor the expression and distribution of the native proteins in the S, H, and the pellet (P) fractions from cultures of B. duncani-infected and control uninfected human erythrocytes (Figure 1b) as well as mouse erythrocytes collected from uninfected and infected animals (Figure 1c). Interestingly, whereas these antigens could be readily detected in all three fractions from in vitro cultured B. duncani-infected human erythrocytes (Figure 1b), BdV234 and BdV38 were found predominantly in the H and P fractions prepared from mouse blood collected from B. duncani-infected C3H/HeJ mice, with levels in the plasma (Pl) fraction almost undetectable (Figure 1c). As expected, no signals could be detected in the fractions isolated from uninfected erythrocytes (Figures 1b and c).

Evidence of vesicular-mediated secretion of BdV234 and BdV38 from B. duncani-infected erythrocytes

Recent studies in B. microti have demonstrated that several proteins of these parasites are actively exported through a vesicular-mediated export mechanism [22,25]. To assess the mode of export of BdV234 and BdV38, the culture S and H fractions of B. duncani-infected human erythrocytes, as well as the Pl and H fractions of B. duncani-infected mouse erythrocytes were subjected to ultracentrifugation at 120,000 × g for 14 hours at 4°C to separate the soluble (Us/UHs) and vesicle-associated membrane (Up/UHp) fractions. The resulting fractions were then analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-BdV234 and anti-BdV38 antibodies. As shown in Figure 1c, the proteins were primarily found associated with the Up/UHp fractions in both human and mouse-infected samples (Supplementary Figure 1a). Using the vesicle isolation system, ExoQuick®, to separate the S fraction (Es/EHs) from the vesicle-rich P fraction (Ev/EHv) on Pl, S, and H fractions collected from uninfected or B. duncani-infected blood (Supplementary Figure 1b), we found that both antigens are associated with the vesicle-rich fractions (Figure 1d). Together these data demonstrate that BdV234 and BdV38 are exported by B. duncani-infected erythrocytes through a vesicular-mediated mechanism. Additionally, further IFA results confirmed the localization and cellular distribution of the BdV234 and BdV38 secretory proteins in B. duncani (Supplementary Figure 2).

Development of B. duncani antigen capture Assays (BdACAs)

The discovery that BdV234 and BdV38 could be readily detected in the H and environment of B. duncani-infected erythrocytes led us to investigate the use of these two proteins as biomarkers of an active B. duncani infection. We used the anti-BdV234 and anti-BdV38 antibodies to design antigen capture sandwich ELISA assays (BdACAs) to detect the exported proteins in B. duncani-infected blood (Figure 2a). The BdACAs were optimized using recombinant BdV234 and BdV38 proteins (Figure 2b) and subsequently used to detect native proteins in fractions prepared from B. duncani-infected human erythrocytes at different parasitemia levels. Both BdV234 and BdV38 were detected in the S and H fractions of infected erythrocytes and their levels increased with increasing parasitemia levels before reaching a plateau at parasitemia levels between 6–10% (Figures 2c–d). No signals could be detected using S and H fractions from uninfected human RBCs. Consistent with the immunoblot data, BdV38 and BdV234 could not be detected in serum and Pl fractions from blood collected from B. duncani-infected mice (Figure 2e) but were readily detected in the H fractions prepared from B. duncani-infected mouse erythrocytes (Figure 2f).

Evaluation of BdACAs using samples from B. duncani-infected human and mouse erythrocytes

The minimum level of the BdACAs in detecting B. duncani was evaluated by analyzing their performance using a culture of B. duncani-infected human erythrocytes at varying parasitemia levels following a 2-fold dilution from a culture at 9×106 infected RBCs (iRBC) per ml. The data were compared to those obtained by quantitative PCR (qPCR) and microscopy analyses conducted on the same samples (Figures 3a–c). For the BdACAs, the limit of detection (LOD) was defined as 3x the limit of blank (LOB). Using these parameters, Bd38ACA detected BdV38 in wells containing as low as 103 iRBCs, whereas Bd234ACA detected BdV234 in wells containing as low as ~2×103 iRBCs (Figures 3b–c). As anticipated, no reactivity was observed using uninfected human RBCs. To further estimate the lowest parasitemia levels that could be detected using these assays, a culture of B. duncani with an initial parasitemia of 0.5% at 5% hematocrit, was incubated at 37°C, and the samples were collected and analyzed by microscopy, BdACA and qPCR (Figures 3d–f). Our results demonstrated that the BdACA method accurately identified parasitemia levels as low as 0.5% (Figures 3e–f) and showed an increase in the levels of secreted antigens concomitant with increased parasitemia (Figures 3e–f). Similarly, to validate the BdACA assay in samples from infected animals, blood was collected daily from C3H/HeJ mice following inoculation with 106 B. duncani-infected erythrocytes until day 7 when animals succumbed to infection. Whereas qPCR detected B. duncani 18S recombinant DNA within hours post-inoculation, Bd38ACA and Bd234ACA detected the antigens as early as 2- and 3-days post-infection, respectively (Figures 3h–i).

BdV38 and BdV234 as biomarkers for evaluating anti-babesial treatment efficacy

An ideal biomarker of active Babesia infection is one whose levels show an excellent positive correlation with parasitemia levels and does not remain in the blood following clearance of infection due to drug treatment. To assess whether BdV38 and BdV234 meet these criteria, BdACA assays were used on samples collected following treatment with either vehicle or tafenoquine (TQ). Parasite cultures at 1% parasitemia were incubated at 37°C and collected at 12-hour intervals over 72 hours following treatment with either vehicle (dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)) or TQ at 3 μM and 6 μM [26]. Analysis of Giemsa-stained blood smears showed a rapid decline in parasitemia to undetectable levels in TQ-treated cultures compared to untreated (UT) cultures where parasitemia levels were above 8% 72-hours post-inoculation (Figure 4a). Analysis of culture samples using the BdACA assay showed a positive correlation between BdV38 levels and parasitemia in both UT and treated samples (Figure 4b). In contrast, qPCR analysis of the same samples showed levels of B. duncani DNA in TQ-treated cultures that were similar at the beginning as well as the end of the treatment (Figure 4c). Similarly, we compared the BdACA assay to detect BdV38 to microscopy and qPCR in whole blood collected at different time points from uninfected mice or mice that were inoculated with 106 B. duncani-infected erythrocytes and treated with either vehicle (polyethylene glycol-400 (PEG-400)) or TQ (Figures 4d–f). Untreated animals succumbed to infection by day 6 post-inoculation when parasitemia ranged between 18–27%, whereas treated animals survived the infection and cleared the parasites by day 6 post-inoculation (Figure 4d). Consistent with the analysis of Giemsa-stained blood smears, the BdV38ACA also showed a rapid decline in BdV38 levels in TQ-treated animals (Figure 4e). In contrast, qPCR analyses showed detectable parasite DNA even after the parasites were cleared (Figure 4f). Similar findings were observed using the BdACA-BdV234 assay (Supplementary Figure S4).

Evaluation of Bd38ACA and Bd234ACA assays

To evaluate the BdACAs, blood samples from immunocompromised mice infected with B. microti as well as cultures of B. divergens (Rouen87 strain), Babesia MO1, and P. falciparum (3D7 strain) propagated in vitro in human erythrocytes at 10% parasitemia each were examined for the presence of Bd38ACA (Figures 5a–b) and Bd234ACA (Figures 5c–d). No cross-reactivity was detected in these samples, suggesting that the assays are specific for B. duncani-secreted proteins. As a control, BdACA38 detected high levels of the antigen in blood from B. duncani-infected human erythrocytes or blood from infected mice, but no signal from uninfected cultures or mice was detected (Figures 5a–d)

Blood screening using Bd38ACA

To evaluate the effectiveness of BdACAs as screening tools for detecting active B. duncani infections, we employed the Bd38ACA assay to screen 1731 whole blood specimens from diverse locations across the US. These encompassed 200 human whole blood samples (100 B. microti-positive and 100 B. microti-negative) from the ARC; 770 human whole blood samples from the LDB, previously screened for Lyme disease and B. microti; and 761 whole blood samples from white-footed mice (Peromyscus leucopus), a natural reservoir host, collected from Western Pennsylvania (Figure 5e). In this study, the lower LOD (OD450 = 0.3) was defined as 3 × LOB (Figure 5e). All 1731 samples, analyzed in this study, showed OD450 values below 0.6, which when compared to the standard curve for B. duncani parasitemia levels corresponds to parasitemia levels below 0.55% (Figure 5f). Of these 1731 samples, eight had OD450 values between 0.31 and 0.59. Among the 761 white-footed mouse samples, 756 showed signals below the LOD and five samples showed signals above the LOD but below an OD450 of 0.35 (< than 2x LOD). Similarly, out of the 770 LDB samples, three showed signals above the LOD with OD450 of 0.31, 0.49, and 0.59. Samples with OD450 signals above the LOD using the BdV38-based detection assay were further confirmed to be within this same range when tested using the BdV234ACA assay (not shown), suggesting that these samples either carry B. duncani at parasitemia levels below 0.5% or the BdV38 and BdV234 assays detect antigens from other closely related pathogens. To gain further insights into the source of the signals in these samples, PCR-based amplification analyses were conducted using the primer pairs listed in Supplementary Table 2. Using primers designed to be specific for B. duncani (specific to HSP-70, CelTos, and BdV38 genes), B. microti (specific to BmITS and BmGPI12 genes), B. divergens (specific to AMA-1 gene) and B. MO1 (specific to the helicase gene), our PCR analyses showed that these samples do not contain DNA from these species (Supplementary Figures 5–6). To assess whether these samples contain DNA from other Babesia species, we selected samples with the highest OD450 among these for PCR analysis using a previously reported primer pair [27] that amplifies a 200 bp of the 18S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene from multiple Babesia species (Figure 5e). This analysis was conducted on nine samples including one from ARC, three from Lyme Biobank, and five from field mice (Supplementary Figures 7a–b). The data showed that six of the nine samples tested positive by PCR. Sequencing of the 200 bp PCR product revealed sequences with 98–100% identity to 18S rRNA genes from Babesia species of Clade VI (B. divergens, capreoli, B. odocoilei, and B. venatorum) (Supplementary Table 3). We further designed a new primer set against conserved regions of the 18S rRNA genes of Babesia species that amplify a 736 bp fragment. Using this primer set against all nine samples, we found five (field mice) samples to be positive by PCR (Supplementary Figures 7c–d). However, sequencing of the fragments failed to identify a specific species at more than 90% sequence identity. Together, these data suggest that of 1731 samples, eight correspond to unknown species of Babesia likely closely related to B. duncani. However, the exact identity of this new species could not be determined due to the limited amount of material available for large-scale genomic analyses.

Discussion

Herein, we report the development of the first antigen capture assays, BdACAs, for the detection of active B. duncani infection. The assays detect the presence of B. duncani-secreted antigens BdV38 (31 kDa) or BdV234 (27 kDa) in blood samples from infected cultures or animals very accurately. The BdACAs exhibit remarkable response in detecting these secreted B. duncani antigens from as little as a single drop of blood, equivalent to 1.6×107 RBCs, in both human and mouse samples.

Furthermore, the assays specifically distinguish B. duncani infections when tested against other human babesiosis-causing parasites (B. microti, B. divergens (Rouen87), Babesia MO1) or related protozoan species (P. falciparum) that causes human malaria.

One key advantage leveraged in our approach is the use of an in-culture-in-mouse (ICIM) model system of B. duncani [28] infection, which allowed us to systematically examine different aspects of our assay, including optimizing the assay using various sample types such as H, S, whole blood, Pl, and serum.

Here, we have used an integrated approach consisting of genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic analyses to identify secreted proteins of B. duncani, which could be suitable as biomarkers of active infection from different fractions (S, H, and P) (Supplementary Table 1). Among the pool of exported proteins, BdV234 and BdV38 were specifically chosen due to their substantial peptide abundance and notably elevated expression levels (Supplementary Table 1). We reasoned that those proteins with higher abundance and containing signal sequences were predominantly destined for the extracellular space, where they could play a crucial role in eliciting cell signaling and facilitating communication between host and parasite to promote infection propagation. To our surprise, our initial attempts to detect BdV234 and BdV38 directly from the Pl or serum of B. duncani-infected mice were unsuccessful. However, by employing ultracentrifugation and the ExoQuick approach, we were able to purify extracellular vesicles (EVs) that contained these parasite proteins. The EVs containing these B. duncani proteins may play a crucial role in the trafficking and delivery of these proteins, allowing them to be expressed on the outer membrane of infected RBCs similar to that reported for B. microti [22,25]. Further investigation is warranted to explore the precise mechanism by which these proteins are sorted and packaged into EVs, as well as their specific role in mediating their transport to the surface of infected RBCs. Understanding the molecular processes involved in this vesicular transport machinery will contribute to our overall comprehension of B. duncani pathogenesis and host-parasite interactions.

This study assessed the field suitability of Bd38ACA by screening a diverse array of human and mouse whole blood samples. The assay exhibited minimal cross-reactivity with both human and field-derived white-footed mouse samples. However, a subset (0.4%) of samples showed low reactivity with Bd38ACA, at levels significantly lower in comparison to the positive control samples. Subsequent PCR analysis confirmed the absence of B. duncani parasites in these samples but the exact organisms, likely a species closely related to B. duncani, that caused the cross-reactivity detected in the BdACA assays remain to be further characterized.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that the BdACA assays, BdV38ACA and BdV234ACA, are reliable diagnostic tests for detecting active B. duncani infection and are suitable for large-scale screening of the blood supply. Further optimization of these assays is warranted to test uninfected and infected human blood samples and compare with the current method (NAT assays) for blood screening. The development of such a diagnostic tool is a significant step forward in the fight against B. duncani and can significantly improve the accuracy and efficiency of diagnosis, treatment, and surveillance of this emerging TBD.
