Personal experience with abortion influences how we practice as obstetrician–gynecologists
Authors: Katherine J. Kramer, Sarah Ottum, Danna Ghafir, Capricia Bell, Mitchell Reider, Caroline McClure, Maurice-Andre Recanati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate whether a personal history of having an abortion, or knowing someone close who did, has an impact on how a physician practices.MethodsA web-based survey was sent to Ob/Gyn residents asking about personal abortion experience, demographics, religious background, political views, residency program metrics, and intent to provide abortion. Pearson Chi-square and Mann–Whitney U tests were performed to assess whether or not personal history of abortion (PHA) was significantly associated with personal characteristics or training patterns.ResultsOb/Gyn residents with a personal history of abortion were older in age (p = 0.002), sought out additional training in family planning (p = 0.009), considered it extremely important that their program offers family planning training (p < 0.001), and were much more likely to intend to become an abortion provider themselves post-residency (p < 0.001).ConclusionPersonal experience is associated with training and practice patterns among Ob/Gyn residents, a finding that has implications for an Ob/Gyn workforce under pressure from increasing legislative restrictions to abortion care. Ob/Gyn residents should be supported in their training goals to combat moral injury associated with the inability to provide evidence-based care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00404-026-08369-4.

What does this study add to the clinical work

These personal histories are a primary driver of practical goals, which is critical for workforce planning as legislative restrictions on abortion care and training continue to increase.

Introduction

Family planning is one of the most common reasons reproductive-aged women visit their obstetrician–gynecologist, and abortion is one of the most common medical and surgical procedures performed in the United States [1]. Because of the need for safe and accessible abortion and contraception care, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists has supported abortion education for residents to increase the availability of trained providers [2]. In 1996, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) required residency training programs to integrate abortion training within the curriculum on an “opt-out” or “opt-in” basis [3]. After the US Supreme Court’s Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization overturned Roe v. Wade, the ACGME revised Obstetrics and Gynecology (Ob/Gyn) program requirements in September of 2022 to necessitate that all programs offer abortion training on an “opt-out” basis [4].

Despite this accreditation requirement, access to abortion training remains variable, as nearly half of Ob/Gyn residency programs are in restrictive jurisdictions for abortion care [5]. Recent studies have found that Ob/Gyns in states with abortion bans are already experiencing moral injury, as these restrictions hinder their ability to provide evidence-based abortion care [6, 7].

Each individual resident carries unique personal experiences that have the potential to inform their approach to care. In this paper, we seek to determine if a personal history of having had an abortion or knowing someone close who did have one (PHA) influences how that individual physician practices.

Materials and methods

Web-based survey

Under IRB approval (IRB#074617MP4X), a link (https://waynestate.az1.qualtrics.com/jfe/form/SV_7VssqjccfquwnVb) to an anonymous, voluntary Qualtrics (Qualtrics International, Provo, UT, USA) open survey (Fig. S1) was sent to each Obstetrics and Gynecology residency training program director in the United States with a request to forward the survey to their residents. This dataset was used in a recently published study which examined a resident’s intent to provide abortions post-residency [8]. Informed consent was obtained electronically prior to the start of the survey. The two-page survey was developed by the authors and the technical functionality of the survey was tested prior to distribution. The survey asked if the resident had personally had an abortion or knew someone close to them that did, as a single question (independent variable). Collected data included the following: demographics, religious background, political views, residency program metrics, and intent to provide abortion (Table 1). Survey items were not randomized or alternated, with no adaptive questions. There were 15 questions per page displayed in the survey, which was in total 2 pages long. There were no questions marked as required to check for survey completeness upon submission. Respondents were able to review or revise their responses by navigating backwards prior to submission. Unique survey responses were determined through IP addresses (69% of surveys came from unique addresses) and enforced through personalized cookies, preventing duplicates among the respondents who used a shared work computer. In addition, prevention of duplicates was performed using Qualtrics internal system rather than simply discriminating by IP address, and log file analysis was not applicable. Participants were given the option of submitting an email if they desired to partake in a random drawing for a Starbucks gift card. States were grouped into regions as defined by the U.S. Census Bureau [9], political affiliation was grouped as republican or democrat based on choice of Presidential candidate, religion was analyzed as categorical data, and case numbers (contraceptive visits, tubal sterilizations, long-acting reversible contraceptive insertions, and abortions) were analyzed as continuous data. Data were collected over a 3-month period. All data, including demographic information, were protected from unauthorized access by password protection and were encrypted and stored on a secure server only accessible to the principal investigator. Statistical analysis

Deidentified survey data were pasted into an Excel spreadsheet and this convenience sample was analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (IBM Analytics). Partially completed surveys were included in the analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample. The Pearson Chi-square test was performed to test the association between PHA (a categorical variable) and other categorical variables. The nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was performed to test the association between PHA and ordinal variables. A raw p value < 0.05 was considered significant. To control the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER, false positives) across multiple independent comparisons, the Holm correction was applied.

Results

At the time of this survey, there were 4991 Ob/Gyn residents training in 295 ACGME-accredited programs in the United States [10]. The convenience sample of survey respondents includes 429 respondents (8% of the U.S. total) who started the survey, with 409 completing the majority of questions (a 95% completion rate). Eighty-nine percent (89%) of respondents identified their gender as “Female”. There was a near equal distribution of responses by geographic region and year in training (Table 1). A total of 383 survey respondents (93.6% response rate) answered the single question regarding their personal history of an abortion experience (either their own or someone they know) with a nearly even split between “yes” and “no” responses.

Those that either had a personal history of abortion or knew someone who did tended to be older in age (p = 0.002), lean democrat (p = 0.005), train at opt-out programs (p = 0.040), seek out additional training in family planning (p = 0.009), and were more likely to intend to provide abortion care post-residency (p < 0.001). When considering residency applications, those with a personal abortion experience considered it “extremely” or “very” important that their residencies have family planning training (p < 0.001). Discussion

Despite recent restrictions to abortion access, abortion remains one of the most common medical and surgical procedures in Ob/Gyn care [11]. According to the Guttmacher Institute’s Monthly Abortion Provision Study, over 1 million abortions were performed by a clinician in states without bans in 2023 [12]. Notably, there has been an 11% increase in clinician-provided abortions in these states since 2020. While all residents are required to obtain similar levels of knowledge on contraception and abortion, approximately 40% of Ob/Gyn residents are in states with restrictive abortion legislation [13]. Variable opportunities to receive adequate abortion training add complexity to the implications of this study’s findings.

Each resident brings a personal experience which impacts practice patterns [14]. In this survey of Ob/Gyn residents, we found that approximately half of respondents had a personal experience with abortion care, as patients themselves or as someone who knows a patient closely. When interviewing for residencies, individuals with PHA were significantly more likely to consider it “extremely” or “very” important that the program has family planning training (p < 0.001). However, the personal and clinical experience cumulatively gained from each year of residency did not impact PHA. This suggests that the personal experience with abortion happens before residency and that this experience influences program selection and future practice goals. Ob/Gyn residents with PHA were also more likely to be committed to offer abortion services themselves after graduation. As such, they sought out residency programs that were “opt-out” and sought additional training in family planning to gain more experience. Possibly, these individuals may have realized the effect that abortion had on their careers, their lives, or on the life of those close to them. Further research is needed to follow these residents through training and beyond.

Notably, despite abortion being a common procedure, affecting approximately one in three individuals assigned female at birth, nearly half of surveyed Ob/Gyn residents reported no personal experience with abortion for themselves or someone close to them. This discrepancy raises important educational and cultural considerations, suggesting that a proportion of residents may lack exposure to the lived experiences of patients seeking abortion care. Additionally, this finding raises questions about the perceived approachability of Ob/Gyn residents for abortion-related disclosures, as limited dialog may perpetuate science and stigma even within medical training environments.

While data suggested that those leaning republican had significantly lower PHA (27% vs. 53%), it is unclear if this group had overall lower abortion rates or if the subject of abortion was less discussed within that group. Unlike other investigators, we found that religion [15] and religious upbringing, as measured by regular attendance at services, size of the town and geographic area where the resident grew up, and region of the country where they are undergoing training were not correlated with PHA. These findings suggest that having had an abortion, or knowing someone close who did, is a universal experience that is not bound by religion or geography.

Implications

The findings of this study have implications for the Ob/Gyn workforce in the US. The legal landscape for abortion has significantly restricted access to care, training opportunities, and the ability to provide evidence-based abortion care. It is important to emphasize the finding that nearly half of survey respondents indicated PHA, among which training and practice goals are informed by personal experiences. For these residents, the ability to access abortion training and provide abortion care has become uncertain in the last several years. As of August 2024, 14 states have banned abortion and 14 others have implemented restrictive legislation [16]. Given the proportion of Ob/Gyn residents that have an interest in family planning training and abortion care informed by PHA, the lack of training opportunities has the potential to further exacerbate the well-documented burden of moral injury and distress in the field of Ob/Gyn [6, 17].

Limitations

This survey has several limitations. The study did not adequately address the impact of race and ethnicity on PHA. The data, collected for a study on intention to provide abortion, also did not differentiate between having a personal history of abortion and knowing someone close who did. We deliberately chose to be inclusive of female partners of male residents as such information can help gain additional insights. While our sample size was well distributed geographically and represented every region of the United States nearly equally, we did not, however, sample every program. Furthermore, as a cross-sectional survey, causality could not be established. It is also possible that there may be residual confounders that we were unable to control for, such as a bias in residents who chose to partake in this survey.

Conclusion

Having personally had an abortion, or knowing someone close who did, can influence an Ob/Gyn’s training and practice goals. These findings are important in the context of a legal landscape that restricts abortion care in jurisdictions in which approximately 40% of Ob/Gyn residents practice. Residents should be supported in their pursuit of family planning training to address the ongoing moral injury crisis in the field.
