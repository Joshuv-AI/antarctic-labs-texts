Left Atrial Appendage Closure in Patients Refusing Oral Anticoagulation: The LAAC‐REFUSAL Study
Authors: Roberto Galea, Tommaso Bini, Kasper Korsholm, Alexander Sedaghat, Adel Aminian, Vivian Vij, Juan Perich Krsnik, Konstantina Chalkou, Georgios Siontis, Laurent Roten, Jens Erik Nielsen‐Kudsk, Lorenz Räber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe benefit of left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) in patients refusing oral anticoagulation (OAC) despite medical recommendations is unknown. We aim to assess the efficacy and the safety of LAAC in patients refusing OAC.MethodsData on consecutive patients with atrial fibrillation undergoing percutaneous LAAC between 2009 and 2022 in 4 European centers were collected and patients undergoing LAAC due to refusal of OAC (refusal group) were compared with patients receiving LAAC for more established indications (control group). The primary end point of the study was the composite of cardiovascular death, stroke, or systemic embolism. The secondary end points included technical success and procedural complications. Events during follow‐up were compared with those predicted by CHA2DS2VASC and hypertension, abnormal renal/liver function, stroke, bleeding history or predisposition, labile International Normalized Ratio, elderly, drugs/alcohol concomitantly (HASBLED) score risks.ResultsOf the 2649 patients submitted to LAAC, 119 were performed due to OAC refusal (4.5%). Two‐hundred‐thirty‐eight (8.9%) control patients were identified. Refusal as compared with the control group included younger and lower‐risk patients. At 3 years, the primary end point was significantly lower (4.2% versus 17.2%; adjusted hazard ratio: 0.37; 95% CI: 0.14–0.99; P=0.048) in the refusal in respect to the control group. Technical success and procedural complications were similar between groups. In the refusal group, the annual rate of thromboembolic events and major bleedings were 2.3% and 1.9%, respectively, corresponding to 62% and 47% risk reductions as compared with the rates expected by risk scores.ConclusionsIn this multicenter cohort of consecutive patients with atrial fibrillation submitted to LAAC, OAC refusal appeared as an infrequent indication. In this subgroup, LAAC was confirmed to be feasible and safe. The ischemic outcomes rates at 3 years are promising. Further studies are needed to better understand the potential effects of preferring LAAC over OAC.Keywords: adherence, atrial fibrillation, intolerance, left atrial appendage closure, non‐compliance, oral anticoagulation refusalSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

In a multicenter cohort of patients undergoing percutaneous left atrial appendage closure, the left atrial appendage closure procedure was rarely performed in patients refusing oral anticoagulation, yet it showed promising short‐ and long‐term clinical outcomes.

In a multicenter cohort of patients undergoing percutaneous left atrial appendage closure, the left atrial appendage closure procedure was rarely performed in patients refusing oral anticoagulation, yet it showed promising short‐ and long‐term clinical outcomes.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Left atrial appendage closure might be considered as stroke prevention strategy even for very selected patients who are rigidly drug therapy averse, provided that patients are adequately informed regarding the current evidence gap between left atrial appendage closure and direct oral anticoagulation and are willing to take the antithrombotic regimen recommended after the procedure.

Left atrial appendage closure might be considered as stroke prevention strategy even for very selected patients who are rigidly drug therapy averse, provided that patients are adequately informed regarding the current evidence gap between left atrial appendage closure and direct oral anticoagulation and are willing to take the antithrombotic regimen recommended after the procedure.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia and a major cause of morbidity and mortality because of the risk of cardioembolic stroke. 1 Oral anticoagulation (OAC) reduces risk of ischemic stroke by almost 70% and represents the first‐line prevention therapy. 2 , 3 However, since OAC increases the risk of bleeding, patients and caregivers are sometimes reluctant to use this therapy. Several large real‐life studies showed that a significant percentage of patients with AF do not regularly take OAC. 4 , 5 The reasons for this undertreatment include history or risk of major bleeding or opposition to chronic use of medications. The latter could be related to medication costs (especially in countries where costs are not fully reimbursed), recurrence of minor side effects not bleeding related, or merely refusal of chronic therapy.

Randomized studies comparing left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) with vitamin K antagonists showed comparable therapeutic efficacy between the 2 therapies. 6 However, direct OAC (DOAC) have become the preferred oral anticoagulants due to a more favorable risk–benefit profile compared with vitamin K antagonists 7 Accordingly, in the latest guidelines for management of AF, LAAC has been allocated a Class IIa and IIb indication (in the American and European Guidelines, respectively) among patients with AF and history of severe bleeding due to a nonreversible cause. 3 , 8 However, no recommendations have been provided about patients with AF with OAC adherence issues, since such a population has never been investigated.

We therefore assessed the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of the LAAC procedure in a multicenter cohort of patients with AF who refused or were unable to take OAC despite medical recommendations. Furthermore, we compared the clinical outcomes observed in this population with those observed in a matched population with AF submitted to LAAC due to an established clinical indication as contraindication or failure of OAC.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

Four European high‐volume centers were involved in this retrospective analysis: Bern University Hospital (UH) (Switzerland), Aarhus UH (Denmark), Bonn UH (Germany), and Charleroi UH (Belgium). All LAAC procedures performed in the above centers between January 2009 and December 2022 were considered for this analysis. All participating centers identified and provided data on any LAAC procedures in patients with OAC refusal as main indication (refusal group). This group included patients refusing to take OAC lifelong despite medical recommendations and disclosure of potential risks of LAAC. All participating centers provided data on 2 corresponding control cases (control group) for each refusal case. Control cases were collected by identifying non‐refusal cases temporally adjacent to the respective refusal procedures to mitigate selection bias as possible. The study was approved by an institutional review committee at each participating center, and the subjects gave informed consent.

Study Data and Outcomes

The main indications for LAAC, baseline characteristics, antithrombotic therapy, and investigations before LAAC were collected. Left atrial appendage anatomy parameters as locally assessed by transesophageal echocardiography, LAAC procedural characteristics, and antithrombotic therapy at discharge were reported. 9 Data related to the transesophageal echocardiography follow‐up were collected. Clinical follow‐up was recorded from 1 year after procedure up until the last known follow‐up available.

The 2 study groups were compared in terms of 4 outcomes including the following: (1) composite of cardiovascular death, stroke, or systemic embolisms; (2) major bleedings (Bleeding Academic Research Consortium 3–5); (3) LAAC technical success (defined as the successful implantation of LAAC device without a residual peri‐device leak >3 mm); and (4) procedural complications including death, stroke, or major bleeding occurring within 7 days after LAAC.

The rates of thromboembolic (including stroke, transient ischemic attack, and systemic embolisms) and major bleeding events observed during the follow‐up of the refusal group patients were compared with those expected by the CHA2DS2Vasc and HASBLED Scores, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD or median and interquartile range as appropriate, and categorical variables as a percentage. Baseline and procedural characteristics were compared using Student t tests, Mann–Whitney, or χ2 test as appropriate. Clinical event rates were estimated using the Cox proportional hazard model, adjusted according to several variables including age, sex, CHA2DS2Vasc, and hypertension, abnormal renal/liver function, stroke, bleeding history or predisposition, labile International Normalized Ratio, elderly, drugs/alcohol concomitantly (HASBLED) Scores. Estimates of the adjusted hazard ratios (adjHR) and 95% CI for each variable are presented. The rates of thromboembolic events or major bleedings observed in refusal patients were compared with those expected by their CHA2DS2Vasc and HASBLED Scores, respectively. 10 , 11 The expected thromboembolic event rate was calculated for each refusal patient based on his/her CHA2DS2VASc score, the 1‐year thromboembolic event rate reported by previous publication 12 for patients with AF with similar CHA₂DS₂‐VASc score, times the follow‐up duration of that patient. Then, these expected individual rates were summed across all refusal patients and divided by the summed years under observation of all refusal patients to yield the expected number of events per patient‐year at risk. The Standardized Event Ratios with 95% CI were derived using indirect standardization as previously described. 12 The same approach was adopted with bleedings taking into account the HASBLED Score and the relative main publication. 11 A statistical significance threshold of 0.05 was accepted for hypothesis testing. IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 was used.

RESULTS

Study Population

A total of 2649 LAAC procedures prospectively and consecutively collected in the participating centers between January 2009 and December 2022 were reviewed (Figure 1). Based on the main indication for the LAAC procedure, 119 patients were assigned to the refusal group. Among them, DOACs were refused by 67 patients (56.3%), while vitamin K antagonists were refused by 33 patients (27.7%). In the remaining 19 patients (16%), the specific type of OAC refused could not be determined. The control group consisted of 238 patients and were selected by the investigators based on the time of LAAC procedure temporally adjacent to the respective patients who refuse OAC. The main clinical indications to LAAC in the control group included history of major bleeding (160 [70.5%]) and high bleeding risk without history of bleeding (67 [28.1%]), history of thromboembolic events and/or left atrial appendage thrombus despite OAC (8 [3.4%]), or others (3 [1.3%]).

Refusal patients were younger (68.1±10.1 versus 75.0±8.6; P <0.001) and with a significantly lower percentage of arterial hypertension (80% versus 88%; P=0.039), diabetes (15% versus 26%; P=0.022) or chronic kidney dysfunction (9% versus 19%; P=0.014) as compared with control patients. Procedural characteristics are summarized in Table 2. A transesophageal echocardiography evaluation was performed before and/or during the procedure in the vast majority of patients (300 [84%]). The left atrial appendage morphological parameters did not differ between the 2 groups, with one‐fifth of LAAs having a chicken wing shape (58 [20%]). Thrombus was detected in less than one‐twentieth of overall cases with no difference between the 2 groups (3% versus 4%; P=0.757). Procedural Outcomes

The LAAC device was successfully implanted in the vast majority of procedures in both groups (95% versus 98%; P=0.191) (Table 2). The composite of procedural complications did not differ between the 2 groups (1.7% versus 4.6%; adjusted risk ratio: 0.56; 95% CI, 0.13–2.43; P=0.440). The majority of procedural complications components were numerically albeit not significantly in favor of the refusal group, with no death or stroke and only 2 major bleedings (1.7%) observed in the latter group (Table 3).

Clinical and Imaging Follow‐Up

At a mean follow‐up of 3 years (1131 days), the composite of cardiovascular death, stroke, or systemic embolism occurred in 46 patients, of which 5 (4.2%) occurred in the refusal and 41 (17.2%) in the control groups (adjHR, 0.37 [95% CI, 0.14–0.99]; P=0.048) (Table 3), with a difference driven by non‐procedure‐related events (Figure 2). The rate of stroke (1.7% versus 5.5%; adjHR 0.22 [95% CI, 0.04–1.19]; P=0.078) trended lower in the refusal as compared with the control group. The rates of cardiovascular death and major bleeding did not differ between the 2 groups (2.5% versus 13.1%, adjHR 0.41[ 95% CI, 0.12–1.38]; P=0.148); 5.0% versus 10.5%, adjHR 0.44 [95% CI, 0.17–1.18]; P=0.104). Consistently, at a mean follow‐up of 4 months (133 days), a total of 290 (81%) patients underwent a transesophageal echocardiography assessment and the rate of device‐related thrombus or peri‐device leak did not differ between the 2 study groups (6% versus 3%; P=0.193; 18% versus 17%; P=0.872).

Observed and Expected Events

The observed annualized rate of thromboembolic events was 2.3%/y, which translates into a 62% risk reduction compared with the 5.9%/y predicted by baseline CHA2DS2VASc score (3.4±1.8) for patients with AF without anticoagulants (Table 4). The mean HASBLED score of study population was 2.2±1.1 (annual risk for bleeding 3.6%). The annual major bleeding rate observed after LAAC was 1.9% (1.2% after excluding procedural events), which corresponds to a 47% (65% in nonprocedural events) major bleeding risk reduction.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective observational multicentre study, including 119 patients submitted to LAAC due to refusal to adhere to OAC, shows the following main findings:

In European routine clinical practice, OAC refusal despite medical recommendations appears as an infrequent indication to perform LAAC.

In European routine clinical practice, OAC refusal despite medical recommendations appears as an infrequent indication to perform LAAC.

Patients submitted to LAAC due to OAC refusal are patients with AF with significantly lower risk as compared with those submitted to LAAC with more established indications (ie, mainly high bleeding risk). However, both populations were treated with a similar technique and achieved comparable procedural outcomes.

Patients submitted to LAAC due to OAC refusal are patients with AF with significantly lower risk as compared with those submitted to LAAC with more established indications (ie, mainly high bleeding risk). However, both populations were treated with a similar technique and achieved comparable procedural outcomes.

At 3 years after LAAC, in the refusal group the composite of stroke or systemic embolism trended lower as compared with the control group, and was similar to that reported by DOAC trials.

There are several clinical indications for LAAC reported by recent consensus documents. 13 In some patients, even different reasons to close the left atrial appendage might coexist. In our retrospective analysis of consecutive LAAC cases performed in 4 European centers, the main indication for LAAC procedure was reviewed and was represented by failure to accept/take OAC in 4.5% of procedures. This observation is in line with what was observed in previous large prospective studies. 14 , 15 In the Amulet IDE trial, the authors reported that 4.7% of randomized patients underwent LAAC due to “patient's preference/lifestyle.” 14 A higher percentage (17%) was reported in the Amulet Global Registry. 15 However, this LAAC subgroup also included patients with AF who underwent LAAC after experiencing a stroke despite OAC. 15 The reasons why patients are not willing to take OAC are various and involve fear of side effects, cost, and impact on lifestyle. 16 In our analysis, due to its retrospective design, the reason of OAC refusal was not systematically collected and included low tolerance of non‐bleeding‐related side effects in approximately one‐fifth of cases. However, since a relevant percentage of study patients was enrolled in countries without a free national healthcare system, we cannot exclude that the economic aspect played a role in the patient's choice.

Currently, the vast majority of patients undergoing LAAC consists of patients with AF with several comorbidities and high risk of bleeding and/or stroke. In the latest randomized clinical trials conducted on LAAC, study patients had high percentages of history of relevant bleeding (70%–90%) or stroke (25%–40%). 14 , 17 , 18 As a consequence, high rates of mortality at 1 to 3 years after procedure were observed. 18 , 19 Our study is the first to describe the baseline characteristics and long‐term clinical outcomes of a minority of patients with LAAC undergoing intervention because of refusal or failure to take OAC. The particularity of this LAAC population is the relatively lower risk profile and the better long‐term clinical outcomes as compared with patients undergoing LAAC for more established indications (eg, high risk or history of bleeding). The lower rates of hard clinical outcomes observed in the refusal as compared with the control groups might corroborate the common believe that the different stroke and bleeding rates observed between LAAC and OAC trials are explained by the different risk profile of patients included. 7 , 14 In this regard, it is not surprising that the stroke rate observed in our control group (5.5% at 3 years) was similar to that reported at the 3‐year follow‐up of the largest randomized clinical trial so far performed on LAAC (5.3%), whereas the rate observed in our refusal group (0.5% per year) was more in line with the annual event rate reported by DOAC trials with similar risk populations (1.0%–1.2% per year). 20 , 21

Finally, the annual rates of thromboembolisms or major bleedings observed after refusal procedures did not significantly differ from those estimated by relative risk scores. However, this observation should be taken with caution due to the small sample size and the limited prediction values of these score. Furthermore, the recently published OPTION trial, including 1600 patients with AF with CHA2DS2VA Score ≥2 and submitted to a transcatheter AF ablation, showed that LAAC was noninferior to DOAC in terms of composite of overall death, stroke, or systemic embolism at 36 months after randomization (5.3% LAAC versus 5.8% DOAC; P <0.001 for noninferiority). 22 The ongoing Champion‐AF (clinicaltrial.gov‐ NCT04394546) and Catalyst (clinicaltrial.gov‐ NCT04226547) trials, randomizing patients with AF with a CHA2DS2VA Score≥2 to either undergoing LAAC (followed by maximum 3 months of OAC or dual antiplatelet therapy) or continuing DOAC, will provide us a definitive answer about the noninferiority of LAAC to DOAC in terms of thromboembolisms. Until the main results of the above 2 studies are available, management and shared decision‐making when considering LAAC in patients with AF refusing OAC remain challenging. The results of the current study suggest that LAAC might be considered a stroke prevention strategy even for very selected patients who are rigidly drug therapy averse, provided that the patient is adequately informed regarding the current evidence gap comparing LAAC to DOAC and is willing to take the antithrombotic regimen recommended after the procedure (no refusal patient was discharged without antithrombotic therapy).

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. The observational and retrospective design of the study may have introduced bias and unmeasured confounders. The analysis of clinical events was adjusted including a limited number of variables due to the small number of events. However, these variables included multiparameters score encompassing the vast majority of the variables not well balanced between the 2 study groups. A propensity‐matched score analysis was not possible for defining the control group due to the data unavailability of the whole LAAC cohorts treated at the participating centers. The main indications for LAAC, imaging, and clinical outcomes were investigator‐reported and not independently adjudicated. However, each participating center was provided with clear definitions of each variable before starting the data collection, including how to define the main indication for LAAC. Centers included in this study were those reporting at least 5 refusal patients treated with LAAC during their cumulative experience, leading to potential overestimation of this LAAC subpopulation and selection bias with respect to the centers reported. There was considerable variability in the LAAC technique between the participating centers, especially regarding the imaging used to plan/guide LAAC with half of procedures performed without echocardiography guidance and so with a potential overestimation of the procedural complications. 23 , 24 Yet, no difference in terms of LAAC technique was observed between the 2 study groups. Half of the procedures were performed with implantation of old‐generation devices (ACP or Watchman 2.5), limiting the broad applicability of these results to the current LAAC devices. 25 A significantly higher percentage of patients was discharged with a limited duration of OAC in the refusal as compared with the control group, because the latter reportedly had a higher bleeding risk precluding the use of OAC after LAAC. 26 , 27 , 28 Finally, the small size of the study population may limit the generalizability of the results in terms of feasibility and safety. However, observed rates of procedure‐related complications and technical success are in line with those reported by the largest LAAC trials so far conducted. 14 , 22 , 29

CONCLUSIONS

In this multicenter cohort of consecutive patients with AF submitted to LAAC, OAC refusal appeared as an infrequent indication. In this subgroup, LAAC was confirmed to be feasible and safe. The ischemic outcomes rates at 3 years suggest a good efficacy of LAAC in preventing stroke in these patients. Results of ongoing trials comparing LAAC with OAC are needed to better inform patients with AF about the potential effects of preferring LAAC over OAC.
