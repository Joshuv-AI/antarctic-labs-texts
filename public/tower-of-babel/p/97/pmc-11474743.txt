Exploration of the relationship between general health-related problems and subclinical coronary artery disease: a cross-sectional study in a general population
Authors: Moniek Y Koopman, M Yldau van der Ende, Jorn J W Reijnders, Robert T A Willemsen, Rykel van Bruggen, Jan Willem C Gratama, Bastiaan L J H Kietselaer, Pim van der Harst, Rozemarijn Vliegenthart
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore associations between general health-related problems and subclinical coronary artery disease (CAD), determined by CT coronary calcium score (CT-CCS), in a general population.DesignA cross-sectional design.SettingThis study was performed in a prospective population-based cohort, examining the health and health-related behaviour of individuals living in the Northern Netherlands.ParticipantsThe initial cohort comprised 6763 participants ≥45 years of age who underwent CT-scanning. Participants were included for the current analysis if they filled in three validated questionnaires (Symptomatic Checklist-90, Research and Development Survey-36 and Reviving the Early Diagnosis of CardioVascular Diseases questionnaire (RED-CVD)) and did not have a history of cardiovascular disease. The final analysis included 6530 participants.Primary outcome measureBackward-stepwise and forward-stepwise logistic regression analyses were performed to determine associations between general health-related problems and subclinical CAD (CCS≥100 and ≥300).ResultsThe median age was 53 years (25th, 75th percentile: 48, 58); 57% were women. CRCS≥100 was found in 1236 (19%) participants, 437 (12%) in women and 799 (29%) men and CCS≥300 in 643 (9.9%) participants of which 180 (4.8%) were women and 463 (16.6%) men. Therefore, using health-related symptoms does not seem useful to pre-select for CT-CCS.Trial registration numberCCMO Register, NL17981.042.07 and NL58592.042.16.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is one of the leading causes of death worldwide.1 The number of individuals with CAD, and its impact on society, is expected to increase in coming years.2 3 Early recognition of the presence of subclinical CAD can help to initiate early preventive interventions, which could prevent future cardiovascular events.46

Chest pain is the most common symptom for the presence of CAD in both women and men, and a common reason to consult a (primary care) physician.7 Rose questionnaire identified typical chest pain symptoms in population-based studies and showed an association with CAD, defined by a coronary calcium score (CCS)≥100.8 However, research has shown that CAD and even obstructive CAD can be present in individuals with atypical or non-specific problems.7 9 10 For example, in the Prospective Observational Longitudinal Registry of Patients with Stable Coronary Artery Disease (CLARIFY) registry, 12.2% of cardiovascular-related events occurred in patients without prior chest pain symptoms.11 As chest pain is not associated with the presence of subclinical CAD, an early manifestation of atherosclerosis, it is of importance to investigate how subclinical CAD can be identified in women and men in a general population for primary prevention. The latest guideline on cardiovascular prevention of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) state that CT-CCS is ‘the best-established imaging modality to improve cardiovascular risk stratification’. However, it is noted that the CCS does not provide direct information on total plaque burden, and that cost-effectiveness of large-scale CT-screening needs further study.12 A recent study found that subclinical CAD, defined by a CCS of ≥100 based on CT, can be identified when a population is selected for pre-screening based on classic cardiovascular risk factors (body mass index ≥30 kg/m2, hypercholesterolaemia, hypertension, diabetes, smoking and positive family history of CAD). However, in order to detect 23% of individuals with subclinical CAD almost three-quarters of the general population (73%) would have to undergo a CT-scan.13 Cost-effectiveness of such an approach is highly unlikely. Pre-screening based on general health-related symptoms could provide a strategy to pre-select individuals for CT-CCS and improve the cost-utility of CT-CCS screening in a general population. For example, psychosocial problems (eg, stress, anxiety, depression) and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) have previously been associated with cardiovascular-related events.9 14 15 Furthermore, determining the presence of general health-related problems does not require more burdensome tests (eg, laboratory tests), requested by a physician and can be assessed at home at little costs.4 14

More than two decades ago, no association was found between psychological factors and subclinical CAD, however, the association was researched in a predominant young (39–45 years) male US military population.16 Subsequent studies explored potential associations between anxiety and depressive symptom and CCS, yielding mixed results.17 18 As of yet, there is no conclusive evidence on the relationship between broad-spectrum health-related problems and the presence of subclinical CAD and the differences between women and men. Therefore, the aim of this study is to explore associations between general health-related problems and subclinical CAD, identified by CCS, in women and men of a general population.

Method

Lifelines and ImaLife cohort studies

For this study, data from the ongoing Imaging in Lifelines (ImaLife) cohort were used. Extensive descriptions of Lifelines and ImaLife have been previously published.19 20 In short, Lifelines is a multidisciplinary prospective population-based cohort study examining in a unique three-generation design the health and health-related behaviour of 167 729 persons living in the North of the Netherlands. It employs a broad range of investigative procedures in assessing the biomedical, socio-demographic, behavioural, physical and psychological factors which contribute to the health and disease of the general population, with a specific focus on multimorbidity and complex genetics.19 Extensive questionnaires regarding the broad range of health-related topics were administered at baseline (2006–2013) and every 1.5 years after baseline. In addition, physical and laboratory measurements (eg, blood, urine, blood pressure) were also obtained at baseline and every 5 years thereafter.19 Lifelines participants who completed the second visit and who were ≥45 years of age were invited to the substudy ImaLife. ImaLife uses state-of-the-art CT-scanning procedures to study early imaging biomarkers of cardiovascular disease, lung cancer and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.20 Written informed consent was given by all Lifelines and ImaLife participants and both studies were approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of the University Medical Center Groningen (2007/152 and 2016/436) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.19

Participants

Individuals were included in the current study if they received a CT-scan in the ImaLife study between 2017 and 2019.20 Individuals were excluded if they had a self-reported history of heart failure, stroke, narrowed carotid arteries, myocardial infarction, coronary angioplasty or bypass surgery, based on questionnaire results of the second visit.

Coronary calcium scoring

A low dose non-contrast cardiac CT-scan was performed with a third-generation dual-source CT scanner (SOMATOM Force, Siemens, Germany), using ECG triggering at 60% of the RR interval (the time between two consecutive R-wave peaks). Tube voltage was set at 120 kVp and tube current at 64 quality reference mAs/rot. Images were reconstructed with a slice thickness of 3.0 mm. The CCS was quantified by semi-automated coronary artery calcium software (CaSc, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) using Agatston’s method.21

To determine the presence of subclinical CAD, two CCS cut-off values were used: CCS≥100 and ≥300. According to the ACC/AHA guideline 2019 (American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association) a CCS≥100 can be used as an indicator of increased CAD risk and as a starting point for the use of preventive interventions.6 The use of CCS≥300 was recommended in the ESC guideline in 2016.22 In the latest ESC guideline (2021) CCS is described as ‘the best imaging modality to improve cardiovascular risk stratification’, but an explicit CCS cut-off value was not mentioned.12 Nonetheless, previous research showed that CCS≥300 is a more optimal cut-off value than CCS≥400 for identifying high risk categories of CAD.12 17 23 24

General health-related questionnaires

Three validated questionnaires were used to determine the presence of general health-related problems (online supplemental table 1): the Symptomatic Checklist (SCL-90), the Research and Development Survey (RAND-36) and Reviving the Early Diagnosis of CardioVascular Diseases questionnaire (RED-CVD).4 25 26 The SCL-90 is a multidimensional questionnaire, consisting of nine categories of which one, the somatisation category, was used in this study. The questions in the somatisation category give insight into the experienced physical distress in the past 7 days, using a 5-point Likert scale (0=‘not at all’ to 4=‘extremely’). Scores range between 0 and 48 where a higher sum score indicates higher physical distress.27 With the RAND-36 questionnaire the HRQoL is measured using 36-items divided over eight categories and calculated separately for each category. The extent of the experienced HRQoL is determined using 5-point and 3-point Likert scales. Scores range between 0 and 100, where a score of 50 or less is associated with impaired HRQoL and a score of 100 with optimal HRQoL.14 26 The RED-CVD questionnaire is part of the ongoing RED-CVD trial performed in primary care, and pre-selects individuals to undergo further diagnostic screening for CAD, heart failure (HF) and atrial fibrillation (AF).28 The questionnaire was created using the baseline assessment of Lifelines4 and consists of 11 items. Scores range between 0 and 50, where a sum score of ≥20 indicates the need for further diagnostic screening by a physician.4

Cardiovascular risk factors

Cardiovascular risk factors were derived from Lifelines data, collected at the second visit.19 Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure of ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure of ≥90 mm Hg or the use of antihypertensive medication. Hypercholesterolaemia was defined as total cholesterol of ≥6.5 mmol/L or the use of cholesterol-lowering medication. Additionally, low-density lipoprotein (cut-off value ≥3.0 mmol/L), high-density lipoprotein (cut-off value ≤1.0 mmol/L for men and ≤1.2 mmol/L for women) and triglyceride levels (cut-off value ≥1.7 mmol/L) were available. Diabetes mellitus and rheumatoid arthritis were based on self-report or reported medication use for these diseases. Body mass index (BMI; kg/m2) and waist-to-hip ratio (waist circumference/hip circumference) were calculated. Smoking status was categorised as smoking (current and former smokers) and non-smoking. Smoking status was defined by using the following self-reported questions: ‘do you smoke cigarettes or roll-up cigarettes?’ and ‘have you stopped smoking?’.

Statistical analyses

Continuous, normally distributed, variables are presented with mean and SD and categorical variables as percentages. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median (25th to 75th percentile). Categorical variables and the CCS cut-off values were compared using the χ2 test. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed continuous variables in order to determine differences between women and men. Statistical significance was determined as a p value<0.05. For the association between questionnaire items, cardiovascular risk factors and CCS, backward-stepwise multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed for women and men separately. Cut-off value was p=0.10 for entry and p=0.05 for removal. Forward-stepwise logistic regression was performed to validate the model, of which the cut-off and removal was set at p value of 0.05. Bonferroni correction was performed to adjust for type 1 error (false positives) due to multiple testing (α/number of performed test). Therefore, the remaining items from the multivariate logistic regression, with a p value≤0.001 were considered as clinically significant. Lastly, results from the regression analyses were reported with p value and when significant, ORs and 95% CIs. Linear regression analysis and backward-stepwise multivariate regression analysis were performed, for women and men separately, for associations between the total scores of the questionnaires and cardiovascular risk factors. Cut-off value was p=0.10 for entry and p=0.05 for removal. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata IC V.13, StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA.

Patient and public involvement

There was no structural involvement of the public in setting up the Lifelines cohort. However, Lifelines does have a participant advisory board that is consulted periodically and receives reports regarding new developments within the field of research. Furthermore, Lifelines have an extensive website where participants can find information, a quarterly newsletter with extensive information on current affairs and a ‘service office’ to answer questions and comments from participants.

Coronary calcium score

The CCS distribution is presented in figure 1. The median CCS for the total cohort was 0 (IQR: 0–50.4). A CCS≥100 was present in 19% (n=1236/6530) of the total population, in 12.0% (n=437/3744) of women and in 29.0% (n=799/2786) of men. Corresponding percentages for CCS≥300 were 9.9% (n=643/6530), 4.8% (n=180/3744) and 16.6% (n=463/2786), respectively.

Questionnaire outcomes

In online supplemental table 1, the total scores and the scores of each individual questionnaire item are shown. The experienced physical health of the total cohort, determined by the SCL-90 questionnaire, ranged from ‘slightly distressed’ to ‘distressed’ and showed slightly higher score in women than in men (p<0.001), indicative of slightly more experienced physical health distress by women. The largest difference in individual scores between women and men was identified for the ‘painful muscles’ item, with a higher median score in women, however, the difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.059). Regarding the RAND-36 questionnaire, the total scores of the eight categories ranged from 70 to 100 (‘slightly impaired’ to ‘optimal’ HRQoL). A slightly impaired HRQoL was found for the ‘general health’ category, showing a slightly higher experienced HRQoL score in women than in men (p=0.03). An impaired HRQoL for women and men was determined for the following individual items: limitations while performing vigorous activities, the expectation of health worsening, personal rating of general health and rating of general health compared with the year before. Lastly, the median total score of the RED-CVD questionnaire was higher than the sum score of 20 (median, 23, 25th/75th percentile, 15–32) for the entire population, indicating that further diagnostic screening for CAD, HF and AF would be advised in a considerable part of the population (66.7%). Significant differences were found by sex (p<0.001): in men (77.8%), further diagnostic screening would be advised2034 in contrast to women (58.4%, 20, 11–23).

Cardiovascular risk factors

In women and men, the presence of cardiovascular risk factors was associated with CCS≥100 (table 2), with the strongest association in women for hypertension (OR=3.55, 95% CI: 2.89 to 4.36, p<0.001) and in men for diabetes (OR=3.88, 95% CI: 2.66 to 5.67, p<0.001). In the multivariate logistic model, age, hypertension and hypercholesterolaemia remained associated with CCS≥100. Similar associations were seen for CCS≥300 (tables2 3). Additional linear regression and backward-stepwise multivariate regression analysis were conducted to explore the relationship between the total questionnaire scores and cardiovascular risk factors. Both analyses showed that diabetes, smoking and BMI were significantly correlated with the total SCL-90 score, whereas age showed a slightly negative correlation with the total SCL-90 score in the multivariate model (online supplemental tables 2a,b and 3). Additionally, all cardiovascular risk factors in women and men were significantly correlated with the total RED-CVD score (online supplemental tables 4a,b). In the multivariate model, age, diabetes, smoking and BMI in women and age, hypertension, diabetes, smoking and BMI in men showed a positive correlation with the total RED-CVD score (online supplemental table 5). Varying cardiovascular risk factors showed predominantly negative correlations with the total scores of the RAND-36 questionnaire categories (online supplemental tables 6a,b and 7). Cardiovascular risk factors showed the strongest correlations with total scores of the ‘physical functioning’ and ‘general health’ category.

General health-related problems and coronary calcium score ≥100

In the univariate logistic regression model in women (table 2), associations with CCS≥100 were found for two RED-CVD questionnaire items and one RAND-36 questionnaire item; the expectancy of health worsening (OR=1.13; 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.21, p=0.001), reduced exercise intolerance (OR=1.09; 95% CI: 1.01 to 1.17, p=0.018) and the total score of the emotional well-being category (OR=1.02; 95% CI: 1.01 to 1.03, p=<0.001), respectively. Experiencing limitations in performing vigorous activities and in walking more than 1 km showed minimally lower odds for the presence of CCS≥100 in women (OR=0.99; 95% CI: 0.98 to 0.99 for both, p<0.001 and p=0.001, respectively). In the multivariate logistic regression model (table 3), none of the health-related items showed an association with CCS≥100.

In men, in the univariate model (table 2), an association with CCS≥100 was found for reduced exercise intolerance (OR=1.14; 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.23, p<0.001) of the RED-CVD questionnaire. Experiencing limitations in bending, kneeling or stooping (OR=0.99; 95% CI: 0.98 to 0.99, p=0.001) showed minimally lower odds for CCS≥100 and being very nervous (OR=1.01; 95% CI: 1.00 to 1.01, p<0.001) showed a minimally elevated OR with CCS≥100 (table 2). As in women, in the multivariate logistic regression model (table 3) none of the health-related items were associated with CCS≥100 in men.

General health-related problems and coronary calcium score ≥300

In women, in the univariate model (table 2), an association with CCS≥300 was found for the total RED-CVD score (OR=1.06, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.08, p<0.001). In the multivariate logistic regression model (table 3), an association with CCS≥300 was found for the ‘general health’ category of the RAND-36 questionnaire (OR=1.92, 95% CI: 1.56 to 2.37, p<0.001).

Also in men, associations with CCS≥300 concerned items derived from the RED-CVD questionnaire: reduced exercise intolerance (OR=1.15, 95% CI: 1.0 to 1.25, p=0.001) and the total RED-CVD score (OR=1.07, 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.09, p<0.001). The presence of headache showed reduced odds for the presence of CCS≥300 (OR=0.55; 95% CI: 0.38 to 0.79, p=0.001). In the multivariate model (table 3), none of the health-related items were associated with CCS≥300.

Discussion

This large general population study explored the possible association between general health-related problems and subclinical CAD, defined as of CCS≥100 and ≥300, respectively, in women and men. In univariate analysis, some health-related items of the questionnaires were weakly associated with subclinical CAD. After correction for cardiovascular risk factors and multiple testing, only general health expectation in women showed increased (nearly doubled) odds of CCS≥300.

CT-CCS has proven to be a very sensitive test for subclinical CAD in (a)symptomatic populations, and a predictor of cardiovascular events beyond classical risk factors.29 This study shows that subclinical CAD (CCS≥100) is present in a substantial part of the general population (19%). These individuals could possibly benefit from the early detection of subclinical CAD and subsequent initiation of (non-)medical treatment to prevent for cardiovascular events.12 30 The ongoing ROBINSCA trial (the Dutch Risk Or Benefit IN Screening for Cardiovascular disease) will provide evidence on the benefit of early detection of subclinical CAD in a large general population (n=43 447). It aims to investigate the effectiveness of cardiovascular risk screening using the SCORE (Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation) or CT-CCS and its influence on treatment and outcome.31 In addition, the EARLY-SYNERGY trial (early diagnosis of ischaemic heart disease to prevent acute myocardial infarction and sudden cardiac death) tests the presence of subclinical, obstructive CAD, determined by CCS≥300 and abnormal stress myocardial perfusion MRI, in an at-risk general population. It will test the hypothesis that subclinical obstructive CAD is not uncommon in the general at-risk population and that early detection of subclinical obstructive CAD improves cardiovascular-related outcomes.32 Yet, a major question remains: how can we select the appropriate at-risk population to justify performance of CT-CCS and thus avoid broad testing of many low-risk individuals without CAD?33 Home-based self-assessment, through questionnaires on health-related problems and e-Health, could be an alternative for the early identification of at-risk individuals who could benefit the most of early diagnosis and treatment.13

This current study included questionnaire results on a broad spectrum of health-related problems to determine a possible relationship with subclinical CAD. Previous studies investigated the relationship between psychosocial factors (anxiety, depression, anger, chronic stress and hostility) and subclinical CAD but found inconsistent results.16 17 34 For example, a meta-analysis focusing on the relationship between depression and CCS showed a positive association in 12 studies, no association in 10 studies and a negative association in 2 studies.17 Yet, underlying mechanisms could be involved in both subclinical CAD and psychological factors such as depression, for example, inability to regulate blood pressure, hypercortisolaemia and inflammation and should be addressed in future research.17 35 Furthermore, in our study we found that the general health expectation in women, as determined by the ‘general health’ category of the RAND-36, was associated with subclinical CAD (CCS≥300), in multivariate analysis. To our knowledge, this study is the first one that found this association in asymptomatic individuals of a general population. However, research performed in chest pain patients, showed that women with cardiovascular disease reported a lower general health expectancy compared with men.36

Overall, our results indicate that identifying subclinical CAD in individuals in a general population by only using general health-related items seems not very useful for (clinical) practice. The findings of this study suggest that general health-related problems are not (or only weakly) related to subclinical CAD. However, the absence of a relationship does not exclude the presence of subclinical CAD; it merely shows that sensitivity of these problems for elevated CT-CCS is poor. Our results underscore the need for further research into health-related problems and the presence of subclinical CAD, as questionnaires could serve as an easy home-based pre-screening tool (without measuring risk factors). However, this study showed that cardiovascular risk factors were better indicators of subclinical CAD, which is in line with prior research.13 In addition, the relationship between health-related problems and CT-CCS could possibly be explained by the relationship between the cardiovascular risk factors and general health problems. We found that cardiovascular risk factors were correlated with physical distress (SCL-90), with the possibility of undiagnosed HF, AF or CAD (RED-CVD) and with a decreased HRQoL (RAND-36). Furthermore, results from the RED-CVD questionnaire, designed for primary care, showed no significant association with an elevated CT-CCS. A positive RED-CVD score suggests the possible presence of undiagnosed HF, AF or CAD.4 It is plausible that individuals with a positive test result may have HF or AF rather than CAD, which could explain the lack of association with CCS≥100 in our study. Recent research in an at-risk population (patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and diabetes) showed the most notable increase in HF diagnosis using the RED-CVD questionnaire results.37 Even though CT-CCS testing is non-invasive, cheap and associated with low radiation, there are still barriers that limit the use of CCS as a pre-screening tool.38 The cost-effectiveness of pre-screening of the population with the presence of one or more cardiovascular risk factors, for example, is questionable, as a high number of individuals would have undergo CT-scanning.13 Also, there are no strong recommendations for the use of CCS in prevention guidelines.39 Future research is needed to gain more insight into determining which individuals could benefit the most from early detection of subclinical CAD and cost-effectiveness.

One of the strengths of this study is that a broad spectrum of general health-related items was included and that these items came from validated questionnaires. Second, state-of-the-art imaging was used for subclinical CAD evaluation. Third, a large, truly population-based cohort was studied. Lastly, we furthermore explored the differences in expressed health-related problems between women and men. Research in symptomatic patients has indicated that even though there is overlap in experienced chest pain problems between women and men, women often express their problems differently than men.40 41 This could also be an underlying reason why the overall experienced general health expectancy in women was associated with subclinical CAD. General health expectancy is based on self-perceived health which is influenced by the overall perception of both physical and psychological health and often reflects an underlying (possibly atherosclerotic) disease.42 This study also has some limitations. This study involved a population-based cohort where the presence of CCS≥100 and ≥300 was relatively low. Even though this cohort consisted for 57% of women, the number of women with a CCS≥300 was low. This could be a result of the fact that women develop CAD on average 8–10 years later than men, as well as the age distribution of the cohort.43 Furthermore, in the latest ESC guideline,12 CCS is highlighted as best-established imaging modality for risk stratification, but it is also noted that individuals with low CCS could have non-calcified plaque and that cost-effectiveness of CT-CCS screening remains undetermined.12 Previous research in Western and Asian populations shown that a CCS 0 indicates a very low CAD and major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) risk over a period of 5 years.4446 Further research is needed on cost-effectiveness of CT-CCS screening, although initial results are promising.47 This study has a cross-sectional design, and provides only a snapshots of associations without capturing the dynamic nature of subclinical coronary atherosclerosis. The questionnaire and CT-scan were performed as part of the same research round; hence no conclusions regarding causality between problems and CCS can be drawn. Importantly, the questionnaires were performed before the CT-scan and participants were unaware of their CCS. We recognise that using data from self-reported questionnaires could affect the reliability or results. Bias could arise from socially desirable answers, difficulties recalling information or misunderstanding the questions.48 These biases could have influenced the results, potentially leading to either underestimating or overestimating the relationship of problems to the CCS. In the context of this analysis, no tests were performed to assess the questionnaires reproducibility. However, previous research has shown test–retest values of the RAND-36 questionnaire between 0.58–0.82 after 2 months and 0.40–0.75 after 6 months, which was deemed satisfactory for detecting health status differences.49 Lifelines results demonstrated the performance of this questionnaire to distinguish between chronically impaired individuals and a healthy comparison group.50 The SCL-90 questionnaire showed a test–retest value of 0.82 after 1 week,51 and Lifelines results indicated its effectiveness in discriminating between individuals with and without somatic conditions.52 For the relatively new RED-CVD questionnaire, reproducibility still needs to be assessed. Lastly, the research population consisted of generally middle-aged Caucasian individuals. Therefore, the results could differ for an older population (≥60 years of age), as the presence and severity of coronary calcium increases with age,53 and for those with another ethnicity.

This large general population study shows that pre-selecting individuals for assessment of subclinical CAD based on only health-related problems does not seem useful, as associations between health-related problems and elevated CCS were poor. In fact, our study supports the paradigm that subclinical CAD is truly subclinical and that it is traced more likely by identifying classical risk factors, than by identifying general health-related problems. Future research should focus on determining the best (home-based) ways of identifying individuals who could benefit the most from early detection of subclinical CAD and should focus on the cost-effectiveness of early detection of subclinical CAD in a general population.
