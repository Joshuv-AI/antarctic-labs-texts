PlantSetDelta: Mining regulatory features to enable interpretable gene set analysis in plants
Authors: Bo Wang, Zhanxiang Zong, Shaochen Jia, Xu Han, Liang Guo, Hu Zhao
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Gene set analysis has become a fundamental strategy in genomics, offering insights into how groups of genes function in concert within biological processes. Classical approaches, such as Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (Ashburner et al., 2000) and gene set enrichment analysis (Subramanian et al., 2005), map gene sets to curated functional categories; however, their reliance on existing annotations limits their scope. These limitations are particularly pronounced in plants, where regulatory complexity and lineage-specific gene families complicate functional annotation. Moreover, most current approaches emphasize enrichment without considering regulatory determinants, such as sequence features or transcription factor (TF)-binding profiles.

Here, we introduce PlantSetDelta, an interpretable machine learning toolkit designed to systematically identify key regulatory features that distinguish biologically meaningful gene sets (Figure 1). By integrating TF-binding profiles with k-mer sequence patterns derived from promoter and terminator regions, the toolkit enables cross-gene and cross-species comparisons of regulatory signatures. It provides a generalizable framework for hypothesis generation, functional annotation, and insight into gene regulation in plant systems.

PlantSetDelta: An interpretable framework for comparison of gene sets using regulatory features.

(A) Overview. Users input biologically meaningful gene sets for comparison (e.g., two groups). PlantSetDelta integrates regulatory features, trains classifiers, and identifies key features that distinguish the sets. The features are ranked by their occurrence across multiple random seeds (top 10 by default), with ties resolved by mean normalized importance.

(B) Regulatory region definition. For each gene, the promoter (−1 to +0.5 kb relative to the TSS) and the terminator (−0.5 to +1 kb relative to the TTS) are used for feature extraction.

(C)k-mer–based classification. k-mer frequency profiles are calculated using promoter and terminator regions and assembled into sequence-derived feature matrices, which are used to train classifiers to discriminate between genes.

(D) TF-binding-based classification (with TF data). When TF-binding data are available in the target species, peaks are intersected with fixed 100-bp bins within promoter and terminator windows to construct a bin-level feature matrix for classification.

(E) Sequence-to-TF modeling and cross-species inference (without TF data). A DeeperDeepSEA model trained in Arabidopsis thaliana predicts TF-binding profiles directly from DNA sequences. This model is then applied to corresponding regions in Brassica napus, Oryza sativa, and Zea mays to generate predicted TF-binding features for the same classification task. Species-specific DeeperDeepSEA models trained in 10 plant species are also available.

(F) Application 1: scRNA-seq in A. thaliana. When comparing mesophyll marker genes with non-mesophyll markers, PlantSetDelta identifies DET1, a TF involved in photomorphogenesis and light signaling.

(G) Application 2: TWAS in B. napus. When comparing TWAS-significant and non-significant genes related to seed oil content (SOC), PlantSetDelta identifies a SOC-associated NAC TF.

(H) Additional application scenarios. Examples include identification of (1) regulatory features underlying DEGs in mutants, (2) consensus motifs in TF-bound genes, and (3) stress-associated regulatory features. The framework generalizes to any biologically meaningful gene set comparison.

Abbreviations: TSS, transcription start site; TTS, transcription termination site; TF, transcription factor; scRNA-seq, single-cell RNA sequencing; TWAS, transcriptome-wide association study; SOC, seed oil content; DEG, differentially expressed gene.

Because expression-level classification is a well-established and biologically informative task in gene regulation studies (Akagi et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024; Peleke et al., 2024; Washburn et al., 2019), we first evaluated the PlantSetDelta framework by distinguishing between high- and low-expression genes as a representative case study. Regulatory features, including 30-bin TF-binding profiles and k-mer frequencies, were extracted from promoter (−1 to +0.5 kb) and terminator (−0.5 to +1 kb) regions in Arabidopsis thaliana to capture local regulatory potential (Peleke et al., 2024) (Figure 1A–1D). TF-binding features achieved strong predictive performance for binary expression labels across multiple models, with the best models reaching accuracies above 0.8. These features also supported the prediction of continuous expression levels, with R2 around 0.3 (Supplemental Figure 1; Supplemental Tables 2 and 3). For k-mer features, benchmarking across different k values identified k = 7 as a suitable setting for subsequent analyses (Supplemental Figures 2A and 2B; Supplemental Tables 4 and 5). Using this setting, k-mer-based classification performed well across A. thaliana, leaf-specific A. thaliana datasets, Brassica napus, Oryza sativa, and Zea mays, achieving mean accuracies of 0.718, 0.737, and 0.747, respectively, in the latter three cases. These results further highlight informative sequence features associated with expression variation (Supplemental Figures 2C–2F and 3; Supplemental Tables 6–8).

k-mer features can be calculated de novo from each genome sequence without cross-species transfer; we sought to test whether a sequence-based TF-binding representation could be learned in one species with comprehensive TF data and then applied across species. Using the extensive TF-binding datasets available in A. thaliana, we trained a DeeperDeepSEA model to directly predict TF binding using promoter and terminator sequences (Supplemental Figures 4A–4C; Supplemental Table 9). We then applied this model to the corresponding regions of B. napus, O. sativa, and Z. mays to predict TF-binding features for each species. Using these features, we trained classifiers to distinguish between high- and low-expression genes in each species; this approach achieved consistently strong performance and identified the TF-binding bins most predictive of expression levels (Supplemental Figures 4D–4L; Supplemental Table 10). Predictive performance also varied across TF families (Supplemental Figure 5). In addition, we independently trained DeeperDeepSEA models in 10 plant species (Baumgart et al., 2025). These species-specific models are provided as resources for deriving TF-binding features in target or closely related species (Figure 1E; Supplemental Figure 6; Supplemental Table 11). We also extracted one-hot representations of promoter and terminator regions from A. thaliana, B. napus, O. sativa, and Z. mays and trained convolutional neural networks to classify genes according to their expression levels (Supplemental Figure 7).

Using TF-binding and k-mer features from A. thaliana, we trained multiple machine learning models with binary labels that indicate high- and low-expression genes to quantify the benefit of feature integration. Under a fixed classification setting, the combined features showed strong predictive power, with the best models achieving mean accuracies above 0.80 (Supplemental Figure 8; Supplemental Table 12). We further assessed the robustness of this conclusion under alternative regulatory region definitions (e.g., transcription start site-only, transcription termination site-only, and UTR-inclusive or UTR-exclusive windows) and observed broadly consistent performance patterns and feature rankings (Supplemental Figures 9 and 10; Supplemental Table 13). We also repeated the evaluation using sequence-similarity-aware and gene-family-aware training and test data splits, confirming that overall performance and prioritized features remained stable under these more stringent conditions (Supplemental Figures 11 and 12; Supplemental Tables 14 and 15). As an additional negative control, label permutation reduced performance to chance levels across models (Supplemental Figure 13).

We then formalized this approach into a generalizable pipeline capable of analyzing biologically meaningful gene sets—including TF-bound and unbound genes, up- and downregulated genes, stress-responsive genes, single-cell-derived cell-type marker genes, transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS)-significant and non-significant genes, and tissue- or organ-specific genes—to identify key regulatory features underlying their differences.

As a first application, we compared gene sets defined by WRKY42 binding (bound vs. unbound) in A. thaliana using k-mer features derived from promoter and terminator regions. The best-performing model achieved a mean accuracy of 0.816, and the top-ranked k-mers closely matched the canonical WRKY42 motif (Supplemental Figures 14A–14D; Supplemental Tables 16 and 17). We assembled hundreds of RNA-sequencing datasets from mutant studies and focused on dozens of TF-related perturbations. Across these datasets, regulator-associated signals were identified among the top-ranked features in 62% of cases (Supplemental Figure 14E). Representative examples included WRKY15 and KAN1; in these cases, the identified features included WRKY-family and KAN1-binding signatures, and the most important sequence features matched or were reverse complements of the corresponding motifs (Supplemental Figures 15A–15F; Supplemental Tables 18–21).

We next analyzed a single-cell RNA-sequencing dataset from A. thaliana (Figure 1F) to define mesophyll marker genes and a corresponding non-mesophyll gene set. DET1, a TF involved in photomorphogenesis and light signaling, frequently appeared among the top-ranked signals (Dong et al., 2014) (Supplemental Figures 16A and 16B; Supplemental Tables 22 and 23). Although GO enrichment was dominated by broad categories, such as transcription-related activity, kinase activity, and membrane-associated functions, the top-ranked regulatory features were associated with DET1 (Supplemental Figure 17A). We then compared up- and downregulated genes from A. thaliana drought stress data. The model separated the two groups and identified ABI5 among the top-ranked signals (Collin et al., 2020) (Supplemental Figures 16C and 16D; Supplemental Tables 24 and 25), whereas GO enrichment mainly reflected stress- and defense-related functions (Supplemental Figure 17B). For seed oil content TWAS in B. napus, comparison of TWAS-significant and non-significant genes revealed enrichment of NAC family features associated with oil-related traits (Tan et al., 2022) (Figure 1G; Supplemental Figures 16E and 16F; Supplemental Tables 26 and 27). Notably, the enriched GO terms did not directly include lipid metabolism but instead reflected broader processes such as transcriptional regulation, cell wall biogenesis, and lignin metabolism (Supplemental Figure 17C). Finally, in A. thaliana, we identified root-, seed-, and bud-specific genes and trained a three-class classifier to distinguish among them. ARR12, a regulator of root development, was among the top signals distinguishing the root-specific gene set (Xie et al., 2018) (Supplemental Figures 16G and 16H; Supplemental Tables 28 and 29).

In summary, our workflow demonstrates robustness across diverse, biologically meaningful gene sets and pinpoints regulatory features that may underlie their differences (Figure 1H). We implemented this workflow as PlantSetDelta, which provides precomputed TF-binding and k-mer features for A. thaliana, B. napus, O. sativa, and Z. mays, together with an A. thaliana-trained DeeperDeepSEA model for sequence-based TF-binding prediction. To enhance accessibility for non-expert users, we also designed a user-friendly web interface for running PlantSetDelta analyses and visualizing the results (https://rgmi.hzau.edu.cn/plantsetdelta). For other plant species, users can execute the full pipeline by supplying a reference genome and annotation (Supplemental Figure 18). Although binary encoding of TF-binding regions is retained as the default to ensure compatibility with standard BED-format datasets, we recommend prioritizing quantitative encoding when signal-intensity data (e.g., peak scores) are available, as this may improve predictive performance. Conceptually, PlantSetDelta adds a complementary, regulation-aware layer to gene set analysis: rather than stopping at functional enrichment, it integrates sequence patterns and TF-binding signals to explain why gene sets differ. Using this framework, researchers can identify previously unrecognized regulatory features that distinguish gene sets. This capability facilitates hypothesis generation, interpretation of transcriptomic variation, mechanistic prioritization of candidate regulators, and crop improvement applications in which regulatory insights can inform breeding strategies.
