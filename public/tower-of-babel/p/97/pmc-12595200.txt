Causal association between rheumatoid arthritis and pancreatic cancer: a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jujie Li, Hao Hua, Luyao Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveElucidating the causal relationship between rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and pancreatic cancer (PC) remains methodologically challenging due to limitations inherent in observational studies. This study applied a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) design to investigate the potential causal interplay between RA and PC, aiming to uncover shared pathogenic pathways and genetic predispositions through comprehensive instrumental variable analysis.MethodsUtilizing genome-wide association study (GWAS) meta-analysis data, we systematically screened and incorporated independent single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with RA and PC as instrumental variables. The primary analytical approach was the inverse-variance weighted fixed-effects model (IVW-FE). To ensure methodological rigor and validate causal inferences, we complemented this with several approaches: simple median, weighted median, and MR-Egger regression. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, and pleiotropy was evaluated using MR-Egger intercept analysis. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out approach. All effect estimates are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsSeventy-seven SNPs met the predefined inclusion criteria. MR analyses revealed that elevated genetic susceptibility to RA was causally associated with an increased risk of PC (OR = 1.187, 95% CI = 1.355–1.039, P = 0.011). MR-Egger regression analysis indicated no significant pleiotropic effects (intercept p = 0.434). Cochran’s Q statistics indicated no substantial heterogeneity in the causal estimates for RA (P = 0.064). Consistent with these findings, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses confirmed the absence of influential outlier variants in the instrumental variable sets. However, there was no significant causal association between PC and the risk of RA (OR = 0.991, 95% CI = 0.957–1.027, P = 0.620).ConclusionsOur results support a unidirectional causal relationship, identifying RA as a potential risk factor for the development of pancreatic cancer, with no evidence for a reverse causal effect.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03919-9.Keywords: Two-sample Mendelian randomization, Rheumatoid arthritis, Pancreatic cancer, Genetic variants, Risk factor

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is a highly aggressive malignancy of the digestive system characterized by insidious onset, rapid progression, and extremely poor therapeutic outcomes and prognosis [1]. Its global incidence and mortality rates are consistently rising [2]. Upon diagnosis, pancreatic cancer exhibits rapid dissemination and metastasis to adjacent organs and tissues [3]. Approximately 20% of patients may achieve extended survival through surgical resection, while over 80% develop metastatic disease, including peritoneal metastasis and malignant ascites formation, which significantly compromises patients’ quality of life [4]. However, the precise etiology and underlying molecular mechanisms of pancreatic carcinogenesis remain incompletely understood. Consequently, comprehensive investigation into the etiology of pancreatic cancer is of paramount importance for facilitating early diagnosis and developing effective therapeutic strategies.

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent inflammation, which may involve visceral organs in some patients [5]. RA is pathologically characterized by symmetrical, polyarticular synovial inflammation, primarily manifested as joint inflammation, pain, and functional impairment. Without prompt and effective therapeutic intervention, this condition progressively leads to the destruction of articular cartilage and bone tissue, subsequently resulting in joint deformity and permanent functional disability [6]. These progressive pathological alterations markedly deteriorate patients’ daily functioning and create considerable economic pressures on healthcare infrastructures. Emerging epidemiological evidence has demonstrated a significant association between RA and elevated cancer risk, particularly for site-specific malignancies including pulmonary neoplasms [7], hematologic malignancies [8], and cervical carcinoma [9]. Paradoxically, this autoimmune disorder appears to confer a protective effect against breast and colorectal malignancies [10]. Nevertheless, the potential causal relationship between RA pathogenesis and oncogenesis remains to be elucidated.

Mendelian randomization (MR) represents a robust analytical approach in epidemiological research for causal inference, utilizing genetic variants strongly associated with exposure factors as instrumental variables to assess causal relationships between exposures and outcomes [11]. The inherent nature of genetic variations, which are established at conception and maintain remarkable stability across the human lifespan, confers MR analyses with substantial advantages in minimizing biases from reverse causality and potential confounders when contrasted with traditional epidemiological approaches [12]. Notwithstanding their widespread application, traditional observational investigations are fundamentally constrained by inherent methodological limitations and demonstrate significant susceptibility to confounding bias. Specifically, observational designs struggle to fully address residual confounding and reverse causation where undetected pancreatic cancer might influence inflammatory markers years before diagnosis. These limitations impede definitive causal inference regarding the relationship between RA and PC. In light of these methodological challenges, our investigation implements a bidirectional two-sample MR design to elucidate the causal interplay between RA and PC. This robust analytical paradigm offers valuable perspectives for understanding the pathogenesis and informing clinical management of these comorbid conditions.

Method

Data sources

By searching the Open GWAS (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets) database for pooled genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on the association between RA and PC, two sets of GWAS data with relatively large sample sizes and a relatively high number of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were obtained, and then subjected to two-sample MR analysis. This study is reported as per the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) -MR guidelines [13].

Selection of genetic variants

Exposure-related genetic variation at the P < 5 × 10− 8 level was obtained from genome-wide (GWAS). The genetic variation must satisfy the three main assumptions of MR analysis (Fig. 1): (1) the association assumption, that the SNP is strongly associated with exposure; (2) the independence assumption, that the genetic variation is independent of confounders affecting ‘exposure and outcome’; and (3) the exclusivity assumption, that the genetic variation can only contribute to the outcome through exposure, and not by other means. The linkage disequilibrium parameter was set (r2 < 0.001), i.e., two SNPs were perfectly linkage-balanced (the assignment of these two SNPs was completely random), and the genetic distance was set at 10,000 kb to select SNPs to ensure their independence and to exclude the effect of linkage disequilibrium (LD) on the results. To ensure that the selected IVs have enough power for detecting the causal effect of exposure on the outcome, we calculated the F statistic of selected IVs with an online tool(https://sb452.shinyapps.io/overlap) [14]. Also, it was verified that the F-value of the screened SNPs was greater than 10 to ensure strong correlation. In addition, we searched the PhenoScanner V2 database (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac. uk) for all phenotypes associated with the instrumental variables for the two diseases separately, excluded SNPs associated with confounders (P < 10− 5), and then excluded SNPs associated with intermediate allelic returns and those associated with outcome SNPs that were highly correlated (P < 10− 8), and finally the remaining SNPs were used as instrumental variables in this study. MR analysis

Our study conducted various MR methods to confirm the causal relationship between RA and PC. IVW method was conducted as the primary method to estimate the causal effect between exposure and outcome, which was calculated as the effect size of the association between SNP and outcome divided by the effect size of the association between SNP and exposure. The MR-Egger regression method, the weighted mode method, and weighted median method were also used to estimate causal relationship. The weighted median method provides robust mendelian estimates even when 50% of instrument variables are invalid [15]. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were displayed to estimate relative risk caused by the presence of the disease of interest.

Statistical analysis

The MR method was used to test for horizontal multiplicity, and the leave-one-out method was used to analyse the sensitivity of the results. The I2 statistic was utilized to assess the heterogeneity of the associations from different data sources. Cochran Q statistic was used to evaluate the heterogeneity among SNPs’ estimates in each association. When it was P < 0.05, indicating heterogeneity, and it was necessary to use the IVW method of the random-effects model to further calculate. In addition, the P-value for the MR-Egger intercept less than 0.05 indicates the evidence of horizontal pleiotropic effects. the MR-PRESSO approach was also performed to detect and correct horizontal pleiotropic outliers for all reported results, and P-value for Global test less than 0.05 indicated the presence of horizontal pleiotropic outliers. MR-Egger regression which allows the intercept to be freely estimated as an indicator of average pleiotropic bias can identify some violations of the standard instrumental variable assumptions.

This two-sample MR analysis was performed using R software (version 4.1.2 R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.6) and MR-PRESSO packages (version 1.0.0). Results with P < 0.05 and power greater than 80% were considered statistically significant.

Results

The final instruments in the MR analyses

Through a rigorous selection process, SNPs associated with potential confounding factors were systematically excluded. Additionally, to ensure the robustness of the instrumental variables, one palindromic SNP (rs7278257) exhibiting intermediate allele frequencies were removed from the subsequent MR investigations. Following these stringent criteria, a final set of 77 high-quality SNPs was identified and retained for subsequent analysis. All the F-statistics for instruments were larger than the empirical threshold of 10, demonstrating the small possibility of weak instrumental variable bias. Comprehensive details regarding the associations between the selected SNPs and the respective exposures are presented in table S1.

Causal association between RA and PC

The IVW (Inverse-Variance Weighted) analysis revealed that, compared to individuals without RA, those genetically predicted to have RA exhibited a 18.7% increased risk of developing PC (OR = 1.187, 95% CI = 1.355–1.039, P = 0.011). Consistent findings were observed using the weighted mode method (OR = 1.306, 95% CI = 1.119–1.523, P = 0.001), the MR Egger (OR = 1.270, 95% CI = 1.024–1.575, P = 0.033), and the weighted median method (OR = 1.231, 95% CI = 1.018–1.489, P = 0.032), further supporting the association between RA and an elevated risk of PC (Table 1). The effect of each SNP locus on pancreatic cancer is shown in Fig. 2A, and scatter plot of the causal relationship is in Fig. 3A.

Causal association between PC and RA

Through a rigorous screening process, five SNPs were identified and utilized as instrumental variables in the MR analysis, with no observed associations with the confounding factor. All SNPs exhibited F-statistics exceeding 10, thereby effectively minimizing the potential for weak instrument bias. Additional details are summarized in table S2. The IVW-FE (OR = 0.991, 95% CI = 0.957–1.027, P = 0.620), the weighted median method (OR = 0.988, 95% CI = 0.945–1.032, P = 0.576), the MR Egger (OR = 0.953, 95% CI = 0.868–1.045, P = 0.378), and the weighted mode method (OR = 0.973, 95% CI = 0.911–1.038, P = 0.452) indicated no significant causal relationship between PC and an increased risk of RA (Table 1). The forest plot and scatter plot of the causal relationship between genetically predicted PC and the risk of RA are shown in Figs. 2B and 3B. Therefore, we concluded that no significant evidence supports a causal effect of PC on RA, though limited instrument strength may obscure small effects.

Sensitivity analysis

As shown in Table 2, Cochran’s Q test indicated no significant heterogeneity among the SNPs (Q = 95.566, P = 0.064). The MR-Egger intercept test (P = 0.434) confirmed the absence of horizontal pleiotropy, underscoring the robustness of the MR results. Figure 4A also illustrates negligible publication bias and non-directional horizontal pleiotropy. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis revealed that the exclusion of individual SNPs did not significantly impact the overall findings, as depicted in Fig. 5A. In the reverse MR analysis, sensitivity analyses demonstrated no heterogeneity among the SNPs (Cochran’s Q test, Q = 2.214, P = 0.697) and the MR-Egger intercept test (P = 0.430) found no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Table 2). Funnel plots also indicated the stability of the instrumental variables associated with RA and PC, as shown in Fig. 4B. Leave-one-out analysis, as presented in Fig. 5B, further validated the stability of the MR results. Based on comprehensive and thorough tests, the overall conclusions of our study were unlikely to be strongly influenced by bias.

Discussion

The association between RA and PC has attracted growing clinical interest, yet the genetic underpinnings of this relationship remain poorly understood. This MR study assessed their potential causal relationship. IVW analysis revealed a positive causal effect of RA on PC risk, supported by sensitivity analyses. This MR approach offers robust evidence for a causal influence of genetic susceptibility to RA on PC risk.

These findings align with a prior study that also reported a positive association between genetic liability to RA and PC risk [16]. The findings of this study are consistent with previous research. However, that study focused on East Asian populations, whereas ours examined Europeans with a larger sample size. Conversely, a Japanese cohort study reported a slightly lower PC incidence in female RA patients compared to the general population [17]. The discrepancy between our results and earlier reports may be attributed to insufficient adjustment for potential confounders in previous research. Therefore, the role of RA in the pathogenesis of PC warrants further investigation.

Although the underlying mechanism of the association between RA and PC is still to be investigated, our findings are biologically plausible based on the available evidence. Firstly, Mitochondria maintain relative stability in number, morphology, and spatial structure through biological processes, such as biogenesis, fission, fusion, and autophagy, which are collectively called mitochondrial homeostasis [18]. An imbalance in the mitochondrial homeostatic environment will affect immune cell energy metabolism, apoptosis, and inflammatory signaling [19]. Abnormal mitochondrial homeostasis is active in rheumatoid arthritis [20]. Nonetheless, mitochondrial homeostasis regulation serves as a key factor in the development and progression of pancreatic cancer [21]. Notably, pancreatic cancer stem cells are highly dependent on mitochondrial metabolism to retain their stemness [22]. Secondly, metabolic reprogramming is dysregulated in rheumatoid arthritis [23]. And, metabolic reprogramming drives the development and advancement of pancreatic cancer [24]. From another perspective, Abnormal mitochondrial dynamics in RA not only perpetuate synovial inflammation but also generate mitochondrial DAMPs, including mtDNA, mtROS. These mediators escape into the systemic circulation, potentially inducing DNA damage and inflammatory signaling in pancreatic tissue [25, 26]. Furthermore, studies have revealed significant differences in the microbial composition of the gastrointestinal tract and oral cavity between RA patients and healthy individuals, with RA patients exhibiting higher microbial diversity and abundance [27]. And, microbial communities can contribute to the development of pancreatic cancer by releasing inflammatory molecules, which play a critical role in shaping the tumor microenvironment and promoting oncogenesis [28]. For example, Fusobacterium hwasookii nucleatum, known for its ability to induce inflammatory responses, may serve as a mediator for the impact of RA on PC [29]. Thus, RA can promote the release of inflammatory mediators via multiple pathways, ultimately fostering the initiation and progression of pancreatic cancer. Additionally, RA, characterized as a chronic systemic immune-mediated condition, promotes autoimmunity and alters mast cell behavior, potentially fostering the onset of invasive pancreatic cancer [30, 31]. Collectively, these pathways exemplify how RA propagates a multilevel inflammatory cascade, spanning intracellular, metabolic, microbial, and immune axes, that disrupts pancreatic tissue homeostasis. This provides a mechanistic basis for the Mendelian randomization results, positioning RA as a systemic disease that remodels the host environment to favor pancreatic oncogenesis.

The current study adopted MR, using SNPs as instrumental variables. This approach leverages stable and enduring genetic variants, facilitating direct evaluation of exposure effects while minimizing the impact of social, lifestyle, and other confounding variables. Furthermore, MR reduces the potential for reverse causality compared to retrospective studies. By utilizing publicly available GWAS datasets, the study achieved a substantial sample size, optimizing time and resource efficiency. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to validate the robustness of the results [32].

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. Firstly, the GWAS data for RA and PC were exclusively from European populations, and their applicability to other regions, such as China, remains uncertain. Secondly, the datasets were based on 2021 statistics, as more recent exposure data were not available, which may limit the accuracy of the findings regarding recent disease trends. Although confounding factors were minimized, the diversity of RA and PC means that the selected datasets may not fully represent all european populations. Thirdly, the study did not include stratified analyses by gender or age, which could have provided deeper insights into disease mechanisms. Additionally, MR is limited to assessing linear relationships between diseases and cannot address nonlinear dynamics. Moreover, the reverse MR analysis employed only 5 SNPs due to the limited availability of genetic instruments for pancreatic cancer that satisfied MR assumptions. While sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the null finding, reduced statistical power precludes definitive exclusion of a minor causal effect. Finally, as an inherent limitation of the MR approach, our study provides genetic evidence for causality but does not include experimental validation or direct clinical cohort validation of the observed association. While MR minimizes confounding, ultimate biological confirmation requires integration with mechanistic and prospective clinical studies. Thus, the observed association between the two diseases needs to be corroborated through other epidemiological studies [33].

Conclusion

Through a bidirectional MR analysis, this study investigated the causal links between RA and PC, identifying RA as a causal risk factor for PC. However, further in vitro and in vivo experiments are needed to elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms. Additionally, clinical intervention studies are required to determine whether the prevention and treatment of RA can mitigate the risk of PC.
