Managing Severe Bicuspid Aortic Stenosis, Aortic Coarctation, and Impaired Left Ventricular Function
Authors: Oscar A. Perez Orpinel, Félix Damas de los Santos, Jessy Steve Masso-Bueso, Oscar A. Lozano-Cruz, Hugo Rodríguez-Zanella, Javier Molina-Méndez, Alejandro Villalobos, Eduardo A. Arias
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation commonly coexist, diagnosis in the eighth decade of life is exceptionally rare.Case SummaryA 78-year-old male patient with a history of hypertension was admitted with a 2-year history of progressive dyspnea on minimal exertion. After diagnostic workup, he was diagnosed with severe bicuspid aortic stenosis, aortic coarctation, and severely impaired left ventricle systolic function (left ventricle ejection fraction 12%). Due to high surgical risk, a stepwise percutaneous strategy was adopted, starting with coarctation stenting which improved the left ventricle ejection fraction to 32%, allowing for successful transcatheter aortic valve replacement using a snare-assisted technique.DiscussionTreating the coarctation first facilitated valve delivery and reduced afterload, improving systolic function, and ultimately reducing the need for ventricular support during transcatheter aortic valve replacement.Take-Home MessageA stepwise approach is crucial in managing high-risk patients with complex structural heart disease, demonstrating the importance of meticulous preprocedural planning.

History of Presentation and Past Medical History

A 78-year-old male patient with long-standing, difficult-to-control hypertension was referred to our hospital due to a 2-year history of progressive dyspnea on minimal exertion. Physical examination revealed a grade II/IV systolic murmur at the upper left sternal border, bilateral basal crackles, and jugular venous distension. Peripheral examination showed diminished lower limb pulses, cool extremities, and bilateral edema. Differential Diagnoses

Based on the clinical findings, heart failure was suspected, likely secondary to aortic stenosis, given the signs of systemic and pulmonary congestion and the characteristic murmur. However, peripheral artery disease and aortic coarctation were also considered, prompted by diminished pulses and a significant upper-lower limb blood pressure gradient.

Initial Investigations

An initial transthoracic echocardiogram showed a left ventricle ejection fraction (LVEF) of 12% and a bicuspid aortic valve with severe low-flow, low-gradient stenosis. Given the suspicion of aortic coarctation, due to its association with bicuspid aortic valve and physical findings, an aortic computed tomography angiography was performed. The study confirmed aortic coarctation, tortuosity of the descending aorta, and a horizontal aortic root with an 80° angulation (Figure 1). The computed tomography images of the aortic valve revealed a severe calcification, with an aortic annulus diameter of 22.9 mm, perimeter of 71.5 mm, and a 398.7 mm2 (Figure 2). Management

The patient was deemed high surgical risk (Society of Thoracic Surgeons: 9.53% perioperative mortality) due to severely depressed left ventricle (LV) systolic function. Therefore, a staged percutaneous approach was selected, beginning with aortic coarctation percutaneous angioplasty with a 24 × 48-mm covered stent to relieve coarctation and re-evaluate LV systolic function.

Two weeks later, follow-up echocardiography showed marked improvement in LVEF (32%), allowing for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) without mechanical circulatory support (Figure 3, Video 1). After thoughtful consideration of the challenges posed by the case, including navigating the transcatheter heart valve (THV) across the aorta and the coarctation stent, addressing the horizontal aortic root and vascular tortuosity, and managing the severely calcified bicuspid aortic valve, a 25-mm intra-annular self-expanding THV was selected for implantation.Figure 3Echocardiographic Assessment(A and B) Apical 4-chamber and parasternal long-axis views from the initial transthoracic echocardiogram showing severely reduced left ventricular systolic function (LVEF: 12%) with low-gradient aortic stenosis (mean gradient: 20 mm Hg). (C and D) Corresponding echocardiographic views 2 weeks after treatment of aortic coarctation with a covered stent. Notable improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF: 32%) is observed, along with an increased aortic mean gradient (42 mm Hg), consistent with improved forward flow following reduction of afterload mismatch.

After placement of a double-curved Lunderquist extra-stiff guidewire (Cook Medical) in the LV, a snare-assisted technique was used to navigate through the aorta and coarctation stent from the contralateral femoral access (Figure 4, Video 2). Predilatation was performed with a 22 × 40-mm Maxi-LD balloon (Cordis). Valve deployment under rapid pacing at 180 beats/min ensured stability and adequate valve depth and position (Figure 5) and a final aortogram after THV deployment, with no evidence of complications (Video 3). The patient did not require permanent pacemaker implantation following the procedure. Outcome and Follow-Up

At 3-month follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic. Follow-up echocardiography showed an LVEF of 32%, with a normally functioning prosthetic valve, without evidence of paravalvular leaks (Video 4).

Discussion

This case illustrates the value of a structured, stepwise approach to managing complex structural heart diseases. We presented a patient with severe aortic stenosis, aortic coarctation, reduced LVEF, multivessel coronary artery disease, and a horizontal, tortuous aorta. The coexistence of a bicuspid aortic valve and aortic coarctation is not uncommon; however, what stands out, in this case, is that the diagnosis was made in a patient in their eighth decade of life despite no prior history of congenital heart disease.

Bicuspid aortic valve is the most common congenital heart defect, and 7% of patients with this condition have concomitant aortic coarctation. Conversely, a bicuspid aortic valve is present in 70% to 75% of patients with aortic coarctation.1 Aortic coarctation in adulthood is uncommon; when it does present later in life, the most frequent manifestation is recoarctation rather than a de novo diagnosis. The combination of severe aortic stenosis and untreated aortic coarctation is rare.2

Given the complexity of this case, we chose first to address the aortic coarctation, as this approach provided 2 key benefits:1.Facilitating vascular access through the aorta for TAVR.2.Reducing left ventricular afterload to achieve ventricular remodeling and improve LVEF.

The treatment of aortic coarctation in adults includes surgical repair or percutaneous balloon angioplasty, with stent placement. However, stenting is considered the first-line treatment.3

Following the coarctation treatment, there was a significant improvement in left ventricular systolic function, eliminating the need for mechanical circulatory support during TAVR. Mechanical circulatory support in TAVR may be considered in patients with complex coronary artery disease and severely reduced LVEF to minimize periprocedural risk. In our patient, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was not required but was included in the preprocedural planning as a contingency.4

Contralateral valve snaring is a useful and simple technique for facilitating the navigation of THVs across recently implanted devices—such as aortic stents—and preventing device deformation or displacement. Valve delivery was facilitated by snare-assisted traction, which allowed precise control of the device's trajectory, promoting coaxial alignment as it traversed the stent and advanced through the horizontally oriented aortic root. This technique ensured optimal positioning within the annular plane despite the anatomical complexity. However, we recommend that snare use should be performed with caution and always in conjunction with high-support guidewires and careful advancement to minimize the risk of vascular injury.

Notably, a self-expanding transcatheter valve was chosen for this case. While balloon-expandable valves are commonly employed in bicuspid anatomies and current literature does not have evidence on its use, our institution has developed substantial expertise with self-expanding platforms in this patient population. In this particular case—characterized by a small annulus—self-expanding valves offer superior hemodynamic performance compared to balloon expandable, as reported by the SMART trial.5

We acknowledge that this case could also have been successfully managed with a balloon-expandable valve using an active delivery system such as Delivery Flex; however, we present this as an alternative platform capable of achieving comparable outcomes.

This complex case was successfully managed with 2 interventional procedures, demonstrating the importance of meticulous planning and multidisciplinary heart team evaluation.

Conclusions

TAVR in congenital heart disease entails unique challenges. This case, marked by severe bicuspid aortic stenosis, coarctation, and LVEF, was successfully managed through a stepwise strategy, allowing LVEF improvement and obviating the need for ventricular support during TAVR.
