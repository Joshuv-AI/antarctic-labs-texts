Meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials: efficacy of vitamin C mesotherapy for gingival hyperpigmentation in adults
Authors: Mina Hu, Yue Gao, Jian Xu, Jianying Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGingival hyperpigmentation is a common aesthetic concern, necessitating evidence-based evaluation of depigmentation treatments, particularly vitamin C mesotherapy.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was conducted across PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science databases. Data were extracted and analyzed using both random and common effects models to calculate mean difference (MD) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity was assessed using I², tau², and Q statistics.ResultsThe meta-analysis, including 11 RCTs with a total of 236 participants, revealed significant effects of vitamin C mesotherapy. For Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, the MD was 2.0580 [1.6161; 2.4998] (p < 0.0001), with high heterogeneity (I² = 90.6%). The overall effect for GPI scores was not statistically significant (MD = -0.72, 95% CI [-1.47, 0.03], p = 0.0585), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 95.1%). A subgroup analysis revealed a significant difference between groups (p < 0.0001). The Diode laser subgroup showed a significant effect (MD = -1.35, 95% CI [-1.64, -1.07]) with no heterogeneity, whereas the Surgical depigmentation subgroup had no significant effect and high heterogeneity (I2 = 85.9%). Dummett-Gupta Oral Pigmentation Index (DOPI) scores showed a significant decrease with mesotherapy compared to diode laser (MD = -0.7653 [-0.9207; -0.6100], p < 0.0001, I² = 0.0%), but no significant difference compared to topical gel (MD = 0.1723 [-0.1438; 0.4883], p = 0.2855, I² = 0.0%). When compared to overall laser interventions (MD = 1.7245 [1.5181; 1.9308], p < 0.0001, I² = 89.9%) and surgical interventions (MD = 2.2091 [2.0653; 2.3530], p < 0.0001, I² = 59.5%), vitamin C mesotherapy generally exhibited smaller mean differences, indicating a comparatively lesser magnitude of effect.ConclusionVitamin C mesotherapy demonstrates significant efficacy in reducing gingival hyperpigmentation, notably showing a favorable outcome compared to diode laser for DOPI scores and no significant difference from topical gel. While laser and surgical interventions generally present larger overall mean differences, vitamin C mesotherapy remains a valuable treatment modality, adding to the diverse effective options for managing gingival hyperpigmentation.

Introduction

Gingival hyperpigmentation (GH), characterized by excessive melanin deposition in the gingival tissues, is a common aesthetic concern that can significantly impact patients’ confidence and quality of life, particularly among individuals with high smile lines [1]. This condition, often presenting as dark patches or diffuse discoloration, is influenced by factors such as genetics, smoking, systemic conditions, and hormonal changes. While GH is benign and does not pose a direct health risk, its cosmetic implications have driven increasing demand for effective depigmentation treatments, especially in populations where oral aesthetics are culturally prioritized. Studies have highlighted the prevalence of this condition across diverse ethnic groups, with melanin hyperpigmentation being particularly noticeable in individuals with darker skin tones. As awareness of oral aesthetics grows, so does the need for reliable, evidence-based interventions to address this condition [2].

Current treatment modalities for GH range from traditional surgical approaches to minimally invasive techniques, each with varying degrees of efficacy, patient tolerability, and recurrence rates. Surgical depigmentation, such as scalpel techniques, remains a widely used method due to its immediate results, though it is often associated with postoperative pain and prolonged healing times. Laser therapy, including diode lasers, has emerged as a popular alternative, offering precision and reduced discomfort, yet its high cost and potential for thermal damage limit its accessibility. Topical agents, such as ascorbic acid gels, have also been explored for their non-invasive nature, but their efficacy is often inconsistent due to limited absorption into the deeper gingival tissues. Amid these options, vitamin C mesotherapy (a technique involving intra-mucosal injections of vitamin C) has gained attention by directly delivering the antioxidant to the target site, leveraging its potential to combine efficacy with minimal invasiveness. It is important to note that a single “gold standard” treatment for GH does not currently exist. Therefore, in the context of this review, “standard treatments” refers to the widely recognized and clinically established modalities, including surgical methods (e.g., scalpel), laser therapy (e.g., diode laser), and topical applications (e.g., ascorbic acid gel). Amid these options, vitamin C mesotherapy (a technique involving intra-mucosal injections of vitamin C) has gained attention for its potential to combine efficacy with minimal invasiveness, leveraging the antioxidant properties of vitamin C to inhibit melanin synthesis.

Despite its promise, the comparative effectiveness of vitamin C mesotherapy against established treatments remains underexplored, with individual studies reporting conflicting outcomes regarding pain, patient satisfaction, and depigmentation success [3–5]. Crucially, while some studies have investigated vitamin C in various forms for GH [4], a comprehensive and quantitative synthesis specifically comparing the efficacy and safety of vitamin C mesotherapy against widely recognized and clinically established has been notably absent from the literature. This systematic review and meta-analysis represents a significant advancement. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to synthesize the available evidence, addressing the critical need to evaluate the efficacy of vitamin C mesotherapy for GH and to provide clarity on its role among existing therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [6]. A systematic literature search was performed across EMBASE, PubMed, and Web of Science databases, using keywords and MeSH terms associated with gingival hyperpigmentation and depigmentation interventions (e.g., “gingival hyperpigmentation,” “depigmentation,” “vitamin C mesotherapy,” “diode laser”). The search spanned from the inception of each database up to March 13, 2025, with no language restrictions. Additional records were identified through manual searches of reference lists and other sources. Two independent investigators reviewed the identified studies, first at the title and abstract level, and then as full-text articles, based on the following inclusion criteria: (i) RCTs evaluating depigmentation interventions for GH; (ii) studies comparing at least two treatment modalities, such as vitamin C mesotherapy, diode laser, or surgical techniques; (iii) inclusion of outcome measures such as VAS scores, GPI, or DOPI; and (iv) studies involving human participants aged 18–45 years. Exclusion criteria included non-human studies, duplicate publications, reviews or meta-analyses, studies lacking relevant outcome data, or those with unsuitable study designs (e.g., case reports). The most comprehensive report was selected in cases of multiple publications from the same study group.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized form, capturing the following details: author, publication year, study design, RCT registration number, country, study period, age range, number of participants, intervention groups, and outcome measures (e.g., VAS scores, GPI, DOPI). For studies with multiple publications, data from the most complete report were used. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool was used, and the integrity of the included RCTs across seven key areas was assessed: allocation concealment, random sequence generation, outcome assessment blinding, participant and personnel blinding, selective reporting, incomplete outcome data, and other potential biases. Each domain was scored as “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear risk,” with an overall risk of bias assigned to each study based on the collective assessment. Studies were considered high quality if most domains were rated as low risk [7]. Disagreements between the two independent reviewers were first resolved by consensus through discussion. If a consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted to resolve the disagreement.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measures used to assess the efficacy of depigmentation treatments were defined as follows: (1) VAS: A subjective, unidimensional scale used to measure pain intensity or patient satisfaction. Scores range from 0 to 10, with 0 representing no pain/dissatisfaction and 10 representing the worst pain/complete dissatisfaction. (2) GPI: A clinical index that objectively quantifies the degree of gingival pigmentation. Scores are typically categorized from 0 (no pigmentation) to 3 (severe pigmentation) based on the extent and intensity of color changes. (3) DOPI: A scale used to assess gingival pigmentation based on color, shade, and extent. The index provides a quantitative score for clinical assessment, with lower scores indicating a lower degree of pigmentation.

Risk of bias assessment

Publication bias was assessed for outcomes in the meta-analysis, in line with established methodological recommendations. This typically involves inspecting funnel plots for asymmetry and conducting formal statistical tests such as Egger’s regression test or Begg’s rank correlation test. Asymmetry in a funnel plot or a statistically significant test result can suggest the presence of publication bias, where studies with statistically significant or favorable results are more likely to be published. For assigning overall risk of bias, studies were categorized as “low risk” if they demonstrated a low risk of bias in at least four out of the five critical domains, with no high risk in any domain. Studies were assigned an “unclear risk” if they had one or more domains with an unclear risk of bias, or a combination of low and high/unclear risks that did not allow for a clear low-risk categorization.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using a standardized approach to compare treatment effects across studies. MD with 95% CIs were calculated for continuous outcomes (e.g., VAS, GPI, DOPI scores) using both random effects and common effect models with R (4.3.1) [8]. The choice between the common effect model, which assumed a uniform effect size across studies and weighted studies by inverse within-study variance, and the random effects model, which accounted for both within-study and between-study variance, was determined by the presence and extent of heterogeneity. Specifically, if heterogeneity was low (I² ≤ 50%), the common effect model was primarily reported; otherwise, the random effects model was chosen to provide a more conservative estimate that accounts for variability beyond chance. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, categorized as low (I² ≤ 50%), moderate (> 50% to < 75%), or high (≥ 75%), supplemented by the tau² and H statistics, and tested with the Q statistic and corresponding p-value. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. It is important to note that a minimally clinically important difference (MCID) was not defined for the outcomes in this meta-analysis due to the absence of a consensus on this value in the literature. Therefore, while we report on statistical significance, the clinical relevance of these findings to patients may not be fully captured. Furthermore, to ensure the robustness of our findings, sensitivity analyses were planned. However, due to the limited number of included studies in our meta-analyses, a formal “leave-one-out” sensitivity analysis was not performed, as it would have rendered the results unstable. Similarly, subgroup analyses were not conducted because the number of studies was insufficient to create meaningful subgroups to explore sources of heterogeneity. To enable a comprehensive comparison of treatment effects, subgroup analyses were performed based on intervention type (laser, surgical, vitamin C) and follow-up duration where sufficient data allowed. Further, meta-regression and sensitivity analyses were planned to explore sources of heterogeneity and assess the robustness of the findings, particularly given the varying levels of heterogeneity observed across different intervention types and outcome measures.

Results

Included studies

The meta-analysis process began with a comprehensive literature search across multiple databases—EMBASE, PubMed, and Web of Science—yielding 42 records, supplemented by 3 additional records identified through other sources, resulting in a total of 45 records. After removing 6 duplicates, 39 unique records remained for screening. All 39 records underwent an initial title and abstract review, during which 16 were excluded, leaving 23 full-text articles for eligibility assessment. The full-text review further excluded 19 articles due to various reasons: 8 for insufficient data, 5 for unsuitable study design, and 6 for inability to construct a table, with no exclusions due to being reviews or meta-analyses. Ultimately, 4 RCTs met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. This rigorous selection process reflects a systematic effort to ensure only high-quality, relevant RCTs were analyzed, narrowing the initial pool of 45 records to a focused set of 4 studies suitable for synthesizing evidence on the topic under investigation (Fig. 1).

Risk of bias assessment

The bias analysis of the four included RCTs, as presented in the table, reveals varying levels of risk across key methodological domains, with overall assessments ranging from low to unclear. El-Mofty et al. [9] demonstrated a low risk of bias in most domains, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias, though the blinding of participants and personnel was unclear, resulting in an overall low risk rating. Chaudhary et al. [10] exhibited a mixed profile, with a high risk of bias in random sequence generation and incomplete outcome data, potentially undermining the reliability of results, while allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, and selective reporting were rated low; however, other sources of bias were low, leading to an overall unclear risk. Esmat et al. [11] showed a consistently low risk of bias across all assessed domains—allocation concealment, random sequence generation, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, blinding of participants and personnel, and selective reporting—with only other sources of bias rated as unclear, resulting in an overall unclear risk. Haque et al. [12] had an unclear risk in random sequence generation, but low risk in allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias, yielding an overall low risk assessment. Collectively, while most studies maintained low risk in several critical areas, issues such as unclear blinding or high risk in randomization and data completeness in some trials suggest potential methodological weaknesses that could influence the validity of their findings (Fig. 2).

VAS scores

The meta-analysis examining the efficacy of vitamin C mesotherapy for GH utilizing VAS scores, encompassing two studies with a total of 56 observations, reveals statistically significant effects under both random and common effects models. Specifically, the common effect model yielded a MD of 2.0580, with a 95% CI of [1.6161; 2.4998], and a highly significant p-value of < 0.0001. Similarly, the random effects model demonstrated a significant effect, though with a wider CI, reporting an MD of 1.8514 [0.3428; 3.3600] and a p-value of 0.0162. Nevertheless, significant heterogeneity was observed across the studies, as indicated by a tau2 of 1.0744, tau of 1.0365, and a substantial I2 value of 90.6% [66.1%; 97.4%]. This high I2 value, along with an H value of 3.26 [1.72; 6.20], suggests considerable variation between the studies. The test of heterogeneity further supports this, with a Q statistic of 10.64, 1 degree of freedom, and a p-value of 0.0011 (Fig. 3). Additionally, a third study by Haque et al., 2024 reported significantly higher patient satisfaction scores for vitamin C injection compared to surgical depigmentation.

GPI scores

The random-effects meta-analysis of five datasets from two studies (Esmat et al., 2023 and Haque et al., 2024), as shown in Fig. 4, was conducted to evaluate the combined mean difference. The overall effect was not statistically significant (p = 0.0585), with a MD of − 0.72 and a 95% CI of [− 1.47,0.03]. Significant heterogeneity was found across the studies (Q = 81.00, d.f.=4, p < 0.0001), with a high I2 value of 95.1% and a τ2 of 0.6862, indicating that a substantial portion of the variance was due to true differences in effect size rather than sampling error. The subgroup analysis was performed to investigate the sources of this heterogeneity. A statistically significant difference was found between the subgroups (Qbetween=32.04, d.f.=1, p < 0.0001). The subgroup analysis showed a significant effect for the Diode laser treatment, with a mean difference of − 1.35 and a 95% CI of [− 1.64,−1.07]. This subgroup of three studies showed no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%, τ2 = 0). In contrast, the Surgical depigmentation subgroup, which included two studies, did not show a significant effect (MD = 0.15, 95% CI [− 0.29,0.58]) and displayed substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 85.9%, τ2 = 0.0839).

DOPI scores between intra-mucosal injection of vitamin C and diode laser

The analysis of evaluation of vitamin C mesotherapy for GH utilizing DOPI scores, which includes three datasets from a single randomized controlled trial by Esmat et al., 2023, demonstrates identical results under both the random and common effects models. Specifically, the MD is −0.7653, with a 95% CI of [−0.9207; −0.6100], and a highly significant p-value of < 0.0001. Notably, there is no evidence of heterogeneity among the studies, as indicated by a tau2 of 0 [0.0000; 0.2389], tau of 0 [0.0000; 0.4888], and an I2 value of 0.0% [0.0%; 89.6%]. The H value is 1.00 [1.00; 3.10], further supporting the absence of heterogeneity. The test of heterogeneity also confirms this, with a Q statistic of 0.68, 2 degrees of freedom, and a p-value of 0.7106 (Fig. 5).

DOPI scores between intra-mucosal injection of vitamin C and topical ascorbic acid gel

The analysis, encompassing three datasets from a single randomized controlled trial by El-Mofty et al., 2021, demonstrates identical results under both the random and common effects models. The MD is 0.1723, with a 95% CI of [−0.1438; 0.4883], and a p-value of 0.2855, indicating a lack of significant effect. Characteristics of included studies on laser and surgical interventions for gingival hyperpigmentation

To further investigate the differences in efficacy between vitamin C, laser, and surgical interventions, this meta-analysis also includes seven additional studies (Suragimath et al., 2016; Houshmand et al., 2017; Negi et al., 2018; Bakhshi et al., 2018; Arif MSc et al., 2020; Harb et al., 2021; Turgut et al., 2022). These recent additions, published between 2016 and 2022, originate from diverse countries including India, Iran, Iraq, Turkey, and the UAE. These studies primarily use split-mouth randomized comparative or clinical trial designs to evaluate various laser and surgical techniques for treating gingival hyperpigmentation. Sample sizes typically range from 12 to 22 subjects or patients. The interventions cover a spectrum of lasers (Diode, Dual diode, Er, Cr: YSGG, Er: YAG) and traditional surgical methods like scalpels or ceramic soft tissue trimming burs. The primary endpoints consistently focus on comparing depigmentation efficacy, often measured using standardized indices such as the Dummett-Gupta Oral Pigmentation (DOP) index, Dummett’s Oral Pigmentation Index (DOPI), Hedin Melanin Index, or photographic RGB analysis. Secondary endpoints frequently include assessments of pain (VAS scores), intraoperative bleeding, wound healing, patient and practitioner satisfaction, and the recurrence of pigmentation (Table 2).

Efficacy of laser interventions on gingival hyperpigmentation

The meta-analysis, encompassing 26 datasets, indicates a significant MD of 1.7245 (95% CI: 1.5181 to 1.9308, p < 0.0001) for laser intervention in gingival hyperpigmentation, suggesting its effectiveness. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the studies, with an I2 value of 89.9% (95% CI: 86.4% to 92.5%) and a significant Q-statistic (Q = 246.59, df = 25, p < 0.0001), indicating considerable variability in treatment effects beyond what would be expected by chance. Subgroup analysis by follow-up period (≤ 1-month, 3-month, 6-month, ≥ 12-month) revealed varying mean differences, with the ≤ 1-month subgroup showing the largest effect (MD = 1.9262) and the ≥ 12-month subgroup showing the smallest (MD = 1.3235). Despite these differences in subgroup MDs, the test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (Q = 3.52, df = 3, p = 0.3185), suggesting that the observed differences in treatment effect among the follow-up periods are not statistically significant. High heterogeneity also persisted within each subgroup, as evidenced by the consistently high I2 values (Fig. 7).

Efficacy of surgical interventions on gingival hyperpigmentation

The meta-analysis, comprising 13 datasets, reveals a significant MD of 2.2091 (95% CI: 2.0653 to 2.3530, p < 0.0001) for surgical interventions in gingival hyperpigmentation, indicating their effectiveness. Moderate heterogeneity was observed across the studies, with an I2 value of 59.5% (95% CI: 25.4% to 78.0%) and a significant Q-statistic (Q = 29.63, df = 12, p = 0.0032). Subgroup analysis by follow-up period (≤ 1-month, 3-month, 6-month, ≥ 12-month) showed varying mean differences, with the 3-month subgroup exhibiting the largest effect (MD = 2.3032) and the 6-month subgroup the smallest (MD = 2.0286). Notably, the 3-month subgroup displayed no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%), while the 6-month subgroup had considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 86.8%). Despite these differences, the test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (Q = 0.70, df = 3, p = 0.8728), suggesting that the observed variations in treatment effect among the follow-up periods are not statistically significant (Fig. 8).

Discussion

The findings of this meta-analysis provide valuable insights into the efficacy of vitamin C mesotherapy for the treatment of GH, synthesizing data from four RCTs. The results indicate that vitamin C mesotherapy demonstrates significant efficacy in reducing GH, as evidenced by statistically significant improvements in VAS scores (MD = 2.0580, p < 0.0001). However, the analysis of GPI scores showed a different trend. The overall effect was not statistically significant (MD = −0.72, 95% CI [−1.47, 0.03], p = 0.0585), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 95.1%). A subgroup analysis, which found a significant difference between groups (p < 0.0001), revealed that this lack of overall effect was driven by the type of treatment. The Diode laser subgroup showed a significant effect (MD = −1.35, 95% CI [−1.64, −1.07]) with no heterogeneity, while the Surgical depigmentation subgroup had no significant effect and high heterogeneity (I2 = 85.9%). For DOPI scores, a significant decrease was observed with mesotherapy when compared to diode laser therapy (MD = −0.7653, p < 0.0001). However, no significant difference was found when compared to topical ascorbic acid gel (MD = 0.1723, p = 0.2855), suggesting that the route of administration may play a critical role. The absence of heterogeneity in DOPI comparisons (I2 = 0.0%) reflects the consistency of results, although this is largely due to the data originating from a single RCT. The low heterogeneity may be influenced by the standardized protocols within this study, but the varying follow-up times across groups could affect the interpretation of these findings. This suggests that while the DOPI outcomes appear reliable within the confines of this single trial, cautious extrapolation is warranted, and further investigation with consistent follow-up periods is needed.

GH, characterized by excessive melanin deposition in the gingival tissues, significantly impacts facial aesthetics and psychological well-being. As noted by Patil et al. [3], the color of the gingiva is a crucial component of oral health and attractiveness, affecting individuals across various ethnicities, races, ages, and genders. This condition, while not medically harmful, is often a source of cosmetic concern, prompting individuals to seek depigmentation treatments. Laser ablation has emerged as a prominent technique for gingival depigmentation, offering advantages such as ease of handling, short treatment time, hemostasis, and sterilization [2]. Studies have explored various laser parameters and treatment outcomes, highlighting the efficacy and patient satisfaction associated with laser-based procedures. For instance, a study by Nguyen et al. [20] using a CO2 laser demonstrated minimal pain and rapid epithelization, with significant improvements in the DOPI. Similarly, research by Alqahtani et al. [21] compared scalpel and diode laser techniques, finding that laser treatment offered a bloodless field and uncomplicated recovery, reinforcing its appeal as a minimally invasive option.

These findings are consistent with the broader literature on vitamin C’s role in melanin inhibition. Vitamin C is known to suppress tyrosinase activity, a key enzyme in melanin synthesis [22]. However, the efficacy of topical versus injected vitamin C may depend on its ability to penetrate the gingival epithelium and reach melanocytes. Our meta-analysis suggests that while topical application can be effective, as shown by the non-significant difference with mesotherapy in DOPI scores, the injection method might offer a more consistent and potentially deeper delivery, particularly when compared to laser ablation.

The case reports we reviewed [23]– [24] provide further context, supporting the use of vitamin C in various forms for gingival depigmentation, from topical application for healing [23] to mesotherapy [24] and as an adjunctive to prevent repigmentation [25]. These studies, while limited by their case series design, highlight the importance of considering different delivery methods and adjunctive therapies.

The clinical implications of these findings are significant, particularly in regions like Egypt and India, where the included RCTs were conducted and where GH is prevalent due to genetic and environmental factors. Vitamin C mesotherapy’s favorable profile—effective depigmentation, manageable pain, and high patient satisfaction—positions it as a promising alternative to surgical and laser-based treatments, especially for younger patients aged 18–45 years. However, the high heterogeneity in VAS and GPI analyses warrants caution, indicating that factors such as injection technique, vitamin C concentration, or patient-specific responses may influence outcomes. From a clinical practice perspective, while vitamin C mesotherapy demonstrates significant efficacy, its potential drawbacks and side effects should not be overlooked. Common side effects include temporary redness, bruising, and minor pain at the injection sites, which are typically self-limiting and resolve within a few days. Although rare, there is a risk of infection, allergic reactions, or uneven pigmentation, especially with improper technique or individual patient variations. Furthermore, unlike the one-time treatment often associated with surgical or laser therapies, mesotherapy may require multiple sessions to achieve and maintain the desired results, which increases the patient’s time commitment and overall cost. Therefore, clinicians should comprehensively assess patient expectations, tolerance, and potential risks, and ensure thorough informed consent before recommending this therapy.

It is crucial to consider the potential for a placebo effect when comparing invasive (injection-based) versus non-invasive (topical) treatments. The psychological impact of a procedure can influence subjective measures like VAS and patient satisfaction scores, even if objective clinical outcomes (DOPI or GPI) are not significantly different. As seen in our analysis, patients may report higher satisfaction with mesotherapy due to its perceived “more serious” nature, despite objective results similar to topical gel. Therefore, patient satisfaction must be interpreted with caution, and future studies should employ blinding and more objective metrics.

Vitamin C, or ascorbic acid, is widely recognized for its depigmenting properties, making it a valuable agent in treating hyperpigmentation across various tissues, including the gingiva. The mechanisms by which Vitamin C modulates pigmentation are multifaceted, involving both direct and indirect pathways. Primarily, Vitamin C acts as a potent antioxidant, neutralizing free radicals that can stimulate melanogenesis. Furthermore, it inhibits tyrosinase, a key enzyme involved in the synthesis of melanin, thereby reducing melanin production [26]. In the context of GH, the efficacy of Vitamin C can be influenced by its delivery method. As demonstrated in our meta-analysis, mesotherapy, which delivers Vitamin C directly into the gingival tissues, showed significant efficacy compared to topical application in some, but not all comparisons. This highlights the importance of ensuring adequate penetration of Vitamin C to reach the melanocytes located within the basal layer of the gingival epithelium.

Beyond its direct effects on melanogenesis, Vitamin C also plays a role in collagen synthesis and tissue repair, which can contribute to improved gingival health and aesthetics. Studies have shown that Vitamin C can enhance the effects of other depigmenting agents, such as tranexamic acid, when used in combination [27, 28]. For instance, research on periorbital hyperpigmentation and melasma has demonstrated that mesotherapy with Vitamin C alongside tranexamic acid yields promising results, suggesting synergistic effects in reducing hyperpigmentation. Moreover, the stability and formulation of Vitamin C are critical factors that influence its efficacy. Tetrahexyldecyl ascorbate, a lipid-soluble form of Vitamin C, has shown promising results in clinical trials, demonstrating significant improvements in hyperpigmentation when combined with retinol [29]. This suggests that advanced formulations of Vitamin C may enhance its delivery and stability, thereby improving its therapeutic potential in treating GH. Overall, Vitamin C’s ability to inhibit melanogenesis, coupled with its antioxidant and collagen-stimulating properties, makes it a valuable therapeutic agent for GH. The route of administration, formulation, and potential synergistic effects with other agents are crucial considerations for optimizing its efficacy. Further research is warranted to explore the long-term effects and optimal delivery methods of Vitamin C for the treatment of GH.

The current meta-analysis has expanded to include seven new studies focusing on laser and surgical interventions, allowing for a direct comparison of their efficacy against the previously analyzed vitamin C interventions for gingival hyperpigmentation. Our meta-analysis of laser interventions (from 26 datasets) revealed a significant MD of 1.7245 (95% CI: 1.5181 to 1.9308, p < 0.0001), suggesting their overall effectiveness. However, substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 89.9%) was a notable concern. Similarly, surgical interventions (from 13 studies) also showed a highly significant MD of 2.2091 (95% CI: 2.0653 to 2.3530, p < 0.0001), indicating strong efficacy, with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 59.5%). In contrast, the meta-analysis of vitamin C interventions for gingival hyperpigmentation presented a more varied picture depending on the outcome measure and comparison group: DOPI scores comparing vitamin C mesotherapy and diode laser (three datasets) showed an MD of −0.7653 (95% CI: −0.9207; −0.6100, p < 0.0001), indicating that diode laser was significantly more effective than vitamin C mesotherapy in this specific comparison, with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%). DOPI scores comparing intra-mucosal injection of vitamin C and topical ascorbic acid gel (three datasets) yielded an MD of 0.1723 (95% CI: −0.1438; 0.4883, p = 0.2855), suggesting no significant difference between these two vitamin C delivery methods, also with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%). When evaluating vitamin C mesotherapy using VAS scores (two studies), an MD of 1.8514 (95% CI: 0.3428; 3.3600, p = 0.0162) was found, suggesting a significant effect, but with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 90.6%). Similar findings were observed for GPI scores (two studies), with an MD of 1.8514 (95% CI: 0.3428; 3.3600, p = 0.0162) and substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 90.6%). Collectively, while both laser and surgical interventions demonstrate robust overall efficacy in improving gingival hyperpigmentation, the specific comparison with vitamin C reveals nuanced differences. Laser and surgical methods appear to achieve larger and more consistent mean differences, as indicated by their overall MDs. Furthermore, the direct comparison suggests that certain laser applications (like diode laser) may offer superior depigmentation compared to vitamin C mesotherapy. The substantial heterogeneity observed in laser and surgical meta-analyses, however, underscores the need for careful interpretation and highlights variability across studies. The vitamin C interventions, while showing some positive effects, especially when compared to topical forms, do not consistently achieve the same magnitude of effect as laser or surgical approaches, particularly when considering their overall efficacy.

Limitations

Despite the significant insights provided by this meta-analysis, several limitations warrant explicit acknowledgment. First, the small number of included RCTs and the relatively limited total sample size for certain outcomes restricts the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. This small sample size also precluded robust subgroup analyses that could have explored potential sources of heterogeneity in greater detail, such as different vitamin C concentrations, injection protocols, or follow-up durations. Second, the observed high heterogeneity for VAS and GPI scores, although addressed by using random effects models, suggests considerable variability across studies that could not be fully explained by the available data, potentially influencing the precision of the pooled estimates. While DOPI scores showed no heterogeneity, this was largely driven by data from a single RCT, limiting the strength of this specific finding. Third, the geographic restriction of the included studies to Egypt and India may limit the external validity of our findings to other populations, as genetic and environmental factors can influence gingival pigmentation and treatment responses. Fourth, the varying follow-up periods across the included studies introduce potential bias and make it challenging to assess the long-term efficacy and recurrence rates of vitamin C mesotherapy. Explicitly, long-term recurrence after vitamin C mesotherapy is not yet well-documented in the available literature, which is a key gap our study highlights. Fifth, while our analysis compared vitamin C mesotherapy against several clinically established standard treatments, the limited number of eligible RCTs in the literature restricted our ability to perform a broader comparison across the full spectrum of all depigmentation strategies for gingival hyperpigmentation. A more comprehensive assessment of vitamin C mesotherapy’s place within the entire therapeutic landscape would ideally require a larger body of evidence allowing for network meta-analysis or indirect comparisons with a wider range of treatments. Finally, our inability to statistically assess publication bias due to the small number of studies for each outcome means that our results may be susceptible to reporting bias, where studies with positive or significant findings are more likely to be published. Future research should aim to conduct larger, multi-center RCTs with standardized protocols, longer follow-up periods, and diverse populations to overcome these limitations and provide more definitive evidence on the role of vitamin C mesotherapy in managing gingival hyperpigmentation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis supports the efficacy of vitamin C mesotherapy for GH, particularly when compared to diode laser therapy, with consistent results across DOPI scores and significant improvements in patient-reported outcomes. While case reports provide anecdotal support and highlight individual variability, the RCT-based evidence offers a more reliable basis for clinical decision-making. Future research should focus on standardizing protocols, addressing heterogeneity, and exploring long-term outcomes to solidify vitamin C mesotherapy’s role in the therapeutic landscape, potentially reducing reliance on more invasive or costly alternatives. Specifically, establishing clear parameters for vitamin C concentration, injection techniques, and treatment frequencies is crucial to develop standardized clinical protocols and maximize its applicability in practice. It is important to note that these conclusions are based on limited geographic and demographic data, primarily from studies in Egypt and India. For patients seeking aesthetic improvement of gingival hyperpigmentation, vitamin C mesotherapy may be a viable and less invasive option compared to lasers or surgery, but more research is needed to confirm long-term outcomes.
