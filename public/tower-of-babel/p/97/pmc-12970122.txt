Collective action for responsible global health data sharing and use
Authors: Alfredo Mayor, Busiku Hamainza, Arantxa Roca-Feltrer, Hamtandi Magloire Natama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSharing data and biospecimens is both a scientific imperative and an ethical duty in research and public health, yet global asymmetries in capacity and power risk perpetuating inequality rather than alleviating it. To genuinely advance global health equity, data sharing efficiency should be measured not by the volume or speed of data transfer, but by the deliberate flow of benefits, capacity and decision-making authority to structurally disadvantaged regions. This requires a complementary shift. First, advanced partners must move from directing to enabling, focusing on building local technical, analytical and governance capacities to ensure solutions are rooted where they are needed most. Second, less-resourced actors must reframe data sharing not as a risky obligation, but as an opportunity for leadership—actively shaping research agendas and harnessing the power of open science. Consequently, sharing mandates must be explicitly tied to tangible interventions and demonstrated successes in improving health outcomes, supported by sovereign, federated data systems and reformed academic incentives that valorise capacity building and equitable collaboration as core research outputs. We, therefore, urge health researchers, funders, publishers and agencies to collectively transform sharing of data and biological materials into an equity-centred process that actively rectifies historical power imbalances. By shifting emphasis from the data themselves to the people, systems and processes that translate data into action, we can ensure sharing serves as a true bridge to equity—one that actively advances global health for all.

The power and challenges of sharing data

“I am human because I belong. I participate. I share.” With this quote, Nobel laureate Desmond Tutu distilled the meaning of Ubuntu1—an African philosophy that places community and sharing at the core of human survival and prosperity. Sharing ideas or resources for the common good is a fundamental skill that fosters empathy, cooperation and problem-solving. It strengthens our relationships with others and maximises our chances of survival and collective well-being.2 In an increasingly interconnected world, sharing data, the lifeblood of informed action and innovation, is essential for societies to thrive in a rapidly evolving landscape.

Data refer to facts and statistics collected through observations, measurements or responses.3 When analysed properly, data can provide a more accurate understanding of the world, enabling users to make better decisions.4 5 Whether collected to support individual healthcare delivery, assess health system’s performance, inform public health interventions, or for research purposes (figure 1), health data are vital to save and improve lives, reduce health inequalities and benefit society.6 Combining diverse data sets allows mathematical and statistical modelling (including artificial intelligence) to generalise more effectively to real-world scenarios and minimise bias arising from the specific characteristics of each data set.7 Therefore, sharing data (and biological samples, which involve tangible materials and the data describing those materials) enables a more in-depth analysis that fosters scientific progress. The likelihood of thorough scrutiny and checks by data reusers creates an incentive to handle data more carefully, ultimately safeguarding the integrity of science and maintaining public trust.8 Finally, data sharing helps all parties involved to become part of a larger, richer community, fostering a sense of generosity that promotes the common good.

Data archetypes, domains and features of health data that promote sharing. Medical and public health data are often grouped into four broad archetypes based on their relevance to the individual, population, health system or research. Individual-level data refer to information about a single person, typically gathered to deliver safe and effective healthcare. This encompasses, among others, sociodemographic information, clinical records, genetic information, vital signs, symptoms, physical attributes measured through wearable devices, treatment histories and outcomes. In contrast, public health data consists of aggregated population-wide metrics—like disease incidence or vaccination rates—used to spot trends and guide preventive strategies. This category includes both routine data from established systems (eg, vital statistics, surveillance) and non-routine data from specific initiatives like outbreak investigations or ad hoc surveys. Health system data focus on the performance and structure of the healthcare system itself to monitor delivery of care. Finally, health research data are generated through formal studies to advance scientific understanding of diseases, diagnosis, treatments, prevention and healthcare delivery, forming the foundational evidence for medical and public health practice. Technical and governance procedures differ significantly between data types. Prospective research data (eg, clinical trials), collected under strict protocols with specific consent, is shared following international policies to ensure transparency and reproducibility (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors),43 though challenges include managing confidentiality and access. Conversely, routinely collected data offers valuable real-world scale but is harder to deidentify and relies on complex broad consent, making its reuse more difficult. In general, data sharing and use are determined by identifiability (personal vs non-personal data), the controlling entity (public or private), the data source (primary if collected firsthand or secondary if obtained from existing sources) and accessibility (open if freely available or restricted when requiring some degree of authorisation). The success of data sharing further relies on data coverage (completeness, representativeness and timeliness), quality (granularity, accuracy and consistency), usability (accessible, understandable and interoperable), and safety (impartial, confidential and appropriate for its purpose).

Making data available to others covers a continuum of practices that range from sharing condensed information, such as aggregate or summary measures, to providing open data, which anyone can use for any purpose. Data can be shared to fulfil the objective for which it was initially collected (primary data) or for reuse in secondary analyses. Regardless of the approach taken, there are several challenges to ensuring the right data are in the right place at the right time9 (figure 2). Privacy imposes a delicate balance between accessibility and security. Technical and governance issues (which differ significantly between data types), and funding limitations can compromise data trust, integrity and ultimately, data sharing.10 Motivational obstacles rooted in feelings of ownership can hinder data sharing due to fear of competitive disadvantage, data misuse or criticism about the quality of the generated data.4 5 Overcoming these barriers is needed to fulfil scientists’ and public health professionals’ mission of improving global health through data sharing.1113

Contextual factors that influence data sharing throughout the data cycle. The journey of data from its initial creation to its final use in informing decisions consists of four core stages, each critical for ensuring data integrity and utility. The planning phase involves defining data collection methodologies, management procedures and robust data governance to ensure ethical and secure handling from the outset. In the input phase, raw data are acquired into a system for processing. The quality and structure of the data entered here are paramount, as they directly dictate the reliability and efficiency of all subsequent stages. The process phase includes data management, storage, sharing and analysis to convey meaningful information, including the careful preparation, selection and contextualisation of data to ensure its accuracy and fitness for reuse and analysis (data curation). The final output stage focuses on deriving and applying value from the processed data. This includes making sense of analytical results to draw conclusions (interpretation), communicate insights effectively using visualising tools, and use outcomes to enable informed decision-making. The cycle is closed by secure data retention or disposal. Throughout this continuum, several barriers can impede effective data sharing. The high costs of implementing and maintaining interoperable, secure health IT systems pose a significant financial challenge. Legal and ethical barriers stem from concerns about informed consent, data ownership and patient privacy, ambiguous and contradictory regulations across countries, a lack of perceived reciprocity and intellectual property disputes. Preserving data quality, ensuring findability and maintaining interoperability and security present significant technical and operational barriers. The governance barriers stem from poorly coordinated data collection that creates redundant silos, lack of leadership to drive sustainable improvements in data management and sharing, and restrictive data-sharing practices that encourage hoarding. Finally, human-centric barriers to sharing—including a lack of awareness regarding data value, concerns over control and ownership, general distrust, perceived risks (eg, criticism or opportunity costs), and insufficient incentives—discourage participation and create motivational and behavioural obstacles for both individuals and organisations.

Responsible data sharing

Context matters

Sharing health data is clearly a scientific responsibility and an ethical obligation. However, understanding the context is fundamental to ensuring that data sharing addresses historical imbalances in resources and benefit flow.14 This reflection will discuss data sharing issues in contexts with limited resources, mainly observed in the former colonised countries in the globe’s southern hemisphere. Centuries of colonialism and exploitation have created political, economic and epistemological imbalances that lay the foundation for dependent relationships.15 16 These asymmetries tend to empower people and institutions from the Global North, enabling them to conduct research into health issues affecting the Global South. Limitations in infrastructure (eg, data repositories) and technical capacities for data management and analysis compromise the control and sovereignty over the data produced in less-resourced regions, which typically bear a high disease burden,17 and hinder the benefits of data sharing.18 Insufficient legal and ethical provisions for data transfer, usually defined by institutions in wealthier countries, increase the complexity of sharing data and associated benefits. Finally, different motivations for data sharing, with well-resourced countries pursuing security and innovation (eg, preventing pandemics and addressing transnational threats) and less-resourced countries searching for access to technology and expertise, can lead to conflicting priorities and collaborations difficult to navigate.

Therefore, translating data into new knowledge may be slower in less-resourced countries.17 In contrast, well-resourced countries have higher technical support and data expertise, enabling them to analyse and publish data quickly. Consequently, it is more common for researchers from the Global North to analyse and interpret data from the Global South than the reverse.14 Data sharing benefits often accrue primarily to those who perform the analysis because they possess the expertise and tools to extract value from the data, which are not generally as readily accessible to those who generate the data. The disconnect between where the data are generated (often where it should be used) and where they are analysed can compromise the interpretation of data outputs, prevent insights from reaching practitioners and decision-makers, and hinder the career development of scientists in countries with fewer resources.19 These asymmetries create an uneven playing field, compromising several stages of the knowledge production cascade and ultimately affecting data sharing.4 20 Failing to consider the context of global health inequalities when sharing data under current mandates risks exacerbating existing disparities.6 21 To share data responsibly, a ‘3E’ framework must place equity and ethics at its core, redefining efficiency as a means to serve these goals.14

Equitable data sharing

Equity aims to equalise opportunities by allocating resources according to different needs. As data exist within a cyclical process, embedding equity in data sharing requires effort at every stage of the data chain, including design, collection, processing, analysis and the use of resulting insights (figure 2). Guided by the principle of subsidiarity,22 which promotes the leadership and autonomy of those closest to the problem, the ultimate goal is for local actors to manage and analyse their data while ensuring collective benefits through collaboration where necessary. Therefore, responsible data sharing requires balancing the rights and responsibilities of data contributors, generators and analysts.23 However, most data-sharing guidelines, toolkits and checklists2432 focus on making data available to those who want to analyse the data (online supplemental text 1) and very few of them include an equity lens3338 (table 1). To strengthen this dimension, the focus must evolve from a purely technical interpretation of ‘accessibility’ towards a sovereignty-aware, barrier-reducing and equity-centred approach.

Ethical data sharing

As health information can be linked to individuals, shared data should adhere to appropriate consent protocols and be managed securely to minimise re-identification risks following procedures described in several guidelines (online supplemental text 1). However, data protection and privacy laws vary considerably globally3941 and usually do not provide clear guidance on how data and biological specimens should be shared, especially concerning secondary analysis and cross-border sharing.41 Uncertainty about the application of data protection laws and fears of sanctions limit the widespread sharing and use of data, thereby compromising global health research efforts.42 While safeguarding against the misuse of personal information is essential, the benefits of clinical trial data sharing—such as protecting patients from redundant physical risks and maximising the utility of collected data—must be carefully weighed against the potential privacy risks of re-identification.43 Excessively restricting data collection and sharing can conflict with other equally valuable social goals,8 as societies that do not make the most of their health information assets risk exposing their citizens to inefficient or harmful care.44 More empirical evidence is needed on the actual risks of re-identification, moving beyond assumptions, particularly when data is shared through secure, remote-access systems.

Efficient data sharing

Adequate infrastructure and skills to manage, analyse and interpret shared data is needed for timely and accurate data exchange. Data have to be transmitted without loss or corruption, accessible when needed, and protected from unauthorised access or manipulation using dependable data management systems. Interoperability—of both data and platforms—is essential for seamless sharing across systems and organizations, eliminating the need for extensive reconfiguration. Appropriate governance, mandates and standards are needed to ensure that data and metadata are findable, accessible, usable and preserved for long-term use by others45 (see online supplemental text 1 for data interoperability, preservation and usability frameworks). However, fragmentation and inconsistency in the norms for health data sharing,17 together with data dispersion, provenance and heterogeneity across different population health topics (each of which varies in methodology, format and data management protocols),18 impose significant technical challenges in terms of sharing, integrating and managing multidisciplinary data.46

Collective action throughout the data life cycle

Equitable health data sharing is not just a technical challenge; it is also a social, ethical and political endeavour that must be carefully planned. Intentional actions must be taken throughout the whole data cycle and ecosystem (figure 3) to make data sharing possible, easy, normative, rewarding and equitable.14 47

Health data sharing ecology. A dynamic network of diverse stakeholders, each playing distinct yet interconnected roles in the continuous cycle of collecting, managing, sharing and using data, defines the data sharing ecosystem. Mapping these actors helps clarify their responsibilities, complementarities and the exchange of value within the system. Data contributors (the individuals who are the subjects of the data, such as patients and citizens; their rights and privacy must be protected as a foundational principle) and data generators (the entities that create or capture data, such as healthcare providers, research institutions and government agencies). Data custodians and intermediaries include data collectors (governmental, private, non-profit or academic organisations that gather data from various generators), data intermediaries (platforms and service providers that store, process and facilitate data access, such as cloud providers and data brokers), and data stewards (entities responsible for the management, integrity and availability of a specific dataset). Data analysts and scientists transform raw data into insights, predictions and informed decisions. Finally, end data users leverage processed data for application-specific purposes, including policymaking and public health planning (Governments), advocacy, monitoring and impact assessment (non-profit organizations and civil society), product development (businesses) and communication (mass media). Governance and enabler actors of this data ecosystem include data regulators and policymakers (government agencies, data protection authorities and ethics committees that establish and enforce rules for data use and privacy), technology providers (software vendors and companies that build the essential tools and infrastructure for working with data), patient and advocacy groups (promote responsible data access, ethical use and patient rights), and investors and funding bodies (provide the necessary capital to support health data research and infrastructure).

Demonstrate the value of sharing

For data to break free of silos and achieve impact, its entire lifecycle—from collection to analysis—must be guided by public health needs (figure 4).8 This requires appreciating the value of data for decision making and close collaboration between researchers, analysts and health officials to transform raw data into accessible, actionable insights for end users.19 Viewing data as a public good with the potential to advance societal well-being creates a stronger ethical and practical incentive for sharing than frameworks based solely on individual control.5 However, balance is needed to ensure that the enthusiasm of researchers, organisations and funders for their specific data analysis and interests when using shared data does not inadvertently lead to coercive attitudes that can compromise country representatives and agencies.16 Clear codes of conduct and alignment with local needs can ensure that data sharing benefits the community, not individual agendas. Such alignment requires engaging communities from the outset in the design, governance and application of data30 following established methods and principles and real-world models.4851 Furthermore, interpreting and disseminating findings ethically and accurately by all end-users is key to avoiding unsupported conclusions that can compromise appropriate decision making. This includes mass media, which, due to the datafication of journalism,52 now functions as a powerful data consumer with the capacity to spread misinformation and disinformation, contributing directly to the ‘infodemic’ threat.53 Training health journalists in ethical reporting principles (https://healthjournalism.org/principles) that adhere to international regulations (eg, World Trade Organization), transparent corrections and digital media literacy can prevent the stigmatisation and economic damage caused by sensationalist news, such as the travel bans following the announcement of the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variant; online supplemental text 2).4 15 Finally, robustly demonstrating how data sharing improves public health outcomes54 beyond its citation advantage55—and actively championing these successes—can provide the necessary impetus to make such sharing the norm.

Collective action for equitable data sharing. Achieving a fair and equitable future through data sharing demands collective action across distinct levels. First, cultivating a shared data culture with community involvement and clear public benefit. Second, understanding the broader data cycle and ecosystem as well as its stakeholders. Third, building trustworthy systems with controlled access and recourse. Fourth, removing contextual barriers in settings where the needs are the highest with tailored technology and skills. Fifth, implementing models to share benefits equitably among all contributors. Sixth, committing to responsible, equity-focused leadership with aligned incentives. And finally, fostering research environments that value capacity building and collaboration as core outputs. Here, we summarise key actions for each of these different levels in order to drive an equitable global health data sharing system.

Zoom in and out to plan equitable data sharing

Getting the broadest possible view of the data cycle (including the nature of the data and the procedures for collecting them, processing and analysis; figure 3) is essential to identify contextual barriers and enablers, the complementarities that can be leveraged, and the processes that should be targeted for improvement.8 Several frameworks and active processes can help achieve this broader perspective (Data Lifecycle Maps, the DAMA-DMBOK,56 data ecosystem tools, data provenance and lineage tracking).57 The planning of data collection with the potential for sharing in mind facilitates future transference of the data, as it enables the provision of information to patients regarding the utilisation of their data and guides the collection of essential data for future use.58 It is important to acknowledge that data sharing comes at a cost (human and technical), especially for institutions challenged by core fragilities in data management capacity. This is only justified when the data is both technically reusable59 and scientifically trustworthy,60 the latter being ensured at the point of collection through sound experimental design, robust methodologies and rigorous quality control (figure 4). As many data sharing needs peak when the project that sustains data collection is approaching its end, alternative funding models are required to ensure sustainability without impeding access to data (eg, resource pooling, which distributes costs and benefits across a consortium, membership or data trusts, and fee-based revenue models, which recover operational costs from users).17 Making longer-term commitments to strengthen country data systems instead of investing in parallel setups can minimise the fragmentation that undermines the sharing and reuse of data.61

Build trustworthy data-sharing systems

Trust creates a cooperative environment where individuals who donate information and those who collect the data feel confident that their interests will be protected and promoted.8 19 However, trust should not be the prerequisite for data sharing, but result from reliable data exchange systems that replace the need for blind faith with demonstrable evidence of security, fairness and responsibility (figure 4). A three-level foundation is essential for building such trusted data environments.18 First, dependability (being reliable, secure and available while ensuring data integrity and confidentiality) is achieved when robust security measures are integrated with flexible, policy-driven access mechanisms62 (see online supplemental text 1 for dependability frameworks). Second, the provision of checks, balances and audit trails that define how data is accessed, used and changed is key to preventing errors, misuse and concentration of power, consequently building trust by creating safety and fairness. The third principle is accountability, which refers to clearly assigning actions to responsible individuals or entities and to impose meaningful consequences for any breaches of the rules, thus providing a mechanism for recourse. Effective governance ensures accountability by strategically balancing rulemaking, enforcement and incentives.30 61 When these elements (dependability, controls and accountability) work together, they create a powerful virtuous cycle: trust enables sharing because participants who feel safe are willing to share more and higher-quality data; better data drives better outcomes; and success reinforces trust.

Resolve contextual barriers for equitable data sharing

There is no one-size-fits-all solution for data sharing, as each model depends on the nature of the data and the intended use. Centralised repositories maximise transparency but often require sophisticated data curation skills and bandwidth. The peer-to-peer model often relies on informal networks that can marginalise less-established institutions. Secure remote access environments reduce risk of data loss and leakage by minimising downloading the data, but they have limitations, for instance, in conducting Individual Participant Data meta-analyses. Enhancing health data sharing demands a multifaceted strategy (figure 4) that strengthens, where lacking, infrastructure and institutional frameworks, such as policies, education and data steward positions (see refs. 6367 for real-world examples). A pivotal shift is underway from centralised data repositories in the Global North towards sovereign, federated storage within countries of origin which allows for remote, preapproved access and analysis without moving the data itself,47 62 thus respecting data custodianship, oversight and autonomy of data producers. Critical to this effort is a comprehensive strategy—informed by a multistakeholder review—to improve interoperability at the data (through harmonised standards and compatible systems),17 62 68 organisational (via unified approaches to reduce fragmentation)69 and legal level (by creating a harmonised ecosystem that protects citizens without stifling sharing through excessive regulation).44 Ethics committees are a vital, yet underutilised, resource for defining ethical guidelines, recommending (and potentially enforcing) data sharing plans, and strengthening public trust.68

Share benefits equitably

Ensuring an equitable distribution of benefits from shared data requires moving from transactional partnerships to outcome-oriented transformative collaborations16 which promote the localisation of value chains where the needs are the highest. The benefits extend beyond public health impact (figure 4) and can be directed to improve data literacy of communities or tailor health services to their specific needs.30 70 For data generators at risk of being marginalised in the data value chain,34 sustained capacity-building—not one-off training—can enhance technical and analytical skills through the development of data standards36 and collection tools,71 biorepositories, open-source digital health platforms,72 as well as training in bioinformatics, data management71 and ethical governance.50 Secondary data users can benefit from access to diverse datasets and the crucial contextual knowledge needed to interpret them accurately. Engaging data producers early through the use of data-sharing and biospecimen-sharing platforms such as the ADBEx platform (www.adbex.org) and coordinating data requests and analysis to avoid duplication are fundamental to building mutual benefits. Robust data-sharing agreements, though tedious to implement, are powerful tools for establishing terms acceptable to all parties.6 8 The widespread adoption of equitable agreement templates, such as the PHA4GE Microbial Data-Sharing Accord,36 alongside established frameworks for ethical benefit-sharing,50 73 can be mandated by institutions, funders, ethics committees and data repositories to effectively streamline and enforce this process.17 41 45 Although mutuality is a core principle, data sharing should not automatically be expected to yield equal gains. Instead, efforts must actively direct benefits to where structural disadvantages are most significant. International benefit enforcement mechanisms (table 1) should also acknowledge the inequalities of health capacities between nations to rectify existing imbalances.35 However, the absence of a direct, immediate benefit for data generators should not justify restricting data sharing, as the primary goal is to maximise public health impact.

Proactive leadership

For researchers and institutions with limited data capacity, gaining autonomy requires strategic planning throughout the entire data cycle (figure 4). This means action-oriented leadership to allocate the right resources, create the necessary conditions and implement consistent monitoring to track progress. This proactive approach moves them beyond reliance on external help and toward genuine self-determination.16 20 Taking an active role in question formulation, rather than merely supplying the raw data, is the key to inverting the traditional power structure of data sharing and analysis, thereby transforming data generators from passive subjects into architects of the research. The open science ecosystem also offers a significant strategic advantage to Global South researchers, who can avoid costly data collection and pursue innovative research avenues by repurposing existing datasets. This represents a unique opportunity to transform a traditional disadvantage into a tool for empowerment and global scientific engagement, therefore positioning less-resourced institutions and researchers as equal partners in analysis. Short-term wins which solve urgent problems, like data badges, simple agreements and targeted training, can build momentum and inform the long-term systemic changes, such as harmonising the legal ecosystem, establishing ethical governance and securing mandated funding. Monitoring progress indicators (online supplemental text 3) is key to guiding and sustaining efforts to adopt fair and equitable data practices.

Responsible science

The academic system should emphasise research on cost-effective, locally adapted technical solutions for data sharing and capacity building as a core research value, instead of basing success only on publications and securing more funding.5 15 17 20 Adopting sharing and reuse as key criteria in grant awards and career advancement—aligned with frameworks such as the DORA Declaration,74 the TOP Guidelines75 and the Hong Kong Principles76—can drive widespread compliance. When combined with feedback mechanisms that recognise and reward the tangible impact of shared data, these practices create a powerful and sustainable incentive for open science. A more collaborative academic mindset is essential to mitigate competitive data-hoarding and authorship inequity,77 foster sustainable capacity-building over short-term ‘quick fixes’,16 and curb the overstatement of results78—a practice that otherwise risks distorting public health priorities and eroding public trust. Finally, career advancement in public health should prioritise tangible public health impact over narrow academic metrics like the “impact factor”.79 The formal recognition of dissemination channels beyond scientific journals—such as national bulletins, professional conferences and knowledge-sharing platforms—is critical for developing a public health system that is both responsive and effective in its core mission.

It is to use, it is to share

Ursula K. Le Guin’s seminal Anarresti proverb—“Nothing is yours. It is to use. It is to share. If you will not share it, you cannot use it” (“The Dispossessed”, 1974)—encapsulates a philosophy of collectivism and mutual aid that provides a powerful framework for equitable data sharing. To realise this ideal, we must confront the existing imbalances in data and knowledge production capabilities across global partnerships.16 Achieving a just data ecosystem demands leadership and accountability from those with the least data capacity, supported by global allies which act as humble facilitators ceding control, investing in local systems and ensuring the process upholds the principle of subsidiarity.22 This shift must be backed by dedicated funding and clear intent, and it entails asserting sovereignty over data and biological samples, that is, respecting legal and governance frameworks that place custodianship in the hands of local donors and generators. The discourse must therefore move beyond mere data (and sample) access to focus on the people and processes that make data possible, particularly in systems with lower capacity.17 Such a people-centric data ecosystem will empower scientists worldwide to effectively use the data they collect, support their national governments at the science-policy interface and ensure no country is left behind in the digital transformation.20 In resource-limited settings, significant health improvements are frequently achieved by building systems that apply existing knowledge, not just by generating new data.6 23 The key is to strike a balance where data collection, sharing and analysis complements, rather than impedes, the deployment of available tools. As William Foege cautioned,80 data collection is only justified if it leads to action: “The reason for collecting, analyzing, and disseminating information on a disease is to control that disease. Collection and analysis should not be allowed to consume resources if action does not follow.”
