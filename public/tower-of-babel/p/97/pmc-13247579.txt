A mixed-corpus scientometric dataset of precision livestock farming publications and patents, 2006–2025
Authors: Babatope E Akinyemi, Janice M Siegford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis data article presents two harmonized corpora assembled from five major bibliographic and patent databases to support scientometric, bibliometric, and technology landscape research on Precision Livestock Farming (PLF) and farm animal welfare. The publication corpus (n = 1,738 records; 27 fields) integrates records retrieved from the Web of Science (WoS) Core Collection and Scopus, covering peer-reviewed articles published between 2006 and 2025. The patent corpus (n = 3,793 records; 24 fields) consolidates exports from Lens.org, Espacenet (EPO), and PATENTSCOPE (WIPO), spanning granted patents and patent applications filed across the same 20-year window. Both corpora underwent systematic cross-database deduplication, field-level harmonization, and quality verification prior to deposit. The deposited records represent the complete deduplicated dataset; the associated findings paper applies additional eligibility screening, yielding an analytical subset of n = 1,594 publications and n = 3,239 patents. Data are structured in CSV, with a detailed field dictionary, completeness statistics, and multi-valued field delimiters documented herein. Together, the corpora constitute the first publicly available mixed-corpus PLF dataset linking peer-reviewed academic output with patent activity, enabling replication of the analyses reported in the associated findings paper as well as independent investigations into topic modelling, co-authorship network analysis, technology forecasting, and longitudinal benchmarking of PLF innovation. Background

Precision Livestock Farming (PLF) is broadly defined as the application of continuous, automated monitoring technologies—including sensors, computer vision systems, wearable devices, and machine learning algorithms—to support real-time, evidence-based management of individual farm animals [9]. PLF systems have been deployed for dairy cow lameness detection, automated estrus detection, broiler gait scoring, swine cough monitoring, and a range of other welfare-relevant applications [10]. As PLF has matured from a laboratory concept into a commercially deployed technology class, both the volume of peer-reviewed literature and the number of PLF-related patent filings have grown substantially, creating a rich but fragmented information landscape [4].

Scientometric analysis—the quantitative study of scientific publication patterns using bibliometric indicators [11]—has been applied to characterize this literature, typically using a single bibliographic database. Studies by Tzanidakis et al. [12] and Stygar et al. [10] have reviewed PLF publications using narrative or systematic approaches, while bibliometric analyses drawing on WoS or Scopus alone have mapped co-authorship networks and keyword trends [1,2]. Parallel to the scientific literature, patent records constitute a complementary and largely underutilized data source for understanding PLF innovation: patents reflect commercially motivated inventive activity, are subject to independent prior-art examination, and provide structured metadata on technology domains (via International Patent Classification codes), inventors, applicants, and filing jurisdictions that is not available in publication databases [13].

A mixed-corpus approach—simultaneously analyzing publications and patents—captures the science–technology interface in ways that neither corpus alone can achieve. It can reveal whether academic research activity precedes or follows patent filings, identify which PLF sub-domains have transitioned from research to commercial development, and flag persistent gaps where scientific evidence has not yet been translated into commercially deployed systems [14,15]. Implementing such an approach requires comparable, cross-deduplicated corpora that share a common study window and consistent field representations, as well as sufficient metadata richness to support temporal slicing, geographic analysis, and thematic classification. To our knowledge, no such publicly available dataset previously existed for the PLF domain.

The present data article describes the assembly, structure, and documentation of two harmonized corpora constituting the empirical foundation of the associated findings paper [3]. Their publication as a standalone data article serves a distinct purpose: whereas the findings paper presents analytical results and their interpretation for a PLF audience, this data article addresses the methodological and structural properties of the datasets themselves—documenting field definitions, coverage statistics, deduplication decisions, and harmonization choices—so that independent researchers can critically evaluate data quality, identify appropriate use cases, and conduct analyses that were outside the scope of the original study.

Data Description

The deposit comprises four files: two primary data files and two metadata files. The primary files are plf_publications_merged.csv (publication corpus, n = 1,738 records, 27 fields) and plf_patents_final_clean.csv (patent corpus, n = 3,793 records, 24 fields). The metadata files are the data dictionary (Supplementary File S3 of [3]) and the Python pre-processing scripts used for deduplication and harmonization (Supplementary File S4 of [3]).

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Database selection

Database selection was guided by three criteria: (a) global coverage of the target domain, (b) complementarity with other selected databases, and (c) availability of a machine-readable bulk export facility. For publications, WoS Core Collection was selected as the primary source because it provides citation counts, Keywords Plus, and Research Areas fields not available elsewhere; Scopus was added to capture journals indexed exclusively in Scopus, consistent with the complementarity approach recommended for reducing systematic coverage bias [16]. For patents, Lens.org was selected as the primary source because it aggregates records from more than 95 national and regional patent offices and provides forward citation counts and family size metadata in a single export [17]; Espacenet was added for DOCDB family numbers (essential for cross-jurisdictional family-level deduplication); and PATENTSCOPE was added to maximize coverage of PCT applications, particularly from jurisdictions underrepresented in Lens.org.

Search strategy design and execution

The search strategy followed the PRISMA 2020 framework adapted for mixed bibliographic–patent corpora [18]. For both publications and patents, the Boolean search strings comprised three conceptual blocks connected by AND operators: (1) PLF technology terms (sensors, automated systems, real-time monitoring devices); (2) target species or general livestock terms; and (3) welfare- or health-outcome terms. Publication searches were executed in the Title, Abstract, and Author Keywords fields. Patent searches used the Title and Abstract fields in Lens.org and PATENTSCOPE, and the Title and Claims fields in Espacenet. For patent searches, an IPC classification filter was applied as a mandatory AND constraint restricting results to subclasses A01K (Animal Husbandry) and A01J (Dairy Husbandry); this reduced the Lens.org candidate universe from more than 53,000 candidate records to 1,005 active patents and was validated against a 25-patent seed set. All searches were conducted between January and April 2026. Complete Boolean search strings for all five databases are provided verbatim in Supplementary File S1 of the associated findings paper [3].

Data retrieval and export

Publication records were exported from WoS in BibTeX format and from Scopus in CSV format. Patent records were exported from Lens.org and Espacenet as CSV files and from PATENTSCOPE in Excel format. All export files were retained in their original format prior to processing and are available as raw data supplementary files in the Zenodo deposit to support audit of the pre-processing decisions documented below. Espacenet exports are capped at 500 records per download session; the 500 Espacenet records represent the maximum retrievable under this constraint. PATENTSCOPE was queried with the Single Family Member filter set to False, which maximizes recall but may introduce duplicate family members; this design decision is documented as a known limitation (see Section 5).

Cross-database deduplication

Publication deduplication used the DOI as the primary matching key. Records were considered duplicates if their normalized DOIs (lowercase, prefix-stripped) were identical. For the 2.0% of records lacking a DOI, title-based string matching was applied after removal of punctuation and stop words; pairs with a Jaro-Winkler similarity score ≥0.95 [19] were flagged as candidate duplicates and reviewed manually. This process identified 794 duplicate records (31.4% of the combined raw corpus), all of which were removed; the WoS record was retained as the primary source where both databases contained the same article, because WoS provides additional fields (Keywords_Plus, Times_Cited) not available in the Scopus export.

Patent deduplication applied a normalized identifier matching strategy implemented in Python (see Supplementary File S4 of [3]). For each record pair across databases, a match was declared if any of the following normalized identifiers were identical: Lens Display Key, PCT application number, primary publication number, or (for Espacenet records) DOCDB family number. This procedure identified 54 cross-database duplicate records (1.3% cross-database overlap rate), which were resolved by retaining the record from the database with greater field richness (Lens.org > PATENTSCOPE > Espacenet). The full patent exclusion log, including the resolution rationale for each duplicate, is provided in Supplementary File S2 of the associated findings paper [3].

Field harmonisation

After deduplication, all records were mapped to a common field schema. For the publication corpus, 27 target field names were defined to accommodate the union of WoS BibTeX tags and Scopus CSV column headers. Fields present in one database but absent in the other were retained with blank values for records from the other database, preserving information completeness. For the patent corpus, 24 target field names were defined. Source-specific fields (e.g., Lens_ID, DOCDB_Family_Number, PATENTSCOPE_App_Id) were retained as separate columns rather than merged, to preserve provenance and enable database-specific downstream analyses. Date fields were normalized to ISO 8601 format (YYYY-MM-DD) where possible; PATENTSCOPE’s DD.MM.YYYY date format was converted programmatically. Publication Year was extracted as an integer from the publication date field and added as a derived column (Year) to both corpora to facilitate temporal filtering without date-parsing dependencies.

Screening and eligibility assessment

Following deduplication and harmonization, records in both corpora were screened for relevance. Publication records were assessed for eligibility based on four criteria: (a) the record is a peer-reviewed journal article (Document_Type = ‘Article’); (b) the abstract or title contains substantive PLF content; (c) the article is in English; and (d) the publication year falls within 2006–2025. Patent records were assessed against three criteria: (a) the record relates to a device, system, or method applicable to monitoring or managing farm animal health, behavior, or welfare; (b) the record is in a livestock-relevant IPC subclass; and (c) the publication year falls within 2006–2025. Screening was conducted by two independent reviewers with discrepancies resolved by consensus. The PRISMA-adapted flow diagram documenting record counts at each stage is provided in the associated findings paper [3].

Quality assurance

Field completeness was calculated for each column as the proportion of non-null, non-empty values across all records. Fields with completeness below 90% were flagged in the data dictionary (Supplementary File S3 of [3]) and in Table 2, Table 3 of this article. To verify data integrity after Python-based processing, a random 10% sample of records (n = 174 publications; n = 379 patents) was manually compared against the source database records; no systematic data conversion errors were identified. The R analysis script (deposited on Zenodo alongside the datasets) includes a validation step that checks for the presence of all required columns and reports field-level completeness at runtime, providing an automated data integrity check for end users.

Limitations

The following limitations of the deposited datasets should be considered by users planning downstream analyses:

•Search coverage. The corpora represent the output of a defined five-database search strategy; records not indexed by WoS, Scopus, Lens.org, Espacenet, or PATENTSCOPE are not captured. Researchers requiring exhaustive coverage in niche jurisdictions (e.g., East Asian national patent databases not fully indexed by Lens.org) should supplement the patent corpus with targeted searches in national database portals.

•Language restriction. Both corpora are restricted to records with English-language title, abstract, or keyword fields. Non-English PLF literature (particularly from Chinese, German, and Spanish research communities) is systematically underrepresented. This restriction was applied because cross-linguistic normalization of keywords and author names was outside the scope of the current assembly.
