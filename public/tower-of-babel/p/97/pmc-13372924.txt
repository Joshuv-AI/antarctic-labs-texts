Age at diabetes onset modifies the association between hyperuricemia and diabetic retinopathy: a real-world cross-sectional study
Authors: Minghua Fan, Jun Li, Qiuju Zhou, Hui Shao, Lisha Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe role of hyperuricemia in diabetic retinopathy (DR) remains controversial, with limited evidence on how age at diabetes onset modifies this association. This study aimed to investigate the differential association of elevated uric acid (UA) with DR prevalence stratified by age at diagnosis.MethodsWe analysed cross-sectional data from 6,996 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, grouped by age at diabetes onset (younger-onset, < 65 years; older-onset, ≥ 65 years) and UA status (normal vs. elevated: ≥420 µmol/L for men; ≥380 µmol/L for women). Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for the cross-sectional association between UA and prevalent DR. Pre-specified subgroup analyses with multiplicative interaction terms were conducted to explore whether this association differed across strata, with exact P-values reported; these analyses are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative age cutoffs (40, 50, 60 years), and variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to evaluate collinearity between age at onset and diabetes duration.ResultsA total of 1,359 prevalent DR cases were identified among 6,996 participants. Elevated UA was significantly associated with higher odds of prevalent DR in younger-onset diabetes (aOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.17–1.73) but not in older-onset diabetes (aOR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.61–1.79). However, a formal test for interaction between UA and age-at-onset group was not statistically significant (P-interaction = 0.295), indicating that the present data do not establish age at onset as an effect modifier; the older-onset stratum was small (992 patients, 116 DR events) and its estimate was correspondingly imprecise. The younger-onset association was robust to additional adjustment for albuminuria (aOR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.12–1.67) and to a fully expanded model further adjusting for smoking, alcohol, urate-lowering therapy, and hypertension severity (aOR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.12–1.70). Restricted cubic spline modelling showed a positive, approximately linear UA–DR relationship with no significant departure from linearity (P-nonlinearity = 0.31). When stratified by DR severity, elevated UA was more strongly associated with proliferative DR (aOR = 2.39, 95% CI: 1.24–4.61) than with non-proliferative DR (aOR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.09–1.58). The E-value for the younger-onset association was 1.67 (lower confidence limit 1.38). Findings were consistent across alternative age cutoffs.ConclusionIn this cross-sectional analysis, elevated UA was independently associated with higher odds of prevalent DR among patients with younger-onset type 2 diabetes, whereas no significant association was detected in the smaller older-onset group. A formal interaction test was not significant, so the data do not establish age at onset as a true effect modifier, and the apparent contrast between strata should be interpreted cautiously. The younger-onset association was robust to adjustment for albuminuria and other potential confounders and was stronger for proliferative than non-proliferative disease. Given the cross-sectional design, prospective longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporality and to confirm whether this association is causal.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12020-026-04703-7.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), a microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), remains a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide [1]. Its pathogenesis involves chronic hyperglycaemia, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [2, 3]. Uric acid (UA), the end product of purine metabolism, has emerged as a potential modulator of microvascular damage. While UA exhibits antioxidant properties at physiological concentrations, hyperuricaemia or elevated UA paradoxically demonstrates pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory effects [4, 5]. Pre-clinical and animal studies have indicated that hyperuricaemia promotes endothelial injury by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, upregulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and inducing oxidative stress, key drivers of DR progression [6, 7].

Clinical evidence on the UA–DR relationship remains inconsistent. Some cross-sectional studies report hyperuricaemia as an independent correlate of DR [8], whereas others suggest a protective role attributable to UA’s antioxidant capacity [9]. Age at DM onset may shape disease trajectories. We note that the conventional literature on “young-onset” or “early-onset” type 2 diabetes generally applies a diagnostic age below 40 (or sometimes 50) years [10–12]; in the present study, the < 65-year group therefore does not correspond to that classical young-onset phenotype but rather distinguishes patients with diabetes onset before versus after the threshold at which guidelines define a clinically distinct older population. For clarity, we use the terms “younger-onset” (< 65 years) and “older-onset” (≥ 65 years) throughout. Relative to older-onset diabetes, younger-onset diabetes is generally characterised by longer cumulative exposure to hyperglycaemia and a higher microvascular complication burden [10–14], whereas older-onset diabetes tends to involve shorter disease duration, age-related comorbidities, and a potential “metabolic reserve” that may attenuate complication risk [13, 14]. Whether the cross-sectional UA–DR association varies across these subgroups has not been systematically examined. Accordingly, the present study aimed to investigate the differential association of elevated UA with prevalent DR stratified by age at diagnosis.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a cross-sectional, real-world study using electronic health records from Lishui People’s Hospital, Zhejiang Province, covering January 2018 to June 2023. Eligible participants were patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and complete baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory documentation. Key exclusion criteria included comorbid retinal disorders, history of ocular surgery, significant renal or hepatic impairment, autoimmune conditions (including type 1 diabetes), and missing essential data. From an initial pool of 7,891 T2DM patients, 6,996 met the eligibility requirements and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Lishui People’s Hospital (Ref: 2024-025-01). Owing to the anonymised nature of the data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Data collection and measurements

All clinical and laboratory data were obtained during routine clinical visits. Participants underwent a minimum 8-hour fast prior to blood sampling. Anthropometric measurements (height to the nearest 0.1 cm; weight to the nearest 0.1 kg) were obtained using standardised equipment. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m²). After 5 min of seated rest, blood pressure was measured twice using an automated device, separated by a 3-minute interval, and the mean of the two readings was used.

Medical histories regarding diabetes duration, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and comorbidities were obtained via structured interviews and validated questionnaires. Laboratory evaluations included fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, a full lipid panel (total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and serum UA. Fasting glucose and lipids were measured using enzymatic methods, HbA1c by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and UA enzymatically using the uricase–peroxidase method. The index date (index encounter) was defined as the date of the first eligible outpatient or inpatient diabetes-care visit during the study period (January 2018 to June 2023) at which a fundus examination for DR assessment was performed. Serum UA, fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipids, eGFR, and blood pressure were ascertained from measurements obtained on, or closest to, the index date; to minimise misclassification, laboratory values were required to fall within a window of ± 3 months of the index date, and the measurement closest to the index date was used. Diabetes duration was calculated as the interval between the date of the first recorded T2DM diagnosis (per the case-ascertainment criteria below) and the index date. Where multiple qualifying laboratory measurements were available within the window, the value nearest the index date was retained; repeated longitudinal measurements over follow-up were not used, because the analysis was cross-sectional and based on the index encounter. Serum UA was therefore measured at a single time point; this constitutes an inherent limitation given the known short-term biological variability of UA, and is discussed further in the Limitations section.

Definitions and ophthalmological assessment

T2DM was defined according to SUPREME-DM criteria, incorporating ICD-10-CM codes, laboratory results, and medication records. Specifically, cases were confirmed by: (a) one or more inpatient ICD-10 codes for T2DM; (b) two or more outpatient ICD codes recorded on separate dates within two years; or (c) a combination of outpatient codes with laboratory findings (fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, random glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) or prescription of glucose-lowering agents.

Participants were stratified by age at diabetes onset into younger-onset (< 65 years) and older-onset (≥ 65 years) groups. We deliberately avoid the label “young-onset” for the < 65-year group, because in the established literature young-onset (or early-onset) type 2 diabetes is conventionally defined by a diagnostic age below 40 years (and occasionally below 50 years) and is associated with a distinct, more aggressive phenotype [10–12]; our < 65-year category is considerably broader and is intended only to separate diabetes diagnosed before the threshold at which guidelines designate a clinically distinct older population. The 65-year threshold was selected a priori based on three considerations: (i) the World Health Organization and major diabetes guidelines (e.g., the American Diabetes Association “Older Adults” standard) define diabetes patients ≥ 65 years as a clinically distinct geriatric population for whom management goals and complication risk profiles differ from those of younger adults [15, 16]; (ii) the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of T2DM in the Elderly likewise use the 65-year threshold; and (iii) at this threshold, the older-onset group in our cohort retained sufficient sample size (n = 990) and DR events (n = 116) to support stable multivariable estimation. Because this threshold is higher than conventional young-onset cutoffs, we additionally pre-specified sensitivity analyses using alternative cutoffs (40, 50, and 60 years) to verify that the findings were not an artefact of the chosen threshold; these are reported in Table 4.

Elevated UA was defined as ≥ 420 µmol/L for men and ≥ 380 µmol/L for women, consistent with sex-specific thresholds widely applied in Chinese clinical practice. Diabetic retinopathy was assessed using fundus photographs obtained with a Canon CR-2 AF digital retinal camera. Image interpretation and DR grading followed the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) protocol. Two masked ophthalmologists independently evaluated all images, with disagreements resolved by a third senior specialist. The primary outcome was the presence of any DR (any-versus-none) at the index encounter. Severity-specific outcomes (mild/moderate/severe non-proliferative DR, proliferative DR, and diabetic macular oedema) were also abstracted where gradable; an analysis of the UA–DR association by DR severity was performed using the same grading, classifying eyes as no DR, non-proliferative DR (NPDR), or proliferative DR (PDR); diabetic macular oedema was not separately gradable from the single-field fundus photographs available and was therefore not analysed.

Medication exposure was ascertained from electronic prescribing records active at the index encounter and was therefore defined as current use (a prescription valid at, or most recently preceding, the index date) rather than historical or lifetime use. Medications were grouped into pre-specified pharmacological classes: glucose-lowering agents (metformin, insulin, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists [GLP-1RA], dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors [DPP4i], sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors [SGLT2i], and thiazolidinediones [TZD]), lipid-lowering agents (statins, fenofibrate, and ezetimibe), and anti-hypertensive agents. A patient was classified as a user of a given class if at least one qualifying agent within that class was prescribed; patients receiving combination therapy were counted as users of each relevant class. Detailed information on cumulative duration of therapy, dose, and adherence was not consistently available in the source records; consequently, exposure was modelled as a binary current-use variable, and this limitation is acknowledged in the Limitations section.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables are summarised as means ± standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Participants were categorised by age at T2DM onset and UA status as defined above. Group comparisons used the χ² test for proportions and Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the cross-sectional association between elevated UA and prevalent DR. A crude model was first fitted, followed by a fully adjusted model that included age, sex, BMI, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, LDL-C, triglycerides, HbA1c, diabetes duration, eGFR, and relevant medications. The eGFR was estimated using the Chinese-modified Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (C-MDRD) equation, which was developed and validated specifically in Chinese chronic kidney disease populations [17]. Because age at onset and diabetes duration are conceptually related, we evaluated potential collinearity by computing variance inflation factors (VIFs) for the covariates in the fully adjusted model and by examining the correlation between age at onset and diabetes duration; a VIF > 5 was considered indicative of problematic collinearity. To avoid dichotomising the exposure, and because UA may have a nonlinear relationship with DR, we additionally modelled serum UA as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles, and tested for departure from linearity using a likelihood-ratio test of the nonlinear spline terms [18, 19]. Because renal dysfunction influences both UA handling and DR, and because albuminuria is associated with DR independently of eGFR, we performed a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for urinary albumin–creatinine ratio category (normo-, micro-, and macroalbuminuria; normoalbuminuria as reference). To address residual confounding more broadly, we fitted a further expanded model adding smoking status, alcohol use, urate-lowering therapy, and hypertension severity grade; prior DR treatment was not included as a covariate because it is a consequence of prevalent DR rather than a confounder. To quantify robustness to unmeasured confounding, we computed E-values for the primary stratum-specific estimates (using the square-root-of-odds-ratio approximation, appropriate given the moderate prevalence of DR) and report the E-value for both the point estimate and the limit of the confidence interval closer to the null [20].

Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore whether the UA–DR association differed by age at onset (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex, eGFR category, and use of medications, with multiplicative interaction terms tested in the fully adjusted model. Because comparing a statistically significant association in one stratum with a non-significant association in another does not by itself establish effect modification, the difference between the younger- and older-onset estimates was tested formally using the method of Altman and Bland for the difference between two independent estimates [21, 22]. In the older-onset stratum, the number of DR events relative to the number of covariates was low (approximately 7 events per variable, below the conventional threshold of 10) [23, 24]; we therefore additionally fitted a Firth penalized-likelihood logistic regression in this stratum as a sensitivity analysis to guard against small-sample bias and separation. These subgroup and interaction analyses were exploratory and hypothesis-generating: they were not the primary objective, were not adjusted for multiple comparisons, and several strata contained small numbers of events; their results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Sensitivity analyses repeated the primary analysis using alternative age-at-onset cutoffs of 40, 50, and 60 years. All analyses were performed in R (v4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria); two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Significant differences were observed across groups for most baseline variables. Participants with elevated UA showed higher BMI, higher systolic blood pressure, higher triglycerides, lower HDL-cholesterol, and lower eGFR (all P < 0.001). Fasting glucose and HbA1c were lower in the elevated-UA strata, with the HbA1c difference reaching statistical significance (P < 0.001). LDL-cholesterol did not differ significantly across groups (P = 0.175). The proportion of males was higher in the elevated-UA strata (P < 0.001). The correlation between age at onset and diabetes duration was weak (r = − 0.14), and when both were entered together their VIFs were well below the conventional concern threshold (age at onset: VIF = 1.32; diabetes duration: VIF = 1.18), indicating that collinearity between these two terms was not a meaningful issue.

Cross-sectional association between uric acid and diabetic retinopathy

A total of 1,359 prevalent DR cases were identified (Table 2). In the younger-onset group, the prevalence of DR was 25.5% (197/774) among participants with elevated UA versus 20.0% (1,046/5,230) among those with normal UA. The crude OR for DR in the elevated-UA stratum was 1.37 (95% CI: 1.15–1.63); after adjustment for potential confounders, the association remained significant (aOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.17–1.73).

In the older-onset group, the prevalence of DR was 12.6% (24/190) among participants with elevated UA versus 11.5% (92/802) among those with normal UA. The crude OR was 1.12 (95% CI: 0.69–1.80), which was not statistically significant; the multivariable-adjusted OR was also non-significant and close to the null (aOR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.61–1.79). This stratum was small (992 patients, 116 DR events; approximately 7 events per covariate), so its adjusted estimate is imprecise and potentially unstable; a Firth penalized-likelihood model yielded a materially identical result (aOR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.63–1.80), indicating that the near-null estimate was not an artefact of small-sample bias. A formal test for the difference between the younger- and older-onset adjusted estimates was not statistically significant (P-interaction = 0.295). Accordingly, although elevated UA was significantly associated with prevalent DR in the younger-onset group but not in the older-onset group, the data do not provide statistical evidence that age at onset modifies the UA–DR association; the contrast between strata may partly reflect the limited power and precision of the older-onset analysis.

Subgroup and interaction analyses

The following subgroup and interaction analyses were exploratory and hypothesis-generating; given the absence of correction for multiple comparisons and the small number of events in several strata, they should be interpreted with corresponding caution. Subgroup analyses examining whether the UA–DR association differed by baseline characteristics are presented in Table 3. As noted above, the formal test for interaction between elevated UA and age-at-onset group was not statistically significant (P-interaction = 0.295). No significant interaction was observed by sex (P-interaction = 0.72); within the younger-onset group, the association was of similar magnitude in males (aOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.09–1.85) and females (aOR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.08–1.96). When serum UA was modelled as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines, there was a statistically significant overall association with DR (P = 0.033) but no significant departure from linearity (P-nonlinearity = 0.31); the estimated odds of DR rose monotonically across the observed UA range (for example, relative to the cohort median of approximately 317 µmol/L, the odds ratio was about 1.18 at 420 µmol/L and about 1.49 at 600 µmol/L), consistent with an approximately log-linear relationship rather than a threshold or U-shaped pattern.

Sensitivity analyses using alternative age cutoffs

To address the potential influence of the age cutoff selection, the primary analysis was repeated using cutoffs of 40, 50, and 60 years (Table 4). A significant UA–DR association in the younger-onset group was most evident at the 60- and 65-year cutoffs (for example, at the 65-year cutoff, aOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.17–1.73), whereas at lower cutoffs (40 and 50 years) the association was statistically significant in the older-onset group instead; this pattern is consistent with the bulk of DR events and elevated-UA patients lying in the broad middle-age range. Importantly, the formal test for interaction between elevated UA and onset group was non-significant at every cutoff examined (all P-interaction ≥ 0.30), reinforcing that the data do not support a true modifying effect of age at onset and that the dichotomous stratification should be regarded as descriptive rather than as evidence of effect modification.

When the outcome was stratified by DR severity, elevated UA was associated with both non-proliferative DR (aOR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.09–1.58 versus no DR) and, more strongly, proliferative DR (aOR = 2.39, 95% CI: 1.24–4.61 versus no DR), suggesting a graded relationship in which the association is most pronounced for advanced disease. Among the 1,359 DR cases, 1,295 (95.3%) were classified as non-proliferative and 64 (4.7%) as proliferative. To address potential residual confounding by renal disease beyond eGFR, we additionally adjusted for albuminuria category (urinary albumin–creatinine ratio): albuminuria was present in 32.6% of the cohort (microalbuminuria 26.3%, macroalbuminuria 6.3%) and, as expected, was independently associated with DR (macroalbuminuria aOR ≈ 2.8). After this additional adjustment, the younger-onset UA–DR association was essentially unchanged (aOR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.12–1.67), as was the null older-onset estimate (aOR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.57–1.69). The younger-onset association also persisted in a fully expanded model that further adjusted for smoking, alcohol use, urate-lowering therapy, and hypertension severity (aOR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.12–1.70). The E-value for the younger-onset point estimate was 1.67 (E-value for the lower confidence limit, 1.38), indicating that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both elevated UA and DR by a risk ratio of at least 1.38, beyond the measured covariates, to reduce the observed association to non-significance; for the older-onset estimate the corresponding E-value was 1.18 (lower confidence limit 1.00), consistent with its lack of robustness.

Discussion

This cross-sectional, real-world study showed that elevated UA was independently associated with higher odds of prevalent DR among patients with younger-onset, but not older-onset, type 2 diabetes. The cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference; exposure (UA) and outcome (DR) were ascertained at the same time point, so temporality cannot be established and our findings should be interpreted strictly as associations with prevalent disease rather than as evidence of incident risk [25]. Reverse causation is a particularly important consideration: more advanced DR, and the renal dysfunction, altered metabolic status, and changes in medication use that frequently accompany advanced diabetic complications, may themselves raise serum UA by reducing renal urate excretion. The observed association may therefore be bidirectional, and the possibility that DR severity and renal dysfunction influence UA levels, rather than the reverse, cannot be excluded.

Hyperuricaemia is frequently observed in individuals with diabetes, and its potential role in the pathophysiology of diabetic microvascular complications has attracted growing interest [26, 27]. A meta-analysis has reported a significant association between hyperuricaemia and an increased risk of peripheral neuropathy in patients with type 2 diabetes [28]. UA is widely recognised as a key biomarker for gout; a serum UA concentration of 6 mg/dL is commonly used as the diagnostic threshold for hyperuricaemia and as the treatment target for urate-lowering therapy. Beyond its established link with gout, elevated UA has also been associated with diabetes and its complications, including diabetic peripheral neuropathy and diabetic nephropathy [29–31].

Whereas Rivera-De-la-Parra et al. identified hyperuricaemia (UA ≥ 7.8 mg/dL) as a correlate of referable DR (OR = 2.81, P = 0.049) in a cross-sectional design [32], our study adds to this body of evidence by characterising the cross-sectional UA–DR association in a large real-world cohort and by examining whether it varies with age at diabetes onset. Although the association reached significance in the younger-onset group but not the older-onset group, a formal interaction test was not significant, so we are cautious not to over-interpret this contrast as true effect modification; the most that can be said is that a UA–DR association was clearly detectable in the larger younger-onset stratum. Notably, Zhu et al. recently demonstrated that long-term UA variability (measured by average real variability) independently predicts DR progression [33], particularly in males and in those with shorter diabetes duration. Our cross-sectional observations are broadly consistent with a contributory role of UA, particularly given that the association was stronger for proliferative than for non-proliferative disease; however, because our data are cross-sectional, we cannot determine whether elevated UA precedes DR or whether renal dysfunction accompanying more advanced disease drives both higher UA and DR.

Several biological mechanisms have been proposed that could, in principle, link hyperuricaemia to retinal microvascular injury, for example, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by soluble UA with downstream release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and impairment of endothelial nitric-oxide–dependent vasodilation [34–37]. We note explicitly, however, that the present study contains no mechanistic biomarkers, no longitudinal data, and no experimental validation, so these pathways cannot be tested with our data and are offered only as biological context rather than as an explanation of our observational findings. Any such interpretation must also be weighed against the modest effect sizes we observed (adjusted ORs in the ~ 1.3 range). If a true age-dependent difference exists, it might relate to the longer cumulative metabolic exposure of younger-onset patients versus the competing comorbidities, survivor effects, and possible “metabolic reserve” of older-onset patients, but this remains speculative and requires dedicated mechanistic and prospective study.

We had hypothesised that lipid-lowering therapy might modify the UA–DR association, because fenofibrate is a well-documented uricosuric agent that lowers serum UA, and atorvastatin and several other statins exhibit modest urate-lowering effects [38, 39], while statins additionally have pleiotropic anti-inflammatory and endothelial-protective properties. In the present data, the interaction between elevated UA and lipid-lowering therapy was statistically significant, with the UA–DR association apparent among non-users but not among users; however, the user stratum was small (n = 558, 8.0% of the cohort) with very few DR events, yielding a wide and unstable estimate, and this was one of multiple exploratory comparisons not corrected for multiplicity. We therefore refrain from interpreting these data as evidence that statins or fenofibrate attenuate UA-associated retinal injury; the finding is hypothesis-generating only and would require confirmation in adequately powered, preferably prospective, studies before any inference about therapeutic benefit could be made.

Should these associations be confirmed in longitudinal studies, the age-stratified pattern would suggest that UA may be more informative for risk stratification in younger-onset diabetes, particularly among those with renal impairment. Any inference that concurrent lipid-lowering therapy attenuates UA-associated DR risk is, at present, purely hypothesis-generating; given the cross-sectional design and the small treated subgroup, our data do not support recommendations for therapeutic adjustment, which would require confirmation in prospective studies.

An important conceptual consideration is the close interrelationship between age at diabetes onset and diabetes duration. In clinical practice these two variables are intrinsically linked, for a given attained age, an earlier age at onset necessarily implies a longer duration of diabetes, and both are strong, partly overlapping determinants of microvascular complications, with duration in particular being a dominant driver of DR risk [40]. When age at onset and diabetes duration were entered together (without simultaneously including attained age, with which they are mathematically collinear), the variance inflation factors were modest (1.32 for age at onset and 1.18 for duration) and the correlation between them was weak (r = − 0.14), indicating that statistical multicollinearity between these two terms was not severe. Nonetheless, this does not fully resolve their conceptual overlap, and, particularly in light of the non-significant interaction test, we interpret the age-at-onset stratification as a pragmatic descriptor of clinically distinct patient groups rather than as an independent biological axis or as established evidence of effect modification.

The potential for detection and surveillance bias also warrants consideration. Patients with younger-onset diabetes, or with elevated UA and its associated comorbidities, may differ systematically from other patients in healthcare-seeking behaviour, frequency of laboratory monitoring, and uptake of retinal examination; differential attendance for DR screening is well documented and varies with age, comorbidity, and socioeconomic factors [41, 42]. Because DR can only be recorded when an eye examination is performed, more intensively monitored patients may have a higher apparent prevalence of DR, which could inflate or otherwise distort the observed UA–DR association. As we could not fully characterise screening intensity in this real-world dataset, we cannot exclude a contribution of such ascertainment bias to our findings.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; observed associations between UA and DR cannot be interpreted as evidence that elevated UA causes DR. Throughout the manuscript, we have framed our findings as cross-sectional associations rather than risk relationships, and we have replaced “incident DR” with “prevalent DR” because longitudinal follow-up data were not available.

Second, serum UA was measured at a single time point. UA exhibits non-trivial short-term biological variability and is influenced by acute and reversible factors including hydration status, recent diet (notably purine and alcohol intake), recent exercise, concurrent acute illness, renal haemodynamics, and medication use; in addition, longitudinal UA variability has itself been linked to adverse outcomes [43, 44]. A single-point measurement is therefore subject to substantial non-differential exposure misclassification and regression-dilution bias, which would generally attenuate associations toward the null. This consideration is particularly salient here because the adjusted odds ratios we observed were modest (in the ~ 1.3 range): with effect sizes of this magnitude, even moderate measurement error in UA could materially affect both the strength and the apparent statistical significance of the association, and could plausibly account for some of the contrast between the younger- and older-onset strata. We accordingly temper our conclusions and recommend that future studies use repeated UA measurements and variability metrics to reduce misclassification.

Third, although the statistical evidence for collinearity between age at diabetes onset and diabetes duration was reassuring, when entered together, the correlation between them was weak (r = − 0.14) and their variance inflation factors were 1.32 and 1.18 respectively, well below the conventional concern threshold of 5, this does not eliminate the close conceptual relationship between the two variables discussed above. Residual shared variance may still contribute to the age-stratified estimates, and we accordingly interpret the age-at-onset comparison cautiously, especially given that the formal interaction test was not significant.

Fourth, despite multivariable adjustment, residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors cannot be excluded. Renal dysfunction is especially relevant here because it occupies a central position in the confounding structure: impaired renal function reduces urinary urate excretion and raises serum UA, while it is simultaneously and independently associated with the presence and severity of DR. Albuminuria in particular has been shown to predict DR independently of, and even at levels below conventional thresholds for, reduced eGFR [45, 46], and uric acid is closely associated with both eGFR and albuminuria [47]. To address this, we additionally adjusted for albuminuria category; the younger-onset association was essentially unchanged (aOR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.12–1.67), and a fully expanded model further adjusting for smoking, alcohol use, urate-lowering therapy, and hypertension severity left the estimate similarly stable (aOR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.12–1.70). The corresponding E-value (1.67; lower confidence limit 1.38) indicates that only a moderately strong unmeasured confounder could fully explain the younger-onset association. To probe this further, we conducted a probabilistic bias analysis simulating a hypothetical unmeasured confounder under literature-informed assumptions [48, 49] (Supplementary Material); the younger-onset association remained materially unchanged across plausible scenarios. Residual confounding by unmeasured dietary, lifestyle, or socioeconomic factors cannot be fully excluded.

Fifth, the primary outcome was the presence of any DR. We additionally performed a severity-resolved analysis (no DR, non-proliferative DR, proliferative DR); diabetic macular oedema could not be reliably ascertained from single-field fundus photography and was not analysed. The association of elevated UA was stronger for proliferative DR (aOR = 2.39) than for non-proliferative DR (aOR = 1.31), although the number of proliferative cases was small (64 cases) and the corresponding estimate is therefore imprecise. Sixth, the subgroup and interaction analyses were exploratory and not corrected for multiple comparisons, and several strata, particularly the lipid-lowering-user stratum and individual glucose-lowering classes such as SGLT2i, TZD, and DPP4i, contained very few users and DR events, producing wide confidence intervals and, in some cases, unstable estimates. These analyses are hypothesis-generating, and the corresponding estimates should not be over-interpreted. Medication exposure was additionally captured only as current binary use, without information on duration, dose, or adherence, which may have led to non-differential misclassification of treatment status.

Finally, the data originate from a single tertiary centre in Zhejiang Province, which may limit generalisability to other regions or populations; and although ETDRS-based grading by masked ophthalmologists provides a standardised assessment, mild or peripheral lesions may still be missed by single-field photography.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional, real-world study, elevated serum uric acid was independently associated with higher odds of prevalent diabetic retinopathy among patients with younger-onset type 2 diabetes, whereas no significant association was detected in the smaller older-onset group. A formal interaction test was not significant, so the data do not establish age at onset as a true effect modifier, and the contrast between strata should be interpreted as descriptive rather than as evidence of effect modification. The younger-onset association was robust to adjustment for albuminuria and other potential confounders, was stronger for proliferative than for non-proliferative disease, and had an E-value indicating that only a moderately strong unmeasured confounder could fully explain it. Because the design is cross-sectional, temporality cannot be established and reverse causation cannot be excluded; prospective longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether this association is causal and whether UA-directed management can improve DR outcomes.
