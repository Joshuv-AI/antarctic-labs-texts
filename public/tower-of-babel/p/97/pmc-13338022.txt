Tumor-draining lymph nodes shape the tumor microenvironment through enrichment of tumor-reactive effector-exhausted CD8+ T cells
Authors: Deyang Kong, Lin Feng, Yu Sun, Huiru Zhang, Jialiang Fan, Yueyi An, Yunlong Wu, Yuelu Zhu, Qi Zhang, Renshen Xiang, Shuaibing Lu, Wenjing Yang, Wei Pei, Zheng Wang, Shengdian Wang, Jianqing Xu, Haizeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tumor-draining lymph nodes are central to anti-tumor immunity and influence the response to immunotherapy. Colorectal cancers with microsatellite instability and microsatellite stability differ substantially in tumor biology, prognosis and sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade. However, the differences in tumor-draining lymph nodes between these two subtypes and their contribution to distinct anti-tumor immune responses remain poorly understood. Here, we performed an integrative multi-omics analysis to systematically compare tumor-draining lymph nodes and tumor microenvironments in these two colorectal cancer subtypes. We identified a distinct population of CD8+ T cells that displayed terminal exhaustion while retaining strong anti-tumor activity. These cells were highly tumor reactive, associated with tertiary lymphoid structure formation and enriched in microsatellite instability colorectal cancers. Lineage tracing indicated that they originated in tumor-draining lymph nodes. In microsatellite instability colorectal cancers, tumor-draining lymph nodes contained more pre-exhausted T cells, dendritic cells with enhanced antigen-presenting capacity and fewer regulatory T cells, thereby providing both precursors and a supportive niche for the development of these effector-exhausted T cells. Meanwhile, within tumors, IL21+CD4+ T cells and CXCL10+ myeloid cells promoted the differentiation and recruitment of these cells through cytokine signaling and increased major histocompatibility complex expression. Colorectal cancer cells with microsatellite instability further reinforced this immune ecosystem by activating the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes pathway. Collectively, our findings identify effector-exhausted CD8+ T cells as key mediators of favorable immunity in microsatellite instability colorectal cancers and highlight the pivotal role of tumor-draining lymph nodes in shaping the tumor immune landscape.

Introduction

Lymph nodes are critical sites for antigen presentation and T cell priming and serve as central hubs in the cancer-immunity cycle.1 Increasing evidence highlights the pivotal role of tumor-draining lymph nodes (TdLNs) in orchestrating anti-tumor immunity and modulating responses to immunotherapy. TdLNs can function as reservoirs for CD8+ T cells, where initially primed cells can traffic to the tumor microenvironment (TME) for further activation and expansion into effector-like cytotoxic T cells.2,3 CD4+ T cells in TdLNs also exert key immunoregulatory functions that influence anti-tumor responses through multiple mechanisms.4,5 Critically, pre-exhausted T cells (Tpex) residing in TdLNs have been identified as key mediators of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) efficacy.2,3 Upon ICB treatment, these T cells are activated and expanded within TdLNs and subsequently migrate into the tumor, where they exert anti-tumor effects.6,7 This process is often accompanied by clonal replacement, whereby intratumoral T cells are supplanted by clonally expanded TdLN-derived counterparts. Targeted delivery of anti–PD-L1 to TdLNs has also been shown to enhance therapeutic outcomes,8 further underscoring the importance of TdLNs in modulating anti-tumor immunity and optimizing immunotherapeutic efficacy.

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is categorized into microsatellite-stable (MSS) and microsatellite-instable (MSI) subtypes,9 which differ profoundly in tumor biology, patient prognosis, and therapeutic response.10,11 Notably, while MSI tumors demonstrate remarkable sensitivity to ICB,12–18 the majority of MSS tumors remain resistant to such therapies.13 These distinct immunological behaviors make MSI and MSS CRC ideal models to investigate mechanisms of anti-tumor immunity and immunotherapy response. Considerable efforts have been made to characterize the distinct immune landscapes of MSI and MSS CRC, revealing that MSI tumors are often associated with robust immune infiltration and active anti-tumor responses.19–24 Clinically, MSI patients tend to have more lymph nodes retrieved but a lower proportion of positive nodes, suggesting enhanced systemic immune surveillance.25 However, most existing studies have focused on the primary tumors, and the immunological differences in TdLNs between MSI and MSS CRC and their impact on the TME remain poorly understood, partly due to the difficulty of acquiring fresh TdLN specimens.

Another emerging concept is the heterogeneity of exhausted T cells. Traditionally viewed as dysfunctional, exhausted CD8+ T cells (CD8+ Tex) are now understood to encompass a spectrum of states, from stem-like Tpex to terminally exhausted subsets.26–30 Tpex cells retain self-renewal capacity and have been associated with improved prognosis and superior responses to ICB.29–31 Intriguingly, a subset of phenotypically terminally exhausted CD8+ T cells that paradoxically retained potent anti-tumor activity (IFN-γ+TIM-3+CD8+ T cells) have been identified in multiple myeloma,32 challenging the notion that terminal exhaustion equates to irreversible dysfunction. However, whether such effector-like exhausted CD8+ T cells exist in solid tumors and how different Tex states differ between MSI and MSS CRC remain unanswered questions.

To address these gaps, we performed an integrative analysis of 195,298 single cells using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and paired T/B cell receptor sequencing (scTCR/BCR-seq), together with complementary approaches including spatial transcriptomics (ST) and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF). We systematically compared the TME and TdLNs between MSI and MSS CRC and found that the immune status of TdLNs shapes the immune composition of the TME and, together with the TME, drives the expansion of tumor-reactive effector-exhausted T cells (Texeff). MSI TdLNs were enriched with CD4+ and CD8+ Tpex and exhibited reduced regulatory T cells (Tregs), providing both a sufficient precursor pool and a favorable niche for Texeff development. Meanwhile, the MSI TME contained abundant IL-21+CD4+ Texeff cells and CXCL10+ macrophages that further facilitated CD8+ Texeff differentiation and recruitment. MSI tumor cells also contributed by upregulating interferon-stimulated genes and activating the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) pathway. Together, the TdLNs and TME form an integrated ecosystem that supports the generation and accumulation of CD8+ Texeff cells in MSI CRC.

Results

Single-cell atlas of TdLNs and primary tumors in MSI and MSS CRC

To comprehensively compare the anti-tumor immune landscapes of MSI and MSS CRC, we performed single-cell analysis on samples from 16 CRC patients (7 MSI, 9 MSS). We collected colorectal tumor (CRT) and normal (CRN) tissue samples and isolated CD45+ and CD45- cells via flow cytometry. Additionally, we obtained non-metastatic TdLNs and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) samples from these patients. In total, 58 samples were collected, comprising 23 CRT, 12 CRN, 12 PBMC and 11 TdLN samples. After stringent quality control, we obtained transcriptomic profiles from 195,298 single cells. Based on gene expression patterns, cells were clustered into eight major lineages: conventional αβT cells (CD8+ and CD4+ T cells), unconventional T and natural killer (NK) cells (γδT/NK cells), B/plasma cells, myeloid cells, mast cells, epithelial cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 1b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 1a). TCRα and/or TCRβ sequences were successfully assembled in 66,501 of 78,044 αβT cells (85.2%), with a median assembly success rate of 86.8% across samples. Paired TCRα and TCRβ sequences were identified in 52,372 αβT cells (67.1%), which were used for subsequent clonotype analysis.

TdLNs were primarily composed of conventional αβT cells and B/plasma cells. PBMCs were enriched for αβT and γδT/NK cells, with a low proportion of B/plasma and myeloid cells. CRN contained the highest proportion of B/plasma cells, whereas CRT showed dominant infiltration by conventional αβT cells, particularly CD8+ T cells, followed by B/plasma cells and myeloid cells (Fig. 1d, and Supplementary Fig. 1b, c).

Given their central roles in anti-tumor immunity, CD8+ T cells were further classified into seven subclusters (Fig. 1e, f). CD8T_CCR7 and CD8T_GZMK subsets were predominantly found in TdLNs, CD8T_FGFBP2 was mainly detected in PBMCs, CD8T_CD160 was most abundant in CRN, while CD8T_CXCL13 and CD8T_HSPA1A were enriched in CRT (Fig. 1f, g). CD8+ T cells from different tissues exhibited distinct activation and functional states. TdLN-resident CD8+ T cells expressed low levels of exhaustion markers, while those from PBMCs showed high levels of effector molecules. Tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells co-expressed the highest levels of effector and exhaustion markers compared to those in other tissues, likely reflecting sustained antigen exposure within the TME (Fig. 1f, g).

Distinct exhaustion states of CD8+ T cells in CRC TdLNs and TME

To further dissect the phenotype and function of CD8+ T cells in CRC, we applied four distinct scoring metrics to evaluate naïve, memory, effector, and exhaustion characteristics of CD8+ T cells, respectively (Fig. 2a–c, Supplementary Fig. 2a, b, and Supplementary Table 2). Based on these scores, six transcriptionally distinct CD8+ T cell subtypes were defined (Fig. 2d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 2c). Subtype 1, expressing high levels of naïve-associated genes (e.g., SELL, LEF1, TCF7 and CCR7), was classified as naïve CD8+ T cells (CD8_Tnaive). Subtype 2 exhibited elevated TCF7 and minimal exhaustion markers (e.g., TOX, PDCD1), resembling tumor-specific memory T cells (Ttsm),31 and was classified as CD8_Ttsm. Subtype 3, expressing TCF7, GZMK, TOX and PDCD1, was classified as the progenitor of exhausted CD8+ T cells (CD8_Tpex). Subtype 4 exhibited moderate expression of both effector and exhaustion markers and was termed intermediately exhausted CD8+ T cells (CD8_Texint). Subtype 5 showed a relative “terminal” exhausted profile. Compared to subtype 4 (CD8_Texint), subtype 5 showed a transcriptional profile with relatively lower effector gene expression and higher exhaustion signatures, and was therefore annotated as terminally exhausted CD8+ T cells (CD8_Texterm). Notably, subtype 6 expressed the highest levels of both effector and exhaustion markers, indicating a terminally exhausted yet functionally active state, and was designated as CD8_Texeff. CD8_Tnaive, CD8_Ttsm and CD8_Tpex cells were enriched in TdLNs, while CD8_Texint, CD8_Texeff and CD8_Texterm cells predominated in the TME (Fig. 2g). The presence of CD8+ T cells in various exhaustion states within CRT and TdLNs was further confirmed using mIF (Fig. 2h, i). Pseudotime analysis revealed a progressive differentiation trajectory from CD8_Tnaive to CD8_Ttsm and CD8_Tpex, transitioning into CD8_Texint, and ultimately diverging into either CD8_Texeff or CD8_Texterm (Fig. 2j, and Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). These trajectories aligned with the bifurcated differentiation model proposed in chronic viral infection models.33 Together, these results suggest that CD8+ T cells in TdLNs and the TME exist in a continuum of exhaustion states, with TdLNs likely serving as a reservoir for tumor-infiltrating effector populations.

CD8_Texeff cells demonstrate potent anti-tumor function and proliferative capacity

Among CD8+ T cell subtypes, CD8_Texeff cells displayed a distinct transcriptomic profile characterized by high expression of effector molecules (e.g., GNLY, GZMB, GZMA, NKG7, PRF1, KLRD1, IFNG), highlighting their capacity for tumor cell elimination. Concurrently, these cells expressed high levels of immune checkpoint molecules, such as PDCD1, LAG3, CTLA4, TIGIT and HAVCR2 (Figs. 2d and 3a). Notably, CD8_Texeff cells also demonstrated active interactions with other cells: high CXCR6 expression may facilitate their recruitment and retention within the TME, while elevated IL2RB expression suggests enhanced responsiveness to IL-2 and IL-15, potentially promoting their activation and expansion. Pathway enrichment analysis confirmed the activation and cytotoxicity of CD8_Texeff cells (Fig. 3b). Transcriptional profiling identified BATF and ZNF121 as key regulators of CD8_Texeff cells (Fig. 3c, and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), and BATF is a well-established transcription factor driving effector CD8+ T cell differentiation.35 Using mIF, we confirmed that CD8_Texeff cells expressed cytotoxic molecules such as GzmB (Fig. 2h) and were significantly enriched in the tumor and invasive margin (Fig. 3d, and Supplementary Table 3), further supporting their contribution to local anti-tumor immunity.

To further assess the functional status of CD8_Texeff cells, we performed flow cytometry on seven CRC tumor samples (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 3c, and Supplementary Table 4). Based on TCF-1 and PD-1 expression, we first defined TCF-1+PD-1⁻CD8+ T cells as CD8_Tnaive (Q1), TCF-1+PD-1+CD8+ T cells as CD8_Tpex (Q2), and TCF-1⁻PD-1+CD8+ T cells as an aggregated exhausted pool containing CD8_Texint/Texeff/Texterm cells (Q3) (Fig. 3e). We observed that this aggregated exhausted pool (Q3) contained a higher proportion of effector molecule–expressing cells than the CD8_Tpex population (Q2) (Supplementary Fig. 3d).

We then further subdivided Q3 according to PD-1 and GzmB expression into three subsets: TCF-1⁻PD-1lowCD8+ T cells (Q4), corresponding to CD8_Texint; TCF-1⁻PD-1hiGzmB+CD8+ T cells (Q5), considered the counterpart of the CD8_Texeff subset; and TCF-1⁻PD-1hiGzmB⁻CD8+ T cells (Q6), corresponding to the CD8_Texterm subset (Fig. 3e). TCF-1⁻PD-1hiCD8+ T cells (Q5/Q6) were more functional than TCF-1⁻PD-1lowCD8+ T cells (Q4) (Supplementary Fig. 3d). We also found that CD8_Texeff cells readily produced IL-2, TNF-α and IFN-γ, providing direct functional evidence for their effector capacity (Fig. 3f). Moreover, within the TCF-1⁻PD-1hi compartment, effector cytokine–producing cells predominantly exhibited the CD8_Texeff phenotype rather than the CD8_Texterm (Fig. 3g). Together, these data indicate that, despite exhibiting features of exhaustion, CD8_Texeff cells retain strong cytokine-producing capacity and represent a major functional subset in the CRC TME.

In addition to cytotoxicity, CD8_Texeff cells exhibited enhanced proliferative capacity. Compared to other CD8+ T cell subtypes, CD8_Texeff cells showed higher expression of proliferation-related genes (Fig. 3a). Remarkably, approximately 80% of MKI67+CD8+ T cells were CD8_Texeff cells (Fig. 3h, and Supplementary Fig. 3e). These findings collectively demonstrate that CD8_Texeff cells are both highly functional and proliferative and likely represent a key effector component of the anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response in CRC.

CD8_Texeff cells exhibit the strongest tumor reactivity

Analysis of paired scTCR/BCR-seq data revealed that about 75% of CD8_Texeff cells harbored tumor-specific TCRs, defined as clonotypes that are predominantly and clonally expanded in CRT31,36 (Fig. 3i, and Supplementary Fig. 3f). In comparison to other CD8+ T cell subtypes, CD8_Texeff cells contained significantly more large and hyperexpanded clones (twofold and sevenfold higher than CD8_Texint cells), suggesting robust antigen-driven proliferation. The STARTRAC-expa index further confirmed that CD8_Texeff cells exhibited the highest clonal expansion among all subtypes (Fig. 3j).

To independently validate tumor reactivity, we applied the NeoTCR8 score, a computational tool designed to predict neoantigen-specific CD8+ T cells within tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs).37 CD8_Texeff cells achieved the highest NeoTCR8 scores across all exhaustion states (Fig. 3k). Consistently, the phenotype of CD8_Texeff cells closely resembled that of tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells identified in other studies38,39 (Fig. 3l), reinforcing their tumor-reactive properties.

CD8_Texeff cells also displayed markedly higher levels of CXCL13 (Figs. 2d and 3a), a chemokine initially associated with exhausted T cells but now increasingly recognized as a marker of tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells.40 To investigate whether CXCL13 could serve as a defining characteristic of CD8_Texeff cells in CRC, we categorized the cells in CXCL13-expressing subcluster (CD8T_CXCL13) as CXCL13+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3m, and Supplementary Fig. 3g) and found that 84% of CD8_Texeff cells belonged to this compartment (Fig. 3n). Conversely, most CXCL13+CD8+ T cells (83%) were classified as CD8_Texeff, with only 11% and 6% assigned to CD8_Texint and CD8_Texterm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3h). mIF staining confirmed CXCL13 expression in CD8_Texeff cells, validating it as a marker of this tumor-reactive subset (Fig. 3o). Collectively, these data establish CD8_Texeff cells as a dominant tumor-reactive CD8+ T cell population and confirm CXCL13 as a reliable phenotypic marker.

CD8_Tpex and CD8_Texeff cells are preferentially enriched in MSI CRC

In TdLNs, scRNA-seq revealed that CD8+ T cells were mainly represented by the clusters CD8T_CCR7 and CD8T_GZMK (Fig. 1g, and Supplementary Fig. 4a). From a functional perspective, these TdLN CD8+ T cells could be further assigned to CD8_Tnaive, CD8_Ttsm, CD8_Tpex and CD8_Texint states (Fig. 2g, and Supplementary Fig. 4a). CD8+ T cells from MSI CRC TdLNs were enriched for CD8T_GZMK and pre-exhausted states (CD8_Ttsm and CD8_Tpex) as well as CD8_Texint state, whereas those from MSS TdLNs predominantly exhibited CD8T_CCR7 and CD8_Tnaive phenotypes (Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b), indicating a more immunologically activated TdLN microenvironment in MSI CRC. Differential gene expression analysis further showed that CD8+ T cells in MSI CRC TdLNs expressed higher levels of effector molecules (e.g., KLRD1, KLF2, GZMH, TNFAIP3) and chemokines (e.g., CCL3, CCL4, XCL2), and pathway enrichment analysis revealed that genes upregulated in MSI TdLN CD8+ T cells were predominantly associated with activation of immune responses (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). TCR repertoire analysis further underscored the functional relevance of TdLN CD8+ T cells. We observed extensive clonal overlap between CD8+ T cells in the TME and TdLNs within individual patients, with ~30.1% of shared TCRs predicted to be tumor-specific (Supplementary Fig. 4e). Tumor-specific CD8+ T cells in TdLNs were predominantly found in CD8_Tpex and CD8_Texint states rather than CD8_Tnaive, and these tumor-specific cells showed more pronounced clonal expansion in MSI than in MSS TdLNs (Supplementary Fig. 4f, g), suggesting that MSI TdLNs act as a more active reservoir of tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells.

Within the TME, MSI CRC displayed a significantly greater abundance of CD8_Texeff cells, while CD8_Texint and CD8_Texterm cells were more abundant in MSS CRC (Fig. 4c). These trends were consistently observed in both the validation dataset Cell-cohort19 (Supplementary Fig. 4h, i) and the mIF-cohort (Fig. 4d–f, and Supplementary Fig. 4j). Moreover, CD8_Texeff cells in MSI CRC expressed higher levels of CXCL13, effector molecules (GZMA, GZMB, GZMH, KLRD1), and immune checkpoint genes (CTLA4, HAVCR2, LAG3, TIGIT) than their counterparts in MSS CRC (Supplementary Fig. 4k). Activated (TNFRSF9+) and proliferative (MKI67+) CD8_Texeff subpopulations were also markedly expanded in the MSI CRC TME (Supplementary Fig. 4l). Notably, CD8_Texeff cells expressing tumor-specific TCR from MSI CRC also showed increased effector gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 4m). These findings suggested that CD8+ T cells in MSI CRC exhibited a more activated profile, with more pre-exhausted CD8+ T and CD8_Texeff cells enriched in TdLNs and the TME respectively.

Clinical significance of CD8_Texeff cells: associations with tumor stage, prognosis and immunotherapy response

We next investigated the association between CD8_Texeff cell abundance and clinical parameters in CRC. In addition to their enrichment in MSI CRC, CD8_Texeff cells were more abundant in early-stage (stage I-II, non-metastatic) than in late-stage (stage III, with lymph node metastasis) CRC patients (Fig. 4g). When stratified by both microsatellite status and stage, no significant difference was observed between early-stage MSI and MSS tumors (p = 0.140). However, in late-stage patients, MSS CRC exhibited significantly fewer CD8_Texeff cells than MSI CRC (p = 0.036, Fig. 4h).

To validate these findings, we developed a CD8.Texeff score derived from genes highly expressed by CD8_Texeff cells (Supplementary Table 5). In the TCGA-COAD dataset, MSI tumors showed significantly higher CD8.Texeff scores than MSS tumors. Moreover, the CD8.Texeff score in late-stage MSS CRC patients was significantly lower than that in early-stage MSS CRC patients (Fig. 4i). These results indicate that MSS CRC is a heterogeneous group, in which a subset of non-metastatic MSS tumors exhibits a CD8_Texeff-rich immune phenotype more closely resembling that of MSI CRC, potentially associated with a more favorable prognosis and immunotherapy responsiveness.

Prognostically, a higher CD8.Texeff score was associated with improved overall survival in MSS CRC (Fig. 4j) and remained prognostic even within the same clinical stage (Supplementary Fig. 5a). To assess predictive value for immunotherapy, we reanalyzed GSE179351, a dataset of MSS CRC and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) patients treated with ICB therapy and radiation.41 In responders, the CD8.Texeff score increased post-treatment (Fig. 4k) and was significantly higher than in non-responders (Fig. 4l), supporting a link between CD8_Texeff enrichment and ICB responsiveness.

To further evaluate the role of CD8_Texeff cells in the course of ICB therapy, we reanalyzed an independent longitudinal scRNA-seq dataset of CRC patients treated with ICB (GSE236581),36 which includes samples collected at multiple treatment time points. Using the same analytical pipeline as in our cohort, we subdivided CD8+ T cells into six subsets corresponding to distinct activation and exhaustion states (Supplementary Fig. 5b). In this cohort, complete responders (CR) displayed the highest proportion of CD8_Texeff cells at baseline (Fig. 4m, and Supplementary Fig. 5c). As therapy progressed and tumors regressed in CR patients, the frequency of CD8_Texeff cells gradually decreased, whereas the proportions of CD8_Texint and CD8_Tpex cells increased over time (Supplementary Fig. 5d). In contrast, in partial responders (PR), the proportion of CD8_Texeff cells increased during treatment, while the proportions of CD8_Texint and CD8_Tpex cells decreased (Supplementary Fig. 5d). These longitudinal dynamics support the notion that CD8_Texeff cells play an important role in anti-tumor immunity and that a high pre-treatment abundance of CD8_Texeff cells is associated with favorable ICB response, suggesting that CD8_Texeff may serve as a potential predictive biomarker. Collectively, these findings highlight the clinical relevance of CD8_Texeff cells in stratifying CRC prognosis and predicting immunotherapy benefit.

An immune potential score combining CD8_Tpex and CD8_Texeff signatures effectively predicts prognosis and immunotherapy response across different tumor types

Given the critical functions of CD8_Tpex and CD8_Texeff cells2,31,36 and their positive correlation in abundance (Supplementary Fig. 5e), we hypothesized that the co-enrichment of these subsets characterizes a MSI-like, immunologically active TME. To capture this coordinated contribution, we developed a composite Immune Potential (IP) score derived from single-cell defined gene signatures of CD8_Tpex and CD8_Texeff cells. We identified genes significantly upregulated in CD8_Texeff and CD8_Tpex cells relative to other CD8+ T cell states and derived a simple three-gene score based on GZMK, CXCL13, and CD8A. This IP score reliably reflects the abundance of CD8_Tpex and CD8_Texeff cells in CRC patients (Supplementary Fig. 5f).

The IP score proved to be a robust predictor of patient prognosis and immunotherapy benefit. In the TCGA-COAD cohort, higher IP scores were associated with significantly improved survival outcomes (Fig. 4n). Furthermore, compared to scores based solely on CD8_Texeff markers or other published metrics,42 the IP score demonstrated superior performance in predicting ICB therapy responses across multiple tumor types. In dataset GSE91061,43 which includes 65 patients with melanoma or non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treated with anti–PD-1 and anti–CTLA-4 therapies, responders had significantly higher IP scores than non-responders (Fig. 4o), with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.74 for predicting treatment response (Fig. 4p). Similarly, in dataset GSE93157,44 which included patients with melanoma, NSCLC, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) undergoing immunotherapy, higher IP scores were associated with prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) and increased response rates to ICB (Fig. 4q, r).

To further evaluate the translational relevance of the IP score, we validated it in an independent retrospective cohort of 85 CRC patients. Tumor sections from this cohort were stained by immunohistochemical (IHC) for CD8, CXCL13 and GZMK. In this cohort, MSI tumors expressed higher levels of all three markers than MSS tumors (Supplementary Fig. 5g). Moreover, the distribution of IP score differed between patients with and without recurrence or metastasis (Supplementary Fig. 5h). The IHC-derived IP score effectively stratified patients according to prognosis (Supplementary Fig. 5i), and among MSS CRC patients, those with high IHC-IP scores also had significantly better survival than those with low scores (Fig. 4s). Notably, this score demonstrated strong prognostic performance and outperformed CXCL13 alone in predicting recurrence or metastasis (Fig. 4t).

Among these subtypes, CD4_Tpex cells were defined as cells with high naïve and memory scores and were characterized by enriched expression of ANXA1, CXCR5, IL7R and CD69 compared to other cells (Fig. 5c, and Supplementary Fig. 6e). CD4_Tpex cells were distributed in both TdLNs and the TME (Fig. 5d), and shared TCR sequences between CD4_Tpex and CD4_Texeff cells indicated that CD4_Tpex cells could serve as precursors for CD4_Texeff cells (Supplementary Fig. 6f). CD4_Texeff cells exhibited a strikingly similar phenotype to CD8_Texeff cells and simultaneously expressed the highest levels of effector and immune checkpoint genes (Fig. 5c). Notably, CD4_Texeff cells expressed IFNG, CXCL13, CCL4 and CCL5, resembling the Th1-like cells previously reported in CRC.23 They also expressed IL21, but lacked the expression of Tfh lineage-defining transcription factor BCL6 (Fig. 5c, and Supplementary Fig. 6e). A CD4-focused mIF panel identified IL-21+IFN-γ+CXCL13+CD4+ T cells in the TME (Fig. 5e), consistent with the transcriptional features of the CD4_Texeff subset. Moreover, CD4_Texeff cells were predominantly located in the tumor (Fig. 5d, and Supplementary Fig. 6g), showed high NeoTCR4 scores and underwent significant clonal expansion, which indicated their strong tumor reactivity (Supplementary Fig. 6h, i).

In MSI CRC TdLNs, CD4+ T cells predominantly exhibited Tpex-like characteristics, while CD4+ T cells in MSS CRC TdLNs were more likely to display Tregs feature (Fig. 5f). Furthermore, CD4+ T cells from MSI CRC TdLNs exhibited a higher CD4_Tpex to Tregs ratio than those from MSS CRC (Fig. 5g, and Supplementary Fig. 6j). Prior studies indicated Tregs in TdLNs could suppress Th1 differentiation from Tpex cells,4 consistent with our finding that Th1-like CD4_Texeff cells were less abundant in MSS CRC than in MSI CRC (Fig. 5h). These differences were further validated using the Cell-cohort (Supplementary Fig. 6k). In particular, through mIF, we also confirmed the increased CD4_Tpex to Treg ratio in MSI TdLNs (Supplementary Fig. 6l), and additionally observed that CD4_Texeff cells were more abundant in MSI tumor and invasive margins (Fig. 5i–k, and Supplementary Table 6).

A positive correlation between CD4_Texeff and CD8_Texeff cell abundance in the CRC TME was observed in both our cohort and the Cell-cohort (Fig. 5l, and Supplementary Fig. 6m). IL-21 and IFN-γ, secreted by CD4_Texeff cells, are known to promote the differentiation of CD8+ T cells into effector phenotypes.45 Consistent with the enrichment of CD4_Texeff cells, CD4+ T cells in MSI CRC TME exhibited significantly higher expression levels of IFNG and IL21 (Fig. 5m), whereas their respective receptors, IL21R and IFNGR1, were highly expressed on CD8_Texeff cells and on CD8+ T cells in early exhausted states, such as CD8_Tpex and CD8_Texint (Fig. 5n). Additionally, the expression of IL21R on CD8+ T cells was significantly higher in MSI CRC than in MSS CRC (Fig. 5m). In line with these findings, analysis of ST-cohort24 revealed close spatial proximity between CD4_Texeff with CD8T_CXCL13 (most of which correspond to the CD8_Texeff state) cells within tumor regions (Fig. 5o). These observations are therefore consistent with a model in which IL-21 and IFN-γ produced by CD4_Texeff cells contribute to the differentiation and maintenance of CD8_Texeff cells and support the view that TdLN–TME immune milieu in MSI CRC favours the generation of CD4_Texeff cells, which in turn may help sustain a CD8_Texeff-rich TME via IL-21/IFN-γ–mediated interactions.

We next categorized DCs into four subclusters based on gene expression profiles (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Given the limited number of DCs in our cohort, we analyzed DC distribution in the Cell-cohort and observed a significant enrichment of cDC1 cells in the MSI CRC TME (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d), a subset recently linked to improved survival and immunotherapy response.47

To determine whether similar differences extend to TdLNs, we performed a parallel analysis of DC subsets in TdLNs. Although the overall numbers and proportions of total DCs and DC subsets in TdLNs did not differ significantly between MSI and MSS CRC patients (Supplementary Fig. 7e), transcriptomic profiling revealed clear functional differences, particularly within the activated DC (aDC) subset. In MSI CRC TdLNs, aDCs expressed higher levels of genes associated with antigen processing and presentation and T cell activation, indicative of a more immunostimulatory phenotype (Fig. 6d, e). By contrast, aDCs in MSS CRC TdLNs showed increased expression of the Treg-attracting chemokines CCL17 and CCL22, as well as the immunosuppressive molecule CD274 (PD-L1), consistent with a more tolerogenic profile (Fig. 6d). In line with our observations in the TME, DCs in MSI TdLNs also displayed higher interferon-response scores than those in MSS TdLNs (Fig. 6f).

Similar to DCs, macrophages in the MSI CRC TME exhibited enhanced T cell chemotactic and antigen-presenting capabilities as well as demonstrated a pronounced response to interferon signaling (Supplementary Fig. 7f). They highly expressed TNF, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules, and a series of chemokines, such as CXCL9, CXCL10 and CXCL16 (Fig. 6g). Notably, CXCR6, the receptor for CXCL16, was significantly elevated on CD8_Texeff cells as revealed before (Fig. 3a). Consistently, pathway enrichment analysis confirmed that MSI CRC macrophages displayed strong T cell chemotactic activity (Fig. 6h).

Macrophages were further classified into thirteen distinct subclusters (Fig. 6i). Notably, a subset of macrophages (Mac_CXCL10) exhibited significant CXCL9/CXCL10 expression and resided mainly in the TME (Supplementary Fig. 7g, h). Moreover, a substantial increase in Mac_CXCL10 was observed within the MSI CRC TME (Fig. 6j).

We further analyzed cell-cell interactions between macrophages and T cells in the TME. The results showed that Mac_CXCL10 cells had active crosstalk with both CD8_Texeff and CD4_Texeff cells (Fig. 6k), recruiting them via the CXCL9/10/11-CXCR3 and the CXCL16-CXCR6 signaling pathway (Fig. 6l). Analysis of the ST-cohort further revealed that Mac_CXCL10 cells were predominantly localized at the tumor-stroma boundary (Fig. 6m). Notably, the spatial distribution of CD8_Texeff cells closely aligned with that of Mac_CXCL10 cells, and their abundance exhibited a strong positive correlation across different tissue regions (Fig. 6n, o, and Supplementary Fig. 7i). This suggested that Mac_CXCL10 cells could effectively facilitate the recruitment of CD8_Texeff cells into the TME.

In summary, myeloid cells in MSI CRC acquire an interferon-driven phenotype marked by enhanced antigen presentation and chemokine secretion, facilitating CD8_Texeff cell activation and enrichment in the immune-activated TME.

We then investigated the potential mechanisms underlying TLS formation. A co-regulated module of immune cell populations was identified in CRC, comprising CD8_Texeff, CD4_Texeff, Tfh, cDC1, Mac_CXCL10 and B cells (Fig. 7d), suggesting close cellular interactions among these populations. Intercellular communication analysis revealed that CXCL13+ T cells, including CD8_Texeff and CD4_Texeff cells, recruited CXCR5+ Tfh and B cells via the CXCL13-CXCR5 signaling axis, a process critical for TLS formation49 (Fig. 7e). In our cohort, the number of CXCL13+ T cells was positively correlated with TLS density as assessed by IHC (Fig. 7f). Additionally, in the TCGA-COAD dataset, both CD8_Texeff and CD4_Texeff gene signatures showed a strong correlation with TLS formation score (Fig. 7g). mIF further confirmed the presence of CXCL13+CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within TLS (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Mac_CXCL10 cells were also found to be associated with TLS formation (Fig. 7f). In the ST-cohort, we observed an increased prevalence of TLS adjacent to both Mac_CXCL10 and CD8_Texeff cells (Figs. 7h and 6n), suggesting that CXCL10+ macrophages can support TLS formation, probably by recruiting Texeff cells as previously described.

We next characterized the phenotype of T cells within TLS using mIF. T cells in TLS were predominantly CD4+ T cells, most of which exhibited a PD-1–TCF-1+ naïve phenotype. A subset displayed a PD-1+TCF-1+ Tpex phenotype, and another subset consisted of CD4+ T cells capable of secreting IFN-γ or IL-21 (Fig. 7i). CD8+ T cells were less abundant and were mainly of the naïve phenotype, although Tpex and effector-like (GzmB+) CD8+ T cells were also present (Fig. 7j). Notably, CD8+ T cells were more densely clustered around CD4+ T cells producing IFN-γ or IL-21, suggesting that CD4+ T cells may provide supportive signals to CD8+ T cells within TLS. Overall, the cellular composition of TLS closely resembled that of TdLNs. Their formation was strongly associated with CXCL13+ cells, and TLS may function as reservoirs of stem-like T cells that contribute to anti-tumor immunity, a notion supported by the correlation between increased TLS abundance and improved prognosis in TCGA-COAD (Fig. 7k).

Consistent with these patterns, MSI CRC tumor cells showed significant upregulation of interferon-response-associated genes, including MHC class II molecules and the chemokine CXCL10 (Fig. 8e, f), suggesting an enhanced capacity for tumor antigen presentation and T cell chemotaxis. Interferon-response scores were significantly higher in MSI than in MSS tumor cells (Fig. 8g), indicating that these immunostimulatory transcriptional features are closely linked to interferon signalling in the tumor compartment.

Prior studies have shown that MSI CRC tumor cells frequently activate the cGAS–STING pathway and produce type I interferons.50 Consistently, we observed significantly higher cGAS–STING activation scores in MSI compared to MSS CRC tumor cells (Fig. 8h), with increased expression of key pathway components also confirmed in the TCGA-COAD dataset (Supplementary Fig. 9f). IHC staining further demonstrated higher STING protein expression in MSI than in MSS CRC (Fig. 8i, j).

In contrast, MSS CRC tumor cells upregulated stress-related genes such as HSPA1A, along with PLA2G10, a gene recently linked to T cell exclusion and proposed as a potential immunotherapy target51 (Fig. 8e, f). IHC staining validated elevated PLA2G10 protein expression in MSS CRC compared with MSI CRC (Fig. 8k, l). At the transcriptomic level, PLA2G10 expression negatively correlated with both the “T cell activation” and “IFN-γ response” module scores (Pearson p < 0.001 for both), and PLA2G10-high tumors contained fewer intratumoral CD8+ T cells by IHC (Fig. 8m). Notably, PLA2G10 expression was inversely correlated with STING expression (Supplementary Fig. 9 g), further implicating it as a negative regulator in MSS CRC.

Together, these data indicate that MSI CRC tumor cells are characterized by interferon-driven transcriptional programs and higher cGAS–STING pathway activity, whereas MSS CRC tumor cells preferentially upregulate PLA2G10 which is associated with reduced T cell infiltration.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically compared the microenvironments of TdLNs and primary tumors between MSI and MSS CRC. Notably, this is the first study to characterize the immunological divergence of TdLNs between the two subtypes and to demonstrate their pivotal role in orchestrating anti-tumor immunity. We identified a distinct subset of tumor-reactive CD8_Texeff cells that exhibited a terminally exhausted phenotype yet simultaneously retained potent anti-tumor functionality. These cells were significantly enriched in MSI tumors and were positively associated with the formation of TLS, which is linked to enhanced anti-tumor immunity. Further investigation revealed that these CD8_Texeff cells originated from TdLNs. Compared to MSS cases, TdLNs in MSI CRC contained higher frequencies of pre-exhausted CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, dendritic cells with enhanced antigen-presenting capacity and fewer Tregs. This distinct immune context provides both abundant precursors and a favorable niche supporting the differentiation and expansion of Texeff cells. Simultaneously, within the MSI CRC TME, various cell types provided substantial support for the development and recruitment of CD8_Texeff cells. This support was manifested by upregulated expression of MHC molecules across various cell types and increased secretion of cytokines conducive to T cell proliferation, differentiation, and chemotaxis, such as IL-21 and CXCL10. MSI CRC tumor cells also contributed to the support by expressing interferon-driven features and activation of the cGAS-STING-type I interferon pathway. Collectively, these findings indicate that the immune contexture of TdLNs shapes the TME and cooperatively drives the expansion of tumor-reactive CD8+ Texeff cells.

Our results reveal marked differences in the phenotype and functionality of CD8+ T cells between MSI and MSS CRC. Overall, CD8+ T cells in MSI patients showed stronger antigen-stimulated activation in both TdLNs and the TME. MSI TdLNs harbored a higher prevalence of TCF7+ pre-exhausted T cells, whereas MSS TdLNs were predominantly enriched for CD8+ T cells with naïve phenotypes. The expansion of pre-exhausted T cells in TdLNs has been associated with favorable responses to immunotherapy in multiple studies.29–31,52,53 Notably, we identified a distinct subset of CD8_Texeff cells within the CRC TME. These cells co-express high levels of inhibitory checkpoints alongside cytotoxic effector molecules, indicating that despite their terminally exhausted phenotype, they retain potent anti-tumor functionality. This subset was markedly enriched in MSI tumors than in MSS tumors. The identification of these functionally active terminally exhausted T cells in CRC challenges the conventional view that terminal exhaustion implies complete T cell dysfunction52,54,55 and aligns with recent findings in hematologic malignancies.32 The presence of abundant CD8_Texeff cells in MSI CRC contributes to the superior anti-tumor immunity and responsiveness to immunotherapy observed in these patients. In contrast, these Texeff cells were absent in MSS CRC, where CD8+ T cells exhibited lower tumor specificity and diminished functional potential.

We also identified a significant correlation between CD8_Texeff cells and the presence of TLS. TLS are ectopic lymphoid structures associated with improved patient prognosis and enhanced immunotherapy response in multiple cancer types.49,56–58 The density of TLS at the invasive margin of MSI CRC tumors was significantly higher than in MSS CRC, suggesting that TLS may play a crucial role in supporting effective anti-tumor immune responses. We hypothesize that TLS functions similarly to lymph nodes, serving as reservoirs for pre-exhausted T cells and capable of promoting their activation. The presence of TCF-1+ T cells in TLS further supports this concept, as these cells can gradually differentiate into effector-like phenotypes under the influence of IL-21 and IFN-γ secreted by CD4+ T cells within the TLS. Thus, TLS likely contributes to effective anti-tumor immunity by supporting effector cell maturation.

We further investigated the mechanisms underlying the enrichment of CD8_Texeff cells in MSI CRC. We identified that CD8_Texeff cells derived from TdLNs. In MSI CRC, TdLNs exhibited a markedly more activated immune profile compared to MSS counterparts, characterized by significantly higher frequencies of pre-exhausted CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, a more immunostimulatory DC compartment, alongside a reduced abundance of Tregs. TCR analyses suggest that Tpex cells residing in TdLNs can migrate into the tumor and differentiate into Texeff cells within the TME. In contrast, MSS CRC TdLNs were dominated by Tregs, which suppressed anti-tumor immunity through direct inhibition of T cell activation and preventing the differentiation of CD4+ Tpex cells into IFN-γ-producing Th1 cells.4 The paucity of effective Th1-like CD4_Texeff cells within MSS tumors may consequently impair the generation of CD8_Texeff cells due to diminished helper signals such as IL-21 and IFN-γ. This finding is consistent with previous reports emphasizing that the maturation of CD4+ Tpex into Th1 is critical for orchestrating effective anti-tumor immunity.4 These findings support that TdLNs serve as essential reservoirs for anti-tumor CD8_Texeff cells, wherein an immunoactivated TdLN environment supplies the tumor with effector-capable T cells, while an immunosuppressive TdLN fails to provide this support. Consistent with this concept, clinical evidence shows that preserving non-metastatic TdLNs may improve patient outcomes,2,59 and that effective ICB therapy requires the recruitment of new T cell clones from lymph nodes to the tumor.60 By elucidating these differences, our study highlights an overlooked aspect of the cancer-immunity cycle, the influence of TdLNs, and suggests that modulating the immune state of TdLNs could enhance immunotherapy outcomes.61 For instance, expanding and reinfusing autologous pre-exhausted T cells from non-metastatic LNs has been proposed as a strategy to bolster anti-tumor immunity in liver cancer patients.31

The divergent TME of MSI and MSS CRC also plays a pivotal role in shaping the distinct CD8+ T cell landscape. Specifically, MSI tumors generate a multitude of immunostimulatory signals that promote the development and maintenance of CD8_Texeff cells, whereas MSS tumors lack many of these supportive factors. For instance, MSI tumors are enriched with CD4_Texeff cells that secrete IL-21 and IFN-γ, cytokines critical for driving CD8+ T cell differentiation and sustaining their effector functions.4,45,62–64 Correspondingly, CD8_Texeff and CD8_Tpex cells in CRC highly express IL21R and IFNGR1, indicating that they are direct recipients of these helper-derived signals. Moreover, APCs in MSI CRC, including DCs and macrophages, exhibit heightened immunoactivity. These APCs demonstrate increased responsiveness to interferons, with elevated MHC molecule expression and upregulation of T cell chemotactic factors such as CXCL9 and CXCL10, facilitating priming and efficient recruitment of CD8+ T cells within the tumor.65–68 In addition to shaping the immune compartment, MSI and MSS CRCs differ in tumor cell–intrinsic programmes. In MSI tumors, we observed increased activity of the cGAS–STING axis and a robust type I interferon response, consistent with previous reports that abundant cytosolic DNA in MSI CRC can trigger STING signalling.50,69,70 This interferon-driven state was associated with upregulation of MHC class II molecules and CXCL10 in tumor cells and with an inflamed TME enriched for effector T cells. Furthermore, this process may establish a positive feedback loop in which infiltrating CD8_Texeff cells secrete IFN-γ, further amplifying local immune activation.19 Together with earlier experimental work showing that STING activation in tumor cells can promote immunogenic cell death and enhance anti-tumor immunity,71,72 these observations are compatible with a model in which cGAS-STING-type I interferon pathway contributes to the immune-supportive niche characteristic of MSI CRC. By contrast, MSS CRC tumor cells preferentially upregulated PLA2G10 and proliferation- and stress-related programmes and resided in a less inflamed microenvironment. We found that PLA2G10 expression was inversely associated with CD8+ T cell infiltration and with transcriptional modules related to T cell activation and IFN-γ responses. These correlative findings align with functional data from a murine CRC model showing that PLA2G10 overexpression impairs T cell migration and infiltration into tumors, thereby dampening anti-tumor immunity.51

Simultaneously, our findings hold significant clinical implications. We observed that an increased number of CD8_Texeff cells often predicts a favorable patient prognosis and immunotherapy response. Although predominantly enriched in MSI CRC, CD8_Texeff cells were also found in a subset of early-stage MSS CRC. This finding aligns with the results of multiple clinical trials. In stage IV disease, the objective response rate to anti-PD-1 therapy is approximately 40% in MSI CRC, but 0% in MSS CRC,13 while in neoadjuvant trials for early-stage MSS CRC, pathological response rates have reached approximately 27%.18 These data suggest that immune responsiveness in MSS CRC may be underestimated, particularly in early disease stages. We further developed an IP score based on CD8_Tpex and CD8_Texeff features, which robustly stratified prognosis and predicted response across tumor types, showing promise for broader clinical application.

In summary, our research identified that the CD8_Texeff cells were the key contributors to the favorable prognosis and enhanced immunotherapy response of MSI CRC and highlighted the critical role of TdLNs in the development and recruitment of CD8_Texeff cells. This work not only provides insights into the mechanisms underlying enhanced anti-tumor immunity in MSI CRC but also offers novel therapeutic strategies to enhance immunotherapy responses, particularly for MSS CRC patients.

However, several limitations exist in our study. First, despite efforts to include as many MSI cases with paired TdLN samples as possible, our sample size remains limited and does not include patients with distant metastases. Second, due to the limited detection of stromal cells, our analysis of stromal components in CRC remains insufficient. Third, although we used multiple approaches to support the presence of T cells with distinct exhaustion states in CRC, these populations are still defined primarily on the basis of transcriptional profiles. More detailed functional and cellular studies will be needed to fully establish their lineage relationships and in vivo roles. Fourth, while our data are consistent with a model in which IL-21 and IFN-γ produced by CD4_Texeff cells contribute to the differentiation and maintenance of CD8_Texeff cells, further experiments are needed to validate this mechanism. Future studies incorporating targeted functional experiments in relevant model systems will be important to delineate the mechanisms that drive the distinct tumor and TdLN microenvironments in MSI versus MSS CRC.

Materials and methods

Human specimens

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (CHCAMS, NCC2018-069). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects prior to collection. This study included 58 samples from 16 treatment-naïve patients with CRC for scRNA-seq and scTCR/BCR-seq analysis. For IHC, an additional validation cohort of 140 untreated patients with CRC was included. And for flow cytometry, fresh colorectal tumour tissues were obtained from untreated patients undergoing radical resection at CHCAMS between October 2025 and December 2025. Detailed clinical and pathological information is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Sample collecting and processing

Samples were collected in accordance with clinical practice guidelines for CRC, including primary tumors, matched adjacent normal areas located at least 5 cm from the tumor margin, TdLNs, and 6 mL of peripheral blood. TdLNs were defined as regional lymph nodes that directly receive lymphatic drainage from the tumor-bearing bowel segment and represent the earliest nodal basin involved in tumor metastasis as well as a primary site of tumor antigen presentation and initiation of anti-tumor immune responses.73–75 In this study, TdLNs were collected from surgical specimens and defined as lymph nodes located within approximately 2 cm of the primary colorectal tumor along the regional mesenteric drainage basin. All TdLNs included for single-cell analysis were pathologically confirmed to be non-metastatic.

Immediately after surgical excision, fresh tumor tissue, matched adjacent normal tissue, and TdLNs were placed in a tissue preservation solution and transported to the laboratory on ice. Samples were rinsed twice with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Servicebio). Necrotic and fatty portions were removed, and the remaining tissue was minced into ~1–2 mm³ fragments. Tissue pieces were transferred into RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and enzymatically dissociated using a gentleMACS Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) at 37 °C for 40 min following the manufacturer’s instructions. After digestion, the suspension was filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer (SmartStrainer, Miltenyi Biotec). Enzymatic activity was quenched by adding RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS, and cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 500 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 3 mL red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer (TIANGEN) and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Lysis was stopped by adding PBS. Cells were then centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the final pellet was resuspended in 1× PBS for subsequent experiments.

Peripheral blood was collected into EDTA anticoagulant tubes, gently mixed, and kept on ice during transport. PBMCs were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation using HISTOPAQUE-1077 (Sigma-Aldrich). Briefly, whole blood was carefully layered onto an equal volume of HISTOPAQUE-1077 without disturbing the interface. After centrifugation at 1220 × g for 10 min, the mononuclear cell layer was transferred to a new tube and washed with 1× PBS. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 300 × g for 10 min. Residual RBCs were removed using RBC lysis buffer at room temperature, followed by two washes with 1× PBS. Finally, cells were resuspended in sorting buffer (PBS supplemented with 1% FBS) for downstream analyses.

Single cell sorting and data generation

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from freshly resected colorectal tumor tissues (CRT), matched normal tissues (CRN), TdLNs and peripheral blood. For CRT and CRN samples, cell suspensions were stained with 7-AAD (BD Pharmingen, 13-6993) and anti-CD45 (BD Pharmingen, 555482), and sorted on a BD FACSAria™ III cell sorter. In tumor samples, both CD45+ immune cells and CD45⁻ non-immune cells (epithelial and stromal cells) were collected for scRNA-seq, allowing profiling of both tumor and immune compartments. In contrast, for CRN samples, we intentionally enriched for immune cells by sorting only CD45+ viable cells.

For TdLNs, two processing strategies were used. In 7 patients, TdLN suspensions were analyzed without CD45-based sorting and all viable cells were loaded for scRNA-seq. In 4 patients, TdLNs were stained with 7-AAD and anti-CD45, and only CD45+ viable immune cells were sorted. All TdLNs were pathologically confirmed non-metastatic lymph nodes, which are expected to be dominated by lymphocytes with very few epithelial cells. PBMCs were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation and analyzed directly after confirming acceptable cell viability, without additional CD45-based sorting.

The concentration of single-cell suspensions was adjusted to 500–1200 cells/µl, and between 10,000 and 18,000 cells were loaded per sample for 10× Chromium Single Cell 5′ gene expression and V(D)J library construction (10x Genomics), following the manufacturer’s protocols. Gene expression and TCR/BCR libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system. Detailed information on the processed fractions and recovered cell compositions for each patient and tissue type is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

scRNA-seq data processing

Raw sequencing data were processed using the Cell Ranger Single-Cell pipeline (version 3.1). Reads were mapped to the human reference genome (GRCh38), and a gene-by-cell unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrix was generated. Downstream analyses were conducted in R with Seurat (version 4.1.2). We performed stringent quality control to filter out low-quality cells based on three metrics, including the total UMI counts, the number of detected genes, and the proportion of mitochondrial gene UMI counts. Specifically, cells with (1) less than 2000 UMI counts, (2) less than 500 detected genes, or (3) more than 20% mitochondrial gene counts, were filtered out. Meanwhile, genes detected in fewer than 50 cells were excluded. After filtering, the remaining count matrix was normalized in Seurat using NormalizeData, which performs library-size scaling followed by log-transformation, and the resulting normalized expression matrix was used for subsequent analyses.

Dimension reduction and unsupervised clustering

Dimensionality reduction and unsupervised clustering were carried out using the standard Seurat workflow. Briefly, the 2000 most variable genes were identified with FindVariableFeatures (selection.method = “vst”). Then, gene expression values were scaled using ScaleData, while regressing out total UMI counts and the percentage of mitochondrial transcripts. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed with RunPCA on the scaled matrix of highly variable genes, and the first 50 principal components were retained for downstream steps. The Harmony algorithm76 was used to integrate data from different patients, followed by graph-based clustering.

During the first round of clustering, we annotated each cluster by the expression of canonical cellular makers, including PTPRC, EPCAM, PECAM1, COL1A1, CD3E, TYROBP, CD19, MS4A1, CD68, CD14, FLT3, TPSAB1 and MKI67. The combinative expression pattern of these markers could help identify major cell types, including T/NK cells, B/Plasma cells, myeloid cells, stromal cells and epithelial cells. Clusters showing concurrently high expression of two or more lineage-defining markers were considered putative doublets and were excluded from subsequent analyses. Further to this, for each major cell type, we performed a second round of unsupervised clustering to identify fine-grained cell subpopulations using the above-described procedure, but limited to only the expression matrix of cells from the analyzed major cell type.

scTCR/BCR-seq data processing

scTCR/BCR-seq data were processed using the Cell Ranger toolkit, which aligned reads to the GRCh38 human reference genome and assembled TCR/BCR sequences. TCR/BCR information was then assigned to corresponding transcriptomic profiles using the scRepertoire package (version 1.4.0).77 In total, 85.2% of T cells were detected with high-quality TCR. Cells displaying identical TCR pairs were considered clonally related, constituting the same clonotype. To assess clonal expansion among CD8+ T cells, we utilized entropy-based STARTRAC metrics. The STARTRAC-expa index was calculated using the Startrac.run function from the Startrac R package (version 0.1.0).23 Mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells were identified based on TCR analysis and were excluded from both the clone tracing and clonotype analysis.

Cell scoring and definition of T cell exhaustion states

We collected classic gene sets representing the different states of naïve, memory, effector and exhaustion cells by reviewing the literature (Supplementary Table 2). Using the R package AUCell (version 1.16.0),78 we scored T cells from TdLNs and the CRT for naïve and memory states. The effector and exhaustion scores for each cell were determined by calculating the 90th percentile expression of all genes within the respective gene sets. To avoid the influence of outliers, we defined the score for each cell as the median score of its nine nearest neighboring cells, determined by Euclidean distance in the UMAP plot.

For CD8+ T cells, we first stratified cells by the naïve score. Cells with higher naïve scores, reflecting stronger stem-like features, were assigned to one of CD8_Tnaïve, CD8_Ttsm or CD8_Tpex cells, whereas cells with lower naïve scores, representing more differentiated states, were assigned to the CD8_Texint, CD8_Texeff, or CD8_Texterm candidates. Among the naïve-high cells, further classification was performed using the memory score. Memory-low cells were defined as CD8_Tnaïve, memory-high cells as CD8_Tpex, and cells with an intermediate level of memory scores as CD8_Ttsm. For the naïve-low cells, subtyping was based on effector and exhaustion scores. Cells with concurrently high effector and exhaustion signatures were defined as CD8_Texeff, those with high exhaustion but comparatively lower effector features as CD8_Texterm, and those with intermediate effector and exhaustion levels as CD8_Texint. The cutoff values for different scores were determined by calculating the inflection point at the end of the density peak for each subtype.

For CD4+ T cells, we first removed lineage-committed Treg, Tfh and Th17 cells based on canonical markers, and then applied the same four scoring metrics (naïve, memory, effector and exhaustion) to the remaining CD4+ T cell population. CD4+ T cells with high naïve scores were considered to have stem-like features and were further divided into CD4_Tnaive and CD4_Tpex according to their memory scores (low versus high memory signature, respectively). Cells with low naïve scores were classified into CD4_Texint, CD4_Texeff and CD4_Texterm according to their relative effector and exhaustion scores using criteria parallel to those applied for CD8+ T cells.

To strengthen the phenotypic definition of T cell states, we used the FindMarkers function in Seurat to identify genes significantly upregulated in target T cell state compared with all other T cell states (adjusted p < 0.05 and expressed in > 50% of cells within the target state).

Trajectory analysis

To investigate the functional and exhaustion transitions within CD8+ T cells, and to delineate potential lineage differentiation among these cells, we utilized the monocle package (version 2.22.0).79 For this analysis, we focused on CD8+ T cells derived from TdLNs and the TME. We employed the dispersionTable function within the monocle package to identify highly variable genes critical for capturing the dynamic changes within these cells. Dimension reduction was performed using the DDRtree method. Subsequently, cells were ordered through the inferred pseudotime to illustrate their differentiation progress.

Identification of cell transcription factors

The package pySCENIC (version 0.12.1)78 was used to assess the enrichment of transcription factors and the activity of regulons in different CD8+ T cell subtypes.

Cell‒cell communication analysis

The CellChat package (version 2.1.2)80 was employed to analyze differential cell-cell communication networks at the signaling pathway level.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF)

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues were cut into 4–5 μm sections and were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by heat-induced antigen retrieval. After cooling, endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched and non-specific binding was blocked according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining was performed using a tyramide signal amplification (TSA)–based system (AlphaXTSA® 8-color fluorescent staining kit, AlphaX) on an AlphaXPainter® X48 automated stainer. Staining was carried out in iterative cycles of primary antibody incubation, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody binding and tyramide fluorophore deposition, with heat-mediated stripping between cycles. Two antibody panels were used, including CD8, CD4, GzmB, TCF-1, FoxP3, IFN-γ, CXCL13, PD-1, TIM-3, IL-21 and pan-cytokeratin (PanCK). The specific antibody clones, vendors, fluorophore assignments and working concentrations for each marker are listed in Supplementary Table 7. After completion of all staining cycles, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and slides were coverslipped with an anti-fade mounting medium.

Whole-slide fluorescence images were acquired on a ZEISS Axioscan 7 scanner using appropriate filter sets and ZEN 3.3 software. For each fluorophore channel, excitation and emission ranges were set according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and exposure times were optimized for each marker (detailed settings are provided in Supplementary Table 7). Single-cell segmentation and phenotyping, as well as quantification of marker-positive cells and cell densities (cells/mm²), were performed using HALO 3.5 image analysis software (Indica Labs). Regions of interest (ROIs) were annotated by an experienced pathologist using QuPath [81] (version 0.5.1) and included tumor core, invasive margin, normal-adjacent areas and tertiary lymphoid structures where present. For quantitative analyses, 5 ROIs were randomly selected within each compartment per case. For each ROI, we quantified the density of marker-positive cells (cells/mm²) and the proportion of marker-positive cells within the corresponding cell population.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For IHC, FFPE sections (4–5 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or EDTA (pH 9.0), followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidase and non-specific binding. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against PLA2G10 (Novus Biologicals, NBP1-93706), STING (CST, 13647), CD20 (CST, 48750), CD8 (CST, 70306S), CXCL13 and GZMK (Abcam, ab270408 and ab282703) at their optimized dilutions. After washing, slides were incubated with secondary antibodies and developed with DAB chromogen, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated and mounted.

IHC scoring was performed by two experienced pathologists, blinded to all clinical and molecular data, using QuPath81 (version 0.5.1). The tumor invasive margin for each section was determined by joint review, and a final annotation was reached by consensus. Positive cells were initially identified automatically by the software, which was used to calculate the density of positive cells (cells/mm²) and the H-score. The automated counts were then manually curated under blinded conditions by both pathologists. For cases in which the relative difference between the two corrected results was large, the slides were jointly re-reviewed and a consensus value was adopted as the final result; for the remaining cases, the mean of the two corrected values was used. TLS was defined as lymphoid aggregates comprising >50 CD20+ B cells. Only TLSs within 1 mm around the tumor invasive margin were considered for scoring.

Flow cytometry

A multiparameter flow cytometry panel was used to assess CD8_Texeff function and phenotypes. The antibodies included anti-CD45-APC-Cy7 (eBioscience), anti-CD3-PerCP-Cy5.5 (BioLegend), anti-CD8-PE-Cy7 (eBioscience), anti-PD-1-FITC (eBioscience), anti-TCF-1-Pacific Blue (Cell Signaling Technology), and anti-GzmB-PE (eBioscience). For cytokine detection, anti-IFN-γ-APC(eBioscience), anti-IL-2-APC(eBioscience), and anti-TNF-α-APC (eBioscience) were used in separate staining tubes, as no ex vivo stimulation was performed.

Freshly prepared cells were resuspended in staining buffer (PBS with 3% FBS) and blocked with human IgG (eBioscience) to reduce non-specific binding. Surface markers were stained for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark and washed twice. For intracellular staining of TCF-1, GzmB, IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience) following the manufacturer’s protocol, washed with the kit-provided permeabilization buffer, and incubated with intracellular antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min. After washing, samples were resuspended in FACS buffer and immediately analyzed.

Data were acquired on a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Compensation was performed using single-stained controls, and isotype and fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were included for gating. A minimum of 10,000 events per sample were recorded. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1).

Definition of tumor-specific TCR

To identify tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells, we defined tumor-specific TCRs as those detected in both lymph nodes and tumor tissues, with at least twice as many cells expressing the TCR in tumor tissue compared to lymph nodes,31 or those detected exclusively in tumor tissues but absent in normal colorectal tissues with at least three cells expressing the TCR.36 When CD8+ T cells express a tumor-specific TCR, they are considered tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells.

Signature genes of T Cells in different exhausted states

We used the FindMarkers function in Seurat and defined the top 50 genes that were highly expressed in a specific exhausted T cell state (adjusted p-value < 0.05), compared to other exhausted T cell states, as signature genes for that particular cell type. Specifically, for the CD8.Texeff score the top 75 genes ranked by log₂(fold change) was selected. The detailed gene sets are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

In single-cell data, package AUCell was used to score each cell, while in bulk transcriptome data, package GSVA (version 1.50.0) was applied to score each sample.

Obtaining and processing of publicly available datasets

To compare our findings with existing data, we downloaded the processed sRNA-seq data generated by Pelka et al.19 (Cell-cohort, accession no. GSE178341) and Chen et al.36 (accession no. GSE236581) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). We performed quality control and clustering using the same procedures described above. Subsequently, we applied the same scoring methodology and gene sets (Supplementary Table 2) used in our analysis to compute transcriptional scores for tumor-derived CD8+ T cells in these datasets.

Bulk RNA-seq datasets (GSE179351,41 GSE91061,43 GSE9315744) of tumor samples along with corresponding clinical and treatment information were also downloaded from the GEO database. The gene expression data and clinical information from TCGA-COAD dataset were obtained using the R package TCGAbiolinks. GSVA was used to score the samples from each patient, and comparisons were made across different patient categories, or patients were stratified into different groups based on the scores to compare their prognoses.

Spatial transcriptomics data for CRC patients were obtained from STOmicsDB of China National GenBank Database (accession number: STT0000036, https://db.cngb.org/stomics/project/STT0000036).24 Data was processed using Seurat with SCTransform normalization. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed using RunPCA, FindNeighbors, and FindClusters functions in Seurat. The tumor regions were delineated according to the definitions provided in the dataset. RCTD82 was applied to infer the cellular composition of each bin based on our single-cell transcriptomic reference. AUCell was used to score each bin based on the cell gene sets mentioned before. The 12 genes used to define TLS included: CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL8, CCL18, CCL19, CCL21, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11 and CXCL13.34 Spatial colocalization patterns were identified using mistyR.83

Statistical analyses

The Mann–Whitney U-test was used to compare the fractions of cell clusters or expression scores between different sample types. When the sample size was insufficient for the Mann–Whitney U-test, the permutation test was used to compare differences between two groups.84 Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test from the “survminer” and “survival” R packages. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses and figure preparation were conducted using R software (version 4.1.2).
