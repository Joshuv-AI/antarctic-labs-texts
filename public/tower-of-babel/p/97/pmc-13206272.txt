Distinct Prescription Patterns Emerge Years Before ALS Diagnosis: A Nationwide Registry‐Based Study
Authors: Magne Haugland Solheim, Trond Riise, Marianna Cortese, Ola Nakken, Ole‐Bjørn Tysnes, Jannicke Igland, Kjetil Bjornevik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe prodromal phase of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is poorly defined. We aimed to characterize prescription drug use patterns in the pre‐diagnostic period by analyzing nationwide prescription data to identify the earliest divergence between individuals who developed ALS and matched healthy controls. We used this divergence as an indirect marker to estimate the onset and duration of the prodrome.MethodsWe conducted a nested case‐control study using nationwide Norwegian registries (2005–2019). ALS cases were individually matched to 100 controls by sex, age, and education level using incidence density sampling. Drug prescription data were gathered from the Norwegian Prescription Database (NorPD). We calculated prescription rates up to 10 years before diagnosis, performed lag‐time analyses, and used machine learning to predict ALS based on drug prescription patterns.ResultsWe identified 2,084 incident patients with ALS and 208,400 matched healthy controls. Overall, changes in prescription patterns occurred 2 to 3 years before ALS diagnosis. Among specific drug groups, 25 of 42 therapeutic drug classes were prescribed more frequently to patients with ALS than matched controls. Muscle relaxants and bone disease treatments were prescribed significantly more frequently 6 and 5 years before diagnosis, respectively.InterpretationPrescription pattern changes occurred as early as 6 years before ALS diagnosis. These findings are consistent with a prodromal phase preceding the clinical stage of ALS, which may last several years. In contrast, the broad increase in medication use during the final year before diagnosis likely reflects increased health care utilization as patients seek treatment for the various emerging symptoms of the clinically manifest disease. ANN NEUROL 2026;99:1516–1525

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive adult‐onset neurodegenerative disease with unknown etiology and with limited treatment options that can slow its progression. The disease has a particularly poor prognosis, with a typical survival time from the first ALS symptom of 20 to 48 months. 1 Approximately 1 in 400 people in the general population develops ALS during their lifetime. 2

Despite efforts to create diagnostic criteria that increase sensitivity and reduce the time to diagnosis, the average time from the first symptom until the diagnosis is still approximately 12 months. This figure has remained similar over the last decade and across countries, 3 , 4 including Norway. 5 The diagnostic process can be prolonged because early symptoms, which can range from progressive weakness to the cognitive and behavioral changes of the ALS‐frontotemporal dementia (FTD) spectrum, 6 can be nonspecific and overlap with other conditions. Although ALS is ultimately diagnosed based on its clinical features, this overlap means that a comprehensive evaluation is often needed to establish diagnostic confidence and rule out potential mimics. There is, therefore, considerable interest in improving our understanding of the earliest phases of the disease.

One proposed framework for the progression of ALS divides the pre‐symptomatic phase into a pre‐manifest stage, detectable only by biomarkers, and a prodromal stage marked by subtle signs or symptoms. 7 In this model, the transition to the symptomatic phase is marked by phenoconversion, the emergence of definite clinical signs, which eventually leads to a formal diagnosis. 7 Although the boundaries between these stages are not sharply defined, this framework is consistent with the long‐standing concept that the neurodegenerative process begins long before the onset of symptomatic weakness, 8 , 9 which is also supported by evidence of a motor prodrome, termed mild motor impairment (MMI), 10 elevation of certain biomarkers, including neurofilament light chain (NfL) 11 , 12 and group‐level changes in metabolic profiles. 13 , 14 Whereas the nonspecific symptoms of the prodromal phase can be challenging to capture directly, they may manifest in measurable behaviors, such as changes in health care utilization or prescription drug use. Identifying these indicators of the prodromal phase may therefore help clarify the onset of this early stage of the disease.

To address this, we conducted a study using data from nationwide Norwegian registries to investigate prescription patterns among patients with ALS and a matched control group for up to 10 years prior to ALS diagnosis. Our objective was to determine when prescription patterns first diverge between these groups, using this divergence as an indirect marker to characterize prescription drug use patterns in the pre‐diagnostic period.

Methods

Data Sources

Data from 3 national registries were included: the Norwegian Patient Registry (NPR), the Norwegian Prescription Database (NorPD), and the demographic registry of Statistics Norway. NPR provided data on all hospitalizations and outpatient visits in Norway with International Classification of Disease 10th edition (ICD‐10) diagnosis code G12.2 for motor neuron disease from 2009 to 2019, including the year and month of diagnosis. NorPD provided data on all drug prescriptions dispensed at pharmacies in Norway since 2004. Data from Statistics Norway (SSB) provided information on death, emigration, birth year, and level of education. Ethical approval and exemption from informed consent were given by the Western Norway Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REK West: 2017/1508).

Study Design and Study Population

Data on the entire Norwegian population living in Norway on January 1, 2004, obtained from SSB were linked to data from NorPD and NPR using the unique identification number available for all residents in Norway. Based on this source population we performed a nested case‐control study. For each ALS case, defined according to the algorithm described below, 100 controls were randomly selected and matched on sex, age, and education. Controls were selected from persons who were alive and without ALS diagnosis at the index date (date of ALS diagnosis of the corresponding case). The relatively large number of controls was chosen to obtain precise estimates of prescription patterns for the comparison group.

Case Ascertainment and Definition

ALS cases were ascertained with data from both NPR and NorPD. Individuals were classified as ALS cases if they had either at least 2 registrations with ICD‐10 G12.2 in NPR, or one G12.2 in NPR and one Riluzole prescription in NorPD. This algorithm has previously been validated on Norwegian medical records. 15 For the period 2005 to 2009, when NPR data were not available, cases were defined as individuals with at least 2 Riluzole prescriptions. As only approximately 75% of patients with ALS in Norway use Riluzole, 15 we estimate that we missed approximately 25% of the patients during this period. To ensure that we only included incident cases, we excluded individuals with G12.2 registrations in 2009 and no prior Riluzole prescription, because they could have been diagnosed with ALS before 2009. We also excluded individuals with Riluzole prescriptions in 2004, because these individuals could potentially have been diagnosed with ALS before 2004. Incident cases of ALS were therefore defined for the period 2005 to 2019. The date of ALS diagnosis was defined as either the date (year and month) of the first registration in NPR or the first Riluzole prescription, whichever came first. Whereas these dates usually coincide, some patients initiated Riluzole treatment after their first G12.2 registration. Among patients with both NPR and Riluzole registrations, 95% of them got their first Riluzole prescription less than 6 months after the first registration in NPR.

Prescription History

We included data on all drug prescriptions dispensed from January 2004 until the index date for all individuals in the nested case‐control dataset. For each prescription, we had information about the date (year and month) of dispensing, and the type of drug classified according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system. This is a hierarchical classification developed by the World Health Organization where the first levels indicate the anatomic organ or system on which the drug works, whereas the second level represents therapeutic subgroups. 16

The observation period before the year of diagnosis (index date) varied between 1 and 16 years. Still, to ensure sufficient statistical power when studying the exposure to drugs, we limited our analysis to prescription patterns up to 10 years prior to diagnosis. First, we studied overall medication use, regardless of drug type. Second, we described the pattern according to ATC level 1 and level 2. To ensure sufficient power for the estimation of the single medication groups, we only included groups where at least 10 ALS cases got at least one prescription in the 10th year before diagnosis.

Statistical Analyses

We used 3 approaches to estimate the difference in prescription history between patients with ALS and healthy controls. First, we performed latency analyses to examine differences in prescription patterns at specific time points before diagnosis. Because individual observation time varied and not all individuals were followed for the full 10 years before diagnosis, we used prescription rates to describe prescription patterns. This rate‐based approach allowed us to include all individuals in the calculations for their entire observation period. Prescription rates were defined as the number of persons receiving at least one prescription in a time interval divided by person time in that interval. Prescription rates were modeled for different ATC categories using Poisson regression with person time as exposure and time to diagnosis and case control status as covariates. The results were presented graphically for cases and controls separately or as rate ratios (RRs). Trends in monthly prescription rates over time were modeled with natural cubic splines, first for prescription of any medication of any type, then for ATC level 1 (anatomic groups). Finally, for medications classified according to ATC level 2 (therapeutic subgroups), we calculated yearly RRs. Significance was tested with Poisson regressions with robust standard errors and fixed effects for matched sets to account for matching. For prescriptions with ATC level 2, there were many groups, and the RRs are therefore presented in a heatmap.

Second, we performed lag time analyses in which prescriptions occurring within specific lag times before the event (ALS diagnosis) were disregarded. This approach is often used to prevent or assess the risk for reverse causality, that is, when the disease causes exposure. 17 In our study, we assumed that changes in prescription patterns were caused by ALS symptoms before diagnosis, and we wanted to investigate when these changes occurred. Specifically, we fitted repeated Cox proportional hazards models to the full cohort data, with time‐varying exposures (any prescription) successively lagged by monthly increments up to 10 years. The model with 1‐month lag excluded prescriptions from the month prior to diagnosis; the model with 2‐month lag excluded prescriptions from the 2 months prior to diagnosis, etc. Each model used age as the time scale and was adjusted for sex and education. The estimated hazard ratios (HRs) were presented graphically with lag time on the x‐axis.

Last, we used machine learning to predict ALS based on prescription patterns of combinations or groups of drugs in different time intervals before diagnosis. We performed least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)‐penalized conditional logistic regression on the nested case‐control data using prescriptions at ATC level 2 given in successive 3‐month intervals (from 1–3 up to 118–120 months before the index date) as predictors. The penalty terms in the regressions were determined by 10‐fold cross‐validations. Prediction accuracy for each interval was measured by the concordance index.

Results

From January 2005 through December 2019, there were 2,084 incident ALS cases identified. During the early period, 2005 to 2009, there were approximately 100 new cases annually, increasing to roughly 150 new cases per year in 2010 to 2019 when data from NPR were available. The mean age at ALS diagnosis was 66.9 years. There were more male patients than female patients and male patients were, on average, younger at the time of diagnosis. The individual observation periods ranged from a minimum of 1 year to a maximum of 191 months (almost 16 years) before the ALS diagnosis (or index date in the controls) (see the Table1).

Even in early follow‐up stages (ie, years before ALS diagnosis), the prescription rates were slightly elevated in patients with ALS compared with healthy controls (Fig 1A). The difference began to widen at approximately 4 years and reached statistical significance at approximately 30 months before the index date, with a slight increase in prescriptions among patients with ALS compared with controls. Then, it became markedly pronounced approximately 1.5 years before diagnosis. A similar pattern was found in the lag time analyses (Fig 1B). The HRs were above one for all time lags and increased gradually until a marked upward shift at approximately the 1 year lag. The predictive value of medication patterns was apparent in the early follow‐up period, increasing slightly 4 years before diagnosis, with a marked increased predictive value emerging approximately 2 years before that index date (Fig 1C).

In analyses of prescriptions at group level (ATC level 1), there was a shift in prescription patterns emerging approximately 2 years before ALS diagnosis, with a sharp increase for several drug categories (Fig 2). Drugs related to blood and blood‐forming organs (group B), anti‐infectives for systemic use (group J), nervous system (group N), and respiratory system (group R) all showed marked increases. There was also a sharp increase in drugs related to the alimentary tract and metabolism (group A), but the increase started approximately 1 year later. By the end of the observation period, nervous system and respiratory system prescriptions had the highest relative monthly use, prescribed almost 75% more frequently to ALS cases than controls in the month preceding diagnosis. Several drug groups showed gradual increases throughout the study period. Prescriptions of drugs related to the genitourinary system and sex hormones (group G) and the musculoskeletal system (group M) gradually increased from the start of the follow‐up period. Systemic hormonal preparations (excluding sex hormones and insulins) were initially less used by patients with ALS but showed an increasing trend in prescriptions starting approximately 5 years before the diagnosis. The relative rate for nervous system medications (group N) showed a slight increase through the whole observation period with a sharp increase toward the end of the period prior to diagnosis. Use of dermatological drugs (group D), antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents (group L), and sensory organs (group S) medications declined in patients with ALS compared with controls during the observation period.

Some medication groups were consistently more or less frequently used during the observation period. Before the sharp increases toward the end of follow‐up, patients with ALS consistently used medications related to alimentary tract and metabolism (group A), blood and blood‐forming organs (group B), and systemic hormonal preparations (excluding sex hormones and insulins; group H) groups less often than controls. On the other hand, medications related to the genitourinary system and sex hormones (group G), musculoskeletal system (group M), and nervous system (group N) were more frequently used by patients with ALS throughout the 10‐year observation period.

In analyses of prescriptions on ATC level 2 (therapeutic subgroups), most drug classes were more frequently prescribed to patients with ALS than controls at some point during this period, but the marked shift occurred in the last years preceding the ALS diagnosis. Of the 42 therapeutic drug classes included, the relative rates were significantly increased for 25 of the drug classes in the year preceding the ALS diagnosis. For the majority of these drug classes including medications from the alimentary tract and metabolism group, anti‐thrombotic, vasoprotective, corticosteroids, and anti‐infective groups, the prescription rates were only significantly increased for patients with ALS in the last year before the diagnosis. For other drug classes, including vitamins, urologics, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) medications, and nasal preparations, the rates were significantly increased in the 2 years preceding the ALS diagnosis. Throughout the whole period before diagnosis, the patients with ALS used beta blockers, diuretics, cardiac therapy, diabetes, and anti‐obesity drugs less often than the controls. Some commonly used cardiovascular system drugs like statins were not differentially used in patients with ALS and controls, which is why there was no apparent difference when the drug groups were compared at ATC level 1 (see Fig 2).

Muscle relaxants showed elevated prescription rates already 6 years before the ALS diagnosis (Fig 3). This group was followed by drugs for the treatment of bone diseases, which had increased rates 5 years before the diagnosis. Further, the relative prescription rates for psycholeptics and psychoanaleptics were increased 4 years before the diagnosis, whereas the rates for antiepileptics were significantly increased from 3 years before diagnosis.

Discussion

In this nationwide nested case‐control study of 2,084 patients with ALS, we observed distinct changes in prescription patterns starting years before ALS diagnosis. Our findings distinguish between 2 pre‐diagnostic periods: a likely symptomatic phase beginning 1 to 2 years before diagnosis, characterized by sharp increases in prescriptions for drugs related to respiratory, neurologic, and musculoskeletal conditions, and an earlier, longer prodromal phase. Evidence for this longer prodrome comes from significantly increased prescription rates for muscle relaxants, bone disease treatments, and psychotropic medications starting 6, 5, and 4 years before diagnosis, respectively.

When interpreting these findings, it is critical to distinguish the true prodrome from the clinically manifest but undiagnosed phase of the disease. This latter period corresponds to the known diagnostic delay of approximately 1 year, 3 , 4 with some patients experiencing delays of more than 18 months. 18 The sharp increase in prescriptions during this time, with 25 of 42 drug classes being used more frequently in the year preceding diagnosis, is consistent with this phase. The rise in prescriptions for specific drug groups during this time, such as respiratory and urologic medications, likely reflects the treatment of emerging symptoms. For example, the rise in prescriptions for respiratory medications may correspond to the onset of dyspnea or bulbar dysfunction, such as sialorrhea (excessive salivation), whereas the increased use of urologics is consistent with autonomic symptoms like a spastic bladder. These prescription patterns during the 1 to 2‐year pre‐diagnostic period align with biomarker studies showing markedly elevated NfL levels in this timeframe, 7 , 12 , 19 reflecting accelerating neuroaxonal damage. 20 Although NfL elevation reflects axonal loss that can occur in both clinically silent and prodromal stages, its marked rise coincides with the increased health care utilization we observed, consistent with a phase of accelerating pathology immediately preceding clinical recognition.

Independent of these later changes, several earlier prescription signals point to a longer prodromal phase based on their biological plausibility and consistency with emerging evidence on pre‐symptomatic ALS. The earliest signal we observed was an increase in the use of muscle relaxants, beginning approximately 6 years before diagnosis. Use of muscle relaxants has previously been associated with an increased risk of ALS, even when prescriptions given in the last 3 years before diagnosis were ignored, 21 which is consistent with musculoskeletal symptoms being among the earliest signs of ALS. This finding aligns with proteomic studies identifying skeletal muscle‐derived proteins as among the most prominently elevated plasma biomarkers in ALS, 22 with population‐based proteomic evidence of molecular changes in muscle, immune, and metabolic pathways years to decades before diagnosis, 23 , 24 and with recently proposed research criteria for MMI that formalize the concept of a recognizable prodromal motor syndrome. 25 In a study of individuals carrying genetic mutations for ALS, this prodromal period was observed to last from a few months to several years, and in one case involving an individual with a SOD1 I113T mutation, it was potentially as long as 10 years based on self‐report. 10 We observed, furthermore, an increased rate of drugs for bone diseases starting 5 years before ALS diagnosis. These drugs, primarily used in the treatment of osteoporosis and prescribed mainly to women, suggest that bone health deterioration may begin pre‐diagnostically, a concept supported by evidence of shared genetic links to ALS risk 26 and by studies reporting that fractures are associated with increased ALS risk. 27 Alternatively, this may reflect an increased risk of trauma in the years immediately preceding an ALS diagnosis, which could lead to more fractures and subsequent workup for and detection of underlying bone disease. This is supported by a meta‐analysis showing the association between trauma and ALS was strongest for injuries occurring within 5 years of diagnosis. 28 Last, we also observed increased use of psycholeptics (mainly anxiolytics, hypnotics, and sedatives) and psychoanaleptics (mainly antidepressants) that extended beyond 2 years before diagnosis, and could be related to depression, sleeping disorder, and anxiety, which are common non‐motor symptoms occurring early in the disease. 29 , 30 , 31

Although the changes in prescription patterns we observed could reflect specific symptoms of a prodromal phase, they could also arise from surveillance bias. This bias occurs when individuals seeking health care for early, nonspecific symptoms are more likely to be diagnosed with and treated for other conditions. However, in the context of studying the prodromal phase of ALS, the bias itself can be a meaningful signal, reflecting how a prodromal disease process alters health‐seeking behavior. Therefore, the broad and sustained increase in medication use may serve as an indirect marker of the onset of this phase, pointing to a broader change in health‐related behavior rather than focusing on individual signs and symptoms.

Interpreting the direction of these associations requires careful consideration of their timing. A key challenge is distinguishing between prescription changes that are markers of the prodromal phase (a consequence of the disease reflecting reverse causation) and those that represent potential risk or protective factors (a contributor to the disease). The temporal pattern of use is crucial for this distinction. This concept was clearly demonstrated in a recent study on statins, which found that short‐term use was associated with an increased ALS risk due to reverse causation, whereas long‐term use was associated with a protective effect. 32 Changes that only emerge in the years immediately preceding diagnosis, like the sharp increase in respiratory and neurological medications, are most plausibly a consequence of the emerging disease. Whereas it is theoretically possible that some of these short‐term changes could represent triggers for the disease, this is less plausible given that most of the medications involved are commonly used in the general population. In contrast, differences in medication use between future ALS cases and controls that are stable and persistent throughout the entire 10‐year pre‐diagnostic period are less likely to be explained by reverse causation. Our observations of consistently lower medication use for metabolic and cardiovascular conditions among future patients with ALS are a clear example of the latter. This persistent pattern suggests it is not a prodromal signal but instead reflects underlying metabolic factors that may be protective against ALS, a finding supported by numerous etiological studies. 21 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39

Our findings have direct implications for etiological studies seeking to identify risk factors for ALS. We have shown that there were sharp increases in the use of several drugs among patients with ALS several years before diagnosis. These increases are likely due to the appearance of ALS symptoms. For studies seeking to identify risk factors, these findings are important to consider. Based on our findings, we recommend implementing a minimum 3‐year lag in analyses on risk factors to minimize the risk of reverse causality.

Our study has several strengths. With data from nationwide Norwegian registries, we could analyze the complete prescription history for up to 10 years before diagnosis. As the data come from compulsory registries with automatic registration of data, we do not depend on recalled information on use of drugs, which can lead to misclassification in prospective studies and recall bias in case‐control studies. In addition, because of the nationwide study design, we could include a large number of incident patients with ALS. Our study also has some limitations. Before 2009, only Riluzole prescriptions were used to identify ALS cases. Because Riluzole is not used by all patients with ALS, we miss approximately 40 cases per year during this period. Whereas Riluzole users may differ from non‐users, we observed similar results when restricting the analyses to patients diagnosed after 2009. The ICD‐10 code G12.2 that was used to identify ALS cases also includes other motor neuron diseases like primary lateral sclerosis and progressive spinal muscular atrophy. However, because the vast majority of those with G12.2 registrations are patients with ALS, 15 it is unlikely that this has influenced the results. The registries lack clinical data such as the site of disease onset, ALS type (familial or sporadic), and lifestyle factors (body mass index [BMI], smoking habits, and physical activity) that could have improved the interpretation of our findings. Furthermore, some medications are rarely used and could therefore not be reliably assessed. Additionally, not all ALS cases and matched controls had 10 years of pre‐diagnostic data because some individuals had an index date early in the observation period. Still, this is unlikely to introduce bias as the rate‐based analysis, analogous to survival analysis, is designed to correctly account for these varying observation periods by utilizing all available person‐time. Variability in diagnostic delay may have affected some of the results. Still, it is unlikely that this can fully explain the earliest observed changes, which occurred up to 6 years before ALS diagnosis, as few patients experience a diagnostic delay longer than 2 years. Last, as many medications are used for multiple diseases, it can be challenging to make inferences about specific symptoms.

In conclusion, we observed distinct prescription patterns starting years before ALS diagnosis. We observed changes as early as 6 years before the disease was diagnosed. These findings are consistent with a prodromal phase that occurs before the clinical manifest stage in ALS, and this phase may last for several years.
