Associated Factors and Prevalence of Postpartum Depression and Anxiety Disorders Among Newborn Turkish Mothers
Authors: Ayca Asena Sayın, Omer Faruk Uygur, Bilge Burcak Annagur, Ali Annagur, Seockhoon Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of postpartum depression (PPD) and anxiety disorders among postpartum mothers. Additionally, it examined the effect of comorbidity on PPD. Methods This study included 206 mothers with babies between 0 and 6 weeks of age who visited the pediatric department for routine health checks. First, Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition and demographic data were collected from the participants. Subsequently, the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support, the Beck Anxiety Inventory, and the Maternal Attachment Inventory were used to evaluate all mothers. Results We found that 6.8% (n=14) of the mothers evaluated had PPD, and 21.4% (n=44) had an anxiety disorder. The most common anxiety disorder was generalized anxiety disorder (16.0%, n=33). The percentage of females with comorbid PPD and anxiety disorder was 3.8%. We then compared the postpartum and non-PPD groups in terms of socio-demographic characteristics and found a significant difference pertaining only to economic status. Socio-economic status was lower in the group with PPD. In addition, social support received during pregnancy was significantly lower in the group with PPD. A history of depression (including peripartum depression) and/or comorbid psychiatric disorders was identified as related to PPD. Conclusion This study suggests that anxiety disorders are common among postpartum women and are three times more common than PPD within this group. Furthermore, 57.1% (8/14) of the females in our study with PPD also suffered from an anxiety disorder.Keywords: Anxiety, Depression, Postpartum period, Social support

INTRODUCTION

Perinatal depression and anxiety disorders significantly impact women across all societies and socioeconomic groups [1]. eISSN 1976-3026 OPEN ACCESS These mental health conditions can arise during pregnancy and persist into the postpartum period, posing substantial challenges for mothers. Such disorders adversely affect a woman’s psychological well-being and hinder her ability to carry out daily activities, bond with her infant, and maintain healthy relationships with other family members [2,3]. Research has shown that maternal mental health issues can disrupt parenting practices, leading to difficulties in attachment and interactions with infants, which are crucial for the child’s emotional and social development. Recent studies indicate that maternal depression and anxiety can also influence neurodevelopmental outcomes in children by affecting essential neurotrophic factors that facilitate brain growth and connectivity [4-7]. This disruption may result in long-term developmental issues, underscoring the urgent need to address maternal mental health as an integral component of prenatal and postpartum care.

Despite the recognition of these issues, a wide range of re-ported prevalence rates for postpartum depression (PPD) exists, primarily due to methodological differences and variations in population samples across studies. Most research indicates that the prevalence of PPD ranges from 6% to 20%, with some studies suggesting even higher rates among at-risk populations [8-14]. These variations can be attributed to differences in sample sizes, demographic characteristics, and the assessment tools used for diagnosis. Furthermore, cultural perceptions surrounding mental health can influence how symptoms are recognized and reported. Understanding the prevalence and implications of perinatal depression and anxiety is crucial for developing effective screening and intervention strategies. As awareness of these conditions continues to grow, there is an increasing emphasis on conducting research that examines both the psychological and physiological aspects of maternal mental health to ensure comprehensive care for mothers and their infants.

Globally, prevalence rates of PPD and anxiety vary. PPD affects approximately 10%–20% of new mothers, with anxiety disorders ranging between 4%–21% depending on the population and methodology [15,16]. In Turkey, recent studies report PPD prevalence rates between 15%–25%, highlighting the significant burden on maternal mental health in this context [17,18]. These findings underscore the necessity of investigating these disorders within Turkish populations to guide culturally appropriate interventions.

Sociodemographic variables such as economic status, education, and marital status have been shown to influence the risk of PPD and anxiety [19]. Psychiatric history, including previous depressive or anxiety episodes, is a strong predictor of postpartum mood disorders [20]. Medical factors like pregnancy complications and infant health can also affect maternal psychological outcomes postpartum [21]. Thus, these variables were included to comprehensively assess their associations with PPD and anxiety in this study.

Maternal-infant attachment plays a crucial role in both maternal mental health and child development [22]. Secure attachment is associated with lower PPD rates [23]. Moreover, perceived social support acts as a protective factor against PPD and anxiety by buffering stress and enhancing coping [24]. Therefore, examining attachment and social support provides valuable insight into the psychosocial dimensions of postpartum mental health.

The primary purpose of this study was to determine the prevalence of PPD and anxiety disorders in the first 6 weeks of the postpartum period among Turkish mothers. Additionally, we examined the effect of comorbidity and related factors on PPD. We hypothesized that lower socioeconomic status, previous psychiatric history, insecure attachment, and lower social support would be associated with increased prevalence of PPD and anxiety disorders.

METHODS

Participants

This cross-sectional case-control study employed the Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) (SCID-I). The population of this study consisted of 206 mothers with a baby between 0 and 6 weeks of age who presented to the Selçuk University Faculty of Medicine Department of Pediatric for routine health checks. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) mothers aged between 18 and 45 years; 2) ability to speak, read, and write Turkish fluently; 3) absence of serious obstetric complications during pregnancy or delivery; and 4) mothers of healthy, term newborns, defined as infants born at or after 37 completed weeks of gestation and weighing at least 2,500 grams at birth. The exclusion criteria included: 1) mothers of infants diagnosed with congenital anomalies or metabolic disorders. The exclusion criterion was mothers of infants diagnosed with congenital anomalies or metabolic disorders.

Initially, 268 pregnant females were screened in the clinic. Patients who did not meet the study’s criteria (n=46, 17.2%) were excluded (24 mothers had serious obstetric complications, 12 mothers had babies with congenital anomalies, and 10 mothers had babies with metabolic disorders), and 16 females (6%) declined to participate in the study. Finally, 206 consecutive patients were included in the study.

This study was approved by the Selçuk University Medical Faculty’s Ethics Committee (approval number: 2015/19, approval date: 08.12.2015). The study’s objectives and procedures were explained, and written informed consent was obtained from the participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Data were collected from December 2015 to June 2016.

Procedures

The objectives and procedures of the study were explained to all participants, and written informed consent forms were obtained. After the babies’ pediatric examinations, psychiatric interviews were performed to determine whether psychiatric disorders were present during the postpartum period of the participants using the SCID-I. To detect any psychiatric disorders during pregnancy, postpartum, and non-pregnancy periods, patients’ histories during these periods were discussed in detail. A psychiatrist (Dr. AS) performed the psychiatric diagnoses. Each interview lasted approximately 1 hour. All the participants completed the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) and the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), and the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) and the Maternal Attachment Inventory (MAI) were administered to all mothers.

Information such as birth weight, birth week, and birth head circumference were obtained from available medical records. Sociodemographic information (e.g., age, education, marital status, place of residence, employment status, and monthly income) was collected using a structured sociodemographic data form developed for this study. Economic status was assessed using self-reported monthly income and categorized relative to the minimum wage at the time of the study (approximately 500 USD/month): low economic status (<500 USD/month, below the minimum wage), moderate economic status (500–999 USD/month, around 1–2 times the minimum wage), and high economic status (≥1,000 USD/month, ≥2 times the minimum wage).

Measures

SCID-I

SCID-I (clinical version) is a semi-structured interview instrument used to establish Axis I psychiatric disorders according to DSM-IV criteria. This instrument is widely used in clinical practice and for research purposes worldwide. The instrument has six modules. The interview instruments have been standardized for Turkish populations [25]. Although the DSM-5 was published in 2013, this study employed the SCID-I because it was the validated and widely accepted diagnostic tool available in Turkey at the time of data collection (2015– 2016). Furthermore, the diagnostic criteria for PPD did not substantially change between DSM-IV and DSM-5, ensuring the clinical relevance and comparability of the findings. In DSM-IV, a “postpartum onset” specifier is applied to mood episodes whose onset occurs within 4 weeks after delivery, whereas DSM-5 replaced this with a broader “peripartum onset” specifier that also includes episodes beginning during pregnancy. In the present study, we focused on depressive episodes emerging in the early postpartum period and, for pragmatic reasons related to routine care in our setting, defined the postpartum assessment window as 0–6 weeks after birth, corresponding to the routine 6-week postnatal check. Accordingly, the episodes identified here should be interpreted as early postpartum-onset depression, representing a subset of DSM-5 peripartum-onset depression, and our prevalence estimates are therefore most comparable to studies assessing early PPD rather than the full peripartum period described in DSM-5 literature.

EPDS

The EPDS was included in all postnatal questionnaires to measure depressive symptomatology. Although this 10-item self-report instrument does not diagnose PPD (diagnosis is possible only through a psychiatric interview), it is the most frequently used measure to assess postpartum depressive symptomatology and identify at-risk mothers. Using a cutoff score of >12, at 6 weeks postpartum, the EPDS has a sensitivity of 68%–95% and a specificity ranging from 78%–96% compared with a diagnosis of major PPD established through a psychiatric interview [26]. The Cronbach’s alpha for this sample was 0.87. The Turkish version of the EPDS has been validated [27].

BAI

The BAI is a 21-item self-report questionnaire. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“severely”). The total score ranges from 0–63 [28]. The Turkish version of the BAI used in this study has been validated in Turkish populations [29]. The Cronbach’s alpha for this sample was 0.89.

MAI

The MAI was developed [30] and tested to provide a practical measure of maternal affectionate attachment. MAI items were identified from the literature, and their construct validity was assessed by experts who numerically rated them for relevance. This scale includes 26 items and uses a 4-point Likert-type measurement. The overall score is obtained by collecting responses to all the questions in the inventory, which are scored as “always=4,” “often=3,” “sometimes=2,” or “never=1.” The total score range is between 26 and 104. High scores indicate strong maternal attachment. The Turkish version of the MAI used in this study has been validated in Turkish populations [31]. The Cronbach’s alpha for this sample was 0.78.

MSPSS

This scale was developed by Zimet et al. [32] to measure perceived social support. The MSPSS is a 12-item scale designed to measure perceived social support from three sources: family, friends, and a significant other. The total score range is between 12 and 84. High scores indicate that perceived support is high, and low scores indicate a perceived lack of support. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version have been confirmed [33]. The Cronbach’s alpha for this sample was 0.91.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 for Windows (IBM Corp.). The χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test, as necessary, were performed to compare categorical variables. The Kolmogorov– Smirnov test was used to analyze whether test distributions were normal. For comparisons within the same study group, the t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test (when data were not normally distributed) was used for continuous variables. A value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. An a priori power analysis, assuming a PPD prevalence of 10% in the target population, an expected odds ratio of approximately 2.5 for key psychosocial risk factors, a two-sided α of 0.05, and 80% power for a two-group comparison (PPD vs. non-PPD), indicated a minimum required sample size of approximately 150 participants. All analyses were univariable and unadjusted; multivariable models with PPD as the outcome were not fitted, and the reported associations should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

RESULTS

The mean age of mothers was 27.85±6.43 years in the PPD group and 27.47±5.53 years in the non-PPD group, with no significant difference between groups (p=0.805). All participants were married and most of them lived in urban areas. The majority had completed high school or higher education, and employment rates were low among mothers. Of all the females, 54 (26.2%) met the criteria for at least one psychiatric disorder during the postpartum period, 14 (6.8%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 4.1–11.1) met the criteria for PPD, 44 (21.4%; 95% CI, 16.3–27.5) met the criteria for an anxiety disorder, 3 (1.5%) met the criteria for a somatoform disorder (or conversion disorder), and 2 (1%) met the criteria for an eating disorder (or binge eating disorder) according to SCID-I. Anxiety disorders were distributed as follows: 33 (16.0%) met the criteria for generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), 13 (6.3%) met the criteria for obsessive compulsive disorder, and 3 (1.5%) met the criteria for panic disorder. Eight females (3.8%; 95% CI, 2.0–7.5) had presented with comorbid PPD and anxiety disorder. Additionally, 3 (1.5%) met the criteria for bipolar disorder; these 3 cases were in remission, and they did not experience any manic or depressive episodes during the study.

Statistical analyses revealed no significant differences between the two study groups in terms of age, settlement, educational level, employment status, education level of partner, or employment status of partner. DISCUSSION

This study was conducted in Central Anatolia, Turkey, and investigated the prevalence of PPD and anxiety disorders; it is one of the largest studies to date using the SCID. The PPD prevalence (6.8%) found in this study aligns with those reported in studies using similar methods [11,34,35]. Notably, the prevalence of anxiety disorders (21.4%) was higher than that of depression during the first 6-week postpartum. The prevalence of comorbid PPD and anxiety disorders was 3.8%, and contributing to the limited body of research on comorbidities in this context.

The prevalence of PPD is usually lower in structured clinical trials than in scale-based trials. In one of the first studies on the subject, Gotlib et al. [36] reported a 3.4% incidence of depression occurring in the postpartum period in the absence of significant depression during pregnancy. Chandran et al. [37] reported a PPD incidence of 11%. Felice et al. [35] found an 8.7% prevalence of depression at 8 weeks postnatally. In recent studies, Kitamura et al. [38] reported the incidence of major depressive episode during puerperium to be 5%. In a study conducted recently in Turkey, Akman et al. [34] found a prevalence of new-onset major depression during the first 6 weeks postpartum of 6.3%. In a recent naturalistic longitudinal study, Banti et al. [11] reported the prevalence of PPD to be 9.6%. Consequently, our findings appear to be consistent with the results of these studies. The prevalence of cliniciandiagnosed PPD in our sample (6.8%) appears lower than the 15%–25% rates reported in many Turkish studies; this discrepancy is likely explained by methodological differences. Most previous Turkish studies have relied on self-report screening instruments (such as the EPDS) and relatively liberal cutoff scores, often assessing depressive symptoms over a broader postnatal period (e.g., several months), which tends to yield higher prevalence estimates. In contrast, we used a clinicianadministered SCID and focused specifically on major depressive episodes emerging in the early 0–6-week postpartum win-dow among females attending routine follow-up in a tertiary care setting. Taken together, these factors probably place our estimate at the lower end of the true spectrum of postpartum depressive symptomatology in the wider community and should be considered when comparing our findings with the existing Turkish literature.

In our study, PPD was unrelated to age, educational level, employment status, marital status, partner’s education level, and partner’s employment status. Some authors have reported that maternal stress, depression, and psychiatric disorders are related to lower birth weight [39,40]. However, we found no connection between PPD and birth week, birth weight, or head circumference. Additionally, we found no relationship between PPD and primiparity, unplanned pregnancy, type of delivery, or type of anesthesia. The factors significantly associated with PPD were: excessive nausea and vomiting during pregnancy, lack of social support, lack of help with baby care, feeding exclusively with formula, and low economic status. These findings corroborate previous research [18,38,41,42]. Furthermore, we observed a significantly higher likelihood of other psychiatric disorders in the PPD group. This suggests that PPD alone may increase the risk of other psychiatric disorders or vise versa, and PPD may adversely affect treatment outcomes.

Anxiety disorders are the most prevalent of all psychiatric conditions. Nearly one-third (28.8%) of the adult population will suffer from an anxiety disorder at some time in their life. Women are approximately 1.5 times more likely to suffer from an anxiety disorder than men [43]. However, compared to the available research and health service focus on PPD, perinatal anxiety disorder has received little attention [14]. In studies on perinatal anxiety disorder, the prevalence ranges from 4% to 21% [1-8]. The notably higher prevalence of anxiety disorders compared to depression in the postpartum period observed in our study is consistent with prior research, which suggests that perinatal anxiety is a common yet often underrecognized condition [14,20]. Several neurobiological mechanisms may contribute to this pattern, including hormonal fluctuations such as alterations in cortisol and oxytocin levels that affect stress regulation during the postpartum period [44]. Psychologically, new mothers may experience heightened vigilance and worry related to infant care, which can exacerbate anxiety symptoms [45]. Social factors such as inadequate social support, economic stress, and role transitions also play critical roles in increasing vulnerability to postpartum anxiety [46]. Together, these multifactorial influences may explain why anxiety disorders often present more frequently than depression postpartum, highlighting the need for tailored screening and interventions.

Our findings indicate that anxiety disorders are common among postpartum females, occurring three times more frequently than depression within this group. Most females in our study with an anxiety disorder were diagnosed with GAD. This predominance of GAD among perinatal anxiety disorders is in line with previous interview-based studies, such as the work by Fairbrother et al. [14], which also reported GAD as the most frequent perinatal anxiety diagnosis when full DSM criteria were applied rather than screening cut-offs. Furthermore, at least half of the women in our study with PPD also suffered from an anxiety disorder. Both the present study and recent studies [2,7,14] have shown that anxiety disorders are more common than depression during the postpartum period, yet this issue is often neglected. Anxiety disorders frequently result in significant impairment and are associated with high levels of healthcare service utilization and indirect health costs. The findings have important implications for the treatment of females with postnatal disorders, as treatment approaches may vary significantly for anxiety, depression, and comorbid presentations. Future studies should focus on the treatment options and outcomes of perinatal anxiety disorders and comorbid depression.

Our study has several limitations. First, it was performed in a tertiary care center with a relatively small number of patients for a prevalence study. Therefore, the results cannot be generalized to all childbearing females. In addition, because participants were recruited during routine well-baby visits in a tertiary care setting and mothers of preterm medically complicated infants were excluded, our sample likely represents a relatively healthier cohort, which may have biased our prevalence estimates downwards. Second, we did not include mothers of babies with metabolic syndrome and congenital anomalies. Third, the study did not include mothers who gave birth prematurely. Fourth, since our study was cross-sectional, we could not report on the longitudinal status of the participants during their follow-up. Finally, we conducted multiple statistical comparisons and did not apply a formal correction for multiple testing, particularly for exploratory analyses; therefore, some of the observed associations should be interpreted with caution and ideally replicated in independent samples. Nevertheless, our study provides more reliable data than other self-report studies because it is based on clinical interviews.

In conclusion, despite some limitations, the present study suggests that anxiety disorders are three times more common than depression among postpartum females (with a prevalence of 21.4% versus 6.8%). In our study, the majority of females with an anxiety disorder suffered from GAD (16%). Additionally, at least half of the females with PPD suffered from an anxiety disorder. In light of these findings, routine screening for anxiety symptoms, in addition to PPD, should be integrated into standard postpartum care, particularly during early follow-up visits. However, these results should be confirmed by prospective studies with larger sample sizes. It is important to recognize that postnatal disorders are not limited to depression, are heterogeneous in nature, and that a onesize-fits-all treatment approach is inadequate. Comorbidity is high in those with PPD and must be considered carefully when planning treatment.
