Enhancing representativeness in population-based surveys to improve data quality and decision-making
Authors: Stefano Rousset, Lorena Charrier, Michela Bersia, Rosanna Irene Comoretto, Paola Dalmasso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOnline surveys often suffer from low response rates, raising concerns about the representativeness and validity of resulting estimates. We evaluated the impact of calibration weighting on data from a cross-sectional online survey on mental health and well-being conducted among university students at the University of Torino, Italy, which had a ~ 10% response rate (eligible population ~ 79,000). Calibration was performed on data from 5,284 students using the raking method and auxiliary variables (sex, course area, and course cycle) to improve representativeness. The unweighted estimate for depressive symptoms prevalence (46.9%, 95% CI: 45.5–48.3) closely matched the weighted result (46.6%, 95% CI: 45.1–48.1). Estimates for suicidal behavior risk were also consistent (34.4%, 95% CI: 33.0–35.7 vs. 34.9%, 95% CI: 33.5–36.4), with only a slight difference observed for anxiety symptoms (72.2%, 95% CI: 70.9–73.4 vs. 69.6%, 95% CI: 68.2–71.0). Unweighted and calibrated estimates were also similar for well-being outcomes (mean overall well-being score: 31.8, 95% CI: 31.4–32.1 vs. 31.9, 95% CI: 31.5–32.3, respectively). These findings suggest that robust estimates may still emerge from large-scale online university surveys exploring mental health outcomes, despite low participation. Further research is needed to assess whether calibration methods yield consistent results across different populations, outcomes, and survey designs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17298-2.Keywords: Population-based survey, Representativeness, Mental health, Well-being, University students, Non-response bias, Calibration, Raking methods.Subject terms: Epidemiology, Statistics

Introduction

Survey-based research is widely used for assessing various aspects of life, including mental health and overall well-being, in the general population and specific subgroups. In particular, cross-sectional surveys among university students have been demonstrated to provide valuable tools for a deeper understanding of academic experiences, behaviors, and mental health outcomes1–3. These methods allow for population representativeness but are limited by the challenge of achieving a high response rate. A general decline in response rates has been observed in surveys over time, in parallel with the increasing use of online surveys4. Potential reasons for this decline might be the use of longer questionnaires and the increase in the number of surveys, making people less likely to respond once invited to participate5,6particularly in psychological surveys. Indeed, while online surveys potentially allow extending the study involvement of a larger population, they generally present lower response rates than those conducted through other methods, such as in-person and telephone interviews5–8. For instance, a meta-analysis of 1,071 online surveys found an average online survey response rate of 44.1%9, and mental health university students’ surveys show even lower response rates. In a US survey, the response rate ranged from 13 to 27% across colleges and periods10while a German study found low and decreasing response rates among university students across time, from 14% in 2019 to 4.7% in 202111. Such low response rates, even in population-based surveys12,13make these studies susceptible to non-response bias, introduced when non-respondents and respondents show different levels of the key features examined in the study14. Hence, such an issue could potentially compromise the generalizability and accuracy of the estimates15,16. Literature on non-response bias (defined as self-selection bias in case of no sampling) has shown that non-respondents tend to display different characteristics compared to respondents regarding several socio-demographic features, such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, and educational level17,18. In particular, it has been shown that males, younger and less educated subjects, and people with a previous diagnosis of a mental disorder are underrepresented in surveys assessing mental health problems19. Hence, it is essential to carefully address the issue of non-responsiveness during the various research stages (i.e., study design, data collection, and data analysis) to obtain valid estimates and improve generalizability. During data analysis, the most widely used methods to handle non-response are propensity score adjustment, which models the response probability and incorporates it into the weighting scheme to reduce bias20and multiple imputation, which fills in missing responses by drawing from a distribution based on observed data21. While these methods can be effective, they often rely on model assumptions and may be sensitive to model misspecification22,23. A weighting approach can also be used to improve estimation accuracy and adjust for differential non-response. Post-stratification is the most straightforward and used technique for computing adjusted weights and does not require any assumptions24. However, it can lead to sparse or empty cells when multiple variables are used, increasing variance and bias24–26. For these reasons, some calibration methods have been developed to overcome this problem. They compute survey weights so that the weighted sample aligns with known population totals concerning variables available for both the respondents and the target population (defined as auxiliary variables), aiming to correct differential response patterns and reduce bias27. More specifically, calibrated weights have been shown to produce less biased estimates when using socioeconomic and demographic information as auxiliary variables28–30. In particular, the raking method does not rely on correct model specification, and it is considered a cost-effective calibration method for improving the accuracy of estimates in the context of wide national surveys31despite the residual bias could persist if the auxiliary variables do not fully capture the differences between respondents and non-respondents32.

Beyond wide national surveys, calibration methods are not routinely used in survey research in educational and psychological contexts11,33partially due to a lack of relevant auxiliary information for the target population. However, the problem of representativeness should be considered routinely to ensure the findings’ generalizability and science replicability34. In addition, to our knowledge, no previous study has been conducted to evaluate the impact of calibration specifically on university students’ survey data.

This study aims to assess the potential impact of calibration weighting on survey estimates and its effectiveness in addressing the challenges associated with low response rates in university student surveys. We analyzed data from a survey conducted among students at the University of Torino (Italy)35comparing prevalence estimates of mental health problems-including anxiety, depression, suicidality, and overall well-being, before and after applying calibration weighting. The goal was to assess the robustness of these estimates following the application of calibration methods.

Methods

Survey data

The data used in this work was derived from a multi-center cross-sectional online survey conducted from May to November 2023 at the University of Torino (Italy), Sevilla (Spain), and Lusòfona (Portugal) to investigate university students’ mental health and well-being35. Since most respondents belonged to the University of Torino (89.7%), the analyses in this study focus exclusively on the Italian subsample.

The target population included 79,778 students registered with the University of Torino during the 2022/2023 academic year. All the eligible students received an institutional email with a link to an anonymous online questionnaire implemented on the REDCap platform of the University of Torino36,37. Representativeness was pursued with a study design that invited the overall target population to participate in the survey and used reminders, ensuring respondents’ anonymity. The survey did not offer incentives to participants to reduce the risk of selection bias due to economic reasons. A detailed description of the inclusion criteria and the enrolment procedure is provided in the published article35.

Among the eligible students, 8,773 responded to the online questionnaire, yielding an overall response rate of 11.1%. Additionally, 78 respondents (0.9%) did not provide informed consent and were excluded. Since the low response rate could have impacted the representativeness15,16calibration weighting was implemented to adjust for potential self-selection bias.

The analysis further excluded 3,411 students who had missing data on at least one of the auxiliary variables. Hence, the final sample comprised 5,284 students (60.2%). Figure 1 shows the flowchart of respondents’ selection.

Questionnaire

The online questionnaire explored several domains whose complete descriptions can be found elsewhere35. The present study analyzed mental health and well-being data, sociodemographic characteristics, academic features, and social support.

Mental health and well-being

Mental health was assessed using validated tools focused on depression, anxiety, and suicide risk. Depression was measured with the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2)38while anxiety was assessed with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2)39. Both are brief self-report scales where participants rate the frequency of symptoms on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Scores of 3 or higher suggest the presence of relevant symptoms. Suicidal ideation and behaviors were evaluated using the Suicide Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R)40a validated self-report tool that includes four items covering ideation, past attempts, and future risk. Total scores range from 3 to 18, with a score of 7 or higher indicating high risk. Before completing the SBQ-R, participants were informed about the sensitive nature of the questions, and the section was made optional.

Well-being was measured using the Mental Health Continuum-Short Form (MHC-SF)41a validated 14-item self-report scale. Participants rated how often they experienced the specific state for each item on a scale from 0 (none of the time) to 5 (all of the time), with higher total scores indicating better well-being. Items 1–3 (score range: 0–15) evaluate emotional well-being, characterized by positive affect and life satisfaction; items 4–8 (score range: 0–25) assess social well-being; items 9–14 (score range: 0–30) evaluate psychological well-being; an overall well-being score (from 0 to 70) was computed by summing all the three sub-scales.

Sociodemographic characteristics, academic features, and social support

The sociodemographic characteristics included age and socioeconomic status (SES). SES was evaluated using the MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status42a 10-run visual ladder where participants placed themselves based on their perceived social standing (1 = lowest, 10 = highest). Academic features included study location, evaluated with a multiple-choice question (“Studying in hometown”, “Commuting for studying”, or “Moving for studying”), and academic progress, assessed with the options: “Perfectly on track”, “Slightly late”, or “Delayed”.

Social support was measured using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS)43a validated psychometric tool. This 12-item scale assesses perceived social support across three subscales (Family, Friends, and Significant Other Support), each consisting of 4 items. Participants rated their agreement on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Subscale scores were the average of the four related items, while the overall support score was obtained by summing all item scores and dividing by 12. All scores range from 1 to 7, with higher scores indicating greater perceived support.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the characteristics of the survey respondents and the target population. One-sample proportion tests were conducted to evaluate differences by sex, course area, and course cycle.

Mental health and well-being, sociodemographic characteristics, academic features, and social support were described using proportions for categorical variables and means for numerical variables, with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

The calibration weighting in this study was based on the three previously described auxiliary variables (sex, course area, and course cycle), adjusting initial sampling weights to ensure that the weighted sample aligns with known population marginal totals of the auxiliary variables. Given that these variables were categorical with a limited number of categories, the raking method, also known as iterative proportional fitting (IPF), was used for calibration25,26,44. It iteratively adjusts initial survey weights to match the weighted marginal distributions of auxiliary variables to their known population totals, accounting for differential response rates across subgroups25,26,44.

Each unit in the dataset was initially assigned a base weight. Since this cross-sectional survey invited all eligible students, it was assumed every student had the same probability of inclusion, and all observations were assigned equal weights. The raking algorithm was then applied iteratively, adjusting weights to match population totals for the first auxiliary variable (e.g., sex), the second (e.g., course area), and the third (e.g., course cycle), independent of the order in which they were introduced in the procedure. The algorithm continued cycling through all auxiliary variables until convergence was achieved, meaning that the weights stabilized and remained close enough to the population totals without requiring further significant adjustments.

Crude and weighted proportions and means were calculated for all variables of interest, along with their 95% confidence intervals. Comparisons between unweighted and weighted estimates were performed using z-tests for both proportions and means, as appropriate. Additional calibration was performed within study areas using area-specific distributions of sex and course cycle.

Associations between the three auxiliary variables and the mental health and well-being outcomes were assessed using either the chi-square test or one-way ANOVA, as appropriate.

To ensure compatibility of categories between respondent and population data, 3,411 students (39.2%) with missing data on at least one of the three auxiliary variables were excluded. Differences between excluded and included students were assessed using t-tests and chi-square tests. A sensitivity analysis included calibration on the full sample (N = 8,695) by adding a “missing” category44.

Since the missing value rate of mental health and well-being variables was around 8–13% (N = 451 for PHQ-2, N = 443 for GAD-2, N = 640 for SBQ-R, and N = 711 for overall MHC-SF score), a further sensitivity analysis included a multiple imputation of these variables using the multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) approach21. The raking algorithm was then applied to each imputed dataset (n = 20), and the unweighted and weighted estimates obtained in each dataset were combined into overall estimates (means and proportions with confidence intervals) using Rubin’s rule45.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0. In particular, calibration was performed with the “rake” function from the “survey” package and multiple imputations with the “mice” function from the “mice” package.

Ethical considerations

Informed consent was obtained from all the included subjects. The data were collected anonymously, and no personally identifiable information was recorded. The original cross-sectional survey on which this study is based received approval from the institutional ethics board of the University of Torino (Prot. no. 0059546, January 30, 2023).

The study adhered to international guidelines and regulations and was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Characteristics of the survey respondents and target population

Figure 2 presents the distribution of general characteristics among the 5,284 survey participants and the target population of 79,778 students by sex, course area, and course cycle. The study sample showed an overrepresentation of females (75.7% vs. 62.0%), post-graduate students (27.2% vs. 21.6%), and medical, natural, and veterinary students (19.6% vs. 12.2%, 18.6% vs. 16.8%, and 2.3% vs. 1.6%, respectively), compared to the target population. Furthermore, an underrepresentation of students attending humanistic and philosophical sciences (26.6% vs. 28.7%), law sciences (5.7% vs. 9.4%), and social and economic sciences (24.9% vs. 28.6%) was observed. Mental health and well-being

As shown in Fig. 3, nearly half of the students (46.9%) reported depressive symptoms, 72.2% experienced anxiety symptoms, and 34.4% were at high risk of suicidal behavior. Raking calibration had minimal impact on the estimates for PHQ-2 and SBQ-R, while a slight decrease was observed for GAD-2, with the proportion of students reporting high anxiety symptoms declining from 72.2 to 69.6% (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S2). Respondents exhibited low overall well-being, with a mean MHC-SF score of 31.8 (95% CI 31.4–32.1) consistent across the emotional (8.4, 95% CI 8.3–8.5), social (7.0, 95% CI 6.9–7.2), and psychological domains (16.4, 95% CI 16.2–16.6) (Supplementary Table S2). These estimates remained stable after applying calibrated weights (Supplementary Table S2). Additionally, the results remained essentially unchanged after multiple imputations were performed to address missing data on mental health and well-being variables (Supplementary Table S3).

Unweighted and weighted estimates of anxiety symptoms (GAD-2), depressive symptoms (PHQ-2), and suicidal behaviour (SBQ-R).

Mental health and well-being outcomes were also explored within study areas, using area-specific distributions of sex and course cycle to obtain calibrated estimates. Both unweighted and weighted estimates for GAD-2, PHQ-2, SBQ-R, and MHC-SF were consistent across study areas, with largely overlapping confidence intervals (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table S4).

Unweighted and weighted estimates of anxiety symptoms (GAD-2), depressive symptoms (PHQ-2), and suicidal behaviour (SBQ-R) stratified by study area.

Finally, we found significant associations between the three auxiliary variables and the mental health and well-being outcomes (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). In particular, sex was significantly associated with the prevalence of depressive (p = 0.044) and anxiety symptoms (p < 0.001) (Table S5), as well as with overall well-being (p < 0.001) (Table S6). Course area was strongly associated with all outcomes, while course cycle was associated with suicidality (p = 0.033) (Table S5) and the social domain of well-being (p = 0.01) (Table S6).

Characteristics of excluded students

Supplementary Tables S7 and S8 report the characteristics of the 3,411 respondents excluded from the final dataset due to missing data in at least one of the three auxiliary variables used for calibration weighting (sex, course area, and course cycle). Compared to the final sample, the excluded respondents were slightly older (mean age of 24.3 years vs. 23.6 years), more likely to be studying in their hometown (33.7% vs. 30.9%), and more frequently delayed in their studies (25.2% vs. 20.0%) (Supplementary Table S7). Considering mental health outcomes, the most notable difference was observed in the PHQ-2 score, with a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms among excluded students (49.9% vs. 46.9%) (Supplementary Table S8). Differences in MSPSS and MHC-SF were non-significant (Supplementary Tables S7 and S8). As a sensitivity analysis, calibration on the full sample (N = 8,695), adding a “missing category” was also performed without observing significant modifications of the unweighted and calibrated estimates (Supplementary Tables S9 and S10).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the impact of calibration weighting on estimates of mental health and well-being derived from an online survey conducted among university students in Torino (Italy)35. Given the survey’s low response rate, calibration was performed using the raking method to mitigate potential self-selection bias and enhance the representativeness for key auxiliary variables (sex, course area, and course cycle). Several outcomes were analyzed before and after applying calibrated weights concerning sociodemographic characteristics, academic features, social support, mental health, and well-being.

Respondents’ representativeness

In the present study, survey respondents showed some distribution discrepancies for the measurable auxiliary variables (i.e., sex, course area, and course cycle) compared with the target population of university students. While differences in sample representativeness are well documented in psychological research46,47to our knowledge, this is the first study comparing the distribution of the characteristics between survey respondents and the population in a university student setting on mental health themes. In particular, we observed a notable overrepresentation of females and postgraduate students (who are likely to be older, suggesting an underrepresentation of younger subjects), consistent with patterns reported in previous surveys17,19,28,29. Additionally, medical, natural, and veterinary science students were overrepresented, while those in humanistic, legal, and socio-economic fields were underrepresented. Further survey research could compare the produced evidence to provide a more comprehensive picture.

Impact of calibration

Overall, we found a general consistency between unweighted and calibrated estimates, with no significant differences in age, socioeconomic status, academic progress, and social support. The only slight difference was observed in the studying location: the calibrated estimate showed fewer students who reported moving for their studies than unweighted ones (33.5% vs. 35.3%). These findings suggest that course area and cycle variations did not substantially impact estimates of the key sociodemographic and academic characteristics.

Given the growing concerns regarding students’ psychological conditions, mental health and well-being outcomes were a key focus of this study48. As previously reported35a high proportion of students reported symptoms of depression (46.9%) and anxiety (72.2%), and more than a third (34.4%) were at high risk for suicidal behavior. After applying calibrated weights, only a slight decrease in reported anxiety symptoms was observed (69.6% vs. 72.2%), likely due to correcting the sample’s overrepresentation of females—who typically report higher anxiety levels49. The well-being indicator remained essentially unchanged after raking, suggesting minimal bias from self-selection. These results remained consistent even after multiple imputations were performed on the missing outcome values, reinforcing the findings’ robustness. Our findings might have been biased by the exclusion of a high proportion of students (38.9%) due to missingness in at least one of the auxiliary variables used for calibration. The excluded group exhibited some differences compared to the final sample, showing higher mean age (24.3 vs. 23.6 years), being more frequently delayed in their study (25.2% vs. 20%), and displaying a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms (49.9% vs. 46.9%). However, both unweighted and calibrated estimates were essentially unchanged after calibration on the full sample of 8,695 students, suggesting that excluding a significant proportion of respondents had a negligible impact on the results.

Additionally, our analysis identified significant associations between the auxiliary variables used for calibration and the outcomes related to mental health and well-being, especially for sex and course area. This supports the decision to include these variables in the weighting process. Nevertheless, the estimates remained relatively stable even after applying the calibrated weights. This suggests that, while these variables seem to be relevant to the phenomenon being studied, they could only explain a limited amount of the observed variation in mental health outcomes. Their potentially modest explanatory power is reasonable, considering the complex nature of mental health.

Globally, the stability of estimates after applying calibration suggests that mental health and well-being outcomes were robust to differences in respondents’ composition in terms of sex, area of study, and course cycle, underscoring the severity of psychological distress among Italian university students and its public health relevance3,35. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare unweighted and calibrated estimates from a population-based online survey on mental health among university students. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, as they pave the way for further research on the topic, which could verify the findings of the present work.

Implications for research

The findings of this study have significant implications for mental health survey research involving university students. First, they promote a scientific debate on the importance of calibration techniques in addressing potential non-response and self-selection biases, particularly when response rates fall below 70%50. However, the relatively small changes observed in most estimates suggest that well-designed, population-based online self-administered surveys in a university setting may be less affected by non-response bias. In such cases, calibration techniques may not be necessary to interpret the results correctly, potentially because of the sufficient sample capability of capturing the population variability. This is particularly relevant for researchers in population-based surveys, as calibration techniques are not always feasible, requiring access to high-quality, comparable auxiliary information for respondents and the target population. Beyond these specific contexts, the results of the present work encourage the wider adoption of calibration techniques as a good scientific practice to make high-quality statistical inferences from mental health and university students’ population-based online survey data51.

Strengths and limitations

The relevant strengths of this study are its large sample size and the rigorous methodology used for calibration, which is based on high-quality auxiliary data available for the target population. Moreover, the original survey design also enhanced generalizability by inviting the entire target population and using reminders to participate35thus avoiding sampling errors.

Some limitations must also be acknowledged. First, while calibration helps address non-response bias, it does not account for potential misreporting or social desirability effects, which is the tendency of survey respondents to answer questions in a manner they believe will be viewed favorably by others52. However, online self-administered surveys are generally less sensitive to this bias, and applying weights can mitigate its impact53. Second, about 40% of respondents were excluded from the primary analysis due to missing data on at least one of the auxiliary variables. Even if sensitivity analyses using the full sample yielded similar results, supporting the robustness of the findings, future surveys with a low rate of missing data are warranted to further confirm the produced evidence54.

Third, calibration was implemented using merely three auxiliary variables (i.e., sex, course area, and course cycle) due to data availability constraints. Some bias may remain if these variables were not strongly associated with non-response or key outcomes27,32. Including additional variables like age and socioeconomic status in future calibration models could improve the accuracy of weighted estimates18.

Fourth, while mental health estimates were consistent between raw and calibrated data at the population level, generalizing this consistency to specific subgroups should be done cautiously. Although similar patterns were observed across study areas, assuming that subsamples could capture their variability is challenging. Further research with broader population data is needed to assess the reliability of subgroup estimates.

Conclusion

Calibration weighting improved the representativeness of the present survey with respect to sex, course area, and course cycle without significantly altering most estimates. The calibration procedure showed a negligible impact on most sociodemographic, academic, and psycho-social characteristics, suggesting that the unweighted estimates broadly captured the students’ population variability. This consistency reinforces the robustness of our original findings35suggesting that the over- or underrepresentation of specific subgroups in the respondents did not necessarily hinder the validity of results from large and well-designed population-based online surveys exploring mental health outcomes in a university setting. The present work provides a valuable starting point for exploring the application of raking methods in survey research. In particular, although our findings are based on data from a single university in Italy, they offer useful insights into how these techniques can enhance the robustness and validity of estimates in student populations. Future research is needed to assess whether similar approaches yield consistent results in diverse settings, including a range of populations, outcomes, and survey designs. Such efforts could contribute to the broader scientific discussion on data quality, replicability, and external validity in mental health survey research34.
