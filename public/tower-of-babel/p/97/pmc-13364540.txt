Cardio‐Protective Effect of GLP‐1 Agonists in OSA Patients: A Study of Cardiovascular Risk
Authors: Morgan Lane, Alex Jin, Boston Andersen, Alec Kadrie, Sage Hebert, Evan Thomson, M Boyd Gillespie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is highly associated with obesity and often results in significant cardiovascular complications. This study aimed to assess cardiovascular outcomes in OSA patients treated with GLP‐1 agonists (GLP1a) versus CPAP.MethodsThis retrospective cohort analysis using TriNetX datasets (2014–2024) compared cardiovascular outcomes between four pairs of cohorts, after propensity score matching (PSM): OSA+CPAP +/− GLP1a, OSA+Obesity + CPAP +/− GLP1a, OSA—CPAP +/− GLP1a, and OSA+Obesity—CPAP +/− GLP1a. Additional research is required to determine whether obesity status modulates the observed benefits among patients with OSA.Keywords: cardiovascular outcomes, glucagon‐like Peptide‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1a), obstructive sleep apnea

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a multifactorial disease affecting close to 1 billion people globally [1] and is associated with cardiovascular and neurovascular comorbidities [2, 3]. The obesity endotype is a major cause of OSA due to increased mass of the pharynx and neck causing partial and complete collapse of the upper airway during sleep [1, 4]. Although continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) remains the gold standard for OSA management in adults, CPAP has a poor compliance rate and has not demonstrated significant improvement in cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events [5]. Given the strong association between obesity and OSA, recent studies have examined the possibility of Glucagon‐like Peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists as a viable adjunct or alternative to CPAP in the treatment of OSA [6].

GLP‐1 agonists (GLP‐1a) such as semaglutide and liraglutide have transformed the management of obesity and type 2 diabetes [7], with growing evidence pointing toward their broader metabolic and cardiovascular benefits [8]. GLP‐1As reduce appetite, promote weight loss, and improve insulin sensitivity [7] and may exert effects independent of weight reduction such as reducing systemic inflammation and improving vascular endothelial function [9]. Emerging studies have reported reductions in the Apnea‐Hypopnea Index (AHI) and OSA severity among patients on GLP‐1 therapy [6, 10] even in the absence of significant weight loss [11]. Current data on the effectiveness of these medications for the treatment of OSA have been limited to small trials or short follow‐up periods, and few studies have explored the broader impact of GLP‐1 therapy on OSA‐related cardiovascular morbidity using real‐world data.

In order to better understand how emerging GLP‐1As influence long‐term cardiovascular outcomes in patients with OSA, there is a need for additional investigation in populations containing both CPAP user and nonuser patients. This study aims to evaluate OSA patients stratified by GLP‐1 prescribed, CPAP prescribed, and obesity status in order to evaluate whether GLP‐1 therapy is associated with reduced long‐term cardiovascular complications and to determine their potential role as adjunctive or alternative therapies in the management of OSA‐related comorbidities.

The aims of this study are to analyze the use of GLP‐1 receptor agonists in treating OSA in patients with various comorbidities, and to compare OSA complications (hypertension, cardiac disease or events) in the following 4 pairs of cohorts, for a total of 8 groups: + CPAP +/− GLP1a, + Obesity + CPAP +/− GLP1a, − CPAP +/− GLP1a, and + Obesity—CPAP +/− GLP1a.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective cohort analysis compared outcomes of OSA patients treated with or without a GLP‐1 agonist. Datasets were obtained from the Clinical Trials Network of Tennessee, consisting of 19 hospitals and large practices across the state, within the TriNetX Research Network. TriNetX utilizes de‐identified data, displaying combined and summarized results from electronic health records with individual records obfuscated. TriNetX met the HIPAA Privacy Rule by way of Expert Determination in 2020 [12]. Additionally, this study was considered exempt by the University of Tennessee Institutional Review Board (RB number: 25–11,044‐NSHR).

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with an OSA diagnosis between 2014 and 2024 were identified using ICD‐10 code G47.33. OSA patients were then identified by the presence or absence of obesity (BMI ≥ 30, ICD code E66.0) as well as CPAP prescription (CPT code 94660) to create specific subgroups. Within each subgroup, cohorts were defined based on the presence or absence of GLP‐1 agonist prescription (ATC code A10BJ) in patients with a preexisting or same‐day OSA diagnosis. OSA diagnosis, GLP‐1 prescription initiation, and CPAP prescription initiation served as exposure‐defining events and as the index date for OSA only, GLP‐1 prescribed OSA patients, and CPAP prescribed OSA patient subgroups, respectively. There were eight groups in total. Demographic variables such as age, sex, race, and BMI as well as preexisting comorbidities were analyzed to identify baseline differences between each pair of cohorts.

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

The primary outcomes of interest included cardiovascular events, cardiac interventions, and emergency department (ED) visits, which were measured 1 day to up to 10 years after diagnosis of OSA and GLP‐1 therapy initiation or 1 day up to 10 years after initial OSA diagnosis for GLP‐1 non‐prescribed cohorts. These outcomes were collected using ICD‐10 and CPT codes listed in the Appendix A.

Statistical analyses were conducted using TriNetX analytics tools, ensuring consistency and reproducibility of findings. PSM was performed using logistic regression to balance baseline characteristics between GLP‐1 prescribed subjects (+GLP1) and non‐prescribed subjects (–GLP1) prior to outcome assessment. Covariates included demographic variables such as age, sex, race, and relevant comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity/overweight status, chronic pulmonary disease, smoking status, and baseline cardiovascular disease. Baseline covariates included prior diagnosis of cardiovascular outcomes measured in this study. However, pre‐index diagnoses were treated as baseline comorbid conditions and were not considered incident outcomes during follow‐up after the index date; only events occurring after the index date for each cohort contributed to outcome incidence. A full list of covariates utilized for PSM is listed in Appendix A. Relative risk (RR) calculations with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were calculated to compare outcomes between matched cohorts. A significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Following initial data analysis, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was used to account for multiple comparisons across correlated clinical outcomes. p values were then adjusted using a false discovery rate of 5%, preserving statistical power while limiting false‐positive findings. The adjusted p value was set at ≤ 0.006.

Results

A total of 3,925,130 eligible patients were screened within the TriNetX Clinical Trials Network of Tennessee database. Of these, 133,050 adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a diagnosis of OSA between 2014 and 2024 were identified using the ICD‐10 code G47.33. Patients were stratified by CPAP prescription, obesity status, and prescribed GLP‐1a therapy. Before PSM, large imbalances in cohort size existed between GLP‐1 prescribed and non‐prescribed across all strata defined by CPAP prescription and obesity status. After 1:1 PSM, cohort sizes were matched to available GLP‐1 prescribed populations within each stratum (Figure 2). Matched cohort sizes were as follows: OSA –CPAP (n = 5690), OSA+CPAP (n = 280), Obese OSA –CPAP (n = 2940), Obese OSA+CPAP (n = 180).

Cardiovascular Outcomes

In the –CPAP +GLP1 cohort, patients prescribed GLP1 agonist therapy had significantly lower risks of multiple cardiovascular events (Figure 1): myocardial infarction (RR 0.650 [0.523–0.808], ARR 1.2%, p = 0), heart failure (RR 0.500 [0.357–0.701], ARR 0.9%, p = 0), cardiac arrest (RR 0.300 [0.199–0.450], ARR 1.2%, p = 0), ventricular arrhythmias (RR 0.385 [0.278–0.532], ARR 1.4%, p = 0), atrial fibrillation/flutter (RR 0.833 [0.762–0.912], ARR 2.6%, p = 0), and percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) or coronary arterial bypass graft (CABG) interventions (RR 0.333 [0.163–0.681], ARR 0.4%, p = 0.002). No statistically significant differences were observed in stroke or pulmonary hypertension between +GLP1 and –GLP1 groups in this –CPAP cohort.

Among +CPAP patients, GLP‐1 prescription was associated with a significantly decreased risk of atrial fibrillation/flutter (RR 0.700 [0.542–0.905], ARR 10.7%, p = 0.006), with no other significant differences observed in cardiovascular outcomes.

Similarly in the Obese –CPAP cohort, patients on prescribed GLP‐1 therapy had decreased risks of myocardial infarction (RR 0.727 [0.548–0.966], ARR 1%, p = 0.027), heart failure (RR 0.600 [0.383–0.941], ARR 0.7%, p = 0.024), cardiac arrest (RR 0.400 [0.239–0.670], ARR 1%, p = 0), and ventricular arrhythmias (RR 0.571 [0.389–0.840], ARR 1%, p = 0.004). In this group, no differences were noted in atrial fibrillation/flutter, PCI/CABG procedures, stroke, or pulmonary hypertension.

The Obese +CPAP group showed a significantly decreased risk of myocardial infarction in those prescribed GLP‐1 therapy (RR 0.333 [0.168–0.661], ARR 11.1%, p = 0.001). No other significant differences were observed between these groups.

Emergency Department Utilization

Across all cohorts without prescribed CPAP, patients prescribed GLP‐1 therapy demonstrated an increased risk of ED visits. OSA patients without CPAP prescribed GLP‐1 therapy had a relative risk of 1.162 [1.091–1.237], ARR 3.9%, p = 0 and OSA patients with obesity and without CPAP prescribed GLP‐1 therapy had a relative risk of 1.275 [1.171–1.389], ARR 6.5%, p = 0. This pattern was not observed in CPAP prescribed subjects, where no significant differences in ED visit rates were found between +GLP1 and –GLP1 groups.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we found that the prescription of GLP‐1 receptor agonists was associated with significantly reduced cardiovascular morbidity among patients with OSA, particularly in those not prescribed CPAP therapy. Specifically, GLP‐1 prescription was related to a decreased risk of myocardial infarction, heart failure, cardiac arrest, and both ventricular and atrial arrhythmias. These protective effects were observed in both obese and non‐obese OSA subgroups. This suggests that a GLP‐1a prescription is associated with improved cardiovascular outcomes independent of weight loss and obesity. Among patients using CPAP, GLP‐1 receptor agonist prescription was associated with a lower risk of atrial fibrillation/flutter and, in obese patients, a reduced risk of myocardial infarction.

Current literature has evaluated the cardioprotective qualities of GLP‐1 receptor agonists. Multiple trials using GLP‐1 receptor agonists have demonstrated an association with decreased blood pressure [6, 10, 13]. In patients with diabetes mellitus, GLP‐1 receptor agonists have been shown to decrease thromboembolic events. Other studies demonstrated reductions in vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis in animal models [6]. Post‐MI patients also exhibited improvement in systolic function following GLP‐1 infusion [6]. Our manuscript is consistent with such findings in the literature and expands the body of knowledge by evaluating cardiovascular outcomes such as myocardial infarction and heart failure.

It is well‐established in literature that GLP‐1 receptor agonist use can reduce AHI in obese patients with OSA [14, 15]. Tirzepatide has been FDA approved for OSA treatment in adults with obesity [16]. While prescription of GLP‐1 receptor agonists is becoming increasingly used in patients with OSA and concurrent obesity, the application of GLP‐1 receptor agonists to specifically reduce cardiovascular complications in OSA patients regardless of obesity status remains an area for future inquiry to possibly expand the utility of such medications in OSA patients.

The mechanisms by which GLP‐1 receptor agonists exert cardioprotective effects in OSA patients are likely multifactorial. The resulting weight loss from these medications may result in reduced upper airway adiposity and a reduction in overall systemic inflammation [17]. These physiological changes may contribute to a reduction of OSA severity; therefore, indirectly lowering cardiovascular risks. Global weight loss achieved with GLP‐1 therapy may lead to decreased cardiac strain and increased ability to exercise. Likewise, the metabolic effects of GLP‐1 receptor agonist use on established markers of cardiovascular risk, like glycemic control and lipid profiles, provide another plausible mechanism [18, 19]. Finally, the mechanical impact of changes in blood pressure with GLP‐1 receptor agonist use is another possible explanation for our observed risk reduction in cardiovascular complications of OSA patients prescribed GLP‐1 s.

Across all non‐CPAP cohorts, GLP‐1 recipients demonstrated increased rates of ED visits. A similar trend was not observed in subjects prescribed both CPAP and GLP‐1 therapy. All cohorts without CPAP that utilized GLP‐1 s had increased risk of ED visits, irrespective of obesity. The data available did not indicate the reason for ED visits. This may be related to common gastrointestinal symptoms associated with GLP‐1 receptor agonist use such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, abdominal pain, cholelithiasis, and pancreatitis [20]. This effect, however, was not observed in cohorts prescribed CPAP and GLP‐1 s. Therefore, it is unclear whether the ED visits in the non‐CPAP cohort were a result of GLP‐1 use or another confounding variable, or if CPAP use is a protective factor against GLP‐1 receptor‐related hospitalizations. Either way, the combination of CPAP and GLP‐1 appears to be associated with reduced acute ED visits in the OSA population than from either therapy alone. Further research in this area would be necessary to fully understand these findings.

Limitations and Future Directions

The AHI scale was not included in the TriNetX database as available data. Our methodology for this paper was to examine the well‐established cardiovascular side effects independent of AHI. However, AHI may correlate better with disease severity, possibly leading to differences in cardiovascular outcomes. Possible future directions include a clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy of GLP‐1 agonists in a clinical setting that would support long‐term follow‐up. In addition, the data could only identify subjects prescribed therapy (CPAP and GLP‐1 agonist) but had no method to verify adherence to prescribed therapy. The data likewise did not account for alternative OSA therapy such as surgery, which is increasingly being offered to patients who cannot adhere to CPAP. In addition to adherence, socioeconomic status and healthcare utilization patterns may influence observed associations. Patients prescribed GLP‐1 agonists or CPAP may differ systematically in terms of access to care, frequency of medical encounters, insurance coverage, and overall health‐seeking behavior. Additionally, weight loss has many benefits that can indirectly affect cardiovascular outcomes, and the weight loss achieved with GLP‐1 agonist prescription may serve as a confounder, as weight loss could not be stratified due to a limited dataset.

This study is subject to limitations related to cohort entry and outcome measurement. Because index dates differed between cohorts (OSA diagnosis vs. GLP‐1 initiation vs. CPAP initiation), there is potential for Immortal Time Bias due to delayed entry into the GLP‐1 or CPAP cohort. Outcomes that occurred prior to each cohort's index date were not included. However, because outcomes were defined using diagnosis codes, it is possible that events occurring prior to GLP‐1 or CPAP initiation may still be listed as a diagnosis following the index event. This can potentially affect temporality and underestimate therapy benefit. Outcomes were evaluated across multiple cohorts without adjustment for multiple testing, resulting in an increased risk of a Type 1 error. Additionally, some of the outcomes measured may be affected by small cohort populations present in a few groups (n = 280, n = 180).

The exact GLP‐1 agonist, dosage, and duration of prescription for each patient's care were not specified in TriNetX. While in the same class of medication, the GLP‐1 agonists are known to be heterogeneous [8]; a sufficiently powered clinical trial using a single GLP‐1 receptor agonist would be helpful in determining a specific efficacy. Last, identifying specific patient subgroups that benefit most from the addition of a GLP‐1 agonist would be beneficial in treatment guidelines centered around inclusion of a GLP‐1 receptor agonist.

Conclusions

GLP‐1 receptor agonists have been growing in popularity for a myriad of conditions. Using a large, certified database, we found that GLP‐1 receptor agonists prescription was associated with improved cardiovascular outcomes in obese patients specifically, as well as the full cohort of OSA patients regardless of prescribed CPAP use. CPAP prescription alone lacked the same association with cardiovascular benefits. The exact cardiovascular metric differed depending on the group. A longitudinal study of the effects of GLP‐1 agonists in the setting of OSA would be useful to determine patient subgroups that benefit most, as well as variation in efficacy across GLP‐1 agonist medications.
