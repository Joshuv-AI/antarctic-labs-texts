Perioperative anesthetic management and procedural sedation in a child with type 1 congenital long QT Syndrome undergoing serial procedures: a case report
Authors: Youbing Tu, Dajun Xing, Zhongjie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCongenital long QT syndromes (cLQTS) are inherited cardiac channelopathies that predispose pediatric patients to ventricular arrhythmias, particularly Torsades de Pointes (TdP), during the perioperative period. Data regarding repeated anesthetic and sedation exposures in this high-risk population remain limited.Case presentationWe present an 8-year-old boy with genetically confirmed LQT1 and multiple systemic comorbidities who underwent five general anesthetics and four procedural sedations within a four-month timeframe. Management focused on preserving hemodynamic stability, attenuating autonomic fluctuations, minimizing drug-induced prolongation of cardiac repolarization, and ensuring immediate availability of rescue resources. Anesthesia was maintained via balanced volatile or intravenous regimens, while procedural sedation employed dexmedetomidine-midazolam based anxiolysis. Introduction

Congenital long QT syndromes (cLQTS) are inherited cardiac channelopathies characterized by delayed ventricular repolarization and susceptibility to Torsades de Pointes (TdP) [1], a polymorphic ventricular tachycardia that may culminate in syncope, seizures, or sudden cardiac death [2, 3]. Over 90% of cases are attributable to mutations in KCNQ1 (LQT1), KCNH2 (LQT2), or SCN5A (LQT3), with LQT1 typically triggered by adrenergic stress and LQT3 by bradycardia [2, 4].

The pediatric perioperative environment poses substantial risks, as surgical and anesthetic stresses disrupt autonomic and ionic homeostasis [3, 5]. In LQT1, IKs loss-of-function mutations impair QT interval adaptation to tachycardia, rendering patients vulnerable to sympathetic-mediated arrhythmias [2, 3, 5]. Clinical evidence demonstrates that preoperative anxiety or inadequate sedation can trigger sudden ventricular fibrillation [6]. Procedural stimuli, including painful peripheral intravenous cannulation, laryngoscopy and surgical incision, elicit catecholamine surges that heterogeneously shorten the ventricular refractory period, exacerbate transmural dispersion of repolarization (TDR), and induce early afterdepolarizations (EADs)—the electrophysiological substrate for TdP [2, 3, 5]. Furthermore, various perioperative agents, including certain volatile anesthetics, neuromuscular blockers, and antiemetics, risk exacerbating QTc prolongation or autonomic instability [3, 7].

Despite these hazards, consensus-based anesthetic guidelines for cLQTS remain limited, with clinical practice largely reliant on small-scale studies [8, 9]. While Whyte et al. reported a low perioperative TdP incidence, recent prospective data from a national audit documented significant complications, including ventricular tachycardia and bradycardia, in 3.3% of pediatric anesthetics [10, 11]. These data highlight the need for individualized risk assessment and careful reporting of repeated exposures. Here, we report the clinical course of an 8-year-old boy with genetically confirmed LQT1 who underwent five general anesthetics and four procedural sedations within four months, illustrating how established risk-reduction principles were applied in one complex clinical setting.

Case presentation

Patient Information

An 8-year-old boy (34.8 kg, 114 cm) with genetically confirmed LQT1 (KCNQ1 c.1095T > G missense mutation) was admitted for evaluation of recurrent syncope of six years’ duration and consideration of implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) therapy. Since age 21 months, the patient had experienced monthly episodes of stress- or exertion-provoked syncope, typically preceded by irritability and agitation, and characterized by tachycardia (150–170 bpm) and loss of consciousness. Baseline 12-lead ECGs revealed a narrow QRS complex and a QTc of 462–463 ms (Bazett formula); as QRS duration was normal, the JTc interval was not utilized for perioperative risk assessment. He was maintained on oral propranolol (10 mg twice daily). Family history was notable for LQT1 and ICD utilization in three paternal relatives, including one case of sudden nocturnal death.

The patient’s medical history was marked by complex neurodevelopmental and multi-organ comorbidities relevant to perioperative management. Neurological status included refractory focal epilepsy managed with lacosamide, persistent left-sided hemiparesis post-corticectomy, and hydrocephalus treated with a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt. Additional diagnoses included cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorder, and global developmental delay, though no unifying genetic etiology beyond the KCNQ1 mutation was confirmed. Significant systemic issues included chronic gastrointestinal ulceration, neurogenic bladder requiring intermittent catheterization, and glaucoma. Physical examination revealed marked short stature and diffuse muscle wasting, with an unremarkable airway (Mallampati I) and stable cardiac status. Neurological examination confirmed stable right-sided strength (grade V) and residual left-sided deficits (grade II–III).

Anesthetic and Sedation Timeline

Over a four-month period spanning three admissions, the patient underwent five general anesthetics and four procedural sedations for multidisciplinary indications (Tables 1 and 2). All general anesthetic procedures were conducted by a senior pediatric anesthesiologist, with continuous ECG rhythm monitoring, defibrillator availability, and emergency intravenous magnesium sulfate on standby, and perioperative 12-lead ECG assessment when clinically indicated. Cardiac risk assessment was prioritized at the outset. The selection of anesthetic techniques was strategically stratified based on clinical acuity and airway safety requirements. The first general anesthetic was undertaken for the implantation of an insertable cardiac monitor (ICM) to capture rhythm data during spontaneous syncope, thereby informing the clinical decision regarding permanent ICD implantation; esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy were performed during the same session to evaluate chronic feeding intolerance and constipation. Subsequent interventions, including brain MRI and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) for nutritional and medication delivery, required general anesthesia to ensure prolonged immobility and airway protection in the setting of severe reflux esophagitis and neurological complexity. Later, recurrent fever and ventriculoperitoneal shunt dysfunction required shunt revision, followed by removal of the infected shunt and conversion to an external ventricular drain. Diagnostic imaging not requiring airway support was performed via a standardized sedation protocol utilizing intranasal dexmedetomidine and oral midazolam.

General anesthesia and procedural sedation strategy

A standardized framework governed all clinical encounters, centered on preserving hemodynamic stability, attenuating autonomic fluctuations, minimizing drug-induced prolongation of cardiac repolarization, and ensuring immediate availability of rescue resources. Preoperative preparation included review of QT-prolonging medications, confirmation of uninterrupted beta-blocker and antiepileptic therapy, and correction of electrolyte (potassium, magnesium, calcium), glucose, temperature, and ventilation abnormalities. This phase was concluded by 12-lead ECG analysis and cardiology verification of the insertable cardiac monitor. Intraoperatively, management was directed by a senior pediatric anesthesiologist with continuous ECG rhythm monitoring, immediate defibrillator availability, and emergency intravenous magnesium sulfate on standby, with attention to gentle airway manipulation and readiness to treat Torsades de Pointes(TdP). Anesthetic strategy was individualized: etomidate was selected initially for hemodynamic neutrality during early rhythm phenotype clarification [12], while subsequent procedures used single-bolus propofol (2-2.5 mg/kg) with opioid titration because available pediatric data suggest that isolated propofol boluses do not aggravate baseline QTc intervals or transmural dispersion of repolarization [13, 14]. Maintenance involved low-to-moderate sevoflurane (1–2%) titrated with remifentanil (0.15–0.2 µg/kg/min) to blunt sympathetic responses [14–16], alongside rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) for neuromuscular blockade. Sugammadex (2 mg/kg) was utilized for reversal to avoid the autonomic volatility associated with anticholinesterase-anticholinergic combinations [17], and fluid management was maintained with isotonic solutions supplemented with dextrose only when clinically indicated. Postoperative monitoring was conducted in a monitored bed setting with continuous electrocardiographic surveillance for a minimum of 24 h after each procedure. Serum electrolytes were reassessed within two hours of emergence and corrected when indicated, and oral beta-blocker and antiepileptic therapy were resumed as soon as enteral intake was re-established.

For diagnostic imaging outside the operating room, a standardized non-intravenous sedation protocol-intranasal dexmedetomidine (2–2.5 µg/kg) combined with oral midazolam (0.12–0.25 mg/kg)-was employed to achieve reliable sedation and patient immobility during prolonged, noise-intensive examinations. This regimen was prioritized to ensure high-quality imaging by minimizing agitation and preventing arousal induced by the MRI environment. Dexmedetomidine was chosen for its sympatholytic profile and negligible impact on cardiac repolarization, with dosing restricted to a single administration under continuous hemodynamic monitoring to manage potential bradycardic risks.

Outcomes and follow-up

Across all nine anesthetic and sedation encounters, no episodes of Torsades de Pointes, sustained ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, severe bradycardia, or cardiac arrest occurred. Hemodynamic parameters remained within 20% of preoperative baselines, and serial 12-lead electrocardiograms obtained the morning after each procedure demonstrated no clinically significant change in QTc. No unplanned hospital or intensive care unit admissions were required.

Discussion

A prospective national audit of pediatric cLQTS reported serious complications in 3.3% of anesthetics; the upper 95% confidence interval reached 9.3% [11]. This case records five general anesthetics and four procedural sedations over four months in an 8-year-old boy with genetically confirmed LQT1. Management focused on metabolic and hemodynamic stability, attenuation of autonomic fluctuations, reduction of net drug-induced repolarization burden, and availability of rescue resources, including defibrillation, intravenous magnesium sulfate, and pediatric cardiology support. No Torsades de Pointes, sustained ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, treatment-requiring bradycardia, or cardiac arrest was documented.

Myocardial rhythm stability in cLQTS depends partly on metabolic and physiological homeostasis, because electrolyte, glucose, temperature, and ventilation abnormalities can increase early afterdepolarizations and transmural dispersion of repolarization [5, 18]. In this case, perioperative fluid management used individualized isotonic balanced solutions or normal saline-based regimens according to fasting duration, estimated blood loss, and clinical status. The rationale was to avoid iatrogenic hyponatremia and its attendant risk of cerebral edema, while maintaining stable electrolytes and volume status. Dextrose-containing supplementation was used only when clinically indicated to reduce fasting-related hypoglycemia.

Mitigating arrhythmic risk also depends on genotype-specific autonomic management. In LQT1, KCNQ1-related loss of IKs makes adrenergic surges an important trigger [3]. In this patient, chronic propranolol was continued, remifentanil was titrated to blunt responses to laryngoscopy, surgical incision, and neurosurgical manipulation, and temperature was controlled to reduce shivering-related sympathetic activation [3, 18]. Sugammadex was used instead of anticholinesterase-anticholinergic reversal combinations to avoid avoidable heart-rate variability. Though data on the impact of sugammadex on pediatric myocardial repolarization dynamics are lacking, adult QT-dispersion data and case-level experience support its cautious use when neuromuscular reversal is required [17]. Postoperative care extended the same principles through monitored electrocardiographic surveillance, multimodal analgesia, and resumption of propranolol and antiepileptic therapy when enteral intake allowed [4].

Drug selection required consideration of cumulative electrophysiological effects. Midazolam was used for anxiolysis because it has neutral ventricular repolarization kinetics [19], and chronic propranolol and antiepileptic therapies were maintained. Etomidate was selected for the first anesthetic to prioritize hemodynamic stability during early rhythm characterization. Later inductions used single-bolus propofol with opioid titration, reflecting pediatric data suggesting that isolated propofol boluses do not worsen baseline QTc or transmural dispersion of repolarization [13, 18]. Rocuronium was used for neuromuscular blockade, while succinylcholine was avoided because of autonomic effects, potassium release, and QTc prolongation [20]. Sevoflurane was kept at low-to-moderate concentrations and paired with remifentanil; this approach does not remove the repolarization burden of volatile agents, but may reduce sympathetic responses during surgical stimulation [15, 18]. Ondansetron and droperidol were avoided, and dexamethasone was used for antiemetic prophylaxis when needed [21, 22]. When reversal was required, sugammadex was chosen to avoid the chronotropic variability associated with neostigmine-anticholinergic combinations [23, 24].

Rescue preparation remained a practical component of care. A bedside defibrillator, intravenous magnesium sulfate, and a pathway for pediatric cardiology consultation were available during the encounters [11]. The team also had pre-calculated magnesium dosing (30–50 mg/kg bolus), external defibrillation protocols (2–4 J/kg), and access to pacing if clinically required. These measures do not prevent arrhythmia by themselves, but they reduce delay if TdP, ventricular fibrillation, severe bradycardia, or unstable rhythm deterioration occurs.

The non-intravenous sedation approach was used only for selected non-painful diagnostic examinations. For this child, separation anxiety, confinement, intravenous (IV) cannulation and MRI-related acoustic stimulation could provoke distress, and he was unable to communicate or cooperate with the requirement for physical immobility due to neurodevelopmental comorbidities. Intranasal dexmedetomidine with oral midazolam allowed sedation without initial IV placement in these specific diagnostic settings. Dexmedetomidine may induce bradycardia, necessitating careful monitoring, and is generally avoided in pause-dependent phenotypes such as LQT3, in which bradycardia is a known arrhythmogenic trigger [25]. In this LQT1 patient, no treatment-requiring bradycardia or arrhythmia was documented during the monitored sedation encounters. This experience should be viewed as a case-specific strategy, not as a broadly validated sedation protocol.

The main strength of this report is the within-patient description of nine multidisciplinary encounters, eliminating inter-individual genetic and physiological variability. Several inherent limitations bind its generalizability. As a single case report of a child with LQT1, extrapolation to LQT2 or LQT3 remains uncertain because these subtypes have distinct pathophysiological triggers. The encounters also occurred in a specialized pediatric tertiary hospital with immediate pediatric cardiology and electrophysiology support, which may not be available in all institutions. Although no malignant events were documented, the sample size is insufficient to exclude rare complications or determine comparative safety. Future multicenter registries could better define subtype-specific perioperative repolarization dynamics, continuous metabolic trends, and heart-rate variability under anesthesia.

Conclusion

This case describes one child with genetically confirmed LQT1 who underwent five general anesthetics and four procedural sedations over four months without documented malignant arrhythmia, treatment-requiring bradycardia, or adverse cardiac events. The management approach emphasized metabolic, hemodynamic, and autonomic stability together with immediate availability of rescue resources.
