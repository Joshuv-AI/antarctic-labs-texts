Towards closed-loop precision psychiatry: Integrating MRI biomarkers for individualized care of major depressive disorder
Authors: Qing-Lin Gao, Xiao Chen, Francisco Xavier Castellanos, Bin Lu, Chao-Gan Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMagnetic resonance imaging (MRI) biomarkers have shown considerable potential in elucidating the neurobiological underpinnings of major depressive disorder (MDD). However, clinical translation of these biomarkers remains limited due to reliance on group-level analyses, which fail to capture the individual variability inherent in MDD. Precision psychiatry, which advocates for individualized approaches, offers a framework that could enhance the clinical utility of MRI biomarkers across multiple domains, including diagnostic classification, treatment response prediction, and individualized interventions. Despite this potential, current research applying MRI biomarkers to MDD within the framework of precision psychiatry remains fragmented, lacking an integrated clinical system that seamlessly combines these components. This review introduces the concept of a closed-loop clinical system, emphasizing the integration of diagnostic classification, treatment response prediction, and individualized interventions into a unified approach at the individual patient level. We summarize recent advances in these three clinical domains, highlight existing fragmentation, and discuss the challenges of achieving a cohesive system. Finally, we propose that the integration of MRI biomarkers into a closed-loop clinical system, as envisioned by precision psychiatry, holds great promise for the individualized management of MDD, improving clinical outcomes from diagnosis through recovery.Keywords: magnetic resonance imaging, major depressive disorder, precision psychiatry, biomarkers, individual variability

Background

The diagnosis of major depressive disorder (MDD) predominantly relies on subjective methods, such as patient self-reports, caregiver observations, and clinician evaluations. While this symptom-based approach is clinically effective, it is constrained by a lack of neurobiological support, which affects both its objectivity and its potential for clinical success (Dunlop et al., 2019; Kim, 2019). To overcome this limitation, the identification of biomarkers that reflect underlying psychopathological processes has become a crucial focus of research in recent decades (García-Gutiérrez et al., 2020).

Biomarker measurement often involves advanced biomedical techniques, such as neuroimaging, which transform biological signals into quantifiable metrics. Among these technologies, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a pivotal tool for investigating biomarkers in patients with MDD, owing to its exceptional spatial accuracy and non-invasive nature (Abi-Dargham & Horga, 2016). MRI encompasses multiple modalities, each tailored to assess distinct aspects of brain structure and function. Structural MRI modalities, including structural T1-weighted imaging and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), are effective for visualizing brain anatomy and identifying structural abnormalities. Functional MRI (fMRI), encompassing both resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) and task-based fMRI (tb-fMRI), provides valuable insights into the physiological underpinnings of behavioral processes such as perception, decision-making, and emotion. These techniques are invaluable for detecting abnormal brain mechanisms associated with mental disorders, including MDD (Abi-Dargham et al., 2023).

Although MRI biomarkers for MDD hold considerable promise for clinical applications, their effectiveness is limited by the predominance of group-level analyses. While such studies can shed light on the general pathological mechanisms of MDD, they fail to account for individual variability, diminishing their applicability in personalized clinical decision-making (Singh & Rose, 2009; Kraus et al., 2023). This heterogeneity in MDD, characterized by diverse clinical presentations and underlying mechanisms, implies that patients may exhibit distinct brain abnormalities, respond differently to identical treatments, and experience divergent disease progression and outcomes. Failing to account for these individual differences impedes the attainment of more precise and clinically relevant biomarkers (Abi-Dargham et al., 2023; Kirkpatrick et al., 2021).

Precision psychiatry, which integrates biological and clinical data to tailor interventions to individual patients, has made significant strides in MDD research in recent years. By focusing on individual variability, precision psychiatry aims to enhance diagnosis, treatment prediction, and prevention (Borrione et al., 2020). The incorporation of big data and artificial intelligence has further advanced this field, enabling the extraction of meaningful information from large-scale brain imaging datasets and advancing the identification of individual-level biomarkers (Z. S. Chen et al., 2022). In this review, individualized biomarkers refer to those that guide clinical decisions for individual patients, such as diagnosing MDD, determining its subtype, or predicting the efficacy of specific therapies. These biomarkers offer quantitative assessments of abnormalities at the individual level, demonstrating superior performance compared to traditional group-level methods (Kraus et al., 2023).

This review summarizes recent advances in MRI technology within the context of precision psychiatry for MDD and offers recommendations for future research directions. Although it does not attempt to comprehensively review all relevant literature, it highlights a selection of promising studies. The first three sections explore empirical research employing MRI technology in precision psychiatry, focusing on diagnostic classification, treatment response prediction, and individualized interventions for MDD patients. The final section discusses strategies for integrating precision psychiatry into a cohesive closed-loop clinical system, facilitating individualized management from diagnosis through recovery (see Fig. 1). This integrated system would transcend the current “trial-and-error” model, ushering in an era of predictive, preventive, individualized, and participatory medicine, thereby enhancing the clinical translation of MDD treatments.

Research advances in diagnostic classification within precision psychiatry for patients with MDD

Diagnostic classification plays a crucial role in clinical decision-making by extracting essential diagnostic information from a broad range of clinical data. Traditional methods, which rely on clinical scales, are often limited by subjective biases, prompting a shift toward precision psychiatry (Buch & Liston, 2021). This paradigm shift moves away from a conventional “one-size-fits-all” diagnostic approach toward more refined subtype classifications and individualized treatment strategies, thereby offering new perspectives on personalized diagnostics (Arns et al., 2022). In this context, MRI has emerged as a pivotal tool, with studies broadly categorized into two areas: differential diagnosis (distinguishing MDD from healthy controls or other disorders) and subtype classification (identifying subgroups within MDD). Based on research showing that fMRI biomarker reliability improves with sample size (from 30 to 200), we set 200 as the threshold to differentiate between small and large sample sizes (X. Chen et al., 2018). Detailed information on sample sizes and relevant parameters is provided in Tables 1 and 2. In a 2012 consensus report on diagnostic imaging markers in psychiatry, the American Psychiatric Association research council proposed that clinical biomarkers should demonstrate at least 80% sensitivity and specificity to be considered suitable for clinical application (Botteron et al., 2012). However, it is crucial to emphasize that the reproducibility of these results should not be overlooked, as generalizability across different settings is vital for their clinical reliability, especially in studies with small sample sizes. Small sample sizes are particularly prone to inflated performance estimates, especially when complex models are employed, as these models tend to overfit relative to simpler approaches.

Large-scale studies on diagnostic classification

Structural MRI (sMRI) has emerged as a promising tool for identifying neuroanatomical subtypes in MDD. Large-scale data-driven studies utilizing machine learning (ML) algorithms have identified two to three distinct subtypes based on patterns of gray matter volume alterations (D. Chen et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2022). For example, the subtype and stage inference (SuStaIn) algorithm revealed three atrophy subtypes originating in the anterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus, and frontal/orbitofrontal cortex, respectively, each associated with unique clinical symptom profiles and gene expression patterns (D. Chen et al., 2023). Similarly, the heterogeneity through discriminative analysis (HYDRA) algorithm identified two distinct patient dimensions: one group exhibited relatively preserved gray and white matter with preserved volume reductions, while the other demonstrated extensive volume reductions (Fu et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2022). These findings provide evidence for the existence of biologically distinct MDD subtypes with potential clinical utility.

rs-fMRI studies have explored both differential diagnosis and subtype classification through innovative analytical methods. Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) achieved 81.5% accuracy in classifying MDD patients versus healthy controls (HC) (Qin et al., 2022), while an ensemble model integrating GCN, graph attention network (GAT), and GraphSAGE achieved an upsampling accuracy of 71.2% in distinguishing MDD from HC and 77.8% in differentiating first-episode drug-naïve MDD from recurrent MDD, suggesting potential effectiveness in diagnosing MDD and identifying its subtypes (Venkatapathy et al., 2023). Unsupervised learning techniques have also identified reproducible neurophysiological subtypes, such as two distinct functional connectivity (FC) patterns within the default mode network (Liang et al., 2020) and six biotypes with clinical relevance derived from hierarchical clustering of regional circuit scores (Tozzi et al., 2024b). Temporal-adaptive GCNs (TAGCNs) have demonstrated the ability to capture both static and dynamic FC patterns, yielding improved classification performance compared to several state-of-the-art methods (Yao et al., 2020). Additionally, unsupervised clustering of rs-fMRI data based on lifespan trajectories of FC strength has revealed two reproducible neurophysiological subtypes of MDD, which have been proposed as biomarkers for subtype classification (X. Sun et al., 2023). These approaches aim to capture the functional heterogeneity of MDD and offer potential biomarkers for individualized diagnostics, though their clinical applicability requires further validation.

Combined structural and functional MRI approaches present both opportunities and challenges for large-scale diagnostic classification. While some multimodal studies have reported promising results—such as 86.3% classification accuracy using sleep disorder-related imaging features (Shi et al., 2021), others have reported more modest performance (e.g., 48.1–62.0%) with significant distributional overlap between groups (Winter et al., 2023, 2022). This variability probably reflects methodological differences in feature selection, analysis pipelines, and sample characteristics across studies. Notably, the integration of relevance vector regression and XGBoost algorithms has been reported to show promise in enhancing predictive accuracy within multimodal frameworks (Shi et al., 2021).

In summary, large-scale neuroimaging studies in MDD increasingly leverage ML and deep learning techniques to explore neuroanatomical and functional heterogeneity. sMRI studies have identified subtypes linked to gray matter atrophy, while fMRI studies show varying accuracy in differentiating MDD from HC or comorbid psychiatric disorders. However, the clinical utility of these biomarkers is limited by significant variability in classification performance and overlapping neurobiological features across diagnostic groups. Methodological diversity, including unsupervised clustering and hybrid imaging approaches, further complicates the standardization of diagnostic criteria. These challenges highlight the complexity of MDD neurobiology and emphasize the need for harmonized protocols and biologically grounded subtype definitions to improve diagnostic consistency and clinical applicability.

Diagnostic classification based on small samples

Small-sample studies in sMRI have employed advanced analytical techniques for diagnostic classification. The multiple kernel learning (MKL) approach effectively differentiated patients with MDD from those with bipolar disorder (BD), revealing significant differences in gray matter volume and white matter microstructure with 73.6% accuracy (Vai et al., 2020). Models integrating explainable artificial intelligence techniques, such as layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP) algorithms, have identified features of brain texture abnormalities that successfully classified patients with recent-onset depression against HC with accuracy exceeding 72% (Korda et al., 2024). The level of extracted features may also affect predictive performance; for example, features derived from high-order minimum spanning tree (MST) brain networks resulted in a 20% improvement in classification performance between patients with MDD and HC, compared to the low-order MST-MBN (Y. Li et al., 2023).

fMRI studies in small samples have highlighted dynamic FC features as robust biomarkers. Methods based on high-order FC and support vector machines (SVM) applied to dynamic resting-state FC (rsFC) have distinguished bipolar depression from unipolar depression, achieving an accuracy of 70.4% (H. Zhang et al., 2022). Furthermore, tb-fMRI has been investigated for identifying biotypes of treatment-resistant depression (TRD) based on baseline FC between the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) during cognitive tasks. TRD patients with impaired connectivity (cognitive biotype +) were distinguished from those with intact connectivity (cognitive biotype −), providing new insights that correlated with cognitive control performance and validating the biotype classification (Tozzi et al., 2024a).

The integration of multiple modalities in small samples has also utilized advanced analytical techniques for diagnostic classification. Studies combining sMRI, DTI, and fMRI data differentiated MDD from HC (77.4% accuracy), BD from HC (63.3% accuracy), and MDD from BD (79.5% accuracy) using SVM and recursive feature elimination in leave-site-out cross-validations (Y. Liu et al., 2022). A multi-connectivity representation learning network (MCRLN) was proposed to enhance MDD diagnosis by integrating structural, static, and dynamic FC, demonstrating superior performance on clinical datasets (Kong et al., 2023). Gaussian process classifier (GPC) analysis based on thalamic gray matter density achieved an accuracy of 96.6% while functional indicators such as the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations were less effective (H. Li et al., 2021). Deep neural networks (DNNs) using predefined brain regions reported 72.8% classification accuracy (Z. Liu et al., 2022).

In summary, small-sample studies have employed innovative strategies, combining multimodal imaging and advanced analytical techniques for diagnostic classification. However, most studies lack validation using truly independent datasets, and small sample sizes, when combined with complex models, are susceptible to overfitting. While these methodological advancements represent efforts to extract meaningful information from constrained datasets and offer new insights into diagnostic classification, the accuracies reported must be interpreted with caution. Moreover, integration of these technologies into clinical practice remains an ongoing challenge, requiring standardized methodologies to ensure reproducibility and clinical applicability. Future work should balance methodological innovation with robust validation and biological coherence to enhance clinical relevance.

Research progress in treatment response prediction in precision psychiatry for patients with MDD

Treatment response prediction is pivotal in clinical decision-making for MDD; however, predicting the most effective treatments and objectively assessing outcomes remains a significant challenge. Accurate prediction of therapy effectiveness prior to treatment or early detection of efficacy changes during treatment could enable timely adjustments, minimizing recovery delays and improving clinical outcomes. Growing efforts are being made by studies utilizing large multi-center datasets and advanced algorithms to enhance treatment response prediction accuracy. This section reviews large- and small-sample studies (large: ≥200 patients, small: <200 patients) in treatment response prediction, categorizing findings by intervention method (pharmacotherapy, neuromodulation therapy, and multi-therapy combinations). Large-scale studies and advanced algorithms for predicting antidepressant and multi-therapy efficacy

Antidepressant treatment response prediction research has been advanced by large-scale neuroimaging studies. Brain activation patterns elicited during emotional conflict tasks demonstrated a significant correlation (r = 0.49, P < 0.001) between predicted and observed changes in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) scores following sertraline treatment. Relevance vector machines (RVM) suggested that emotional conflict regulation provides more precise predictions compared to traditional clinical variables (Fonzo et al., 2019). Additionally, individualized FC has been explored as a promising predictive approach. One study using network-restricted strength predictive modeling (NRS-PM) derived a pretreatment connectome fingerprint, achieving a predictive correlation of r = 0.19 (P = 0.03) for symptom improvement (Fan et al., 2020). Another study combining connectome predictive modeling (CPM) with lasso regression reached prediction accuracies of R² = 0.22 for the sertraline group and R² = 0.31 for the placebo group (K. Zhao et al., 2023). The semi-supervised ML clustering algorithm HYDRA was used to enhance predictive capabilities, improving the accuracy of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) response prediction by identifying distinct neuroanatomical subtypes. A significant SSRI treatment response of 51.1% was observed in Subtype 1, compared to 46.9% in Subtype 2 (Fu et al., 2024).

Multi-therapy prediction models have explored the potential for individualized treatment selection. A model using rsFC data identified three patient subgroups with distinct treatment preferences, achieving accuracies of 83.1% for SSRIs, 68.7% for stimulation therapies (rTMS/ECT), and 66.7% for selective serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) (X. Wang et al., 2022). A meta-analysis of 22 studies using ML analysis of MRI data achieved an overall area under the curve (AUC) of 0.84, with 77% sensitivity and 79% specificity, and found no significant difference in classification performance between electroconvulsive therapy and medication outcomes or MRI modalities (Cohen et al., 2021). These findings demonstrate how neuroimaging biomarkers might inform treatment selection across therapeutic modalities, though cross-dataset validation remains challenging.

The integration of large-scale neuroimaging data and advanced ML techniques has contributed to advances in predicting treatment responses for patients with MDD. These models are developed to predict the effectiveness of various therapies by analyzing complex brain patterns. Although these approaches report promising predictive accuracy in specific studies, their clinical implementation is hindered by challenges such as ensuring robust cross-dataset validation and addressing the variability of treatment responses across individuals. Standardized models are needed to enhance the reliability of predictions and facilitate their application in clinical practice.

Small-sample studies using ML algorithms to predict antidepressant and neuromodulation therapy efficacy

Small-sample antidepressant prediction studies have begun to explore the potential of ML models in predicting the efficacy of antidepressants. An ensemble learning model combining rs-fMRI and genetic data predicted early-stage treatment response with a reported accuracy of 86%, significantly outperforming FC-only models (61% accuracy) (Pei et al., 2020). SVM models reported 79.4% accuracy for escitalopram monotherapy response based on rsFC features (Tian et al., 2020), while individualized rsFC showed a reported significant correlation (r = 0.345, P < 0.001) with dysphoric symptom severity and correlated with improvement following a 2-week antidepressant treatment (Y. Zhao et al., 2023). DTI-based ensemble learning predicted early SSRI/SNRI response with a reported accuracy of 75% at primary sites and 70% at out-of-sample sites (Xue et al., 2021). Radiomics analysis of multiscale sMRI achieved a reported accuracy of 88.2% in predicting early improvement in adolescents, exceeding traditional imaging markers (74.8%) (Ma et al., 2023).

Studies have also begun investigating the prediction of neuromodulation therapy responses in small sample sizes. Integration of rs-fMRI with SVM yielded reported predictive accuracies of 85–95% for rTMS response based on blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal power and FC features (Cash et al., 2019; Hopman et al., 2021). CPM predicted approximately 30% of the variance in improvement in the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) score for TRD based on rTMS-induced connectivity changes (Ge et al., 2022), while baseline abnormal signal propagation patterns in the ACC predicted the efficacy of Stanford Accelerated Intelligent Neuromodulation Therapy (SAINT) (Mitra et al., 2023). For ECT prediction, CPM analysis of rsFC reached a reported remission prediction accuracy of 76.2% (H. Sun et al., 2020); the use of sMRI data to predict individual responses to ECT yielded a classification accuracy of 69% (Gärtner et al., 2021).

Small-sample studies have demonstrated the potential of ML models to predict treatment responses in patients with MDD, particularly for antidepressant and neuromodulation therapies. However, the current evidence base is characterized by significant limitations, most notably the small sample sizes employed and the corresponding heightened risk of model overfitting and inflated performance estimates. Importantly, the generalizability of these models beyond the specific cohorts and methodologies used in their development remains largely unproven. To improve clinical applicability, future research should focus on validating these models in larger, more diverse patient populations, with an emphasis on ensuring robust cross-validation techniques.

Research progress in individualized intervention for patients with MDD

Patients with MDD exhibit significant individual differences, encompassing not only neurobiological variations but also key demographic and clinical characteristics such as age, sex, and illness duration. This heterogeneity suggests that “one-size-fits-all” treatments are often less effective. Addressing these individual differences by implementing targeted interventions can significantly improve treatment efficacy. Current research on individualized interventions for MDD, particularly involving MRI, has predominantly focused on neuromodulation therapies, particularly TMS, for treating TRD. This body of research can be divided into two main areas: (i) MRI-guided individualized localization of TMS targets and (ii) the stability of targets identified through this localization method, along with the underlying mechanisms. MRI-guided individualized TMS target localization

Studies focusing on target localization have frequently employed rs-fMRI to derive individualized target coordinates for therapeutic interventions. The selection of brain regions for defining these targets varies across research, with particular emphasis on the FC between the DLPFC and the subgenual ACC (sgACC) (Cash et al., 2021a). Evidence suggests that individualized target selection anchored in this connectivity metric significantly enhances treatment outcomes for both TRD and MDD, with a study reporting response rate exceeding 90% after 5 days of open-label treatment for individualized localization techniques using the SAINT protocol (Cole et al., 2020). The SAINT protocol demonstrated substantial symptom alleviation through the application of individualized rs-fMRI-guided targeting, resulting in an average symptom reduction of 52.5% after a 4-week intervention period (Cole et al., 2022). Moreover, a correlation between enhanced FC between the sgACC and DLPFC in MDD patients and symptomatic improvement following TMS further substantiates the merits of individualized targeting methodologies (X. Chen et al., 2025). Collectively, these findings indicate that FC-guided target localization markedly improves TMS efficacy compared to conventional localization methods.

Several studies underscore the potential of using individualized targets based on FC among diverse brain regions to further optimize treatment outcomes for rTMS. Techniques such as individualized resting-state network mapping (RSNM) and FC analyses centered on the DLPFC's interconnections with the DMN, sgACC, and right anterior insula (rAI) have demonstrated considerable effectiveness (Mielacher et al., 2020; M Moreno-Ortega et al., 2020; Siddiqi et al., 2023, 2021). These innovative approaches demonstrate promising advantages relative to traditional localization methods, particularly in the context of individualized treatments for TRD (Morriss et al., 2024; Pszczolkowski et al., 2022).

In addition to the above FC-guided techniques, novel methodologies for accurately identifying stimulation targets are emerging. Some studies have employed functional brain network controllability (fBNC) alongside optimal control analyses to ascertain the most effective stimulation targets tailored to individual patients, thereby enhancing the therapeutic impact of individualized TMS while concurrently minimizing required control energy (Fang et al., 2022). Moreover, ML techniques, such as the “Hollow-tree Super” (HoTS) method, have facilitated the creation of individualized brain atlases that identify FC features linked to symptom relief in MDD patients, substantially improving the efficacy of rTMS interventions (Taylor et al., 2023). Subtype-guided target localization has also exhibited significant promise, with symptom improvement rates of 90% and 70.73% observed among specific MDD subtypes following targeted stimulation of the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and occipital cortex, respectively (Xiao et al., 2024). Additionally, this body of research highlights the imperative to distinguish between distinct brain regions correlating with various symptom clusters, thereby refining individualized therapeutic strategies and advancing clinical interventions for MDD patients (Siddiqi et al., 2020).

The use of tb-fMRI or DTI has introduced new perspectives for personalizing rTMS target localization. One notable study employed tb-fMRI to activate the visual cortex (VC), utilizing BOLD signals to ascertain individualized rTMS targets, which resulted in significant enhancements in depressive symptomatology (Z. Zhang et al., 2021). Another study used DTI data to determine optimal TMS coil placement for non-invasive stimulation of deep brain regions, such as Brodmann area 25 (BA25). This study established a linear relationship between TMS intensity and BOLD signal strength, further validating the activation potential of structural connections (Luber et al., 2022). Collectively, these methodologies, which merge advanced imaging techniques with individualized target selection, have markedly augmented the efficacy of rTMS in treating MDD, emphasizing the extensive potential of tailored therapeutic interventions.

Recent advancements in MRI-guided TMS target localization have shown that individual differences in brain connectivity play a crucial role in optimizing treatment efficacy. FC-based target localization represents a key method for personalizing TMS therapy for patients with TRD. The application of individualized targeting techniques has led to improved outcomes, suggesting that a tailored approach to TMS is more effective than standard protocols. Ongoing research is focused on refining these individualized methods to enhance their scalability and clinical adoption.

Reliability and mechanisms of MRI-guided TMS target localization

In the realm of MRI-guided individualized TMS, key challenges include target stability and technical optimization. Extending rs-fMRI scanning time to 28 min or employing clustering methods can significantly enhance the stability of individualized targets, reducing intra-individual variability to within 1 cm and thereby improving treatment efficacy (Cao et al., 2023). Alternatively, even relatively short 10-min fMRI scans can produce effective individualized targets for the DLPFC (J. Sun et al., 2022). Longitudinal studies have confirmed that the accuracy of individualized target localization remains high even 1 year after initial treatment, underscoring the sensitivity and reliability of these methods in clinical settings (Cash et al., 2021b). Additionally, innovative strategies such as E-field modeling combined with graph-based algorithms, like “Targeted Functional Network Stimulation” (TANS), have outperformed traditional approaches by more precisely targeting specific functional networks, further validating the promise of individualized TMS for treating patients with MDD (Lynch et al., 2022).

Compared to conventional TMS methods, MRI-guided individualized TMS more effectively accounts for variations in individual anatomical structures and FC, enabling precise localization of brain regions associated with symptom improvement. This approach enhances the precision of target identification by considering personalized brain networks and structural variations, resulting in enhanced treatment efficacy. MRI-based methods facilitate more accurate and reliable target localization, which significantly contributes to optimizing TMS treatment for patients with MDD (Cash et al., 2021b; Yuan et al., 2023).

Studies on individualized TMS targeting have shown promising results in improving treatment efficacy for patients with MDD. These studies typically focus on identifying brain networks related to treatment response, utilizing both structural and functional MRI to guide target selection. Although these studies have explored the neurobiological mechanisms of brain connectivity, they have not considered the impact of demographic and clinical covariates such as age, sex, and illness duration on biomarkers and treatment response. The exclusion of these factors from the analysis overlooks their significant role in individual variation, which is essential for personalized treatment. Future research should integrate these patient-specific variables for a more comprehensive understanding. Besides, the small sample sizes limit the generalizability of their findings. To translate these approaches into routine clinical practice, larger studies involving diverse populations are necessary to validate the real-world effectiveness of MRI-guided TMS. Moreover, further advancements in data analysis techniques and target identification methods are crucial for optimizing treatment outcomes and ensuring more precise clinical interventions.

Integration of diagnostic classification, treatment response prediction, and individualized intervention: Toward closed-loop clinical systems

The previous sections of this review explored the potential of MRI-based biomarkers within the framework of precision psychiatry for patients with MDD, highlighting recent advances in diagnostic classification, treatment response prediction, and individualized interventions. While each module has established a foundation for clinical application, its independent development and investigation have failed to yield an effective closed-loop system, resulting in limited interconnectivity among them. Although diagnostic classification provides the neurobiological underpinnings for treatment response prediction, and such prediction informs individualized interventions, the absence of a unified framework for cross-domain collaboration substantially impedes the realization of their synergistic potential.

Accordingly, the development of an effective closed-loop clinical system is essential. Such a system should integrate diagnostic classification, treatment response prediction, and individualized interventions into a dynamic, interdependent network. In this integrated framework, patient data are gathered through demographic information, clinical, psychological scales, and MRI biomarkers initially, providing a baseline for the system. The diagnostic classification process includes differential diagnosis (e.g. distinguishing between HC and MDD) and subtype classification, which aids in predicting the most suitable treatments and their efficacy for different patient subtypes. Following this, treatment response prediction is performed, forecasting the therapeutic response over time, which helps in selecting the most appropriate treatment, determining its duration, and anticipating patient outcomes. The system culminates in individualized interventions, where therapy is tailored to the patient's unique needs based on the predicted response. Post-treatment assessments then refine the diagnosis by evaluating treatment efficacy, ensuring continuous adjustment and optimization of care. This feedback loop supports personalized and adaptive treatment strategies, improving patient outcomes over time (see Fig. 1). Despite the promising benefits of this approach, achieving this integration faces several challenges. The following sections detail these core challenges and outline prospective research directions aimed at achieving this integration (see Table 5).

Core barriers to developing a closed-loop clinical system

Fragmentation of biological mechanisms in closed-loop clinical systems

A principal barrier to closed-loop clinical systems is the fragmentation of neurobiological mechanism explanations across diagnostic classification, treatment response prediction, and individualized interventions. This fragmentation prevents the establishment of a comprehensive closed-loop causal chain linking subtype, prediction, and intervention. For instance, sMRI studies have identified a subtype characterized by preserved gray and white matter volumes in specific regions relative to HCs who respond better to SSRI treatment (Fu et al., 2024). However, this biomarker has not been incorporated into treatment selection criteria, highlighting the disconnect between diagnosis findings and treatment selection.

Bridging this cross-domain causal gap requires the development of a longitudinal cross-module validation framework. Emerging work in multimodal data integration laid foundational steps toward such a framework. For example, baseline rs-fMRI data and individualized FC have been utilized to simultaneously predict treatment outcomes and categorize patients into subgroups with preferential response to either sertraline or placebo. In subsequent clinical treatment, the "sertraline-preferred" and "placebo-preferred" groups demonstrated significantly higher remission rates compared to the non-preferred group (sertraline: 68.8% vs. 5.6%, placebo: 72.2% vs. 2.3%, P < 0.0001) (K. Zhao et al., 2023). In another study, remission depression (RD) and non-remission depression (NRD) groups, based on escitalopram response, were distinguished by significantly higher rsFC between the sgACC and fronto-parietal network regions, including the bilateral DLPFC and bilateral inferior parietal lobule (IPL), compared to the NRD group. The rsFC between the sgACC and FPN is a valuable predictor of antidepressant response in MDD patients, with the predictive voxels primarily located within the FPN (Y. Wang et al., 2024).

Further integration has been explored by combining prediction with intervention through real-time monitoring. For instance, a single concurrent rTMS-fMRI session was used to assess acute rTMS-induced FC changes, with CPM applied to predict clinical outcomes after a 4-week rTMS treatment. The results suggest that acute rTMS-induced connectivity changes in TRD may reflect macro-level neuroplasticity, which is associated with interindividual variability in rTMS treatment response (Ge et al., 2020). Additionally, individualized E-field maps have been applied to explore variability in TMS target FC across subjects, revealing significant inter-individual differences that current group-average FC fail to account for. Harita et al. (2022) demonstrate initial success in constructing partial integration chains by linking subtype classifications with efficacy prediction and targeted intervention.

Nevertheless, a comprehensive mechanistic understanding of the causal relationship between brain activity changes and clinical responses remains elusive. Although many studies have successfully predicted responses to various treatments, the causal link between subtypes and treatment responses has not been clearly elucidated. Most existing research relies on data modeling and prediction but does not directly explore the causal mechanisms between brain activity changes and clinical efficacy. This limitation obstructs the development of a continuous optimization pathway from diagnosis to intervention through a closed-loop clinical system.

Fragmented methodological pipelines: Inconsistent data practices impede cross-domain Integration

Across diagnostic classification, treatment response prediction, and individualized intervention research in MDD, inconsistencies in scanning protocols and methodological pipelines present another substantial obstacle to cross-domain integration. Despite the use of the same biomarkers across multiple fields, there is a lack of standardized definitions, collection methods, and analytical procedures, which leads to challenges in cross-domain data reuse. This impedes the effective connection and collaborative development between different modules (Abi-Dargham et al., 2023; Kirkpatrick et al., 2021). To achieve effective closed-loop integration, it is essential to address the issues of standardization and cross-disciplinary consistency to ensure seamless communication of research findings across modules (X. Chen et al., 2022; Wanner et al., 2025).

First, the heterogeneity in biomarker definition and measurement significantly impedes translational consistency. For instance, while biomarkers in diagnostic studies are typically used to identify subtypes of MDD, variations in types of imaging equipment, data acquisition protocols, or the brain regions selected can result in substantial inconsistencies (Hannon & Bijsterbosch, 2024; Pilmeyer et al., 2022). Meanwhile, biomarkers used for treatment response prediction or individualized intervention are not directly comparable, limiting the generalizability of predictive models and intervention strategies (Bayer et al., 2022; Monsour et al., 2020). This variability underscores the urgent need for standardization, as only by addressing these issues can research results from different modules be effectively integrated (Kirkpatrick et al., 2021).

Second, data processing workflows are often inconsistent across studies, with variability in data preprocessing techniques (e.g. denoising, normalization, smoothing) and statistical analysis (e.g. statistical threshold setting, choice of regression analysis methods) (Jahanshad et al., 2025; Y.-W. Wang et al., 2023). These discrepancies directly lead to incomparable research results, affecting the reliability of models and the sharing of data across modules (X. Chen et al., 2022; Koshiyama et al., 2022).

To address these challenges, it is essential to establish unified data standards spanning all aspects, from data collection, preprocessing, and analysis (X. Chen et al., 2022; Y.-W. Wang et al., 2023; Wanner et al., 2025). Establishing cross-disciplinary methodological standards will not only ensure data comparability but also promote synergistic module integration, accelerating progress toward precision psychiatry (Bayer et al., 2022; Koshiyama et al., 2022). Multi-center alliances and interdisciplinary collaboration will be critical to developing and disseminating these standardized frameworks (X. Chen et al., 2022; Koshiyama et al., 2022; Wanner et al., 2025).

Scalability of individualized approaches

In the field of precision psychiatry, individualized approaches are undoubtedly central to future development. However, despite the vast potential of individualized treatment, its current implementation faces significant challenges related to scalability. Individualized methods require the tailoring of every aspect, from diagnostic classification and treatment response prediction to intervention strategies, based on the unique neurobiological characteristics of each patient. However, issues such as low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), high data heterogeneity, and the high cost of technology severely limit the widespread clinical adoption of individualized treatment plans.

First, individualized treatment necessitates a precise assessment of the patient's neurobiological features, followed by the development of tailored clinical strategies. Although recent research on neuroimaging biomarkers has provided abundant data on individual differences, the SNR of these data is generally low, which hampers the effective scaling of individualized treatment across large populations (Kraus et al., 2023). Subtle neuroimaging biomarkers at the individual level are easily masked by technical noise or population heterogeneity, making it difficult for models to maintain stability across larger, more heterogeneous clinical samples (Fraessle et al., ).

Second, individualized treatment faces dual challenges in terms of technology and cost in clinical practice. Technologically, obtaining high-quality individual brain network data requires extended imaging scan times. For example, a study showed that a single rs-fMRI scan must last 28 min to achieve the brain network stability necessary to be used for targeting TMS treatment, a duration that is difficult to manage in routine clinical settings (Cao et al., 2023). From a data processing perspective, individualized algorithms require complex and innovative computational workflows to quantify individual variation (Harita et al., 2022; M. Moreno-Ortega et al., 2019). For instance, individual-specific functional network maps have been generated using multi-echo fMRI data, incorporating InfoMap algorithms and E-field modeling to ensure exceptional reproducibility and reliability across various states and temporal points (Lynch et al., 2022). Even with high-precision imaging data, specialized teams are needed to eliminate the impact of individual physiological noise and anatomical differences through complex algorithms (Viessmann & Polimeni, 2021). This heavy reliance on advanced equipment and interdisciplinary technologies significantly restricts the implementation of individualized treatment in resource-limited healthcare settings.

To overcome the scalability challenges of individualized treatment, multiple issues related to data quality, cost, and technical complexity must be addressed. Enhancing the SNR of data, optimizing individualized treatment algorithms, and promoting broader technological dissemination will be key to future development.

Static biomarkers for dynamic disease trajectories

The diversity of clinical symptoms and the continuous changes in brain neural activity in patients with MDD result in fluctuating disease progression. However, previous studies still rely primarily on biomarkers obtained from single-point assessments, such as a single brain scan. This leads to medical decisions that are often misaligned with the real-time fluctuations in the patient's condition and the adaptive changes in neural networks during treatment.

In terms of diagnostic classification, although rs-fMRI FC analysis at a single time point can categorize patients with MDD into different subtypes (X. Wang et al., 2022; Y. Zhao et al., 2023), this method only reflects the brain features at the time of the scan and cannot monitor the real-time changes during treatment. Notably, dynamic changes of FC have been shown to directly affect the response to magnetic stimulation therapies (Ge et al., 2020). Treatment response prediction models exhibit clear limitations. For example, studies have demonstrated that brain connectivity changes following short-term use of antidepressants, such as citalopram, can predict long-term efficacy (Harita et al., 2022; Kloebl et al., 2020). However, most models still rely solely on baseline data collected before treatment, overlooking the impact of brain network dynamic adjustments induced by treatment on prognosis (An et al., 2024). Regarding intervention implementation, rTMS is often targeted at fixed landmarks, such as the DLPFC. However, treatment itself alters the connectivity between the target brain regions and other networks, such as the DMN, through neuroplasticity mechanisms (M. Moreno-Ortega et al., 2019). Due to the lack of real-time monitoring of these dynamic effects and the absence of parameter optimization, treatment effects often diminish as the number of sessions increases.

The technological challenges stem primarily from insufficient data integration capabilities. Diagnostic studies often adopt a single-time-point design and fail to track the evolution of brain features at different stages of the disease (An et al., 2024). Treatment response prediction models rarely incorporate key indicators from the treatment process, such as state-specific changes in brain network connectivity (Javaheripour et al., 2023). While individualized intervention technologies can localize individualized targets based on a patient's baseline brain network features, they are unable to dynamically adjust treatment plans as the disease progresses.

To overcome the challenges posed by static and dynamic mismatches, the field needs to develop biomarkers capable of tracking disease progression over time. By addressing core issues such as technical standardization and algorithm adaptability, these dynamic indicators can be systematically integrated into a closed-loop clinical system.

Future directions: Building closed-loop clinical systems

Cross-domain causal unification of biomarkers

Building a cross-domain causal inference framework faces significant challenges in achieving closed-loop clinical systems. Although traditional biomarkers and neuroimaging data provide insights into brain function and structure, they predominantly reflect correlations between brain regions rather than exploring causal relationships (Friston, 2011). To address this limitation, recent studies have attempted to integrate multimodal biomarker information through causal inference frameworks. In particular, the application of neurostimulation techniques in patients with MDD, such as rTMS, which rely on the principles of neuroplasticity to induce brain function remodeling, aims to establish causal chains and predict treatment responses by selectively modulating the activity of specific brain regions (Hobot et al., 2021). The core hypothesis of this framework is that functional abnormalities in specific brain regions are closely related to depressive symptoms, and by modulating these brain regions, symptoms can be alleviated. Causal inference then verifies these changes, thereby facilitating the development of more precise and individualized treatment strategies (Frandsen et al., 2024; Siddiqi et al., 2020).

However, despite some preliminary clinical successes with neurostimulation technologies, several challenges remain in causal inference. First, traditional FC analysis, relying on Pearson's correlation coefficients, fails to capture signal propagation, limiting causal accuracy (Mitra et al., 2023). Granger causality (GC) and effective connectivity (EC) models offer more accurate causal inference by quantifying neural interactions; GC measures the predictability of neural time series and the direction of information flow, while EC estimates causal direction and strength, with studies showing their complementary roles (Allegra et al., 2024). For example, GC analysis has been used to personalize iTBS target selection, improving treatment outcomes (Morriss et al., 2024). Dynamic causal modeling (DCM), coupled with generative models, has been employed to predict clinical trajectories in patients, with research showing that DCM-derived effective connectivity can effectively distinguish between different clinical trajectories in depression (Frässle et al., 2020). While DCMs are valuable for deciphering causal interactions among neuronal populations in fMRI, their utility is often constrained by computational limitations, typically analyzing a small subset of brain areas. To overcome this, a novel hybrid approach, the transformer encoder DCM decoder (TREND), was proposed, which combines a transformer encoder with physiological DCM to improve scalability and preserve nonlinearities, demonstrating superior accuracy and speed even in large-scale networks (Nag & Uludag, 2024). These advancements underscore the transformative potential of directed connectivity approaches for precision psychiatry.

At the same time, the underlying brain mechanisms modulated by neurostimulation techniques remain incompletely understood. Recent studies have combined FC data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) with individualized E-field maps to create more accurate brain region modulation maps. These individualized FC patterns better reflect the regulatory relationships between brain regions during neurostimulation, offering new perspectives for further elucidating the causal mechanisms of neurostimulation (Harita et al., 2022; Lynch et al., 2022). These studies suggest that causal inference frameworks can help identify and validate specific brain network patterns in depression patients, allowing for the design of individualized treatment plans.

In the future, it will be essential to develop a cross-domain causal inference framework driven by neurostimulation, enabling a closed-loop clinical system of diagnostic classification, treatment response prediction, and individualized interventions. This neurostimulation-driven causal inference framework not only reveals the neurological mechanisms underlying MDD but also holds significant clinical translational value for the implementation of closed-loop clinical systems. However, the application of causal inference still faces considerable challenges. Current cross-disciplinary studies often focus on short-term efficacy validation, lacking long-term longitudinal data to support the sustained validity of causal relationships. To overcome this issue, future research should emphasize longitudinal validation of causal inference models and integrate more clinical data and biomarkers to further enhance the precision and reliability of causal inference.

Interoperable standards through multi-center consortia

To promote the integration of closed-loop clinical systems, the establishment of interoperable standards is essential. Given the inherent complexity and heterogeneity of MDD, multi-center alliances across institutions and regions offer an effective solution. Through collaboration, different research teams can share data, exchange experiences, and collectively establish unified data standards and analytical methods. Such initiatives not only enhance the reliability of MDD research but also facilitate the global implementation of treatment protocols (Lu et al., 2024).

A key challenge in establishing multi-center alliances lies in addressing the heterogeneity of data across institutions. In neuroimaging research, although techniques such as fMRI are widely used in MDD-related brain network research, variations in equipment, scanning parameters, and data processing methods across research centers can impact data comparability (Yan et al., 2019). Consequently, the development of standardized scanning protocols and data preprocessing procedures is critical to the success of multi-center alliances. For example, the Depression Imaging Research Consortium (DIRECT) is trying to improve cross-center data comparability by establishing unified fMRI scanning standards and data processing workflows, facilitating the systematic analysis of brain structural and functional changes related to MDD (X. Chen et al., 2022). The DIRECT's Phase 2 project integrated large-scale data from 1660 patients with MDD and 1341 HC, revealing the stable neurobiological marker of sgACC-DLPFC FC across groups. By establishing a group template to guide individualized TMS target localization, treatment efficacy significantly surpassed that of traditional group-targeted approaches, improving precision treatment (X. Chen et al., 2025).

Moreover, the success of multi-center alliances also depends on the establishment of unified data storage and sharing platforms, ensuring seamless data integration among different research teams. Data sharing accelerates the validation of new theories and facilitates the development and optimization of individualized treatment plans. For instance, DIRECT has partnered with multiple research institutions to create an open, shared brain imaging database, providing high-quality data resources for international research. The DIRECT project has integrated fMRI data from 17 hospitals and 25 research teams to build the world's largest publicly accessible depression brain imaging database (http://rfmri.org/REST-meta-MDD) (X. Chen et al., 2022, 2025). Based on this, the consortium further promoted the second phase of DIRECT research, expanding the sample size and achieving standardized surface analysis (using DPABISurf), extending the data to more patients and controls. These global collaborative alliances provide valuable insights for the future of precision psychiatry in MDD. Notably, normative frameworks based on shared databases can quantify an individual's deviation by comparing brain function with baseline data from healthy populations, significantly improving the sensitivity of abnormal detection and the precision of individualized interventions (Bethlehem et al., 2022).

Furthermore, multi-center alliances can collaborate on prospective studies to validate the clinical applications of cross-domain biomarkers. Large-scale longitudinal studies offer new perspectives, such as the stable network features predictive of psychopathological dimensions identified in adolescent tracking datasets (Cui et al., 2022), and stable associations of cross-diagnostic neuropathological factors (Xie et al., 2022). Long-term tracking of structural imaging data has also revealed biologically stable biomarkers, such as brain region morphology similarity indices (e.g., Morphometric INverse Divergence, MIND) and volume change parameters (e.g. Jacobian determinants, JDs), which provide a biological foundation for predicting clinical outcomes (Holz et al., 2023; Sebenius et al., 2023).

Thus, by establishing interoperable standards through multi-center alliances, not only can data heterogeneity be addressed, but the global application of precision psychiatry in MDD can also be advanced. In the future, with technological progress and deeper interdisciplinary collaborations, multi-center alliances will play an increasingly important role in advancing precision psychiatry research and practice.

Effective and feasible individualized approaches

To achieve closed-loop clinical systems, effective and feasible individualized approaches must be explored. This requires breakthroughs in two key areas: improving the quality of individual brain imaging data and developing more precise methods for quantifying individual differences. Recent advances in both areas have provided new directions for clinical applications.

In terms of enhancing data quality, novel MRI technologies have significantly improved signal detection capabilities. Multi-echo fMRI, which collects brain activity signals from multiple time points, effectively reduces random noise interference. For instance, traditional single-echo scans require 30 min to obtain reliable data, while multi-echo technology achieves the same accuracy in just 10 min (Lynch et al., 2020). This technique enhances the reliability of FC measurements even with shorter scan times. Such improvements are especially valuable in clinically significant brain regions where traditional single-echo fMRI methods often face limitations. However, multi-echo fMRI introduces challenges, including the complexity of the acquisition process and the necessity for advanced signal processing algorithms to optimize results. These complexities may hinder its widespread adoption in routine clinical settings.

Another important advance is ultra-high-field MRI (UHF-MRI) (≥7T), which, with submillimeter resolution, can clearly reveal the fine structure of brain networks (Viessmann & Polimeni, 2021). The superior SNR and spatial resolution enhance test–retest reliability and facilitate better longitudinal tracking, allowing for more precise detection of subject-specific FC and activation patterns compared to 3T (Calabro et al., 2025). For example, 7T equipment can detect subtle changes in brain FC after treatment, effectively revealing individual differences in brain function even in small-scale studies (Neuner et al., 2022; Roalf et al., 2024). Advanced sequences like MP2RAGE further stabilize biomarkers by generating uniform images with reduced bias from partial volume effects (Calabro et al., 2025). However, challenges such as heightened sensitivity to motion artifacts and field inhomogeneities can undermine reproducibility if left uncorrected (Calabro et al., 2025). Additionally, physiological noise and variations in preprocessing pipelines introduce further variability in fMRI data, compromising the stability of biomarkers. To maximize UHF-MRI's potential, specialized preprocessing pipelines focusing on distortion correction, motion correction, and SAR management are essential (Calabro et al., 2025).

In terms of quantifying individual differences, researchers have developed various innovative methods. Individualized scoring systems identify abnormalities by comparing a patient's brain features to those of HC. For example, the generalized additive model for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) constructs normative references for non-linear growth trajectories of gray matter volume or the whole-brain functional connectome, quantifying the degree of deviation in patients and thereby improving clinical relevance (Bethlehem et al., 2022; L. Sun et al., 2023). The mean correlation distance (MCD) method detects individualized abnormal patterns in FC through mixed statistical models (Tan et al., 2024). Some studies have also integrated rs-fMRI and tb-fMRI data to further improve the precision of individualized scoring, ensuring statistical rigor and consistency by comparing FC maps (Tozzi et al., 2024b).

In the area of innovative statistical modeling, the common orthogonal basis extraction (COBE) algorithm decomposes FC matrices into shared and unique components, highlighting the individual connectivity features closely related to changes in depressive symptoms (K. Zhao et al., 2023). This method allows researchers to capture brain activity patterns directly associated with the patient's pathological state, enhancing the sensitivity and accuracy of individualized analyses. Similarly, the NRS-PM method generates individualized brain connectivity fingerprints by assessing the connection strength between nodes, predicting symptom improvement following treatment, and accurately identifying unique brain activity patterns associated with MDD (Fan et al., 2020).

Customized brain mapping methods are used to construct individualized brain system maps, tailoring them based on population-level functional maps to reflect a patient's unique brain functional organization. These “functional patches” have been found to be significantly correlated with depressive symptoms, behavior, and cognitive deficits (Sacchet et al., 2024). This approach enables researchers to identify the functional networks most relevant to the patient's symptoms and pathological features, offering more targeted intervention strategies for individualized treatment. Additionally, an iterative parcellation strategy was employed to derive individualized functional regions for each participant, with this individualized approach outperforming conventional group-based methods by optimizing the cortical connectivity matrix based on each participant's FC data (Lebois et al., 2021). Furthermore, recent studies have attempted to establish a multidimensional assessment system that considers brain structure, function, and clinical indicators to evaluate individual treatment responses (Fu et al., 2023).

Dynamic monitoring-guided adaptive interventions

Incorporating temporal dynamics into clinical research is essential for achieving closed-loop clinical systems. Due to significant individual differences in symptom trajectories and treatment responses, traditional static methods based on single assessments struggle to capture the dynamic nature of the disorder. By applying three strategies—longitudinal tracking, real-time monitoring, and dynamic brain network analysis—it is possible to gain a deeper understanding of the dynamic biological mechanisms underlying MDD.

Longitudinal studies provide key insights into the long-term progression of MDD. For example, the GEParD (Genetics and Epigenetics of Pharmaco- and Psychotherapy in acute and recurrent Depression) protocol combined sMRI, serum proteomics, and multimodal biomarkers to track dynamic changes in brain structure, FC, and inflammatory biomarkers through long-term patient cohort tracking during antidepressant treatment (Lichter et al., 2023). Advanced computational models, such as graph-theoretical predictive models (GPMs), have been employed to predict (hypo)manic symptoms and anhedonia over time, demonstrating the model's capacity to forecast symptom changes and performance (Pizzagalli et al., 2023). However, these studies face challenges, including sample attrition and data bias, which can be mitigated by multi-center standardized protocols and collaborative learning techniques to improve data reliability.

Breakthroughs in real-time monitoring technologies bring clinical research closer to real-world conditions. For example, concurrent TMS-fMRI trials can dynamically capture the acute and transient alterations in FC during treatment (Ge et al., 2022). Individualized real-time fMRI neurofeedback (rtfMRI NF) enables patients to self-regulate the connectivity between the insula and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, thereby influencing neurobiological mechanisms (Maywald et al., 2022). The innovative use of wearable devices further expands monitoring capabilities, with ecological momentary assessment (EMA) via smartphones enabling the recording of daily mood fluctuations. EMA facilitates real-time, personalized tracking of neurobehavioral changes, offering precise and dynamic assessments of symptom progression and treatment responses at the individual level (Lenze et al., 2020). Additionally, magnetoencephalography (MEG), when combined with ML algorithms, uncovers dynamic pathological mechanisms in psychiatric disorders by providing high spatiotemporal resolution, thereby enabling the detection of rapid neural reactivations essential for understanding complex cognitive processes and their disruptions in these conditions (McFadyen & Dolan, 2023). Together, these technologies create an early warning system, offering precise time windows for timely intervention.

Dynamic brain network analysis methods reveal spatiotemporal patterns that traditional static studies fail to capture. Module allegiance (MA) analysis has demonstrated that the stability of the ACC across different brain networks is strongly associated with the efficacy of escitalopram treatment; patients with lower MA values show a diminished response to escitalopram (Tian et al., 2020). A novel approach integrates functional brain network controllability and optimal control analysis, leading to personalized rTMS protocols, effectively guiding the depressive resting brain state towards a healthy resting brain state, significantly reducing the required control energy, and enhancing model fitting accuracy, thereby outperforming standard rTMS treatment (Fang et al., 2022). Additionally, a spatially dynamic rs-fMRI approach with a functional chronnectome model identified time-varying abnormalities in brain networks that were not detectable by static functional connectivity methods, revealing a dynamic imbalance between the limbic, FPN, and DMN networks in adolescence-onset MDD patients, which is linked to depression severity and symptom variability (Marchitelli et al., 2022).

By embedding the temporal dimension into the entire diagnostic and treatment process, MDD treatment will transition from fixed protocols to strategies that are precisely tailored to individual needs. This dynamic monitoring-based healthcare system not only improves treatment accuracy but also optimizes interventions based on real-time physiological and psychological states, paving the way for new directions in tackling the complex nature of MDD.

Conclusion

This review highlights recent advances in MRI studies within the framework of precision psychiatry, emphasizing their critical role in diagnostic classification, treatment response prediction, and the development of individualized interventions for patients with MDD. Drawing on the latest research, it proposes future directions for integrating these advances into cohesive closed-loop clinical systems. Key challenges to achieving such closed-loop clinical systems were identified, and strategies were outlined that present promising solutions to address these challenges effectively. The ultimate translational goal is the establishment of closed-loop clinical systems, which would facilitate individualized management from diagnosis through recovery. Such systems would overcome the limitations of current “trial-and-error” models and usher in a new era of predictive, preventive, individualized, and participatory medicine.
