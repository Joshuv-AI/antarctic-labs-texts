Prevalence of undernutrition and associated factors among children aged 6–59 months in rural model households in the Central Zone of Sidama regional state, Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study using robust Poisson regression analysis
Authors: Amenu Asfaw Gujo, Hiwot Hailu Amare, Mahlet Birhane Estifanos, Mekonnen Birhanie Aregu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUndernutrition remains a public health challenge in Ethiopia, despite several nutrition programmes. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the prevalence of undernutrition and its associated factors among children aged 6–59 months in rural model households in the Central Zone of the Sidama regional state.DesignThis study employed a community-based cross-sectional design between 4 July and 4 August 2024.SettingThe study was conducted in selected woredas of the Central Zone of the Sidama regional state.ParticipantsA multi-stage sampling technique was used to select 627 children aged 6–59 months in the model households. Model households, defined as those that attended at least 75% of the 96-hour training sessions and successfully implemented 75% of the Health Extension Program (HEP) packages, were awarded certificates.Primary outcomeThe primary outcomes of this study were undernutrition, specifically stunting, underweight and wasting.Secondary outcomeThe secondary outcome focused on factors associated with stunting, underweight and wasting. Bivariable and multivariable robust Poisson regression analyses were used to identify the associations between outcome and risk factors. The strength and significance of associations were assessed using adjusted prevalence ratios (APRs) with 95% CIs and p<0.05.ResultsAlthough multilevel analysis was performed to account for woreda and kebele clustering, no significant effects were found. Simple robust Poisson regression findings are therefore presented. Stunting prevalence was 37.6%, underweight 13.9% and wasting 9.4%. Being male is linked to a higher prevalence of stunting (APR 1.37, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.69), underweight (APR 1.72, 95% CI 1.12 to 2.65) and wasting (APR 1.73, 95% CI 1.003 to 2.98). Conversely, spoon-feeding (APR 0.61, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.95), mothers as housewives (APR 0.56, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.76) versus farmers and maternal household decision-making (APR 0.29, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.70) were associated with lower prevalence of stunting. Additionally, larger households (≥5 members) were also associated with higher prevalence of underweight (APR 1.61, 95% CI 1.04 to 2.52) and wasting (APR 1.88, 95% CI 1.09 to 3.24).ConclusionsOur findings indicate a higher prevalence of stunting, accompanied by moderate rates of wasting, among children residing in rural model households in the Sidama region. Notably, practices such as spoon-feeding and maternal involvement in decision-making were associated with a lower prevalence of stunting. Conversely, larger family size was linked with a higher prevalence of underweight and wasting among children. To address these challenges, it is essential to promote hygienic feeding practices via the HEP, empower women in their communities, advocate for family planning to manage family size and strengthen community-based nutrition programmes. These measures could help combat undernutrition in rural areas.

Introduction

Undernutrition is a significant contributor to poor health among children worldwide. According to a 2022 report by the WHO, an estimated 149 million children under the age of five experienced stunting, while 45 million were classified as wasted.1 Undernutrition encompasses various conditions, including wasting (low weight-for-height), stunting (low height-for-age) and underweight (low weight-for-age).1 Alarmingly, reports from UNICEF, WHO and the World Bank indicate that in 2023, two out of five stunted children, or 43%, resided in Africa.2 Evidence from 31 sub-Saharan African countries shows a prevalence of 26% for stunting, 21% for underweight and 6% for wasting among children aged under five.3 In East African countries, stunting rates vary significantly, ranging from 22% to 53%.4

In Ethiopia specifically, the 2019 mini Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) reported that 37% of children under five were stunted, 21% were underweight and 7% were wasted.5 The burden of undernutrition is particularly severe among children living in rural areas.5 In Southern Ethiopia, the rates for stunting, underweight and wasting were reported at 36.4%, 20.3% and 6.3%, respectively. A 2023 report from the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) indicated that the prevalence of stunting among children under five in the Sidama region—where our study is conducted—was 35%, while the rate of wasting was 14%.6 Various factors contribute to undernutrition, including parental education and occupation, child sex and age, maternal age, breastfeeding status, family size, household income and food insecurity.7 8

Despite ongoing efforts in Ethiopia to implement nutrition programmes aimed at reducing nutritional problems, undernutrition continues to pose a public health challenge.9 Since its establishment in 2003, the Health Extension Program (HEP) has aimed to provide primary healthcare services at the community level.10 This programme, which initially included 16 intervention packages, now comprises 18 packages designed to equip Health Extension Workers (HEWs) with the knowledge and skills necessary to promote health within households.11 By focusing on health promotion at the household level in rural areas, the HEP seeks to reduce child undernutrition.11 12 The programme has made remarkable progress across four primary pillars: disease prevention and control (covering HIV/AIDS, TB, malaria and first aid); family health (encompassing maternal and child health, family planning, immunisation, nutrition and adolescent and reproductive health); hygiene and sanitation (including comprehensive waste management, water safety, a healthy home environment, insect and rodent control and food and personal hygiene); and health education and communication.12 It has played a vital role in expanding essential health services to all Ethiopians, with a special focus on improving the health of women and children. Reports show that the programme has significantly reduced under-five mortality rates.11 However, undernutrition remains a critical factor contributing to child mortality. Understanding the importance of nutrition, the programme includes it as a core component, ensuring that all its interventions address child health issues, including undernutrition, both directly and indirectly. Within the framework of the HEP, families in rural areas are classified as model or non-model households.13 This recognition is granted after HEWs conduct comprehensive training sessions that last a total of 96 hours. Households that attend at least 75% of these sessions and successfully implement 75% of the HEP packages are designated as model households. Consequently, there are high expectations for a reduced prevalence of undernutrition among families recognised as model households.

The Sidama region, where our study is situated, is predominantly rural, with the majority of its population engaged in subsistence agriculture, primarily through smallholder farming. Coffee serves as the principal cash crop, while Enset (Ensete ventricosum), often referred to as false banana, is a vital staple root crop that provides essential food security for the rural communities in the area. Despite the region’s fertile land and significant agricultural potential, it grapples with persistent food insecurity, high rates of child undernutrition and limited access to healthcare services. Reports indicate that nutrition-related issues among children are prevalent, with alarming rates of undernutrition.14 15 However, to our knowledge, few studies have specifically assessed the prevalence of undernutrition and its associated factors among children residing in rural model households. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the prevalence of undernutrition and its associated factors among children aged 6–59 months in rural model households in the Central Zone of the Sidama regional state.

Methods

Study setting and period

The study was conducted from 4 July to 4 August 2024, in selected woredas of the Central Zone of the Sidama regional state. This zone comprises six woredas and one administrative town, with Yirgalem serving as its capital. Yirgalem is located 42 kilometers from Hawassa, the capital city of the Sidama region and 315 kilometers south of Addis Ababa. The Central Zone is equipped with four hospitals, including two general hospitals and two primary hospitals.

The study focused on specific rural kebeles within three woredas: Dale, Wonsho and Loka Abaya. Dale woreda contains 34 kebeles (32 rural and two urban), with a total of 34 health posts and 10 health centres. The total population of Dale woreda was estimated to be 254 652, representing approximately 53 768 households, and with 59 203 under-five children. Wonsho woreda consists of 19 kebeles (17 rural and 2 urban), supported by 17 health posts and five health centres. The total population of Wonsho woreda was estimated to be 134 730, representing approximately 22 904 households, and with 22 488 under-five children. Loka Abaya woreda has 27 kebeles (26 rural and one urban) and is served by 26 health posts. During the survey period, the total population of Loka Abaya woreda was 124 711, representing approximately 26 704 households, and with 19 108 under-five children.

Study design and population

This study employed a community-based cross-sectional design. The source population comprised all children under the age of five living in rural model households within the Central Zone of the Sidama region. The study population consisted of randomly selected under-five children from these rural model households in designated woredas of the Central Zone. Both the children and their mothers or caregivers were included in the study.

Eligibility criteria

All children aged 6–59 months who had resided in the selected rural model households for at least 6 months during the data collection period were included in the study. While we intended to exclude physically disabled children and those with generalised oedema due to the challenges associated with taking anthropometric measurements, no such cases were identified during data collection. Similarly, we planned to exclude mothers or caregivers with serious illnesses that would prevent them from responding, but again, no cases were found.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size for this study was determined using the single population proportion formula. For the primary objective, we based our calculations on the following assumptions: a stunting prevalence of 44.7% among model households in northwest Ethiopia,13 a 95% CI and a 5% margin of error. Additionally, we accounted for a 10% non-response rate, resulting in a calculated sample size of 418. Given that we employed a multi-stage sampling technique, we multiplied this figure by a design effect of 1.5, yielding a final sample size of 627.

For the secondary objectives, we calculated the sample size based on the proportions of factors associated with undernutrition using EPI Info software. Specifically, we considered the proportion of stunting among children using unimproved water sources, which was 44%, compared with 24% for those using improved water sources.13 With a 95% CI and 80% power, the calculated sample size for this factor was 194. Additionally, we assessed the proportion of stunting by sex, finding rates of 32% in males and 18% in females,16 which resulted in a required sample size of 326 with the same confidence and power parameters. However, since the sample size calculated for the primary objective was larger than that for the secondary objectives, we opted to use the largest sample size of 627 for this study.

We employed a multi-stage sampling approach to select 50% of the woredas in the Central zone of Sidama, resulting in three woredas: Dale (32 rural kebeles), Wonesho (17 rural kebeles) and Loka Abaya (26 rural kebeles). These woredas were chosen through a simple random sampling method. From the total of 75 rural kebeles across these woredas, we further conducted a simple random sampling to select 12 kebeles: five from Dale (Bera Chala, Wecho, Magara, Wara and Hida), three from Wonesho (Fero, Bokaso and Hunikute) and four from Loka Abaya (Argeda, Feleka, Chanecho and Gorbesala). The total population of children aged 6–59 months across these kebeles was 12 106, with the sample size distributed proportionally based on this population. In the chosen kebeles, model households with children aged 6–59 months were identified using family folder records. Finally, a simple random sampling method was employed to select children from each kebele based on the identified lists in these family folders. In households with multiple eligible children (6–59 months), one was selected via lottery method (see figure 1).

Study variable

Dependent variable: Undernutrition (stunting, wasting and underweight).

Independent variables: In this study, we investigated child and parent individual-level factors, alongside household-level factors. Child and parent individual-level factors: included child sex, child age, exclusive breastfeeding status, method of complementary feeding, duration of daily feeding, vaccination status, mother’s or caregiver’s age, mother’s marital status, maternal and paternal education, maternal and paternal occupation, antenatal care visits, place of delivery and postnatal care visits for the child in this study. Household-level factors: encompassed head of household characteristics, family size, number of under-five children in the household, maternal participation in household decision-making, household food insecurity and household wealth.

Operational definition

Model households, defined as those that attended at least 75% of the 96 hours of sessions and successfully implemented 75% of the Ethiopian HEP packages, were awarded certificates.17 Undernutrition encompasses three key conditions: stunting, underweight and wasting. Stunting is characterised by a height-for-age Z-score that falls below −2 SD. Underweight refers to a weight-for-age Z-score below −2 SD. Wasting is defined as a weight-for-height Z-score below −2 SD.18

Household food insecurity was assessed over the past 30 days or 4 weeks recall period using nine questions from the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS).19 20 The questions included: (Q1) worrying about food, (Q2) unable to eat preferred foods, (Q3) eating a limited variety of foods, (Q4) eating unwanted foods, (Q5) eating small meals, (Q6) eating fewer meals in a day, (Q7) having no food of any kind in the household, (Q8) going to sleep at night hungry and (Q9) going a whole day and night without eating anything. Responses were scored as 0 for ‘No’ and 1 for ‘Yes’. The frequency of each item was coded as rarely (1), sometimes (2) or often (3). Households with no affirmative responses or only a ‘No’ for all but Q1 were considered food secure. Mild food insecurity was indicated by a score of 2 or 3 for Q1, or 1, 2 or 3 for Q2, or 1 for Q3 or Q4, with no responses for Q5–Q9. Moderate food insecurity included scores of 2 or 3 for Q3 or Q4, or 1 or 2 for Q5 or Q6, with 0 for Q7–Q9. Severe food insecurity was defined by a score of 3 for Q5 or Q6, or 1, 2 or 3 for Q7–Q9.

Data collection tools and procedure

Data were collected via KoboToolbox mobile application using interviewer-administered semi-structured questionnaire that was adapted after reviewing articles.51922 The questions contain socio-demographic related characteristics, maternal healthcare practices during pregnancy and lactation of a child in this study, child characteristics, household environmental health related conditions and HFIAS questions. Mothers responded to the HFIAS questionnaire on behalf of all household members. Anthropometric measurements, children’s body weight and length or height were measured using the standard procedure.23 For every child, weight, length or height was measured. Children aged 6–23 months were measured for length while in recumbent position on a board with a moveable headpiece and a wooden base. Children aged 24–59 months were measured for height in standing position to the nearest 0.1 cm. The weight of children under 2 years of age or who still cannot stand was weighed with the mother holding them. In this case, we primarily measured the weight of the mother and then measured the mother and baby together with baby and then taken the baby’s weight by subtracting the both weight with the mother. Weight was measured in kilograms to the nearest 0.1 kg using a digital weight scale which was checked against a standard weight for its accuracy on a daily basis. Calibration of the instrument against zero reading was made following each measurement of every child. Children were measured with light clothing and without shoes. Nine data collectors participated in the study. 3 days’ training was given to the supervisors and data collectors before collecting the data.

Data quality control

The data collectors received training in order to assure the quality of the data. Pre-test was done on 5% of the sample size in one of the kebeles that was not part of the study prior to data collection. The questionnaire was translated into local languages, Sidaamu Afoo, for the fieldwork and back translated to English to check its consistency. Data collectors received extensive training on measurements of weight and height, on probing techniques and local events in order to reduce recall bias. Every day, measurements and instrument validation were carried out. The collected data’s completeness was checked. The variables were coded and cleaned.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were coded, cleaned and exported to SPSS V.23 and Stata V.17 for analysis. Descriptive analysis was used to describe the percentages and number of distributions of the respondents by socio-demographic characteristics and other relevant variables in the study. The household wealth index was calculated using principal component analysis (PCA) based on 12 household assets variables. Variables were recoded into a binary format—‘0’ indicating absence and ‘1’ indicating presence. Assumptions for PCA were verified, and internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin sampling adequacy values. The first principal component, explaining the most variance, was used to construct the wealth index. Based on this analysis, we categorised wealth status into three groups: poor, medium and rich. To calculate the household food insecurity, the answers for frequency of occurrence for the nine HFIAS questions were summed with a range of 0–27. The food insecurity was categorised as mild, moderate and severe food insecurity. Additionally, we also classified households as food secure and food insecure.

Z-scores were obtained by converting nutritional data indices from anthropometric measurements using WHO Anthro software to calculate height-for-age, weight-for-age and weight-for-height. Descriptive statistics were calculated to describe relevant variables with frequency, percentage, mean and SD. The presence of multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor, and values below 10 were considered in multivariable analysis. We employed a robust Poisson regression analysis to identify factors linked to stunting, underweight and wasting. This choice was driven by several key reasons: the model directly estimates prevalence ratios, making it easier to interpret associations. Moreover, since the prevalence of stunting and underweight exceeded 10%, using this model helps avoid the overestimation of ORs that can occur with logistic regression.24 25 This approach is particularly beneficial for interpreting binary outcomes in cross-sectional studies. We initially applied a multilevel analysis to account for potential clustering effects at the woreda and kebele levels. However, as no significant clustering was detected at either level, we proceeded with reporting findings from a simple robust Poisson regression analysis without considering the random effects. While using robust Poisson regression analysis, the model’s fit was assessed using the deviance and Pearson statistics. Models for stunting, underweight and wasting demonstrated excellent goodness-of-fit. Both the deviance and Pearson χ2 statistics consistently yielded p values of 1.000 across all outcomes.

The independent variables that demonstrate a relationship with the outcome at p<0.25 in binary robust Poisson regression model are included in the multivariable robust Poisson analysis. However, to account for potential confounders, we retained some variables with p≥0.25 in our analysis. In the stunting model, we included the following variables with p≥0.25: child age, breastfeeding status, maternal education, paternal occupation, marital status, antenatal care visits for the child in this study, head of household, family size, food insecurity and wealth status. Therefore, in the stunting model, we included child sex, age, breastfeeding status, method of complementary feeding, duration of daily feeding, head of households, maternal and paternal education, maternal and paternal occupation, mother’s marital status, antenatal care visits, place of delivery, number of under-five children in the household, family size, maternal participation in household decision-making, household food insecurity and household wealth.

In the underweight model, we included child sex, age, breastfeeding status, method of complementary feeding, duration of daily feeding, child vaccination status, head of households, paternal education, maternal and paternal occupation, mother’s marital status, antenatal care visits, place of delivery, number of under-five children in the household, family size, maternal participation in household decision-making, household food insecurity and household wealth.

In the wasting model, we included child sex, age, breastfeeding status, duration of daily feeding, child vaccination status, head of households, paternal education and occupation, mother’s marital status, antenatal care visits, place of delivery, postnatal care visits, number of under-five children in the household, family size, maternal participation in household decision-making, household food insecurity and household wealth.

In the multivariable robust Poisson regression model, p<0.05 was taken into consideration to declare the statistical significance. We reported both crude prevalence ratio and adjusted prevalence ratio (APR). The APR with 95% CI was estimated to determine the strength of the association.

Patient and public involvement

In this study, we only analysed survey data, no patients or public involvement in this study.

Variations and risk factors associated with stunting, underweight and wasting

To account for potential clustering within woredas and kebeles, we conducted a multilevel analysis. The null (intercept-only) models revealed negligible clustering across all outcomes. Low intra-cluster correlation coefficients (ICCs) indicated that the random variability at the kebele and woreda levels was not statistically significant, suggesting that most variance occurred at the individual level. In the stunting model, the ICC was 1.29×10⁻¹⁵ at the kebele level and 2.85×10⁻³⁶ at the woreda level. For the underweight model, the ICC was 0.01 at both the kebele and woreda levels. Similarly, in the wasting model, the ICC was 0.002 at both levels. Given that the random effects were not statistically significant across any of our outcome models—stunting, underweight or wasting—we proceeded with reporting findings from a simple robust Poisson regression analysis.

On the other hand, in the underweight and wasting model, crude and APRs for some variables differed, indicating potential confounding. These variables included child age, breastfeeding status, daily feeding duration, child vaccination status, head of household, mother’s marital status, antenatal care visits, place of delivery, maternal participation in household decision-making, household food insecurity and wealth status. While subgroup analyses were performed to investigate potential interactions, none were statistically significant.

Factors associated with stunting among children

In bivariable robust Poisson regression analysis, sex of child, method of complementary feeding practices, duration of feeding practices in a day and maternal occupation were associated with stunting in children. In multivariable robust Poisson regression analysis, several factors significantly associated with stunting. The prevalence of stunting was 1.37 times higher among male children (APR 1.37, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.69). In contrast, children who were spoon-fed had 31% lower prevalence of stunting (APR 0.61, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.95) compared with those bottle-fed. Similarly, children whose mothers were housewives exhibited 44% lower prevalence of stunting (APR 0.56, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.76) compared with those with farmer mothers. Additionally, the prevalence of stunting was 71% lower in children whose mothers participated in household decision-making (APR 0.29, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.70) compared with those with fathers as sole decision-makers (table 4). Factors associated with underweight among under-five children

In bivariable robust Poisson regression analysis, sex of child, child age, breastfeeding status, method of complementary feeding practices, duration of feeding practices in a day, child vaccination status, head of household, maternal occupation, current marital status of mother, antenatal care visits and place of delivery for the child under this study, number of under-five children in the household, family size, maternal participation in household decision-making, household food insecurity and wealth status were associated with underweight in children. In the multivariable robust Poisson regression analysis, the prevalence of underweight was 72% higher among male children compared with female (APR 1.72, 95% CI 1.12 to 2.65). Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of undernutrition and its associated factors among children under 5 years old in rural model households in the Central Zone of Sidama regional state, Ethiopia. In this study, we employed multilevel analysis to account for clustering effects at both the woreda and kebele levels. However, our findings revealed no significant variations. As a result, we opted to present the simple robust Poisson regression results without incorporating random effects. Our findings revealed a high prevalence of undernutrition among children in this region, with stunting rates particularly alarming and wasting classified as moderate—both of which represent significant public health challenges according to WHO guidelines.26 We identified several factors linked to stunting, underweight and wasting. Notably, being male, the method of feeding (specifically using a spoon), maternal occupation and the involvement of mothers in household decision-making were significantly associated with stunting. Additionally, we observed that the prevalence of underweight and stunting was notably higher among male children and those living in households with large family size.

The prevalence of stunting identified in this study was 37.6%, which aligns with the 2023 findings from the EPHI that reported a stunting rate of 35% in Sidama.6 However, this figure is significantly higher than those reported in previous studies. For instance, previous studies indicated stunting rates of 14.7% in Sidama,27 25% in Southern Ethiopia28 and 26.6% in Kenya.29 This difference may be attributed to the fact that many of these earlier studies were conducted in urban settings, which differ significantly from our study’s context. Conversely, our findings also revealed a lower prevalence of stunting—37.6%—compared with the pooled rates from a recent systematic review on Ethiopia, which reported a rate of 40%,30 44.7% and 49.4% reports from northwest Ethiopia,13 31 while the EDHS analysis from 2000 to 2019 indicated a rate of 48%.32 Other countries studies reported stunting rates of 41.5% in Kenya and 41% in Uganda.29 33 These discrepancies could stem from differences in the time periods studied, the specific settings of the research and varying socioeconomic conditions across the regions.

Our finding of a 14% prevalence of underweight is consistent with other studies conducted in Southern Ethiopia34 and among model households in Northwest Ethiopia.13 However, it is higher than the 4.4% and 9% prevalence reported in other studies from the same region.27 35 In contrast, our prevalence rate is notably lower than those recorded in Sidama, where studies have reported rates of 20.5%36 and the Mini EDHS 2019, which indicated a prevalence of 21%.5 While South Sudan reported a significantly higher prevalence rate of underweight 28.7%37 and in low- and middle-income African countries, it was noted at 17.6%.38 The observed discrepancies in prevalence rates may be attributed to variations in study settings, sample sizes, socio-economic conditions and the time periods during which the studies were conducted.

The prevalence of wasting in our study was 9.4%, which is consistent with findings from other areas in Southern Ethiopia, where rates were reported at 9% and 10%,35 39 as well as among model households in Northwest Ethiopia, which reported a rate of 10%.13 However, our finding is lower than the 14.7% prevalence reported in another area of Southern Ethiopia.34 Conversely, our results are slightly higher than the wasting prevalence reported in the mini EDHS for both Ethiopia and Sub-Saharan Africa, which indicated rates of 7%.5 40 The differences in study findings may be attributed to variations in study settings, periods and sample sizes.

Several studies have demonstrated that boys are more susceptible to undernutrition than girls.41 In this study, boys had a significantly higher prevalence of stunting, underweight and wasting. This increased vulnerability is often attributed to biological factors, as males are generally more susceptible to infectious diseases due to differences in their immune and endocrine systems.42 In addition to biological factors, cultural caregiving practices play a critical role. While many rural Ethiopian settings traditionally favour male children, specific cultural contexts exist where females receive preferential household attention, resulting in more favourable nutritional outcomes compared with their male counterparts. Moreover, older female children often spend more time within the domestic sphere, providing them with incidental access to food during preparation. Conversely, male children tend to spend more time playing outdoors, which increases their risk of exposure to various environmental pathogens and infections.

Notably, children who were fed with a spoon had a lower prevalence of stunting compared with those who were fed with a bottle. Previous studies have also indicated that children who were fed complementary foods with a bottle are at a higher risk of stunting than those who were fed with a spoon.43 This association may be linked to hygiene issues, as bottle-feeding can increase the risk of gastrointestinal infections, which in turn can lead to diarrhoea. This makes children more susceptible to undernutrition.

Several studies have indicated a link between maternal occupation and child stunting.4446 Children whose mothers are housewives were less likely to experience higher prevalence of stunting compared with those whose mothers work as farmers. This difference may be attributed to the fact that housewife mothers often spend more time with their children, allowing them to provide adequate nutrition and care.

Previous studies have established a link between undernutrition and maternal involvement in household decision-making.4752 Our finding indicates that children whose mothers actively participate in household decisions have lower prevalence of stunting compared with those whose fathers are the sole decision-makers. This finding suggests that when women are empowered to make decisions, they are more likely to prioritise their own health and that of their children. This empowerment can facilitate access to healthcare and resources, ultimately improving child nutrition and overall well-being. However, it is important to interpret this finding with caution, as the number of children whose mothers participated in household decision-making was few in this study.

Large family size is a significant risk factor associated with poverty. This study, consistent with previous findings,39 53 54 found that children living in households with five or more family members are more likely to experience higher prevalence of underweight and wasting. This may be due to the fact that larger households often struggle to provide sufficient food and adequate healthcare for their children during times of illness.

Our study focused on children living in rural households which are recognised as model families, by implementing the HEP packages. We employed multilevel analysis to explore potential clustering effects at both the woreda and kebele levels. However, since we did not observe significant clustering effects, we opted to report simple robust Poisson regression results instead. It is important to note that this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to establish causal relationships between independent and outcome variables, as it only captures data at a single point in time. This limitation prevents us from determining whether the outcome variables precede the independent variables. While we evaluated potential confounding by comparing crude and APRs, it is important to acknowledge that there may still be unknown or unmeasured confounding factors that could influence the findings of this study. The association between maternal decision-making and stunting should be interpreted with caution. First, the small sample size of mothers involved in decision-making lacks broad representativeness. Second, reverse causality cannot be ruled out—healthier children may simply enable mothers to take a more active role in the household. Additionally, the data collected to assess food insecurity levels may have been influenced by social desirability bias. Respondents might have over-reported severe food insecurity in an attempt to qualify for support, such as food aid, or under-reported it due to cultural stigma surrounding food scarcity. Furthermore, the breastfeeding status and duration of feeding in a day could also be affected by recall bias and social desirability bias. Although our study was conducted in rural communities with children from households recognised as model families, the findings may not be generalisable to all children across Ethiopia. Nonetheless, these results may accurately represent children in similar rural areas.

The findings of this study, which focuses on children living in rural model households, have significant policy implications. The recognition of these model households is facilitated through the implementation of various health extension packages delivered by HEWs. Given their proximity to communities, HEWs play a crucial role in supporting families to reduce undernutrition in Ethiopia. Despite notable improvements in under-five mortality rates, many children continue to suffer from the effects of undernutrition. Ethiopia has launched several programmes aimed at combating childhood malnutrition, yet children remain vulnerable to stunting, underweight and wasting, as highlighted in multiple studies.55 Despite the region’s fertile agricultural land, coffee and livestock production, chronic child undernutrition remains a stagnant crisis, with prevalence rates showing little improvement over several decades. Contrary to expectations that child undernutrition would decline in households designated as model families, this study found that the prevalence of undernutrition remains high and comparable to the national average. This concerning finding highlights an urgent need to re-evaluate and update the existing intervention programmes provided by HEWs. Additionally, it is essential to reassess the criteria used to categorise rural families as model or non-model households in order to more effectively address child health issues through the programme, including undernutrition.

Our finding indicates that being male increases the risk of undernutrition. While the biological factors contributing to this susceptibility cannot be altered, it is possible to mitigate the burden of undernutrition linked to poor cultural practices and caregiving through targeted education. For instance, practices such as feeding children with a spoon, maternal employment status, the involvement of mothers in household decision-making and family size have all been associated with undernutrition. Promoting hygienic feeding practices in rural households is essential through HEP intervention packages; our study found that children who were fed with a spoon had lower prevalence of stunting. Furthermore, maternal employment plays a vital role in child nutrition and overall health. In rural areas, increased maternal engagement with children can enhance their nutritional status, making it imperative to advocate for this involvement. Additionally, maternal participation in household decision-making is associated with better healthcare-seeking behaviour for sick children, further contributing to the reduction of undernutrition. While the HEP four pillars—disease prevention, family health, hygiene and health education—have significantly improved rural health, our findings among children in model families highlight a critical gap. To meet national targets and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), we must re-evaluate how these interventions are delivered and how model status is verified to ensure they translate into better childhood health outcomes. Collectively, these efforts align with both national and global objectives to decrease nutrition-related morbidity and mortality in childhood, ultimately supporting the success of SDGs.
