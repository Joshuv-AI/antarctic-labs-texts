Dynamics and correlation of flavor compounds and bacterial communities in mustard (Brassica juncea) and wild jujube (Ziziphus spinosa) co-fermentation
Authors: Dan Li, Xiaoli Ma, Pengxue Ren, Jiayang Zhao, Aiguo Luo, Shengli Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMustard and wild jujube are highly distinctive agricultural products, with mustard being widely used in northern China for preparing pickled vegetables. However, the potential of their co-fermentation to develop novel food products with enhanced flavor and quality remains underexplored. This study aimed to investigate the correlation between physicochemical properties, volatile compounds, and the structural dynamics and interactions of microbial communities during the mixed fermentation of mustard and wild jujube powder. The fermentation process was monitored for changes in pH, total titratable acidity, and nitrite content. Volatile compounds were analyzed to identify key flavor components, while high-throughput sequencing was employed to track microbial community succession. Correlation analysis was subsequently used to examine associations between environmental factors, bacterial dynamics, and flavor formation. The results indicated that mixed-fermented mustard exhibited a faster pH decline, increased total titratable acidity, and lower nitrite content during fermentation. Flavor analysis revealed that co-fermentation reduced the sulfur compound content by 28%. Thirteen key volatile compounds were identified throughout fermentation. At the phylum level, the dominant microbial community shifted from Proteobacteria in the early stages to Firmicutes in later stages. At the genus level, the abundance of Stenotrophomonas increased, while Pseudomonas and Micrococcus decreased. Correlation analysis indicated that pH, total acidity, nitrite, and Pseudomonas were associated with bacterial community structure and the formation of multiple volatile compounds. This study provides novel insights into the flavor and microbial characteristics of mixed-fermented mustard and wild jujube products, offering a scientific basis for developing fermented vegetable products with enhanced flavor profiles.Keywords: Dynamic evolution, Mix fermentation, Flavor compounds, Microbial community, Correlation analysis

Introduction

Fermented vegetables represent a diverse category of processed and preserved foods, renowned for their consumer-favored advantages. These include an extended shelf life (Chen, Cheng, et al., 2022), reduced economic losses (Capozzi et al., 2017), as well as improved product quality and enhanced prebiotic properties (Ciniviz & Yildiz, 2020; Pazla et al., 2024; Selim et al., 2024a; Selim et al., 2024b). Commonly fermented varieties encompass Chinese cabbage, radish, cowpea, chili pepper, cucumber, and bamboo shoots (Gunawardena et al., 2024). Mustard (Brassica juncea var. napiformis), a member of the Brassicaceae family, is characterized by its strong adaptability and high yield (Tian et al., 2021). It is rich in various proteins, vitamins, and minerals (Engels et al., 2012; Yu et al., 2022).

Fermented mustard enjoys widespread popularity due to its unique flavor, crisp texture, and palatable taste (Xiao et al., 2020). It also serves as an essential ingredient in numerous dishes, such as sauerkraut fish and Zhacai noodles. Currently, the annual production of fermented mustard tuber in China has reached 5.2 million tons, generating a market value exceeding 1.2 billion USD (Yu et al., 2022).

Wild jujube (Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa) is a woody plant belonging to the Rhamnaceae family (Guo et al., 2017), primarily produced in regions such as Hebei, Shaanxi, and Shanxi provinces. It thrives in warm and dry environments and is widely distributed in hilly areas, mountainous regions, plains, slopes, roadsides, and open fields. Beyond its ecological adaptability, wild jujube holds notable value for its distinctive flavor profile and potential role in food fermentation. The application of wild jujube powder in the fermentation of mustard leverages its distinctive flavor and natural components to enhance the sensory profile of the fermented product. This approach addresses the common challenge of product uniformity in traditional fermented mustard, offering a novel strategy for diversifying and enriching product varieties. While wild jujube is also recognized for its economic significance and nutritional value, containing various nutrients and phytochemicals in its leaves, pulp, and seeds (Hua et al., 2021), the current study focuses primarily on its contribution to flavor development during co-fermentation.

The unique flavor of fermented vegetables is primarily formed by microorganisms, whose metabolites include organic acids, free amino acids, and volatile compounds (Luo et al., 2021). It is well-established that lactic acid bacteria (LAB), such as Lactobacillus, Leuconostoc, Weissella, and Pediococcus, often dominate various fermented vegetable products, including Korean kimchi, Chinese Paocai, and European sauerkraut (Di Cagno et al., 2013; Guan et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2021). Studies have indicated that LAB can produce metabolites like organic acids, alcohols, and esters, which impart a “sour” taste and distinctive flavors to fermented foods (Al-Assil & Wahwah, 2024; Liu et al., 2017).

While extensive research has focused on the fermentation dynamics of single-substrate or traditional vegetable systems, a growing body of literature highlights the potential of co-fermentation strategies involving the addition of fruits or adjuncts to modulate flavor profiles. For instance, the incorporation of fruits such as apple, pear, and jujube into vegetable fermentations has been shown to introduce additional carbon sources and bioactive compounds, thereby influencing microbial community succession and metabolic pathways (Chen, Geng, et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2020). Specifically, jujube (Ziziphus jujuba) and its varieties have been utilized in fermented products like jujube vinegar and jujube wine, where they contribute to the formation of esters and other aromatic compounds due to their high sugar and triterpenic acid content (Cai et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023). Furthermore, studies on the incorporation of various herbs and fruits into vegetable fermentation demonstrate that such additions can selectively enrich specific LAB strains and enhance the sensory attributes of the final product (Liu et al., 2023; Park et al., 2021). However, these studies have primarily focused on commonly used fruits such as apple and pear, while the potential of underutilized fruits like wild jujube in vegetable fermentation remains largely unexplored.

However, despite these advances in fruit-vegetable mixed fermentations, specific research on the co-fermentation system involving mustard tuber (Brassica juncea) with wild jujube powder remains notably scarce. While jujube has been studied in certain food applications, the precise role of wild jujube powder, particularly its indigenous microbiota, dietary fiber, and unique phytochemicals, in modulating the microbial community assembly and flavor metabolism during mustard fermentation has not been systematically explored.

To address this knowledge gap, the present study investigated the co-fermentation of mustard tuber with wild jujube powder (ZFBJ). We comprehensively characterized the dynamic changes in flavor compounds using an electronic nose, an amino acid analyzer, and headspace gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-GC–MS). Simultaneously, the succession of bacterial communities was profiled via Illumina MiSeq high-throughput sequencing. By integrating these datasets through correlation analysis, we further identified the core microbial taxa associated with key flavor formation. This multi-omics approach provides a comprehensive understanding of the ZFBJ fermentation ecosystem and establishes a scientific foundation for developing novel fermented vegetable products with enhanced sensory properties.

Materials and methods

Preparation of mustard fermented with wild Jujube

Mustard tubers (Brassica juncea var. napiformis) were purchased from a local market in China. Fresh mustard tubers (500 g) were washed, cleaned, and cut into fine strips (4 mm × 8 cm). For the experimental group (designated ZFBJ: Mixed Fermentation with Mustard and Wild Jujube), the shredded mustard tubers were thoroughly mixed with 4% (w/w) salt and 5% (w/w) wild jujube powder. Sodium chloride (food grade, China Salt Co., Ltd., China) was used for brine preparation. The 4% salt concentration was selected based on traditional mustard pickling practices (4–6% salt) and is within the range reported in vegetable fermentation studies (Xiong et al., 2016). The 5% wild jujube powder addition level was selected based on previous studies reporting 5% as an optimal concentration for fruit-vegetable co-fermentation (Kwon et al., 2025). The control group (designated CK: mustard fermentation without jujube) was prepared similarly but without the addition of wild jujube powder. The mixture was then transferred into a sterilized 4 L fermentation jar, and 1 L of boiled-cooled water was added. The experiment was performed in triplicate. The jar was tightly sealed with a lid to maintain anaerobic conditions and stored at 18 °C for 30 days. Anaerobic conditions were confirmed by a resazurin indicator strip that remained colorless throughout fermentation. These conditions were selected based on traditional mustard pickling practices in northern China. After 30 days of sealed fermentation, the final product, designated as ZFBJ (mixed fermentation with mustard and wild jujube), was obtained. Samples (brine: mustard, 1:1, w/v) were aseptically collected on days 0, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 20, 25, and 30 of fermentation. Each collected sample was assigned a unique code indicating the fermentation day and replicate: D0, D2, D4, D6, D9, D12, D15, D20, D25, and D30, where the suffix −0 denotes the CK group (e.g., D0–0, D2–0), and the suffixes −1, −2, and − 3 denote the three biological replicates of the ZFBJ group (e.g., D0-1 to D0-3, D2-1 to D2-3). All samples were immediately stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. For physicochemical and flavor analyses, the collected samples were used directly without additional treatment, except for homogenization and centrifugation as described in Section 2.3. For microbial analysis, brine samples were stored at −80 °C without further processing.

Chemical composition of wild jujube powder

The wild jujube powder used in this study was a commercial food-grade product sourced from China. According to the manufacturer's specification, the powder contains high levels of fermentable sugars (approximately 50–70% of dry weight, mainly fructose, glucose, and sucrose), protein (3–6%), dietary fiber (10–12%), and various bioactive phytochemicals characteristic of Ziziphus species, including saponins (e.g., jujubosides A and B), triterpenic acids (betulinic acid, oleanolic acid, ursolic acid), flavonoids, and organic acids. These compositional characteristics are consistent with previous reports (Guo et al., 2011; Li et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2010).

Determination of physicochemical properties

Each sample (60 g) was first homogenized using a high-speed homogenizer (T18 digital Ultra-Turrax, IKA, Staufen, Germany) and then centrifuged at 8000×g for 4 min at 4 °C using a refrigerated centrifuge (H2050R, Cence, Changsha, China). The resulting supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. The pH value was measured using a pH meter (STARTER 2100, OHAUS, Shanghai, China). Total titratable acidity (TTA) was determined according to a previously reported method (Gao et al., 2018) by titration with 0.01 M sodium hydroxide prepared from NaOH pellets (≥99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) to an endpoint of pH 8.2 ± 0.2. The TTA value, expressed as percentage of lactic acid, was calculated using the formula: TTA (%) = (V × N × 0.09 × 100)/W, where V is the volume of NaOH consumed (mL), N is the normality of NaOH (0.01 mol/L), 0.09 is the milliequivalent factor for lactic acid (g/meq), and W is the sample weight (g). The nitrite concentration was measured following the Chinese National Standard (GB 5009.33-2016) using the naphthylethylenediamine dihydrochloride method with reagents of analytical grade (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Analysis of flavor compounds

Determination of free amino acids

Free amino acids were determined according to a previously reported method (Qi et al., 2021). Briefly, 2.0 g of sample was homogenized as described in Section 2.3 and mixed with 2 mL of 15% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid solution (≥99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), followed by incubation for 2 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was analyzed for free amino acid content using an amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi, Japan). Similar acid extraction methods have been validated in fermented products such as soy sauce, with recoveries ranging from 79% to 99% (Guidi & Gloria, 2012).

Electronic nose analysis

Analysis was performed using an electronic nose (ENS-iPro, Insent, Beijing, China) according to a previously described method (Zhao et al., 2023) with minor modifications. Briefly, 5.0 g of sample was placed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, mixed with 15 mL of distilled water, and sealed. The tube was gently shaken and then allowed to stand for 30 min prior to measurement. The operational parameters were set as follows: gas flow rate, 0.4 L/min; sample flushing time, 100 s; pre-sampling time, 5 s; data acquisition time, 90 s. All measurements were conducted at room temperature.

Analysis of volatile compounds

For the analysis of volatile compounds, three independent biological replicates were prepared for each treatment group. From each replicate, a 5.0 g sample was homogenized under the same conditions described in Section 2.3, mixed with 5 mL of saturated sodium chloride solution (prepared using NaCl ≥99.5%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) in a 20 mL headspace vial, and then incubated at 60 °C for 20 min.

The volatile compounds were determined using a gas chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC-Q-TOF/MS) system (Agilent 5977C GC/MSD; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an Agilent HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness). The GC oven temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature of 50 °C (4 min), increased to 100 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min (5 min), then raised to 250 °C at 5 °C/min (5 min). The mass spectrometer conditions were as follows: injector temperature, 250 °C; transfer line temperature, 280 °C; helium carrier gas flow rate, 1.0 mL/min; splitless injection mode; mass scan range, m/z 40–600. Volatile compounds were identified by comparing their mass spectra (MS) and retention indices (RI) with data from the NIST mass spectral library (Version 1.6) (Gao et al., 2020). The relative content of each compound was calculated based on its peak area using the peak area normalization method.

Volatile compound data preprocessing

To ensure data quality for subsequent multivariate statistical analysis, the raw dataset of volatile compounds obtained by GC–MS was preprocessed using a two-step filtering strategy.

First, a prevalence-based filtering approach was adopted following the common strategy for handling high-dimensional data in metabolomics studies (Bijlsma et al., 2006): compounds with a detection rate below 50% in any treatment group across all biological replicates were excluded. This step aimed to reduce the interference of sparse variables on multivariate statistical models such as OPLS-DA.

Second, known background contaminants were identified and removed. Specifically, siloxanes, which are well-documented artifacts originating from GC column bleed, inlet septa, or vial cap septa (English, 2022; Huang et al., 2024), were manually identified based on their characteristic mass spectra and retention indices, and subsequently excluded from the dataset prior to any statistical analysis. This step was essential to eliminate non-biological interferences that could compromise the identification of true flavor-related metabolites.

After the two-step preprocessing, the remaining compounds were retained for further analysis. All data preprocessing was performed using SIMCA-P software (Version 14.1, Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden).

Analysis of microbial diversity

Total microbial DNA was extracted from ZFBJ brine samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Bacterial DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For each treatment group, three independent biological replicate samples were subjected to DNA extraction and subsequent sequencing. The V4-V5 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with the specific primers 515F and 907R. The PCR products were excised from a 2% agarose gel (molecular biology grade, Biowest, Spain) and purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen, Corning, NY, USA). The amplicon library was sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Biomarker Technologies Corporation, Beijing, China).

The Trimmomatic v 0.33 software was employed to filter the raw reads obtained from the sequencing process. Subsequently, the identification and removal of primer sequences was conducted using the Cutadapt 1.9.1 software to obtain the clean reads. Denoising, sequence splicing, and removal of chimeric sequences were then performed using the DADA2 plugin in QIIME2 2020.6 to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Diversity analysis (Chao1, Shannon and Simpson indices), variance analysis and correlation analysis were performed based on the ASV feature table (Haas et al., 2011; Liu & Tong, 2017).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS software (version 27.0), followed by Duncan's multiple range test for multiple comparisons among means. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Graphs were generated using Origin software (version 2025b). Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using the SIMCA-P software (Version 14.1).

Results and discussion

Analysis of physicochemical properties

The changes in the physicochemical properties during the fermentation of mustard tuber with wild jujube powder (ZFBJ) are represented by the dynamic profiles of pH, TTA, and nitrite, as shown in Fig. 1. The pH of ZFBJ decreased rapidly during the initial fermentation stage (0–5 days), dropping from 4.28 to 3.99, reflecting a swift accumulation of organic acids in the early phase. In contrast, the control group exhibited a significantly slower decline, with pH decreasing from 5.31 to 5.00 over the same period. Although the magnitude of pH change (ΔpH ≈ 0.29 for ZFBJ vs. ΔpH ≈ 0.31 for control) was similar in both groups during this stage, the absolute pH values of ZFBJ were consistently and significantly lower, indicating a more acidic fermentation environment from the outset. Subsequently (5–30 days), the pH of ZFBJ remained within 3.80–4.10 with minor fluctuations, whereas the control group maintained a higher range of 4.80–5.00 throughout fermentation. Overall, the final pH of ZFBJ significantly decreased from 4.28 to 3.92 (p < 0.05), while the control pH decreased from 5.31 to 4.86. This result confirms that the addition of wild jujube powder significantly enhanced the acidification capacity of the system (Yang et al., 2025). This enhanced acidification is likely due to the higher sugar content in wild jujube powder, which provides additional fermentable substrates for organic acid production by lactic acid bacteria (Li et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025). When the system pH remains at a consistently low and relatively stable level, combined with TTA data, it can serve as an indicator for assessing the fermentation progress and maturity.

Changes in pH (A), TTA (B), and nitrite (C) in ZFBJ and the control group during fermentation.

TTA exhibited an increasing and then stabilizing trend throughout the fermentation period. During the first 15 days, the TTA in the ZFBJ group increased significantly from 5.25 to 7.45 g/kg (p < 0.05), while the control group showed a markedly smaller increase, rising only from 1.46 to 2.09 g/kg. The rise in TTA originated from acid production by microbial fermentation, and its subsequent stabilization indicated that a dynamic balance was reached between acid production and consumption. According to the established criterion that fermented vegetables are considered mature when “TTA > 3 g/kg and pH < 4.0” (Zhang et al., 2016), the ZFBJ fermentation in this study met these maturity conditions by day 9. In contrast, the control group failed to achieve both key indicators. This finding is consistent with previous reports on fermentation involving wild jujube (Li et al., 2022).

During the first 25 days of ZFBJ fermentation, the nitrite concentration increased, reaching a peak value of approximately 1.60 mg/kg on day 25, after which it declined to 1.33 mg/kg. In contrast, the control group exhibited more pronounced nitrite accumulation, with a peak concentration of 3.17 mg/kg, followed by a subsequent decrease. This difference can be attributed to two complementary mechanisms. First, in the low pH environment of ZFBJ, nitrite (NO₂−) reacts with H+ to form unstable nitrous acid, which can further decompose into gases such as nitric oxide and volatilize, thereby reducing the nitrite content (Zhao et al., 2021). Second, and more importantly, the microbial nitrate-reductase activity plays a critical role in nitrite dynamics. During early fermentation, nitrate-reducing bacteria (e.g., Enterobacteriaceae) convert nitrate to nitrite via membrane-bound nitrate reductase (Nar) and periplasmic nitrate reductase (Nap) (Hu et al., 2025). As fermentation progresses and lactic acid bacteria become dominant, nitrite reductase (Nir) produced by LAB species such as Lactobacillus and Pediococcus actively degrades nitrite (Zeng et al., 2019). The lower nitrite peak in ZFBJ likely reflects both enhanced acid-mediated degradation and more efficient enzymatic reduction by the adapted microbial community. This trend is consistent with previous studies on fermented vegetables (Huang et al., 2020). At the end of fermentation, the nitrite concentration was well below the maximum limit of 20 mg/kg specified by the Chinese food safety standard (GB 2762-2017), indicating that the product complies with the nitrite limit for fermented vegetables.

Analysis of flavor compounds

Analysis of free amino acid data

Free amino acids (FAAs) not only contribute directly to the complex flavor profile of fermented foods but are also considered metabolic precursors for conversion into smaller flavor molecules, thereby aiding the development of the unique flavor of fermented vegetables (Chen et al., 2019). The changes in FAAs during ZFBJ fermentation are shown in Fig. 2 and Table 1. As presented in Fig. 2A, the total FAA content accumulated gradually in the early fermentation stage (0–6 days) and subsequently decreased to 608.75 mg/kg by the end of fermentation. Generally, the concentrations of most FAAs increased initially and then decreased as fermentation progressed. This trend is consistent with previous reports on Paocai fermentation (Jung et al., 2013). The observed variations in FAA levels are associated with their expression and biosynthesis (Xiao et al., 2022). Different amino acids distinctly influence flavor. Aspartic acid (approximately 9%), serine (approximately 38.9%), and glutamic acid (approximately 13.3%) were identified as the most abundant FAAs during fermentation. Serine imparts a sweet taste, while aspartic and glutamic acids contribute umami sensations. During ZFBJ fermentation, the changing concentrations of these predominant FAAs directly influenced the evolution of the flavor profile. The accumulation of FAAs in the initial stage likely established the foundational sweet and umami notes, while subsequent fluctuations fine-tuned the overall flavor (Park et al., 2016). In addition, comparative analysis with a control (CK) group (Fig. S1) revealed distinct amino acid dynamics. Notably, the CK group exhibited significantly lower levels of sweet and umami amino acids, coupled with a higher content of bitter amino acids, compared to the ZFBJ fermentation. These contrasting profiles highlight that the specific fermentation conditions and microbial consortium characteristic of ZFBJ are crucial for directing proteolysis and subsequent metabolism towards a more favorable taste balance.

Based on sensory attributes, FAAs were categorized into sweet, bitter, and umami amino acids (Zhao et al., 2016; Bachmanov et al., 2016; Mi et al., 2022), with the results presented in Fig. 2B. In the heatmap, the color representing sweet amino acids transitioned from reddish in the early stage to orange and yellow as fermentation progressed, visually indicating their continuous reduction. This corresponded with quantitative measurements showing a significant decline in sweet amino acid content, from 344.76 mg/kg at the initial fermentation stage to 261.83 mg/kg by day 30. The initial content formed an important foundation for the sweet taste in ZFBJ. This decrease may be attributed to the consumption of sweet amino acids through enzymatic reactions or microbial metabolism during fermentation (Hu et al., 2022). As the primary contributors to the sweet characteristics of ZFBJ, the high initial content of sweet amino acids may have contributed to the development of a clean, sweet base note in the product, based on their established taste properties (Mi et al., 2022). Their sustained decrease during fermentation not only may have influenced the overall taste profile but also may have participated in the formation of ZFBJ's unique complex flavor profile, as these compounds are known to serve as precursors for volatile flavor compounds such as aldehydes and furans via Strecker degradation or the Maillard reaction in fermented food systems (van Kranenburg et al., 2002; Zhao et al., 2016). However, direct precursor–product relationships between specific FAAs and volatile compounds in ZFBJ fermentation remain to be elucidated. The content of bitter amino acids changed from 186.74 mg/kg initially to 195.23 mg/kg on day 30, while umami amino acids changed from 154.29 mg/kg to 144.99 mg/kg. As observed in Fig. 2B, the contents of bitter and umami amino acids remained relatively stable throughout the fermentation process.

Analysis of electronic nose data

The electronic nose analysis results (Fig. 3A) indicated that fermentation time had a significant impact on the flavor profile of ZFBJ. The radar chart (Fig. 3A) displays the response patterns of different electronic nose sensors across various fermentation time points, with distinct colors representing different fermentation days. Each sensor responds to a broad range of volatile compounds, and the combined sensor array provides a fingerprint of the overall volatile composition. The data revealed that the flavor intensity of ZFBJ initially increased and then decreased as fermentation progressed, with notable alterations observed in sensors such as W1S, W1W, and W2W. These changes suggest a significant influence of fermentation time on compounds including methylated compounds, including compounds commonly associated with methylated compounds, sulfur-containing compounds, and aromatic compounds, reflecting the dynamic evolution of volatile composition throughout the fermentation process. A comparison with the CK group (Fig. S2) further underscored the unique flavor development in ZFBJ. Notably, the CK group demonstrated markedly lower response values for the key sensors W1S, W1W, and W2W compared to the ZFBJ fermentation, indicating a substantially reduced production of the corresponding volatile flavor compounds (methylated, aromatic, and organic sulfides) under control conditions.

Dynamic changes in electronic nose sensor responses during the fermentation of ZFBJ. (A) Radar chart of sensor profiles; (B) Heat map of sensor intensity.

As observed in Fig. 3B, the W1W sensor exhibited a deep red color from days 9 to 12, indicating changes in the abundance of volatile compounds detected by this sensor during this phase, which coincides with the formation of flavor substances in ZFBJ (Wang & Chen, 2024). Concurrently, Fig. 3A demonstrates intense fluctuations in sensor responses within the same period. Integrated with physicochemical validation, this stage was marked by a decrease in nitrite content, a decline in pH, and an increase in total acid concentration. These collective changes suggest that this interval may represent a period of relatively high microbial metabolic activity and substance transformation. During this phase, prolific acid production by microorganisms lowered the pH, thereby inhibiting harmful bacteria, while simultaneously driving the conversion of nitrogen-containing compounds and carbohydrates into flavor substances. This stage could thus be considered an important phase in the development of ZFBJ fermentation quality (Seesaard & Wongchoosuk, 2022).

Analysis of volatile compounds

Following data preprocessing (as detailed in Section 2.4.4), which involved excluding compounds with a detection rate below 50% in any treatment group as well as known background contaminants (siloxanes), a total of 34 volatile compounds were retained for subsequent analysis (Table S1).

A total of 142 volatile compounds were detected in this study, from which 34 volatile compounds were selected for analysis. These included 4 sulfur-containing compounds, 4 carboxylic acids and esters, 6 aldehydes, 10 alcohols, 9 nitrogen-containing compounds, and 1 heterocyclic and other compounds. The distribution of volatile compounds varied considerably across different fermentation time points (Fig. 4A and B). Sulfur-containing compounds were the most abundant group (Fig. 4A), with allyl isothiocyanate and 2-Phenylethyl isothiocyanate being particularly prominent, collectively accounting for 76.73% of the total volatile compounds. Allyl isothiocyanate has been reported as the primary source of the pungent note in leaf mustard (Zhao et al., 2007), while 2-Phenylethyl isothiocyanate is associated with spicys in mustard seeds (Frank et al., 2010). As fermentation progressed, the total content of volatile compounds decreased, whereas the variety of volatile flavor compounds increased from 32 to 36 by the end of fermentation. Specifically, the contents of sulfur-containing compounds, nitrogen-containing compounds, and heterocyclic/other compounds decreased over time. In contrast, the levels of alcohols, aldehydes, carboxylic acids, and esters increased. These changes in volatile compounds are likely attributable to esterification, oxidation, and the action of secondary metabolites produced by microorganisms during ZFBJ fermentation (Gao et al., 2020).

Dynamic changes in volatile compound profiles during the fermentation of ZFBJ. (A) Composition of key volatile flavor compounds; (B) heatmap of volatile compound distribution.

Compared to the control group without wild jujube powder, ZFBJ effectively suppressed the formation of key off-flavor compounds during fermentation. As shown in Table S1, the contents of two primary sulfur-containing off-flavor substances were significantly reduced in ZFBJ: 2-Phenylethyl isothiocyanate decreased by 84.9%, and allyl isothiocyanate was reduced by 25.4%. This indicates that the active components in wild jujube powder effectively inhibited or promoted the conversion/degradation of undesirable flavor compounds. This observation is consistent with the possibility that modulation of the microbial community structure could affect the conversion of glucosinolate precursors into isothiocyanates (Šalić & Šamec, 2022).

To comprehensively characterize the overall changes in volatile compounds during ZFBJ fermentation, multivariate analysis was performed on the 39 volatile compounds using SIMCA-P14.1. The principal component analysis (PCA) score plot is shown in Fig. 5A. The first principal component (PC1) and the second principal component (PC2) accounted for 58.8% and 19% of the total variance, respectively, with a cumulative contribution rate of 77.8%. This indicates that PC1 and PC2 effectively capture the primary information of the samples and can be used to distinguish samples from different fermentation days. The PCA score plot revealed a clear stage-specific shift in sample distribution along the principal component space as fermentation progressed, indicating that the intrinsic characteristics of the samples evolved over time, and samples from different fermentation stages possessed distinguishable features. Integrating the results from both principal component analysis (PCA, Fig. 5A) and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA, Fig. 5B), the samples could be distinctly classified into four groups: Group 1 (day 0), Group 2 (days 2, 4, and 6), Group 3 (days 9, 12, 15, 20, and 25), and Group 4 (day 30).

Multivariate analysis of volatile compound profiles during the fermentation of ZFBJ. (A) PCA score plot; (B) HCA plot; (C) OPLS-DA score plot; (D) permutation test; (E) VIP plot of OPLS-DA.

Based on the grouping of volatile compounds in PCA and HCA analysis, OPLS-DA analysis was performed to determine the differences between compounds in Group 1, Group 2, Group 3, and Group 4. The R2 and Q2 values were 0.676 and 0.601 (Fig. 5C and D), respectively, with an intercept of −0.483 (p < 0.05), indicating that the OPLS-DA model was suitable for the analysis and prediction. The OPLS-DA scores scatter plot showed that Group 1, Group 2, Group 3, and Group 4 were distinguished by volatile compounds. Furthermore, variable importance in projection (VIP) was used to identify the volatile compounds responsible for this separation (Fig. 5E). Thirteen volatile compounds with VIP scores >1.0 were selected as potential discriminant markers, including three alcohols (cyclopropylmethanol, cyclobutanol, cyclobutylmethanol), two aldehydes (benzaldehyde, phenylacetaldehyde), three carboxylic acids and esters (Non-2-ynoic acid, palmitic acid, acetic acid), three nitrogen-containing compounds (5-methylhexan-2-amine, 3-phenylpropionitrile, N-methyl-2-phenylethan-1-amine), and two sulfur-containing compounds (2-phenylethyl isothiocyanate, allyl isothiocyanate). These 13 volatile compounds showed significant changes in abundance during ZFBJ fermentation, suggesting that they may contribute to the development of the characteristic flavor profile.

Analysis of microbial diversity

Sequencing depth and coverage

After quality filtering, a total of 2,362,576 high-quality reads were obtained from 40 samples, with an average of 59,064 reads per sample (range: 39,391–69,890). Rarefaction curves were generated by plotting the number of observed features (ASV) against sequencing depth. As shown in Fig. S3, all rarefaction curves approached a plateau, indicating that the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the majority of microbial diversity in each sample. Good‘s coverage values ranged from 0.9995 to 1.0000 (mean: 0.9998), indicating that >99.9% of the estimated species diversity was covered at the current sequencing depth.

Analysis of amplicon sequence variant (ASV)

The distribution of ASV during the fermentation of ZFBJ is shown in Fig. 6. The number of ASV reflects the diversity of the microbial community, where a higher ASV count generally indicates potentially greater community diversity. As shown in Fig. 6A, the number of ASV varied noticeably across different time points, with a significant increase observed on day 15 compared to other sampling times. This indicates that microbial community diversity changed over the course of fermentation. Fig. 6B shows that “Core: 92” represents the number of core ASV shared across all time points. These persistent ASV represent key microbial taxa that likely play important roles throughout the ZFBJ fermentation process.

Analysis of operational taxonomic units (ASV) during the fermentation of ZFBJ. (A) Histogram of ASV distribution; (B) Venn diagram of ASV distribution.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, the microbial community during fermentation consisted of both a stable core population and dynamic unique taxa that adapted to the fermentation process. This structural dynamic ultimately resulted in significant fluctuations in the total ASV numbers across different time points. Such community succession may directly influence the types and yields of fermentation metabolites.

Alpha diversity analysis

Fig. 7 evaluates the bacterial alpha diversity during ZFBJ fermentation, including the Chao1 index, Shannon index, and Simpson index. The results revealed that all three indices exhibited an initial decrease followed by a subsequent increase, suggesting a successional pattern where bacterial diversity rapidly declined in the early fermentation stage and then gradually recovered. During the initial phase, the harsh anaerobic and acidifying environment created strong selective pressure, suppressing most microorganisms and leading to a sharp decline in species richness and evenness. Meanwhile, a few acid-tolerant dominant species proliferated rapidly, resulting in increased dominance. As fermentation progressed into the later stages, the environment stabilized. The growth of early dominant bacteria slowed, providing ecological niches for other acid-tolerant and anaerobic microorganisms. This facilitated the recovery of community richness and evenness, ultimately establishing a more diverse, balanced, and stable community structure. This finding aligns with previous reports (Zhang et al., 2021), collectively supporting the concept that microbial communities in vegetable fermentation systems follow a fundamental pattern of deterministic selection and competitive succession. This principle illustrates how communities undergo intense deterministic selection initially, followed by competitive succession, ultimately forming a diverse and stable functional community structure.

Analysis of bacterial diversity

Fig. 8 analyzes the microbial community at the phylum (Fig. 8A) and genus (Fig. 8B) levels during the fermentation of ZFBJ. At the phylum level, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota, and Actinobacteria were predominant in the initial stages, with other phyla detected at relatively low abundances (Yang et al., 2020). In contrast, the control group maintained a relatively stable microbial structure throughout fermentation without undergoing drastic directional succession. In ZFBJ, Proteobacteria showed the highest relative abundance initially but decreased continuously as fermentation progressed, becoming significantly lower in the later stages. Proteobacteria includes many aerobic/facultative anaerobic bacteria, which thrived in the early fermentation environment but were gradually outcompeted by more acid-tolerant taxa as the system became more acidic and anaerobic. In the control group, the dominance of Proteobacteria persisted, with no significant decline in its relative abundance, indicating that the addition of wild jujube powder was a key driver in the competitive inhibition of Proteobacteria. Conversely, Firmicutes, initially low in abundance in ZFBJ, increased significantly and became one of the core dominant phyla by the later stages. Firmicutes includes typical functional fermentative bacteria, such as lactic acid bacteria, which utilize carbohydrates to produce acids and thrive in the acidic conditions of the later fermentation stages, playing a critical role in driving ZFBJ fermentation. In the control group, the increase in Firmicutes was minimal, with its relative abundance remaining low throughout the process, demonstrating that wild jujube powder provided essential substrates or growth stimulation for the rapid proliferation of Firmicutes. Actinobacteria, present at moderate levels initially in ZFBJ, decreased continuously and became negligible in the later stages. As mostly aerobic bacteria, Actinobacteria struggled to compete in the anaerobic environment of the later fermentation stages. Bacteroidota, present at low levels initially, declined rapidly and nearly disappeared by the mid to late stages. This phylum is relatively sensitive to environmental changes, and acid production during fermentation likely contributed to its elimination. In the control group, the decline in both Actinobacteria and Bacteroidota was significantly slower, with their relative abundances higher than in ZFBJ at multiple time points. This suggests that the addition of wild jujube powder accelerated the elimination of these non-acid-tolerant or aerobic groups by rapidly reducing pH and altering the redox potential.

Microbial community composition during the fermentation of ZFBJ. (A) Relative abundance at the phylum level (Top 10); (B) Relative abundance at the genus level (Top 10).

At the genus level, Stenotrophomonas, Pseudomonas, and Microbacterium were the most dominant genera in the initial stages of ZFBJ fermentation. This profile differs from previously reported findings for Sichuan Paocai, where Enterobacter, Pseudomonas, and Klebsiella were identified as the primary genera in the initial fermentation phase (Xiao et al., 2018). As fermentation progressed, two genera, Haloarcula and Candidatus Nitrocosmicus, were detected in the control group but were absent in ZFBJ. Haloarcula, a group of extremely halophilic archaea, persisted and maintained a certain relative abundance in the control group but showed no significant growth in ZFBJ. This indicates that the addition of wild jujube powder effectively inhibited the proliferation of Haloarcula, potentially by altering the system's osmotic pressure or providing specific antimicrobial components, thereby creating an ecological environment unfavorable for the growth of halophilic archaea. Candidatus Nitrocosmicus, which plays a significant role in the nitrogen cycle, was only detected in the control group and persisted throughout its fermentation. This suggests that the acidic environment in ZFBJ was likely unfavorable for the survival of these ammonia-oxidizing archaea. The relative abundance of Stenotrophomonas was notably high, establishing it as a dominant genus. Its proportion increased further during the later stages of ZFBJ fermentation. In contrast, the relative abundance of Pseudomonas generally exhibited a decreasing trend as fermentation progressed. Pseudomonas species are predominantly aerobic or facultatively anaerobic. As the system became increasingly anaerobic and acidic, this environmental shift became unfavorable for their growth and reproduction, leading to a gradual decline in their relative abundance (Li et al., 2023). Similarly, the relative abundance of Microbacterium gradually decreased throughout ZFBJ fermentation. In this process, acid-producing microorganisms such as lactic acid bacteria metabolize to produce organic acids like lactic acid and acetic acid, causing the system pH to drop progressively. Pseudomonas exhibits poor tolerance to acidic environments; when the pH decreases to a certain level, the enzymatic activity within its cells is inhibited, cell membrane stability is compromised, and its growth and reproductive capacity are significantly reduced.

Correlation analysis between bacterial community and physicochemical properties

Variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis is a commonly used method for screening environmental factors. It represents the ratio of the variance of a regression coefficient estimate to the variance that would exist if the independent variables were not linearly correlated. A higher VIF indicates more severe multicollinearity (Salmerón-Gómez et al., 2025). Generally, a VIF value greater than 0 and less than 10 indicates no multicollinearity; a VIF approximately equal to or greater than 10 but less than 100 suggests strong multicollinearity; and a VIF greater than or equal to 100 indicates severe multicollinearity. It is widely accepted that environmental factors with VIF values exceeding 10 have significant multicollinearity issues (Neter et al., 1996). Environmental factors with a VIF greater than 10 were excluded from the analysis. This screening process was iterated until all selected environmental factors had VIF values below 10. The VIF values for pH, TTA, and nitrite were all below 10 (Table 2), indicating negligible multicollinearity and confirming their suitability for subsequent analysis of bacterial community influences. To evaluate the effects of these three physicochemical properties on the bacterial community during ZFBJ fermentation, a correlation heatmap analysis was conducted (Fig. 9). The results revealed that Pantoea exhibited a relatively strong positive correlation with nitrite, and a moderate positive correlation with TTA, while showing no significant correlation with pH. Enterobacter and Klebsiella also displayed positive correlations with nitrite and TTA, though these associations were weaker than those observed for Pantoea. In contrast, Stenotrophomonas and Paucibacter demonstrated negative correlations with nitrite and TTA. Pseudomonas, Microbacterium, and Sphingobacterium showed mixed correlation patterns: Pseudomonas was negatively correlated with nitrite and TTA but positively associated with pH; Microbacterium exhibited a negative correlation with nitrite and TTA, and no clear association with pH; Sphingobacterium displayed a positive correlation with nitrite and TTA but a negative correlation with pH. These findings indicate that pH, TTA, and nitrite play significant roles in shaping the bacterial community structure during fermentation, and different bacterial genera exhibit distinct and specific responses to these physicochemical properties.

Regarding free amino acids, serine and alanine showed significant correlations during ZFBJ fermentation. Alanine was positively correlated with Stenotrophomonas (r = 0.56), whereas serine was negatively correlated with Klebsiella (r = −0.45). Pseudomonas was identified as the most crucial genus in ZFBJ fermentation, demonstrating direct and diverse correlations with multiple volatile compounds and amino acids, a finding consistent with previous studies on cucumber pickling (Tang et al., 2024).

Role of wild jujube powder in shaping microbial community and aroma formation

The effects of wild jujube powder on microbial community and aroma can be attributed to its high sugar content (50–70% dry weight) and bioactive compounds (Guo et al., 2011; Hua et al., 2021). The abundant sugars selectively promote lactic acid bacteria growth and provide substrates for flavor compound formation. This sugar-mediated modulation distinguishes ZFBJ from traditional mustard fermentation.

Conclusions

This study systematically elucidated the structural dynamics and interaction mechanisms of physicochemical properties, flavor compounds, and microbial communities during the co-fermentation of mustard and wild jujube powder. The results demonstrated that this mixed fermentation strategy effectively suppressed the formation of sulfur-containing off-flavor compounds while enriching sweet-tasting amino acids, thereby contributing to the development of a distinctive flavor profile. Microbiome analysis revealed a successional pattern from Proteobacteria to Firmicutes, with pH, TTA, and nitrite content identified as the key environmental factors driving this community shift. Notably, the genus Pseudomonas emerging as a potential key genus associated with multiple flavor compounds.

This study provides an important reference for the precise flavor regulation of fermented vegetables. Future research should focus on: validating the key metabolic pathways of core genera such as Pseudomonas involved in flavor compound metabolism using metatranscriptomics and other techniques; optimizing fermentation parameters to directionally modulate flavor formation and facilitate industrial-scale production; and further investigating the mechanisms by which wild jujube powder enhances the quality of the mustard fermentation system.
