Postcancer rehabilitation: multidisciplinary exercise – programme organisation and feasibility
Authors: Chloé Drozd, Quentin Jacquinot, Sophie Paget-Bailly, Laura Mansi, Marie-Justine Paillard, Fernando Bazan, Loic Chaigneau, Erion Dobi, Julien Viot, Guillaume Meynard, Morgan Goujon, Lorraine Dalens, Victor Pereira, Edgar Robin, Jean Farret, Carole Gagnepain, Ophélie Simon, Christine Fagnoni-Legat, Fabienne Mougin, Nathalie Meneveau, Elsa Curtit
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough the benefit of supportive care in the postcancer period is now well demonstrated, its implementation in the patient journey remains challenging. This article describes the development, since 2015 and in routine care, of supportive postcancer care comprising a multidisciplinary rehabilitation programme (MRP) based on exercise for patients with early breast cancer.MethodsAs part of quality control, we reviewed all patient files since the programme was implemented. Patient data regarding the type of cancer, clinical and pathological factors, and treatment were recorded in a computerised database.ResultsFrom April 2015 to January 2024, 655 patients participated in the MRP. The programme lasts for 14 weeks, totalling 126 hours of face-to-face programme, with a maximum of 8 patients per group, in 5 different centres. A multidisciplinary professional team provide supportive care. The MRP is mainly based on supervised physical exercise and patients also participate in social, psychological, dietary support and educational sessions. Supervised physical exercise includes cardiorespiratory endurance work through specific sessions on ergometers or outdoor walking and adapted physical activity sessions to improve muscular capacities (endurance, strength and flexibility).ConclusionWe describe here the practical implementation of a routine multidisciplinary supportive care programme, based mainly on physical activity, for post-treatment breast cancer patients. We report almost 9 years of experience with the programme. We show that offering this programme in the postcancer setting and in clinical routine practice is feasible and can be maintained in the long term.Keywords: Breast, Supportive care, Rehabilitation, Quality of life, Survivorship

Introduction

Supportive oncological care aims to reduce the impact treatment side effects and to improve health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among patients, on the physical, psychological and social levels, taking into account the diversity of their needs, regardless of their place of care.1 The ‘postcancer’ period (ie, following surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy) is characterised by fatigue, musculoskeletal pain and feelings of anxiety or depression, which have a very strong impact on patients' HRQoL.2,8 This postcancer period can be particularly difficult, and supportive oncological care plays a decisive role in enabling patients to regain acceptable HRQoL. Postcancer deteriorations in HRQoL are likely to be long-lasting and can compromise patients’ engagement in physical and social activities, as well as their return to employment.9,11 Physical deconditioning plays a critical role in these symptoms and impairment of HRQoL leads to a decrease in physical activity, thereby initiating a vicious circle, whereby the lack of physical activity compounds the deconditioning.

Clinical trials assessing supervised physical exercise programmes for patients with cancer have shown an improvement in HRQol and general well-being, as well as a significant reduction in fatigue.12,14 Guidelines recommend supervised physical activity, adapted nutritional behaviour and the implementation of personalised programmes in the ‘postcancer period’. However, it is challenging to set up a post-treatment programme that enables cancer survivors to participate in appropriate physical activity, and receive lifestyle advice and psychological support.15 The quality of the programme, procedures for organising it, geographical accessibility, financial support and dissemination of information all contribute to determining the success of such a programme, and the benefits that patients may derive from it.

While the benefits of supportive care in the postcancer period are now well established, the implementation in the patient journey remains challenging. This article describes the development, since 2015 and in routine care, of a supportive postcancer care programme comprising a multidisciplinary rehabilitation programme (MRP) based on exercise, for patients with early breast cancer.

Methods

The programme was designed in line with the French National Cancer Institute’s strategy for comprehensive postcancer support. It was developed in 2015 in a French region covering an area of 16 000 km2 and with a population of 1.2 million inhabitants. A Federative Regional Cancer Institute supervises cancer care throughout the region, and patients are cared for in eight different establishments, including one university hospital (figure 1). Besides the healthcare establishments, five different centres were able to provide the supportive oncological care required in this postcancer programme (figure 1). The programme content was developed based on the scientific literature and the expert opinion of healthcare providers in the field of occupational health, oncology, sports medicine and physiotherapy.

Initially, the postcancer programme was intended for patients with early breast cancer who had received chemotherapy and had completed their treatment (except for endocrine therapy and/or adjuvant anti-HER2 targeted therapy, which could be still ongoing during the programme). However, as it is implemented in routine care, all requests were considered. The only conditions for patients to participate were that the patients were willing to do so and that their medical oncologist confirmed the absence of any contraindication to the postcancer programme.

Results

Feasibility and organisation of the MRP

From April 2015 to January 2024, among 3028 patients treated with (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy for early breast cancer, 655 (21.6%) patients participated in the multidisciplinary postcancer rehabilitation programme. To participate in the rehabilitation programme, the patients were allocated across five different centres, according to their place of residence (table 1). More than 98% of patients completed the whole MRP. No serious adverse events linked to the programme occurred (such as acute coronary syndrome or bone fracture). Patient participation in the programme is completely free of charge and funded by the French social security, including full reimbursement of the cost of transport.

Description of the MRP

General description

Patients followed the MRP for a total 14 weeks, 3 hours per day on 3 days each week, totalling 126 hours of face-to-face participation. There was a maximum of eight patients per group. First, all patients had a consultation with a physician, for an overall assessment of their physical and mental status. Their cancer history and needs in terms of supportive oncological care were taken into account (figure 2).

Supervised physical exercise

The physical exercise programme was supervised by teachers of adapted physical activity or by physiotherapists. Each week, a minimum of six sessions of 45 min (a maximum of eight sessions/week) of activity were proposed to patients (4.5 hours/week). The programme consisted of two main parts. The first part consisted of cardiorespiratory endurance work, through specific sessions on ergometers or outdoor walking. The second part consisted of adapted physical activity sessions to improve muscular capacities (endurance, strength and flexibility).

In each session, the intensity of the exercise was adapted to the patient’s physical condition. The activity was monitored using the Borg scale to rate the patient’s perceived exertion before and after each session.16 The self-reported 20-point Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) was used to estimate the exercise intensity. Scores of the RPE range from 6 (no exertion at all) to 20 (maximal exertion).

For cardiorespiratory endurance work, three sessions per week consisted of endurance training on an ergometer (exercise bike, treadmill or elliptical bike). This programme was intermittent work based on the Square-Wave Endurance Exercise Test validated by Gimenez et al.17 During each session, heart rate was continuously measured with a fingertip pulse oximeter (Onyx Vantage 9590, Nonin Medical, USA).

Outdoor walking sessions (Nordic or classic walking) could also be proposed in addition to, or in place of the sessions on ergometers.

Each endurance session included a 5–10 min warm-up, a 45 min main component, and a final 5 min recovery period, or relaxing and stretching exercises. The exercise intensity was set to an RPE rating of 11–15.16

For the adapted physical activity sessions (minimum two sessions/week), several forms of exercise were offered, namely strength exercises using weight machines (upper and lower limb work) or small equipment (elastics, dumbbells, bands, steps, etc …) during soft gym sessions or circuit training programmes. In this way, the muscles involved in the activities of daily living were exercised (biceps, triceps, deltoid and trapezius muscles; knee and hip extensors and flexors and deep abdominal and back muscles related to posture). In each session, patients carried out 2 series of 8–15 repetitions for each exercise with an intensity of 11–15 RPE.

Occasionally, according to patients’ needs, exercise sessions targeting coordination, balance or body-mind activities such as yoga, Pilates, sophrology and relaxation were proposed.

Dietary intervention

Patients could meet the dietician regularly and individually according to their needs and objectives (weight maintenance, loss or gain). A maximum of one consultation per week and per patient could be performed. During these consultations, the patients received personalised advice according to their objectives (to modify their eating habits and behaviours) but also information concerning the utility of following the recommendations of the European code against cancer, as well as the Mediterranean diet, to control weight and energy intake.

Psychological care

At the request of patients or professionals, individual consultations could be offered during the programme. Patients who needed such support could have one consultation per week. Nevertheless, the frequency of the sessions was at the discretion of the psychologist and/or the patient according to their needs. Psychologists use different techniques (eg, mindfulness therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy and cognitive–behavioural stress management) to support patients in dealing with mental distress, anxiety, affective disorders, body image and depression.

Social intervention

Patients could receive social support from a professional or on personal request, during the programme. The aim of the social support intervention was to detect social and financial vulnerabilities. In addition, social workers supported the patients and directed them towards available support systems to facilitate their return to work.

Health education intervention

The health education intervention consisted of 13 scheduled group sessions (approximately 1 session/week of 45 min). These sessions were composed of theoretical and practical workshops to help patients to modify their behaviours in terms of physical activity, sedentary lifestyle, dietary habits or workshops to help to manage emotions and stress. Thus, different professionals were involved in the programme (physiotherapists, APA teachers, dieticians, psychologists and social workers) and led sessions on different topics, as follows:

Discussion

The goal of this article was to describe an example of a multidisciplinary supportive care programme for postcancer patients, initiated in 2015. Although the place of supportive care in oncology, and especially physical activity, is now well recognised, its implementation in clinical routine practice faces multiple obstacles, especially in the postcancer period. We describe here the practical implementation of a routine multidisciplinary supportive care programme for postbreast cancer patients, with almost 9 years of experience. We show that offering this programme in the postcancer setting in routine care is feasible and can be maintained over the long term. This longevity can be explained by several factors. The first is that it is based on a permanent, wide-scale communication campaign aimed at patients and professionals (in private practice and in the hospital setting) as well as patient associations. In fact, patients receive a lot of information documents, starting from the time of diagnosis, informing them about supportive oncology care during and after treatment. In addition, continuing medication education is offered to all professionals who care for patients (nurses, nursing assistants, doctors, etc) in order to inform them and train them in screening for physical and psychosocial vulnerability, and in the prescription of MPR. Furthermore, working groups with professionals involved in the programmes and hospital professionals were created from the outset of the first programme, in order to facilitate communication between professionals, and with a view to improving practices.

Another strong point of this MRP is that it is completely free of charge for patients who have social security coverage. It should be noted that the total cost of this MPR is, on average, €5376 per patient (€128/half-day), not including transport costs. The total cost of the programme and transport was fully financed by the French healthcare system. Indeed, in a constrained healthcare environment, where the search for financial savings is a hallmark of healthcare systems in many countries around the world, we are fortunate in France to be able to offer this programme free of charge to our patients via the French universal social security system. Otherwise, the number of participants would likely be considerably lower.

Another strength of this programme is its harmonisation. Indeed, regardless of the location of the supportive care centre, the duration, frequency and type of professionals were identical, thus guaranteeing equality of care, with no difference across establishments or locations (rural or urban areas). Moreover, the geographical distribution of the five centres makes it possible to guarantee local access, thus limiting travel and consequently fatigue and the costs associated with travel.

This work also has some limitations. First, it is an evaluation of practices from a quality control perspective. We only have retrospective data obtained from patients’ medical records, and this programme was not associated with a prospective research project. As a result, we do not have, for example, any data on the outcomes of side effects of cancer treatments, on the date of return to work or any formalised psychological and neurocognitive assessment. Patients did not complete satisfaction forms, although the feedback they gave to their oncologists was extremely positive. Nevertheless, a survey was carried out among all oncologists and radiotherapists, and around 50 patients who participated in the programme, to ask their opinion about possible developments or modifications that could be made to increase reception and care capacities. The results of the survey highlighted, among other things, the amount of positive feedback (from both doctors and patients), which at least partially explains the consistently high number of prescriptions each year, thereby perpetuating the activity of the partner establishments. This is probably one of the reasons why this programme has been able to continue over time.

Due to the many side effects experienced as a result of the cancer and its treatment, patients, may have supportive care needs for many years after diagnosis, and multidisciplinary care can help address these needs and reduce the physical and psychological effects of cancer and its treatment. A study by Schmid-Büchi et al evaluated the supportive care needs of patients with breast cancer during treatment, and the results showed that their needs were predominantly related to the domains of physical daily living, healthcare system and information, and psychological needs.18 Findings from a previous study among the same population approximately 1 year after finishing medical treatment were similar.19 Currently, the benefits of various supportive care interventions are well established.20 An accumulating body of evidence supports the benefits of physical exercise and nutritional management, during and after anticancer treatments, and these interventions are now recommended in routine practice.21 22 Multidisciplinary supportive care, integrating nutrition, exercise and the psychosocial environment, alongside oncological treatments, is now regarded as the new paradigm to improve patient survival and HRQoL.21

One of the challenges today is to promote access to, and implementation of multidisciplinary supportive care programmes during anticancer therapy, but also after treatment, in the postcancer period, in order to return to a ‘normal life’ for patients. This survivorship care, in the field of supportive care, is different from care delivered at the same time as cancer treatment. The findings of a meta-analysis about a rehabilitation intervention and return to work, highlight variability across interventions for women with breast cancer, in intervention effectiveness, content and delivery, in the currently available published literature.23 Only one study reported positive results in terms of work outcomes, which could be partially explained by its multidisciplinary format, providing exercise, psychological and dietary advice. In recent years, few studies have investigated the effects of a multidisciplinary approach, and these studies are rarely carried out on a large sample of patients with exhaustive assessments. Several studies have evaluated the effects of a multidisciplinary supportive care programme on physical performance, reported outcomes (HRQoL, fatigue, anxiety and depression) and return to work in the post-treatment period, and results have shown significant benefits in cancer patients.20 24

Conclusion

Supportive care after cancer treatment is essential and should be available, affordable and accessible for all patients. Returning to a ‘normal life’ is often difficult and patients should be accompanied. To date, MRPs have shown their effectiveness in limiting side effects and improving the physical condition of patients after breast cancer treatments. Physical activity is one of the key elements of postcancer care and can improve both physical side effects and patients’ mental health. We describe here the practical implementation of a routine multidisciplinary supportive care programme for postbreast cancer patients, with almost 9 years of experience. We show that offering this programme to hundreds of patients in the postcancer setting and in clinical routine practice is feasible and can be maintained over the long term.
