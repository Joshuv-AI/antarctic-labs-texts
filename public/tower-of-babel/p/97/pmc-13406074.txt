Global burden of fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injuries, 1990−2021: A systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Jiale Shao, Yuanqiang Zhu, Mingsheng Xie, Huiyi Ding, Shiji Li, Le Qiu, Fei Wang, Sheng Liu, Xulin Chen, Xiao Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injuries represent a significant global health burden but are often understudied. Previous research has typically focused on macro-level trends, neglecting the underlying causes of these changes. Our study addresses this gap by using advanced methodologies, particularly focusing on regional disease trends during the 2019 to 2021 pandemic period, and proposes targeted interventions based on these insights.Using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 data, we analyzed age-standardized incidence (ASIR), prevalence, mortality, and Disability-Adjusted Life Years attributed to fire, heat, and hot substances. Trends were quantified via estimated annual percentage change, while risk factors, decomposition analysis, and health inequalities were assessed. Future burden from 2022 to 2050 was projected using a Bayesian age-period-cohort model.In 2021, fire, heat, and hot substances caused an estimated 9198,164 incident cases globally (95% uncertainty interval (UI): 7592, 028–10,784,350), with an ASIR of 118.82 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 97.49–139.41), showing a decline from 1990 levels. However, the rate of decline slowed significantly between 2019 and 2021. Southern Latin America had the highest regional ASIR (484.32; 95% UI: 401.38–560.57), while Lesotho saw the greatest ASIR increase (EAPC = 0.70%). Health inequities worsened between 2019 and 2021 (−155.2892–28.0021), with the disease burden shifting towards lower Socio-demographic Index regions. Epidemiological changes contributed 573.43% to the global decrease in incidence. Occupational injuries were the leading risk factor. Projections suggest that from 2021 to 2050, the ASIR will decrease by 41.77%, while prevalence cases will increase slightly by 12.53%.While the overall epidemiological trends of fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injuries remain favorable, the observed plateau in nonfatal burden reduction during 2019 to 2021 underscores the need for more comprehensive and targeted healthcare policies. Moving forward, public health strategies must adopt an integrated “education-prevention-treatment” approach that specifically targets 3 high-risk groups: Children aged 0 to 14 years (developmental vulnerability); Older adults (age-related physiological risks); Populations in low-SDI regions (health system limitations). This demographic-specific framework will be essential for addressing the evolving epidemiology of thermal injuries in the coming decades.

1. Introduction

Fire, heat, and hot substances pose a multifaceted global health threat, causing both immediate injuries and chronic health consequences.[1–3] Thermal burns - tissue damage primarily caused by heat, radiation, electricity, or chemicals - represent just one aspect of this significant burden. Global estimates indicate approximately 8.4 million (95% uncertainty interval (UI): 6.5–10.4 million) burn injuries occurred in 2019, with the World Health Organization data revealing these exposures claim over 180,000 lives annually through burn injuries alone.[4,5] Beyond burns, these hazards contribute substantially to respiratory complications, cardiovascular stress, and mental health disorders, creating a cumulative strain on healthcare systems worldwide.[6–9] Despite advancements in burn management, including fluid resuscitation and surgical techniques, such exposures remain a leading cause of global Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs), particularly in resource-constrained regions.[10,11] The economic impact is equally severe, with fire-related injuries consistently ranking among the most financially burdensome medical conditions to treat.[12–15] These alarming statistics underscore the critical need for enhanced preventive measures and global health attention commensurate with the scale of this public health crisis.

While the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database offers comprehensive epidemiological data, current literature exhibits several critical limitations. First, existing studies predominantly concentrate on descriptive analyses of temporal trends in burn injuries, failing to investigate the complex relationships between disease burden variations and underlying socioeconomic determinants.[1,4,16] Second, the research scope remains narrowly focused on burn-specific outcomes, neglecting the broader spectrum of health impacts caused by fire, heat, and hot substances.[4] Most importantly, current evidence lacks predictive modeling of future disease burden trajectories, significantly diminishing its utility for evidence-based health policy formulation and preventive strategy development. These knowledge gaps collectively hinder the establishment of targeted, cost-effective interventions commensurate with the scale of this public health challenge.

Unlike previous studies, this research utilizes GBD data to analyze and assess the burden of fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injuries across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. For the first time, it projects the health burden trends over the next 30 years and focuses specifically on the differential changes during the COVID-19 pandemic period (2019–2021). By applying Joinpoint regression, health inequality models, and decomposition analysis, the study explores the underlying epidemiological drivers of these changes. The findings can help allocate healthcare resources more effectively to high-risk populations and regions, support the development of targeted prevention and treatment strategies, improve patient quality of life, reduce avoidable medical costs, and provide valuable insights for policymakers.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source

The GBDs, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study provides dynamic estimates of disease burden and up-to-date evidence to inform healthcare policy.[17] The latest iteration, GBD 2021, covers 371 diseases and injuries, 88 risk factors, and 204 countries and territories.[18,19] The data related to fire, heat, and hot substances in this study were sourced from the Global Health Data Exchange and its affiliated tools (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), using the search formula ‘Fire, heat, and hot substances’. We utilized the GBD tool to extract the following data: age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), Age-Standardized Mortality Rate (ASMR) and age-standardized disability-adjusted rate (ASDR) from 204 countries, 21 GBD regions, and 5 Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) level regions.[20] These data were extracted and stratified by cause, year, sex, age, SDI, country, and territory.[13]

In terms of age selection, we included “all ages” and “age-standardized” for analysis, as well as the following specific age ranges: 0 to 14 years, 15 to 19 years, 20 to 24 years, 25 to 29 years, 30 to 34 years, 35 to 39 years, 40 to 44 years, 45 to 49 years, 50 to 54 years, 55 to 59 years, 60 to 64 years, 65 to 69 years, 70 to 74 years, 75 to 79 years, 80 to 84 years, 85 + years. These datasets serve as indicators to comprehensively assess the burden from multiple perspectives. Additionally, we retrieved global population projection data for the period 2022 to 2050 from the GBD database to further assess the future trends related to fire, heat, and hazardous substances. This paper specifically addresses fire, heat, and hot substances in the traditional sense, namely fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injuries.

The SDI is a composite indicator that reflects a country’s level of development, based on lag-distributed income per capita, the average educational attainment of individuals aged over 15 years, and the total fertility rate among those under 25 years.[21] The national economic development level is categorized into 5 groups: low, medium-low, medium, high-medium, and high, SDI-based on the quintiles of the SDI value, ranging from 0 to 1. Additionally, we obtained global population forecast data for 2017 to 2100 from the GBD database (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/record/ihme-data/global-population-forecasts-2017-2100) to predict future trends of fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injuries.

2.2. Statistical analysis

2.2.1. ASRs

We calculated the ASIR, ASPR, ASDR, and ASMR, along with 95% UIs for each location and year. This was achieved by using the GBD World Standard Population as the reference for age standardization, which helped control for potential confounding caused by differences in age distributions.[18] The age-standardized rate (ASR) per 100,000 population was derived by multiplying the age-specific rates (ai) by the number of cases (or weight; wi) in each age group (i) of the reference standard population, summing these products, and then dividing by the total sum of the reference population’s numbers (or weights).[18] The formula is as follows:

2.2.2. Joinpoint regression

To elucidate the temporal trends, the Joinpoint Regression Program developed by the National Cancer Institute (Rockville, MD) was employed to calculate the annual percentage change (APC) along with the 95% confidence intervals (CI).[22,23] This rigorous statistical approach provides a nuanced understanding of how the disease burden has evolved. The terms “decrease” or “increase” were used when the slope (APC) of the trends was significantly different from zero, with a P-value <.05 considered statistically significant.[24] Sensitivity analysis was performed using Joinpoint version 5.3.0, available at https://surveillance.cancer.gov/joinpoint/.

2.2.3. Estimated annual percentage change

The Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC) was used to reflect the trend in ASR over a specific period. The 95% CI for the EAPC was calculated using a linear regression model.[25] To compute the EAPC, we utilized a linear regression model in which the natural logarithm of the ASR was regressed against time (calendar year). The trend in the ASR is interpreted as follows: if both the EAPC and the lower bound of its 95% CI are positive, the ASR is considered to be increasing; if both the EAPC and the upper bound of its 95% CI are negative, the ASR is deemed to be decreasing; and when the CIs overlap, the ASR is regarded as stable.[26]

2.2.4. Cross-country social inequalities analysis

The unequal distribution of fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injuries burden across countries is measured using the slope index of inequality (SII) and the health inequality concentration index, which represent absolute and relative gradient inequality, respectively.[27,28] The slope index was calculated by regressing national DALY rates against an SDI-associated relative position scale, defined by the midpoint of the cumulative population range ranked by SDI. Heteroskedasticity was addressed using a weighted regression model.[29] The concentration index was calculated by integrating the area under the Lorenz curve, fitted with the cumulative DALY fraction and the cumulative relative distribution of the population ranked by SDI. An absolute value of 0.2 to 0.3 is considered a high level of relative inequality.[28]

2.2.5. Bayesian age-period-cohort

The temporal trends were analyzed using a Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model to overcome the identifiability constraints inherent in traditional Age-Period-Cohort analyses. Model estimation was performed through the integrated nested Laplace Approximation algorithm, with all analyses implemented using the BAPC and integrated nested Laplace packages in R.[30–33]

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; Bell Laboratories, now part of Lucent Technologies), with two-sided P-values < .05 indicating statistical significance. Ethical approval was not required for this study as it is based entirely on anonymized and aggregated data from the GBD database. This analysis of de-identified data does not constitute human subjects’ research.

3. Results

3.1. Global disease burden caused by fire, heat, and hot substances

In 2021, a total of 9198,164 new cases caused by fire, heat, and hot substances (95% UI: 7592,028–10,784,350) were reported globally, with ASIR declining from 176.06 (95% UI: 226.87–320.38) to 118.82 (95% UI: 97.49–139.41) per 100,000 (EAPC: −1.54 [−1.62 to −1.47]; Table 1). Prevalence increased to 111.73 million (95% UI: 94.07–132.53 million) despite ASPR decreasing from 2117.26 to 1334.94. Mortality cases fell to 117,406 (95% UI: 92,583–135,184), with ASMR showing a significant reduction from 2.74 to 1.48 (EAPC: −2.18 [-2.33–−2.04]; Tables 2 and 3).

3.7. Risk factor of burden caused by fire, heat, and hot substances

In 2021, globally, fire, heat, and hot substances were primarily related to 2 risk factors: occupational injuries (87.75%) and alcohol use (12.25%) (Fig. S5, Supplemental Digital Content 8, Supplemental Digital Content, Supplemental Digital Content 4). There is a general trend where higher SDI areas have a lower proportion of occupational injuries. The High-SDI region was 71.48%, while the lowest-middle SDI region had the highest proportion at 93.95%. Longitudinal analysis from 1990 to 2021 revealed a steady decline in ASDR of 2 risk factors globally (Fig. S6, Supplemental Digital Content 9, Supplemental Digital Content, Supplemental Digital Content 4). EAPC for alcohol use was −2.56 (95% CI: −2.81–−2.31), and for occupational injuries, it was −2.89 (95% CI: −3.01–−2.77) (Table 4). Within the SDI regions, both high-SDI and high-middle SDI areas showed significantly higher ASDR due to alcohol use compared to other regions, while occupational injuries were markedly lower. Overall, both alcohol use and occupational injuries exhibited a downward trend across 21 regions, with some fluctuations observed in specific areas. For instance, in the Caribbean region, the ASDR caused by occupational injuries increased steadily between 2003 and 2011. Similarly, in Eastern Europe, the ASDR due to alcohol use increased steadily between 1999 and 2003.

4. Discussion

This study, based on the GBD database, comprehensively investigates the global burden of fire, heat, and hot substances, which continue to pose substantial health risks to global health systems.

Previous studies have often been limited to macro-level descriptions, analyzing data from perspectives including region, gender, age, time, and SDI.[1,4,16] However, they have largely overlooked the underlying causes of these changes, focusing primarily on broad patterns rather than in-depth exploration of the driving factors behind the variations in disease burden. Our study first goes beyond these limitations by employing advanced methodologies such as joinpoint regression, health inequality models, and decomposition analysis to provide a scientific explanation for the shifts in disease burden across regions. By incorporating risk factor analysis, we aim to uncover the intricate relationships between disease burden changes and variables such as time, SDI, and regional distribution. Furthermore, we utilized the BAPC model to predict the global burden from 2022 to 2050, offering valuable insights into future trajectories and potential risks. The findings from this study emphasize the critical importance of addressing these pressing health issues through targeted interventions and policy measures. Our research provides a more granular understanding of the factors contributing to the global burden of fire, heat, and hot substances, making it a significant contribution to the field and offering a more nuanced approach to combating these health risks in the future.

This study finds that while the ASMR related to fire, heat, and thermal substances has continuously declined from 1990 to 2021, the rate of decline in the burden of nonfatal diseases has slowed in recent years, reflecting an evolving epidemiological pattern. This differential trend may be attributed to several factors: Improved prevention and treatment levels alongside the relative increase in nonfatal burdens. In some regions, significant progress in burn and scald prevention and emergency treatment has led to a marked reduction in mortality. However, with improved survival rates, the reduction in the burden of nonfatal diseases has slowed. Survivors often face long-term sequelae such as chronic pain, psychological trauma, and functional disabilities.[5,34] The GBD 2021 study indicated that the pandemic significantly worsened healthcare accessibility in low-SDI regions, which may have worsened the prognosis of nonfatal burns and other traumatic diseases.[35–37] A survey in 237 African cities found that treatment delays (78.2%) were the leading cause of poor outcomes, with severe shortages of intensive care resources and protective education.[38] The compounded impact of the pandemic and existing resource limitations has hindered the reduction of the nonfatal disease burden. Clinical management of burns in the elderly presents unique challenges. While pediatric burn treatments have seen significant progress with early escharotomy, skin grafting, and metabolic regulation, elderly patients experience longer recovery periods and higher risks of shock and deep burns due to physiological changes such as impaired fluid homeostasis and dermal thinning.[34,39–42] Furthermore, the lack of specific surgical guidelines for the elderly, combined with anesthesia risks, complicates their treatment and rehabilitation.[43–46] As global aging accelerates, it is urgent to develop targeted safety prevention measures, treatment standards, and rehabilitation systems for the elderly.

According to the decomposition analysis, epidemiological changes were the principal driver behind the declining disease burden.[47–49] This substantial contribution highlights improved clinical management, particularly in high-SDI regions where advanced burn care protocols and preventive education have been widely implemented.[50–53] However, population growth exerted countervailing pressure, especially in Eastern/Western Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where it offset potential gains from epidemiological improvements. These findings underscore that socioeconomic development and healthcare infrastructure critically determine incidence patterns, suggesting low-SDI regions require targeted prevention strategies to break this stagnation.[54–58]

Longitudinal risk factor analysis highlights that occupational injuries are the primary contributor to DALYs across all regions, far exceeding the impact of alcohol-related factors. While middle and high-SDI regions have made significant progress in reducing occupational injury burdens, low-SDI regions show limited progress, reflecting deficiencies in workplace safety regulations and training.[59,60] In high-SDI regions, the implementation of workplace safety standards has been linked to notable reductions in occupational burn injuries.[61] On the other hand, alcohol-related disease burdens have decreased in high-SDI regions due to stringent control policies, but remain stable in low-SDI areas where such measures are often absent.[62,63] These trends emphasize the importance of enhancing occupational safety standards and implementing targeted prevention strategies to reduce fire/heat-related burdens in low-SDI settings.[64,65]

Males are more frequently exposed to occupational hazards, with 68.7% (95% CI: 65.2–72.1) of work-related burns occurring in industrial, construction, and agricultural sectors.[66,67] High-risk behaviors, such as handling hazardous materials, further amplify their susceptibility to burns.[68,69] In contrast, females face disproportionate domestic exposures, with cooking-related burns accounting for 42.3% of female DALYs in low- and middle-income countries.[55] Notably, the rise in female labor force participation has contributed to an increased risk of occupational burns, especially in manufacturing sectors. Children (ages 0–14) demonstrate higher incidence rates due to developmental factors, including limited risk perception and inadequate supervision, which contribute to 58.9% of pediatric cases.[70] Older adults (60+) exhibit higher mortality rates compared to younger age groups, reflecting age-related physiological decline and impaired wound healing capabilities.[71] These findings underscore the urgent need for demographic-tailored interventions. For males, enhancing workplace safety programs, particularly in male-dominated industries, is critical. For females, modifying kitchen designs to reduce cooking-related burns and providing preventive education on domestic safety are essential. Additionally, childproofing initiatives and specialized geriatric burn protocols are necessary to address the distinct needs of children and older adults, respectively.

The BAPC model predicts that this epidemiological transition presents 2 key challenges: the slowing reduction in nonfatal burden calls for improvements in healthcare infrastructure and more targeted public health policies, while the persistently high incidence among older adults (60+) jeopardizes the achievement of the World Health Organization 2030 targets. Three critical interventions emerge: the development of geriatric-specific prevention protocols addressing age-related vulnerabilities, occupational safety reforms in low-SDI regions to reduce workplace injuries, and equitable resource allocation to strengthen emergency care capacity and safety education. These measures must be regionally adapted to effectively mitigate health inequalities.[5]

This study represents the first comprehensive application of joinpoint regression analysis, health inequality modeling, and decomposition methods to systematically investigate the spatiotemporal patterns and underlying drivers of fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injury burden across different regions. Our findings indicate a notable deceleration in the rate of reduction for both ASIR and ASPR between 2019 and 2021, alongside an increase in nonfatal disease burden. This trend may be attributed to advancements in medical technology over the past 3 decades, which have increased survival rates for fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injuries, compounded by the COVID-19 pandemic, which has exacerbated healthcare inaccessibility and health inequalities, as well as challenges in preventing such injuries among the elderly. Moreover, the more rapid decline in disease burden in high-SDI regions is linked to better healthcare treatment levels and more comprehensive safety education. Males face a more severe burden of fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injuries.

The innovative analytical framework provides robust epidemiological evidence to inform targeted prevention strategies. However, several limitations warrant consideration. When interpreting global injury burden trends, it is crucial to acknowledge the widespread underreporting and data gaps for injury incidents in low-SDI regions. The generally poor quality of raw data from these areas primarily stems from weak health monitoring systems (e.g., a lack of healthcare facilities and professionals in remote areas), incomplete official reporting mechanisms, and potential underreporting due to socio-cultural factors. Consequently, data from low-SDI countries in the GBD database may contain significant incompleteness and measurement errors. These systemic data defects concretely impact trend analyses: firstly, they lead to an overall underestimation of the true incidence of injuries, potentially masking the actual disease burden; secondly, incomplete data or fluctuations in reporting rates over time can cause misinterpretation of trends. For instance, an apparent increase in recorded cases might reflect temporary improvements in reporting systems rather than a genuine rise in injury incidence. Therefore, the observed trends in this study, particularly for low-SDI regions, should be interpreted as a conservative estimate of the true situation under existing data limitations, highlighting the urgent need to strengthen injury surveillance and data collection capacities in these areas.

5. Conclusions

Despite generally declining trends in fire-, heat-, and hot substance-related injuries, the plateau in nonfatal burden reduction during 2019 to 2021 highlights the need for more comprehensive and targeted healthcare policies. The projected increase in prevalence cases highlights the dual imperative of both reducing mortality and improving quality of life for survivors through enhanced treatment protocols. The pandemic-induced exacerbation of health inequalities serves as a critical reminder to strengthen global preparedness for public health emergencies. Particular attention must be paid to low-SDI regions, where improvements in occupational safety standards, workplace education, and emergency care capacity are urgently needed to address the disproportionate burden of work-related injuries. Notably, the rising incidence among older adults (60+) signals an emerging challenge, as aging populations face unique vulnerabilities to these injuries. Moving forward, public health strategies must adopt an integrated “education-prevention-treatment” approach that specifically targets 3 high-risk groups: Children aged 0 to 14 years (developmental vulnerability); Older adults (age-related physiological risks); Populations in low-SDI regions (health system limitations). This demographic-specific framework will be essential for addressing the evolving epidemiology of thermal injuries in the coming decades.
