The effect of musculoskeletal health literacy on 90-day emergency department return, readmissions, and post-operative adherence following primary total shoulder arthroplasty
Authors: Cristian Escalera, Noah C. Stevens, Qutaiba Alradawneh, Savino Stallone, Yungtai Lo, Konrad I. Gruson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of limited health literacy on patient adherence with post-operative instructions, in addition to the utilization of health care resources following elective shoulder surgery, remains discordant within the existing literature.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the electronic health records for patients who underwent elective total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) at a single institution. Baseline demographics, including age, gender, prior ipsilateral or contralateral shoulder surgery, implant type, and pre-operative emergency department (ED) visit within 12 months, were collected. Musculoskeletal health literacy (MHL) was assessed using the validated 9-item Literacy in Musculoskeletal Problems questionnaire. All-cause 90-day ED return and readmission was tabulated. Post-operative adherence with return for follow-up and adherence with surgeon instructions regarding motion and sling use was prospectively recorded.ResultsThere were 226 cases included, of which 148 (65%) were female. The median age for the cohort was 67.5 years (interquartile range: 60, 73) and 42 (19%) patients had undergone a prior contralateral TSA. Sixty (27%) patients demonstrated adequate MHL. On multivariable analysis, limited MHL (LMHL) demonstrated a significant association with 90-day post-operative ED return (odds ratio: 3.59, 95% confidence interval: 1.17-10.95, P = .025). Health literacy, however, was not associated with 90-day all-cause readmissions, overall adherence, or adherence with post-operative surgeon instructions regarding shoulder motion or sling use (all P > .05).ConclusionThe prevalence of LMHL prior to elective TSA remains high. Assessing LMHL prior to elective TSA could reduce unnecessary health care utilization patterns including the risk for post-operative 90-day ED return.

Health literacy represents the capacity of an individual to obtain, process, and understand basic information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions.4 Prior studies have demonstrated that less than half of American adults possess the skills necessary to make informed health-related decisions.4,5 Furthermore, musculoskeletal literacy has been reported to be lower than general health literacy among patients seeking orthopedic care and may impede a patient's communication with their orthopedic provider.16 Puzzitiello et al14 found that 25% of patients undergoing primary total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) demonstrated limited health literacy (LHL) using the 4-item Brief Health Literacy Screening Tool. In a retrospective study examining elective shoulder arthroscopy using a validated musculoskeletal health literacy (MHL) screening tool, the authors reported a far higher prevalence of inadequate MHL at 69%.20 Risk factors identified for inadequate MHL include older age, lower income levels, and lower levels of education achieved.13

Although likely procedure specific, patient adherence with post-operative instructions, aftercare follow-up, and activity restrictions undoubtedly plays an integral role in improving clinical outcomes and a reduction in potential complications.9,17,22 Smith et al20 found no significant association between limited MHL (LMHL) and post-operative adherence with premature self-directed shoulder motion, sling use, or attendance of <50% of prescribed therapy visits within 90 days post-operatively following shoulder arthroscopy. Furthermore, the authors in that study found no relationship between MHL and all-cause 90-day emergency department (ED). Other authors reported a 46% nonadherence risk for rehabilitation protocols following lower extremity surgery, though they could not identify any independent patient demographic predictors, including educational status.1 To reduce health care expenditures in an environment of limited resources, efforts at mitigating potentially unnecessary post-operative utilization patterns during the episode of care should be undertaken. Puzzitiello et al14 found that patients with LHL undergoing primary TSA had a significantly longer hospital length of stay, though the mean difference was not considered clinically relevant. In contrast, there was no association of LHL with inpatient opioid consumption, inpatient costs, or discharge disposition. Finally, in a study examining patients seeking both surgical and nonsurgical elective spine care for chronic lower back pain, the authors found that patients with LHL were more likely to utilize medications for chronic back pain, more likely to seek chiropractic care, and less likely to seek out specialist treatment.6 However, regression analysis was not performed making it difficult to determine whether other confounders played a role in the reported findings. The discordant results suggest that other patient- or disease-related factors more directly impact resource utilization patterns compared with health literacy.

Therefore, the purpose of the current study is to determine the association between LMHL and: (1) all-cause 90-day post-operative ED return; (2) 90-day readmission; (3) adherence with post-operative follow-up; and (4) adherence with post-operative restrictions regarding sling use and early shoulder motion following primary TSA. A secondary aim was to determine any patient- or surgical-related risk factors predictive of the above outcome measures. We hypothesized that patients with LMHL would experience a higher risk for all-cause 90-day ED return/readmissions and demonstrate worse adherence with post-operative instructions.

Materials and methods

A retrospective analysis of all elective primary anatomic and reverse TSA performed by a single fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeon between July 2019 and August 2025 at a single tertiary-referral teaching hospital was performed. Surgical cases from February 2020 through December 2020 were excluded given the confounding effects of the COVID pandemic. Institutional review board approval was obtained [#2022-14383] for the study. Inclusion criteria included patients who underwent an elective TSA with a minimum 90-day follow-up period. Patients with incomplete demographic data were excluded.

The electronic health record (EHR) was used to extract baseline patient demographic data, including gender, age, self-identified race, marital status, distance from home-to-clinic, body mass index, documented depression and anxiety, insurance type (Medicaid vs. Medicare vs. commercial vs. workers compensation), smoking status (never vs. former vs. current), age-adjusted Charlson-Deyo Comorbidity Index, and any patient visit to the ED within 12 months prior to shoulder arthroplasty. Sociodemographic status was determined via the state and national Area Deprivation Index with lower values representing lower levels of socioeconomic disadvantage. The 5-item modified fragility index, a concise comorbidity-based tool for predicting post-operative outcomes, was tabulated.19 All patients completed the Literacy in Musculoskeletal Problems questionnaire, a validated nine-item multiple-choice survey assessing patient knowledge of musculoskeletal anatomy, conditions, and terminology during their final presurgical visit, which took place 1-2 weeks prior to the scheduled surgery. A score ≥ 6 denotes adequate MHL (AMHL) and a score < 6 indicates LMHL.

Each patient indicated for shoulder arthroplasty received pre-operative education by a certified health education specialist (CHES). The CHES designation represents advanced certification beyond a bachelor's degree, and that person develops and implements educational programs for patients to maximize their musculoskeletal health and well-being. Starting with the initial evaluation, after the patient has made the informed decision to proceed with elective TSA, the specific phases of the perioperative course were outlined for the patient and family members to improve active engagement. Decision aids, such as models and sample radiographic images of TSA, were presented to the patient. The discussion included: (1) the duration of use and positioning of the abduction sling; (2) the timing and progression of post-operative physical therapy (PT); and (3) the timing of post-operative outpatient visits. To promote communication during the post-operative period, the patient and family member was provided with direct contact information for the CHES should any urgent issue arise. Specifically with regards to PT, each phase of therapy was discussed with the patient both pre-operatively and at each subsequent follow-up visit. A therapy referral reflecting the surgeon-specific instructions was provided for the therapist. Early post-operative follow-up visits were scheduled within 1 week after surgery, at 3-4 weeks, at 6 weeks when sling use was discontinued, and at 12 weeks.

Clinical outcome measures

Ninety-day emergency department return/readmission

The EHR was queried to determine whether the patient presented to the ED within 90 days of surgery for any reason. Each visit was broadly categorized as either medical or surgical in nature, and its timing relative to surgery was recorded. Surgical reasons involved surgical site complications such as drainage or pain, whereas medical reasons were denoted by the organ system affected. Furthermore, all-cause readmissions within 90 days were recorded.

In the current study, the reasons for nonadherence were categorized broadly as: (1) lack of sling use; (2) early active operative shoulder motion despite surgeon instructions; (3) attendance at formal PT <50% of scheduled visits; and (4) performance of exercises with the therapist discordant with written referral and verbal surgeon instructions to the patient.

Adherence with sling use/movement restrictions

At each post-operative visit, patients were specifically queried and observed as to whether they were wearing their sling as instructed and whether they were compliant with the surgeon-directed motion restrictions of the operative shoulder. Patient removal of the abduction portion of the sling was recorded; however, for the purpose of this study, this was not considered nonadherence.

Adherence with physical therapy

Patients asked to provide the surgeon with paperwork related to attendance at PT. If the percentage of visits attended vs. the total prescribed was <50%, the patient was denoted as noncompliant. In addition, at each follow-up visit after therapy was initiated, patients were asked to demonstrate which specific exercises they were performing and from what position (supine/standing). These responses were then compared with the written PT referral provided and the therapist progress notes. This comparison was performed to minimize patient recall bias. Any deviation from the written therapy instructions was categorized as nonadherence.

Statistical analysis

Differences in demographic and medical characteristics between patients with MHL score ≥ 6 and < 6 were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and chi-squared or Fishers exact tests for categorical variables. Logistic regression was used to identify variables associated with ED visit within 90 days after surgery, overall adherence, adherence with post-operative visits only, adherence with sling use/premature shoulder motion only, and adherence with written instructions to the therapist only. Variables with a P value <.25 in initial analysis were considered candidates for multiple logistic regression. Final models retained only variables that remained significant at the P < .05 significance level, except for MHL, which was included a priori. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Ninety-day return to emergency department

There were 38 (16.8%) patients who experienced a 90-day return to the ED, comprising 49 total visits. Of the total visits, 6 occurred within 7 days post-operatively, 21 occurred between 8 and 30 days post-operatively, and 22 occurred between 31 and 90 days after surgery. There were significantly more medically-related ED returns occurring between 31 and 90 days compared with ≤7 days (P < .005). Patients with LMHL demonstrated significantly increased independent risk for all-cause 90-day ED return (odds ratio [OR]: 3.57, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.17-10.91, P = .026). Depression demonstrated a significant association with 90-day ED return on bivariate regression analysis (OR: 2.25, 95% CI: 1.09-4.63, P = .029), though this relationship no longer held following multivariable regression. Ninety-day readmission

There were 15 (6.6%) all-cause 90-day readmissions that occurred. There was no significant association found between MHL and 90-day readmissions (P = .125).

Overall post-operative adherence

There were 129 (57.1%) patients who demonstrated overall nonadherence in the 90-day post-operative period. Of these, 43 (33.3%) patients demonstrated nonadherence in more than one category. Of the remainder, 8 patients (6.2%) were deemed noncompliant solely with regards to sling use, 12 patients (9.3%) initiated early shoulder motion despite surgeon-placed restrictions, 20 (15.5%) participated in <50% of prescribed PT visits, 23 (17.8%) performed exercises during therapy that were incongruent with the written referral and in-office education, and 23 (17.8%) missed a scheduled post-operative visit. Depression was significantly associated with lower overall post-operative adherence (OR: 0.33, 95% CI: 0.16-0.68, P = .003), while being married (OR: 2.44, 95% CI: 1.33-4.45, P = .004) was associated with greater post-operative adherence. Adherence with sling use/early shoulder motion

A total of 22 (9.7%) patients reported nonadherence with sling use, of whom 14 patients also demonstrated concomitant adherence issues with other post-operative restrictions. Of note, 29 patients did not adhere to instructions specifically regarding use of the abduction portion of the sling. There were 31 (13.7%) patients who performed early shoulder motion that was discordant with surgeon instructions, of whom 12 cases were isolated instances of nonadherence. Discussion

The major findings of this single-institution study were that LMHL and presentation to the ED within 12 months prior to elective TSA were significantly associated with 90-day all-cause return to ED. However, LMHL was not associated with overall post-operative adherence, in addition to adherence with restrictions with regards to sling use, early active shoulder use, or adherence to surgeon-imposed PT protocols.

We found that LMHL was independently associated with 90-day all-cause ED return following elective TSA, representing increased health care resource utilization tendencies. Furthermore, LMHL was not found to correlate with all-cause 90-day readmissions. Shahid et al18 reported that patients with inadequate health literacy on general internal medicine units were 3 times more likely to visit the ED within 90 days of discharge, even after controlling for multiple patient demographics. Furthermore, the authors similarly found no association between health literacy and readmissions. Griffey et al,7 in a study utilizing the same health literacy assessment tool, found that patients with inadequate health literacy had a nearly 2-fold higher ED utilization than those with adequate health literacy and were more likely to have at least one return ED visit within 14 days following an initial ED presentation. The discordance between the relationship of health literacy with ED return vs. readmissions may reflect the inability of a patient with LMHL to discern between expected postsurgical symptoms and urgent perioperative issues, difficulty in understanding discharge instructions, and/or a reduced ability to navigate the pathway for reaching the orthopedic team as opposed to true post-operative surgical complications. As such, there was no observed concomitant increase in readmissions. Of the 3 surgical 90-day ED returns within 7 days, 1 was related to shoulder pain, 1 was for assistance adjusting the sling, and 1 dealt with a persistent facial droop thought to be related to the interscalene catheter that had been noted prior to discharge. These patients all demonstrated a Literacy in Musculoskeletal Problems score <6. Smith et al,20 however, reported no relationship between MHL and 90-day ED return following elective shoulder arthroscopy. In that study, the authors identified depression and/or anxiety, in addition to having ≥ 3 visits to the ED within 12 months pre-operatively, as independent predictors of 90-day ED return. Another study on elective TSA found that a history of anxiety, development of an ipsilateral post-operative complication, or an ED visit within 12 months prior to surgery were independently predictive of 90-day ED return.19 The consistent findings of a prior ED visit within 12 months of surgery are concordant with the current study, whereas mental health diagnoses were no longer predictive in the current study following regression analysis. Routine pre-operative assessment of MHL and identification of patients with pre-operative ED visits may help to reduce the risk for potentially unnecessary 90-day ED return.

In the current study, there was no correlation found between MHL and overall post-operative adherence. However, patients with a diagnosis of depression were 76% less likely to remain compliant following elective TSA. In contrast, in a recent meta-analysis of acute and chronic illness, Miller et al11 found that health literacy correlated significantly with treatment adherence. Further analysis suggested that the relationship between health literacy and adherence was greater in nonmedication regimens, such as in lifestyle or exercise as those behaviors involved instructions that required greater health literacy. The authors suggested that teaching methods involving reinforcement could potentially mitigate this negative effect. Similarly, Qin et al15 found that patients with LHL were less likely to adhere to Enhanced Recovery after Surgery protocols for perioperative care regarding colorectal surgery. While we did not formally assess the impact of the CHES on adherence in this study, we believe that the extensive engagement and education of the patients both pre- and post-operatively with regards to the importance of sling use and timely therapy, irrespective of MHL, likely played a role in our findings. Kadakia et al8 found an overall limited comprehension of the injury sustained, procedure performed, and post-operative instructions following orthopedic trauma surgery. The authors reported worse comprehension amongst patients with lower educational level achieved. A subsequent study determined that text and pictorial interventions resulted in improvement in post-discharge instruction comprehension compared with verbal instructions alone and recommended standardized instructions pre-operatively with consistent reinforcement post-operatively.21

We found no significant association between MHL and post-operative adherence with follow-up visit attendance. Furthermore, a longer distance from home-to-clinic and a diagnosis of depression were predictive of decreased adherence with follow-up, whereas being married resulted in greater adherence. Interestingly, in a study assessing the time spent seeking outpatient care from a hand specialist, Alokozai et al2 found that LHL was not independently associated with total waiting time, travel time, face-to-face time, and booking-to-appointment time. The authors attributed this to the increased efficacy of electronic health systems allowing providers to more easily contact patients to remind them of upcoming visits, suggesting that patients with LHL can navigate the complexities of the health system. Bender et al,3 in a retrospective study examining adherence with post-operative follow-up visits following trauma surgery, found that male gender, African American race, and traveling >70 miles to clinic were some of the independent noninsurance-related risk factors for missing either the first or second post-operative visit. While there is concordance between our studies with regards to the relationship between increased distance from clinic and greater nonadherence with post-operative visit attendance, they did not include health literacy or diagnosis of pre-operative mental health issues as predictors. A prior study found that a diagnosis of probable depression was an independent risk factor for missing clinic appointments.12 With regards to marital status, we surmise that a patient's partner can assist with practical means of improving adherence, including reminders regarding appointments and helping arrange transportation. Abraham et al1 reported that being married was significantly associated with adherence with appointment follow-up following lower extremity orthopedic surgery, though the relationship did not remain upon multivariable analysis. Pre-operative identification and education of those patients with diagnosed depression and those who have longer travel distances should be undertaken given the potential risk for inferior clinical outcomes in the setting of missed early post-operative follow-up visits after elective TSA.

We found no significant association between LMHL and adherence with either self-reported sling use or with surgeon instructions regarding early active motion of the surgical shoulder. Smith et al20 similarly demonstrated that LMHL was not correlated with overall adherence after shoulder arthroscopy, although the outcome measures in that study included but did not specifically isolate sling use. Our overall self-reported nonadherence prevalence with sling use was approximately 10%. In a study utilizing heat sensors in the sling to objectively measure sling use, Weir et al22 reported a far higher nonadherence prevalence of 55% and demonstrated that patient self-reported adherence to sling use was unreliable and only weakly associated with actual sling use. While the use of self-reporting may have affected actual sling use determination in our study, patients were scheduled for follow-up 3 times within the first 6 weeks post-operatively, providing multiple opportunities during which to query them or observe nonadherence regarding sling use. Weir et al22 further noted that male patients and those meeting criteria for obesity and morbid obesity were significantly more likely to be nonadherent with sling use. The authors published another study demonstrating that patient understanding with regards to the necessity for sling use resulted in significantly higher likelihood of adherence.10 In addition, the authors found that age ≥60 years was associated with greater adherence, similar to our results demonstrating a 5% increased adherence with each year of age.

Limitations to our current study include those inherent to performing a single-institution retrospective review. As such, our study design may limit generalizability to other practice environments with different patient populations, perioperative workflows, or access to post-operative resources. Specifically, our standard practice pre-operative education may have positively influenced post-operative adherence, particularly amongst patients with LMHL. However, this study was not designed to answer that complex clinical question. In addition, although ED encounters within our institution are captured through EHR, patients may have sought post-operative care at outside facilities, potentially leading to an underestimation of 90-day ED utilization or readmissions. Assessment of post-operative adherence relied on documentation within the EHR and patient self-reporting during follow-up visits. As a result, misclassification is possible if patients inaccurately recalled their post-operative behaviors. Specifically, patients may have over-reported adherence to post-operative restrictions due to social desirability, particularly in interactions with their treating orthopedic surgeon. While we adjusted for a broad range of demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical variables, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and unmeasured factors, such as caregiver support and understanding of post-operative instructions, may have influenced outcomes. Despite these limitations, this study is strengthened by the use of a validated MHL assessment, detailed characterization of post-operative adherence across multiple domains, and inclusion of relevant socioeconomic and clinical covariates.

Conclusion

AMHL is imperative for effective shared decision-making and achieving satisfactory clinical outcomes following elective TSA. The significant association between both LMHL and an ED visit within 12 months prior to TSA with 90-day ED return suggests that pre-operative identification of these patients is crucial to minimize this negative clinical outcome metric, particularly for those that are surgery-related. Further research into specific interventions aimed at mitigating LMHL through pre-operative education and post-operative reinforcement, such as the role of a CHES, is warranted.
