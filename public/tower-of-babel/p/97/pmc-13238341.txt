An Unexpected Culprit: Allergic Contact Dermatitis Caused by Benzyl Alcohol in Moist Toilet Paper
Authors: Lucía Martínez Rozas, Marta Isabel Andreu Barasoain, Enrique Gómez de la Fuente, José Luis López Estebaranz
Published: 2026

Case Report

A 53‐year‐old right‐handed woman with no relevant medical or dermatological history was referred to our dermatology department due to a one‐year history of bilateral hand eczema, predominantly affecting the left hand (Figure 1). She worked as a teacher and denied occupational or recreational exposure to chemical substances, as well as any recent changes in cosmetic or hygiene products. Despite using various topical corticosteroids, she experienced only partial improvement, with rapid recurrence of symptoms upon treatment discontinuation. Notably, her condition worsened with certain commercial formulations of methylprednisolone aceponate.

Clinical photograph of the patient showing bilateral hand eczema, predominantly affecting the left hand.

Given the clinical presentation, patch testing was performed using the Spanish Contact Dermatitis and Skin Allergy Research Group (GEIDAC) standard and cosmetic series. At the 96‐h reading, positive reactions were observed to methylchloroisothiazolinone/isothiazolinone (++), methylisothiazolinone (++) and butylhydroquinone (++). Additionally, doubtful positive reactions were recorded for Peru balsam and benzyl alcohol (Figure 2). The patient was informed of the results and provided with educational materials to facilitate allergen avoidance.

Results of patch testing with the Spanish Contact Dermatitis and Skin Allergy Research Group (GEIDAC) standard and cosmetic series. At the 96‐h reading, positive reactions were observed to methylchloroisothiazolinone/isothiazolinone (++), methylisothiazolinone (++) and butylhydroquinone (++). Additionally, doubtful positive reactions were recorded for Peru balsam and benzyl alcohol.

Two months later, the patient reported complete remission after identifying benzyl alcohol in the moist toilet paper she had used habitually for perianal hygiene (held with her left hand) and occasionally for hand cleaning. Its discontinuation also led to the resolution of a concurrent, longstanding pruritus previously misattributed to hemorrhoidal disease.

No other hygiene or cosmetic products used by the patient contained the sensitising allergens identified in the patch test. Furthermore, it was confirmed that the methylprednisolone aceponate formulations that exacerbated her symptoms contained benzyl alcohol as an excipient. A repeated open application test using the moist toilet wipes applied to the forearm over 1 week produced a positive reaction, confirming allergic contact dermatitis (ACD) due to benzyl alcohol.

Discussion

Benzyl alcohol is an aromatic alcohol naturally present in certain plants, essential oils and derivatives such as Peru balsam and propolis [1]. It is commonly used as a fragrance, preservative, local anaesthetic and solvent in various topical and pharmaceutical preparations [1]. Although widely used, benzyl alcohol sensitisation is rare, seen in around 0.2% of patch‐tested patients. Moreover, most reactions to benzyl alcohol are weak, doubtful or irritant‐like, making it a diagnostically challenging allergen [2].

Sensitisation typically occurs in patients with chronic dermatoses (e.g., stasis dermatitis) exposed to topical medical products like corticosteroids, antifungals, antibiotics and antiseptics. Compromised skin barriers, especially on the lower limbs [1, 3] facilitate allergen penetration and sensitisation to related substances such as Peru balsam, fragrance mixes and propolis. Sensitisation from self‐tanning products [4], hair dyes [5] and botulinum toxin saline [6] is rare, but has also been reported.

In recent years, the increasing use of moist wipes has been associated with rising cases of ACD, especially affecting the anogenital region. Methylisothiazolinone and its derivatives are the most commonly implicated allergens in such products [7]. To date, no reports have been found in the literature describing sensitisation to hygiene wipes in which benzyl alcohol is the causative allergen.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of ACD due to benzyl alcohol in moist toilet paper. This case represents a previously undocumented clinical manifestation of sensitisation to a problematic allergen such as benzyl alcohol. This highlights the importance of thorough patient counselling regarding patch test results and appropriate follow‐up.
