Engineering properties and microscopic mechanism of phosphogypsum-rubber composite cemented soil
Authors: Qiang Ma, Yuezhao Li, Hang Shu, Zhongnan Song, Jiwei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the low early strength, brittleness, and poor crack resistance of traditional cement-stabilized soil, this study proposed a novel phosphogypsum-rubber composite cemented soil material. The effects of phosphogypsum (PG) and rubber (R), alone and in combination, on compaction characteristics, unconfined compressive strength, and permeability of cemented soil were investigated. Additionally, the synergistic mechanism was further elucidated from a microscopic perspective through the combination of X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) tests. The comparative analysis indicated that the composite material outperforms the individual components in terms of strength, toughness, and impermeability, demonstrating a unique complementary effect. Specifically, at a cement content of 8%, the addition of 25%PG enhanced the early strength development and overall compressive strength, while the incorporation of 1%R-2%R effectively reduced the maximum dry density, improving toughness and impermeability. The microscopic observations disclosed that the formation of C-S-H gel and ettringite(AFt) played a critical role in improving the mechanical and durability properties. This study effectively addresses the limitations of traditional cement-stabilized soil, offering an innovative and sustainable strategy for reutilizing industrial solid waste, thereby promoting mechanical efficiency and environmental benefits.Keywords: Phosphogypsum, Rubber particles, Cement-stabilized soil, Compressive strength, PermeabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

In geotechnical engineering, the growing focus on sustainability has increased the demand for innovative materials. Cement soil, produced by mixing poor-quality soils with cement, is valued for its mechanical properties and cost-effectiveness. It is widely used in infrastructure construction due to its ease of construction, high strength, and durability1,2, with applications in roadbeds3, foundations4, and earthwork blocks5. However, traditional cement soil suffers from low early strength, poor impact resistance, and limited crack resistance and flexibility6,7, issues that worsen under water immersion. To overcome these limitations, research is focused on optimizing its composition with additional components.

The disposal of waste tires has become a pressing environmental issue, with approximately 1 billion tires reaching the end of their lifecycle annually, a number expected to rise to 1.2 billion by 20308. Improperly disposed tires occupy large land areas and pose environmental and health risks, such as fires releasing toxic gases. Introducing rubber into cement soil represents a promising solution, simultaneously addressing waste tire disposal and improving the toughness and impact resistance of cement soil. Compared with traditional cement soil, the rubber-cement-soil composites exhibit advantageous properties, including low density, good crack resistance, and strong frost resistance9,10. Yadav and Tiwari11 highlighted that rubber-cement-soil composites containing 6% cement and 7.5% rubber fiber are suitable for applications such as fill materials, retaining wall backfills, and small-scale traffic road bases. Wang et al.12 evaluated the dynamic properties of rubber-cement-soil through resonant column tests, finding that increased rubber content reduced the shear modulus while enhancing the damping ratio, resulting in improved dynamic performance compared to untreated soil. Akbarimehr et al.13 examined clay-rubber composites with varying rubber forms and contents, reporting that rubber reduced dry density and peak strength while markedly enhancing ductility and post-peak deformation. Shear tests indicated that an optimal rubber content can preserve or slightly increase shear strength under saturated conditions, depending on rubber geometry and soil-rubber interactions. Wang et al.14 demonstrated that incorporating rubber particles into cement-stabilized soil enhances erosion resistance in chloride and sulfate environments by blocking capillary action, providing elastic buffering, and inhibiting crack propagation. Appropriate rubber content was shown to reduce mass loss, limit ion penetration, and delay strength degradation, improving the durability of cement-soil. Similarly, Al-Subari et al.15 found that replacing cement with rubber fibers increased strength, stiffness, and ductility by 12–15% at 2.5% rubber content. Zheng et al.17 examined the freeze-thaw resistance of rubber-modified cement soil and reported that although the maximum dry density and unconfined compressive strength decreased with rubber addition, freeze resistance improved significantly. However, excessive rubber can reduce strength and impermeability, limiting broader applications. For example, Cabalar et al.17 observed reductions in density, strength, and California Bearing Ratio (CBR) with added rubber in clay-lime mixtures, and Ravichandran et al.18 studied the permeability of clay soils containing 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% rubber particles, finding that permeability increased proportionally with rubber content. Balancing enhanced properties with potential drawbacks remains key to optimizing rubber-cement-soil systems.

Phosphogypsum, a by-product of wet-process phosphoric acid production, generates over 10 million tons globally each year19. Its accumulation occupies land and poses risks to soil and water quality, prompting interest in its comprehensive utilization. In geotechnical engineering, phosphogypsum-based composites have been widely studied to improve suboptimal soils, especially in road construction20,21. Research shows that adding phosphogypsum to cement-stabilized soil enhances early strength and improves microstructure22,23, while reducing cement use, providing both economic and environmental benefits. For example, Zeng et al.24 found that cement soil with 1.4% to 8.6% phosphogypsum exhibited 1.7 to 9.4 times greater unconfined compressive strength than untreated soil, with early strength 1.3 and 2.1 times higher at 3 and 7 days, respectively. Farroukh et al.25 found that phosphogypsum reduced soil sensitivity to water and improved its mechanical properties, including the CBR index, with variations depending on soil particle size and mineral composition. Chen et al.26 explored the dry-wet cycle performance of red clay treated with phosphogypsum and cement, concluding that phosphogypsum significantly mitigated expansion, shrinkage, and cracking in the composite material. Pang et al.27 revealed that phosphogypsum increased the dynamic shear modulus and damping ratio of coastal cement-soil, peaking at 15% content with a 40% improvement over unmodified samples. Furthermore, phosphogypsum promoted the formation of cementitious compounds such as C-S-H, filling soil pores and enhancing interparticle bonding, thereby improving the stiffness and dynamic properties. Similarly, Outbakat et al.28 found through pot experiments that phosphogypsum can effectively promote the formation and stability of soil aggregates through calcium-sodium ion exchange. In soils with relatively low salinity, 45 t/ha of phosphogypsum increased the stability of aggregates by 53% and 95%, respectively, while simultaneously increasing the total porosity of the soil, reducing the bulk density, and partially improving the water retention capacity. Through systematic experiments on highly plastic clay, Degirmenci et al.29 found that phosphogypsum can effectively reduce the soil plasticity index, optimize compaction characteristics, and significantly improve unconfined compressive strength, especially when compounded with cement. These studies confirm the potential of using waste phosphogypsum to improve soil, providing a technical basis for the resource utilization of industrial solid waste and the development of low-cost civil materials.

However, existing studies have primarily examined the modification of cement-stabilized soils with either rubber or phosphogypsum, while their combined effects remain largely unexplored. While rubber enhances the ductility, frost resistance, and reduces the density of cement soil, it may negatively impact impermeability and strength. In contrast, phosphogypsum improves strength and crack resistance but lacks sufficient toughness and impact resistance. Therefore, neither rubber nor phosphogypsum alone can fully satisfy engineering requirements. To fill this gap, this study systematically investigates the synergistic influence of phosphogypsum and rubber particles on the mechanical behavior and microstructural evolution of cement-stabilized soil, elucidating the underlying reinforcement mechanisms.

In this study, phosphogypsum and rubber were jointly incorporated into cemented soil to investigate their synergistic effects on mechanical properties, permeability, and microstructure change of cemented soil. Firstly, specimens with different mix proportions were prepared, and their compaction characteristics and mechanical performance at three curing ages were evaluated through standard compaction tests and unconfined compressive strength tests. Subsequently, microstructural characterization techniques, including scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD), were employed to investigate the formation and evolution of hydration products during curing and to elucidate their influence on the mechanical behavior of the materials at the microscopic scale. Finally, permeability tests were conducted to assess the pore structure and connectivity of the composite, thereby evaluating the effectiveness of soil stabilization in terms of durability, impermeability, and long-term engineering applicability. By combining the strengths of phosphogypsum-cement-soil and rubber-cement-soil, the composite material overcomes individual limitations, resulting in a high-strength, high-toughness material. Furthermore, this solution also promotes the reuse of waste tires and phosphogypsum, aligning with sustainable development goals. The findings lay the foundation for advancing cement-based composites and offer innovative approaches for “treating waste with waste”.

Mixture design

To assess the optimization effects of the phosphogypsum-rubber composite cemented soil on traditional cement soil, five experimental groups (A-E) were established and the unstabilized soil was included as a control group (Control) for comparison. The PG content was defined as the percentage of PG in the total weight of PG and soil. The study examined the effects of PG proportions of 0%, 15%, 20%, 25%, and 30%30 on the engineering properties of cement soil with varying rubber particle contents (0%, 1%, 1.5%, 2%, 2.5%, and 3%) 31. Previous research indicates that the hydration reaction between cement and PG produces significant ettringite32,33. However, excessive cement content can lead to uneconomical mixtures and the formation of monosulfide calcium sulfoaluminate hydrate (AFm), which negatively impacts the internal structure34,33. Based on these considerations, the cement content in the cement soil was set at 8%. The mixture proportions for each group are shown in Table 3.

Specimen preparation

In order to effectively reduce the impact of potential heavy metals in rubber particles on the environment, the rubber particles were stirred and washed in deionized water for 30 min to remove surface free ions and impurities, and then immersed in 0.1 mol/L Ca(OH)2 solution for 12 h to neutralize acidic substances and induce heavy metal ions to form insoluble precipitates, and finally dried for use.The PG and soil were separately ground using a mechanical crusher and sieved through a 2 mm mesh. The screened soil was dried at 105 °C for 24 h, while the PG was dried at 60 °C for 48 h. The materials were prepared according to the mixture design, with accurate proportions of soil, PG, cement, and rubber particles weighed. To ensure a complete reaction between PG and cement, they were mixed thoroughly first, followed by the addition of soil and rubber particles, which were then mixed for 5 min. Water was added based on the OWC of each mix, in equal portions, and stirred until a uniform consistency was achieved. The mixture was then placed in a mold and compacted using the standard Proctor compaction method. After demoulding, the specimens were cured to the corresponding age for testing. The entire specimen preparation process was completed within 1 h.

Compaction test

The compaction test is commonly used to evaluate soil compaction characteristics, determining the MDD and OWC through the compaction curve. Understanding the relationship between MDD and OWC is essential for analyzing soil compaction behavior16. For each mixture proportion, five compaction tests were conducted with varying water contents, with a 2% difference between adjacent values. The compaction test was carried out using the standard Proctor compaction test system. A cylindrical mold (50 mm diameter, 100 mm height) was used for the test. The prepared mixture was added in three layers, with approximately 130 g per layer. A 0.50 kg iron hammer was dropped from 270 mm height to compact the soil, with 30 drops per layer. To prevent delamination, each layer’s surface was leveled before adding the next layer. After compaction, the surface was trimmed, and the soil column was extracted using a hydraulic jack. The mass of the compacted soil was measured with 0.1 g precision. Three representative samples from the column’s central portion were taken, and their water content was determined by direct heating35. The average water content of the samples was used to determine the final optimum water content. The water content-dry density curve (compaction curve) was plotted, and the MDD and OWC were identified from the peak, providing valuable insights into the compaction characteristics.

Unconfined compressive strength test

The unconfined compressive strength (UCS) is the maximum compressive strength of a specimen under uniaxial unconfined conditions, serving as a key parameter for evaluating material bearing capacity under compressive loads36. In this study, cylindrical soil column specimens (50 mm diameter, 100 mm height) were prepared according to the OWC for each mixture, following the same procedure as the compaction test. After demolding, the specimens were wrapped in three layers of plastic film and cured in a standard chamber at 23 °C and 97% relative humidity until the designated curing age. UCS tests were performed according to ASTM D2166 37 using a testing machine with a 19 kN load capacity. Axial compression was applied at 1 mm/min until failure, and the peak strength was recorded. Three parallel specimens were tested for each proportion, with the final UCS calculated as the average. The collected broken UCS test samples were dried using a freeze-drying method to retard the hydration reaction and minimize microstructural alteration. First, the damaged samples were immersed in liquid nitrogen for rapid freezing to fix the microstructure, then transferred to a YTLG-12 C vacuum freeze dryer, where they were dried for 48 h at -50 °C and a vacuum of 0.05 mbar to sublimate the moisture. The dried samples were preserved for subsequent microstructural testing.

Permeability test

Permeability is a key parameter of geostabilization materials in various engineering environments. The permeability coefficient of the material directly affects its drainage and impermeability. The rubber and cementing material composite used in this study may cause changes in permeability due to the large particle size and high porosity in the interface transition zone. Therefore, incorporating permeability testing helps to comprehensively evaluate the stability and environmental adaptability of the material, especially to determine whether it meets the requirements for permeability performance in certain specific scenarios. In this study, evenly mixed samples were prepared in molds (61.8 mm diameter, 40 mm height) and compacted using static pressure in a single step. The specimens were then sealed with plastic wrap and placed in a standard curing box for the specified curing period. A variable head permeability test was conducted in the laboratory at a flow rate of 1 mm/min. Before testing, the glass tube was filled with water to the designated height, and the initial water head difference was recorded. During the test, the water head difference was monitored over time, and the final water head difference was recorded after a time interval T. The permeability coefficient was then calculated using Darcy’s law38.

XRD test

X-ray diffraction (XRD) is a method for analyzing crystal structures by interpreting diffraction patterns, allowing for the identification and quantification of crystal phases within a specimen39. Before this test, specimens damaged during the UCS test were dried, ground into powder, and sieved through a 0.075 mm mesh. The test was performed using a Sharp Empyrean X-ray diffractometer operated at 40 kV and 40 mA, with a scanning range of 2θ = 5°~60° at a step size of 0.02° and a scanning speed of 5°/min. The diffraction data were used to generate X-ray diffraction patterns for subsequent analysis.

SEM test

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) allows for high-resolution observation of surface morphology. The TESCAN CLARA ultra-high-resolution field emission scanning electron microscope was used for this test. Before testing, the dried UCS-damaged samples were shaped into 2 mm×2 mm×2 mm cubic blocks and were gold-coated to improve electrical conductivity. All samples were prepared following the same procedure to ensure consistency in the SEM observations. SEM analysis provided detailed insights into the internal structure of the composite materials, offering a microscopic explanation of changes in their properties40,41.

Results and discussion

Compaction test analysis

To examine the effects of PG and rubber particles on the compaction characteristics of composite materials, the compaction curves for various PG proportions at 0%R and different rubber contents at 25%PG were analyzed. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, the optimum water content (OWC) gradually decreased with increasing PG content, from 25% at 0%PG to 21% at 30%PG, a 4% reduction. This reduction can be attributed to the replacement of part of the natural soil by PG particles with lower water demand, as well as to the sulfate-induced early hydration reactions that reduce the amount of free water required for effective compaction29. Meanwhile, the maximum dry density (MDD) first increased and then decreased with increasing PG content, reaching a peak at 25%PG, where the MDD was 2.4% higher than that of the specimen without PG. This is because when an appropriate amount of PG is added, the fine PG particles act as fillers, while the products formed by the hydration reaction between PG and cement enhance interparticle bonding, leading to a denser microstructure, thereby increasing the MDD. However, when the PG content exceeds the optimal level, excessive PG leads to incomplete hydration, with unreacted particles tending to agglomerate32. This agglomeration can generate weak interfaces, increase structural porosity, and ultimately cause a reduction in the MDD. Moreover, Fig. 5a also compared the compaction curves of unstabilized and stabilized soil specimens. The stabilized specimens all contained 8% cement, while the unstabilized soil was included as a control to highlight the influence of the stabilization process on compaction behavior. Compared to unstabilized soil, cement-stabilized soil without added PG exhibited a higher OWC and a lower MDD. This phenomenon can be attributed to the hydration properties of cement and the resulting changes in microstructure during compaction. Conversely, when PG was incorporated into cement-stabilized soil, the cementing and filling effects of hydration products during compaction not only reduce the demand for free water for interparticle lubrication, but their volume expansion and cementing properties also result in a denser structure. Therefore, compared to unstabilized soil and cement-stabilized soil without PG, stabilized soil with added PG exhibited a lower OWC and a higher MDD29. As shown in Fig. 5b, the rubber content exhibited a limited influence on OWC but a pronounced effect on MDD. With increasing rubber content, the OWC decreased slightly, while the MDD dropped notably. For example, at 25%PG, the OWC at 3%R was 20%, a 2% decrease from 0%R, while the MDD was 1.540 g/cm³, a 5.41% decrease from 0%R. This can be attributed to the low density and poor water absorption of rubber particles, which occupy part of the volume42,43, reducing both OWC and MDD. This observation aligns with the findings of Hidalgo Signes44. Similar trends were observed for other PG ratios, though these details are omitted for brevity.

UCS analysis

Influence of different ratios

The influence of PG on the unconfined compressive strength (UCS) of the composites is discussed below. As shown in Fig. 6, the variation in compressive strength for specimens with different PG ratios at various curing ages was analyzed under fixed rubber content. While multiple rubber content levels were tested, comparative analysis revealed consistent UCS variation patterns across different rubber contents. Therefore, only the cases with 0%R, 1%R (minimum content), and 3%R (maximum content) are presented for discussion. For each testing condition, three parallel specimens were prepared and tested, and the reported values represent the average results. Error bars in the figures represent the standard deviation of the test results.

Compressive strength of different PG ratio at different curing ages, under identical rubber content: (a) 0%R, (b) 1%R, and (c) 3%R.

As shown in Fig. 6a, the UCS of the unstabilized soil remained below 1 MPa at all three curing ages, indicating inherently poor mechanical performance. This behavior can be attributed to the high water sensitivity of this type of soil, which is prone to softening, swelling, and even flow deformation upon water exposure, resulting in insufficient bearing capacity and limited deformation control. Stabilization with PG and cement can significantly improve this problem. For example, at a curing age of 28 days, the maximum UCS of the phosphogypsum-cement stabilized soil was 4.52 MPa, an increase of 366% compared to the unstabilized soil. Therefore, stabilization treatment of this type of soil has significant engineering application value.

Moreover, from Fig. 6, it is evident that with constant rubber content, the UCS of specimens at the same curing age initially increased and then decreased with rising PG ratio. The highest compressive strength occurred at 25%PG, which indicates it as the optimal PG ratio. This suggests that incorporating PG can enhance the strength of rubber-cement-soil composites, but the proportion must be carefully controlled. For example, at 1%R, the average compressive strength of the rubber-cement-soil without PG at the 14-day curing age was 2.44 MPa. With 15%PG and 25%PG, the strengths were 3.47 MPa and 4.18 MPa, respectively, a 42.21% and 71.31% increase over the 0%PG specimen. The initial increase in UCS can be attributed to PG acting as a chemical activator and active filler, which synergistically reacts with cement and soil, optimizing the composition and microstructure of the hydration products in the composite33,39. However, at 30%PG, the UCS decreased to 3.63 MPa, a reduction of 13.16% compared to the 25%PG. This reduction may be associated with the excessive introduction of sulfate-rich phases and fine particles, which cause hydration products to become unstable and decompose in acidic environments, thereby damaging the integrity of the cemented soil skeleton and hindering effective stress transfer45. Interestingly, the UCS of the 20%PG specimen was slightly lower than that of the 15% PG specimen, possibly due to incomplete or insufficient hydration reactions involving PG.

Additionally, the UCS of specimens with the same PG ratio increased with curing age, and the efficiency of strength enhancement improved as PG content increased, a topic elaborated upon in the subsequent section.

Figure 7 presents the UCS of specimens with varying rubber content under a constant PG proportion and curing age. The results show that a slight increase in UCS when 1%R-1.5%R was incorporated, compared with specimens without rubber. For instance, at a curing age of 28 days, the compressive strength of traditional cement soil without PG at 1%R and 1.5%R was 2.83 MPa and 2.76 MPa, respectively, 8.02% and 5.34% higher than the 2.62 MPa at 0%R. This improvement can be attributed to the stress redistribution effects provided by rubber particles, which help delay microcrack initiation and enhance energy dissipation within the cemented soil matrix16. However, when rubber content exceeds 1.5%, the interaction between rubber particles negatively impacts the overall strength11,46, leading to a progressive decline in UCS as rubber content increases, with the strength reduction accelerating as rubber content rises. When the PG content was 25%, the strength of phosphogypsum-cement-soil without rubber was 3.91 MPa after curing for 14 days. The strength at 1%R and 1.5%R was 4.18 MPa and 4.07 MPa, respectively, 6.90% and 4.09% higher than that at 0%R. With further increasing rubber content, the UCS dropped to 3.24 MPa at 3%R, a 17.14% decrease compared to the 0%R specimen. The acceleration of the strength reduction rate is reflected in the increasing slope of the broken line. For example, as seen in Fig. 7b, the line graph of UCS for specimens containing 25%PG with varying rubber contents after 14 days of curing indicates that the slope between 1%R and 1.5%R was 0.22, whereas the slope between 2.5%R and 3%R increased to 0.78. Additionally, it can be clearly seen from Fig. 7 that incorporating PG into rubber-cement-soil substantially enhances the UCS of the composite material. This enhancement may be due to the products generated by the reaction between cement and PG, which strengthen the cementitious matrix and partially offset the strength loss caused by rubber inclusions47.

Compressive strength of specimens with different rubber contents at the same PG ratio under identical curing ages: (a) 7 d, (b) 14 d, and (c) 28 d.

In summary, the influence of rubber content on the UCS of the phosphogypsum-rubber composite cemented soil can be described as follows: as rubber content increases, the compressive strength generally declines. However, small additions of rubber (1%-1.5%) still enhance the strength of phosphogypsum-cement-soil compared to specimens without rubber. This suggests the existence of an optimal rubber content for maximizing strength performance. The strength variations for other PG ratios at different curing ages show a similar trend.

Influence of curing time

To investigate the effect of curing time on UCS, a representative mixture with 2%R and 20%PG was selected. These contents provide a stable and typical mechanical response, allowing the influence of curing time to be examined without interference from extreme mixture proportions. The relationship between the natural logarithm of curing time (lnT) and UCS is shown in Fig. 8.

Relationship between the natural logarithm lnT of curing time and UCS; (a) Ratio of Rubber = 2%; (b) Ratio of PG = 20%.

Figure 8 illustrates that the UCS of the composite increases with lnT, displaying an approximately linear relationship. However, the rate of strength improvement diminishes slightly in the later curing stages. These findings align with Horpibulsuk et al.48 on the strength development and curing periods of cement-stabilized soil, suggesting that the inclusion of PG and rubber does not alter the linear relationship between UCS and curing time. This consistency confirms the robustness of the linear model for describing strength development, even with modifiers like PG and rubber particles.

Figure 8a shows that under constant rubber content, the initial slope of the curve increases with the amount of PG, indicating higher strength improvement efficiency with more PG. For example, when the curing period increased from 7 to 14 days, the strength improvement rates for specimens with 0%PG and 25%PG were 18.23% and 24.03%, respectively. The early strength improvement of rubber-cement-soil with 25%PG was 5.80% higher than without PG, suggesting that PG enhances early strength. This is likely due to PG promoting the early hydration reaction of cement, which accelerates hardening and increases strength33,47,49.

Figure 8b reveals that with constant PG content, as rubber content increases, the initial slope of the curve decreases from 1.28 at 0%R to 0.94 at 3%R. This indicates that the rate of strength improvement diminishes with more rubber content, suggesting that rubber particles hinder strength development. This could be due to: (I) Rubber particles not participating in the hydration reaction, reducing the effective contact area between PG, cement, and water, thus slowing hydration50; (II) The high elasticity of rubber particles, which absorb part of the stress during loading, preventing effective stress transfer to the matrix and impairing overall strength development11,51. These results are consistent with those of He et al.52.

Stress-strain curve analysis

Figure 9 shows the stress-strain curves of specimens with varying proportions under different curing ages. Initially, under vertical load, the axial stress of the phosphogypsum-rubber composite cemented soil increases, then decreases, and finally levels off with increasing axial strain. This process can be divided into four stages: compaction, elastic, yield, and failure. In the compaction stage, the stress-strain curve has a gentle slope due to surface irregularities and internal porosity, followed by a linear relationship in the elastic stage. As load increases, the specimen enters the yield stage, where the curve flattens until reaching the peak stress. Beyond this point, axial stress decreases as strain continues to increase, stabilizing at residual strength, signifying material failure.

Stress-strain curves of specimens with various proportions under different curing ages: (a) 2%R-7d; (b) 2%R-14d; (c) 2%R-28d; (d) 25%PG-7d; (e) 25%PG-14d; (f) 25%PG-28d.

Figure 9a-c show the stress-strain curves for different PG ratios at 2%R under curing ages of 7, 14, and 28 days. Increasing the PG ratio steepens the elastic stage curve and reduces failure strain. For instance, in Fig. 9b, the 25%PG specimen has a steeper slope than the 0%PG (ordinary rubber-cement-soil), indicating greater rigidity. Higher elastic modulus materials generally exhibit less deformation under load53, reducing peak strength axial strain from 2.08% at 0%PG to 1.12% at 25%PG. However, the incorporation of PG markedly enhances peak strength, with the peak stress of 25%PG specimen being 1.7 times higher than that of 0%PG, suggesting improved load-bearing capacity. This improvement is likely due to changes in the microstructure, such as hydration product size, type, and distribution54,55. During failure, specimens with higher strength exhibit more brittleness. For example, 0%PG shows low strength and higher ductility, leading to gradual post-failure stress reduction, while 25%PG fractures rapidly, reflecting brittle failure. This behavior indicates low fracture toughness and limited capacity for plastic deformation absorption. The stress-strain curve shifts from strain softening to strain hardening56. Additionally, increasing curing age for specimens with the same ratio results in a steeper curve, higher peak strength, lower failure strain, and more pronounced brittleness, attributed to more complete hydration reactions, which improve stiffness and strength. These findings align with the trends discussed above, providing further confirmation of the observed behavior.

Figure 9d-f show the effect of rubber content on the stress-strain curves. A small amount of rubber (1%-1.5%) increases peak stress. For example, under 14-day curing, the peak axial stress of the 25%PG-0%R specimen was 3.85 MPa, while for 25%PG-1%R and 25%PG-1.5%R, it rose to 4.20 MPa and 4.11 MPa, respectively. This enhancement can be attributed to the high elasticity of rubber, which fills voids and improves cohesion between rubber and soil particles, thus enhancing mechanical performance16. However, the further addition of rubber particles resulted in a reduction in the peak stress. For instance, at 3%R, the peak stress of specimen decreased by 17.4% compared to without rubber. The variation of peak stress is consistent with the results of Yadav and Tiwari11 and Srivastava57. Rubber particles also increase failure strain, as seen in the 3%R specimen with a peak axial strain of 1.99%, 33.34% higher than the 0%R. This decrease in peak stress and increase in failure strain are attributed to the elastic properties and interactions of rubber particles58,43. Moreover, rubber particles reduce post-failure stress drop and improve residual strength59, indicating that they enhance material toughness, shifting the response from brittle to ductile10,16.

To further quantify the effects of PG and rubber particles on the stiffness and deformability of the composite material, we calculated the corresponding secant modulus (Esec) based on the stress-strain curves. The secant modulus was defined as the slope of the line connecting the origin and the point corresponding to 50% of the peak stress on the stress-strain curve. This definition has been widely adopted for cemented and modified soils to facilitate comparative analysis among materials with different deformation characteristics60,61. Figure 10 shows the variation of secant modulus with PG and rubber contents at different curing ages.

Variation of secant modulus under different PG and rubber contents at three curing ages: (a) effect of PG at 2%R; (b) effect of rubber at 25%PG.

As shown in Fig. 10(a), at 2%R, the addition of PG generally increased the secant modulus compared to the specimen without PG. At 7 days, the secant modulus increased from 128.19 MPa at 0%PG to 169.86 MPa at 30%PG, indicating an improvement in early stiffness. This trend became more pronounced with increasing curing age. The secant modulus reached its maximum at 25%PG. For example, at 28 days, the peak secant modulus at 25%PG reached 329.42 MPa, an increase of 104% compared to 0%PG. This is consistent with the significant linearity and reduced deformation capacity observed in the pre-peak region of its stress-strain curve. When the PG content exceeded 25%, the stiffness began to decrease, possibly because unreacted PG forms weak surfaces, disrupting the particle skeleton and effective interparticle bonding62,32.

Conversely, the effect of rubber content on stiffness, as shown in Fig. 10b for specimens containing 25%PG, followed a different trend. The secant modulus of the composite material reached its maximum around 1%R-1.5%R. For example, at 7 days and 28 days, the secant modulus reached its maximum value of 193.83 MPa and 353.89 MPa at 1.5%R and 1%R, respectively. This indicates that low-dosage rubber particles can act as active fine aggregates, filling pores and enhancing interparticle friction, thereby slightly improving stiffness compared to 0%R63. However, when the content exceeds 1.5%R, the material stiffness decreased compared to 0%R. Under 14-day curing ages, the secant modulus decreased from 287.83 MPa at 1.5%R to 196.73 MPa at 3%R, lower than 225.54 MPa at 0%R. This decrease is mainly due to the fact that the inherent elastic modulus of rubber is lower than that of the surrounding cement matrix, thus forming a flexible zone, reducing the effective load-bearing area, and causing stress concentration at the rubber-soil interface51. As observed in stress-strain analysis, an increase in rubber content led to a flatter ascent in the curve and an increase in peak strain, indicating decreased stiffness and increased ductility. This phenomenon was quantitatively reflected by the secant modulus results.

Overall, the secant modulus results provides a quantitative confirmation for the stress-strain observations. The increase in PG content primarily enhances stiffness by steepening the pre-peak ascending branch through matrix densification and hydration promotion. Although excessive rubber incorporation reduces stiffness, it can improve strain capacity and delay brittle failure by redistributing stress and dissipating energy during loading. The combined effect resultes in a stiffness-ductility balance, highlighting the role of phosphogypsum-rubber modification in adjusting the mechanical properties of cement-stabilized soil.

Cracking pattern analysis

Figure 11 compares the compression failure morphologies of specimens with different PG proportions at 1%R and different rubber contents at 25%PG after 14 days of curing. Figure 11a shows that rubber-cement-soil without PG primarily exhibits plastic failure, where local expansion occurs initially, followed by the development of two longitudinal cracks as load increases. This plastic deformation delays failure, causing a more gradual post-peak decline in the stress-strain curve. With 15%PG, hydration products enhance stiffness, leading to brittle shear failure, as seen in Fig. 11b, where a distinct shear plane forms upon rupture. As PG ratio increases, brittle failure becomes more pronounced and the angle of shear plane increases. Figure 11c shows that 25%PG results in splitting failure, with a wide vertical crack along the axial direction, causing sudden failure and explaining the rapid post-peak stress drop. With further increase in PG, Fig. 11d shows a conical cracking pattern, likely due to excess unhydrated PG flakes, which cause slip deformation and reduce specimen stability64,45. This will be further discussed in the microstructural analysis.

Cracking pattern of specimens under UCS test: (a) 0%PG-1%R; (b) 15%PG-1%R; (c) 25%PG-1%R; (d) 30%PG-1%R; (e) 25%PG-2%R; (f) 25%PG-3%R.

Due to the low elastic modulus of rubber, its inclusion causes significant compressive deformation, leading to expansion failure. At 1%R, the specimen exhibited improved compressive strength and stiffness, with a primary crack on the failure surface, but still showed brittle failure. At 2%R, as shown in Fig. 11e, the cracking pattern shifted to local softening-shear dilatancy failure, with lateral bulging and a few cracks at the bulging points, as the elastic properties of rubber began influencing the behavior. With further rubber addition, Fig. 11f shows more pronounced bulging deformation and numerous staggered cracks at the bulging points. These cracks result from the soft aggregate behavior of rubber particles and surface tensile stress development11. This transition from brittle to ductile failure improves toughness, allowing the specimen to carry load post-failure and enhancing post-peak compressive strength. The cracking pattern aligns with Yadav and Tiwari58.

As shown in Table 4, at the same curing age, the permeability coefficients of the stabilized soils were significantly lower than those of the unstabilized soil. For instance, after 14 days of curing, the permeability coefficient of the unstabilized soil was 4.62 × 10− 6 cm/s, whereas the minimum permeability coefficient of the stabilized soil decreased to 6.43 × 10− 9 cm/s. This substantial reduction in permeability demonstrates that the stabilization treatment effectively enhances the impermeability. The improvement can be primarily attributed to the cementitious substances generated by hydration bind soil particles together and form a hydrophobic coating on their surface, which disrupts pore connectivity and restricts the flow of water in the soil matrix65,66. Moreover, it was observed from Fig. 12a that the inclusion of PG reduced the permeability coefficient. As PG increased, the permeability coefficient first decreased, reaching a minimum, then increased. At 7 days of curing, permeability coefficients for 0%PG and 15%PG were 8.83 × 10− 7 cm/s and 3.31 × 10− 7 cm/s, respectively, representing a 62.51% reduction. At 14 and 28 days, reductions were 65.11% and 60.74%, respectively. This decrease is due to the hydration products of PG and cement filling interspaces in the stabilized soil matrix, enhancing soil structure. Improved interfacial adhesion between particles further reduced water flow paths, decreasing permeability. Figure 12a also shows that permeability decreased with increasing PG up to 25%, after which it increased, likely due to unhydrated flaky PG introducing interspaces and increasing porosity45.

Figure 12b shows that rubber particles initially reduce the permeability coefficient of the composite, reaching a minimum at about 2%R before increasing. Table 4 shows that at 28 days of curing, the permeability coefficient of the 25%PG-2%R specimen was 5.33 × 10− 9 cm/s, 81.36% lower than that of 25%PG-0%R. As rubber content increased further, the permeability coefficient gradually increased. This is due to the high compressibility and expansiveness of rubber particles, which fill interspaces and create impermeable zones, reducing water flow67. However, at higher rubber contents, the particles weaken interparticle bonding and increase interspace ratio, raising the permeability coefficient68. This trend was consistent across other PG proportions. Generally, around 2%R, the material showed optimal impermeability. Over time, as curing progresses, hydration products densified the structure, progressively reducing the permeability coefficient for all mixtures. For instance, the permeability coefficient of the 25%PG-1.5%R specimen decreased from 2.37 × 10− 8 cm/s at 7 days to 7.67 × 10− 9 cm/s at 14 days, and 6.51 × 10− 9 cm/s at 28 days, representing reductions of 67.64% and 72.53%, respectively.

According to the typical permeability requirements for roadbed materials in highway engineering, the permeability coefficient is generally specified to be less than 1 × 10− 4 cm/s. The stabilized soil developed in this study, especially the mixture with 1.5%R to 2%R, showed excellent anti-permeability performance. For example, the permeability coefficient of the 25%PG-2%R specimen at 28 days was 5.33 × 10− 9 cm/s, which is far below the conventional threshold. This shows that PG-rubber stabilized soil not only has improved mechanical properties, but also has sufficient hydraulic stability to be used in roadbed construction, especially in areas where enhanced waterproofing or drainage control is required.

Microstructure

XRD analysis

To investigate the microscopic mechanisms by which PG affects the performance of the rubber-cement-soil composite, XRD tests were conducted on specimens with varying PG proportions at 2%R, as shown in Fig. 13. Phase identification was performed by matching the observed diffraction peaks with reference patterns from the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) PDF-4 database, using the software package HighScore Plus (version 4.9). A qualitative matching approach was adopted, focusing on characteristic peak positions (2θ) and relative intensities. For example, ettringite was identified by its strongest peak near 22°-23°; gypsum was confirmed by prominent peaks at 2θ ≈ 11.6°, 20.7°, and 29.1°; calcite was assigned based on the strong peak at 2θ ≈ 29.2°. The XRD results, compared with PDF-4 database, revealed that the primary hydration products of the composite included ettringite, C‑S‑H, and CaCO3 precipitation, with trace amounts of kaolinite and muscovite from the soil. In the ordinary rubber-cement-soil system, the XRD diffraction peaks primarily corresponded to SiO2 at 2θ ≈ 21.8° and 2θ ≈ 26.6°, with traces of CaCO3 at 2θ ≈ 29.2°. Previous studies58 suggested that the strength of the rubber-cement-soil composites comes mainly from C-S-H gels formed through the hydration of CaO and SiO2, as outlined in Equation (5).The diffraction peaks of C-S-H are mainly distributed at 2θ ≈ 28°~32°. However, it was not directly observed in the XRD spectrum, likely because its peaks overlapped with those of CaCO3, as C-S-H typically does not exhibit sharp peaks in XRD patterns55. CaSO4•2H2O is the primary component of PG. As evident from Fig. 13, the diffraction peak of CaSO4•2H2O at 2θ ≈ 11.6° became increasingly prominent with the addition of PG. Simultaneously, PG, cement, and Al2O3 in the soil reacted to form ettringite, a high-sulfur hydrated calcium sulfoaluminate, as described in Equation (7). Ettringite, sparingly soluble in water, precipitates in voids and is a key factor in enhancing compressive strength24,66. The ettringite diffraction peak at 2θ ≈ 22.9° grew with increasing PG content, but at 30%PG, the peak slightly decreased due to the acidic nature of PG, which reduced pH and caused partial decomposition of ettringite, as described in formula (8)69,70. Moreover, the reduced ettringite peak may also result from CO₂ exposure, causing carbonation and decomposition into CaCO3 and CaSO4•2H2O, as shown in Equation (9).

SEM analysis

The impact of PG on the composite material was analyzed through SEM imaging, focusing on its effect on microstructure and hydration products. Figure 14 presents SEM images of the composite with varying PG ratios, illustrating the microscopic mechanisms behind the changes in strength and permeability. The identification of hydration products in the SEM images was based on their characteristic morphologies (e.g., needle-like ettringite, flocculent C-S-H gel, and plate-like PG), in conjunction with the phase information obtained from XRD analysis and previously reported microstructural features in the literature. SEM observations were used as qualitative evidence to support the phase evolution inferred from XRD results, rather than as a standalone method for phase identification. To minimize rubber particle interference, SEM images were analyzed at a fixed 1% rubber content, with the specific influence of rubber particles discussed separately.

SEM images of specimens with different ratio of PG at 1%R: (a) 0%PG (3.00 k); (b) 15%PG (3.00 k); (c) 25%PG (3.00 k); (d) 30%PG (3.00 k); (e) 0%PG (10.0 k); (f) 15%PG (10.0 k); (g) 25%PG (10.0 k); (h) 30%PG (10.0 k).

Clayey soils have high adhesion, but large interparticle voids result in lower inherent strength. As discussed in the XRD section, the primary strength source in the rubber-cement-soil system is the C-S-H gel. Figure 14e shows that C-S-H gel binds the soil and rubber particles, enhancing strength. During cement hydration with PG, needle-like ettringite crystals form71. Figure 14f at 10.0k magnification for 15%PG shows that lower PG ratios yield fewer ettringite crystals. As PG content increases, Fig. 14g shows more ettringite enveloped by C-S-H gel, forming a spatial network structure that fills voids and improves strength and densification39,46. This explains the enhanced compressive strength and impermeability of the composite. However, at 30%PG, excessive unhydrated PG particles, as shown in Fig. 14h, reduce pH, causing ettringite decomposition and promoting sliding deformation45, compromising structural stability and leading to decreased strength and increased permeability.

Figure 15 compares SEM images of specimens containing 1%R and 3%R at 25%PG. In Fig. 15a, the addition of a small amount of rubber particles shows that these particles are enveloped by soil particles, with the load-bearing framework primarily dominated by soil. The improvements in compressive strength and impermeability with a small amount of rubber can be explained by the following mechanisms: (Ⅰ) Rubber particles function as fine aggregates, effectively filling interspaces within the composite and enhancing matrix density and compressive strength63; (Ⅱ) The irregular shapes of rubber particles generate friction and interlocking between particles, resisting overturning and sliding under higher loads.

SEM images of different rubber content at 25%PG; (a) 1%R (2.00 k); (b) 3%R (2.00 k).

Conversely, as shown in Fig. 15b, specimens with higher rubber content rely predominantly on the significant deformation capacity of rubber particles to bear loads. Given the much lower elastic modulus of rubber compared to the cement matrix, notable deformation mismatches occur between the two under stress43. This mismatch increases the potential for failure at their bonding interfaces. Additionally, the dense distribution of rubber particles reduces both friction and cohesion with soil particles, creating numerous interspaces51. As a result, interfacial bonding strength weakens, making these weakly bonded regions more prone to failure. This phenomenon is the primary factor behind the observed reductions in compressive strength and permeability resistance as rubber particle content increases.

To quantitatively characterize the pore structure of the stabilized soils, image analysis was performed on SEM images (Figs. 14 and 15) using ImageJ software. Representative SEM images were selected at the same magnification to ensure comparability among different specimens. The images were first converted to 8-bit grayscale and subsequently processed using a consistent thresholding method to distinguish pores from the solid matrix. After binarization, pore regions were identified and analyzed using the built-in particle analysis function in ImageJ, with a size filter of 0.01–100 µm2 and a circularity range of 0.1-1.0. The pore area fraction, defined as the ratio of the total pore area to the analyzed image area, was calculated and used as an indicator of relative porosity. This quantitative analysis was conducted to support the qualitative microstructural observations and to facilitate comparison of pore characteristics among specimens with different PG and rubber contents. For each mix proportion, three independent SEM images were analyzed, and the average porosity along with standard deviation was reported in Fig. 16.

The average porosity from SEM image analysis.

The results in Fig. 16 showed that with the addition of PG, the average porosity first decreased and then increased, reaching its lowest point at 25%PG. At 1%R, the porosity of 25%PG was 5.18%, which was approximately 41.27% lower than the porosity of 8.82% for 0%PG, consistent with the formation of dense hydration products that fill the voids between particles. Conversely, under 25%PG conditions, the porosity of 3%R was 7.27%, which was about 40.35% higher than that of 1%R, supporting the observation that excess rubber particles create interfacial gaps and weaken matrix compactness. These quantitative trends were in good agreement with the macroscopic permeability and strength data.

Conclusion

This study investigated the engineering performance of a phosphogypsum-rubber composite cemented soil by comparing it with systems incorporating PG or rubber individually. The results demonstrate that the composite exhibits improved strength, toughness, and impermeability. The main conclusions are as follows:

The incorporation of PG and rubber reduced the OWC of the composite. The MDD increased with increasing PG content up to 25%, beyond which it decreased. The addition of rubber reduced the MDD, with the 25%PG-3%R specimen showing a 5.41% reduction compared to the 25%PG-0%R specimen.

The incorporation of PG and rubber reduced the OWC of the composite. The MDD increased with increasing PG content up to 25%, beyond which it decreased. The addition of rubber reduced the MDD, with the 25%PG-3%R specimen showing a 5.41% reduction compared to the 25%PG-0%R specimen.

Although PG can increase the stiffness of materials, an appropriate amount of PG content can still promote the development of early strength and improve the overall UCS, with 25% being the optimal content. Rubber contents of 1%-1.5% enhanced material compressive strength, while higher contents reduced it. Nevertheless, rubber improved material toughness, with the failure strain increasing by 33.34% at 3%R compared to 0%R specimen. The combined effect of PG and rubber particles can achieve a balance between stiffness and ductility.
