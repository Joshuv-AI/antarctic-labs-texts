Retention in HIV care before and after implementation of a case management program in Malawi
Authors: Lucky Makonokaya, Shalom Dunga, Louiser Kalitera, Rachel Chamanga, Lazarous Msuku, Cathy Golowa, Zuze Joaki, Ackim Sankhani, Geoffrey Singini, Samantha Makoko, Lilian Jiah, Rhoderick Machekano, Godfrey Woelk, Thulani Maphosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRetaining persons living with HIV (PLHIV) on antiretroviral therapy (ART) and preventing loss-to-follow-up (LTFU) is an ongoing challenge. In Malawi, the Elizabeth Glaser Pediatric AIDS Foundation (EGPAF) implemented a facility-based case management initiative targeting PLHIV at risk of discontinuing care. Using a risk assessment tool, the program provided personalized support through psychosocial counselors, adherence officers, and expert clients. Our evaluation assessed the impact of this initiative on retaining newly initiated ART clients.MethodsWe conducted a pre-/post quasi-experimental study using routine data from electronic medical records and case management registers across ten EGPAF-supported health facilities in Blantyre and Zomba districts. Using stratified random sampling, we selected PLHIV who were initiated on ART before (January-June 2019) and after (January-June 2021) implementation of the program, with a 12-month follow-up. We used survival analysis to compare LTFU over the 12-month follow-up period between PLHIV enrolled during the pre-intervention and post-intervention periods, and Cox hazards proportion analysis to assess factors associated with LTFU in the first year.ResultsAmong the 1,466 participants, 33.8% (n = 496) were from the pre-intervention period, and 66.2% (n = 970) were from the post-intervention period. Comparing pre- and post-intervention, there were significant differences in the probability of remaining in care at six months (75.7% vs. 93.9%, respectively, p < 0.001) and twelve months (59.3% vs. 70.7%, p < 0.001). Patients in the post-intervention period had a lower risk of LTFU than those in the pre-intervention period: adjusted Hazards Ratio (aHR) 0.46 (95% CI 0.34–0.63). Participants who initiated on non-DTG-based ART were more likely to be LTFU than those who initiated on DTG-based ART: aHR 1.63 (95% CI 1.11–2.41). Participants who received ART in rural facilities had an increased risk of LTFU than those in urban facilities (aHR: 1.49; 95% CI 1.14–1.96).ConclusionRetention in the care of newly initiated ART patients during their first year was higher after the implementation of the case management program than before program implementation. Scaling up the model could help reduce discontinuity of care in the country and other resource-limited settings.Keywords: Retention in HIV care, Case management program, Malawi, Pre/post evaluation

Introduction

To end the AIDS epidemic, the HIV and AIDS global community has set goals: by 2030, 95% of people living with HIV should know their HIV status, 95% of those who are aware of their HIV status are receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 95% of those receiving ART should have a suppressed viral load [1]. Retaining patients on ART and preventing loss-to-follow-up (LTFU) is an ongoing challenge in HIV care and treatment, and sustaining viral suppression [2]. Retention requires identifying and mitigating any barriers that may affect the optimal effectiveness of treatment, such as drug side effects, and providing counseling and support. Non-adherence to treatment may lead to inadequate viral suppression with the potential consequences of drug resistance, increased HIV transmission risk, decreased immunity, opportunistic infections, poor health, and death [3].

Several factors have been associated with retention and LTFU. These include demographic (age, gender, pregnancy, marital status, and socioeconomic status), structural (distance to the facility), health service (open and hidden facility costs, drug availability, health worker relationship, and reception), and social (stigma and shame, disclosure of HIV status) factors [4–7]. LTFU often occurs relatively early after treatment initiation [8].

Various interventions have been developed and implemented to reduce LTFU and increase retention in HIV care, with varying levels of effectiveness [6, 9–11]. To maximize the use of limited resources to prevent LTFU and improve retention, the development of a tool to identify patients at high risk of LTFU would allow health centers to provide specific targeted management to such patients to prevent LTFU. The Elizabeth Glaser Pediatric AIDS Foundation (EGPAF)-Malawi, which, with funding from PEPFAR and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), supports 304,300 recipients of HIV care in 179 health facilities [12], developed a patient retention risk stratification tool, which assessed the likelihood of LTFU in HIV patients receiving ART. Based on their risk of LTFU, patients received a patient-centered healthcare delivery model (Case Management) encompassing tools and coordinating strategies to optimize continued engagement in HIV care while supporting them in identifying and overcoming barriers that could interfere with achieving personal health-related goals. For each patient, case management comprised an assessment of strengths (self-efficacy) and barriers to ART initiation and adherence, planning and setting goals, and developing patient-specific interventions to address the identified barriers based on their risk score. The approach targeted patients initiating ART, those returning to care after treatment interruption, and those with high viral load results. We evaluated the case management program and its effect on the retention of newly initiated ART patients in a routine program setting in Malawi.

Methods

Case management program overview

Healthcare providers, including adherence support officers (ASOs) and psychosocial counselors (PSSCs), conducted individual interviews using a Risk Stratification (RS) tool during ART initiation. The tool assessed four broad factors in view of determining appropriate risk categories (high, moderate, or low) – Core, Individual/Attitudinal, Socioeconomic, and Structural factors. Each of these four factors had several items that were scored “Yes” or “No” (Yes = 1, No = 0), except for the Core factors; an affirmative “Yes” to any Core factor item categorizes the patient as having a high LTFU risk. Client scores are sorted into three risk categories: high risk (total score of 16–23 or a Yes in any of the items on the Core factors), moderate risk (total score of 8–15), and low risk (total score of 0–7).

The risk categories from the RS tool allowed for tailored, patient-centered case management interventions to ensure continued client engagement in HIV care and treatment. Depending on the level of LTFU risk, clients on ART were placed on different case management interventions referred to as “packages.” The frequency of client engagement for the case management packages varied across the risk categories: weekly for high-risk clients, biweekly for moderate-risk clients, and monthly for low-risk clients. For each risk category, a case management team was assigned to provide appropriate intervention packages that included health education, motivational counseling, enhanced phone adherence support, and enrollment in peer-led support groups. A key feature of the case management strategy was the development of a personalized treatment plan based on individual patient barriers to retention. The case management team included psychosocial support counselors (PSSC), adherence support officers (ASO), clinicians, nurses, and expert clients. PSSCs were certified following two years of formal training; ASO was a professional cadre with in-service counseling training, and expert clients were HIV-positive volunteers with on-the-job training to support enrolled clients. The team was also trained in standard operating procedures (SOPs) that EGPAF developed to guide case management packages.

Study design

We conducted a pre-/post, quasi-experimental study of newly initiated ART patients receiving HIV care in ten EGPAF-supported health facilities in the Blantyre and Zomba districts that fully implemented the case management program in 2021. We included patients who initiated ART between January and June 2019 for the pre-intervention period, and January and June 2021 for the post-intervention period, with a 12-month follow-up for patients enrolled during each period to assess retention.

We collected secondary data from the Electronic Medical Records Systems (EMRS) routinely used to gather patient-level data for individuals receiving HIV care in the country’s ART clinics. Data extraction was performed between 03 February 2023 and 28 February 2023 by Strategic Information and Evaluation officers using ODK Collect® data collection software and Android tablets, and the data were securely stored on an online server. The records were de-identified at the time of data collection, and the authors had no access to information that could identify individual participants.

Study variables

The outcome variable was the treatment status at the end of follow-up, categorized as “retained in care” (defined as attending a drug refill visit within 56 days of their scheduled visit), “transferred to another facility”, “LTFU/defaulted” (defined as missing a scheduled drug refill visit by more than 56 days), or “died”. Independent variables included sex, facility location (categorized as rural or urban) age group (categorized as 0 to 9, 10 to 19, and ≥ 20 years), WHO clinical stage at ART initiation, initial ART regimen (dolutegravir-based or non-dolutegravir-based), risk assessment outcome (low, moderate, or high risk of dropping out of care), and case manager cadre [PSSC, ASO, expert client (EC), or other].

Sampling and sample size

We employed a stratified cluster-based sampling design, with the primary strata being two districts, including ten health facilities. To calculate the needed sample size, in the absence of precise daily counts of newly diagnosed HIV patients (as the facilities reported monthly data only), we assumed, for sampling purposes, that an equal number of patients were detected by each health facility daily. This assumption and the central limit theorem justified a minimum sample size of 50 patients in each facility for the pre-intervention study period.

Patient selection for the study was performed randomly from among those with records in the facility EMRs. We employed a 1:2 sampling approach, identifying one patient in the pre-implementation phase for every two patients identified in the post-implementation phase. Our sample size was calculated using the power two-proportion function in Stata, assuming a 0.2 proportional difference between the pre- and post-study periods within ten facilities, accounting for an 8% correlation between outcomes within facility, targeting an 80% study power, and a significance level of 0.05. This calculation yielded a total sample size of 1500 study subjects, with each facility allocated 50 patients for the pre-study period and 100 patients for the post-study period.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Patient demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and differences between the pre-intervention and post-intervention periods were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were estimated using Poisson regression with person-years as the exposure variable. Time-to-event analyses were conducted to estimate the probability of LTFU. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated for each period and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals for the risk of LTFU within 12 months of ART initiation, comparing the pre-intervention and post-intervention periods while adjusting for sex, facility location, age group, WHO clinical stage at ART initiation, and ART regimen at initiation. Participants who died or transferred out to another health facility were censored at the time of the event. We assumed that the censoring was non-informative. Effect modification by facility location was assessed using a likelihood ratio test comparing Cox regression models with and without an interaction term between intervention period and facility location.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Malawi National Health Science Research Committee (NHSRC) in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki as part of the protocol titled Evaluation of Outcomes Achieved through Integrated HIV/AIDS and TB Prevention, Care and Treatment Programs in Malawi on September 12, 2018, approval number 2130, protocol number 18/09/2130, with a waiver of the requirement for individual consent because routinely collected data from medical records were being retrospectively evaluated. The Advarra Institutional Review Board (IRB) in the United States reviewed and approved the study on November 22, 2019 (protocol number Pro00040441). This activity was reviewed by the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and was conducted consistently with the applicable federal law and CDC policy.

Results

We enrolled 1466 patients; 61.8% (n = 906) were female. The median age was 31 years, and the interquartile range (IQR) was 24–38 years; 66.2% (n = 970) of the patients were from the post-intervention period, 86.2% (n = 837) of whom were enrolled in the case management program. In the pre-intervention period, 59.7% (n = 296) of the participants were female versus 62.9% (n = 610) of the patients in the post-intervention period. The median age was 31 years (IQR, 24–38) for the pre-intervention group and 31 years (IQR, 25–39) for the post-intervention group. The post-intervention period was more likely than the pre-intervention period to have patients starting DTG-based treatment (97% vs. 68%, p < 0.001). Among those enrolled in the case management program during the post-intervention period, 59.4% (n = 497) were classified as having a low risk of dropping out of care, and nearly half of these patients (n = 429) were managed by ASO case managers (Table 1).

Overall, 220 participants were LTFU over 751.7 person-years of observation, corresponding to an incidence rate of 29.3 per 100 person-years (95% CI 25.6–33.4). Twenty-two participants (1.5%) died, while 252 (17.2%) transferred to other facilities over the follow-up period. During the pre-intervention period, 128 participants were LTFU over 261.2 person-years, yielding an incidence rate of 49.0 per 100 person-years (95% CI 41.2–58.3). In contrast, 92 participants were LTFU during the post-intervention period over 490.6 person-years, corresponding to a significantly lower incidence rate of 18.8 per 100 person-years (95% CI 15.3–23.0). The incidence rate of LTFU in the post-intervention period was 61% lower than in the pre-intervention period (IRR 0.39; 95% CI 0.30–0.50) (Table 2).

Figure 1 shows the Kaplan–Meier estimates of retention in care over the first 12 months following ART initiation by intervention period. Retention probabilities were consistently higher among participants initiated on ART during the post-intervention period compared with those in the pre-intervention period throughout the follow-up period (log rank test p < 0.001). At six months, the probability of retention in care in the pre-intervention period was 75.7% (95% CI 71.1–79.7) compared with 93.9% (95% CI 91.8–95.5) in the post-intervention period. At 12 months after ART initiation, the probability of remaining in care was lower in the pre-intervention period than in the post-intervention period: 59.3% (95% CI 52.7–65.3) vs. 70.7% (95% CI 64.0–76.3), respectively. Retention probabilities remained higher among participants initiated on ART during the post-intervention period compared with those in the pre-intervention period throughout the follow-up period (log rank test p < 0.001), after restricting the analysis to participants who initiated on DTG-based regimens (Fig. 2).

In a multivariable Cox Proportional Hazards analysis, participants in the post-intervention period were less likely to be LTFU than those in the pre-intervention period (aHR 0.46; 95% CI 0.34–0.63). Clients who initiated on non-DTG-based ART had a marginally higher risk of LTFU than those who initiated on DTG-based ART: aHR 1.63 (95% CI 1.11–2.41). Discussion

In this study, we sought to evaluate the impact of a case management program on retaining newly initiated ART patients in a routine setting in Malawi. We found that patients in the post-intervention period were significantly more likely to be retained in care than those in the pre-intervention period, at both six and twelve months after initiating ART. The case management program combined evidence-based components, such as motivational interviewing, individual counseling, treatment support, and mobile phone reminders, and achieved gains in retention even when delivered by lower-cadre health workers [10, 13–15]. These findings suggest that structured case management can strengthen early ART retention in routine, resource-limited contexts.

Consistent with findings from other studies, our results show that assigning case managers to specific groups of HIV-positive patients improved retention in care [11, 13–15]. In contrast to other programs, however, most patients in our setting were supported by adherence support officers and expert clients rather than professional counselors or nurses. There was no significant difference in outcomes between patients managed by lay cadres and those managed by professionals. The comparable outcomes suggest that trained lay health workers, under appropriate supervision, could effectively deliver adherence support interventions. While our study did not assess costs, this approach could lower expenses and enhance scalability within public HIV programs.

Additionally, there were significant differences in the probability of retention between patients who initiated ART in the pre-intervention period and those in the post-intervention period at six- and twelve-month post-ART-initiation. This finding was not expected, as many patients on ART experience a higher risk of dropping out of care within the first six months of treatment initiation [16, 17], attributed to the heightened risk of mortality from presenting with Advanced HIV Disease (AHD), the risk of Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS), adverse effects, or a greater likelihood of treatment interruption due to fears, difficulties in disclosing their HIV status to their families, and challenges in coping with living with HIV and lifelong ART [4, 18–22]. This sustained effect may indicate that case management helped patients build adherence skills and confidence to continue care independently. However, this hypothesis could not be tested within our study design. Future mixed-methods studies could explore patient experiences after discharge to identify which components of case management support long-term engagement.

Notably, participants who initiated on non-DTG-based ART regimens had a higher risk of attrition over twelve months than those who initiated on DTG-based ART. Similar findings have been previously reported, attributing the difference to the better tolerability of Dolutegravir than Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs) [23, 24]. Programmatically, these findings support the continued use of DTG-based regimens to improve retention.

Patients who initiated ART in rural areas were at an increased risk of LTFU than those in urban facilities, as observed in previous studies [25, 26]. This pattern likely reflects structural barriers common in rural areas, including limited access to ART services and longer travel distances to health facilities [27–29]. Importantly, although overall LTFU differed by facility location, there was no evidence that the effect of the intervention varied between rural and urban sites. This suggests that the intervention was equally effective across settings and supports its implementation in both rural and urban health facilities.

Our study also found that nearly 14% of eligible patients were not enrolled in the case management program, suggesting gaps in implementation fidelity. Possible reasons include limited coverage by case management staff and patients declining enrollment into the care model. However, the scope of our evaluation could not evaluate the reasons for the gaps in enrollment. Ensuring continuous staff availability and systematic tracking of missed enrollments may improve fidelity in routine rollout.

At about 17%, the transfer rate of participants in our study was comparable with that of other studies in the region [30, 31]. In our study, participants who transferred out were censored as they are programmatically considered as continuing care. However, patients who transfer to other facilities in routine ART programs in the region have been associated with low viral load suppression rates and a higher risk of attrition [30–32]. Strengthening inter-facility communication and harmonizing patient tracking systems could ensure continuity of care and improve patient outcomes.

This study had several limitations. First, it relied on routine data that limited the variables we could include in the analysis to those routinely collected. Second, the pre-/post-design may be affected by unmeasured temporal trends. However, to our knowledge, no major policy shifts occurred during the period. Third, children, elderly patients, and those with AHD were underrepresented. Finally, we could not isolate which specific components of the intervention drove the observed effect.

Conclusion

Retention in the care of newly initiated ART patients during their first year was higher after the implementation of the case management program than before program implementation. These results suggest that structured case management delivered by trained lay health workers is a feasible and effective strategy in routine settings. Further studies should assess the program’s cost-effectiveness, validate the risk stratification tool, and explore mechanisms that sustain engagement beyond program completion.
