Stroke Mortality in Kazakhstan
Authors: Ruslan Akhmedullin, Temirgali Aimyshev, Gulnur Zhakhina, Iliyar Arupzhanov, Antonio Sarria-Santamera, Altynay Beyembetova, Ayana Ablayeva, Aigerim Biniyazova, Temirlan Seyil, Diyora Abdukhakimova, Yuliya Semenova, Abduzhappar Gaipov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke is a major public health concern requiring valid estimates for planning and evaluating health interventions. The GBD (Global Burden of Disease) studies have become a major source of information; however, data sources have historically been a limitation.ObjectivesWe sought to compare stroke mortality estimates in Kazakhstan with those reported by the GBD study.MethodsMortality data were extracted from the Unified Electronic Healthcare System of Kazakhstan (UNEHS). We used the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), Bayesian structural time-series (BSTS), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) to model data from the UNEHS and forecast its trends until 2030. The accuracy metrics were mean absolute error, root mean square error, and mean absolute percentage error. We calculated the standardized difference in mortality estimates between the databases for the observed and forecasted estimates.ResultsThe BSTS, ARIMA, and XGBoost models revealed slight variations in accuracy metrics, which depended on forecasting horizons and mostly favored XGBoost. During 2014-2030, the absolute difference in death counts was 207,108 between the GBD and UNEHS. The GBD estimates were twice as many across both the observed and predicted periods, with a moderate standardized difference (0.73) when considering their average. This study showed a systematic difference between GBD and national data.ConclusionsWe found that UNEHS estimates were not comparable despite our efforts to replicate the GBD methods. Further studies are needed to explore the discrepancies between the national or regional data and GBD. Current limitations related to primary data and reproducibility require caution when interpreting GBD findings.Key Words: ARIMA, BSTS, GBD, machine learning, stroke, XGBoost

The GBD (Global Burden of Diseases) studies have turned into the primary source of information in public health research.1 Despite having a substantial impact on policymaking, its estimates remain incompletely reproducible, with limited data sources.2,3 This led to concerns regarding the accuracy and comparability of estimates from GBD studies, as well as concerns about methods for attributing disease burden and interventions.1,4 Recent studies have compared estimates using data from the GBD with those from the World Health Organization5 and Global Cancer Observatory6,7 for tuberculosis and cancer, respectively. These studies revealed a systematic difference across metrics between institutions over time, highlighting the need for further validation studies, with a particular focus on countries with a high disease burden.

Stroke remains 1 of the leading causes of mortality and a major contributor to disability worldwide.8 A recent GBD study2 revealed an overall decrease in stroke burden during 1990-2021 in most countries. However, this reduction was uncommon across regions, and the risk of premature death was highest in Central and Eastern Europe and Central Asia.9,10 Interestingly, the risk factors for stroke in low- to middle-income countries were similar to those in high-income countries,11 despite the differences in their contribution to mortality. It was suggested that prevention measures are either not broadly used or their effectiveness is unexpectedly low, further establishing it as a critical health concern.

Current stroke statistics12 suggest discrepancies in mortality estimates, highlighting the need for additional comparative data. Given this uncertainty, it has become an important area in epidemiology to analyze historical data and propose appropriate responses. Kazakhstan is a Central Asian country, and both the burden of stroke and the distribution of its risk factors are among the highest worldwide.8,13 Taken together, frequent updates of mortality estimates and their comparison with GBD becomes essential. Although the GBD has performed forecasts of stroke burden in the following decades,14,15 current evidence emphasizes the importance of regularly updating predictions rather than projecting them far ahead, suggesting higher accuracy and adaptability to changes.16 Therefore, this study aimed to analyze historical data on stroke mortality estimates from Kazakhstan, forecast its trends until 2030, and compare the estimates with those from the GBD.

Materials and Methods

Overview and definitions

This time series forecasting study analyzed the mortality estimates of stroke in Kazakhstan. The study was modelled based on historical data from 2014-2023, and predicted its trends into 2024-2030. For these purposes, we incorporated 3 different techniques, namely autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), Bayesian structural time series (BSTS), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and used estimates from the best model for further comparisons based on prediction accuracy measures.

Stroke-related deaths were defined using the corresponding International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. To ensure comparability of the estimates, all corresponding ICD-10 codes were managed in adherence to the GBD approach,14,17 and were as follows: G45-G46.8, I60-I63.9, I65-I66.9, I67.0-I67.3, I67.5-I67.6, I68.1-I68.2, and I69.0-I69.3. The GBD 2021 used vital registration systems, including records of births and deaths, which served as the main data source for burden calculation.14,15 For cause-specific mortality estimates, GBD studies generate standardized estimates by redistributing initial codes via the Cause of Death Ensemble model by time, age, sex, and geographic location.18 Furthermore, starting from GBD 2021, a new redistribution technique for death codes has been introduced.19 This brought multiple causes of death data, where the model relied on evidence from 7 countries with high-quality death certificate data. In particular, it redistributes a specific proportion of death cases from hypertension to intracerebral hemorrhage. This has resulted in a better understanding of the true underlying causes of hypertension listed on the death certificate.19 Similarly, we collected death data in the Unified Electronic Healthcare System (UNEHS) on hypertensive heart disease (I11-I11.9) and then looked for a history of stroke in these patients. Those who had a history of stroke were further recoded as being died of intracerebral hemorrhage.

Study Population

Mortality data have been taken from the UNEHS. The UNEHS was launched in 2003, and was established in 2014. Data collected from health records and databases are used to provide evidence for policymaking, monitoring trends, and assessing health care services. This made medical records available from various data sources, including vital registration systems that were implemented throughout the country.20 The vital registration systems in Kazakhstan continuously collect and update information on live births and deaths (with corresponding causes of death). Thus, the individual (deidentified) patient data in this study encompassed a unique population registry number for each patient, with additional demographic information such as sex, date of birth, age at death, and date of death with the corresponding cause (ICD-10) linked to the death certificate. Although the UNEHS includes all death registrations in the health care system, deaths occurring within penitentiary and military systems are not captured. Therefore, the number of deaths in our data was approximately 5% to 8% lower than that documented by the Bureau of National Statistics of Kazakhstan. The initial data consisted of 1,202,877 death records with no missing values. The population size of Kazakhstan for any given year was obtained from the local Bureau of National Statistics of Kazakhstan.21

Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average

The prediction of disease patterns is a common application of ARIMA models. This provides reliable results for historical data that exhibit seasonality, cyclicity, and trends. We used augmented Dickey test to test the stationarity. The test returned a P value <0.001, suggesting the series is stationary. To identify the model parameters, the “auto.arima” function from the “forecast” package was employed to select the optimal model. This revealed that the best-fitting models for the nonseasonal and seasonal ARIMA cases were: nonseasonal ARIMA(1,1,1) and seasonal ARIMA(0,1,2)(2,0,0)12. Goodness-of-fit estimations were calculated using the information criterion (Akaike information criterion/Bayesian information criterion). Additionally, the Ljung-Box test on the model residuals yielded a P value of 0.52, implying that the residual sequences were white noise (Supplemental Figure 1). To validate the ARIMA model, we split the data into training and validation sets, and used standard accuracy measures to test the predictions (see the following sections).

Extreme Gradient Boosting

The XGBoost incorporates a supervised machine learning (ML) algorithm that offers highly accurate prediction results through its self-learning capacity.22 It has many advantages over existing gradient-boosting methods, including the ability to create a powerful classifier from a collection of weak classifiers. The XGBoost ensemble consists of 3 primary components: optimization of the loss function; a weak learner to produce a prediction; and an additive model to add weak learners to minimize the loss function. In our model building, month inputs were converted to a common format using a one-hot encoding technique and encoded into a matrix, with columns representing a specific time point. Out-of-sample validation, visual diagnostics, and careful parameter selection were used to detect overfitting. Furthermore, the performance metrics on both the training and test sets were checked to determine whether the difference in performance between the training and test sets was within an acceptable range, further suggesting that the model had learned patterns rather than memorized the training data.

Bayesian Structural Time Series

The BSTS analysis incorporates a Bayesian framework23 that contains distinct error mechanisms for each modular component and is well-designed to manage systemic differences in variability. It simulates from the posterior distribution using a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling procedure, which smooths predictions over a wide number of alternative models. We built a univariate BSTS model with a seasonality of 12 months. The model was fitted using the “bsts” package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with default (weakly informative) prior specifications for all model parameters, and the hyperparameters were not modified. This approach ensures that the results are driven primarily by data rather than by subjective prior beliefs. For the model, 4 chains of 5,000 MCMC samples were retrieved, and the first 2,000 samples were discarded as a warm-up (Supplemental Figures 2 and 3).

Measures

The GBD only presents data on a yearly basis; therefore, we aggregated our monthly counts into years and compared them with those from the GBD. For forecasted estimates, the GBD represents several scenarios of stroke deaths, including the reference and combined effects. According to the reference scenario, life expectancy and disease burden are expected to improve over time, albeit at slower rates. The combined effects, in turn, suggest improvements in behavioral, metabolic, and environmental risk factors.15 All comparisons were made with the reference scenario, assuming it to be a more robust reflection of stroke burden. Given the potential impact of COVID-19 on the absolute difference in death counts, we adjusted the variation in the absolute number of deaths for each year by the calculated average number of deaths: (GBD − Kazakhstan/[(GBD + Kazakhstan)/2]). In addition, the GBD-to-UNEHS ratio was calculated as the ratio of the observed counts in GBD to the counts in Kazakhstan for a given year (2014-2030). The accuracy metrics for the model performance were mean absolute error (MAE), root mean square error, and mean absolute percentage error.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a descriptive data analysis on the mortality data from the UNEHS and GBD. We performed analyses using the monthly death counts, assuming better prediction through capturing seasonality, rather than modelling data at the highest level of aggregation.

The data covered the period from January 2014 to December 2023 (120 months), during which mortality trends were somewhat stable in the pre- and post–COVID-19 pandemic years. We decided to split the data into training (108 months) and testing sets (12 months) for the following reason: accuracy tended to improve in previous studies when the testing set size was increased;24,25 however, when the ratio exceeded 90:10, the models showed less significant improvements. In addition, the time span of our dataset was limited to 10 years. Excluding the COVID-19 period from the training data could negatively affect the predictions, especially for the pandemic years. Thus, a 90:10 train–test split ratio was considered optimal, acknowledging tradeoffs in model performance, measures, and resource constraints. This allowed us to analyze temporal patterns of stroke mortality from January 2014 to December 2022 (108 months), forecast their estimates into a year ahead (12 months), and check the model performance using the accuracy metrics we mentioned earlier. Upon selection of the best-fitting model, death counts were forecasted 7 years ahead, aggregated by year, and used to compare estimates between the databases. Data management, cleaning, and analysis were done on Nazarbayev University’s secure web server. The data analysis was performed using R-software (4.3.2), and incorporated specific packages, namely “forecast,” “bsts,” and “xgboost.”

Results

From 2014 to 2023, the total number of stroke-related deaths reported by the UNEHS in Kazakhstan was 107,552 (Supplemental Table 1). The death counts increased from 8,738 (50.92 per 100,000) in 2014 to 10,429 (52.76 per 100,000) in 2023 (Figure 1). Deaths were primarily attributed to ischemic stroke, followed by intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage. The distribution of cases was somewhat similar between sexes and increased with age advancement.

During 2014-2023 and 2024-2030, the GBD estimated 225,562 and 163,140 stroke-related deaths (388,702 in total), respectively (Supplemental Table 2). The UNEHS, in turn, reported 107,552 and 74,042 deaths (181,594 in total), respectively. This resulted in an absolute difference of 207,108 deaths (Figure 1). Discussion

This study comprehensively analyzed the national database of stroke mortality, forecasted its trends by 2030, and compared the estimates to those from the GBD (Central Illustration). When modelling stroke mortality, the accuracy metrics for seasonal ARIMA, BSTS, and XGBoost were mostly comparable, with a slight advantage of the latter. Calculating the normalized differences in mortality, our study revealed a substantial discrepancy between the databases (0.73). The overall difference was 207,108 cases for the observed and forecasted periods combined.

Stroke Mortality Comparison: National Health Data and GBD Study

his study revealed that the GBD (Global Burden of Disease Study) had a 2-fold higher number of deaths due to stroke than the national health records of the Republic of Kazakhstan. This suggests that issues related to the lack of primary data and the reproducibility of GBD methods are partial explanations. UNEHS = Unified Electronic Healthcare System of Kazakhstan.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the ARIMA, BSTS, and XGBoost models, as well as to explore the differences in stroke mortality between national data and GBD. Over the past decades, ARIMA has been widely used to explore disease dynamics and to forecast future scenarios. However, disease patterns often rely on various external factors and rarely follow a linear function of time, making them unsuitable for such nonlinear trends.26,27 By contrast, ML techniques represent a promising alternative that addresses such limitations through self-learning algorithms. Existing evidence comparing these models suggests contradictions regarding the favored approach. Fang et al27 revealed the ML model (XGBoost) outperforms ARIMA in predicting COVID-19 cases in the United States; however, the investigators compared a univariate ARIMA to the multivariate XGBoost. By contrast, studies28, 29, 30 that kept both models equal did not reveal substantial differences in their accuracies. Likewise, some studies have identified BSTS superiority over ARIMA31, 32, 33 and some ML approaches.34 At the same time, ARIMA was found to be better when modelling infectious diseases in some instances.35 Our findings align with these studies; for short-term projections, the BSTS returned the preferred approach. However, for extended horizons, the model performances were mostly similar, with a slight superiority of the XGBoost. Even though ML models might offer better accuracy, given their complex structure, they should be prioritized for situations in which the datasets are large. For limited or noisy data, simpler statistical models might be preferable. Before choosing a forecasting method, the available resources and the current state of evidence should be considered. Prioritizing a problem-oriented approach over a technique-oriented one may become key.

Kazakhstan launched the development of an integrated public health information system to characterize the frequency and determinants of health problems with special public health relevance in the country,20 and introduced various state programs to modify stroke care.36 The country is associated with a pronounced burden of stroke and prevalence of its risk factors in the population, requiring improvements in preventive and treatment strategies. The distribution of stroke subtypes in our study was similar to that in the largest study on stroke epidemiology in Kazakhstan.36 Furthermore, both vital registration completeness and the percentage of vital registration deaths assigned to major garbage codes in Kazakhstan are among the leading worldwide.19 However, the estimates in our study were substantially lower than those of GBD. The death rates for the analysis period remained almost unchanged in both databases. However, their direct comparison revealed a 2-fold difference, despite the fact that the GBD standard population was almost similar to that reported by officials in Kazakhstan.21 Neither the corresponding management of ICD codes14,17,19 nor identical redistribution19 allowed us to reduce the deviation. Such discrepancies are likely driven by differences in methodology, including the selection of the study population. The population from the national data (UNEHS) was restricted to cases recorded in the health care information system, whereas cases from the penitentiary and military systems were not available. This further leads to problems of selection and representativeness of the study population. Current evidence suggests that the distribution of stroke risk factors is high among these populations, and deaths attributed to cerebrovascular diseases are among the top-ranked causes.37,38 However, these cases are beyond the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Healthcare of Kazakhstan, and the number of stroke-related deaths in these populations is unclear. Nevertheless, based on rough estimates, the death reports are up to 8% lower in the UNEHS than in the official statistics of Kazakhstan, which include the entire population of Kazakhstan. Thus, restrictions related to the data source may partially explain the discrepancies observed between the different databases.

The United Nations defines 5 countries as Central Asia (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan). The GBD adds Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia, and Mongolia to Central Asia.8,10,15 Furthermore, when it comes to super-regions, GBD categorizes Kazakhstan into “Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia.”19 Given the spatiotemporal regression tools incorporated into the GBD methodology, such geographical aggregation has the potential to contribute to the observed differences, the impact of which becomes even more concerning considering the limitations in data sources.17,19

The existing literature on studies comparing national data estimates with those from the GBD is limited to a few publications. A recent study39 taking data from the Chinese Centre for Disease Control compared burden estimates of tropical diseases. This resulted in large discrepancies between the databases, with GBD estimates being substantially higher than the national data. Another study40 contrasted the GBD estimates for stroke and ischemic heart disease by comparing the 2 latest GBD iterations for Western European countries. The investigators also revealed notable inconsistencies in incidence and mortality during the same period, suggesting that heterogeneity in exposure to risk factors, access to care, and demographic changes are explanations. In addition, the investigators raised the importance of considering data completeness, case definition, selection bias, and misclassification, especially when reflecting regional/national GBD estimates. These findings highlight the importance of continuously updating, discussing, validating, and enhancing data collection and analytical methods to improve global health metrics.

GBD studies are the most comprehensive approach for analyzing health metrics over time. However, they mostly present modelled data rather than observed. The estimates are often modelled based on incomplete data, which are thought to be improved in each GBD iteration. To provide reliable estimates, the GBD would require a huge amount of data: the GBD 2021 disease burden estimates for 204 countries over 31 years (6,324 country-years). Looking back at the last 3 GBD studies, in GBD 2017,41 the number of countries with incidence data was 62, 61, and 47 for ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, and subarachnoid hemorrhage, respectively. In GBD 2019,42 the number of data sources remained mostly unchanged. The GBD 2021,19 however, does not provide a number of country sources directly, but referring to the site years for vital registrations, we can conclude that the number of data sources remained the same: 22,567 in 2019 and 23,629 in 2021. For case fatality data, the number of data sources was 3 times lower than that for incidence, suggesting substantial limitations in terms of data sources. Furthermore, GBD 2021 introduced the multiple causes of death category to redistribute garbage codes for hypertension, based on evidence from only 7 countries.19 Relying on the INTERSTROKE (Global and Regional Effects of Potentially Modifiable Risk Factors Associated With Acute Stroke in 32 Countries) study,11 there were notable differences in deaths attributable to stroke, despite the similarities in the distribution of risk factors among developed and developing countries. Taken together, these factors might raise concerns regarding idea of extrapolation of findings from country to country. Thus, the current evidence from the GBD remains limited, and any observations should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, mortality estimates are integral to the primary GBD metric—disability-adjusted life-years; therefore, it is necessary to provide a clear understanding of the reasons for the regional and/or national deviations from GBD. Although this study identified substantial differences between UNEHS and GBD, it is encouraging to note that the overall mortality difference was not as pronounced. From 2014 to 2021, GBD estimated 1,134,227 deaths, whereas UNEHS reported 941,040 deaths. Deaths were, on average, 24,148 lower in the UNEHS (18%). This gap decreased during the early COVID-19 pandemic years (2020-2021); however, it is worth noting that the increase in the overall deaths was notably higher for UNEHS: counts increased by 17% and 11% for GBD, and 24% and 21% for UNEHS during 2019-2020, and 2020-2021, respectively (Supplemental Figure 6). Given the overall similarity in all-cause mortality estimates, further efforts should focus on improving data completeness, refining redistribution methods for death causes, and enhancing extrapolation techniques. Further discussion of these aspects is expected to contribute to the enhancement of the estimates and reduce the deviation between the national, regional, and institutional estimates.

Study limitations

We took data from the GBD that standardizes and extrapolated estimates, which could result in deviations for the true burden estimates for a specific country. The study population was defined based on death certificates and ICD-10 codes, which could result in the misclassification of the true causes of death. However, we tried to minimize these limitations by using broad categories of ICD-10 codes, which is consistent with GBD approaches. It is also plausible that some COVID-19 deaths were incorrectly encoded as stroke, especially during the early pandemic period when medical facilities were limited. This represents one of the major limitations of epidemiological studies during COVID-19, which is related to challenges in the proper identification of deaths caused by cardiovascular diseases (including stroke). Underreporting of stroke mortality itself may be another limitation of this study. Furthermore, acknowledging the impact of underdiagnosed hypertension and lack of autopsy in people who died of stroke, the official data may underestimate the burden of stroke in the population. Finally, given the limited amount of historical data (10 years), our forecast 7 years ahead might not reflect future changes. It is crucial to update the estimates as new data becomes available.

Conclusions

This study provides critical insights into the comparability of stroke estimates reported by the GBD and UNEHS. We observed UNEHS to have 2 times lower estimates compared with GBD, which concerns given methodological similarities. Further research is required to investigate the discrepancies between national data and GBD with respect to modelling approaches and limitations. Current issues related to the lack of primary data and reproducibility suggest caution in the interpretation of the GBD findings.
