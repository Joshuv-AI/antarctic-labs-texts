Predicting hospice eligibility among dementia patients using language models
Authors: Thomas H McCoy, Roy H Perlis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) are increasing in prevalence, and access to potential benefits of hospice care remains challenging. Large language models (LLMs), like GPT‐4o, applied to electronic health records (EHRs) could support decisions by estimating mortality risk.METHODSWe analyzed patients with ADRD diagnosis from two academic medical centers. GPT‐4o was used to estimate 6‐month mortality risk from discharge summaries without any retraining or preprocessing. We used Cox regression to assess associations between predictions and time to death.RESULTSOf 9872 individuals, 3563 (36%) died within 6 months. GPT‐4o predictions stratified risk of death within 6 months (log‐rank p < 0.001, area under the curve [AUC] = 0.79); predictions were strongly associated with mortality in Cox regression models (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 31.02 95% confidence interval [CI] 27.44–35.08, p < 0.001) with similar results between sites.DISCUSSIONGPT‐4o can stratify mortality risk using routinely generated documentation, potentially facilitating hospice referral decisions, but more prospective work is needed.Highlights Large language models (LLMs) can estimate 6‐month mortality in patients with dementia.GPT‐4o estimates of mortality risk from discharge summaries were highly discriminative (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.79).Predictions may support hospice referral decisions. Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, clinical decision support, dementia care, electronic health records (EHRs), end‐of‐life care, hospice eligibility, large language models (LLMs), mortality prediction, natural language processing (NLP), prediction modeling

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) are common, costly, and projected to grow more so in the decades ahead. 1 , 2 At present, nearly 7 million Americans are living with ADRD, and this number is expected to double in the decades ahead. 3 Whereas many forms of mortality have decreased in recent decades, deaths attributed to ADRD have increased such that it is now among the leading causes of death in older Americans. 4 Advancing ADRD imparts a substantial burden of disease to patients and places financial, physical, and emotional demands on their caregivers, who are largely family members. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 In the process, the condition costs $196–450 billion annually and costs will grow to $1.4 trillion by 2060. 12

Dementia care, particularly in the later stages of illness, can be poorly matched to need, with high‐intensity treatment like feeding tubes or critical care encounters occurring near the end of life. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Such high‐intensity treatment, through clinical momentum and care fragmentation, can drive further high‐intensity care, 15 , 17 , 18 contributing to the substantial financial burden of dementia care faced by families. 16 Yet a preference for high‐intensity treatment is rare among those with advanced ADRD. 19 , 20 , 21 Alternatively, hospice care can provide superior outcomes for patients and families at the end of life. 22 Attention to goals of care reduces hospital transfers and improves attention to symptom burden without reducing life span 23 ; hospice enrollment increases the odds of dying in a preferred location, reduces odds of hospital death, improves pain management, and reduces unmet needs at the end of life. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 In the process, hospice can reduce the cost of care at the end of life among those with ADRD. 28

Despite the potential benefits of hospice, access remains difficult. Medicare hospice eligibility requires a life expectancy of 6 months or less; however, neither standard mortality models nor disease‐specific scales provide the desired prognostic accuracy. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 Progression of ADRD and approach to end of life do not necessarily move in parallel, and both may include an extended period of phenotypically similar dependency. 34 , 35 This pattern of progression and associated prognostic challenge is a further barrier to hospice enrollment. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 This irregularity of symptom progression and prognostic difficulty combine such that patients enrolling in hospice with dementia go on to experience involuntary live discharge at the highest rates. 40

The use of natural language processing (NLP)—a branch of computer science focused on algorithms applicable to free form human language like that found in doctors notes or clinical research articles 41 —applied to clinical text of hospitalized patients has been shown to predict time to ADRD diagnosis as well as time to death among those with an existing ADRD diagnosis. 42 These simple clinical NLP approaches, driven by counting specific words contributing to a cognitive phenotype, have genomic and postmortem neuropathologic correlates. 43 , 44 , 45 More recently, large language models (LLMs), 46 a form of artificial intelligence (AI) applicable to natural language, have been shown to perform well in predicting other clinical outcomes. 47 , 48 , 49 Here, we examined whether OpenAI's GPT‐4o LLM could stratify risk for 6‐month mortality among individuals with a dementia diagnosis, as a means of facilitating identification of individuals appropriate for hospice referral.

METHODS

We previously identified cohorts with a coded electronic health record (EHR) diagnosis of dementia discharged from two academic medical centers (AMCs). 24 , 27 , 42 Social security vitals and state death certificate data were used to identify time of death. Additional clinical characteristics were extracted from EHRs, consistent with our prior work, to allow univariate contrasts of the clinical populations and practice variation between the two sites, in terms of length of stay, intensive care unit (ICU) care, surgical care, and other markers of course.

For prediction of mortality in ADRD, we prompted a secure deployment of GPT‐4o with temperature set at 0, stating “You are a skilled neurologist evaluating a hospital discharge summary note. Based on the discharge note, please report the probability of death within 6 months, from 0%–100%,” with each discharge summary. Discharge summaries were supplied exactly as recorded in the health record without any form of preprocessing, cleaning, feature enrichment, or modification of any kind. Only the information included by the discharge summary author at the time of discharge was available to the model. No supplemental data, feature engineering, or other local knowledge was used to improve predictability, and thus this constitutes an external validation.

Throughout, the mortality predictions made by GPT‐4o were used exactly as generated by the model without any form of specific retraining. Evaluation of the scores progressed through four phases: (1) analysis in a right censored time to event survival paradigm; (2) application of a standard regression paradigm among those with either an outcome or sufficient follow‐up to confirm the absence of an outcome; (3) analysis as a would‐be classifier; (4) follow‐up explanatory analysis to further characterize the prior results in terms of risk of bias, document composition driving GPT‐4o risk, and by comparison to other possible risk scores. Our primary survival analysis included Kaplan–Meier survival curves by quartile of estimated risk, followed by Cox regression—in both site‐specific and pooled cohorts—to examine the association between predicted risk and mortality. To supplement these primary analyses, among individuals with follow‐up of at least 6 months, we examined the association between predicted risk and mortality in univariate logistic regression along with visualization of receiver‐operating characteristic plots, calibration plots, and decision curves. 50 As a supplemental sensitivity analysis, we compared the summary decision curve analysis of GPT‐4o risk to patient age, document length, and a previously‐published NLP algorithm for extracting cognitive symptom burden.

To enable contingency table analysis of the sort most familiar to clinicians trained in lab test interpretation, we applied Youden's method to dichotomize GPT‐4o risk predictions. 51 To further elucidate those classification results we examined the associations among the Youden's dichotomized GPT‐4o risk estimate and sociodemographic and clinical features, as a means of identifying potential bias in these estimates in terms of gender or race and ethnicity. Finally, as a quantitative means of identifying concepts driving GPT‐4o prediction, we developed a structural topic model with the text of the discharge summaries that were the lone basis of GPT‐4o predictions, allowing both topic prevalence and vocabulary to vary as a function of classification. 52 We used the Mimno Lee method to set the optimal number of topics. 53 Because topics are probability distributions, no specific name is strictly correct 54 ; however, in keeping with prior work on topic modeling we name topics with the top two tokens from the topic the form “< word 1 > ‐ < word 2 > ++” where the “++” reminds readers of the distribution that follows. When selecting top tokens, tokens were ranked by FRequency and EXclusivity (FREX) score. 55

RESULTS

Among the two AMCs (Site A, n = 6049; Site B, n = 3823), the combined cohort of patients with a diagnosis of dementia at or before hospitalization had a mean age of 83 (standard deviation [SD] = 9) and a median age of 84 (interquartile range [IQR] 78–89); 44% (4393/9872) identified as male; 1.6% (158/9872) as Asian, 5.9% (586/9872) as Black, 83% (8157/9872) as White, and 1.0% (100/9872) as another race. Overall, 36% (3563/9872) were deceased within 6 months, whereas 4.6% (452/9872) were discharged with any form of hospice care (Table 1).

The two sites differed significantly in operational characteristics. Patients at Site A were discharged after a median length of stay of 6 days (IQR 3–9), whereas the median length of stay at Site B was 5 days (IQR 3–8, p < 0.001). Site A discharges included prior intensive care unit (ICU) care in 27% (1642/6049) of cases versus 23% (869/3823) of discharges from Site B (p < 0.001). Finally, Site A discharges included surgical care in 16% (973/6049) of cases versus 3.8% (145/3823) of cases at Site B (p < 0.001). Documentation also differed significantly between the two sites, with a mean discharge summary length of 11,935 characters at Site A and 17,701 at Site B. Note length was positively correlated with risk of mortality as predicted by GPT‐4o, such that a 1 SD increase in note length was associated with a 3% increase in predicted mortality (coefficient = 0.03, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.02–0.04, p < 0.001).

Figure 1 depicts survival of the cohort discharged from Site A (Figure 1A) and Site B (Figure 1B) stratified by quartile of predicted 6‐month mortality probability as made by GPT‐4o without any form of fine‐tuning, in‐sample model development, or post hoc recalibration. At Site A, 88% (95% CI 86%–89%; 1319/1506) of patients in the lowest risk quartile remained alive at 6 months compared to 26% (95% CI 24%–29%; 400/1506) of patients in the highest risk quartile. At Site B, 90% (95% CI 88%–92%; 860/952) of patients in the lowest risk quartile remained alive at 6 months compared to 27% (95% CI 25%–30%; 261/952) of patients in the highest risk quartile. Overall, quartile of GPT‐4o predicted risk was associated with survival in log‐rank test stratified by site (χ 2 = 3735, df = 7, p < 0.001). In a Cox proportional hazard model of all‐cause mortality adjusting for clinical and operational characteristics, the raw GPT‐4o predicted probability of 6‐month mortality was significantly associated with increased hazard for death (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 31.02, 27.44–35.08; Table 2) in a pooled cohort combining both sites. Similarly, significant associations between the GPT‐4o‐made predictions of 6‐month mortality and actual hazard for death were observed in separate proportional hazard models for each site individually (Table 2).

Next, in the 99.5% (9832/9872; Site A = 6024, Site B = 3808) of patients with sufficient follow‐up for observation of a 6‐month outcome, we evaluated the predicted probability of mortality—again without any recalibration, in‐sample model fitting, or fine‐tuning—via a cross‐sectional prediction paradigm to supplement survival analysis (Table 3). GPT‐4o predictions of 6‐month mortality were well correlated with observed 6‐month mortality, with an intercept of –0.20 (95% CI –0.25 to ‐0.16) and slope of 1.31 (95% CI 1.25–1.37) in the pooled cohort, approaching ideal calibration when plotted by site (Figure 2A,B). Discrimination was acceptable, with a pooled area under the curve (AUC) of 0.79 (95% CI 0.78–0.80) and an AUC of 0.79 (95% CI 0.77–0.80) at Site A (Figure 3A) and 0.79 (95% CI 0.77–0.81) at Site B (Figure 3B). Decision curve analysis showed an opportunity for use of the raw GPT‐4o predicted probability to produce a net benefit over a range of risk thresholds, including at both the larger Site A (Figure 4A) and smaller Site B (Figure 4B). We also developed a decision curve (Figure S1) comparing the GPT‐4o risk scores in the pooled cohort to alternative risk stratification measures based on age alone, document length (recall the two sites differ significantly in documentation length), and previously validated NLP‐based cognitive symptom burden phenotype, and we found that the predictions of mortality made by GPT‐4o provided the highest net benefit across the clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities.

When GPT‐4o risk scores were dichotomized at the cut‐point identified by Youden's method (p = 0.6), the pooled cohort yielded a sensitivity of 44%, specificity of 93%, positive predictive value of 79%, and negative predictive value of 74% (based on 414 false positives [FPs], 1550 true positives [TPs], 5855 true negatives [TNs], and 2013 false negatives [FNs]). Finally, to quantify semantic characteristics that were driving model performance we developed a structural topic model in which both topic prevalence and topic vocabulary varied by dichotomized classification result. The Mimno Lee method identified 72 topics within the corpus, and the top (by FREX) tokens for each topic are shown in Table S1. Figure S2 shows the estimated difference in topic prevalence by classification result as compared to the modal classification result of TN. Of the 72 topics (Figure S2), one or more , , topics were uniquely less prevalent in each classification result (e.g., FN: Bactrim‐Rash++; FP: Finger‐Reflexes++; TP: Graft‐Bypass++) and one or more was uniquely more prevalent in each classification outcome result (e.g., FN: Lasix–Diuresis++; FP: Tube‐Gtube++; TP: Stroke‐Droop++). One topic was less prevalent in both FN and FP results (Carotid‐Aneurysm++), whereas eight topics were less prevalent in the true cases whether classed as TN and FN results (e.g., Decline‐Memory++) and four were more prevalent in this group (e.g., Injection‐Torsemide++). Only one topic (Fluticasone‐Spray++) demonstrated a differential direction of effect with increased prevalence in TPs and decreased prevalence in FNs.

DISCUSSION

In two different AMCs with substantial differences in patient characteristics, lengths of stay, and documentation patterns as evidenced by note length, we found that GPT‐4o, a general‐purpose LLM applied without any retraining or fine tuning, was able to accurately estimate 6‐month mortality among individuals with a dementia diagnosis. The raw GPT‐4o predictions made on the basis of discharge summary documentation alone, without fine‐tuning or feature engineering of any kind, were well calibrated at both sites.

This GPT‐4o result from zero shot learning in an off‐the‐shelf frontier model extends prior efforts to build this sort of prediction (see, e.g., 56 ), which have generally relied on supervised rather than unsupervised methods. Whereas supervised health care–specific methods raise concern for overfitting and in sample validation, the approach in this manuscript of relying on GPT‐4o sidesteps those issues. This result, based on text only without any augmentation by structured data of any kind, is particularly notable when one considers the difference among hospital documentation patterns as evidenced by document length differences. In survival modeling, the need for surgical care during the index admission was associated with increased hazard for death, and many of the topics identified in post hoc exploratory analysis are consistent with documentation of surgical considerations (e.g., Displaced‐Osteopenia++, Femur‐Bearing++, Graft‐Bypass++, Shunt‐Hydrocephalus++, and Carotid‐Aneurysm++), suggesting a possible direction for prompt engineering efforts or further process mapping building on existing work identifying a critical role for surgeons in hospice care referral. 57 , 58 Although optimism about the benefits of AI in medicine is warranted, concerns about the risk of bias are equally well founded, as biases in referral and utilization patterns exist. 59 , 60 , 61

This initial demonstration could guide further work to improve clinical outcomes for those living with advanced ADRD. Disparities and inefficiencies in hospice utilization are both long‐standing and persistent despite considerable effort to improve referral pipelines. 62 , 63 LLMs are already changing care by acting as digital scribes that automate note preparation, thereby freeing up physician time previously spent on documentation 64 ; early evidence suggests that LLM scribes change practice patterns. 65 , 66 It is possible that those changes could also support optimized hospice care of those with advanced ADRD. As our result using GPT‐4o was based on documentation alone and that documentation was used without any feature engineering or site‐specific customization, it is possible that the output of ambient clinical scribes could be routed directly to risk stratification tools that build on our initial result. The choice to focus on an inpatient context of care is particularly relevant, as patients with ADRD are frequently hospitalized near the end of life 67 , 68 , 69 and those hospitalizations are key moments to reconsider clinical momentum.

Given the ubiquity of ADRD and its impact on patients and families, any opportunity for progress must be considered; however, our results require further investigation, and many of the limitations in hospice referral are unlikely to be addressed by a purely technical system. 70 The introduction of any such system will create new questions about algorithmic fairness and transparency that must be answered to patient and provider satisfaction before clinical use. 71 Moreover, any material change in hospice referral could overtax an already strained system, creating additional threats to distributive justice or dismissing patient access to the aspects of care they most value. 72

We note multiple caveats. First, LLM transparency is limited, and the effort here to explain classification results by topic modeling is not directly connected to the process by which GPT‐4o makes predictions. The emergence of models using chain‐of‐thought or reasoning approaches may help in this regard. 73 Second, these models remain vulnerable to bias, yielding answers that differ by sociodemographic groups, and drift, yielding different answers over time. Further work will be required to address these challenges 74 if such models are deployed in clinical settings. The fact that model performance in this analysis did not vary by race is encouraging; however, the demographic data available in the registration system were limited and modest gender effects were present. Although this work did not involve model training, training data for commercial frontier models are unlikely to be aligned optimally with all possible use cases, and thus evaluation in traditionally underrepresented populations is particularly important. 75 This study also did not have access to structured scales like the Palliative Performance scale or Functional Assessment Staging Tool, and thus could not directly compare classification results. Finally, as any documentation‐driven approach is conditioned on an expert clinician guiding the interview on which that documentation is based—whether written in a conventional sense or captured by an ambient scribe—a system of this kind is necessarily additive to clinician judgment.

Nonetheless, our results suggest the utility of computational approaches employing LLMs like GPT‐4o for disease‐specific mortality forecasting of the sort useful in hospice eligibility discussions. It is encouraging that this result indicates that approaches of this kind could be broadly applicable—that is, may not require retraining at each site—as no site‐specific training or feature engineering was required to obtain generally similar performance of GPT‐4o in two distinct hospitals, which have substantial variation in documentation practices as evidenced by note length variability. Implementing predictions at time of discharge summary completion may enable better planning and use of resources for this large, costly, and rapidly growing clinical population. 2 , 76
