Whole-genome characterization of Newcastle disease virus isolates of sub-genotype VII.1.1 from chickens in Ethiopia
Authors: Berihun Dires Mihiretu, Tatsufumi Usui, Tesfaye Rufael Chibssa, Abde Aliy Mohammed, Tsuyoshi Yamaguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNewcastle disease (ND) is a serious poultry disease in Ethiopia. F gene sequences have shown that the velogenic Newcastle disease virus (NDV) sub-genotype VII.1.1 is prevalent in Ethiopia and other East African countries. However, the F gene sequence alone is not enough to fully understand the pathogenicity, antigenicity, and epidemiological characteristics of NDVs. Whole-genome characterizations of currently circulating virus strains are needed to understand the molecular basis of the pathogenicity and antigenicity differences between vaccine and field strains. In this study, we obtained the complete genome sequences of seven NDV sub-genotype VII.1.1 isolated from chicken farms in Ethiopia and compared them with closely related NDVs and vaccine strains. Whole-genome sequencing revealed that all isolates have genome length of 15,192 nucleotides and are composed of six genes in the order 3′-NP-P-M-F-HN-L-5′. Phylogenetic analysis of the whole genomes showed that sub-genotype VII.1.1 can be classified into three distinct lineages and that the Ethiopian isolates in this study clustered within lineage 1. Unique amino acid substitutions were identified in all six genes of the Ethiopian isolates when compared to closely related NDV strains and commonly used vaccine strains. Some of these substitutions have been linked to antigenic variation. Although sub-genotype VII.1.1 is widespread in Ethiopia and other horn of African countries, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing its whole-genome characteristics in this region. Introduction

Newcastle disease virus (NDV), also known as Orthoavulavirus javaense, is an enveloped, non-segmented, negative-sense single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the genus Orthoavulavirus of the subfamily Avulavirinae [1]. NDV is a causative agent of Newcastle disease (ND), a devastating viral poultry disease associated with large worldwide economic losses [2].

NDVs have three different complete genome lengths: 15,186, 15,192 and 15,198 nucleotides [3] and contain six genes in the order 3′-NP–P–M–F–HN–L-5′. Extracistronic sequences, known as the leader and trailer are found at the 3′ and 5′ ends of the genome, respectively [4]. The six genes are separated by junction sequences composed of three distinct elements: gene-end (GE), intergenic (IG), and gene-start (GS) sequences [5]. These genes encode six structural proteins namely nucleocapsid protein (NP), phosphoprotein (P), Matrix (M), fusion (F), hemagglutinin-neuraminidase (HN), and large protein (L) [6, 7]. There are also two nonstructural proteins, V and W, which are produced by P gene RNA editing [8]. Two of the structural proteins (HN and F) are surface glycoproteins that are important for virus attachments and fusion to the host cell, respectively [9]. Both HN and F induce virus neutralizing antibodies [10, 11]. The antigenic determinates of NDV include HN protein receptor binding sites [12] and neutralizing epitopes [13–15], and F protein neutralizing epitopes [16, 17]. Furthermore, the amino acid sequence at the F protein cleavage site is used to differentiate between avirulent and virulent NDV strains. Avirulent strains have the sequence 112G/E-K/R-Q-G/E-R-L117, while virulent strains are characterized by the sequence 112R/K-R-Q/K/R-K/R-R-F117 [18].

The internal structural proteins NP, P and L, which are associated with viral RNA and form a ribonucleoprotein complex, are essential for RNA synthesis. The L polymerase protein contributes to NDV virulence, with its effect sometimes enhanced by interactions with NP and P proteins [19]. Another internal structural protein, M, plays a vital role in viral assembly by interacting with the nucleocapsid and lipid bilayer [20]. The two non-structural proteins V and W are also reported to contribute to pathogenicity by inhibiting interferon activity [21, 22].

NDVs are classified into two distinct classes, Class I and Class II, based on the complete open reading frame (ORF) of the F gene. Class I comprises a single genotype and is primarily associated with avirulent strains that are commonly detected in apparently healthy wild birds and poultry. In contrast, Class II is more diverse, containing 21 genotypes (I–XXI) and includes both avirulent and virulent strains [23]. Among these genotypes, genotype VII which is classified into three sub-genotypes: VII.1.1, VII.1.2, and VII.2 [23], is widely distributed geographically and is currently one of the most prevalent genotypes globally [24].

Recent studies [25–27] have shown that sub-genotype VII.1.1 of genotype VII is the most prevalent strain in Ethiopia. This sub-genotype has also been reported in geographically proximate countries such as Eritrea [28], Sudan [29], and several countries in the Middle East [30–32]. The virus may have entered Ethiopia from neighboring countries through the movement of poultry and contaminated fomites. It is essential to conduct genetic analysis of currently circulating virus strains to better understand their epidemiology, pathogenicity and antigenicity, and to establish more effective prevention practices. However, all NDV genotype VII studies in East Africa, including Ethiopia, have been limited to F gene sequences [25–29, 33]. In this study, we characterized the complete genomes of NDV isolates from both vaccinated and unvaccinated chickens in Ethiopia and compared them with closely related NDVs and the two commonly used vaccines in the country, LaSota and Hitchner B1 (HB1). Mutations in recent NDV strains may lead to increased virulence and antigenic variation. The present results should help in efforts to develop more effective vaccines against circulating field strains.

Materials and methods

NDVs used in this study

Our previous study has shown that the 25 NDV isolates obtained from Ethiopian chickens belonged to sub-genotype VII.1.1 and were grouped into five distinct clusters based on the partial F gene phylogenetic tree [27]. In this study, seven representative isolates were selected for whole-genome sequencing to reflect the genetic diversity among the five clusters and to represent different geographic locations, vaccination statuses, and production systems of the 25 isolates. The samples included four isolates from vaccinated chickens collected during farm visits and from an outbreak site (NDV/chicken/Ethiopia_BS2T14/2023 (BS2T14), NDV/chicken/Ethiopia_HT2T1/2023 (HT2T1), NDV/chicken/Ethiopia_HT9C15/2023 (HT9C15), and NDV/chicken/Ethiopia_AA3T1/2023 (AA3T1)); two isolates from unvaccinated backyard chickens sampled during farm visits (NDV/chicken/Ethiopia_SZ1T2/2023 (SZ1T2) and NDV/chicken/Ethiopia_WS10C7/2023 (WS10C7)); and one isolate from live-bird-market (LBM) with unknown vaccination history (NDV/chicken/Ethiopia-SBLBM12C1/2023 (SBLBM12C1)). All samples were collected from chickens showing clinical signs consistent with NDV infection, including torticollis, respiratory distress, greenish diarrhea, ruffled feathers, and depression. Virus isolation, antigenicity, and pathogenicity of these isolates were described in the previous study [27].

Whole-genome sequencing and analysis

Viral RNAs were extracted from the NDV positive allantoic fluids using Quick-RNA viral kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA samples were sent to Genome-Lead (Kagawa, Japan) for sequencing. Libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy RNA Directional Library Prep Set (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Cat. No. 1000006385) and sequenced using the paired-end method on the DNBSEQ-T7RS platform (MGI Tech Co., Ltd.) with the DNBSEQ-T7RS High-throughput Sequencing Set. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy RNA Directional Library Prep Set (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Cat. No. 1000006385). The prepared libraries were then sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7RS platform (MGI Tech Co., Ltd.) using the DNBSEQ-T7RS High-throughput Sequencing Set. Sequence analysis was conducted using Geneious Prime 2025.1 (Auckland, New Zealand). Briefly, the paired reads were merged at a normal merge rate. Sequences were assembled by de novo assembly using Tadpole program after excluding sequences with less than 100 bp. The closely related sequences to the contigs were obtained by the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) from local database C-RVDB version 26 [34]. The highest hit was then used to retrieve reference strain from GenBank by BLAST search. Finally, sequence reads were mapped to the reference sequences using Geneious assembler and whole-genome consensus sequences were generated. The whole-genome sequences and open reading frames of all six genes were viewed by A plasmid editor ApE [35]. Nucleotide sequences of closely related NDVs and commonly used vaccine strains in Ethiopia (LaSota, accession number: AF077761.1 and HB1, accession number: AF375823.1) were obtained from GenBank for comparison with Ethiopian field isolates. Pairwise nucleotide and deduced amino acid comparison was conducted by Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo). For amino acid comparisons between Ethiopian isolate proteins and closely related NDVs, the amino acid sequences of each NDV protein of closely related NDVs were aligned using MUltiple Sequence Comparison by Log-Expectation (MUSCLE) in Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis Version X (MEGA X) [36]. Consensus sequences were then generated using Jalview [37]. Whole-genome nucleotide sequences of the seven Ethiopian strains analyzed in this study were deposited in NCBI GenBank/EMBL/DDBJ and can be accessed by their corresponding accession numbers (LC874209-LC874215).

Phylogenetic analysis

Whole-genome nucleotide sequences of the seven Ethiopian isolates, starting from the GS of the NP gene to the GE of the L gene (15,023 bp), excluding the 3′ (leader) and 5′ (trailer) ends, were BLAST-searched to retrieve closely related sequences from the GenBank database. As of January 15, 2025, a total of 163 genotype VII sub-genotype VII.1.1 whole-genome sequences were available in GenBank. The genotypes of these strains were confirmed by phylogenetic analysis using a reference F gene dataset previously reported by Dimitrov et al[23]. After removing duplicates, sequences with missing metadata (location, collection date, host), and recombinant viruses identified using the RDP4 program [38], 118 taxa were retained for phylogenetic analysis (Supplementary Table 1). No recombinant isolates were identified among the Ethiopian isolates. These 118 sub-genotypes VII.1.1 taxa, including vaccine strains (LaSota and HB1) and the Ethiopian isolates, were aligned using MUSCLE in MEGA X [36]. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method with the General Time Reversible (GTR) model and 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The tree was exported in Newick format and visualized using FigTree v1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). The Shimodaira–Hasegawa (SH) test [39] and Approximately Unbiased (AU) test [40] were conducted in IQ-TREE [41] to assess the statistical support for the constructed tree topology.

Evolutionary distance

The average evolutionary distances of the whole-genome nucleotide sequences, each gene ORF nucleotide sequences and deduced amino acid sequences were estimated using Maximum Composite Likelihood model [42] in MEGA X 36). The gamma distribution (shape parameter = 1) was used to analyze the rate variation among sites. Codon positions included were 1st + 2nd + 3rd + Noncoding. All positions containing gaps and missing data were eliminated from the dataset.

Results

Genomic characteristics

All seven Ethiopian NDV isolates had a genome length of 15,192 nucleotides in which the six genes were organized in order of 3′-NP-P-M-F-HN-L-5′. All isolates had 55 leader and 114 trailer nucleotide sequences. The gene-start (GS) of all 5 genes were identical (ACGGGTAGAA), except for the L gene (ACGGGTAGGA). The gene-end (GE) of P and HN (TTAAGAAAAAA) and M and L (TTAGAAAAAA) were identical in all isolates. One nucleotide difference was observed in the GE of NP (TTAGAAAAAAA/G) and F (TTAAGAAAAAC/A). The intergenic (IG) sequences between NP-P, P-M, and M-F were 1 nucleotide. The IG sequences between F-HN and HN-L were 31 and 47 nucleotides, respectively (Table 1).

All six ORFs of the Ethiopian isolates were compared among themselves, with closely related NDV strains from GenBank, and with commonly used vaccine strains (Supplementary Table 2). Comparison of the coding sequence of each gene among Ethiopian NDV isolates showed high nucleotide (96.3–99.91%) and amino acid (96.46–100%) similarities. P and F genes were relatively diverse with nucleotide identities (96.3–99.76%) and amino acid identities (96.46–100%) compared to the other genes. All six of the coding sequences of the Ethiopian NDV isolates were closely related to the Iranian NDV strain (Clone RT 40/Iran, GenBank accession number: ON184061.1) with nucleotide identities (95.88–97.96%) and amino acid identities (94.68–99.18%) identity. Comparison of each gene coding sequence of the seven Ethiopian isolates with LaSota and HB1 vaccine strains showed high genetic variation among all genes. The NP and L genes were the most conserved with sequence identities that ranged from 84.29–85.02% (nucleotide) and 90.39–93.19% (amino acid). The next most conserved genes were the M and F genes with sequence identities that ranged from 82.83–84.29% (nucleotide) and 86.26–88.74% (amino acid). The most diverse were the P and HN genes, with sequence identities ranging from 80.89–82.25% (nucleotide) and 78.48–87.39% (amino acid). The whole-genome nucleotide sequence showed 97.18–99.57% and 97.03–97.4% identity between Ethiopian NDV isolates and the most closely related strain from the GenBank (accession number: ON184061.1), respectively. However, the whole-genome nucleotide sequence demonstrated less identity (81.58–81.97%) with the LaSota and HB1 vaccine strains.

Comparison of amino acid sequences of internal NDV proteins of Ethiopian isolates with other sub-genotype VII.1.1

Comparison of the predicted acid sequences of the polymerase-associated internal NDV proteins (NP, P and L) revealed that all Ethiopian isolates in this study had 9 unique amino acid substitutions in the NP protein, 17 in the P protein, and 30 in the L protein when compared to the consensus sequence derived from 118 NDV strains of sub-genotype VII.1.1 (Table 2). The envelope-associated internal M protein had 4 amino acid substitutions (D2N, G31R, V7M, and R363K) compared to other sub-genotype VII.1.1.

Analysis of surface glycoproteins essential for the virus attachment and fusion to the host cell (HN and F)

The functional domain of the F protein from the seven Ethiopian isolates revealed several distinct substitutions compared to the consensus sequence derived from the 118 sub-genotype VII.1.1 NDV sequences. A total of six substitutions: 4 at the signal peptide (R18W, M20V, S24G and T29I), and 2 at the transmembrane domain (F514V and V520G) were detected in all Ethiopian isolates compared to the consensus sequences. Additional substitutions that were unique to individual Ethiopian strain were also observed. Six isolates except the SZ1T2 had I22T substitution at the signal peptide. SZ1T2 had 4 substitutions: M14T at the signal peptides, S278P at the Heptad repeats b (HRb), and Y504H and Y509A at the transmembrane domain. BS2T14 and HT15C had 4 substitutions: P12S, L13S and I19N at the signal peptide and D170N at the HRa. WS10C7 had 3 substitutions I9F and T17I at the signal peptide, and K78R at the neutralizing epitope. HT2T1 had 1 substitution (L23S) at the signal peptide and SBLBM12C1 had 3 substitutions: M14I and I19T at the signal peptide, and A149V at the HRa. All of the seven Ethiopian isolates had identical amino acid sequences with that of the consensus sequence at the F gene cleavage site (112RRQKRF117). Compared to the commonly used NDV vaccine strains (LaSota and HB1), the Ethiopian isolates had 30 to 37 amino acid substitutions (14 to 16 at the signal peptide, 3 at the cleavage site, 3 at the fusion peptide, 1 to 2 at the HRa, 2 at the HRb, 6 at the HRc and 4 to 5 at the transmembrane domain and 0 to1 at the neutralizing epitope). The 12 cysteine residues and six potential N-linked glycosylation sites showed identical conserved sequences among Ethiopian isolates, consensus sequences derived from genotype VII.1.1 and vaccine strains (Fig. 1).

Phylogenetic and evolutionary analyses

Phylogenetic analysis based on whole-genome sequences revealed that NDV strains from only a few countries have been deposited in GenBank. The vast majority (74.6%, 88/118) of these sequences were for Chinese strains. Based on tree topology, branch support bootstrap value > 90% and evolutionary distance of > 0.05, sub-genotype VII.1.1 NDVs were classified into three distinct lineages, which we designated as lineages 1 to 3. All current Ethiopian isolates, along with three recent NDV strains reported from Russia during the same period, clustered within lineage 1. This lineage also included NDV strains from Ukraine (1), Iran (2), Iraq (4), and Russia (2) that were detected between 2004 and 2021. Lineage 2 was more diverse and had 92 NDV strains that were detected between 1999 and 2019 in China (81), North Macedonia (3), Ukraine (2), Colombia (1) and Egypt (5). Lineage 3 included 14 strains from China (7), Taiwan (6), and Japan (1) that were detected between 1997 and 2019 (Fig. 3). The SH and AU tests supported the topology of the whole-genome tree (p-SH = 1; p-AU = 0.51).

A similar pattern of clustering was observed across all the 6 NDV genes (Supplementary Fig. 1). The average evolutionary distance of whole-genome sequences and each gene ORF and amino acid sequences among the three lineages within sub-genotype VII.1.1 showed that lineage 1 NDVs which include the Ethiopian isolates were more distantly related to the other three lineages, with nucleotide distances ranging from 0.059 to 0.075 for whole-genome sequences (Table 3). Discussion

Whole-genome sequencing of NDVs in this study revealed significant genetic diversity and mutations associated with antigenic variation in sub-genotype VII.1.1. Phylogenetic and evolutionary distance analysis of the whole-genome sequences showed that NDV sub-genotype VII.1.1 can be classified into three lineages based on branch support > 90% and evolutionary distance > 0.05. This classification was supported by the observed bootstrap values and evolutionary distances. In addition, the Shimodaira–Hasegawa (SH) test [39] and Approximately Unbiased (AU) test [40] supported the topology of the whole-genome tree (p-SH = 1; p-AU = 0.51). For this analysis, we included all available NDV genotype VII.1.1 sequences deposited in GenBank as of January 15, 2025. The observed imbalance in lineage sizes reflects limited data availability; for example, 74.6% of the deposited strains originated from China, with most belonging to lineage 2, which contains considerably more strains than lineages 1 and 3. By incorporating all publicly available sequences, our analysis provides the most comprehensive representation of NDV sub-genotype VII.1.1 diversity currently available. To draw more robust conclusions, it is essential to accumulate whole-genome sequences from a larger number of strains isolated from diverse geographic regions in the future. The Ethiopian isolates, along with other recently reported NDVs belonging to lineage 1 (Fig. 3) were genetically distant from the other two lineages (Table 3). Such genetic diversity in NDVs has not been observed in phylogenetic analyses based on F gene sequences. These findings suggest that there is an ongoing evolution within this sub-genotype. The NDVs within the same sub-genotype VII.1.1, were classified with a bootstrap support value ≤ 70% based on the F gene sequence (Supplementary Fig. 1D) according to the criteria established by Dimitrov et al [23]. However, whole-genome analysis showed that the lineages in this study met the sub-genotyping criteria (bootstrap support > 70% and evolutionary distance > 0.05). With the advancement of sequencing technologies and their increased accessibility, whole-genome-based classification may provide a more reliable and accurate approach for NDV genotyping and sub-genotyping than classifications based only on F gene sequences.

To understand the antigenic properties of NDVs isolated in Ethiopia, antigenic determinants of the deduced amino acids of the F and HN genes (which are surface glycoproteins that are important for virus fusion and vaccines attachment) were analyzed. Compared to the commonly used vaccine strains (LaSota and HB1), several amino acid substitutions were detected in the antigenic sites of the Ethiopian isolates. In the neutralizing epitopes of the F protein, three of the Ethiopian isolates had amino acid substitutions (one isolate with the K78R substitution and two with D170N). These substitutions have also been reported in sub-genotype VII.1.1 strains from Egypt and Saudi Arabia [32, 45, 46]. The D170N substitution is found in antigenic epitope A4, positioned at the N-terminus of HRa. Due to its location, this epitope is less exposed and less accessible to antibodies, which significantly limits the potential for escape mutations to arise [47]. In the neutralizing epitope of the HN protein, all of the Ethiopian isolates had the E347Q, N263K, G494D, and I514V substitutions at the neutralizing epitope of the HN protein. These mutations may have contributed to the significant antigenic differences observed between Ethiopian isolates and the HB1 vaccine, as demonstrated by the hemagglutination inhibition (HI) test [27]. Similarly, the E347Q substitution has been reported to reduce HI activity against the LaSota strain [48]. However, these substitutions in the neutralizing epitope of F and HN may not significantly affect the overall antigenicity, as the virus neutralization test showed no antigenic differences between Ethiopian isolates and the HB1 vaccine [27]. Despite this, the significant differences observed in the HI test suggest that these substitutions could still affect the host immune response, indicating that field strain-matched homologous vaccines may be required to ensure effective control of sub-genotype VII.1.1 NDV.

The whole-genome sequences of NDVs isolated from both vaccinated and unvaccinated chickens in this study were closely related to each other. Compared to the consensus sequence of other NDV sub-genotype VII.1.1 strains, several amino acid substitutions were found in all six structural proteins of the Ethiopian NDVs (Table 2, Figs. 1 and 2). Although the functional impact of most of these substitutions remains unknown and requires further investigation, those detected in the L and F proteins have been reported to increase NDV virulence [14]. In this study, six amino acid substitutions (C698, D1531, N1643, R1668, S1758, M1785) and four (A11, S265, E304, A550) associated with increased virulence, as reported by [19], were identified in the L and F proteins, respectively. Interestingly, two of these substitutions in the F protein, S265 (detected only in WS10C7) and A550 (present in all seven isolates), were unique to the Ethiopian isolates but absent in other VII.1.1 strains, suggesting the possible emergence of more virulent variants in Ethiopia.

The complete genome sequence showed that the seven Ethiopian isolates in this study had six genes, a leader and trailer sequences and 12 UTRs and five IG sequences that are characteristics of previously described genotype VII NDVs [49, 50]. We found four recently deposited (February 26, 2025) complete genome sequences of sub-genotype VII.1.1 NDV from the Amhara region and Addis Ababa, Ethiopia (GenBank accession numbers PV189285.1-PV189288.1), which were generated using nanopore sequencing technology. These strains were closely related to our isolates, with whole-genome sequence similarities of 93.98 to 99.52%, confirming that sub-genotype VII.1.1 strains are widely distributed in Ethiopia. However, although the overall similarity was high, MEGA X alignment of the raw data revealed missing sequences in Nanopore sequences, including PV189286.1 at positions 1589–1594, 3061, 6258–6259, 8158–8160, 8191–8195, and 8206–8208; PV189288.1 at positions 1589–1594, 3061, 6258–6259, 8187–8193, and 8204; and PV189285.1 and PV189287.1 at positions 6249–6250. These findings highlight the lower sequence quality of Nanopore data deposited to GenBank from Ethiopian NDV strains. Since Illumina sequencing provides higher-quality data than nanopore sequencing [51], the NGS data generated in this study provides more reliable sequences for analyzing the genomic characteristics of Ethiopian NDV strains than these recently deposited sequences.

Conclusions

Whole-genome sequence analyses revealed that sub-genotype VII.1.1 can be classified into three distinct lineages, with the seven Ethiopian isolates clustering within lineage 1. Unique amino acid substitutions were detected in all six genes of the Ethiopian isolates compared to closely related NDVs and vaccine strains. Some of these substitutions have been previously reported to be associated with increased virulence and antigenic variation. Our analysis also demonstrated significant genetic divergence between the Ethiopian isolates and the commonly used vaccine strains. Although sub-genotype VII.1.1 is widely distributed in Ethiopia and other East African countries, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing the whole-genome characteristics of NDV sub-genotype VII.1.1 strains isolated in this region. The findings of this study are expected to support the development of more effective vaccines targeting the currently circulating sub-genotype VII.1.1 strains.
