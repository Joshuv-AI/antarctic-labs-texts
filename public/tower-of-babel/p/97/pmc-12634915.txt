Use of Dysphagia-Inducing Drugs and Risk of Aspiration Pneumonia: A Cross-Sectional Analysis Using a Japanese Claims Database
Authors: Naoko Hayashi, Mari Yoshida, Narumi Maida, Shingo Kondo, Masanori Ogawa, Hiroki Iwata, Noriko Kobayashi, Katsunori Yamaura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesOropharyngeal dysphagia (OD), a dysfunction in swallowing food or drink, can result from various diseases and adverse drug reactions. OD is a risk factor for aspiration pneumonia (AP). However, the specific drugs causing OD and their incidence rates are not fully understood. This study aimed to identify drugs associated with OD, their incidence rates, and AP risk factors in patients taking these drugs on the basis of the information provided in package inserts.MethodsThis study identified candidate dysphagia-inducing drugs (CDIDs) from Japanese package inserts that listed OD as an adverse reaction. The age, sex, medications, and comorbidities of patients taking CDIDs were analyzed using the JammNet insurance database, purchased from JammNet Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan).ResultsOverall, 54 ingredients were identified as CDIDs. Out of 24,276 patients taking CDIDs, 146 (0.6%) were diagnosed with OD and 76 (0.3%) with AP. Among those with AP, 23 patients (30%) also had OD. OD or AP occurred in patients taking 28 (52%) of the 54 target ingredients. In addition, 13 ingredients had an adverse reaction incidence of 1% or greater for either condition. The top five CDIDs with the highest incidence rates for each diagnosis were clobazam, baclofen, zonisamide, tiapride hydrochloride, and topiramate. Incidence rates of OD and AP were significantly higher with multiple CDIDs than with a single drug (p < 0.05). Logistic regression analysis showed that AP occurrence was significantly associated with males, late-stage elderly individuals, a diagnosis of OD, and constipation.ConclusionsThe results of this study suggest that careful attention should be given to the risk of AP when prescribing CDIDs, particularly for elderly male patients.

Key Points

This study identified 54 drugs in Japan that can cause swallowing difficulties and examined their link to aspiration pneumonia using insurance data.Elderly male patients taking such drugs were found to have a significantly higher risk of aspiration pneumonia.

Introduction

The World Health Organization defines swallowing as the process of moving food and drink through the oral cavity, pharynx, and esophagus into the stomach at an appropriate rate and speed [1]. Dysphagia, an abnormality in this function, is classified into oropharyngeal dysphagia (OD) and esophageal dysphagia [2]. In this study, dysphagia was specifically defined as OD for analysis. Notably, Japanese health data and package inserts do not always distinguish between OD and esophageal dysphagia. A meta-analysis revealed that OD is common in elderly patients, with its prevalence increasing with age [3]. OD can also result from extrapyramidal dysfunction in patients with Parkinson’s disease [4]. Causes of OD include aging, stroke, tumors, Alzheimer’s disease, head injury, drugs, and dry mouth [5]. Case reports and reviews have suggested that dysphagia can be induced by second-generation antipsychotics, antidepressants, and antiepileptics [6, 7]. Parkinson’s disease is characterized by various motor dysfunctions, including dysphagia. A decrease in substance P may be involved in the mechanism of this swallowing dysfunction. Dysphagia-inducing drugs often affect the extrapyramidal system, but the exact mechanisms remain unclear. Many Parkinson’s disease treatments are dopamine agonists, which increase the production of substance P, potentially benefiting swallowing function [8, 9]. However, these treatments still list dysphagia as an adverse reaction. This discrepancy suggests that OD might be influenced by the underlying pathology of Parkinson’s disease, requiring careful interpretation.

Aspiration pneumonia (AP) is a pulmonary disease caused by the entry of secretions contaminated with pathogenic bacteria into the airways, often occurring in patients with impaired oropharyngeal or gastroesophageal motility [10, 11]. According to the 2022 statistical report from the Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare (MHLW), the leading causes of death among Japanese people are cancer, heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, pneumonia, and AP [12]. A case-control study based on a multiple cause of death dataset from 1999 to 2017 in the USA reported an average of 17,616 AP-related deaths per year, with 76.0% of these deaths occurring in individuals aged 75 years or older [13]. A systematic literature review identified risk factors for AP in frail older people, including age, male sex, lung disease, and diabetes mellitus (DM) [14]. AP negatively impacts the prognosis of poststroke patients, with an incidence rate of 14% among patients with stroke. A clinical trial demonstrated that AP rates were 19% in the no-dental care group and 11% in the dental care group over 2 years [15]. Antimicrobial agents are used to treat AP [16]. Poor oral hygiene can lead to AP, making early preventive measures to avoid aspiration crucial.

Japanese drug package inserts list OD as an adverse reaction, providing crucial information for healthcare professionals, especially pharmacists. However, current information is limited, and the impact on OD development and progression to AP in clinical settings remains unclear. In this study, we first listed drugs noted in package inserts as having OD as a potential adverse reaction. We then used an insurance database to assess their actual impact in clinical practice. In addition, we aimed to identify factors associated with the risk of AP in patients taking these candidate dysphagia-inducing drugs (CDIDs).

Methods

Extraction of Drug List from Package Inserts

Dysphagia-inducing drugs were defined as those listing “dysphagia” as an adverse reaction in the 2022 JAMES medication database. In package inserts, OD is often noted as a symptom of neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) in serious adverse reactions. Since OD is just one of many symptoms associated with NMS, it was excluded from the CDIDs list. However, if OD was listed as an adverse reaction in addition to NMS, the drug was included in the CDIDs list. In addition, dopamine agonists listed as causing OD were excluded owing to concerns about obtaining accurate results, as they can also improve OD pharmacologically. However, drugs such as aripiprazole, which is classified as a multi-acting-receptor-targeted antipsychotic (MARTA) and partially stimulates dopamine receptors, were not excluded from the CDIDs list. As a result, the list of CDIDs used in this study may be incomplete. We refer to the drugs extracted from package inserts as CDIDs and categorize them by their pharmacological effect. Drug classification was performed using the system published by the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification.

Study Population

The insurance data was purchased from JammNet Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). It includes both inpatient and outpatient data obtained from the medical claims (receipt data) of insurers contracted with the company. The data encompasses both clinics and hospitals, and the target regions cover urban areas as well as the entirety of Japan. Data regarding patients taking CDIDs were extracted from October to December 2015. To focus on those continuously prescribed CDIDs, patients not prescribed the drug for more than 14 days were excluded. The data included only patients taking CDIDs and analyzed age, sex, medications, and diagnosed diseases. Additional data from JammNet Co., Ltd. included the number of individuals not taking CDIDs and those diagnosed with OD and AP. Data on CDID users by age category were also provided for a more complete picture. Patients with OD and AP were identified using International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes R13 and J69.0, respectively. The incidence of OD and AP for each drug was calculated by dividing the number of patients taking each CDID. Heatmaps were created in Microsoft Excel, showing the incidence of OD and AP, separated by 5-year age intervals. Comorbidities were calculated by summing the diseases present in each patient diagnosed with AP. As each patient can have multiple comorbidities, the total number of comorbidities exceeds the number of patients.

Statistical Analysis

To compare the association between CDID use and the prevalence of OD or AP, patients were divided into those taking and not taking CDIDs using additional data from JammNet Co., Ltd. Statistical analysis was performed using a Pearson’s chi-squared test in Microsoft Excel, with p values (* < 0.05, ** < 0.01, *** < 0.001) considered significant. We evaluated the relationship between the number of CDIDs and disease diagnosis by comparing patients with and without OD to determine whether they were taking single or multiple CDIDs. The same method was used for patients with AP. Logistic regression analysis was performed using SPSS software version 29 to identify factors associated with AP. Explanatory variables included sex, age (elderly, defined as individuals aged 65 years and older, and late-stage elderly, defined as individuals aged 75 years and older), multiple CDID prescriptions, and OD diagnosis.

Results

Identification of Dysphagia-Inducing Drugs

On the basis of the JAMES Japanese drug database, 182 drugs listed OD as an adverse reaction in their package inserts. Of these, 125 medications were excluded as they attributed OD only in the context of NMS. In addition, three drugs—levodopa/carbidopa hydrate, levodopa/carbidopa with entacapone, and pergolide mesylate—used in Parkinson’s disease treatment to promote dopamine secretion and improve swallowing function, were also excluded. Consequently, 54 drugs were defined as CDIDs (Table 1). The most common drugs were psycholeptics, with 19 (35%) identified. Antiepileptic and psycholeptic drugs causing extrapyramidal symptoms with dysphagia were also prevalent. Notably, the list included drugs such as atropine and oxybutynin, which inhibit salivary secretion through anticholinergic mechanisms.

Association Between OD and Aspiration Pneumonia in CDIDs

During the study period from October to December 2015, a total of 392,577 patients were included using the JammNet insurance database. The number of patients diagnosed with OD or AP was calculated for all patients and those taking CDIDs, with percentages determined by age category. Out of the total, 24,276 (6.18%) patients were taking at least one CDID (Table 2). The proportion of patients taking CDIDs was 4.86% (15,515/319,000) among nonelderly patients (0–64 years), 10.05% (6111/60,827) among early stage elderly patients (65–74 years), and 20.78% (2650/12,750) among late-stage elderly patients (75 years and older) (Table 2). Although late-stage elderly patients constitute 11% of the total, they represent 38% of those diagnosed with AP. Figure 1A shows the proportion of CDID users by age group, revealing an increase in CDID use with advancing age. To compare the incidence of OD and AP associated with CDIDs, we examined the proportions of individuals with these adverse reactions. The OD group had a significantly higher rate of CDID use compared with the non-OD group (24% versus 6.2%) (Fig. 1B). Similarly, the AP group had a higher rate of CDID use compared with the non-AP group (22% versus 6.2%) (Fig. 1C). The overall incidence rate of OD was 0.16% (615/392,577), while it was 0.60% (146/24,276) among CDID users (Table 2). The incidence rate of AP was 0.087% (340/392,577) overall, compared with 0.31% (76/24,276) among CDID users. Notably, 30% (23/76) of patients diagnosed with AP also had OD. To further explore these characteristics, we illustrated the incidence rates of OD and AP across different age groups using a heatmap, stratified by medication use (Fig. 1D). The heatmap showed higher incidence rates of OD and AP among elderly individuals across all groups. Conversely, in nonelderly populations, the incidence rates of these adverse reactions increased with CDID use.

To assess the clinical relevance of the list in Table 1, on the basis of package inserts, we analyzed the number of patients taking each drug who experienced OD and AP. This analysis aimed to enhance the list’s practical impact by incorporating real-world clinical data and filtering out irrelevant entries. The incidence rates of adverse reactions for drugs causing OD and AP were calculated by dividing by the number of prescriptions (Table 3). According to medicinal classification, antiepileptic drugs (zonisamide, topiramate, and clonazepam) and antipsychotic drugs (clobazam, haloperidol, quetiapine hydrochloride, and risperidone) showed high incidence rates. Including drugs with adverse reaction rates below 1%, there are 25 drugs that might cause OD and 24 drugs that might cause AP. OD or AP occurred in patients taking 28 (52%) of the 54 target ingredients. Moreover, 13 ingredients had an adverse reaction incidence of 1% or greater for either condition.

Considering that polypharmacy can exacerbate adverse reactions, we examined the relationship between the number of CDIDs and the incidence of adverse reactions. The OD group had a significantly higher rate of taking multiple CDIDs compared with the non-OD group (31% versus 20%) (Fig. 2B). Similarly, the AP group had a higher proportion of patients taking multiple CDIDs compared with those without the diagnosis (34% versus 20%) (Fig. 2C).

Determining the Risk Factors of AP Due to Taking CDIDs

To clarify the risk factors for AP in patients taking CDIDs, we performed logistic regression analysis (Table 4). Among these patients, significant factors associated with AP included male sex, late-stage elderly age, diagnosis of OD, and constipation. Notably, the highest odds ratio (OR) was observed for diagnosis of OD, with a value of 35.44 (95% confidence interval [CI], 20.36–61.68). In Fig. 2C, the AP diagnosis group showed a higher proportion of patients taking multiple CDIDs. However, logistic regression analysis did not reveal a significant correlation between taking multiple CDIDs and the occurrence of AP (p = 0.078). The heatmap in Fig. 1D showed high AP incidence across almost all age groups, with elderly individuals having a high incidence even without CDIDs. Furthermore, Table 4 indicates that there were no significant differences in the elderly factor, but the late-stage elderly factor was strongly related to AP incidence (Table 4).

Discussion

OD and AP are closely related, with various pathophysiological conditions reported as risk factors for each [6, 14]. Aging is a key example, highlighting the increasing importance of these diseases as global populations age [3, 13]. This study focused on drug adverse reactions, investigating the occurrence of OD and AP using a database. The aim was to create a list of CDIDs and identify factors related to the risk of AP in patients taking these drugs.

To explore CDIDs, we identified 54 drugs noted for dysphagia in the adverse reaction section of Japanese package inserts. These included many antiepileptic drugs, psychotropic drugs, and anticholinergics. Figure 1 shows that the older groups are more likely to be taking CDIDs, with an elevated risk of OD and AP. However, the heatmap indicates that CDID use is associated with an increased risk of OD and AP across all age groups, which is particularly concerning.

To determine the incidence rates for OD and AP, we calculated the number of patients taking each drug. Although Japanese package inserts often list the incidence of OD as less than 1%, this study calculated actual values using prescription numbers. Antiepileptic and psycholeptic drugs showed high incidence rates. However, unlike prospective cohort studies, this database did not determine whether the events were caused by the drugs or underlying pathophysiological conditions. Comorbidities, such as stroke, could lead to overestimation [17]. Considering pharmacological effects, dopamine agonists improve OD [8, 9]. Notably, cilostazol, commonly used in stroke management, releases dopamine via substance P as a secondary effect [18]. A randomized trial showed that cilostazol decreases the risk of AP after a stroke [19]. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), often used for hypertension, induce a dry cough [20]. This adverse reaction can prevent the occurrence of AP [21, 22]. These reports suggested that cilostazol and ACEi might suppress the incidence of OD and AP, and they may have been related to the incidence in the patient group in this study. The antiviral drugs initially listed as CDIDs were not present in patients diagnosed with OD and AP. Package inserts mention the probability of OD as an adverse reaction; the frequency was listed as unknown for 31% (17/54) of the drugs. Our previous study highlighted a lack of information on adverse reactions, pharmacokinetics, and other descriptions in the package inserts [23]. This study provides new insights into the prevalence of these adverse reactions for package inserts.

Constipation, insomnia, and gastroesophageal reflux disease are common in elderly patients and were also observed in our study patients diagnosed with AP. Increased stomach acid secretion and daytime sleepiness may be related to AP, although their direct connection is unclear. Constipation may also be an adverse reaction to CDIDs, such as oxycodone hydrochloride hydrate. Older individuals often take multiple drugs, a practice known as polypharmacy, which can lead to adverse reactions. However, a case–control study using logistic regression analysis showed that the number of drugs was not a risk factor for AP [24]. To further analyze this result, we focused only on CDIDs to confirm their association with polypharmacy. Patients diagnosed with OD or AP were predominantly more likely to be taking two or more CDIDs. These results suggest that prescription changes may prevent the occurrence of AP if alternative drugs are available. This study provided novel evidence of a significant association between polypharmacy and AP when focusing specifically on CDID users. In light of the results showing that OD has the strongest correlation with AP in this study, patients experiencing OD as an adverse reaction should switch to an alternative drug before developing AP. In Japan, patients with lung cancer and those who smoke are predominantly male, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease has been reported as a risk factor for AP [6]. Since this database does not include smoking data, further investigation is needed in future studies. In this study, Parkinson’s disease was not identified as a risk factor for AP. It is possible that the subjects in this study were taking dopamine agonists that show improvement in dysphagia. In addition, healthcare professionals might also have been more focused on the swallowing function of patients with Parkinson’s disease compared with other diseases.

There are several limitations to this study on OD and AP using the current methodology. Previous reports have shown that steroidal drugs can decrease swallowing function due to muscle weakness from myopathy, while antianxiety drugs are associated with decreased swallowing function due to dry mouth and reduced consciousness [25]. These reports indicate that these drugs cause adverse reactions on the basis of clinical observations. In this study, these drugs were not included because their adverse reactions were not listed as “dysphagia” on the Japanese package insert. In addition, asenapine maleate, lurasidone hydrochloride, and lomitapide mesilate were released after the period covered by our purchased database, so data on these drugs are unavailable. Furthermore, tiotropium bromide hydrate and olodaterol hydrochloride are only external medicines and were thus excluded from this study. In actual clinical practice, not all patients with dysphagia are diagnosed with “dysphagia” in this dataset. Therefore, the dataset used in this study may result in an underestimation compared with clinical practice. The disease codes in the insurance database, based on ICD-10, may not fully reflect actual symptoms. Furthermore, owing to the nature of the clinical database, the dataset does not include the severity of dysphagia. In addition, the database used in this study is from 2015 and is therefore outdated, necessitating an update. Revisions in package insert information and the release of new drugs necessitate regular updates to the CDIDs. Insurance databases, such as the one used in this study, are valuable tools for maintaining current and accurate information in such cases. Finally, this study is a cross-sectional analysis conducted at a single point in time. Unlike prospective cohort studies, this database does not determine whether the events were caused by the drugs or underlying pathophysiological conditions.

Conclusions

Our study identified patient profiles and drug-related risk factors for AP among those taking CDIDs. Specifically, we found that male patients, late-stage elderly patients, and diagnoses of OD and constipation were associated with an increased risk of AP, providing novel insights into this issue. These insights suggest that careful attention should be given to the risk of AP when prescribing CDIDs, particularly for elderly male patients. Further detailed studies are needed to confirm these findings and facilitate their clinical application.
