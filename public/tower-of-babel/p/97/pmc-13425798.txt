Evaluation of the measles surveillance system in Chereponi District, Ghana, 2019-2024
Authors: Richard Dzeha, Owusu Amponsah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo evaluate the design, implementation, and performance of the measles surveillance system in Chereponi District, Ghana, from 2019 to 2024 using the United States Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Updated Guidelines for Evaluating Public Health Surveillance Systems.Study designCross-sectional mixed-methods surveillance system evaluation.MethodsA mixed-methods evaluation was conducted across all 26 health facilities implementing measles surveillance and 13 purposively selected key informants. Quantitative data were collected through facility assessments and review of Surveillance Outbreak Response Management and Analysis System (SORMAS) records. Qualitative data were obtained through semi-structured interviews. Descriptive statistics, predictive value positive (PVP), Cochran–Armitage trend analysis, and thematic analysis were performed.ResultsTimeliness was the strongest surveillance attribute (100%), followed by data quality (96.2%) and representativeness (88.5%). Acceptability (80.8%), simplicity (73.1%), and flexibility (61.5%) showed moderate performance, whereas usefulness (42.3%) and stability (38.5%) were weakest. During 2019-2024, 167 suspected measles cases were reported, of which 31 were laboratory confirmed (PVP = 18.6%; 95% CI: 13.4-25.3%). Laboratory confirmation rates increased significantly from 0% during 2019-2021 to 30.8% in 2024 (Cochran-Armitage χ2 = 14.57, P < 0.001). Qualitative findings attributed operational weaknesses to inadequate refresher training, staff attrition, transport constraints, delayed laboratory feedback, unreliable internet and electricity, and inconsistent community-based reporting.ConclusionsThe measles surveillance system was functional and supported timely case detection, laboratory confirmation, and outbreak response within Ghana's IDSR framework. However, strengthening workforce capacity, logistics, laboratory feedback, digital infrastructure, and community-based surveillance is essential to improve system stability and advance measles elimination effort.

Introduction

Background

Measles remains one of the most contagious vaccine-preventable diseases and continues to pose a major public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries despite the availability of safe and effective vaccines. It is characterized by fever, cough, coryza, conjunctivitis, and generalized rash, with severe complications including pneumonia, encephalitis, blindness, and death, especially among young children and immunocompromised individuals [1,2]. In 2024, an estimated 11.0 million measles cases and 94,564 deaths occurred globally, highlighting persistent immunity gaps despite ongoing vaccination efforts [3].

Declining vaccination coverage, vaccine hesitancy, and health system weaknesses continue to drive recurrent outbreaks, emphasizing the importance of maintaining high two-dose measles vaccine coverage [4,5].

Measles surveillance involves the systematic collection, analysis, and dissemination of epidemiological and laboratory data to support early case detection, outbreak response, monitoring of immunization performance, and progress toward WHO measles elimination targets [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]].

Ghana implements measles surveillance through the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) framework. The IDSR strategy integrates surveillance activities for epidemic-prone and priority diseases using standardized reporting systems, case definitions, laboratory confirmation, and coordinated outbreak response mechanisms [11,12]. Surveillance reporting is supported through two complementary electronic platforms. The District Health Information Management System II (DHIMS2) captures routine aggregate surveillance and health service data submitted weekly and monthly through the IDSR reporting system. In contrast, the Surveillance Outbreak Response Management and Analysis System (SORMAS) manage individual case-based surveillance, laboratory investigation, contact tracing, outbreak monitoring, and case follow-up. Suspected measles cases are initially entered into SORMAS for case investigation and laboratory monitoring, while summaries are routinely reported through DHIMS2 [13].

Although measles surveillance has improved across the WHO African Region, persistent challenges related to specimen transport, laboratory capacity, logistics, and surveillance quality continue to affect performance [14]. In Ghana, limited district-level evaluations hinder identification of operational gaps needed to strengthen surveillance, improve outbreak preparedness, and support measles elimination [15].

This study evaluated the measles surveillance system in Chereponi District from 2019 to 2024 using the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) surveillance evaluation framework [16]. Specifically, the study assessed the system's design and implementation, examined key surveillance attributes including simplicity, timeliness, completeness, flexibility, stability, usefulness and trends in suspected and laboratory-confirmed measles cases.

Methods

Study setting

Chereponi District located in the North East Region of Ghana and had an estimated population of 96,218 distributed across 206 communities and five administrative health sub-districts during the study period. The district measles surveillance system operates within the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) framework and comprises one district hospital, two health centres, and 22 Community-Based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study area and distribution of health facilities involved in measles surveillance in Chereponi District, North East Region, Ghana, 2019-2024.

Study design

A cross-sectional surveillance system evaluation was conducted using the United States Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Updated Guidelines for Evaluating Public Health Surveillance Systems. The evaluation assessed the design, implementation, and performance of the measles surveillance system in Chereponi District from January 2019 to December 2024. The study employed a mixed-methods approach combining quantitative and qualitative data sources. Quantitative components included assessment of surveillance system attributes. The qualitative component explored stakeholder experiences in surveillance system implementation.

Study population

The study included all health facilities participating in measles surveillance activities within Chereponi District and healthcare workers directly involved in surveillance implementation were eligible for participation.

Sampling and participant selection

All 26 health facilities involved in measles surveillance activities within the district were included in the quantitative facility assessment. Surveillance data reported between January 2019 and December 2024 were reviewed. For the qualitative component, purposive sampling was used to select key informants with direct involvement and experience in measles surveillance activities at district and regional levels.

Case definitions

A suspected measles case was defined according to the Ghana Health Service IDSR guidelines as any person presenting with fever, generalized maculopapular (non-vesicular) rash, and at least one of the following symptoms: cough, coryza, or conjunctivitis, or any patient in whom a clinician suspected measles.

A confirmed measles case was defined as a suspected case with laboratory confirmation by measles-specific IgM antibody testing or epidemiological linkage to a laboratory-confirmed outbreak case.

Surveillance system attributes

The surveillance system was evaluated using standard CDC surveillance system attributes including simplicity, flexibility, acceptability, sensitivity, predictive value positive (PVP), representativeness, timeliness, stability, data quality and usefulness.

Simplicity was assessed by evaluating ease of case detection, reporting procedures, completion of case investigation forms, and use of electronic reporting systems.

Flexibility was evaluated based on integration with DHIMS2, SORMAS, and the IDSR framework.

Acceptability was assessed through participation in reporting, specimen submission, and stakeholder engagement in surveillance activities.

Sensitivity was conceptually defined as the proportion of all true measles cases occurring in the population that were detected by the surveillance system. However, because the total number of true measles cases in the population was unknown and no independent case ascertainment source was available, true surveillance sensitivity could not be directly measured. The study assessed the laboratory confirmation rate, defined as the proportion of suspected measles cases that were laboratory confirmed during each year of the evaluation period.

Predictive value positive was calculated as the proportion of suspected cases that were laboratory confirmed.

Representativeness was assessed by evaluating reporting coverage across all subdistricts and facility types, as well as the distribution of reported cases by person, place, and time.

Timeliness was measured using the proportion of suspected cases reported within 24-48 h, specimen collection intervals, and reporting timelines for weekly and monthly IDSR reports.

Stability was evaluated based on availability of trained personnel, logistics, specimen collection materials, transport systems, communication infrastructure, and laboratory support.

Data quality was assessed based on completeness, accuracy, consistency and availability of surveillance records, including case investigation forms, laboratory records, DHIMS2 reports, and SORMAS records.

Usefulness was assessed by determining whether surveillance information contributed to outbreak detection, identification of high-risk populations, planning of immunization activities, and decision-making at facility and district levels.

Overall assessment of surveillance system functionality

Overall functionality of the surveillance system was determined using the CDC Updated Guidelines for Evaluating Public Health Surveillance Systems. The surveillance system was considered functional when it consistently supported timely case detection, reporting, laboratory confirmation, outbreak response and public health decision-making despite identified operational weaknesses.

Data collection procedures

Primary data were collected using structured facility assessment checklists and semi-structured interview guides across all CHPS compounds, health centres, the district hospital, and the District Health Directorate. The principal investigator (PI) administered face-to-face questionnaires and conducted private interviews with key surveillance managers after obtaining informed consent. Interviews explored surveillance operations, logistics, reporting, supervision, laboratory coordination, community participation, and feedback, and were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and managed using Kobo Collect. Data collection continued until thematic saturation. PI and study supervisor confirmed data saturation jointly through continuous transcript and coding review. Secondary data on measles cases and laboratory outcomes were extracted from the SORMAS database.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analysed using Stata 17 and Microsoft Excel with descriptive statistics and 95% confidence intervals. Qualitative data were transcribed, manually coded, and analysed thematically using an inductive approach. Codes were grouped into themes aligned with CDC surveillance attributes and reviewed with the study supervisor through peer debriefing. Formal member checking was not conducted; however, credibility was strengthened through triangulation of quantitative and qualitative findings, use of a standardized interview guide, and peer debriefing during thematic analysis.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained with reference number (CHRPE/AP/640/25), with administrative permission from regional, district and facility authorities. All interviewees provided written consent.

Qualitative interviews showed that the measles surveillance system was well coordinated within the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) framework. Participants described surveillance officers, disease control officers, and clinicians as working together to ensure timely case detection, reporting, and supervision. One respondent stated that their role was to “conduct supervision to ensure health workers understand case definitions” [KI-02], while another emphasized the need to “ensure the surveillance system is strengthened” [KI-01]. Communication was described as a continuous process from community to national level using phone calls and standard reporting channels. As one clinician explained, “We suspect, collect the sample, fill the form and send it to the district or call the surveillance officer to pick it up” [KI-03]. However, participants identified implementation challenges, including inadequate refresher training, limited transport and laboratory logistics, shortages of surveillance tools, and continued reliance on manual reporting in peripheral facilities, which occasionally delayed specimen processing and feedback.

The surveillance system had a structured IDSR framework, but logistical, digital, and surveillance tool limitations constrained efficient implementation and timely reporting. Active surveillance was routinely conducted through supportive supervision visits, review of outpatient registers, community-based surveillance by Community-Based Surveillance Volunteers (CBSVs), and periodic verification of zero reporting from health facilities. Where facilities that failed to report suspected measles cases for prolonged periods were followed up to determine whether the absence of reported cases reflected true zero incidence or under-reporting.

The measles surveillance system operated within Ghana's IDSR framework, linking community, facility, district, regional, and national levels through DHIMS2 and SORMAS. Suspected cases were investigated, reported, and blood specimens transported to the Tamale Public Health Reference Laboratory for measles IgM ELISA confirmation. Measles surveillance is implemented as an integrated measles-rubella programme in accordance with WHO guidelines. Hence, all measles suspected cases were tested for rubella cases as well from the laboratory. Performance of measles surveillance system attributes

Facility-level assessment

Facility assessment showed mixed surveillance performance. Timeliness was the strongest attribute (100%), followed by data quality (96.2%) and representativeness (88.5%). Acceptability (80.8%), simplicity (73.1%), and flexibility (61.5%) demonstrated moderate performance, while usefulness (42.3%) and stability (38.5%) were weakest. The overall predictive value positive (PVP) was 18.6% (31/167; 95% CI: 13.4-25.3%). Laboratory confirmation rates increased from 0% during 2019-2021 to 9.7% in 2022, peaked at 40.0% in 2023, and remained high at 30.8% in 2024. The Cochran-Armitage trend test confirmed a significant upward trend in laboratory confirmation rates (χ2 = 14.57, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Performance of measles surveillance system attributes and trends in laboratory confirmation rates, Chereponi District, Ghana, 2019-2024.

Key informant perspectives

Qualitative findings explained the operational barriers affecting surveillance performance. Respondents attributed weaknesses to inadequate refresher training, frequent staff transfers, and attrition, leaving newly posted staff unfamiliar with IDSR reporting, SORMAS, and specimen handling. The District Director of Health Services stated, “There is limited IDSR refresher training, and some staff do not fully understand the forms” [KI-04], while a Regional Surveillance Officer observed, “Staff attrition is the main challenge; new staff often lack surveillance training” [KI-08]. Participants also reported inadequate transport, poor internet connectivity, electricity interruptions, and delayed laboratory feedback. One respondent explained, “The challenge has been sometimes logistics for sample transport and communication with surveillance officers” [KI-03], whereas another noted, “Power outages and internet issues sometimes delay surveillance reports” [KI-01]. Community-level surveillance was further weakened by inconsistent reporting from community volunteers, particularly in remote communities.

Discussion

Design and data communication in measles surveillance system

The measles surveillance system in Chereponi District follows Ghana's Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) framework, linking community, facility, district, regional, and national levels through standardized reporting, specimen referral, and feedback mechanisms. This structure is consistent with previous studies in Ghana and other African countries demonstrating that IDSR strengthens coordination, outbreak detection, and public health response [11,18].

The predominance of Community-Based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds and Community Health Nurses highlights the critical role of decentralized primary healthcare in surveillance implementation. Similar findings have been reported in Ghana and Ethiopia, where frontline health workers are central to early case detection because of their close community engagement. However, limited involvement of other professional cadres may reduce system resilience during outbreaks or staff shortages [15,19].

Communication relied mainly on phone calls and WhatsApp for rapid notification and coordination of specimen transport. Such mobile communication platforms have improved reporting timeliness across African surveillance systems by facilitating communication between peripheral facilities and district teams [20,21]. Weekly reporting schedules and routine feedback further indicate functional communication across health system levels.

Despite these strengths, important operational gaps remain. Less than half of facilities had case investigation forms and specimen transportation guidelines readily available. Similar shortages of surveillance tools, laboratory supplies, and transport logistics have been reported in Ghana and Nigeria, limiting surveillance efficiency and outbreak response [15,22]. Continued reliance on manual reporting and limited computer access also reflects persistent digital infrastructure challenges.

Key informants further identified transport difficulties, internet interruptions, electricity outages, delayed laboratory feedback, inadequate refresher training, and staff attrition as barriers to effective surveillance. Similar logistical and workforce constraints have been widely documented across resource-limited African settings, reducing continuity and surveillance performance [[23], [24], [25]]. Although specimens followed the established referral, delays in transport and laboratory feedback occasionally affected timely case confirmation, consistent with reports from the WHO African Region [14].

Performance of measles surveillance system attributes

The surveillance system demonstrated mixed performance across evaluated attributes. Timeliness was the strongest attribute, with all facilities reporting timely data capture and most suspected cases notified within 24 h, indicating good compliance with national IDSR guidelines. Similar findings have been reported in African districts with active supervision and structured reporting systems [25,26]. Timely reporting remains essential for rapid outbreak investigation and interruption of measles transmission.

Data quality was generally good, with facilities reporting adequate information for investigations and relatively complete reporting forms. These findings suggest that surveillance data were sufficiently reliable for epidemiological analysis and public health action, consistent with previous studies in Ghana and Ethiopia [15,19]. However, routine analysis of surveillance data at facility level remained inconsistent, limiting evidence-based local decision-making.

The surveillance system showed moderate simplicity. Although the standard measles case definition was generally easy to apply, fewer respondents considered case-based investigation forms and SORMAS reporting procedures easy to complete. Similar operational challenges have been reported in other low-resource settings where electronic surveillance systems improve reporting but require additional technical capacity among frontline staff [13,20].

Representativeness was relatively strong because suspected cases were identified across all age groups through both health facilities and community volunteers. Community-based surveillance extended case detection beyond health facilities, as reported in previous studies from Ghana and Nigeria [15,17]. Nevertheless, delayed reporting by some community volunteers suggests that community surveillance remains less sensitive than facility-based surveillance.

The system also demonstrated flexibility through integration with other reporting systems and the ability to accommodate additional diseases. However, respondents reported that procedural changes were sometimes difficult to implement because of infrastructure and resource limitations, consistent with findings from other African surveillance systems [24].

Acceptability was moderate. While only slightly more than half of facilities submitted measles case-based forms during the review period, respondents expressed satisfaction with supportive supervision and feedback from higher levels. A similar study found that regular feedback improves reporting compliance and staff participation [26]. However, incomplete use of case-based forms suggests inconsistent application of surveillance procedures.

System stability remained the weakest attribute. Limited funding, inadequate logistics, insufficient refresher training, lack of backup systems, unreliable internet connectivity, electricity interruptions, and staff attrition reduced operational capacity. Similar barriers have been reported in Ghana, Ethiopia, and Kenya, where unstable financing and workforce shortages undermine sustainable surveillance systems [7,15,25].

The increasing laboratory confirmation rate over the evaluation period suggests improvements in laboratory-supported surveillance or increased measles transmission during the 2023-2024 resurgence. Similar trends have been observed across the WHO African Region, where laboratory access and transport constraints influence surveillance performance [14,27]. Because true surveillance sensitivity could not be estimated, future studies should apply complementary approaches such as capture-recapture analysis, record linkage, community case searches, or outbreak investigation data.

The overall positive predictive value of 18.6% should be interpreted cautiously. In measles elimination settings, highly sensitive clinical case definitions intentionally identify many febrile rash illnesses that are subsequently excluded by laboratory testing. Therefore, relatively low predictive value positive may reflect a sensitive surveillance system rather than poor performance, as reported in several WHO African Region evaluations [15,22].

Implications for policy and practice

The findings indicate that while the measles surveillance system functions effectively within Ghana's IDSR framework, weaknesses in logistics, laboratory support, workforce capacity, and community surveillance require targeted action. Ghana Health Service should implement annual IDSR and SORMAS refresher training for surveillance staff, allocate dedicated funds for specimen transport and supervisory visits, establish laboratory turnaround time targets with electronic feedback to districts, and strengthen community-based surveillance through regular volunteer training and supervision. Routine district-level analysis of surveillance and immunization data should also be institutionalized to identify immunity gaps and guide targeted vaccination activities.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study's strengths include its mixed-methods design, integration of facility assessments, key informant interviews, and six years of surveillance data, guided by the CDC surveillance evaluation framework for a comprehensive assessment. Limitations include its single-district setting, reliance on self-reported and routine surveillance data, absence of member checking, and inability to estimate true surveillance sensitivity due to lack of an independent case ascertainment source.

Conclusion

The measles surveillance system in Chereponi District was functional, with strong performance in timeliness, reporting coordination, and data quality, supporting case detection, laboratory confirmation, and outbreak response within Ghana's IDSR framework. However, weaknesses in system stability, logistics, laboratory support, workforce capacity, digital infrastructure, and community surveillance limited overall performance.
