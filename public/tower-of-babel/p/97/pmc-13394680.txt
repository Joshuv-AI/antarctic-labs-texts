Sex Representation in US Stroke Clinical Trials: A Decade of Trends and Challenges
Authors: Chaitali Dagli, Mudasir Andrabi, Tova Cohen, Pranali G Patel, Tapasya Katta, Chen Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveStroke remains a major cause of disability and mortality in the US, with significant sex‐based disparities, and females remain underrepresented in stroke clinical trials. We aimed to examine sex representation in US‐based stroke clinical trials, identify trial characteristics associated with higher female enrollment (≥ 50%), and explore factors influencing recruitment strategies.MethodsThis mixed‐methods study analyzed US‐based stroke clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2020. A thematic analysis of recruitment strategies and eligibility criteria was conducted for trials with ≥ 50% female enrollment. We examined the proportion of female participants enrolled in each trial and trial characteristics, including funding source, principal investigator sex, participant age, and recruitment strategies.ResultsOf 456 eligible trials, only 102 (22%) enrolled ≥ 50% female participants. Compared with trials with < 50% female enrollment, these trials more often included both sexes (99.0% vs. 65.5%) and enrolled a greater median number of female participants (22.5 vs. 10). Female‐led studies were more common among trials with higher female representation (40.2% vs. 37.6%). Qualitative analysis revealed that broader inclusion criteria, hybrid recruitment strategies (e.g., combining hospital outreach), and targeted accommodations for stroke‐related disability were associated with higher female enrollment. Common barriers included exclusionary eligibility criteria, logistical challenges (e.g., transportation), and language limitations.InterpretationsInclusive design, community‐engaged recruitment, and structural accommodations including support for sex‐based roles like caregiving are potentially influential in promoting equitable participation. Addressing sex disparities in stroke research is essential for improving the relevance, effectiveness, and fairness of stroke interventions in clinical practice.Keywords: clinical trials, females, mixed method study, stroke

Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of disability and mortality in the United States (US), with notable inequalities in outcomes and access to care across demographic groups [1]. Females, in particular, face unique challenges in stroke prevention, treatment, and recovery, including higher lifetime risk, older age at onset, and greater post‐stroke disability compared to males [2]. Despite these differences, the representation of females in stroke‐related clinical trials has historically been inadequate, limiting the generalizability of trial findings and potentially leading to imbalances in stroke care [3].

Clinical trials are essential for advancing evidence‐based interventions; however, underrepresentation of specific subgroups, such as females, older adults, and racially or ethnically diverse populations, can skew results and diminish the external validity of study outcomes [4, 5]. Recent policy efforts and funding initiatives have emphasized the importance of inclusive research practices; however, recent analyses highlight the persistent underrepresentation of females in stroke clinical trials, raising concerns about the generalizability of findings and the application of stroke interventions [6]. A meta‐analysis of 115 acute stroke trials published between 2010 and 2020 revealed that females were underenrolled by 5% points relative to their representation in this population [3]. Another systematic review of post‐stroke motor recovery trials conducted in the US between 2019 and 2023 found that 100% of the publications from the studies included in the study reported sex data, and females comprised of only 38% of the participants in these studies [7]. Together, these studies highlight the under‐representation of females in stroke trials; however, none of these studies have focused on the factors influencing the recruitment of females in stroke trials in this country. Gaps remain in understanding how trial design, recruitment strategies, and operational challenges affect the enrollment of females in stroke trials.

To address these issues, we used a mixed‐methods approach, conducting a meta‐analysis and systematic review of stroke trials conducted in the US between 2010 and 2020. This study analyzed a decade of completed US‐based stroke trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. We examined the trial characteristics associated with higher female enrollment rates (50% and above) quantitatively. We also conducted a qualitative thematic analysis of the inclusion/exclusion criteria, recruitment strategies, and identified barriers and facilitators to recruitment. By characterizing patterns in trial design and reporting, our goal was to inform future research practices that promote equal sex representation in stroke trials and improve the applicability of stroke research to diverse patient populations.

Methods

This mixed method study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board to be non‐human subject research. It utilized publicly available data from ClinicalTrials.gov [8, 9]. The study did not involve individual‐level patient data; therefore, informed consent was not required. Data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Data Source and Trial Selection

Clinical trials were identified using the ClinicalTrials.gov search tool (search date: May 12, 2022) [8]. Trials were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) the condition or disease was specified as hemorrhagic or ischemic stroke; (2) the trial was conducted in the US and completed between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2020; (3) the enrolled population included adults (ages 18–64 years) or older adults (≥ 65 years); and (4) the trial was classified as Phase 1–4 or listed as “not applicable” [10, 11].

Trials were included if they assessed incident or recurrent stroke outcomes or evaluated post‐stroke outcomes among adult participants. Trials with any sites located outside the US were excluded. All extracted data were adjudicated to ensure completeness and accuracy. Some information on ClinicalTrials.gov was incomplete, as not all trial records were fully updated. To supplement missing data, we searched for related publications using the official study titles listed in the registry. When available, manuscripts were reviewed to identify updated information or findings not captured in the original trial entries.

Data Extraction and Variables

For each eligible trial, data were extracted on funding source, participant sex distribution, age group (young adults [18–64 years] vs. older adults [≥ 65 years]), sex of the principal investigator (PI), and use of participant incentivization strategies. Sex was recorded as reported in the trial records, representing the participant's biological sex. Funding source was classified using information on the lead sponsor and collaborators. Trials that listed the National Institutes of Health (NIH) or another US federal agency in either role were categorized as federally funded. Industry funding included pharmaceutical or device companies. Trials without a clear federal or industry sponsor, such as those led by universities or non‐profits, were categorized as “other.” To determine the sex of PIs included in this review, we used publicly available sources. Specifically, we examined author contribution statements within published articles and institutional profiles (e.g., university or hospital websites) to identify pronouns or other indicators of sex. In cases where PI sex could not be confidently determined, the investigator was excluded from sex‐based analyses.

Trials were further characterized by whether they enrolled both male and female participants and whether they included midlife and/or older adult populations. The presence of participant incentives (e.g., monetary rewards) was also recorded. These variables were selected to examine patterns in funding, trial leadership (as indicated by PI sex), participant composition, and recruitment practices in relation to female enrollment.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative

We conducted a descriptive analysis of clinical trials registered between 2010 and 2020. Trial characteristics were summarized overall and stratified by female recruitment levels. Categorical variables (e.g., funding source, lead investigator sex, recruitment of both sexes, participant age group, and monetary incentive) were reported as counts and percentages. The number of females recruited was summarized using medians due to skewed distributions. Trials were further stratified by funding source (federally funded vs. non‐federally funded) and sex of enrolled participants (male only, female only, or both). Counts were tabulated by year to examine trends over time.

Qualitative

A thematic content analysis was conducted on the inclusion/exclusion criteria, recruitment strategies, and reported barriers across eligible trials. Using an inductive approach, we coded narrative fields that described the study design, participant eligibility, and recruitment protocols. Codes were grouped into overarching themes that reflected common patterns and strategies contributing to successful female enrollment. Thematic coding was performed iteratively by two investigators. Emergent themes were reviewed and refined through consensus discussion. Themes were organized into four domains: (1) inclusion and exclusion criteria, (2) recruitment strategies, (3) reported barriers and operational challenges, and (4) design recommendations. Reliability of coding was enhanced through double coding of a subset (20%) of the dataset, achieving an inter‐rater agreement of > 85%. Qualitative results were used to contextualize quantitative findings and inform best practices for equitable recruitment in stroke trials.

Results

Quantitative

From 2010 to 2020, a total of 588 stroke‐related clinical trials were identified. After excluding 6 pediatric trials, 80 trials not related to stroke, and 48 multinational trials, 456 trials met eligibility criteria (Figure 1). Of the eligible trials, 102 (22.4%) recruited cohorts composed of 50% or more female participants, while 354 (77.6%) trials had less than 50% female enrollment.

Selection of clinical trials.

Table 1 presents characteristics of trials by female enrollment. Trials with ≥ 50% female participants enrolled a median of 22.5 females, compared with a median of 10 females in trials with < 50% female enrollment. Federally funded trials were similarly represented in both groups, comprising 32.4% of trials with ≥ 50% female enrollment and 34.3% of trials with < 50% female enrollment. Nearly all studies with ≥ 50% female enrollment recruited both sexes (99.0%), compared with 65.5% of studies with < 50% female enrollment (Figure 2). Female‐led studies were slightly more common among trials with ≥ 50% female enrollment (40.2%), compared with 37.6% of trials with < 50% female enrollment. A greater proportion of trials with higher female enrollment included midlife and older adult participants (29.4% vs. 21.9% for trials with < 50% female enrollment). Use of monetary reward systems was infrequent in both groups but slightly more common in trials with ≥ 50% female enrollment (4.9%), compared with 2.3% of trials with < 50% female enrollment.

Qualitative

Among the included stroke clinical trials reporting female participation rates of 50% or higher, the following are the consistent patterns that emerged across eligibility criteria, recruitment strategies, and operational considerations. These factors may inform future trial design to enhance sex representativeness and inclusivity.

Inclusion Criteria

Most studies enrolled participants aged 18 years or older, with common age ranges spanning from 30 to 85 years. Several trials occasionally stratified enrollment based on self‐reported race/ethnicity (e.g., African American, Hispanic, Asian, and European ancestry), reflecting an effort to include historically underrepresented groups. Eligibility typically required a clinical diagnosis of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke within the previous 6 months. Additional functional thresholds, such as independent ambulation or capacity for upper extremity movement, were often stipulated. Most trials required participants to have a caregiver available and be willing and able to attend follow‐up visits. English proficiency was a nearly universal requirement, although a few studies accommodated Spanish‐speaking participants.

Exclusion Criteria

Exclusion criteria were frequently extensive and focused on minimizing clinical risk and ensuring protocol adherence. Common exclusions included cognitive impairment, major psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, major depression), substance use disorders, and significant comorbidities (e.g., congestive heart failure, uncontrolled hypertension, renal or hepatic failure). Additional criteria included magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contraindications (e.g., pacemaker), recent surgeries, pregnancy or lactation, vision or sensory deficits, and prior participation in concurrent clinical studies. Some studies excluded individuals with severe upper limb impairment, likely limiting enrollment of participants with greater post‐stroke disability.

Recruitment Strategies

Studies with higher female participation commonly implemented a combination of clinical‐ and community‐based recruitment approaches. Hospital‐based recruitment through stroke units, rehabilitation centers, and outpatient clinics was frequently paired with outreach via community centers, churches, senior organizations, and digital media. Investigators often collaborated with clinical staff (e.g., physicians, therapists, social workers) to facilitate referrals and screening.

Recruitment materials included flyers, word‐of‐mouth, online advertisements, and direct provider referrals. Notably, several studies intentionally included participants with hemiparesis or other functional impairments, suggesting an effort to reflect real‐world stroke disability profiles.

Barriers and Implementation Challenges

Across studies, multiple barriers to recruitment and retention were reported. These included comorbid medical conditions, logistical issues such as lack of transportation, difficulty complying with study procedures, and the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on participation willingness. Language restrictions, caregiver availability, and perceived burden of follow‐up were also limiting factors. Some studies cited operational challenges, including insufficient funding for participant incentives or underpowered sample sizes due to recruitment shortfalls. Others noted difficulty in long‐term follow‐ups without standardized adherence tracking. These barriers were identified as reported by the investigators or authors in their manuscripts.

Discussion

In our study, we identified persistent disparities in female participant representation in US‐based stroke clinical trials and examined trial characteristics associated with achieving ≥ 50% female enrollment. Despite longstanding NIH guidelines aimed at improving sex balance in clinical research, only 22% of trials met this threshold. We observed no major differences in federal funding, principal investigator sex, or monetary incentives between trials with higher and lower female recruitment, suggesting that these factors alone are insufficient to drive equitable enrollment. Through qualitative review, we observed that trials with ≥ 50% female enrollment frequently incorporated inclusive eligibility criteria and hybrid recruitment strategies, including community‐based outreach mechanisms. These features were commonly reported by investigators and help characterize strategies employed in trials achieving higher female participation.

Our findings are consistent with prior work, including Carcel et al., who analyzed 281 stroke trials between 1990 and 2020. They reported that females comprised only 37.4% of trial participants and were particularly underrepresented in rehabilitation trials, non‐acute interventions, and studies enrolling younger participants [12]. The underrepresentation of females in clinical trials is a longstanding issue across medical disciplines, a problem highlighted by the NIH's 1993 Revitalization Act [13, 14]. Our findings corroborate this pervasive challenge, demonstrating that despite decades of emphasis on inclusion, significant sex disparities persist in this critical area [3, 15]. For instance stroke epidemiology and outcomes often differ by sex [16]; females generally face a higher lifetime risk [17], experience more severe strokes, and often have worse functional outcomes compared to males, especially at older ages [18, 19, 20]. Consequently, trials with inadequate female representation risk producing results with limited generalizability [7], potentially leading to a “one‐size‐fits‐all” approach to care that overlooks crucial sex‐specific needs and nuances. The consistent underrepresentation observed in our study suggests that the evidence base guiding stroke prevention, treatment, and rehabilitation may be disproportionately weighted towards male experiences and responses to interventions, contrasting with the increasing recognition of personalized medicine.

Our quantitative analysis offers intriguing insights into trial characteristics associated with higher female enrollment (≥ 50%). Nearly all studies with higher female participation recruited both males and females (99.0% vs. 65.5% in trials with < 50% female enrollment). While this may seem intuitive, it suggests that trials explicitly enrolling both sexes are more likely to achieve substantial female participation. The slightly higher proportion of female‐led studies in trials with greater female enrollment (40.2% vs. 37.6%) indicates a possible association; however, causal inference cannot be drawn. This aligns with some literature indicating that a diverse research team can positively impact recruitment from diverse populations [21]. Even among trials with higher female enrollment, commonly reported exclusion criteria, such as cognitive impairment, major psychiatric disorders, and significant comorbidities, may limit participation among certain subgroups of females. Females are more likely to experience specific comorbidities or present with non‐specific stroke symptoms that may be misattributed to psychiatric conditions, potentially increasing the likelihood of exclusion [22, 23, 24]. Additional factors, such as caregiver requirements and English proficiency, may also pose barriers. Trial‐specific factors, like upper age limits and restrictive comorbidity criteria, and patient‐specific factors, including logistical challenges, caregiving responsibilities, and perceived risk, can further reduce female participation.

In studies with higher female enrollment, recruitment strategies often combined clinical and community‐based approaches, engaged clinical staff, and included participants with post‐stroke functional impairments. Operational challenges, including funding limits, recruitment shortfalls, language restrictions, and follow‐up burden, were sometimes addressed through flexible designs and remote participation options. These barriers disproportionately affect older females facing socioeconomic challenges. For example, in 2022, about 10.2% of individuals aged 65 and older lived below the poverty line, which may limit access to transportation, caregiving support, or flexible scheduling needed to participate in trials [25, 26]. The impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on participation willingness further highlights the need for flexible and adaptable recruitment models [27]. Systemic issues, such as underfunding and underpowered trials, require broader institutional and funding support. Further, outreach through community centers, churches, senior organizations, digital media, and collaboration with clinical staff helped expand reach and build trust.

A key strength of this study is its comprehensive utilization of a decade of US‐based stroke trial data from ClinicalTrials.gov, offering a robust overview of recruitment trends and practices. The mixed‐methods design allowed for a deeper, more nuanced understanding of the factors associated with higher female enrollment. However, several limitations warrant consideration. The reliance on publicly reported data means that completeness and accuracy are subject to investigator reporting practices, which occasionally resulted in missing information. Not contacting study authors is an additional limitation, as direct clarification could have improved data accuracy and completeness. Future studies could incorporate direct reporting mechanisms or investigator surveys to enhance the reliability of clinical trial publications. Additionally, while the sex of the PI was determined using publicly available information, this method may not capture sex identity and could result in misclassification. Developing standardized approaches for collecting and reporting sex‐related data among research personnel could improve the accuracy and inclusivity of future analyses. While the qualitative features we identified, such as inclusive eligibility criteria, hybrid recruitment strategies, and community‐based outreach, are commonly described in trials achieving ≥ 50% female participation, we cannot determine whether these features are unique to female enrollment or also facilitate recruitment of male participants. Future quantitative studies linking these themes to trial‐level enrollment data across sexes and diverse subgroups are needed to assess their prevalence and potential effectiveness. Importantly, the qualitative analysis was restricted to trials with ≥ 50% female enrollment, and no parallel qualitative assessment was performed on trials with lower female participation. Consequently, the observations are descriptive and exploratory, and no inferences regarding prevalence or relative effectiveness of specific study features can be made.

The focus on US‐based trials also limits direct generalizability to international stroke research. Furthermore, while we identified efforts to include historically underrepresented racial and ethnic groups through stratified enrollment, the quantitative analysis did not delve into the specific representation rates of these groups, which would be a valuable area for future research.

Future research should quantitatively evaluate the prevalence and impact of the qualitative themes identified in this study across trials with varying sex distributions. This would provide more actionable insights into which study design and implementation features most strongly influence female recruitment and could guide evidence‐based strategies to improve sex balance in clinical trials.

Conclusion

Our study provides a critical snapshot of sex representation in US stroke clinical trials over the past decade, highlighting a persistent gap in female enrollment. By integrating quantitative analyses with qualitative insights, we have identified key trial characteristics, operational strategies, and persistent barriers influencing recruitment. Addressing the underrepresentation of females, alongside other diverse populations, is not merely an issue of equity but a scientific imperative.
