Multifaceted Characterization and Therapeutic Evaluation of Co-precipitated Cobalt Ferrite Nanoparticles for Magnetic Hyperthermia Cancer Therapy
Authors: R. Jafrin Reena, N. Arunai Nambi Raj
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim:Magnetic-mediated hyperthermia has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for treating cancer. This technique employs the heat dissipated by the magnetic nanoparticles when subjected to an external varying magnetic field, to bring about localized hyperthermia in tumor tissues. Owing to their conducive and tuneable “physical, chemical, and magnetic” characteristics, cobalt ferrite (CoFe2O4) nanoparticles are recognized as emerging contenders. The aim of the present work was to enhance the magnetic characteristics and guarantee the efficacy of CoFe2 O4 nanoparticles in targeting and eliminating cancer cells.Methods:CoFe2O4 nanoparticles were synthesized using the chemical co-precipitation route and underwent rigorous structural, morphological, and magnetic characterization techniques. The synthesized particles were then subjected to in vitro studies to evaluate their cytotoxicity and antimicrobial susceptibility.Results:The characterization techniques confirmed the cubic structure, ferrite phase, and spherical and magnetic nature of CoFe2O4 nanoparticles. The zeta potential was found to be − 0.0048V (4.8 mV). Cytotoxicity analysis exhibited decreased cell viability with increasing concentrations of CoFe2O4 nanoparticles. Antimicrobial studies displayed good inhibiting properties.Conclusion:The zeta potential of the synthesized CoFe2O4 nanoparticles was found to be higher than that of the breast cancer cells (MCF-7) which proves the synthesized drug to be effective. The in vitro studies also disclose the efficacy of the drug over cancer cells.

INTRODUCTION

The number of cancer cases is said to skyrocket up to 77% by 2050 according to a recent study released by the WHO’s cancer agency. Hence, scientists are coming up with more effective alternative modalities of cancer therapy.[1] Hyperthermia involves the mild elevation of body temperature to 278.15 K (5°C) above the human body temperature.[2] The spatial resolution of hyperthermia is limited. However, magnetic hyperthermia (MHT), circumvented by intravenous administration of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), is a noninvasive and targeted technique that makes use of the magnetic energy losses produced by MNPs in an oscillating magnetic field to remotely induce local heat in tumors.[3] It takes advantage of the fact that the tumor cells exhibit higher rates of glycolysis, increased metabolism, and heightened radiosensitivity.[4] MHT employs superparamagnetic nanoparticles and an alternating magnetic field (AMF) generator to induce thermal heating through Neel relaxation and hysteresis losses. The experimental setup typically comprises a high-frequency power source, precise temperature control and monitoring systems, and a customized sample holder.[5] By generating localized hyperthermia, MNPs can selectively target tumor tissues while minimizing collateral damage to surrounding healthy cells.[6] MHT is more beneficial for inoperable solid tumors located in sensitive areas such as brain, prostate, and breast that are difficult to treat with traditional methods (surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy) and for tumors that require targeted and localized treatment to minimize damage to the surrounding healthy tissues.[7]

Cobalt ferrite (CoFe2O4) nanoparticles are being actively investigated due to their “high anisotropy constant, high coercivity, moderate saturation magnetization (MS), and ease of synthesis.”[8] There are certain parameters to be considered before successfully engineering MNPs for hyperthermia cancer treatment. It is crucial to keep the cytotoxicity aspects in concern. To ensure that the nanoparticles are heated effectively in the oscillating magnetic field, the MS needs to be as high as possible. The capacity of MNPs to be concentrated and driven in a specific region within the body is to be considered.[9] To achieve this, it is necessary to strategically functionalize the surfaces of these MNPs to target the wounded cells and tissues with precision. Furthermore, the nanoparticles’ surface charge and coating may have an impact on how well they interact with biological systems. Thus, to reduce cytotoxicity and increase biocompatibility, MNPs must have their size, surface, charge, and coating optimized.[10]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The co-precipitation route was used to synthesize CoFe2O4 nanoparticles. All the chemicals used are of analytical reagent grade (Sigma-Aldrich chemicals). The raw chemical ingredients included sodium hydroxide (NaOH), ferric chloride (FeCl3), and cobalt chloride hexahydrate (CoCl2⋅6H2O). Co-precipitation of aqueous solutions containing FeCl3 and CoCl2 yielded nanoparticles. First, 0.009 L (3 ml) of 1 mol/L (1 mol) CoCl2⋅6H2O and 0.008 L (8 ml) of 1 mol/L (1 mol) FeCl3 were mixed at room temperature with continuous stirring for 1800 s (half an hour). The pH was gradually raised to 11 from a very low acidic pH of the solution mixture by adding one mol/L (1 mol) NaOH drop by drop at a rate of 2.5 × 10−8 m3/s (1.5 ml/min). The mixture was continuously stirred while being heated to 353.15K (80°C). The aging process was completed after 7200 s (2 h) followed by continuous heating and stirring.[11] After swirling the solution until it reached room temperature, the nanoparticles sedimented to the bottom. The solution was then washed with either hot deionized water or acetone. After being cleansed with acetone, the precipitate was then separated using a centrifugal separator.[12] For 172,800 s (48 h), the nanoparticles were allowed to dry at ambient temperature. After 2400 s (40 min) of grinding, the as-prepared sample’s thermal stability was assessed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) till 1473.15 K (1200°C). The sample was then calcinated at 673.15 K (400°C) for 14,400 s (4 h). The as-synthesized sample was then subjected to several characterization methods.[13]

RESULTS

Thermogravimetric and differential thermal analysis

Using TGA (TA Instrument, USA Model: SDT Q600) technology, the weight loss of the sample and the decomposition of the CoFe2O4 sample were conducted with temperature variation. The experiment was carried out in a nitrogen atmosphere till the temperature of 1473.15 K (1200°C) at 0.333 K/s (20°C/min).[14] From Figure 1, it is evident that the TGA curve exhibited three distinct weight losses, and the differential thermal analysis curve revealed three characteristic peaks (an exothermic peak and a couple of endothermic peaks) for every drop in weight. The first endothermic peak at 345.68 K (72.53°C) and the first 15.77% weight loss resulted from the ejection of moisture, which occurred in the temperature range of 303.15–673.15 K (30°C–400°C).[15] The breakdown of metal hydroxide was demonstrated by the second weight loss (13.33%), an exothermic peak at 760.15 K (487°C), at a temperature range of 673.15 K–1173.15 K (400°C–900°C). The formation of CoFe2O4 was indicated by the third weight loss (9.7%), the second endothermic peak, at the temperature of 1148.15 K (875°C). In all three cases, a weight reduction of almost 38% was observed. The produced nanoparticles were thus sintered for 4 h at 673.15 K (400°C) as the compound was found to be thermally stable at that temperature.[16]

Zeta potential

The Nanosizer (HORIBA SZ-100) was used to measure the particle size and zeta potential for colloidal stability. The zeta potential works based on the principle of dynamic light scattering (DLS).[22] It reveals the particle size and its potential using the DLS phenomena. The smaller the particles, the greater will be its velocity and the scattering will also be more. This will give rise to increased intensity fluctuations in the plot. For the study, 1 g of CoFe2O4 nanoparticles was combined with 4 ml of distilled water, and the mixture was filtered through filter paper and was sonicated for 900 s (15 min). DLS analysis was conducted on the sample under investigation at 298.15k (25°C), maintaining the viscosity of the dispersion medium up to 0.894 × 10−3 Pa.s. A conductivity level of 0.00201 S/m was employed while the electrode voltage was set at 3.4 V. Figure 6 illustrates the scattering light intensity versus zeta potential distribution of the cobalt ferrite nanoparticles. The zeta potential of the as prepared sample was determined to be −4.8 × 10−3 V accompanied by an electrophoretic mobility of −3.7 × 10−9 m2/Vs (−0.000037 cm2/Vs).[23]

Vibrating sample magnetometer studies

The magnetic characteristics of CoFe2O4 nanoparticles that were produced were assessed using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM with dry cryostat 5 Tesla, Nano Magnetic Instruments Ltd.) at room temperature and a magnetic field of 0.5 T (5000 Oe). 0.01 g (10 mg) of the sample was utilized for the study. The synthesized sample’s magnetic hysteresis (MH) loop is displayed in Figure 7. Table 4 lists the values of the several magnetic properties of the samples, which were obtained from the MH plots. The MS when converted to emu/g was calculated to be 24.9 × 10−3 T/g (24.9 emu/g) which provides good heating efficiency of the material. “Spinel ferrite’s magnetic characteristics are influenced by doping, cation dispersion, and particle size.”[24] The remanence magnetization was found to be 16.7 × 10−3 T/g (16.7 emu/g) which indicates the low magnetic memory of the material and coercivity was calculated to be 429.12910−4 T (429.129 Oe). The MH plot reveals the synthesized sample’s ferromagnetic nature. The magnetic moment (nB) was calculated to be 9.703 × 10−24 Am2 (1.046 μB). The squareness ratio that is the ratio of remanence magnetization with MS (Mr/Ms) of the prepared sample was observed to be 0.671.

MTT assay

The cytotoxicity assay of the prepared sample by 3 (4,5 dimethylthiazol 2 yl) 2,5 diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) method was performed at BioMe Live Analytical Centre, 2nd Floor, Kannappa Tower, College Road, Near Alagappa University, Karaikudi, Tamil Nadu. All the cell lines were procured from ATCC, and stock cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin (100 IU/ml), and streptomycin (100 µg/ml) in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37°C until confluent.

The 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay is a widely used method in cell viability studies. This assay involves the conversion of MTT, a tetrazolium salt, to a purple formazan product by mitochondrial dehydrogenase in live cells.[25] To conduct the assay, cells cultured in media containing 10% FBS were trypsinized and adjusted to a concentration of 1.0 × 105 cells/m3 (1.0 × 105 cells/ml). Each well of a 96-well microtiter plate received 0.0001 l (100 µl) of the diluted cell suspension, containing 50,000 Michigan Cancer Foundation – 7 (MCF-7) cells per well, followed by incubation for 86,400 s (24 h) at 98.6°F (37°C) in a 5% CO2 environment.[26] Subsequently, MTT solution was added to each well and incubated again for 14,400 s (4 h).[27] After removing the supernatant, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to dissolve the formazan product, and the absorbance was measured at 590 × 10−9 m (590 nm) using a microplate reader. The percentage of growth inhibition was calculated using the formula: “% Inhibition = 100 − (optical density [OD] of sample/OD of control) ×100.”[28] The half maximum inhibitory concentration value of the synthesized sample was determined to be 1.513 ± 0.9 × 10−6 mol/L (1.513 ± 0.9 µM), indicating its potency in inhibiting cell growth. The bar graph in Figure 9 and the Optical images of MCF 7(Human breast cancer cell line named after the Michigan Cancer Foundation) cells in Figure 10 illustrates the dose dependent decrease in cell viability with increasing concentrations of CoFe2 O4 nanoparticles.[29]

Antibacterial studies

The antibacterial activity of the as-synthesized sample was studied at BioMe-Live Analytical Services, 2nd Floor, Kannappa Tower, College Road, Near Alagappa University, Karaikudi. The well diffusion method was used to assess the tested sample’s antibacterial activity on blood agar. For the antibacterial experiment, the bacterial strain Bacillus subtilis served as the test organism. For the studies, 0.01 l (10 mg) of the tested sample was dissolved in 0.001 l (1 ml) of distilled water. In sterile conditions, the bacterial strain was inoculated onto nutrient agar plates and spread evenly using a glass L-rod with 1 × 10−4 l (100 µl) of cultured medium. Then, a thin film coated with a compound, measuring 0.01 m (10 mm), was affixed to each plate, followed by 86,400 s (24 h) incubation at 98.6°F (37°C). Chloramphenicol was added to distilled water as a control after dissolving the extracts. Each experiment was performed three times for accuracy.[30] The minimum inhibitory concentration or MIC of the synthesized drug was found to be 0.04 kg/l (40 mg/ml). The zone of inhibition of 17 × 10−3 m (17 mm) was observed in 0.1 kg/l (100 mg/ml) concentration. Figure 12 and Table 5 shows that the zone of inhibition increases as the concentration of CoFe2 O4 nanoparticles is increased from 0.04 kg/l (40 mg/ml) to 0.1 kg/l (100 mg/ml).[31]

DISCUSSION

The compound was found to be thermally stable at 673.15 K (400°C) by the TGA curve. Moreover, the spinel ferrite formation was indicated by the characteristic temperature peaks at the range, 1100.15–1173.15 K (827°C to 900°C).[32] Above 1173.15 K (900°C), no discernible weight loss was observed in the TGA graph. This demonstrates that the CoFe2O4 formed is pure. The FTIR results confirm the functional groups of the spinel ferrite with the two main absorption peaks like the O-H stretching vibration represented by the wide absorption peak at 34.26 m−1 (3426 cm−1) and the metal oxide (Fe-O) bond represented by the absorption peak at 5.39 m−1 (539 cm−1). The reported XRD pattern of the synthesized sample justifies the cubic spinel-like structure exhibiting the Fd-3m space group which is in good agreement with the JCPDS card no.: 221086. The mean size of the crystallites was determined to be 20.82 × 10−9 m (20.82 nm). This size is ideal enough to be assimilated by the cells but not washed away by the body’s immune system or the spleen.[33] SEM images substantiate the spherical nature of the synthesized CoFe2O4 nanoparticles. The magnetization of CoFe2O4 resulted in the attractive force in nanoparticles which led to agglomeration. EDX spectra confirm the presence of cobalt, oxygen, and iron and show their elemental composition. This outcome indicates that the atomic proportions of Co, O, and Fe closely matched the expected stoichiometric ratio of the elements in the produced nanoparticles.[34] The zeta potential of CoFe2O4 nanoparticles was recorded to be −4.8 × 10−3 V (−4.8 mV), while that of MCF-7 was −20.3 × 10−3 V (−20.3 mV), suggesting the drug’s effectiveness in targeting cancer cells. Vibrating sample magnetometry revealed the magnetic characteristics of the sample, indicating a ferromagnetic nature based on the MH plot. MHT studies revealed a temperature increase of 15℃ within 30 min of time interval proving the effective heat generation. The heating efficiency can be further enhanced by tuning the magnetic properties of CoFe2O4. This investigation confirms that factors such as particle size, A-B interaction, sample crystallinity, and sintering temperature significantly influence magnetization or magnetic properties. MTT assay reveals good cytotoxicity of CoFe2O4 nanoparticles against the MCF-7 cells.[35] Increased cytotoxicity is observed with increasing concentration of the drug. This study proves that the synthesized drug is ideal enough to kill the tumor cells. Further, the MTT assay carried out at different hyperthermia temperatures justifies the enhancement of the efficacy of CoFe2O4 nanoparticles against the MCF-7 cells with increasing hyperthermia temperatures. The in vitro antimicrobial studies reveal that the synthesized drug shows good antimicrobial properties with increasing concentration. This suggests that the drug is ideal enough to be assimilated by the human body for theranostic purposes.[36] Antioxidant studies reveal reduced oxidative stress and inflammation in tumor microenvironments, which helps in protecting surrounding healthy cells from the damage caused by cancer. Moreover, smaller nanoparticles exhibit heightened enhanced permeability retention (EPR) due to their ability to penetrate deeper into tumors. Irregularly shaped nanoparticles can evade phagocytosis and retain EPR longer than spherical ones. Larger nanoparticles may accumulate in tumors but clear slowly, reducing EPR. Optimal size (1–100 nm) and shape (ellipsoidal or discoidal) can balance accumulation and retention for enhanced EPR. EPR enhances efficient elimination by accumulating nanoparticles in tumors (0–24 h), retaining them for sustained drug release (24–48 h), and enabling clearance from the body (48–72 h). Optimizing time kinetics to prolonged half-life in tumors and increasing area under the curve ensures targeted drug delivery, with reduced systemic toxicity and enhanced treatment efficiency.[37] MHT cancer therapy offers several advantages, including targeted treatment, minimally invasive procedures, enhanced apoptosis, and potential combination with other therapies. In addition, real-time monitoring using magnetic resonance imaging is possible. However, limitations exist, such as nonuniform heat distribution, toxicity concerns, particle agglomeration, limited depth penetration, and tumor heterogeneity. Scalability and regulatory approval also pose challenges. Ongoing research aims to address these limitations and optimize MHT cancer therapy, which has the potential to provide a novel and effective treatment approach for various types of cancer.[38]

CONCLUSION

CoFe2O4 nanoparticles were successfully synthesized using the chemical co-precipitation method. Their cubic spinel structure was confirmed using the XRD pattern. FTIR analyses confirm the formation of ferrite phase. SEM images substantiate the spherical nature of the synthesized sample, and VSM confirms the magnetic nature of the sample. The MS was determined to be 24.9 × 10−3 T/g (24.9 emu/g) which is decent enough to get heated effectively when subjected to the AMF. This implies that a good heating efficiency can be achieved using a minimal dosage during the treatment.[39] The higher zeta potential of the synthesized CoFe2O4 nanoparticles proves the drug to be effective against cancer cells. The in vitro and antioxidant studies depict the efficacy of the drug over the tumor cells. The MTT assay treated at various temperatures also justified the contribution of hyperthermia temperature, to the increased efficacy of CoFe2O4 nanoparticles against MCF-7 cells. The next phase of the research involves surface functionalizing of the synthesized material to ensure biocompatibility and assessing in vivo studies and the potential applications of the CoFe2O4 nanoparticles in biological systems.[40]
