Effects of Physical Activity on Medical Expenses among Individuals with Obesity in Korea: Insights from a Nationwide Study
Authors: Ga Eun Nam, Youn Huh, Kyungdo Han, Kyu-Na Lee, Chung-Woo Lee, Wonsock Kim, Kye-Yeung Park, Hae-Jin Ko, Yoon Jeong Cho, Chong Hwa Kim, Seungjoon Oh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch on the relationship between physical activity and medical expenses among individuals with obesity has been sparse. This study investigates that association using nationwide data from Korea.MethodsWe analyzed data from the National Sample Cohort of the Korean National Health Insurance Service, including 112,531 adults with obesity who underwent at least two health screenings within a 2-year interval between 2009 and 2015. Participants were categorized into four groups based on changes in their regular physical activity during 2 years: non-exercisers, quitters, starters, and maintainers. A two-part model was used to assess the average and excess annual medical expenses per person, including outpatient and hospitalization expenses.ResultsLower levels of physical activity correlated with an increase in both average and excess annual medical expenses per person (P for trend <0.001). Non-exercisers showed the highest average annual medical expenses per person, followed by quitters, starters, and maintainers. The most substantial increases in overall medical expenses were observed among quitters, followed by non-exercisers, starters, and maintainers. These associations were prominent in individuals aged ≥65 years and those with both type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension.ConclusionIn this nationwide study of individuals with obesity, lower physical activity levels were associated with increased medical expenses. Consistently engaging in physical activity might significantly lower medical expenses, particularly among elderly people and individuals with comorbidities. These findings highlight the importance of promoting sustained physical activity as a strategy for managing healthcare costs among individuals with obesity.Keywords: Obesity, Exercise, Medical expense, Hospitalization, Outpatient department

INTRODUCTION

Regular physical activity reduces the risk of non-communicable diseases such as coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and cancer, while also increasing life expectancy.1 Additionally, physical activity improves mental health by reducing depressive disorder and anxiety and enhancing quality of life.2 Despite those benefits, the global prevalence of physical inactivity has increased from 23.4% in 2000 to 31.3% in 2022.3

Physical inactivity is estimated to cause an economic burden of 47.6 billion dollars per year worldwide4 and is responsible for 7.2% of deaths annually.5 Studies have also demonstrated that physical inactivity contributes to higher healthcare costs.4,6 The proportion of medical expenses attributed to physical inactivity varies across studies, ranging from 0.35% to 11.1%.7,8 Most research has focused on the effects of physical activity on healthcare costs in general populations9 or elderly people,10 with few studies examining its effects in specific groups, such as individuals with obesity.

Obesity imposes a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems.11 A nationwide study in Korea reported that medical expenses increased as body mass index (BMI) rose to 35 kg/m2 and waist circumference (WC) increased.12 Effective management of obesity is crucial to prevent escalating healthcare costs because weight loss reduces the risk of various diseases, including T2DM, hypertension, and heart failure.13 Physical activity plays a key role in weight loss14 and is more effective when combined with dietary interventions.15

In Korea, most obesity treatments, with the exception of bariatric surgery, are not covered by insurance,16 creating a significant financial burden for individuals with obesity. This lack of coverage limits access to treatments and underscores the need for evidence about the cost-effectiveness of obesity interventions. Such evidence could support policy adjustments to reduce financial barriers to effective obesity management. However, because most obesity treatments are not currently covered by insurance, estimating the total costs based on national data remains challenging.

To address those challenges, this study uses nationwide data to investigate the associations between the amount of and changes in physical activity and medical expenses in individuals with obesity.

METHODS

Data source and study population

In this study, we analyze data from the National Sample Cohort (NSC) of the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). The NHIS is the single universal insurer covering nearly the entire population of South Korea (approximately 50 million individuals). It provides national health checkups for all South Koreans aged ≥19 years, as well as for all employees, regardless of age. The NHIS database contains comprehensive information on demographic characteristics, health examination results, and medical claims classified according to International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. Since 2015, NHIS data have been made available to qualified researchers who submit study protocols approved by the official review committee. The NHIS-NSC comprises a nationally representative sample of approximately 1 million individuals, accounting for about 2% of the total South Korean population, to ensure the generalizability of findings to the broader population.

From this database, we initially identified 584,212 individuals aged ≥20 years who underwent national health screenings offered by the NHIS between January 1, 2009, and December 31, 2015. We applied the following exclusion criteria: individuals without obesity (BMI <25 kg/m2) (n=393,557), those who did not undergo a follow-up screening within 2 years (n=72,778), and those with missing data on any of the study variables (n=5,346). Ultimately, 112,531 individuals (66,700 males and 45,831 females) were included in the analyses.

This study was performed in accordance with the principles embodied in the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Guro Hospital (approval no. 2022GR0155). This review board waived the requirement for informed consent because the data contained only fully anonymized and non-identifiable information.

Evaluation of physical activity

Physical activity was measured using a standardized self-administered questionnaire based on the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Short Form.17 The questionnaire evaluates the weekly frequency and duration of physical activity across three intensity levels: light, moderate, and vigorous. Each intensity level was assigned a corresponding metabolic equivalent of tasks (METs) value: 2.9 for light-, 4.0 for moderate-, and 7.0 for vigorous-intensity physical activity.18-21 This assignment was based on prior studies conducted in similar populations18-20 and was chosen to minimize the potential for overestimation inherent in self-reported physical activity measures. The 2020 World Health Organization guidelines define vigorous-intensity activity as ≥6.0 METs, which includes the value used in this study.21 The total amount of physical activity (MET-min/week) was calculated as the sum of the frequency of each physical activity multiplied by its intensity and duration per week. Regular physical activity was defined as a total physical activity ≥500 MET-min/week.21 Based on changes in regular physical activity during the 2-year period between two consecutive health screenings, participants were classified into four groups: non-exercisers (inactive at both time points), quitters (active 2 years prior to baseline but inactive at baseline), starters (inactive 2 years prior to baseline but active at baseline), and maintainers (active at both time points).

Medical expenses paid by NHIS

The average annual medical expenses per person were defined as the total yearly expenses covered by the NHIS in Korean won (KRW, 1 USD=1,398 KRW). These expenses included the total cost of treatment and claims incurred by patients during both inpatient and outpatient care, excluding non-insured medical expenses. Outpatient and hospitalization expenses encompassed all expenses related to examinations, procedures, and medical or surgical treatments, with the exception of non-covered services, such as private room fees.

Covariates

The NHIS database contains detailed information on demographic characteristics and lifestyle factors, which were assessed using standardized self-administered questionnaires. Income level was categorized as low income (lowest 20% of the income range) and non-low income (remaining 80%). Smoking status was classified into three groups based on smoking history: never-smokers, ex-smokers, and current smokers. Alcohol drinkers were defined as individuals who consumed >0 g/day of alcohol.

Trained medical staff measured participants’ height, body weight, and WC following standard protocols. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a stadiometer, and body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg on a balance scale with participants wearing a lightweight gown or undergarments. WC was measured at the midpoint between the bottom of the subcostal region and the top of the iliac crest using a fiberglass tape. BMI was calculated as body weight in kilograms (kg) divided by height in meters squared (m2). Blood samples were collected after overnight fasting to determine the serum concentrations of glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and creatinine. T2DM was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL or at least one prescription for an anti-diabetic medication per year under ICD-10 codes E11–E14. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, or at least one prescription for an anti-hypertension medication per year under ICD-10 codes I10–I13 or I15. Dyslipidemia was defined as a total cholesterol concentration of ≥240 mg/dL or at least one prescription for a lipid-lowering medication per year under ICD-10 code E78. Chronic kidney disease was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation.

Statistical analyses

The characteristics of the study population, stratified by sex, are presented as the mean±standard deviation for continuous variables and numbers with frequencies (%) for categorical variables. Group comparisons were conducted using an independent t-test for continuous variables and a chi-square test for categorical variables. The average annual medical expenses per person, along with 95% confidence intervals were analyzed according to baseline physical activity levels and changes in regular physical activity using a two-part model. The analysis was adjusted for age, sex, income, residential area, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, hypertension, T2DM, dyslipidemia, and chronic kidney disease. Stratified analyses were conducted based on age, sex, T2DM, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute). A P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Annual medical expenses per person by the amount of baseline physical activity

Table 2 presents the average and excess annual medical expenses per person according to the baseline physical activity levels of participants. All the average annual medical expenses per person (outpatient, hospitalization, and combined outpatient and hospitalization expenses) showed a tendency to increase as baseline physical activity decreased (P for trend <0.001). Consequently, excess expenses (compared with ≥500 MET-min/week) tended to rise as baseline physical activity decreased. These trends were consistent across both males and females.

Annual medical expenses per person by changes in physical activity

Table 3 presents the average and excess annual medical expenses per person according to changes in regular physical activity among participants. The highest mean annual medical expenses per person (outpatient, hospitalization, and combined outpatient and hospitalization expenses) were observed in non-exercisers, followed by quitters, starters, and maintainers, in that order. Subgroup analyses

Table 4 presents stratified analyses of average and excess annual medical expenses per person according to changes in physical activity among participants categorized by age, sex, and comorbidities (T2DM, hypertension, and dyslipidemia). The results show consistent trends across almost all subgroup analyses. The association between changes in physical activity and average annual medical expenses per person was particularly pronounced in individuals aged ≥65 years (P for interaction <0.001), as well as for those with both T2DM (P for interaction=0.021) and hypertension (P for interaction <0.001), compared with their counterparts. Additionally, a stronger association with excess annual medical expenses per person was observed among individuals with hypertension (but not T2DM), compared with those without it (P for interaction=0.006).

DISCUSSION

This study used a large nationwide cohort of individuals with obesity to examine average and excess annual medical expenses per person based on baseline physical activity levels and changes in physical activity. We found that individuals with lower baseline physical activity had higher average and excess annual medical expenses than those with higher baseline physical activity. Furthermore, individuals who reduced their physical activity experienced the greatest increase in medical expenses. Subgroup analyses revealed that these trends were more pronounced in individuals aged ≥65 years and in those with both T2DM and hypertension, compared with those without those conditions.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies.1,22 Physical inactivity is associated with increased medical expenses because adopting a healthy lifestyle can help mitigate various adverse health outcomes, including T2DM, hypertension, and cancer, while also improving life expectancy.1 A study involving 13,791 Korean adults demonstrated that individuals engaging in 600 to 3,000 MET-min/week had a 27% and 32% reduction in annual healthcare utilization and medical expenses, respectively, compared with those with <600 MET-min/week.23 However, individuals exceeding 3,000 MET-min/week experienced an increase in annual medical expenses due to potential injury. This trend was particularly evident in individuals with ischemia.24 Additionally, a systematic review of 25 studies reported that physical inactivity was associated with a 9% to 27% increase in short-term medical expenses in a general adult population.7 Our study provides further evidence supporting the relationship between regular physical activity and healthcare costs among individuals with obesity, particularly in an Asian context. These findings hold significant implications for health policy, suggesting that promoting regular physical activity, especially in individuals with obesity, could play a critical role in reducing healthcare costs and improving public health.

Although no previous studies have specifically investigated the association between changes in physical activity and medical expenses in obesity, several previous studies have shown that changes in physical activity are associated with a reduced obesity risk and improved health outcomes in specific populations, such as adolescents24 and individuals with dementia.19 A study of approximately 5,700 adolescents found that maintaining physical inactivity from adolescence to adulthood was linked to a higher risk of abdominal obesity in females and overweight in males.24 In addition, maintaining regular physical activity was associated with a 7% reduction in the risk of T2DM.25 A retrospective cohort study of Korean adults with dementia showed that starting or maintaining regular physical activity was associated with a decreased risk of all-cause mortality.19 Moreover, previous studies have shown that medical expenses decreased by 0.4% and 1% when physical activity was maintained for 2 years or more and 3 years or more, respectively.12 Similarly, we also identified associations between changes in physical activity and medical expenses. Specifically, maintaining physical activity was crucial for individuals with obesity because reducing their physical activity was associated with increased medical expenses.

In our subgroup analyses, the associations between changes in physical activity and average annual medical expenses were most pronounced in individuals aged ≥65 years and those with both T2DM and hypertension. Furthermore, the association between changes in physical activity and excess medical expenses was stronger among individuals with hypertension (but without T2DM) than in those without it. These findings suggest that emphasizing physical activity, particularly among elderly people and individuals with comorbidities such as T2DM and hypertension, could result in significant reductions in medical expenses.

According to obesity management guidelines, individuals with obesity are recommended to engage in 150 minutes of aerobic exercise per week, along with strength exercise at least twice per week.15 Obese individuals with comorbidities such as cardiovascular diseases and T2DM should consult their healthcare providers to determine the appropriate type and intensity of physical activity before starting.26 Nonetheless, it is essential to educate individuals with obesity on the long-term benefits of maintaining physical activity. To achieve this, governmental and community support through relevant healthcare policies is crucial.

Our study has several limitations. First, lifestyle factors, such as physical activity, smoking status, and alcohol consumption, were self-reported, which could introduce recall bias. Second, we were unable to adjust for all potential confounding variables related to medical expenses, such as education level and household composition. Third, our study considered only medical expenses covered by the NHIS, excluding non-insured costs and indirect medical expenses. Additionally, it remains uncertain whether the differences in medical expenses were directly attributable to improvements in obesity or specific diseases. However, the beneficial effects of physical activity on obesity and chronic diseases are well established.

Despite those limitations, our study has identified significant associations between changes in physical activity and medical expenses among individuals with obesity in a large nationwide cohort. The large sample size allowed for comprehensive adjustment for confounders and subgroup analyses. Furthermore, the subgroup analyses provide valuable insights into the effects according to age, sex, and comorbidities.

In conclusion, we found that annual medical expenses per person decreased with increasing physical activity among Korean adults with obesity, and individuals who reduced their physical activity had the greatest increase in medical expenses among the groups analyzed. Therefore, promoting and maintaining physical activity could be a key strategy for reducing national healthcare costs. Educating individuals with obesity about the importance of physical activity is essential for alleviating the healthcare burden and minimizing associated costs.
