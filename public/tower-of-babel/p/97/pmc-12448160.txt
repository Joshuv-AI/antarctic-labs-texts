Pain medication associated with decreased physical performance and rehabilitation progress
Authors: Lara Mendes Fernandes, Philippe Vuistiner, Cyrille Burrus, Michel Konzelmann, Bertrand Léger, Marie Besson, François Luthi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

1. Introduction

Most countries are facing an epidemic of analgesic prescriptions, especially opioids.67 A retrospective analysis of drug prescriptions over 11 years (2008–2018) in a Swiss database showed increased use of all analgesics in patients with musculoskeletal injuries. The increase was even greater after minor injury.48 In Switzerland, opioid-related sales and poisonings increased by 91% and 177%, respectively, from 2000 to 2019.26 Early opioid prescribing and long-term opioid use may also be counterproductive to recovery from many conditions because of the impact on reported clinical outcomes, prolonged disability, and increased use of medical resources11,17,22,28,46,52,55,59 even at low doses.35

Most of these consequences have been documented using patient-reported outcome measures. Observational measures, also known as physical performance measures (eg, gait and lifting capacity, strength, and endurance), can provide clinically relevant information about patient functioning in addition to subjective self-reported measures. Studies of an association between analgesic use and physical performance are scarce. The only available data were measured in “pain rehabilitation programs” with people who had had pain for years and were taking high doses of opioids, with relatively small samples and weak statistical performance.14,34,39,53 Opioids were not a barrier to rehabilitation, and both opioid users and nonusers showed improved functional capacity. However, baseline performance was poor, and the relation between analgesics use and physical performance and rehabilitation progress has not been specifically investigated.14,34,39,53

We examined the association between the use of analgesics, including opioids, and physical performance in a large sample of people with chronic musculoskeletal pain (CMSP) requiring rehabilitation. Our main prespecified hypothesis was that analgesic users, particularly opioid users, would perform worse on physical performance tests than analgesic nonusers. The second hypothesis was that both analgesic users and nonusers would show improved functional capacity. For this, we measured gains in physical performance at the end of rehabilitation as well as the likelihood of achieving a clinically significant improvement in performance.

2. Materials and methods

This retrospective observational study was conducted in a Swiss rehabilitation hospital belonging to the Swiss National Accident Insurance Fund (Suva), Switzerland's main accident insurance fund. Every worker in Switzerland is insured against occupational and nonoccupational accidents and their consequences. Treatment expenses and daily benefits are covered (by the accident insurer) until possibility of return to work or the start of a disability pension.23,63 Nevertheless, access to rehabilitation requires financial coverage, which is not universal, but is granted by insurance on a case-by-case basis, subject to medical justification. We conducted an inferential analysis of a sample from a previous descriptive study.33

2.1. Study population

General practitioners, surgeons, or insurance medical advisors referred people of working age (18–65 years) to an interdisciplinary rehabilitation program with vocational components if they presented chronic pain (for >3 months), functional impairment, and inability to return to work after orthopedic injuries resulting from work, traffic, sport, or leisure accidents. Orthopedic injuries occur in the musculoskeletal system, including bones, cartilage, joints, ligaments, muscles, and tendons of the body.37 Common injuries include fractures, ligament tears, tendon tears, and joint dislocations. The recruitment lasted from May 6, 2014, to December 31, 2021.

The aim of the rehabilitation program (4-5 weeks with 3-4 hours of daily therapy, excluding weekends) was to manage pain and improve function, activity, and participation, including a return to work (usual or adapted), using an interdisciplinary biopsychosocial approach according to the recommended practice for patients with chronic pain.29 Our program had no specific analgesic medication reduction component.45

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were age 18 to 65 years and admission to the Rehabilitation Department of the Clinique Romande de Readaptation in Sion, Switzerland, for any injury of the upper and lower limbs, or spine. In case of 2 localizations, the most painful and problematic was retained. Exclusion criteria were being under legal custody; severe traumatic brain injury at the time of the accident, if any (Glasgow Coma Scale score ≤8); spinal cord injury; acute pain (<3 months); polytrauma; burns; and amputation. In case of multiple stays during the inclusion period, only the first was considered.

Normative values are available for healthy adults for the 6MWT,12 HST,18 FTSST,32 and PILE.43,44 Normative values for the SRT are available for age up to 24 years only.64 No normative values exist for the 1MSCT, only values obtained for populations with chronic pain.57,58

The 6MWT, PILE, and SRT were assessed in the whole sample. The 1MSCT and FTSST were assessed only in people with lower-limb and spine injuries. The HST was assessed only in people with upper-limb injuries.

2.4. Independent variables

At admission, participants were asked about their use of analgesic medication, and the medication was recorded in the electronic medical file. Medication intake and prescription during rehabilitation were also monitored. Stockpiled medications were also considered. The following analgesic medications were recorded using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification: paracetamol, metamizole, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), opioids, antiepileptic agents (gabapentin and pregabalin), and some antidepressants (duloxetine, amitriptyline, venlafaxine, and clomipramine). We classified participants into 1 of 3 categories according to the highest level of medication used upon admission: no analgesics (NAs), nonopioid analgesics (NOAs), and opioid analgesics (OPAs). For each type of medication, the number of pills and dosage were available, which allowed for computing an average daily dose by summing total consumption and dividing it by the duration of rehabilitation stay. For each opioid, the dose was converted to morphine-equivalent doses (MEDs), as described.19,50

The following variables were retrieved from the electronic medical record: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), mother language, education level (compulsory school [≤11 years] or higher education [>11 years]), vocational certification, full-time or part-time work before injury, work or leisure injury, time since injury, injury location (upper limb, lower limb, or spine), and number of surgeries. The severity of injury was evaluated with the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS),1 with scores from 1 to 6 (1 = minor, 2 = moderate, 3 = serious, 4 = severe, 5 = critical, and 6 = fatal). Comorbidities were assessed with the Cumulative Illness Rating Scale (CIRS),36 with scores from 0 to 56. Level of pain intensity was assessed with the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI),13,30 with scores from 0 to 10. Neuropathic components of pain were assessed with the DN4 questionnaire, a 10-item clinician-administered questionnaire.5

2.5. Data bias

Data were extracted from the electronic medical record. All functional performance tests were performed within 2 days of admission and within 2 days before discharge, thus minimizing measurement bias.2 Because missing data were rare (<2% in each independent variable), no specific procedure was used to deal with missing data.

2.6. Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean (SD) for continuous variables, except for time since injury, which is given as median (interquartile range [IQR]). The normality assumption of the distributions was checked visually with histograms. Categorical variables are presented with number (%). For simple comparisons between the 3 groups (NA, NOA, and OPA), 1-way ANOVA and the χ2 test were used for continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

Multiple regression models were used to assess the differences in functional capacity between the 3 antalgic groups, considering potential confounding factors. The confounding variables considered were age, sex, pain intensity (BPI), severity of injury (AIS), location of injury, level of education, comorbidities (CIRS), BMI, DN4 score (≥4), and surgery (yes vs no). To evaluate differences in improvement in functional capacity after the rehabilitation stay, we used performance at discharge as the outcome in our regression models and further adjusted for functional capacity at admission.62 Thus, the regression models had 15 factors. The sample size of our study was more than sufficient to comply with the “rule-of-thumb” of 15 observations needed per parameter.24

Pairwise comparisons between the 3 antalgic groups were obtained with contrasts, and Tukey method for multiple comparisons was used to control the error rate. The overall effect of the analgesic groups was calculated by using a likelihood-ratio test. A potential linear trend across the 3 groups was tested using contrasts.

Plots of residuals, Cook's distance, and leverage were calculated as model diagnostics. All functional capacity evaluations except 2, 1MSCT and FTSST (both at admission and at discharge), satisfied the required hypotheses of the regression models. For these 2 outcomes, we used log-transformation of data. The model coefficients were then exponentiated to interpret the results as relative differences. Because the PILE is stopped at a maximal weight of 27.5 kg, we applied a Tobit model with a censoring limit of 27.5, in that people could have reached higher values if they had been allowed to continue.

To evaluate potential multicollinearity among the explanatory variables, we computed the variance inflation factor (VIF).56

To better evaluate the success or failure of rehabilitation, we computed the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for each performance outcome.2 We estimated the odds of progressing for each analgesic group relative to the others with logistic regression models.

Sensitivity analyses were also performed. First, we excluded patients who were prescribed antidepressants because coprescription with analgesics can affect function. Second, we excluded patients who changed their medication group between admission and discharge to assess the potential impact of this change on physical improvement.

For each variable, the number of missing observations was mentioned. All statistical analyses were performed with Stata/SE 18.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX).

The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Commission cantonale d’éthique de la recherche sur l'être humain CER-VD Lausanne, Project-ID 2022-00366). Research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study followed the STROBE guidelines (checklist in the supporting information, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A338).

3. Results

3.1. Participant and descriptive data

Figure 1 shows the flow of participants in the study. We included 4,339 people; 84% were men (Table 1). The mean (SD) age was 44.4 (10.8) years, and the median time from injury to inclusion was 390 days (IQR 258-651). Overall, 85% of participants were working full-time before the injury, and injuries occurred in the professional setting in 56% of cases. Injuries were in the lower limb (42%), upper limb (44%), and spine (14%). Minor injuries (32%) and moderate injuries (54%) accounted for most cases. The mean BPI intensity score was 4.8/10 (2.0), and 28% of participants had neuropathic components of pain. The CIRS score showed a low prevalence of comorbidities.

In our sample, 60% of the patients were taking analgesic at admission. According to their highest analgesics consumption, the distribution of patients between the 3 groups was 1,731 (40%) taking NAs, 1,742 (40%) taking NOAs, and 866 (20%) taking OPAs. Paracetamol (41%) and NSAIDs (20%) accounted for most of the nonopioid analgesics used. Many participants combined several medications, such as NSAIDs and paracetamol. Few were taking “strong” opioids (2%) vs “weak” opioids (18%). Tramadol, codeine, and oxycodone accounted for 96% of prescriptions. For the OPA group, the median MED was 11.7 mg/d (IQR 7.5–20.0).

The 3 groups (NA, NOA, and OPA) differed statistically in most variables. The OPA group contained more women, more participants with spinal injuries, and more participants who had not undergone surgery than the other groups. The main difference between the groups was the intensity of pain in the opioid group (1.7 and 0.5 points higher than the NA and NOA groups, respectively, on the BPI intensity scale). The neuropathic component of pain was also more common in this group. In total, 8% of patients were taking gabapentinoids on admission. This proportion was higher in patients with than in those without a neuropathic component of pain (20% vs 4%).

Only 3.5% of the participants (n = 152) were prescribed antidepressants (5.7% in the NOA group and 6.0% in the OPA group). The distribution of missing values (functional performance tests) among the 3 antalgic groups showed that opioid patients more frequently had missing values (P < 0.001 for all functional tests, except HST unaffected side, P = 0.475).

3.3. Improvement in functional capacity

All 3 groups showed improvement in functional capacity after rehabilitation (Table 4: univariate analysis). The effect of analgesic groups on physical improvement during rehabilitation was weak for most outcomes (Table 5). The global effect (likelihood-ratio test) was not significant for 4 of 7 performance tests. For some measures, the 6MWT, PILE test, HST affected side, and FTSST, significant differences were observed but were small and clinically irrelevant (eg, 7 m for 6MWT distance between OPA and NA groups). Despite these small differences, the tendency for a linear trend across the 3 groups remained (NA > NOA > OPA). According to the VIF values, with a maximum of 3.2, we considered that there was no multicollinearity problem in our multivariate models.

3.4. Sensitivity analysis

In a sensitivity analysis (Table S1 and Table S2, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A336) excluding people taking antidepressants (n = 152), the differences in performance between groups were generally less pronounced, thereby suggesting a potential effect of antidepressants on performance, in addition to that of antalgics.

In the second sensitivity analysis, excluding all people who changed groups during rehabilitation, regardless of group switch (n = 1,289), the differences in performance at discharge tended to increase between the NOA and OPA groups and the NA group (Table S3, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A336). For example, the difference in improvement measured at discharge between the OPA and NA groups increased from 7 m to 12.5 m. Similar differences were found for the PILE, 1MSCT, and FTSST. We also observed reduced progression for the OPA vs NOA group but only for the HST affected side. These observations suggest that the impact of analgesics on rehabilitation outcomes may be more evident in homogeneous groups with stable prescriptions than in the overall sample.

4. Discussion

We examined the association between analgesics and physical performance in people with CMSP requiring rehabilitation. Physical performance at baseline in our sample was below the reference values for healthy adults, as found in other studies of people with CMSP,14,39,53 and was worse for patients taking than those not taking analgesics in all tests studied. We also found a negative gradient in performance with an increase in the WHO analgesic ladder.61,66 OPA patients performed worse than NOA patients, who performed worse than those taking no analgesics. Patients in the OPA group had more severe conditions (pain intensity, neuropathic component of pain, injury severity, surgery, or comorbidities) than other groups, but our models were adjusted for this. The differences observed in the opioid group were for people taking low MEDs.

According to the Canadian Medical Association guidelines,9 people should not take opioids >50 mg MED (ie, 4 times the average dose in this study) for chronic pain. The risk of adverse effects increases at 90 mg MED per day, which is often suggested as the maximum daily dose for chronic pain.49 Therefore, an average daily dose of 11.7 mg MED could be considered a low dose. These doses were much lower than those for people participating in “pain rehabilitation programs” in 2 studies.14,39 In these 2 studies, the mean MED was 28.6 and 39.4 mg/d.14,39 In the study by Crouch et al.,14 the mean 6MWT distance among patients who entered the program was only 364 m. The study by MacLaren et al.,39 which used other performance tests in patients taking opioids, also found poor physical performance. To our knowledge, no other study has measured the association between taking NOAs and physical performance in patients with CMSP. These studies and ours strongly suggest an association between the use of analgesics, especially opioids, and physical performance.

Our second hypothesis (ie, patients taking analgesics, including opioids, have the same chance of improvement in functional capacity as nonusers) was partly confirmed. All groups showed improvement with rehabilitation, which was consistent with findings of former studies with smaller samples and unadjusted statistical models.14,34,39,53 However, in most tests, the OPA group showed less improvement and had a lower chance of achieving a clinically relevant improvement than the NA or NOA groups. Sensitivity analyses showed that when the models were repeated excluding people who changed groups during rehabilitation, the differences between groups increased even further. These results suggest that chronic use of analgesics may be associated with a detrimental effect on the improvement of physical performance, especially with consumption of opioids.

Most limitations of our study are due to its retrospective and noncontrolled design. Analgesic use was measured from the medical record. We cannot guarantee that participants were not also taking over-the-counter or even illegally obtained analgesics, especially opioids. Similarly, we could not monitor analgesic use on weekends. Analgesic use during rehabilitation was controlled by the nursing staff, but the medication process relied on a therapeutic relationship of trust between health professionals and their patients. In addition, the exact duration of analgesic use before rehabilitation could not be monitored, and we were unable to provide sufficiently detailed data to monitor the evolution of treatments during the stay and conduct longitudinal analyses. However, the performance tests used are standardized and therefore comparable. To reduce measurement bias, physiotherapists who were familiar with the physical tests were given regular training and clear instructions (including when and how to encourage patients during the tests). Regardless, these tests are observational and subject to reciprocal influences. Studies show that the beliefs and attitudes of both therapists and patients can influence physical performance.3,65 Moreover, no causal association can be inferred, but the gradient observed between the WHO analgesic ladder and overall physical performance in other studies14,39 suggests that the observed association is likely in the direction of more analgesics linked to less physical performance, rather than the opposite. Given the large number of analyses performed, the occurrence of type 1 errors cannot be excluded. However, most results point in the same direction (ie, a harmful effect of analgesics), which tends to argue against this risk. Finally, the setting of our study limits generalizability to other populations.

The scientific data available to help interpret these results suggest that analgesics and physical performance likely interact in a complex way. Analgesics, including opioids, can be used to improve physical performance in pain-free athletes and are therefore considered doping.25,42 In the short term, analgesics may improve performance by reducing pain perception54; however, this effect may not last more than a few weeks. A meta-analysis of chronic use of strong opioids for noncancer pain and low back pain found low evidence of these drugs reducing pain for 4 to 15 weeks. However, after 6 months of use, the benefit in terms of pain or disability was lost.51 By contrast, 2 recent studies also support the likelihood that physical activity can benefit patients with chronic pain through biological mechanisms.7,8 A qualitative study of patients with fibromyalgia showed that participants themselves perceived the use of opioids (weak opioids, most commonly tramadol) as limiting physical activity47 due to opioid-induced fatigue, drowsiness, restriction of movement and speech, and dependence. Psychological factors (depression, anxiety, avoidance, and catastrophizing) are also associated with high perception of disability and reduced functional capacity, as is analgesic use.15,27 All these elements associated with medium- and long-term opioid use can reduce physical activity and exercise performance.21

In conclusion, our results showed that the prescription of NOAs and low-dose opioids was independently associated with poor physical performance at the beginning of a rehabilitation program in patients with CMSP. The odds of achieving clinically important improvements in most physical tests were weaker for people taking opioids than the NA group. However, because all groups showed improvement, NOAs and opioids should not be seen as a barrier limiting access to rehabilitation.6,20 Regardless, clinicians and patients should be aware of these detrimental effects. Although some patients would likely benefit from painkiller prescriptions, a clinical pathway including early pain education with components dedicated to analgesic withdrawal or dose reduction whenever possible should be promoted.31 The available data primarily focus on opioids. More research is needed to assess impact of long-term use of NOAs on physical performance.
