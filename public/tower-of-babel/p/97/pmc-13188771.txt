The gene mutation pattern of urachal squamous cell carcinoma resembles that of gastrointestinal tumors: a rare case with favorable prognosis following surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy
Authors: Xiaochun Lu, Xiangyun You, Tao Liu, Yanan Wei, Deming Sun, Cheng Yi, Tan Li, Junfeng Yu, Xiaobo Chen, Zhendong Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrachal carcinoma (UrC) is a rare epithelial malignant tumor of the urinary system, with an age-standardized incidence of 0.32 per million population, accounting for 0.35–0.70% of all bladder tumors. Adenocarcinoma is the most common histological subtype of UrC, while squamous cell carcinoma is extremely rare, representing only 2% of all UrC cases, and is associated with a high mortality rate. Due to its occult onset and high malignancy, urachal squamous cell carcinoma (USC) is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, and current understanding of this disease remains limited. Herein, we report a rare case of USC with complete clinical and therapeutic records, with no evidence of recurrence or metastasis identified during a 4-year follow-up period to date. Additionally, exome sequencing was performed, revealing that the gene mutation pattern of the carcinoma cell is closer to that of gastrointestinal tumors.Case presentationA 58-year-old male presented to a local hospital twice with gross hematuria at a 6-month interval. No abnormalities were detected on cystoscopy, urinary exfoliative cytopathology, or pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Five months after the second visit, the patient was referred to our hospital for the third episode of hematuria. Computed tomography urography (CTU) and pelvic MRI identified abnormal signals in the anterior wall of the urinary bladder, suggesting a urachal tumor. Subsequent cystoscopy with biopsy revealed acute and chronic mucosal inflammation with hemorrhage. The patient then underwent extended partial cystectomy combined with urachal and umbilical resection. Pathological examination confirmed moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma of the urachus, which had invaded the muscularis propria and lamina propria of the bladder wall. Exome sequencing was performed on DNA extracted from the patient’s tumor tissue, demonstrating that the tumor’s gene mutation pattern was closer to that of gastrointestinal tumors. Postoperatively, the patient received gemcitabine plus cisplatin chemotherapy, which was switched to docetaxel plus carboplatin adjuvant chemotherapy due to grade III myelosuppression. At the 4-year postoperative follow-up, no local or distant metastatic lesions have been identified, and long-term prognosis remains under surveillance.ConclusionsThis is a rare reported case of USC with long-term survival, accompanied by gene sequencing data. Most currently reported cases of USC are diagnosed at an advanced stage; thus, early diagnosis, complete surgical resection, and adjuvant chemoradiotherapy may contribute to long-term remission. In this patient, despite clear identification of the tumor on cross-sectional imaging, cystoscopic biopsy failed to retrieve tumor tissue, indicating that USC may not penetrate the bladder wall in a very early stage. We favor ultrasound-guided percutaneous biopsy may be a more appropriate diagnostic modality than cystoscopic biopsy in patients who present with small protrusions at the urachal-bladder junction and hematuria, in the absence of other imaging evidence of urinary tract tumors. Background

The urachus is a tubular structure connecting the apex of the fetal bladder to the umbilical cord during embryonic development. After birth, it normally obliterates to form the median umbilical ligament. Autopsy studies have shown that 32% of adults have urachal remnants, which are cylindrical structures partially lined with epithelial fragments, typically extending upward from the bladder apex through the Retzius space to the umbilicus [1]. Failure of urachal obliteration increases the risk of various pathological lesions, including urachal cysts, diverticula, fistulas, and malignant carcinoma [2]. The etiology of urachal carcinoma (UrC) remains unclear, with two main hypotheses regarding its origin: one suggests that UrC may arise from intestinal wall residues left in the cloaca during embryonic development, as it exhibits high histological similarity to colonic or rectal mucosal tumors, including complex glandular structures, mucin production, and occasional signet ring cells. Alternatively, other scholars propose that the urachal smooth muscle layer is continuous with that of the bladder, so the tumor may be related to focal metaplasia of the transitional epithelium lining the urachus [3, 4]. Approximately 90% of UrCs are adenocarcinomas, including simple adenocarcinoma, intestinal-type adenocarcinoma, adenocarcinoma with lymphoepitheliomatoid features, and clear cell urothelial carcinoma. Non-adenocarcinoma histological subtypes are relatively rare, accounting for less than 10%, including sarcoma, transitional cell carcinoma, signet ring cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, papillary urachal adenocarcinoma, urachal neuroblastoma, and urachal small cell carcinoma with neuroendocrine characteristics [5]. The present case involves non-adenocarcinoma USC, an extremely rare pathological type representing only 2% of all UrC cases [6, 7].

Case presentation

The patient was a 58-year-old male. In February 2021, he was admitted to a local hospital with progressive gross hematuria for 2 weeks, accompanied by blood clots, occasional frequency, urgency, and dysuria. Abdominal CT of the urinary system demonstrated bladder hemorrhage, and CTU revealed mild thickening of the superior bladder wall, a blood clot in the right posterior wall, and an indwelling urethral catheter (Fig. 1A). Pelvic MRI showed an underfilled bladder with an indwelling catheter, and no obvious effusion or lymphadenopathy was detected in the pelvic cavity (Fig. 1B, C). Blood routine, urine routine, and red blood cell morphology were examined. No tumor cells were detected on urinary exfoliative cytopathology (Table 1), and no obvious space-occupying lesions were found on urethrocystoscopy. Pathological examination of the biopsy specimen revealed a small amount of well-differentiated urothelial lining, lamina propria edema, small vascular proliferation, dilatation and hyperemia, hemorrhagic foci, and stromal infiltration of neutrophils and eosinophils. The patient was treated with hemostatic therapy during hospitalization and discharged after clinical stabilization. In July 2021, the patient was readmitted to the local hospital with recurrent hematuria for 1 week, without blood clots, but accompanied by frequency and urgency. Urinary CTU showed slight thickening of the upper bladder wall (Fig. 1D). Cystoscopy revealed rough mucosa in the posterior bladder wall, and no tumor cells were found on exfoliative cytopathology. The hematuria improved, and the patient was discharged for observation.

In December 2021, the patient was admitted to the local hospital again with hematuria for 20 days. CT/MR showed a tumor in the anterior bladder wall (Fig. 2A, B), and he was transferred to our hospital. At this admission, the patient had no blood clots but reported groin dull pain, hesitancy, frequency, and urgency without dysuria. Cystoscopy was performed (Fig. 2C, D), revealing a follicular mass approximately 3 cm in diameter with an unclear base on the bladder wall; bilateral ureteral orifices were normal with no bleeding. Biopsy results showed acute and chronic mucosal inflammation with hemorrhage. The patient underwent extended partial cystectomy under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, a firm mass measuring 3 cm in diameter was identified in the anterior bladder wall, with the urachus adhering to the ventral surface of the tumor. The tumor, along with 2 cm of surrounding bladder tissue, the umbilicus, and the entire urachus, was resected en bloc (Fig. 2E, F).

Postoperative pathological examination confirmed moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma of the urachus, invading the muscularis propria and lamina propria of the bladder wall. No perineural invasion or intravascular tumor thrombus was identified, and no tumor was found in the umbilical skin or subcutaneous tissue. Immunohistochemical staining showed: P40 (+), P63 (+), CK5/6 (+), CK7 (focal +), GATA3 (focal weak +), S-100 (nerve marker), D2-40 (lymphatic vessel marker), CD34 (blood vessel marker), P53 (mutant expression pattern), and Ki-67 (+, 40%).

Tumor tissue pathological specimens from the patient were sent to Beyotime Biotech Inc. for sequencing using next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology. Paired-end sequencing of the libraries was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Bioinformatic analysis identified a total of 11,481 mutated genes, among which the top 2,814 genes with the highest mutation rates were further screened out (Table S1). Notably, three key genes associated with colorectal cancer reported in the aforementioned study—KRAS, GNAS, and APC—were all present in the mutation list of this case, suggesting a potential similarity between USC and gastrointestinal tumors [8].

For further validation, To clarify the molecular classification of this urachal squamous cell carcinoma (USC) case, we performed pathway enrichment analysis, gene expression profiling, and unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis on the more than 2,000 mutated genes identified, based on the high‑frequency mutated gene set. Pathway enrichment results showed that the mutated genes were significantly enriched in core characteristic pathways of gastrointestinal tumors: the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (key gene APC), the MUC family-mediated mucin secretion pathway (11 MUC family genes), the HLA-mediated antigen presentation pathway (9 HLA family genes), and the Cadherin adhesion pathway (9 Cadherin family genes). These pathways are all hallmark driver pathways of gastrointestinal tumors such as colorectal cancer. In contrast, for the core pathways of urinary tract tumors (PI3K/Akt and FGFR signaling pathways), only 2 non-core genes (FGFR4 and PIK3C2B) were detected, while only 1 core driver genes (FGFR3). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis further confirmed that this sample clustered into the same branch as 50 colorectal cancer samples (average branch distance 0.28) and belonged to an independent branch from bladder cancer samples (average branch distance 0.83) (Fig. 3).

The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was referred to the Oncology Department of our hospital for adjuvant chemotherapy with gemcitabine plus cisplatin. With regard to chemotherapy, the patient received adjuvant chemotherapy with a total of four cycles, and the detailed regimen is as follows: gemcitabine 1600 mg was administered intravenously on Day 1 and Day 8, and cisplatin 40 mg was administered intravenously on Day 1 and Day 3 for the first cycle. However, Grade Ⅲ bone marrow suppression occurred during the first cycle, so the gemcitabine dose on Day 8 was omitted. For the second, third, and fourth cycles, the regimen was adjusted to docetaxel plus carboplatin: docetaxel 110 mg and carboplatin 460 mg were both administered intravenously on Day 1. During chemotherapy, the patient received supportive care including hepatoprotective, gastroprotective, and antiemetic therapy; recombinant human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor and interleukin-11 (IL-11) injections were also administered as needed to maintain normal white blood cell and platelet counts.

After 4 years of follow-up, the patient currently has no radiological recurrence (Fig. 4A, B) but still presents with frequent micturition and dysuria. A repeat cystoscopy revealed congestion and edema of the bladder mucosa with partial detachment of the mucosal layer on the inner wall of the bladder (Fig. 4C, D). This is considered a long-term complication of chemotherapy (interstitial cystitis). The patient’s symptoms improved slightly after receiving weekly intravesical instillation of sodium hyaluronate.

Discussion

As early as 1870, Hoffmann et al. reported the first case of USC [9]. The main clinical manifestations of UrC reported to date include hematuria (54–85%), pain (40%), bladder irritation symptoms (40%), albuminuria (17–25%), lower abdominal mass (17%), urinary tract infection (8%), umbilical discharge (2%), and the present patient’s primary symptom was hematuria [10]. Given its insidious onset and non-specific clinical manifestations, the diagnosis of UrC is typically based on imaging modalities. CTU, MRI, and PET-CT can detect lesions and metastatic foci early, while cystoscopic biopsy of progressive space-occupying lesions is an effective method for further confirmation [11]. A notable feature of this case is that no obvious tumor lesions were detected on initial imaging and cystoscopic biopsy, which may be attributed to the limited invasion of the bladder mucosa by the urachal tumor in the early stage. However, imaging performed 5 months later revealed a distinct tumor in the anterior bladder wall, indicating rapid tumor progression. This indicates that urachal tumors should be included in the differential diagnosis of patients with unexplained hematuria, particularly those with radiological evidence of suspected urachal remnants, and close follow-up with serial imaging is recommended. Surgical exploration and biopsy may be necessary if clinical suspicion persists.

Although postoperative pathology demonstrated tumor invasion into the lamina propria and muscularis propria of the bladder wall, no malignant cells were detected in two separate cystoscopic biopsies. We speculate that this discrepancy might be attributed to the following reasons: (1) Inflammatory edema may have masked the true transmural invasion of the tumor; (2) The anterior location of the lesion may have led to sampling error during cystoscopic biopsy. In view of these inconsistent findings, we propose that more refined diagnostic approaches merit further investigation, and their clinical value still needs to be verified by additional clinical studies.

Clinical practice guidelines for the diagnosis and management of bladder cancer (including urachal carcinoma) explicitly state that percutaneous needle biopsy is not recommended for localized masses suspicious for urachal carcinoma. The guidelines emphasize that if urachal carcinoma is highly suspected on cross-sectional imaging [computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)] and the tumor is resectable, en bloc resection should be performed directly [12]. The primary reason is that urachal carcinoma is most commonly a mucinous adenocarcinoma with a soft texture; cancer cells may be carried out during needle withdrawal and implanted along the needle tract in the abdominal wall or subcutaneous tissue, leading to local recurrence. Although modern puncture techniques (e.g., trocar needles) have significantly reduced this risk, clinicians typically adopt the most conservative strategy for this rare and highly aggressive malignancy. In the present case, no tumor cells were identified on the initial cystoscopic biopsy, and contrast-enhanced CT and MRI also yielded negative findings.

In this case, no tumor cells were identified in the first cystoscopic biopsies, and contrast-enhanced CT and MRI also showed negative findings. Nevertheless, a urachal tumor was detected in the patient 5 months later. For these reasons, We favor ultrasound-guided percutaneous biopsy (using a short needle with oblique insertion, and avoiding bladder penetration as much as possible) may be a more appropriate diagnostic modality than cystoscopic biopsy in patients who present with small protrusions at the urachal-bladder junction and hematuria, in the absence of other imaging evidence of urinary tract tumors.

Currently, the diagnostic criteria for UrC include: (1) the tumor is located in the bladder dome or anterior wall; (2) exclusion of cystitis glandularis and cystitis cystica; (3) predominant invasion of the bladder muscularis propria or deeper tissues, with a clear boundary between the tumor and the surface epithelium, and no glandular or polypoid hyperplasia of the overlying mucosa; (4) association with urachal remnants; (5) tumor invasion of the bladder wall extending to the Retzius space, anterior abdominal wall, or umbilicus; (6) no evidence of a primary tumor elsewhere. Imaging and surgical specimens of this patient confirmed the tumor was located in the anterior bladder wall and associated with urachal remnants, without umbilical invasion, which is consistent with the diagnostic criteria. The commonly used staging systems for UrC are the Sheldon staging [13] and Mayo staging [2]. The latter is simpler and more widely used in clinical practice. Szarvas et al. reported that Sheldon stage > IIIB is significantly associated with poor survival, while Mayo stage > II is associated with shorter survival. The present patient was classified as Sheldon stage IIIA and Mayo stage II based on pathological findings, and long-term prognosis requires further follow-up [14].

A PubMed search for USC (up to January 2026) revealed only 15 published cases to date (Table S2). To our knowledge, the present case is the 16th reported case of USC. Among these 16 patients, 14 underwent surgical treatment with en bloc resection, and 4 of these 14 patients died of local recurrence within 9 months, highlighting the aggressive nature of this disease [9].

Similar to other pathological types of UrC, surgical resection is the mainstay of treatment for non-metastatic USC. The Canadian Urological Association recommends extended partial cystectomy with complete resection of the urachus and umbilicus for localized urachal cancer [3]. Negative surgical margins and en bloc resection of the urachus and adjacent bladder tissue are critical for improving clinical prognosis, whereas pelvic lymph node dissection has no demonstrated survival benefit in patients without radiological evidence of lymphadenopathy [2, 15]. Preoperative pelvic MRI in this patient showed no obvious lymphadenopathy, so pelvic lymph node dissection was not performed during surgery, and long-term prognosis requires further follow-up.

At the time of treatment, we had limited clinical experience and insufficient relevant literature references for USC; therefore, the patient was treated with standard chemotherapy regimens for urothelial and squamous cell carcinomas. But comparative analysis of gene expression profiles showed that the core mutated genes in this case had a 76% overlap with the TCGA colorectal cancer dataset (TCGA-COAD) but only 21% with the bladder cancer dataset (TCGA-BLCA). Molecular analysis indicates that the molecular characteristics of this USC case are more similar to those of gastrointestinal tumors, providing direct evidence for the hypothesis that it originates from residual intestinal wall tissue in the embryonic cloaca [5] and offering a molecular basis for the optimization of subsequent chemotherapy regimens. In addition, a meta-analysis by Szarvas et al. indicated that chemotherapy with 5‑FU plus cisplatin was associated with significantly improved survival outcomes in patients with urachal adenocarcinoma. Taken together, we propose that chemotherapy regimens commonly used for gastrointestinal malignancies may represent a more optimal therapeutic option for USC in future clinical practice.

Conclusions

This case highlights the rarity and aggressive nature of USC and provides valuable insights into its diagnosis, treatment, and molecular characteristics. Early diagnosis, complete surgical resection, and appropriate adjuvant chemotherapy may improve long-term outcomes. Cystoscopy may be insensitive for early‑stage USC because of limited mucosal invasion. Percutaneous biopsy should be considered a valuable alternative only for highly suspected cases at a very early stage without a definite mass on imaging; it is not indicated once a discrete mass is visualized. Genomic profiling supports that USC is molecularly close to gastrointestinal tumors, likely originating from embryonic intestinal remnants in the cloaca. Larger studies with long‑term follow‑up are needed to validate these findings and optimize management.
