Polysialic acid is associated with immune activation in human peripheral blood nuclear cells
Authors: Sogand Makhsous, Carmanah Hunter, Vincent Gélinas, Charles Joly Beauparlant, Charmaine van Eeden, Mohammed Osman, Arnaud Droit, Lisa Willis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune dysregulation is a persistent state in which cells of the immune system become activated at low levels for long periods of time. If left unchecked, this chronic inflammation can lead to the development of chronic inflammatory diseases, including cancer, heart disease, autoimmune disease, and neurodegenerative disease. Glycans, especially sialic acids, are critical regulators of immune cell functions in health and disease. One specialized form of sialic acid that remains relatively understudied is polysialic acid (polySia), a long homopolymer comprised of α2,8-linked sialic acids in humans. The role(s) of polySia in altering immune cell functions in various immune cell subtypes in both health and disease remains limited but is a rapidly developing area of research. In this study, we used cigarette smoking as a model for chronic inflammation and performed the first in-depth characterization of polySia in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from smokers and age/sex-matched non-smokers. PolySia was expressed across all major myeloid and lymphoid immune cell types, but striking differences were observed among different immune subsets. Surface expression of polySia correlated with prior antigen exposure, especially in T cells, and could be induced within 24–72 hours following activation of naïve T cells. Dysregulation of polySia was observed in monocytes, antibody secreting B cells, and CD8+ T cells from smokers. While no sex differences were observed in healthy human PBMCs, we discovered that smoking had a differential effect on polySia expression in males and females, particularly for non-classical monocytes and CD8+ T cells.

Introduction

Immune dysregulation is a persistent state in which cells of the immune system become activated at low levels for long periods of time which leads to numerous health complications (Furman et al. 2019). A normal immune response is characterized by acute inflammation involving a transient increase in immune activation in response to injury or infection that is self-limiting, and leads to removal of terminally differentiated cells and the development of immunological memory (Li et al. 2007; Lam et al. 2024; Mahmoud et al. 2024). Acute inflammation is required for wound healing, tissue remodeling, and protection from pathogens. In contrast, chronic inflammation is induced through the combinatorial action of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental (e.g. exposure to smoke) factors which contribute to long term tissue damage (Elisia et al. 2020; Caliri et al. 2021). When left unchecked, chronic inflammation can lead to the development of chronic inflammatory diseases, including cancer, heart disease, autoimmune disease, and neurodegenerative disease (Furman et al. 2019; Elisia et al. 2020; Caliri et al. 2021). Collectively, these diseases represent the leading causes of disability and mortality.

Glycans are critical regulators of immune cell functions. All immune cells are coated with a dense layer of glycans, the glycocalyx, which is ideally positioned to modulate interactions with the extracellular environment. Sialic acids in particular are located at the terminal positions of many glycans and play a central role in promoting receptor-ligand interactions that result in dramatic changes in immune cell functions (Wißfeld et al. 2024; Lin et al. 2025). Sialic acids are directly recognized by their cognate receptors, the sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectins (siglecs), which are primarily found on immune cells and regulate numerous aspects of immune activation (Duan and Paulson 2020). The sialic acid–siglec axis has recently garnered significant attention as a critical immune checkpoint pathway with promising therapeutic implications for cancer, autoimmunity, and neurodegenerative disorders (Stanczak and Läubli 2022; Jame-Chenarboo et al. 2024; Wißfeld et al. 2024; Min et al. 2025). Thus, a more comprehensive evaluation of the influence of sialic acids in human immunity is of critical importance.

One specialized form of sialic acid that remains relatively understudied is polysialic acid (polySia), a long homopolymer comprised of α2,8-linked sialic acids in humans (Mindler et al. 2021). Unlike the more common α2,3- and α2,6-linked monosialosides, polySia expression is limited in both the proteins it modifies and the body systems in which it occurs. Indeed, immune cells are some of the few cell types in which polySia is expressed in healthy adults (Colley et al. 2014; Sato and Kitajima 2020). The role(s) of polySia in altering immune cell functions in various immune cell subtypes in both health and disease remains limited but is a rapidly developing area of research. To date, most research has focused broadly on major subtypes of innate immune cells and the role of polySia in their migration (Bax et al. 2009; Rey-Gallardo et al. 2011; Stamatos et al. 2014). However, some studies also suggest that polySia may participate in specific receptor-ligand interactions that regulate the immune response, though mechanistic details are scarce (Kanato et al. 2008; Ono et al. 2012; Shahraz et al. 2015; Thiesler and Hildebrandt 2024). Notably, no mouse homologs of human polySia-binding siglecs (e.g. Siglecs-11 and -16) exist (Duan and Paulson 2020), and sequence and expression differences between polySia-binding siglecs are observed even among primates (Angata and Varki 2023), suggesting a uniquely human role for polySia in immune regulation.

Cigarette smoking remains one of the most pervasive and preventable causes of chronic inflammation and chronic inflammatory diseases worldwide (Elisia et al. 2020; Loretan et al. 2022). It is a well-established driver of systemic inflammation and is associated with numerous pathologies in which polySia dysregulation has been implicated, including cancer, cardiovascular disease, and autoimmune disorders (Caliri et al. 2021). Moreover, smoking induces a hypoxic state, which has been shown to stimulate polySia expression in cell lines (Elkashef et al. 2016). Finally, smoking is known to promote epigenetic changes that contribute to chronic immune cell activation (Saint-André et al. 2024). We therefore consider smoking a natural model for human chronic inflammation that can be leveraged to study polySia biology.

In this study, we performed the first in-depth characterization of polySia in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from smokers and age/sex-matched non-smokers. PolySia was expressed across all major myeloid and lymphoid immune cell types, but striking differences were observed among different immune subsets. Surface expression of polySia correlated with prior antigen exposure, especially in T cells, and could be induced within 24–72 hours following activation of naïve T cells. Dysregulation of polySia was observed in monocytes, antibody secreting B cells, and CD8+ T cells from smokers. While no sex differences were observed in healthy human PBMCs, we discovered that smoking had a differential effect on polySia expression in males and females, particularly for non-classical monocytes and CD8+ T cells.

Results

PolySia is broadly expressed in innate immune cells, with sex-specific changes in expression induced by smoking

Previous attempts to study polySia expression in PBMCs were limited by the technology of the time, resulting in inconsistent or incomplete immune profiling (Curreli et al. 2007; Moebius et al. 2007). To overcome these limitations, we applied high-dimensional spectral flow cytometry to quantify cell surface polySia expression in major and minor immune subsets within human PBMCs. This modern technology enables larger, more complex multicolor panels with improved sensitivity and reduced compensation artifacts, allowing reliable resolution of major and minor immune subsets (McCausland et al. 2021). Spectral flow cytometry thus allows heavily polysialylated cells to be analyzed alongside less polysialylated cells, providing a more complete understanding of relative polySia levels. We detected polySia using a catalytically inactive endosialidase mutant (EndoNDM), which binds polySia with high specificity without cleaving it (Aalto et al. 2001; Jokilammi et al. 2004; Stamatos et al. 2014). To confirm specificity, samples from each donor were treated in parallel with the active endoneuraminidase EndoN, which selectively cleaves α2,8-linked polysialic acid chains (Rutishauser et al. 1985; Pelkonen et al. 1989; Stummeyer et al. 2005). Heat-inactivated EndoN was used as a negative control. Treatment with active EndoN markedly reduced the polySia signal and was used as a threshold for gating polySia+ populations within each subset, whereas heat-inactivated EndoN preserved the signal (Fig. S1A, Fig. 1). Moreover, to further validate the polySia signal, we also stained one sample with the gold-standard α-polySia mAb 735 (Häyrinen et al. 1995; Häyrinen et al. 2002; Nagae et al. 2013) and showed a concordant pattern with EndoNDM staining across immune subsets (Fig. S2). Using this technology, we quantified polySia expression in PBMCs from 32 age- and sex-matched healthy young adults (20–40 years old; 16 smokers and 16 non-smokers) and assessed both the percentage of polySia+ cells and the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of polySia on those cells.

We first analyzed innate immune populations, as most existing literature on the role of polySia in the immune system has focused on monocytes and dendritic cells (Sato and Kitajima 2020). PolySia+ cells were detected across all innate immune populations, including monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), NK cells, and NKT cells, consistent with previous reports for healthy donors (Moebius et al. 2007) (Fig. 1A,B and S1). We observed no sex differences in polySia proportion or MFI in healthy innate immune cells (Fig. S3). Smoking, however, had a major impact on the monocyte population as these cells had a significantly higher frequency of polySia+ cells (P = 0.017) in smokers (Fig. 1B). Long-time, heavy smokers have higher numbers of monocytes (Bergmann et al. 1998; Pedersen et al. 2019; Elisia et al. 2020; Sangani et al. 2023). We therefore looked to see if the observed increase in polySia levels in smokers was associated with increased proportion of monocytes. However, we observed no significant differences in total monocyte proportion between smokers and non-smokers (Fig. S4), likely owing to the younger age of our donors. Similarly, we observed no correlation between total monocyte levels and polySia. Monocytes have two major phenotypes. Classical CD14+CD16− monocytes are phagocytic and migratory, whereas non-classical CD14dimCD16+ monocytes are involved in complement-mediated killing and adhesion (Kapellos et al. 2019). We found that both populations trended towards a higher proportion of polySia+ cells in smokers but observed surprising sex differences in the MFI for non-classic monocytes (Fig. 1C-E). In females, smoking caused a small but not significant decrease in polySia MFI but in males, the effect of smoking was a significant increase in polySia MFI. We cannot rule out the possibility that these sex differences are attributable to smoking intensity and/or duration, as we do not have this information for our samples. In comparison, smoking had a limited impact on polySia expression on dendritic, NK, and NKT cells (Fig. 1B, F and S3). Both NK and NKT cells showed near-uniform polySia expression and MFI intensities were more than an order of magnitude higher than any other innate subset, which was expected due to their high expression of NCAM/CD56, the best-characterized polysialylated protein in humans (Gretenkort et al. 2023). Correspondingly, NKbright cells, which express more NCAM/CD56, had higher polySia MFI than NKdim (Fig. 1F).

Together, these findings demonstrate that polySia is broadly expressed across innate immune cells and suggest that smoking enhances polySia expression, particularly in monocytes. These results support a model in which environmental exposures such as tobacco smoke can dynamically modulate immune cell glycosylation.

Circulating B cells are polysialylated

B cells are central to humoral immunity, with their functional diversity shaped by activation, class switching, and differentiation into antibody-secreting B cells (ASBs). These transitions involve extensive remodeling of membrane architecture, receptor expression, and immune signaling pathways (Cyster and Allen 2019). α2,6-linked sialylation has been well studied in B cell biology for its roles in regulating the influence of CD22 on B cell activation and antibody glycosylation. However, it was long thought that B cells did not express α2,8-linked sialic acids (Curreli et al. 2007; Moebius et al. 2007), despite expressing ST8Sia4 and ST8Sia6 (Uhlen et al. 2010).

To address this gap, we analyzed polySia expression across peripheral B cell subsets (Weskamm et al. 2022) and evaluated how it is modulated in smokers. After excluding T cells, monocytes, and NK/NKT cells, B cells were defined using canonical markers CD19 and CD20, with further stratification based on IgD, IgM, IgG, and CD27 expression to distinguish naïve, memory, class-switched, and antibody secreting populations (Fig. S5). We observed cell surface polySia on all B cell subtypes (Fig. 2A and B). In healthy non-smoking adults, both the proportion of polySia+ cells and the relative MFI increased as cells became more differentiated, with 50% of antibody secreting cells expressing relatively high levels of polySia (Fig. 2B). For most of these cell types, there were no clear sex differences with the exception of antibody secreting B cells, for which healthy males had slightly less polySia than females (Fig. S6). To explore the impact of chronic inflammation, we compared the effect of smoking on B cell polySia profiles (Fig. 2C). While no differences were observed in naïve and memory subsets, the proportion of polySia+ antibody secreting B cells was dramatically reduced in smokers. Again, these changes in polySia+ B cell frequencies were not accompanied by any loss of B cells in smokers (Fig. S4).

Together, these findings demonstrate that polySia is dynamically regulated during B cell differentiation in peripheral blood and suggest that chronic inflammation, as exemplified by smoking, selectively disrupts polysialylation in antibody secreting terminally differentiated B cells. This altered glycosylation landscape may have implications for humoral immune function under inflammatory conditions.

PolySia dramatically increases during T cell activation, especially in smokers

T cells are essential effectors of adaptive immunity, undergoing dynamic phenotypic and functional remodeling during activation, differentiation, and memory formation (Lam et al. 2024). In previous work, we reported polySia expression on bulk CD3+ T cells from human PBMCs using immunoblotting (Hunter et al. 2023a). To more precisely define polySia distribution across T cell subsets, we performed a detailed characterization of T cells from human PBMCs using spectral flow cytometry.

We first focused on naïve (TN), central memory (TCM), effector memory (TEM), and effector (TEFF) subsets within CD4+ and CD8+ compartments of healthy non-smokers (Fig. S1). To our surprise, TN cells expressed no detectable cell surface polySia in either CD4+ or CD8+ subsets (Fig. 3A and B). However, a striking trend of increasing polySia+ cells as cells differentiated emerged that mirrored what we observed in B cells. For CD4+ cells, the percentage of polySia+ cells and the MFI increased as cells differentiated. For CD8+ cells, the proportion of polySia+ cells also increased as cells differentiated to TEM, however no changes were observed in the MFI. Additionally, the polySia+ TEFF cell proportion was reduced compared to TEM, suggesting potential downregulation or shedding of polySia at this terminal differentiation stage or removal of these cells from circulation. Similar to monocytes, no sex differences were observed in healthy human T cells (Fig. S7). To assess the kinetics of polySia regulation during T cell activation, we isolated naïve CD4+ T cells and stimulated them in vitro using soluble α-CD2/CD3/CD28 in the presence of IL-2. The gradual increase in surface polySia we observed over the 72-hour period (Fig. 3C and S8A), suggests that its upregulation does not occur through rapid mobilization from pre-existing intracellular stores. Instead, these data are more consistent with a delayed or regulated induction process, although the precise mechanism remains to be determined.

To investigate whether changes in polysialylation could be attributed to increased expression of its biosynthetic machinery, we quantified mRNA expression of ST8SIA4. ST8Sia4 is the key polysialyltransferase in immune cells (Curreli et al. 2007; Drake et al. 2008; Rollenhagen et al. 2013; Stamatos et al. 2014). We used 18S rRNA expression as the housekeeping gene as this has been shown to be robust in rapidly proliferating T cells (Geigges et al. 2020). We observed no significant changes in ST8SIA4 transcript levels over the activation period (Fig. S8B and C), suggesting other forms of regulation such as post-translational regulation of ST8Sia4 or control of polysialylation via membrane trafficking.

Finally, we evaluated the effect of smoking on T cell polysialylation (Fig. 4A). Smoking had minimal effects in CD4+ T cells, with a minor increase in the proportion of polySia+ CD4+ TEM cells observed only in females (Fig. 4B). However, major differences were observed in CD8+ T cells, especially in the TEFF population, where smoking significantly increased both the proportion and intensity of polysialylation (Fig. 4C). Importantly, we also observed marked sex differences in CD8+ TEFF cells (Fig. 4C). While the proportion of polySia+ cells was higher in both sexes, the increase was substantially larger in males. The MFI for polySia was also greater in male smokers, but not in females. No significant sex-based differences were found in the remaining T cell subsets (Fig. S8). Using smoking as a model of chronic inflammation afforded us the opportunity to further explore CD4+ memory T cell heterogeneity across different T helper populations as healthy non-smokers typically have too few TH17 to accurately measure. We observed an intriguing inverse relationship between polySia+ frequency and MFI in TH1 and TH2 cells (Fig. 4D and S9). While TH1 cells were more likely to be polysialylated, TH2 cells had the highest MFI. These differences suggest specific regulation of polySia in different cell types that is likely to be important for biological function.

To determine whether polySia upregulation influences key effector functions such as IFNγ production or proliferative capacity, we next examined polySia status during early T cell activation. CD3+ T cells were stimulated for 6 hours with α-CD3/α-CD28 coated beads and intracellular IFNγ production was assessed (Fig. S10A,B). Strikingly, IFNγ+ cells were almost exclusively found within the polySia+ population, whereas polySia− T cells displayed minimal to virtually no detectable IFNγ, indicating that IFNγ induction is tightly coupled to the acquisition of polysialylation. Pretreatment with heat-inactivated or active EndoN had no effect on IFNγ induction, suggesting that polySia removal does not impair the ability of activated T cells to initiate cytokine production.

We then assessed later effector responses by culturing bead-stimulated T cells for 12 days in the presence of IL-2, comparing IFNγ secretion and proliferative expansion between active EndoN–treated and control cells (Fig. S10C–D). Secreted IFNγ levels measured every 3 days by ELISA, as well as fold expansion over time, were indistinguishable between groups. Thus, enzymatic removal of polySia did not alter IFNγ secretion kinetics or T cell proliferative capacity.

In summary, these findings reveal that polySia is tightly linked to T cell activation and differentiation. Chronic inflammation in smokers amplifies polySia expression in CD8+ TEFF cells in a sex-specific manner. These data suggest that polySia may not only mark T cell maturation but also contribute to immune dysregulation in inflammatory conditions through glycan-mediated mechanisms. However, despite its strong association with activated and IFNγ-producing cells, polySia itself does not appear to be required for IFNγ production or proliferative responses, indicating that polysialylation serves primarily as a biomarker of activation rather than a regulator of these effector functions.

ST8SIA4 mRNA levels do not correlate with polySia levels across PBMCs

Because ST8SIA4 transcript levels did not mirror the increase in surface polySia during activation, we next asked whether this lack of correlation was restricted to activated T cells or extended more broadly across immune subsets. To address the question of correlation of polysialyltransferase gene expression with cell surface polySia, we re-analyzed publicly available single-cell RNA-seq datasets to quantify ST8SIA2 and ST8SIA4 mRNA frequency and expression across PBMC populations and compared these patterns with our flow cytometry–derived surface polySia measurements (Fig. 5). We modeled our approach on the recently published analysis of sialoglycans in T cells (Lindeboom et al. 2024; Izzati et al. 2025), and assigned cell types using previously established markers (Stephenson et al. 2021; Yoshida et al. 2022). Consistent with previous reports (Uhlen et al. 2010), no detectable levels of ST8SIA2 mRNA were found. Across immune populations, we observed clear and systematic discordance between ST8SIA4 mRNA abundance and surface polysialylation. Natural killer (NK) cells displayed the highest surface polySia by both percent positivity and MFI yet exhibited only intermediate ST8SIA4 transcript levels. In contrast, monocytes and dendritic cells showed strong ST8SIA4 mRNA expression, but displayed only modest surface polySia. CD4+ and CD8+ naïve T cells showed measurable ST8SIA4 mRNA but lacked detectable surface polySia. Among T-cell subsets, only the CD8+ TEFF subset showed a partial correspondence between the presence of ST8SIA4 transcripts and detectable polysialylation.

Overall, these cross-platform comparisons demonstrate that ST8SIA4 transcript presence does not predict surface polysialylation across PBMC subsets. This lack of correspondence implicates post-transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms, including ST8Sia4 protein regulation, enzyme trafficking, substrate accessibility, or additional layers of glycan processing, as key determinants of polySia expression at the cell surface.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of polySia expression across major and minor immune cell subsets in healthy young adults and in the context of chronic inflammation induced by cigarette smoking. By integrating high-dimensional immune phenotyping with glycan profiling, we demonstrate that polySia is broadly expressed in both innate and adaptive immune cells and is dynamically regulated in response to cellular activation, differentiation, and chronic environmental stressors.

Smoking is a known driver of systemic inflammation and heavy smoking significantly alters the composition of circulating immune cells (Elisia et al. 2020; Sangani et al. 2023; Saint-André et al. 2024). Previous comparisons of smokers and non-smokers found that smoking correlated with expansion of classical monocytes and a reduction in naïve CD4+ T cells (Sangani et al. 2023; Saint-André et al. 2024), consistent with sustained immune activation and reduced thymic output. However, most studies focused on older adults with long histories of smoking. We chose to focus on young adults to reduce effects of ageing and associated hormone changes. Thus, while we did find a trend toward more differentiated T cells, the smokers in our study had not yet developed expanded monocyte populations. Together, these findings suggest that alterations in polySia may arise before major shifts in immune cell composition become detectable. Our data therefore support a model in which early dysregulation of polySia reflects the initial stages of chronic inflammatory stress induced by smoking, preceding broader immune remodeling. It remains to be determined whether this increase in polysialylation contributes to downstream dysfunction.

The cell types in which polySia is particularly upregulated in smokers, i.e. monocytes and CD8+ effector T cells (TEFF), are drivers of several pathologies, including autoimmune diseases and cancer (Lam et al. 2024). Smoking induces cellular stress through mechanisms such as oxidative damage (Ellegaard and Poulsen 2016), DNA injury (Ishida et al. 2014), and hypoxia (Wang et al. 2023), all of which could enhance the activity or trafficking of polysialyltransferase enzyme, ST8Sia4, and shift the balance toward increased polysialylation. Changes in polysialylation can significantly alter immune cell behavior and thus may have functional consequences that impact human health and disease. While the classical role for polySia has been linked to cell migration and neural development, emerging data support additional immunomodulatory functions, including as a negative regulator of immune activation (Stamatos et al. 2014; Thiesler and Hildebrandt 2024). For example, polySia suppresses phagocytosis in monocytes (Stamatos et al. 2014) and thus increased polysialylation may impair proper clearing of malignant cells. Similarly, upregulation of polySia in CD8+ TEFF cells could contribute to the reduced cytotoxicity and immune surveillance observed in chronic smokers (Kalra et al. 2000; Chen et al. 2023). It is intriguing that the effect of smoking on polySia expression is so cell-type specific and further supports the idea that polySia expression is tightly regulated. Although polySia was strongly associated with activated and IFNγ-producing T cells, our functional assays show that polySia itself is not required for these responses. Removing polySia with active EndoN did not alter early intracellular IFNγ induction, secreted IFNγ levels, or proliferative expansion. Thus, polysialylation appears to mark, rather than drive, T cell activation, even in the context of chronic inflammation.

Importantly, our scRNA-seq analysis revealed that ST8SIA4 transcript levels do not predict surface polysialylation across PBMC subsets, consistent with the HPA and Monaco datasets from the human protein atlas (Uhlen et al. 2010). Indeed, high surface polySia was observed even in cells with low ST8SIA4 mRNA expression. This uncoupling between transcript and surface polySia levels strongly suggests that post-transcriptional mechanisms, such as altered enzyme activity, Golgi trafficking, substrate availability, or differences in turnover of polysialylated proteins, play a dominant role in regulating polySia. The lack of correlation between polySia levels and ST8SIA4 transcript expression in activated CD4+ TN cells underscores the complexity of this regulation.

Sex is an important biological variable that often gets overlooked in immunology and glycobiology fields (Tannenbaum et al. 2016; Hunter et al. 2023b). Our findings highlight the importance of including sex (biology) and gender (society) based analyses. We had previously observed sex differences in serum polySia levels, with males exhibiting higher amounts of total polySia (Tajik et al. 2020). For PBMCs, the impact of sex was cell type-dependent. Any differences in healthy non-smokers were minor. However, the effect of smoking on polySia was strikingly different for a few key cell types. In total monocytes and CD8+ TEFF cells, higher polySia levels were observed in males while in B cells, the opposite was true. The exacerbation of these changes in male smokers could actually reflect gender differences as, among smokers, men tend to smoke more heavily than women (Peters et al. 2014; Chinwong et al. 2018; Reid et al. 2022). In the case of non-classic monocytes, however, smoking had opposing effects in males compared to females and in CD8+ TEFF cells, smoking only affected females. These data suggest chronic inflammation caused by smoking may induce a sex-specific glycan remodeling effect.

In conclusion, our findings highlight polySia as a dynamic and context-dependent glycan that responds to inflammatory cues. PolySia is a sensitive marker of immune activation and differentiation, especially in T cells, that is dysregulated by smoking. These findings open new avenues for understanding how glycan remodeling contributes to immune dysfunction in chronic inflammation.

Methods

PBMCs

Cryopreserved PBMCs were obtained from STEMCELL Technologies (Table SI). Cells were thawed in a 37 °C water bath for approximately 2 min, followed by washing with Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS). After washing, cells were centrifuged at 180 × g for 10 min at room temperature to prepare for downstream applications. Cell count and viability were assessed using a hemocytometer and trypan blue exclusion.

T cell isolation, activation, and functional assays

Naïve CD4+ T cells were purified by negative selection using the EasySep™ Human Naïve CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit II (STEMCELL Technologies) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, washed PBMCs were resuspended at a concentration of 5 × 107 cells/mL in DPBS supplemented with 1 mM EDTA. Cells were incubated with the isolation cocktail (50 μL/mL) for 5 min, followed by the addition of RapidSpheres™ (50 μL/mL). The sample volume was adjusted to 2.5 mL with DPBS containing 1 mM EDTA. The tube was then placed in the EasySep™ Magnet for 3 min, and unlabeled cells were collected in a fresh tube.

Isolated naïve CD4+ T cells were cultured in ImmunoCult™-XF T Cell Expansion Medium (STEMCELL Technologies) supplemented with 25 U/mL IL-2 (Miltenyi Biotec) at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Cells were activated using ImmunoCult™ Human CD3/CD28/CD2 T Cell Activator (STEMCELL Technologies) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 3 days. Samples were collected immediately post-isolation and at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-activation. PolySia expression on the cell surface was analyzed by flow cytometry, and ST8Sia4 mRNA levels were quantified via qPCR.

In parallel, total CD3+ T cells were isolated using the EasySep™ Human T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, 17,951) following the same negative-selection workflow described above. Immediately after isolation, CD3+ T cells were split equally and treated with either active EndoN or heat-inactivated EndoN. For short-term assays, cells were stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher, 11161D) at a 1:1 bead-to-cell ratio for 6 h before intracellular IFN-γ staining. For expansion assays, CD3+ T cells were activated with Dynabeads (1:1) and cultured in ImmunoCult™-XF medium supplemented with 25 U/mL IL-2 for 12 days. Cell counts were performed on days 3, 6, 9, and 12, and fold expansion was calculated relative to the initial cell number at isolation. Culture supernatants were collected at the same time points for quantification of secreted IFN-γ by ELISA.

Flow cytometry

Detection of polySia with EndoNDM

For the flow cytometry analysis, the cells were incubated with Zombie NIR viability dye (BioLegend) for 10 min at 4 °C to label dead cells. Fc receptors were blocked using Human TruStain FcX (BioLegend). To confirm the specificity of the polySia signal, the cells were treated with either active or heat-inactivated polySia hydrolase (EndoN) at 10 μg/mL for 30 min. EndoN was expressed and purified as previously described (Morley et al. 2009) and heat-inactivated at 100 °C for 10 min. Cells were then stained with antibodies targeting cell-surface markers to identify polySia expression on different populations (Table SII). PolySia was detected using polySia lectin (EndoNDM) conjugated to GFP (Tajik et al. 2020), and added to the antibody master mix at 10 μg/mL. Staining was performed for 30 min on ice, after which the cells were washed and resuspended in 300 μL of wash buffer. The samples were analyzed on a Cytek Aurora flow cytometer, and data were processed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1).

The proportion of polySia+ cells in various populations was determined by gating polySia-expressing subpopulations based on EndoN-treated corresponding samples. Cell counts for each population were normalized to the total live cell count in the respective sample. The proportion of polySia+ cells in each population was then calculated by dividing the normalized count of polySia+ cells by the normalized count of the total population. To normalize the median fluorescent intensity (MFI) of polySia+ cells, the GFP MFI value of the polySia+ subpopulation in the heat-inactivated EndoN-treated sample was subtracted from the GFP MFI value of the corresponding population in the active EndoN-treated sample.

Detection of polySia with α-polySia mAb735

Human PBMCs were first treated with either active or heat-inactivated EndoN, and then Fc receptors were blocked using Human TruStain FcX. Cells were incubated with mAb735 (Absolute Antibody) for 30 min on ice, washed, and subsequently incubated with an anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody (Cell Signalling) for 30 min. Following four washes, Fc receptors were blocked again, and the remaining cell-surface marker antibodies in the flow cytometry panel were added and incubated for 30 min on ice. This sequential staining approach was employed to prevent cross-reactivity between the secondary anti-mouse antibody and other mouse-derived antibodies in the panel, allowing specific detection of polySia with mAb735 while preserving the rest of the panel. All other antibodies in the panel were identical to those used in the EndoNDM labeling experiments above, with the exception of the polySia lectin. A secondary-only control was also included to assess background staining.

Intracellular IFN-γ staining

For the short-term activation assay, total CD3+ T cells were stimulated with Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher) at 1:1 ratio for 6 h as described above. Brefeldin A (Thermo Fisher) was added during the final 4 h of stimulation to block cytokine secretion.

Following stimulation, cells were washed and stained for surface markers CD4, CD8, and EndoNDM conjugated to GFP for 30 min on ice. Cells were then fixed in 2% formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher) for 30 min on ice, washed, and permeabilized using Intracellular Staining Permeabilization Wash Buffer (BioLegend). Intracellular IFN-γ staining was performed for 30 min on ice. Samples were acquired by flow cytometry and analyzed as described above.

ST8SIA4 qPCR

RNA was extracted from activated T cells (described above) using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen). Subsequently, real-time PCR was carried out using Taqman Fast Virus 1 step Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), and ST8Sia4 (Hs00379924_m1) and 18 s rRNA (Hs03928985_g1) gene expression kits (ThermoFisher Scientific), according to manufacturer instructions. ST8Sia4 gene expression was normalized to 18S rRNA. PCRs were conducted in triplicate, and the expression was calculated as ddCT.

ELISA for monitoring secreted IFN-γ

Anti–human IFN-γ capture antibody (Abcam) was allowed to adhere to 96-well plates (ThermoFisher) in 100 μL at 1–2 μg/mL diluted in PBS at 4 °C overnight in a humidified container. All subsequent incubations were performed at room temperature for 1–2 h, and all washing steps were performed three times with 1× PBS, 0.05% Tween-20 (PBS-T). Plates were treated with the following solutions, with washes between each: 300 μL 5% BSA in PBS-T to block, 100 μL diluted culture supernatants, 100 μL mouse anti–IFN-γ detection antibody, and 100 μL HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Goat anti-Mouse IgG-HRP, R&D). After a final three washes, 100 μL TMB substrate (Abcam) was added to each well, and absorbance was measured at 652 nm every 2 min for 30 min. Supernatants from six individual donors were analyzed, each measured in technical duplicate.

Statistics

Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical significance was denoted as follows: P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***), and P < 0.0001 (****).

scRNAseq analysis

Data processing

The raw and normalized counts from the h5ad files from the Izzati et al., 2025 were extracted with the anndata package v0.8.0 (Virshup et al. 2023; Izzati et al. 2025). Then, with CreateAssayObject from the Seurat package v4.4.0, a workable Seurat object was created (Hao et al. 2021).

To find clusters from the raw counts, an in-house R package was used. This package wraps and optimizes all the key steps from a Seurat workflow. First, a filtration was performed by cutting off gene features and counts below 100 with no top threshold to ensure data integrity. Then, Seurat::Normalization was performed with the base parameters. High Variable Genes (HVGs) were found with 2000 features to stabilize the variance in gene expression with the Variance Stabilizing Genes argument. Data were scale with Seurat::ScaleData with the base parameters.

7 Principal Component Analysis (PCA) dimensions were used with Seurat::RunPCA to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset. Then, Seurat::FindClusters was performed with a range of 14 resolutions from 0.1 to 2 to find clusters.

With a set of key marker genes (Table SIII), each cell cluster was assigned to a cell type. Each gene was mapped using a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAPs) calculated by Seurat::RunUMAP with the base parameters.

Assigning T lymphocytes and natural killers

To find subtypes from the T lymphocytes and natural killers group, the dataset was subsampled in two subsets: one with T Lymphocytes CD8+ effector, regulatory T cells, and natural killers, and another one with T Lymphocytes naive, T Lymphocytes CD4+ effector, and regulatory T cells.

For each of these subsets, the same in-house package as described before was performed again, without the normalization, to find subclusters to assign to their proper cell subtype. For the first subset, 15 PCA dimensions were used, and clusters were assigned into either T Lymphocytes CD8+ effector, regulatory T cells, natural killers, or doublets. For the second subset, 16 PCA dimensions were used and subclusters were placed into the following category: T Lymphocytes CD4+ naive, T Lymphocytes CD8+ naive, T Lymphocytes CD4+ effector, regulatory T cells, or doublets.

For each cell type, bubble plots were graphed with homemade functions using ggplot2 (Wickham 2011). Relative expression for each cell type per marker was calculated using the proportion of log1p expression of that cell type compared to all of the others.

PolySia expression

Four genes were present in the Izzati et al., 2025 dataset: ST8SIA1, ST8SIA4, ST8SIA5, and ST8SIA6. For each gene, the average log1p expression was statistically compared with a non-parameter Kruskal-Wallis between all cell types. A post-hoc wilcoxon paired test was performed on individual groups to find comparisons between every cell types.

The log1p expression of each gene was also mapped using a bubble plot with the same parameters described above. Furthermore, violin plots showed the distribution of the expression of each cell expressing this gene above a base threshold of log1p(0.1) for each cell type. UMAPs show the expression of every cell on the entire dataset.
