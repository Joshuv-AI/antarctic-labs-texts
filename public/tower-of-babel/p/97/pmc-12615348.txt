Introduction

Hydatidosis, resulting from the larval stage of Echinococcus granulosus, is a parasitic infection prevalent in endemic regions and represents a major public health concern in areas such as North Africa, the Middle East, and South America. In Tunisia, 1200–1500 new cases are surgically managed each year, highlighting the continued burden of this disease. The liver, mostly, and lungs account for the majority of cases, while unusual localizations represent a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge [1].

Musculoskeletal manifestation is uncommon, representing only 2–3 % of hydatid cases. Isolated intramuscular cysts with no associated visceral involvement are even more exceptional [2]. Such atypical sites can mimic benign or malignant soft tissue tumours, causing delays in diagnosis. We present the case of a hydatid cyst localized in the gluteal muscle and a review the literature on this rare presentation. This case has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [3].

Case presentation

A 47-year-old Tunisian woman from a rural area, with no medical or surgical history, presented with a progressively enlarging mass in her left gluteal region that had developed over several months. A mild local discomfort was the only symptom. There was no fever, weight loss, or systemic signs.

The examination revealed a 6 cm mass in the supero-lateral quadrant of the left buttock. The mass was firm, mobile, non-tender, and non-inflammatory, with preserved overlying skin. No other abnormalities were noted.

A lipoma was suspected initially, but the ultrasound revealed a cystic lesion. A pelvic CT scan was performed with a slice thickness of 1.5 mm using contrast-enhanced arterial and portal venous phases. It showed a well-defined, oval cyst measuring 4.5 cm × 5.7 cm with regular contours and a non-enhancing dense content, without calcifications. The imaging was consistent with a hydatid cyst (Fig. 1). To rule out additional visceral involvement, the patient underwent thoracic and abdominal CT scans, showing no hepatic, pulmonary, or other visceral lesions. Serology testing for hydatid disease was performed using ELISA at a private laboratory, as the test was unavailable at our hospital. The result was positive at 1/640.

Contrast-enhanced CT scan (axial view) showing a well-defined, oval cyst measuring 4.5 × 5.7 cm with regular contours and a non-enhancing dense content, without calcifications, consistent with a hydatid cyst.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia and lasted 90 min, with an estimated blood loss of minimal volume < 100 ml. The patient underwent an elective surgical removal of the lesion. The incision was made directly above the cyst site. Dissection was carried through the skin, gluteal fascia, gluteus maximus fibers, and deep muscular planes until the lesion was reached. The operative field was protected with gauze moistened with hydrogen peroxide. As there was no contact with intestines, colon, or rectum, hydrogen peroxide use was considered safe. The cyst was carefully dissected. A small puncture with continuous strong aspiration was made to decompress the cyst in order to avoid spillage, producing the clear characteristic “rock-water” fluid found in hydatid cysts (Fig. 2). The proligerous membrane was extracted, and the cavity was rinsed thoroughly with 250 ml of 9 % hypertonic saline. Finally, a total pericystectomy was performed and the cyst was removed en bloc (Fig. 3). An active suction drain was left in the cavity. Hemostasis was achieved by thermal cauterization, and the wound was closed layer by layer. The patient did not receive pre- or postoperative Albendazole since there was no intraoperative spillage. It is not in the habit of our department to give anthelmintic treatment for prophylactic purpose. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of a hydatid cyst. Microscopic analysis revealed the outer acellular laminated membrane, along with areas containing protoscolices and hooklets. A mild granulomatous inflammatory reaction was noted. Unfortunately, microscopic images could not be included due to the unavailability of the slides. Immediate postoperative recovery was uneventful. The patient was discharged on day 2 after removing the drain. Postoperative clinical follow-up was conducted at 2 weeks, 1 month, and 3 months, revealing normal recovery without complications. At 6 months, clinical exam was normal. Pelvic CT scan was performed, showing no signs of recurrence. Planned follow-up includes hydatid serology at 9 months and an additional pelvic CT scan at 12 months to ensure continued surveillance with the absence of recurrence.

Discussion

Hydatid disease most frequently affects the liver in 70 % of cases, and the lungs in 20–30 %, due to portal and pulmonary filtration of the parasite [4]. Musculoskeletal involvement is uncommon, estimated at 2–3 % of cases, most often affecting the thigh, shoulder, or paravertebral muscles [2]. Primary hydatid cysts of the gluteal muscle are rare, reported in very few cases in the literature, underscoring the unusual nature of this localization. The pathogenesis in soft tissues remains incompletely understood. Haematogenous dissemination, lymphatic spread, or direct implantation through microtrauma have been suggested as possible mechanisms, though these pathways are not definitively established [5].

Intramuscular hydatid cysts usually present as slow-growing, painless masses. Their indolent course and lack of systemic symptoms often result in diagnostic delays, and they are frequently mistaken for benign soft tissue tumours such as lipomas, fibromas, or simple cysts. Lesion size can vary significantly, and while most remain asymptomatic, larger cysts may compress surrounding structures, occasionally leading to mild discomfort, restricted movement, or functional impairment. Due to their rarity, primary soft tissue hydatidosis may not be initially suspected, particularly in non-endemic regions, increasing the likelihood of misdiagnosis or unnecessary invasive procedures.

Imaging plays a pivotal role in diagnosis. Ultrasound is typically the first-line modality, allowing recognition of cystic morphology, septations, and characteristic internal contents. CT and MRI provide more precise anatomical delineation, assess cyst wall features, and determine the relationship with adjacent structures. These modalities are also essential for ruling out other visceral or multi-organ involvement, which is critical for surgical planning [6]. Serological tests, including ELISA or indirect hemagglutination, can support the diagnosis; however, false negative results are common, particularly in isolated soft tissue cases. In our patient, the combination of epidemiological context, clinical findings, imaging features and positive serology confirmed the diagnosis.

Percutaneous approaches, such as PAIR (puncture, aspiration, injection, re-aspiration), are rarely used for muscular hydatid cysts considering the high risk of incomplete evacuation, difficulty in controlling spillage, and potential injury to adjacent structures [7]. Surgical excision remains the definitive treatment. The primary objective is complete cyst removal without rupture, which is crucial to prevent dissemination and recurrence. Intraoperative precautions include protecting the operative field with barriers, aspirating cyst fluid carefully, and using scolicidal agents such as hypertonic saline [8]. Treatment of hydatid cyst with Albendazole is associated with limiting recurrence and reduction of the size of the hydatid cysts. However, for operable and fully resected cysts, its efficacy has not been well documented [9]. A meticulous surgical approach, combined with careful postoperative follow-up, ensures optimal outcomes and minimizes the risk of recurrence [9].

Primary gluteal hydatid cysts are rare and can be mistaken for more common soft tissue masses. Lipoma is the main differential diagnosis. Typically presents as a soft, mobile, painless mass. Ultrasound or CT shows homogeneous, hypoechoic fat-containing lesions. Unlike hydatid cysts, lipomas lack internal septations or fluid content. Epidermoid cyst is a frequent differential diagnosis. Is presents as a superficial, slow-growing painless mass. Imaging shows well-circumscribed cystic lesions without daughter cysts. Aspiration or biopsy can reveal keratinous material, which differs from hydatid cyst fluid. Also soft tissue sarcoma presents as a firm rapidly enlarging mass. MRI may show heterogeneous signal intensity. Biopsy is usually required for diagnosis. However, biopsy is avoided in suspected hydatid cysts due to the risk of rupture and dissemination. If hydatid cyst is suspected after imagine, biopsy is prohibited [5].

There are other differential diagnoses: abscesses, cystic lymph nodes, hematomas, or benign vascular malformations. Imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI) is necessary for characterization.

Various scolicidal agents are used to prevent dissemination of hydatid cyst contents intraoperatively, depending of the habit of each department. Hypertonic saline (9 %) is effective and widely used. It does not present tissue toxicity, but requires precise concentration. Hydrogen peroxide is also effective, especially for for field protection, when no visceral organs are in contact. It has rapid action and is widely available [8]. However, its use remains controversial across surgical teams, given the potential for local tissue irritation and oxidative damage. The last agent is povidone-iodine. Very effective and the least toxic to surrounding tissue, but may be less available [8]. In our center, we used gauze moistened with hydrogen peroxide for field protection, followed by irrigation with 9 % hypertonic saline. This approach is safe, effective and minimizes dissemination.

This case emphasizes the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges posed by hydatid cysts in atypical locations. Clinicians, particularly in endemic regions, should consider hydatid disease in the differential diagnosis of any slow-growing soft tissue mass. Prompt recognition, thorough imaging assessment, and careful surgical management are essential to achieve favourable outcomes and prevent complications. Expanding awareness of these rare presentations contributes to earlier diagnosis, reduces mismanagement, and informs future clinical practice in endemic areas.

Conclusion

Hydatid cysts occurring primarily in the gluteal muscle are extremely rare. Clinical presentation is often nonspecific, and imaging plays a crucial role in accurate diagnosis. Surgical excision, with the use of scolicidal precautions, remains the cornerstone of treatment. Physicians practicing in endemic regions should consider this unusual presentation to ensure timely recognition and provide appropriate management.
