SETDB1-mediated repression of RhoB promotes EMT and metastatic progression in prostate cancer
Authors: Han Cong, Fatemeh Seilani, Ryan Goettl, Daheng He, Jinpeng Liu, Chi Wang, Xiaoqi Liu, Ruixin Wang, Ka-wing Fong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) remains a clinical challenge, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) contributes to metastatic progression and reduced response to therapy. However, the upstream epigenetic mechanisms that sustain EMT programs in advanced prostate cancer (PCa) are not fully defined. We identify the histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9) methyltransferase SET domain bifurcated 1 (SETDB1) as a key regulator of EMT and metastasis through direct repression of RhoB, the small GTPase. SETDB1 is genomically amplified and transcriptionally upregulated in metastatic CRPC, and SETDB1 depletion reduces cell migration, invasion, and metastatic dissemination. Integrated chromatin profiling and transcriptomic analyses demonstrate that SETDB1 occupies the RhoB promoter and mediates its transcriptional silencing through H3K9 methylation. Restoration of RhoB reverses EMT gene expression and suppresses invasive behavior, whereas RhoB knockdown rescues the effects of SETDB1 depletion, establishing RhoB as a critical downstream effector of SETDB1 function. Androgen signaling inhibitor-resistant PCa models exhibit RhoB loss and EMT activation, linking this axis to therapy-resistant phenotypes. Finally, antisense oligonucleotide-mediated SETDB1 silencing restores RhoB expression and suppresses EMT and invasion in CRPC cell models. Together, these findings define a SETDB1-RhoB epigenetic pathway that promotes EMT and metastatic progression in PCa and may be therapeutically targeted in advanced disease.Subject terms: Prostate cancer, Gene silencing

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) remains one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies and a leading cause of cancer-related death in men. Androgen receptor (AR) signaling is essential for prostate epithelial differentiation but becomes co-opted in cancer to drive growth and survival. Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) is initially effective, but most tumors eventually progress to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), defined by continued tumor growth despite castrate androgen levels. Second-generation androgen signaling inhibitors (ASIs), including abiraterone and enzalutamide, provide additional clinical benefit in CRPC [1, 2], yet tumors frequently acquire resistance to ASIs, which is associated with more invasive phenotypes and poor outcomes. Thus, understanding the molecular pathways that drive metastatic behavior in CRPC, particularly those linked to therapy resistance, is critical for improving patient outcomes.

Epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a transcriptional reprogramming process that enables epithelial cells to acquire invasive mesenchymal phenotypes [3]. Aberrant EMT contributes to metastatic progression and therapeutic resistance in cancer [4–7]. ASI-resistant prostate tumors and model systems consistently exhibit elevated EMT gene signatures and increased cell motility [8–10], yet the upstream regulatory mechanisms linking therapy resistance to EMT remain incompletely understood.

Epigenetic reprogramming plays a critical role in establishing EMT states. The histone H3K9 methyltransferase SET domain bifurcated 1 (SETDB1) is overexpressed in prostate tumors and promotes oncogenic phenotypes [11, 12]. In addition to its catalytic function, SETDB1 acts as a scaffold for chromatin-associated repressors such as TRIM28/KAP1, HP1, and DNMTs, enabling coordinated transcriptional silencing across gene networks [13–15]. However, the downstream effectors through which SETDB1 drives EMT and metastatic progression in PCa are poorly defined.

The small GTPase, RhoB, distinct from the more extensively studied RhoA and RhoC, has emerged as a context-dependent tumor suppressor that maintains epithelial architecture and restrains cell motility [16–18]. RhoB expression is frequently reduced in aggressive cancers [16–18], but its role in PCa progression and EMT regulation has remained unclear.

Here, we identify a SETDB1-RhoB epigenetic axis that promotes EMT and metastatic progression in PCa. We show that SETDB1 directly represses RhoB through promoter-associated H3K9 methylation. Loss of SETDB1 restores RhoB expression, reverses EMT transcriptional programs, and suppresses migration, invasion, and metastasis, whereas RhoB depletion rescues these phenotypes. This establishes RhoB as a critical downstream effector of SETDB1. We further demonstrate that ASI-resistant PCa cell models exhibit RhoB loss and EMT activation, linking this axis to therapy-resistant disease. Finally, antisense oligonucleotide–mediated SETDB1 silencing suppresses EMT and invasive behavior in CRPC cell models, demonstrating that SETDB1 is a plausible therapeutic target. Collectively, these findings define a SETDB1-RhoB epigenetic mechanism that drives metastatic progression and highlight SETDB1-directed gene silencing strategies as a promising therapeutic approach for advanced and treatment-resistant prostate cancer.

Materials and methods

Cell lines, antibodies, and chemical reagents

Human embryonic kidney cell line 293 T and prostate cancer cell lines LNCaP, C4-2B, 22Rv-1, and DU145 were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). MR49F and C4-2R were from Xiaoqi Liu’s laboratory. The 293 T cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1x penicillin streptomycin. The prostate cancer cells were cultured in RPMI1640 with 10% FBS and 1x penicillin streptomycin solution. All cell lines were authenticated by the Short Tandem Repeat method (Genetica DNA Laboratories, Cincinnati, OH, USA). The mycoplasma test was performed using Mycoalert detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The number of passages of cells between thawing and use in the described experiments was less than 20. Matrigel Basement Membrane matrix (356237) was from Corning (Corning, NY, USA), D-Luciferin (LUCNA-1G) was from Gold Biotechnology (Olivette, MO, USA), polyethylenimine (PEI) reagent (23966) was from Polyscience (Niles, IL, USA), and Crystal violet (C3886), polybrene (H9268), Puromycin (P8833), and G418 (A1720) were from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The antibodies used in this study are fully described in Supplementary Table S1.

DNA constructs, transfection, and lentiviral infection

SETDB1 cDNA (82291) from Addgene and RhoB cDNA amplified from HEK293T cells were subcloned into pCR8-topo vector via In-Fusion Cloning (Takara, San Jose, CA, USA) and further subcloned into Gateway compatible Lenti-HA-Flag and Lenti-SFB vector via LR Clonase (Sigma-Aldrich). PSPAX (12260), PMD2.G (12259), and lentiCRISPRv2 puro (98290) were obtained from Addgene (Watertown, MA, USA). Negative Control siRNA (4390843) was obtained from Life Technologies (Thermo Fisher Scientific). pLKO.1 non-targeting shRNA (SHC016), siRNA targeting SETDB1 (SASI_Hs01_0015-0485, SASI_Hs02_0034-4323, SASI_Hs02_0034-4324), shSETDB1 (TRCN0000148112), and shRhoB (TRCN0000047848) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. SETDB1-ASO was synthesized from IDT (Coralville, IA, USA). To construct the pGL3-RhoB promoter, a DNA fragment (1045 nt) within the promoter region of RhoB was amplified by PCR using cDNA from 293 T cells, followed by subcloning into pGL3 vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) using In-Fusion Cloning.

To transiently overexpress genes, PCa cells were transfected with plasmid DNA mixed with X-tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA). Transfection of SETDB1-ASO was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). To generate lentiviral supernatant, lentiviral overexpression or shRNA constructs were transfected along with psPAX and PMD2.G by PEI into 293 T cells for 48 h before the lentiviral supernatant was collected and filtered with a 0.45 μM nitrocellulose syringe filter. Lentivirus transduction in prostate cancer cell lines was conducted under 2 μg/ml polybrene treatment. PCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

RNA isolation, quantitative RT-PCR, RNA-seq, and CUT&RUN-seq

Total RNA was isolated from cells with NucleoSpin RNA isolation kit (Takara, Katsu, Japan). ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) was used for RNA reverse transcription. qRT-qPCR was performed using 2xBullseye EvaGreen qPCR MasterMix (MIDSCI, St. Louis, MO) on a Quantum 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA), and relative expression of mRNA was determined using GAPDH as the loading control. qRT-qPCR data were obtained in triplicate experiments. PCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table S2. For RNA-seq, total RNA was isolated from LNCaP cells treated with siControl and siSETDB1 in triplicate. RNA-seq libraries were prepared from 500 ng DNA-free RNA using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The libraries passing quality control (equal size distribution between 250 and 400 bp, no adapter contamination peaks, and no degradation peaks) were quantified using the Library Quantification Kit from Illumina (KK4603, Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA). Libraries were sequenced paired-end using the Illumina Nova-seq. Sequencing reads were trimmed and filtered using Trimmomatic (v0.39) to remove adapters and low-quality bases. Trimmed reads were mapped to the human reference genome assembly GRCh37 (hg19) transcript annotation using RSEM. RSEM results for normalization and differential expression analysis were performed using the R package of EdgeR. Significantly up/downregulated genes between groups were determined as fold change ≥1 and P value < 0.05. Volcano plot was used to depict differentially expressed genes. The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software and the gene sets in the Molecular Signature Database.

SETDB1 and H3K9me3 CUT&RUN-seq were performed as described previously [19]. DNA libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit according to manufacturer instructions. Paired-end sequencing reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) to remove adapters and low-quality bases. Trimmed reads were mapped to the human reference genome assembly GRCh37 (hg19) using Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1), allowing fragments up to 2 kb to be aligned. The alignment result was further filtered by SAMtools to remove reads unmapped, non-primary alignment reads, reads failing platform, reads mapped to mitochondria, reads mapped to ENCODE blacklisted genomic regions, and multi-mapped reads. PCR duplicates were removed using Picard (v2.26.8). Peaks were called by SEACR v1.3. The coverage track BigWig files were generated with bin size 10 and were normalized by reads per genome coverage using DeepTools (v3.5.1). BigWig files were uploaded into the UCSC Genome Browser for visualization.

Functional assay (colony formation, wound-healing, Transwell migration and Matrigel invasion assay)

For the colony formation assay, 2000–10,000 cells were seeded into a 6-well plate. After 10–14 days of incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by staining with 0.025% crystal violet for 2 h. Transwell migration and Matrigel invasion assays were performed using Transwell inserts (Corning) with an 8-µm pore size. Cells (5 × 10⁴) were seeded into the upper chamber containing 1% FBS supplemented medium. For invasion assays, inserts were coated with Matrigel (1 mg/mL), whereas migration assays were performed without coating. After 48 h, cells that had migrated or invaded to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and imaged. To quantitate migrated/invaded cells, 10% acetic acid was used to dissolve crystal violet stain, and absorbance was measured at OD 560 nm. For the wound healing assay, cells were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured in complete medium over 90% confluence. The middle areas of the wells were scratched with 200 µl tips. Images were taken every 24 h. To avoid the effect from cell proliferation, 1% FBS in the medium was replaced the day before the experiment.

Luciferase reporter assay

RhoB promoter luciferase reporter assays were conducted according to the manual of Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System from Promega. Briefly, LNCaP cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and transfected with siControl and siSETDB1 along with pRL-TK and pGL3-RhoB to assess the effect of SETDB1-silencing on luciferase activity. Luciferase activity was determined by Promega GloMax Plate Reader and normalized against Renilla internal control values.

Animal model

All procedures involving mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Kentucky and complied with all relevant ethical regulations. NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Male mice aged 10–12 weeks were used.

Orthotopic injection and in vivo metastasis assay

Luciferase-labeled DU145 cells expressing pLKO or shSETDB1 were orthotopically implanted via intraprostatic injection to assess metastatic progression in vivo. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and investigators were blinded to group allocation during outcome assessment. Six to seven weeks after inoculation, D-luciferin (150 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally for 10 min. Whole body IVIS images were taken for 60 s exposure using an SII Lago IVIS Imager (Spectral Instruments Imaging, Tucson, AZ, USA). After that, different organs were dissected from euthanized mice, followed by ex vivo IVIS imaging. Region of interest bioluminescence was used to quantify the signals of different metastatic sites by Aura software (Spatial Instrument Imaging).

Immunofluorescence

Cells were grown on poly-D-lysine–coated coverslips and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min at room temperature. After 3 washes with PBS, cells were incubated with a blocking buffer (3% BSA in PBS) for 30 min. Then, cells were incubated with indicated antibodies diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C in a humidity chamber. This was followed by 3 washes and incubation with FITC and TRITC conjugated secondary antibodies (715-095-151 and 711-025-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) at 1:500 dilution for 1 h. DAPI was used to label nuclear DNA. After 3 washes with PBS, coverslips were mounted on glass slides in Prolong Gold antifade reagent (Invitrogen). The cells were imaged by Nikon A1 + -Ti2 confocal system, and images were edited using Adobe PhotoShop (v25.7.0).

Human PCa patient samples and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

TMAs containing human CRPC specimens were obtained from the University of Washington Medical Center Prostate Cancer Donor Program, which is approved by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board. In this study, UWTMA100 Array D-F were investigated, consisting of 1 mm–diameter core triplicates of visceral metastases and bone metastases (number of sites = 81), were collected from 29 patients. In addition, TMAs containing 30 cases of prostate adenocarcinoma were obtained through the Cooperative Human Tissue Network. Slides were stained on the Ventana Discovery Ultra with on-board antigen retrieval for 64 min with Ventana CC2 buffer. Primary antibody was applied for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by incubation with anti-rabbit Ventana OmniMap-HRP, as appropriate, for 20 min and visualized with Ventana ChromoMap DAB. SETDB1 (1:500) and RhoB (1:200) were used. The intensity was scored as negative, weak, moderate, or intense.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise noted, statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism. The details of statistical analysis are described in figure legends. For animal studies, sample size was determined based on power analysis for a two-tailed comparison using Cohen’s d to estimate effect size. Based on preliminary data, the expected effect size was d = 1.5. Under this assumption, a sample size of 8 mice per group was sufficient to achieve 80% statistical power.

Results

SETDB1 gene expression is upregulated in metastatic CRPC

To comprehensively investigate the function of SETDB1 in advanced PCa, we examined whether there is any genomic alteration of SETDB1 in PCa. From the c-BioPortal database, we found that SETDB1 genomic amplification is elevated from 1.8% in primary PCa to 15% in metastatic CRPC (Fig. 1A, Fig. S1A). In addition, SETDB1 mRNA exhibits a significant increase from primary PCa to metastatic CRPC in three independent patient datasets (Fig. 1B–D). Cancer metastasis is the major cause of morbidity for PCa patients [20]. Indeed, high SETDB1 gene expression correlates with worse patient overall survival and disease-free survival (Fig. S1B–D). This analysis indicates that SETDB1 may be involved in advanced PCa metastasis. To address the role of SETDB1 in PCa metastasis, we first knocked down SETDB1 by shRNA in DU145 cells (SETDB1-high) (Fig. S1E) and subjected the cells to a matrigel invasion assay. Our data showed that SETDB1 level, SETDB1-catalyzed H3K9me3 (Fig. 1E), and cell invasiveness (Fig. 1F) are dramatically reduced by knocking down SETDB1. In addition, we employed an orthotopic injection model by inoculating DU145-luc cells expressing shControl (shC) and shSETDB1 into the anterior prostate of NSG mice (Fig. 1G). Six to seven weeks after inoculation, different organs were dissected for ex vivo IVIS imaging. Also, we performed immunoblotting (IB) on protein lysates from dissected prostate tumors, and the data confirmed that tumor cells maintain SETDB1 knockdown at the endpoint (Fig. S1F). Quantification of IVIS imaging revealed that prostate tumors expressing shSETDB1, compared to control, metastasize more slowly to spleen and Hing leg (Fig. 1H). In addition, there is no significant difference in IVIS signal for dissected prostate tumors expressing shC and shSETDB1 (Fig. 1H), which rules out the effect of SETDB1 knockdown on affecting proliferation. To test whether SETDB1 overexpression drives PCa cell metastasis, we overexpressed doxycycline (DOX)-inducible SETDB1-SFB (S: S-tag; F: Flag-tag; B: Biotin-tag) driven by Tet-On system in C4-2B cells (SETDB1-low) (Fig. S1E). Our results demonstrated that DOX addition not only induces the expression of SETDB1 (Fig. 1I), but also promotes C4-2B cells’ invasiveness (Fig. 1J) and wound healing (Fig. 1K). To exclude potential effects of doxycycline (DOX) on cell invasion and migration, we performed Matrigel invasion and wound healing assays in C4-2B cells with or without DOX treatment. The results showed that DOX treatment did not affect cell invasion or migration (Fig. S1G–I). Altogether, these results strongly support the functional relevance of SETDB1 in advanced PCa progression.

RhoB is a downstream and key target of SETDB1

SETDB1 is a histone lysine methyltransferase that epigenetically silences gene expression via H3K9me3. We hypothesized that SETDB1 promotes PCa metastasis via regulating genes involved in cell motility and migration. To define the gene targets and molecular effectors of SETDB1 in PCa, we employed an integrated genomic approach. We interrogated SETDB1, H3K9me3 CUT&RUN-seq, and RNA-seq from two independent siRNAs against SETDB1 versus control. Overall, RhoB is one of the 37 genes that are significantly upregulated by siSETDB1 (Fig. 2A), which are also occupied by SETDB1 and H3K9me3 modification (Fig. 2B). CUT&RUN-qPCR further confirmed decreased SETDB1 occupancy and H3K9me3 enrichment at the RHOB promoter in shSETDB1 cells compared to Control cells (Fig. S2A), supporting direct epigenetic repression of RHOB by SETDB1. In addition, three genes (RhoB, TRIB1, and ARHGAP4) out of the 37 are annotated to GO:0030336: negative regulation of cell migration (Fig. 2C). We examined their correlation with SETDB1 in TCGA dataset, and found that RhoB is the only gene which is negatively correlated (r = –0.31) to SETDB1, indicating it is a major epigenetic target repressed by SETDB1 in prostate tumor. To ensure scientific rigor, we transfected LNCaP (Fig. 2D), C4–2B (Fig. 2E), and DU145 (Fig. 2F) cells by 3 independent siRNA targeting SETDB1, and our data confirmed that RhoB mRNA level is significantly upregulated in siSETDB1-treated cells, compared to control siRNA treatment.

Consistent with mRNA level, RhoB protein level was also dramatically upregulated in these cell lines (Fig. 2G–I). Conversely, we engineered C4-2B cells overexpressing SETDB1-SFB. DOX-inducible SETDB1 overexpression downregulated both mRNA (Fig. 2J) and protein (Fig. 2K) levels of RhoB in C4-2B cells. To rule out potential effects of DOX on RhoB expression, we performed qPCR and IB in C4-2B cells with or without DOX treatment. The results showed that DOX had only a minimal effect on RhoB mRNA and protein levels (Fig. S2B, C). To further confirm whether SETDB1 represses RhoB’s promoter activity, we employed a luciferase reporter system in which the promoter region of RhoB was subcloned in a luciferase construct. Transcription of Luc was significantly enhanced in cells treated by siSETDB1, resulting in stronger luciferase activity (Fig. S2D, E). Additionally, we observed a trend of decreased RhoB expression in prostate tumor with SETDB1 amplification in the SU2C dataset, albeit not statistically significant (Fig. 2L). Nonetheless, there is a significantly negative correlation between SETDB1 and RhoB in patient datasets (Fig. 2M, N). Collectively, we revealed RhoB as a top downstream target of SETDB1 and found that it may act as a molecular effector of SETDB1 in PCa metastasis.

RhoB suppresses PCa metastasis

RhoB is the least studied among Rho family members, especially in the context of tumorigenesis and cancer progression. Several reports support the role of RhoB in the negative regulation of cancer cell motility [18], but the role of RhoB in PCa progression is yet to be fully understood. By reanalyzing three independent patient datasets, we found that RhoB mRNA expression is significantly downregulated from primary PCa to metastatic CRPC (Fig. 3A–C). To examine whether RhoB acts as a molecular effector of SETDB1 in PCa metastasis, we first overexpressed RhoB in C4-2B and DU145 cells (Fig. 3D, E) and then conducted a wound healing assay, a Matrigel invasion assay and a transwell migration assay. Our results indicated that RhoB expression remarkably suppresses PCa cell migration (Fig. 3F, G, Fig. S3A, B) and cell invasion (Fig. 3H, I). To rule out the possibility that RhoB affects cell migration and invasion due to inhibition of cell growth, we plated GFP and RhoB-overexpressing cells for colony formation assay, and our data suggested that RhoB overexpression does not significantly affect clonogenic cell growth (Fig. 3J, K). This result is consistent with published data in PC3 cells [21]. In summary, this result highlights the role of RhoB in suppressing PCa metastasis and suggests the possibility that SETDB1 may promote PCa metastasis by downregulating RhoB expression.

RhoB suppresses the EMT pathway in PCa

It has been shown that RhoB affects the expression of E- and N-Cadherin and thereby alters PCa cell–cell adhesion and migration [22]. However, the previous study lacked in-depth characterization of the EMT pathway upon RhoB overexpression and knockdown. To address this gap, we analyzed the gene expression profile of 12 PCa cell lines using the DepMap portal (Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA). Our analysis revealed that RhoB gene expression is significantly and negatively correlated with Hallmark_EMT and GO_EMT signature scores (Fig. S4A, B). For instance, RhoB gene level is positively associated with epithelial marker TJP1 (Fig. S4C), while it is negatively associated with mesenchymal marker VIM (Fig. S4D). To determine whether RhoB regulates the EMT program, we examined the expression of EMT markers following modulation of RhoB levels. Knockdown of RhoB in C4-2B and 22Rv1 cells decreased the epithelial marker E-Cad and increased mesenchymal markers N-Cad and VIM, respectively (Fig. 4A, B, Fig. S4E). Conversely, overexpression of RhoB in C4-2B, 22Rv1, and DU145 cells increased E-Cad expression while reducing N-Cad and VIM levels (Fig. 4C, D, Fig. S4F, G). To further validate this observation, we performed an immunofluorescence assay followed by confocal imaging. C4-2B cells expressing shRhoB displayed significantly less E-Cad staining along the cell–cell junction (Fig. 4E) relative to shControl, while 22Rv1 cells overexpressing RhoB in HA fusion barely expressed N-Cad (Fig. 4F, indicated by arrows), indicating loss of the migratory mesenchymal phenotype. Furthermore, we performed RNA-seq in DU145 cells with GFP and RhoB overexpression. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that both Hallmark EMT and BYERS [23] gene sets are significantly suppressed upon RhoB overexpression, compared to GFP control (Fig. S4H–I).

To address whether SETDB1 affects EMT via regulating RhoB expression, we knocked down SETDB1 in 22Rv1 and DU145 cells (RhoB-low). Our IB result indicated that SETDB1 knockdown not only leads to upregulation of RhoB, but also results in upregulation of epithelial markers or downregulation of mesenchymal markers (Fig. 4G, H), indicating the reversal of EMT. To demonstrate that RhoB is the key SETDB1-repressed tumor suppressor during PCa metastasis, we performed rescue experiments in which RhoB was silenced in SETDB1-knockdown DU145 cells. Our data indicated that SETDB1 depletion leads to downregulation of mesenchymal marker and upregulation of epithelial marker, which can be remarkably rescued by RhoB co-knockdown (Fig. 4I). In addition, SETDB1 depletion suppresses PCa wound healing (Fig. 4J) and cell invasion (Fig. 4K), whereas RhoB co-knockdown significantly restores these phenotypes (Fig. 4J, K). Taken together, our data revealed that SETDB1 promotes PCa metastasis via the RhoB-loss induced EMT-pathway.

ASI-resistant PCa tumors enriched with EMT gene signature are characterized by RhoB loss

The EMT pathway is deregulated in prostate tumors, which contributes to cancer metastasis and therapeutic resistance [4–7]. To examine whether the EMT pathway is activated in enzalutamide (Enz)-resistant PCa, we reanalyzed RNA-seq data from tumor of PCa patients receiving Enz therapy [24] and from cell lines derived from transplanted prostate tumor of mice receiving Enz treatment [25, 26]. GSEA illustrated that the Hallmark_EMT molecular signature is significantly activated in PCa patients non-responsive to Enz relative to the responsive group (Fig. 5A). Also, EMT pathway activation could be seen in the Enz-resistant PCa line (42D), as compared to the sensitive line (16D) (Fig. 5B). As our previous results unveiled that RhoB may negatively regulate the EMT pathway (Fig. 4), we hypothesized that RhoB downregulation induces the EMT pathway in Enz-resistant PCa. To this aim, we used two pairs of chronic Enz-resistant lines (MR49F vs LNCaP) and (C4-2R vs C4-2B). Our IB result showed that RhoB expression is downregulated in both Enz-resistant MR49F and C4-2R, compared to parental LNCaP and C4-2B cells (Fig. 5C, D). To address whether RhoB downregulation occurs during the development of Enz-resistance, we performed acute Enz treatment for 3 and 7 days using 22Rv1 cells. Our IB result showed that RhoB expression was readily reduced during the course of Enz treatment, with concordant increase of mesenchymal marker N-Cad and VIM expression (Fig. 5E). Altogether, our results support the hypothesis that activation of the EMT pathway in Enz-resistant PCa is attributed to RhoB downregulation.

Targeting SETDB1 in metastatic CRPC

Taking advantage of recent advances made in the development of an antisense oligo (ASO)-based drug approved by the FDA [27, 28], we hypothesized that targeting SETDB1 for degradation by ASO may be a therapeutic approach to treat PCa metastasis. To this aim, we transfected SETDB1-ASO into DU145 and 22Rv1 cells for 4 days. Our IB results showed that ASO-mediated knockdown of SETDB1 leads to remarkable downregulation of H3K9me3, upregulation of RhoB, and, hence, downregulation of mesenchymal markers and upregulation of epithelial markers (Fig. 6A, B). In addition, we performed a Matrigel invasion assay and a wound healing assay in DU145 and 22Rv1 cells. Our data revealed that SETDB1-ASO-mediated downregulation of the SETDB1 protein dramatically suppresses PCa invasion (Fig. 6C, D) and wound healing (Fig. 6E, F). Altogether, these results suggest that targeting SETDB1 is a feasible approach to treat metastatic CRPC.

Clinical expression of SETDB1 and RhoB

To validate the clinical relevance of the SETDB1-RhoB axis, we first examined the protein levels of SETDB1 in tissue microarrays (TMAs) of human PCa tissues via immunohistochemistry. Consistent with its role as a transcription factor, SETDB1 showed strong and punctuated nuclear staining in metastatic CRPC cells (Fig. 7A). More than 90% of the cases of CRPC stained positive for SETDB1 in nucleus, half of which showed moderate/strong staining (Fig. 7B). In great contrast, nearly half of the primary PCa cases exhibited absent SETDB1 nuclear staining and ~30% showed only weak nuclear staining. In matched sets of TMAs, we also performed IHC for RhoB. The staining of RhoB is mostly in the cytoplasm (Fig. 7C). In contrast to increased SETDB1 staining from primary PCa to CRPC, we found a decreased trend of RhoB staining upon disease progression, although the difference between primary PCa and CRPC was not as striking as SETDB1 staining. Almost all primary PCa cases stained positive for RhoB (Fig. 7C). Out of these, more than 50% of the primary PCa cases exhibited moderate/strong staining (Fig. 7D). On the contrary, about 20% of CRPC cases were RhoB-negative, whereas over 60% of RhoB-staining was weak (Fig. 7D). Therefore, TMA of clinical specimens confirmed the elevation of SETDB1 and loss of RhoB in metastatic CRPC as compared with primary PCa.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a SETDB1-RhoB epigenetic axis that drives EMT, metastatic progression, and features of therapy resistance in prostate cancer. We showed that SETDB1 is upregulated in metastatic CRPC and directly represses the tumor suppressor RhoB through H3K9-mediated chromatin silencing. Loss of SETDB1 restores RhoB expression, reverses EMT transcriptional programs, and suppresses invasive and metastatic phenotypes, while RhoB depletion rescues these effects, establishing RhoB as a critical downstream effector of SETDB1 function.

Although SETDB1 overexpression in PCa has been reported [12, 29], and SETDB1 has been implicated in metastatic progression in PCa [12, 30], the mechanisms by which SETDB1 promotes metastasis have remained incompletely defined. Prior studies relied largely on correlative analyses or in vitro migration assays and did not evaluate SETDB1 function using preclinical metastasis models, leaving its causal role in metastatic dissemination unresolved. Moreover, the prior studies did not identify the downstream effectors mediating SETDB1-driven EMT and invasion. In this study, by integrating chromatin profiling and transcriptomic analysis, we identified RhoB as a direct and functionally consequential epigenetic target of SETDB1. Restoring RhoB expression reverses EMT marker expression and suppresses invasive behavior. Conversely, RhoB silencing rescues the effects of SETDB1 depletion on EMT markers, wound healing, and invasion, demonstrating that RhoB is the critical downstream effector through which SETDB1 drives EMT and prostate cancer metastasis.

Epithelial plasticity and EMT are closely linked to therapeutic adaptation in PCa. Androgen deprivation can induce EMT and enhance the invasive potential of PCa, underscoring how AR-pathway suppression can foster mesenchymal programs [10]. Clinical and experimental evidence further indicates that epithelial plasticity contributes to PCa treatment resistance and dissemination [8]. Moreover, dynamic transitions between mesenchymal and epithelial states modulate drug response, including under combined taxane and anti-androgen therapy [9]. In prior reports and our study, RhoB maintains epithelial integrity and constrains cell motility and invasion [22], suggesting that RhoB loss removes a key brake on EMT activation. Consistent with this notion, we observed that RhoB downregulation was accompanied by EMT activation in multiple ASI-resistant PCa models. Notably, acute enzalutamide exposure was sufficient to reduce RhoB expression and increase mesenchymal marker expression, indicating that RhoB repression is an early and dynamic response to therapeutic pressure. Taken together, these findings suggest that SETDB1-mediated RhoB silencing contributes to EMT-associated cellular plasticity during androgen signaling inhibition, linking the SETDB1-RhoB axis to the emergence of aggressive, treatment-refractory disease states.

Our findings highlight the SETDB1-RhoB axis as a mechanistically defined pathway that contributes to EMT activation and metastatic behavior in PCa, suggesting potential therapeutic opportunities. In this study, we found that ASO-mediated suppression of SETDB1 restored RhoB expression and reversed EMT and invasive phenotypes in CRPC cell models, indicating that the metastatic phenotype driven by SETDB1 is reversible when RhoB repression is relieved. While the development of direct enzymatic inhibitors of SETDB1 has been challenging, recent work has introduced UNC10013, a first-in-class covalent allosteric modulator of SETDB1 [31]. UNC10013 provides proof-of-principle that SETDB1 activity can be pharmacologically altered. However, the current compound exhibits limited pharmacokinetic properties and is thus not suitable for in vivo evaluation, limiting further translational application. Taken together, these results suggest that SETDB1-directed gene silencing approaches and future refinement of SETDB1 allosteric inhibitors may offer a plausible therapeutic strategy to restrain EMT-associated metastatic progression, particularly in PCa subtype characterized by RhoB loss and mesenchymal plasticity.

Although SETDB1 amplification is observed in a subset of CRPC cases, our analyses indicate that it shows limited co-occurrence with common genomic alterations, suggesting that SETDB1 dysregulation may represent an independent oncogenic event in advanced PCa. Consistent with this notion, tissue microarray analysis revealed increased nuclear SETDB1 protein expression in CRPC specimens relative to primary PCa (Fig. 7). However, due to the lack of matched genomic data for these samples, we were unable to directly assess the relationship between SETDB1 protein expression and specific mutational contexts. Future studies integrating matched genomic and proteomic profiling will be important to further define the molecular determinants underlying SETDB1 amplification and overexpression in CRPC.

In summary, these findings define SETDB1-mediated repression of RhoB as a chromatin-based mechanism that promotes EMT and metastatic progression in PCa.
