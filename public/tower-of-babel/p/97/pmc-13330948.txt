Adopting common data elements (CDEs) for the National Trauma Research Repository (NTRR): the results of a social determinants of health Delphi survey
Authors: Smith F Heavner, Nicolas W Medrano, Michaela A West, Juan Pablo Herrera-Escobar, Lindsay D Nelson, Karen Lutrick, Terri deRoon-Cassini, Xiaohan Tanner Zhang, Christina Colosimo, Garrett Dennis Hall, Monique Pappadis, Heather X Rhodes-Lyons, Casey Green, The NTRR Investigator Group

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo support the development of the National Trauma Research Repository (NTRR), a multidisciplinary workgroup used a consensus-driven approach to review established social determinants of health data elements and recommend trauma common data elements (CDEs) for inclusion in the NTRR data dictionary.MethodsA 13-member workgroup of military and civilian trauma researchers and data scientists located and reviewed databases, codebooks, screening toolkits, and published articles for data elements relevant to social determinants of health. Identified data elements were reviewed in a three-round Delphi survey and monthly meetings with the workgroup were conducted. Consensus during the Delphi survey was identified with an 80% agreement threshold.Results18 sources were reviewed for data elements relevant to social determinants of health. After the three-round Delphi and post-Delphi workgroup discussions, 29 (42%) data elements, 11 (73%) toolkits, and two (67%) medical terminologies reached consensus for inclusion.DiscussionThe resulting standardized CDEs will improve data harmonization and support consistent data collection in the NTRR. These CDEs represent an initial framework and serve as a foundational starting point for social determinants of health data collection within the NTRR. As researchers use the NTRR, the list of CDEs will grow and evolve with the needs of the trauma research community.Level of evidenceLevel VII.Keywords: socioeconomic factors, Surveys And Questionnaires, Healthcare disparities, patient reported outcome measures

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Trauma social determinants of health (SDOH) research is limited by inconsistent variable definitions and a lack of standardized study-ready common data elements (CDEs), which restricts data comparability and reuse across studies. Although trauma registries and National Institute of Health CDE efforts exist, they do not fully address the needs of cross-study SDOH research or repository-based data sharing.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study establishes a standardized set of National Trauma Research Repository (NTRR) SDOH CDEs with clear definitions and permissible values. These CDEs provide a practical, reusable minimum dataset aligned with NTRR submission and cross-study harmonization needs. The result is an SDOH framework that did not previously exist for trauma research.

Introduction

Traumatic injuries remain a leading cause of death and disability across all age groups in the USA, presenting a persistent public health challenge that has significant societal and economic impact.1 Despite substantial progress in trauma care and prevention, trauma research is hindered by a lack of standardized data elements that enable meaningful comparison and aggregation of findings across studies and institutions.2 Thus, the generalizability and reproducibility of trauma research, particularly for social determinants of health (SDOH), is limited by the variability in how data are defined, collected, and reported.

As a key strategy for promoting data harmonization across research studies, common data elements (CDEs) are standardized, precisely defined variables intended to be consistently collected and used across studies within a given field. These CDEs facilitate data sharing, reduce duplication of effort, and enhance the ability to conduct large-scale analyses and meta-analyses.3,5 The adoption of CDEs can enable investigators to build more comprehensive, interoperable datasets that better support scientific discovery, policy development, and improvements in patient care within trauma research. Improving efficiency fosters the development of evidence-based guidelines.6 The Delphi method is a well-established, systematic approach for achieving consensus among a panel of experts, making it particularly suitable for selecting CDEs.7,10 This iterative process allows for anonymous input, controlled feedback, and statistical aggregation of group responses, which helps to mitigate the influence of dominant individuals and supports balanced decision-making.

The National Trauma Research Repository (NTRR) was established to serve as a centralized platform for storing, sharing, and curating trauma research data. As part of its development, the NTRR convened a series of expert workgroups to identify CDEs in specific study environments or phases of care. These included epidemiology, pre-hospital care, acute hospital care, rehabilitation, and SDOH.

The SDOH Workgroup was tasked with identifying and prioritizing social, economic, and environmental CDEs that influence traumatic injuries and treatment outcomes. Findings from the Summit on the Advancement of Focused Equity Research in Trauma highlighted the need to standardize methodology and data fields that prioritized these data elements.11,16 Increasing evidence highlights the role of SDOH in shaping both the risk and outcomes of traumatic injuries.17 18 Collecting SDOH data allows researchers to go beyond surface-level association and identify upstream social and structural factors. By capturing variables such as income, poverty, food insecurity, and housing status, researchers can track trends, evaluate interventions, and improve clinical protocols. Incorporating SDOH into trauma research provides critical context for understanding injury patterns, addressing health disparities, and designing more effective, equitable interventions.13 19 20 The SDOH Workgroup sought to examine the existing literature and registries to identify evidence-based measures of complex social, demographic, economic, and structural factors with plausible relationships to traumatic injuries and associated short-term, mid-term, and long-term outcomes.

This article describes the systematic approach undertaken by the SDOH Workgroup to identify ‘basic’ SDOH data elements. These SDOH CDEs were defined as data elements recommended to be collected by trauma studies and suitable for inclusion in the NTRR. Using a structured Delphi consensus process, the workgroup engaged a diverse panel of subject matter experts in SDOH to review, refine, and prioritize candidate CDEs. The goal was to develop a consensus-driven set of data elements that would enhance the quality, consistency, and utility of SDOH trauma research contributed to and conducted using the NTRR.

Methods

Members of the Executive Steering Committee and the CDE Steering Committee recommended subject matter experts for the NTRR SDOH Workgroup. Coalition for National Trauma Research (CNTR) gathered information on those recommended to serve, including work environment, trauma subspecialty, research experience, geographic location, and military experience to comprise a workgroup with diverse perspectives from across the USA. A workgroup of 13 subject matter experts was convened. The workgroup was multidisciplinary, including trauma surgeons, critical care specialists, trauma system organizers, family and community health researchers, social scientists, and data scientists.

To identify a set of data elements relevant to SDOH, the workgroup compiled relevant and applicable studies and existing databases for review (Box 1). From these resources, we extracted individual data elements and conducted a frequency analysis to analyze which variables were most commonly used across studies. To further establish the scope of the effort, the workgroup adopted the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke SDOH Framework for Addressing Health Inequities21 as the guiding structure. The workgroup further defined the scope of work to include structural, social status, and intermediate determinants. Structural determinants: the root social, cultural, and political factors that underlie racial, ethnic, and social class patterns in society, health, and well-being. Social status determinants: the demographic and socioeconomic factors that have acquired social meaning because they are proxies for structural determinants of health inequities in ways that have implications for intermediate, intrapersonal, and biological determinants of health. Intermediate determinants: social, built, economic, health, and political environmental factors that influence exposure, vulnerability, and the consequences of individual and population health, illness, and well-being. Through this framework, 37 topic areas were identified to ensure alignment with established domains of SDOH (Box 2) to ensure consistency and promote alignment with existing standards, definitions for each data element were gathered from established repositories, as well as for those data elements that were selected by previous NTRR workgroups. Existing data elements relevant to these topic areas were selected after a predefined hierarchy of precedence.22 The workgroup also recommended including 15 complete toolkits that were identified during the literature review.

CNTR gained extensive experience in configuring, running, and analyzing Delphi surveys through its development of the National Trauma Research Action Plan.23 That project engaged nearly 400 multidisciplinary stakeholders participating in 11 different Delphi panels to identify research gaps and high-priority research questions that should inform extramural research support in the future. Thus, we built on our familiarity with Calibrum’s Surveylet platform to design a similar approach to drive consensus on the CDEs to include in the NTRR data dictionary. The Delphi survey was initially designed to incorporate a traditional five-point Likert scale; however, the NTRR Executive Steering Committee recommended an abbreviated three-response approach to reduce participant cognitive load.

We created a Delphi survey, populating it with the identified data elements along with their definitions and permissible values (PVs), as collected following the order of precedence. The workgroup proceeded through three rounds of consideration in the online Delphi survey, where they could provide comments on data elements recommended for adoption by previous workgroups and rate whether a data element should be included in the NTRR through the response options of ‘include,’ ‘exclude,’ or ‘no opinion’. During round 1, workgroup reviewers had the opportunity to anonymously (1) rate whether a data element should be included in the NTRR, (2) leave comments describing their rationale regarding the inclusion of a data element or preference of definition, and (3) recommend additional data elements or toolkits for inclusion in the next round of the Delphi survey. For a data element or toolkit to reach consensus for inclusion in the NTRR, an 80% agreement threshold was set. Data elements that reached consensus were marked in subsequent rounds so that workgroup members considered only those elements that had not yet reached consensus.

The workgroup completed the three rounds of the Delphi from December 2024 to March 2025, during which time email reminders were periodically sent to encourage timely completion of each round. A total of 10 (77%) workgroup members participated in round 1, nine (69%) in round 2, and 11 (85%) in round 3. The workgroup also met monthly via teleconference to discuss outstanding items for review, including (1) comments left by reviewers, (2) topics with more than one data element that reached consensus, (3) creation of definitions/PVs for data elements that did not exist within the repositories in the order of precedence, (4) data elements selected for inclusion by more than one NTRR workgroup, and (5) adoption of entire toolkits or specific data elements within toolkits. All responses from the Delphi were kept anonymous when shared with the group; however, members were identifiable when providing feedback during the teleconference meetings. Consensus decisions made during these meetings informed revisions to the Delphi survey and the final selection of CDEs and toolkits for inclusion. On completion of these discussions, results were shared with the NTRR CDE Steering Committee and NTRR Executive Committee for final approval.

Results

18 sources were reviewed for SDOH data elements11,22 (Box 1), from which 69 elements covering the 37 topics, 15 toolkits, and three medical terminology standards were included in the Delphi survey. On completion of the three Delphi rounds and post-Delphi workgroup meetings, 29 (42%) data elements reached consensus for inclusion, resulting in 33 data elements recommended for adoption, including the addition of ‘other’ data elements to capture instances where ‘other, specify’ was a PV (box 3). Additionally, 11 (73%) toolkits and two (67%) medical terminologies reached consensus (box 4).

Discussion

The systematic approach used by the workgroup successfully identified a set of basic CDEs to support trauma SDOH research being submitted to or analyzed within the NTRR. Through a rigorous review of existing resources and a structured Delphi consensus process, we were able to build consensus on a large majority of the candidate data elements. The high rate of inclusion (66%) suggests strong alignment among experts regarding fundamental variables necessary for trauma studies.

The success of this effort reinforces the importance of using a Delphi process for developing CDEs. The iterative survey rounds, combined with structured expert discussion, allowed for refinement of both the data elements themselves and their associated definitions and PVs. Specifically, monthly workgroup meetings played a critical role in resolving instances where more than one data element for a specific topic reached consensus.

The Delphi process of selecting CDEs is not without its limitations. The process reflects the perspectives of the participants involved and, therefore, the studies and projects reviewed for data elements were collected based on workgroup member knowledge and previous experience. Consequently, some essential studies may have been overlooked. Additionally, many of the sources included were projects conducted in the USA and, therefore, the recommendations provided may not be applicable to researchers and studies conducted in other countries. Finally, data elements related to gender were removed from consideration to comply with Executive Order 14168.24 An initiative not subject to this limitation may have included these or other CDEs.

The selection of SDOH CDEs for inclusion in the NTRR represents an important step toward systematically capturing broad factors that influence trauma outcomes. Trauma research has focused primarily on clinical and injury-related data, with less consistent attention to social, economic, and environmental factors that may critically shape patient recovery and long-term health. By establishing a consensus set of SDOH CDEs, this effort addresses a significant gap in trauma research infrastructure.

As researchers design and complete studies and contribute their data to the NTRR, the list of CDEs is expected to evolve. New data elements may be proposed based on emerging research needs, novel methodologies, or gaps identified through real-world use. Such an iterative process ensures that the CDEs remain responsive to the needs of the trauma research community, supporting both standardization and innovation over time.
