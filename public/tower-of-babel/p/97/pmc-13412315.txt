DCLK1 mediates airway epithelial barrier disruption through NF-κB activation in severe asthma
Authors: Wun-Hao Cheng, Mei-May Neoh, Kuan-Yuan Chen, Kang-Yun Lee, Po-Hao Feng, Yu-Chih Wu, Lee-Yuan Lin, Fara Silvia Yuliani, Yan-An Wang, Chien-Huang Lin, Bing-Chang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere asthma is characterized by epithelial barrier dysfunction, airway remodeling, and chronic inflammation. Doublecortin-like kinase 1 (DCLK1), a kinase initially identified as a cancer stem cell marker, has been linked to NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses. However, its role in TGF-β-induced epithelial barrier disruption in severe asthma remains unclear.MethodsThe expression of DCLK1 and junctional proteins was examined in bronchial epithelial tissue biopsies and air-liquid interface (ALI) cultures derived from patients with severe asthma and healthy controls. The role of DCLK1 was investigated in vitro using TGF-β-treated BEAS-2B cells and in vivo using ovalbumin (OVA)- and house dust mite (HDM)-induced murine asthma models. Global DCLK1 knockout mice were employed to assess epithelial integrity, airway remodeling, and inflammatory responses in murine asthma.ResultsDCLK1 expression was increased in bronchial epithelial cells from patients with severe asthma. DCLK1 siRNA reversed TGF-β-induced epithelial dysfunction by restoring E-cadherin expression and reducing fibronectin and N-cadherin levels in BEAS-2B cells. Moreover, DCLK1 silencing improved epithelial barrier function, as evidenced by increased transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and E-cadherin expression. Mechanistically, DCLK1 mediates TGF-β-induced EMT via activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. DCLK1 knockout mice exhibited improved lung function and reduced airway inflammatory cell infiltration compared with wild-type mice under both OVA and HDM exposure.ConclusionsDCLK1 promotes airway epithelial barrier dysfunction in severe asthma through NF-κB activation. Targeting DCLK1 may represent a novel therapeutic strategy to restore epithelial integrity in severe asthma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10020-026-01513-5.Keywords: asthma, airway fibrosis, Doublecortin-like kinase 1, EMT, NF-κB, OVA/HDM

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airway characterized by recurrent wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, and coughing (Hammad and Lambrecht 2021). These clinical manifestations typically correlate with underlying physiological alterations, including variable expiratory airflow limitation, mucus hypersecretion, and airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) (Hammad and Lambrecht 2021). Although most patients with asthma can achieve symptom control through a combination of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and long-acting β2-agonists, approximately 10% experience severe asthma (Louis et al. 2022), defined as persistent, uncontrolled disease despite adherence to high-dose ICS alongside other controller medications. Patients with severe asthma frequently depend on adjunctive oral corticosteroids, which are associated with notable adverse effects (Chung et al. 2020). Airway remodeling involves structural changes that contribute to severe asthma, such as epithelial damage, subepithelial fibrosis, increased smooth muscle mass in the airway, angiogenesis, and goblet cell hyperplasia with mucus plugging (Varricchi et al. 2022, Dorscheid et al. 2025). Therefore, airway remodeling represents a promising therapeutic target for strategies to improve clinical outcomes in severe asthma.

Epithelial cells form a critical barrier between the external environment and underlying tissues, predominantly through intercellular junctional complexes (Dorscheid et al. 2025). In bronchial epithelial cells, these complexes include tight junctions at atypical locations composed of transmembrane proteins, such as zonula occludens-1, occludin, claudins, and junctional adhesion molecules (Noureddine et al. 2022). Tight junctions maintain epithelial integrity by regulating paracellular permeability and restricting macromolecule diffusion. Adherens junctions, primarily composed of E-cadherin, are located beneath tight junctions. Exposure to harmful stimuli, such as house dust mites (HDM), viruses, environmental pollutants, or cytokines (e.g., transforming growth factor-beta [TGF-β]), may compromise the integrity of the airway epithelial barrier (Noureddine et al. 2022). Airway epithelial damage can induce epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), which is characterized by the loss of E-cadherin and other epithelial markers and the acquisition of mesenchymal markers, including N-cadherin and fibronectin (Sun et al. 2020). Consequently, impaired barrier function, defective epithelial repair, disrupted cell–cell junctional complexes, and inadequate innate immune responses contribute to chronic airway inflammation and disease progression in severe asthma (Dorscheid et al. 2025, Noureddine et al. 2022, Sun et al. 2020). Epithelial barrier disruption thus plays a central and causative role in asthma pathogenesis.

Doublecortin-like kinase 1 (DCLK1) is a serine/threonine kinase belonging to the microtubule-associated protein family that was initially recognized for its crucial roles in neurogenesis and neuronal migration (Lin et al. 2000). DCLK1 is expressed as multiple isoforms, including a full-length isoform (~ 82 kDa, DCLK1-L) and a shorter C-terminal kinase-containing isoform (~ 45–50 kDa, DCLK1-S). These isoforms are generated from two distinct promoter regions: the upstream 5′ (α)-promoter drives expression of the full-length DCLK1-L, whereas the β-promoter produces the truncated DCLK1-S isoform. Notably, these isoforms exhibit distinct biological functions across different tissues and disease contexts (Ge et al. 2022, Kalantari et al. 2022). Contemporary studies have additionally implicated DCLK1 in the regulation of inflammation and cancer-related processes, such as tumor initiation, progression, EMT, and cancer stem cell maintenance (Wang et al. 2022, Patel et al. 2016). Notably, DCLK1 is a hallmark of intestinal tuft cells, which secrete the alarm cytokine IL-25, in turn activating type 2 innate lymphoid cells and driving Th2-mediated inflammation and fibrosis (Chandrakesan et al. 2015, Gieseck et al. 2018). Furthermore, inhibition of DCLK1 effectively suppresses the proinflammatory caspase-1/interleukin-1β signaling pathway in infected cells, which positions DCLK1 as a promising therapeutic target for inflammatory diseases, including COVID-19 (Undi et al. 2022). Clinically, elevated DCLK1 expression has been observed in the lung tissues of patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and in mouse models of bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis, which reinforces the association of DCLK1 with fibrotic pathogenesis (Pei et al. 2022). In a recent study, DCLK1 induced inflammatory responses in macrophages by activating nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling (Luo et al. 2023). Our previous work also demonstrated that DCLK1 plays a role in thrombin-induced airway inflammation in severe asthma (Yuliani et al. 2022). However, its contribution to epithelial barrier disruption in severe asthma remains unclear, warranting further investigation.

Given the central role of airway epithelial barrier disruption in severe asthma pathogenesis and the emerging understanding of how DCLK1 promotes inflammation and fibrosis, we hypothesized that DCLK1 is a critical contributor to epithelial barrier dysfunction in severe asthma. Accordingly, in this study, we investigated whether DCLK1 is aberrantly expressed in the airway epithelial cells of patients with asthma and asthmatic animal models and examined the molecular mechanisms underlying its involvement in barrier disruption. We demonstrated that DCLK1 expression is significantly elevated in the bronchial epithelial cells of patients with severe asthma and in airway tissues taken from mouse models with ovalbumin (OVA)- and HDM-induced asthma. Our findings also indicate that DCLK1 mediates TGF-β-induced epithelial barrier disruption by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway. DCLK1 knockout effectively attenuated airway inflammation, epithelial barrier disruption, and impaired lung function induced by OVA or HDM challenges in mouse models. These findings highlight a novel functional role and molecular mechanism of DCLK1 in epithelial barrier disruption.

Materials and methods

Airway epithelial cell basal medium (BEGM, C-21260) and supplement mix (C-39165) were purchased from PromoCell (Heidelberg, Germany). Fetal bovine serum (A5670701) and antibiotic–antimycotics (100×; 15240062) were purchased from Gibco (Waltham, MA, USA). Control small interfering RNA (siRNA; scrambled), DCLK1 siRNA, an α-tubulin antibody (T6199), OVA (A5503), tamoxifen (T5648), sunflower seed oil (S5007), and methacholine chloride (A2251) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). A mix of Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus and D. farinae HDMs were obtained from CITEQ biologics (cat. #02.50.85) (Groningen, the Netherlands). Endobronchial biopsy samples were obtained from both groups for histological evaluation and to isolate primary human bronchial epithelial cells. ALI culture of primary human bronchial epithelial cells

Bronchial brushings were obtained by bronchoscopy from control individuals and patients with severe asthma. Cells were immediately transferred into DMEM supplemented with Antibiotic-Antimycotic, pelleted (2,00 g, 5 min, room temperature), and gently resuspended before seeding into T-25 flasks. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ until confluent, with medium changes every 2–3 days. For ALI differentiation, cells were dissociated and seeded onto 12-mm Transwell inserts (0.4-µm pore size, cat. #05021) according to the PneumaCult™ system protocol (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada). Once a tight, confluent monolayer formed, apical medium was aspirated to establish the air-liquid interface, while basolateral PneumaCult™ ALI medium was refreshed every 2–3 days. The apical surface was rinsed with warm PBS as needed to remove accumulated secretions. After 28 days of ALI differentiation, cells were transfected with DCLK1 siRNA and subsequently exposed to HDM (100 ng/mL) for 7 days. The transwell inserts were then fixed and processed for paraffin embedding and sectioning, followed by histological and immunostaining analyses.

Cell culture

Experiments were conducted with bronchial epithelial cells from patients with severe asthma and control individuals and with BEAS-2B cells, which were cultured in BEGM and RPMI-1,640 respectively. Cells were grown in an incubator containing CO2 at 37 °C. Once they achieved confluence, the cells were seeded onto 12-transwell plates for air–liquid interface (ALI) culturing. Subsequently, the cells were seeded onto 6-cm dishes for transfection and immunoblotting. For ChIP assays, the cells were transferred to 10-cm dishes.

HDM- and OVA-induced asthmatic animal model

Because DCLK1 knockout mice were not viable, we established a DCLK1 global knockout mouse model. Dclk1tm1.2Jgg/J mice with loxP sites flanking exon 3 of the DCLK1 locus (DCLK1f/f) and mice carrying tamoxifen-inducible Cre-recombinase (Cre/ERT2) controlled by a regulatory sequence of the Pgk1 gene (C57BL/6-Tg(Pgk1-RFP,-Cre/ERT2)3Narl) were bred from pairs respectively purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and the Rodent Model Resource Center (Taipei, Taiwan). Both strains had a C57BL/6 background. To establish a DCLK1 KO mice model, DCLK1f/f mice were cross-bred with Pgk1-RFP-Cre/ERT2/0 (hemizygous allele) transgenic mice to create mice hemizygous for both alleles. The second cross between DCLK1f/f mice and hemizygous mice from the first cross produced the DCLK1f/f mice carrying Pgk1-RFP-Cre/ERT2/0 used in the subsequent experiments. To delete DCLK1 in whole cells, a stock of tamoxifen in 100 mg/mL of ethanol was diluted in sunflower seed oil to 10 mg/mL. DCLK1 KO mice and control mice (i.e., DCLK1f/f mice with no hemizygous allele for Pgk1-RFP-Cre/ERT2/0) were injected intraperitoneally with the tamoxifen suspension (100 µL of diluted stock) daily for the first 7 days to induce deletion of the floxed DCLK1 coding region. From day 8 onward, injections were administered twice weekly. The resultant DCLK1-knockout mice were referred to as DCLK1f/f/Cre+ mice. To establish an OVA-induced asthma model, DCLK1f/f/Cre+ mice aged 6–8 weeks and control mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 µL of 50 µg OVA emulsified in 2 mg of Alum on days 1, 8, and 15 for senitization. From week 7 onward, DCLK1f/f/Cre+ mice were placed in a nebulization chamber and exposed twice weekly to aerosolized OVA, (1%, 10 mg/10 mL), whereas control mice were exposed to aerosolized phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) generated by an ultrasonic nebulizer. Each exposure lasted for approximately 30 min. The mice were sacrificed at week 12.

For the HDM-induced asthma model, DCLK1f/f/Cre+ mice were created using the same Cre–LoxP and tamoxifen administration protocol as that described for the OVA model. In this model, mice were anesthetized with 2%–2.5% isoflurane and intratracheally instilled with HDM (100 µg/50 µL) on days 0 and 7. Subsequently, HDM (25 µg/50 µL) was intratracheally instilled for 3 consecutive days (days 26, 27, and 28). The mice were sacrificed on day 29 for further analysis.

Lung function and AHR

Lung function was assessed according to the protocol outlined in a previous study (Chen et al. 2021). Pulmonary function in the mice was measured with a FlexiVent ventilator system (SCIREQ, Montréal, Canada). The mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of Zoletil 50 (25 mg/kg) combined with xylazine (10 mg/kg) and connected to the FlexiVent system with an intratracheal tube. The mice were ventilated with a tidal volume of 10 mL/kg at a frequency of 150 breaths/min to reach a mean lung volume similar to that of spontaneous breathing. Each mouse was nebulized with PBS or increasing concentrations of methacholine (6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg/mL). A software-generated script that took three readings per animal was used to evaluate lung mechanical properties, including compliance, resistance, and pressure-volume loop (PV curve).

Cell quantification in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid

A cannula was inserted into the trachea of the mouse models, and the lungs were lavaged three times with 1 mL of fresh PBS. The average lavage fluid recovery was approximately 50%–60%. The lavage samples were centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min, and the resulting pellets were resuspended in 100 µL of PBS. The concentration of inflammatory cells in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), including neutrophils, eosinophils, white blood cells, and lymphocytes, was quantified with a hematology analyzer (ProCyte Dx; IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, ME, USA).

Immunohistochemical staining

The mouse lung tissues were fixed in 10% formaldehyde overnight, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 6-µm sections. For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, the sections were deparaffinized, and antigens were retrieved. Subsequently, peroxidase blocking was performed to neutralize endogenous peroxidase in the tissue. The tissue was then blocked with a blocking solution and incubated with a primary antibody specific to DCLK1 (1:500) and Fibronectin (1:500) for 1 h and subsequently incubated for a further 30 min with secondary antibodies. Protein expression was visualized with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine solution. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted with Permount. All images were quantified using the IHC Profiler plugin.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence (IF) staining, the agarose-embedded human bronchial epithelial cells of healthy control individuals and patients with severe asthma and the paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections of the mice were processed for antigen retrieval. The tissues were blocked with a blocking solution, treated with a DCLK1-specific antibody, and immunostained with conjugated secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488) for 1 hr. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with E-cadherin antibodies for 1 h and with goat antirabbit IgG (H&L) Alexa Fluor 555 for an additional 1 h. The nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) in mounting medium (ab104139, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). To determine the average protein intensity of each cell, the mean fluorescence intensity of protein expression was measured and divided by the total number of DAPI-positive cells with ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

siRNA transfection

Lipofectamine 3,000 reagent with Opti medium was used to transfect cells for 24 h with control siRNA or DCLKI siRNA. The following sequences were used for DCLK1 siRNAs: #1 (sense, 5ʹ-GAUCGAUACUUCAAAGGGA-3ʹ; antisense, 5ʹ-UCCCUUUGAAGUAUCGAUC-3ʹ); #2 (sense, 5ʹ-CAAAGGAGCUCAUUACCAU-3ʹ; antisense, 5ʹ-AUGGUAAUGAGCUCCUUUG-3ʹ). After transfection for 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free medium for overnight incubation. Subsequently, the cells were treated with TGF-β for the indicated time intervals, and Western blotting was performed to analyze the results.

Western blotting

Cell lysates were separated through sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. To block nonspecific antigens, the membrane was immersed in 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with a primary antibody overnight at 4 °C and then with a secondary antibody for 1 h. Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents were used to visualize blots. Quantitative data were obtained with a scientific imaging system (Kodak, Rochester, NY, USA). Densitometric analysis of Western blotting bands was performed with ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

RNA sequencing

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) was performed as described in a previous study (Cheng et al. 2024). RNA was extracted with Trizol reagent (cat. 15596026, Invitrogen, Dallas, TX, USA). The quality of the RNA samples, each of which had a minimum RNA integrity number of 7, was verified with a Bioanalyzer 2,100 (Agilent Technology, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and an RNA 6,000 LabChip kit (Agilent Technology). A SureSelect XT HS2 mRNA Library Preparation kit (Agilent Technology) was used for library preparation, and size selection was performed with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Sequencing-by-synthesis was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Postsequencing data (FASTQ reads) were generated by the Welgene Biotech pipeline (Taipei, Taiwan), which was run on the basecalling software bcl2fastq v2.20 by Illumina. Adapter clipping and sequence quality trimming were performed using a sliding-window strategy with Trimmomatic v0.36. The sensitive HISAT2 alignment program was used to map next-generation sequencing reads to genomes. DEseq (v1.39.0) was used for differential expression analysis, incorporating genome bias detection and correction, within the proprietary pipeline of Welgene Biotech.

ChIP assay

BEAS-2B cells in 10-cm dishes were treated with TGF-β (10 ng/mL) for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated in 10% formaldehyde for 10 min, harvested, and sonicated. Protein A beads were then used to apply anti-p65 and rabbit anti-IgG antibodies for immunoprecipitation. A polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with the following primer sequences was used to amplify the NF-κB response element on the fibronectin and N-cadherin promoter: fibronectin promoter region (sense, 5ʹ-AATTTTCAGGTTGTTCACAG-3ʹ; antisense, 5ʹ-TCTCTGCTTATAGGAGTGTATT-3ʹ) and N-cadherin promoter region (sense, 5ʹ-ACACACTCGCAGACGCTCACA-3ʹ; antisense, 5ʹ-TATCACCACCAGTAGGGTCTATT-3ʹ). The PCR product was evaluated through 2% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Sidak’s post hoc test was performed to compare three or more groups. Unpaired t-tests were performed to compare differences between two groups. AHR was assessed using a two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc test. A p value < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism, version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

DCLK1 expression was upregulated in the airway epithelial cells of patients with severe asthma and mice with HDM/OVA-induced asthma

First, to investigate the role of DCLK1 in asthma pathogenesis, we analyzed DCLK1 expression in public GEO datasets (GSE117489 and GSE61342). We found that DCLK1 expression was significantly increased in patients with asthma compared with healthy controls (Fig. 1A-B). We next examined its expression in the bronchial epithelial cells of patients with severe asthma. IF staining revealed a marked increase in DCLK1 expression in severe asthma tissues, with the increase inversely correlated with the epithelial tight junction marker E-cadherin (Fig. 1C). Western blot analysis further confirmed increased DCLK1 long isoform expression in the bronchial epithelial cells of patients with severe asthma (Fig. 1D). To further validate this observation, we established an ALI culture model with primary human bronchial epithelial cells derived from healthy individuals, patients with asthma, and asthma-derived cells transfected with DCLK1 siRNA. Consistent with the tissue findings, DCLK1 expression was significantly elevated in asthma-derived epithelial cells compared with those of healthy individuals, whereas E-cadherin expression was reduced. Notably, knockdown of DCLK1 restored E-cadherin expression and improved epithelial junction integrity as observed through IF staining (Fig. 1E–F). To extend these findings to asthmatic mice, we conducted IHC staining of airway tissues taken from mouse models with OVA- and HDM-induced asthma, revealing a robust increase in DCLK1 expression in asthmatic mice compared with in PBS-treated controls (Fig. 1G-J).

DCLK1 contributed to TGF-β-induced EMT in human airway epithelial cells

DCLK1 exists as a full-length isoform (~ 82 kDa, DCLK1-L) and a shorter kinase-containing C-terminal isoform (~ 45–50 kDa, DCLK1-S) (Ge et al. 2022). To investigate the differential roles of DCLK1 isoforms in TGF-β-induced EMT. Notably, the expression of the long isoform (DCLK1-L) was increased in response to TGF-β stimulation, whereas the short isoform (DCLK1-S) showed minimal or no change (Fig. 2A-C). To examine the role of DCLK1 in EMT during asthma pathogenesis, we first assessed DCLK1 activation in BEAS-2B cells following stimulation with TGF-β (10 ng/mL) for 10–60 min. Phosphorylation of DCLK1 increased in a time-dependent manner, peaking at 20 min (Fig. 2D). Next, we investigated whether pharmacological inhibition of DCLK1 modulates TGF-β-induced EMT. Treatment with the selective DCLK1 inhibitor DCLK1-IN-1 (10 µM) restored E-cadherin expression and suppressed the TGF-β-induced upregulation of N-cadherin and fibronectin (Fig. 2E-G). To further confirm the role of DCLK1 in EMT, we silenced its expression by using DCLK1 siRNA (50 nM). As we observed with pharmacological inhibition, DCLK1 knockdown reversed the downregulation of E-cadherin and significantly reduced the induction of N-cadherin and fibronectin in TGF-β-treated BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 2H-J). The efficiency of DCLK1 knockdown was validated through Western blot analysis, which confirmed major suppression of DCLK1 protein expression (Fig. 2K). Finally, functional assessment of epithelial barrier integrity revealed that DCLK1 knockdown significantly increased transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and E-cadherin expression in response to HDM stimulation (Fig. 2L-N).

TGF-β induced EMT through the DCLK1/NF-κB signaling pathway in human airway epithelial cells

To further investigate the role of DCLK1 and its associated signaling pathways in EMT development, we established a global DCLK1-knockout mouse model by using the Cre-LoxP gene-editing system (DCLK1f/f/Cre+). Tamoxifen was administered to induce gene recombination, with this achieving efficient DCLK1 deletion (Fig. 3A). As indicated in Fig. 3B, RNA-seq analysis comparing PBS-treated mice with DCLK1-knockout mice revealed alterations in the NF-κB signaling pathway, as identified through KEGG enrichment analysis. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesized that DCLK1 regulates EMT development by activating NF-κB. To validate this hypothesis, we examined the effects of DCLK1 inhibition in BEAS-2B cells. Transfection with DCLK1 siRNA completely abolished TGF-β-induced phosphorylation of p65 (Fig. 3C). The efficiency of p65 knockdown was confirmed through Western blotting (Fig. 3D). Functionally, p65 silencing restored TGF-β-induced loss of E-cadherin and attenuated the upregulation of N-cadherin and fibronectin (Fig. 3E-G). Furthermore, ChIP assays demonstrated that TGF-β increased the capacity of p65 to bind to the promoter regions of fibronectin and N-cadherin (Fig. 3H-I). Collectively, these results suggest that DCLK1 mediates TGF-β-induced EMT by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Discussion

Epithelial barrier dysfunction increases airway permeability to external pathogens and allergens, exacerbating asthma symptoms (Weng et al. 2023). Although treatment with ICS can promote epithelial barrier integrity by redistributing tight junction proteins (Sekiyama et al. 2012), ICS therapy may not fully reverse asthma-induced epithelial damage, and prolonged exposure can even induce caspase-mediated epithelial apoptosis and injury. Therefore, further investigation into mechanisms that restore epithelial barrier function can provide evidence that can guide the development of new therapeutic strategies for asthma. The present study yielded the novel finding that DCLK1 inhibition attenuates TGF-β-induced EMT through NF-κB signaling, thereby improving OVA- and HDM-induced decline in lung function and airway inflammation. DCLK1 may therefore represent a promising target for the treatment of airway barrier dysfunction in severe asthma.

DCLK1 is a pro-oncogenic factor that has been strongly associated with malignant progression and poor clinical prognosis in multiple cancers (Mohammadi et al. 2021, Yan et al. 2022, Ye et al. 2024). DCLK1 inhibition can affect cell survival, apoptosis, the cell cycle, and DNA damage responses, including ATM and γH2AX signaling (Mohammadi et al. 2021). In addition to playing an oncogenic role, DCLK1 is a stem cell marker that regulates EMT. Mechanistically, DCLK1 activates SP1 through the PI3K/AKT/SP1 signaling axis, which binds to SP1 sites within the NF-κB p65 promoter. This process increases promoter activity, driving NF-κB p65 expression and nuclear translocation (Lai et al. 2022). Consequently, epithelial integrity is disrupted, reducing E-cadherin expression and increasing ZEB1 and vimentin levels (Yan et al. 2022, Liu et al. 2019). In this study, RNA-seq analysis revealed enrichment of NF-κB signaling pathways in DCLK1f/f/Cre+. We further demonstrated that DCLK1 directly promotes p65 phosphorylation and regulates EMT-associated changes in airway epithelial cells, including upregulation of fibronectin and N-cadherin and E-cadherin loss. Collectively, these findings indicate that DCLK1 is a key regulator of EMT and epithelial barrier integrity, suggesting that overactivation of DCLK1 may compromise airway defenses against external pathogens and allergens.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that DCLK1 regulates inflammation and fibrosis in multiple lung diseases. During lung tissue injury, thrombin is released, exacerbating airway inflammation (Mercer and Chambers 2013). Our earlier work demonstrated that thrombin upregulates DCLK1 expression in human lung epithelial cells and drives IL-8/CXCL8 release through the DCLK1/YAP signaling pathway (Yuliani et al. 2022). Similarly, a prior study reported that lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation can induce expression of the short isoform of DCLK1 in macrophages, which interacts directly with the IKKβ/NF-κB pathway to mediate LPS-induced lung injury (Luo et al. 2023). In that study, both myeloid-specific DCLK1 deletion and pharmacological inhibition protected against LPS-induced acute lung injury and septic mortality, highlighting the pathogenic role of DCLK1 in pulmonary inflammation (Luo et al. 2023). The present study supports these findings by revealing abundant DCLK1 expression in the bronchial epithelial cells of patients with severe asthma, which is induced by TGF-β stimulation. Notably, DCKL1 deletion in DCLK1f/f/Cre+ mice protected against OVA- and HDM-induced airway inflammation, as evidenced by the reduced infiltration of inflammatory cells. These results extend the pathogenic role of DCLK1 to asthma, highlighting its involvement in epithelial barrier dysfunction and airway inflammation. Collectively, our findings support the role of elevated DCLK1 expression as a shared pathological feature of multiple lung diseases, with these ranging from acute lung injury to asthma and other chronic inflammatory airway disorders. This study links DCLK1 activation to airway epithelial dysfunction and inflammation, providing novel evidence for the potential of DCLK1 as a therapeutic target for severe asthma.

In the present study, DCLK1f/f/Cre+ mice attenuated OVA- and HDM-induced airway inflammation, as evidenced by reduced inflammatory cell infiltration. However, contributions from immune and stromal compartments cannot be fully excluded. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated that DCLK1-expressing macrophages participate in the regulation of inflammatory responses during LPS-induced lung injury, suggesting that immune cell–associated DCLK1 may also contribute to disease modulation (Luo et al. 2023). Importantly, epithelial cell–intrinsic experiments using BEAS-2B cells and ALI culture demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of DCLK1 attenuates TGF-β–induced epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), as reflected by preservation of E-cadherin expression or reduction of fibronectin induction. These findings provide mechanistic evidence supporting a direct epithelial role of DCLK1 that is, at least in part, independent of systemic immune effects. Taken together, our data identify DCLK1 as an important regulator of airway remodeling and inflammatory responses in the asthma environment.

DCLK1 is expressed as multiple isoforms that exhibit distinct biological functions across different tissues and disease contexts (Ge et al. 2022, Kalantari et al. 2022) In colorectal cancer, the DCLK1-S has been reported to be preferentially expressed compared with the DCLK1-L, as demonstrated using isoform-specific antibodies (Sarkar et al. 2017), In pancreatic cancer, both the DCLK1-L and DCLK1-S isoforms have been shown to promote EMT (Ge et al. 2022). In addition to its role in cancer, DCLK1 has been implicated in inflammatory responses. Previous studies have shown that short-term LPS exposure increases the expression of the DCLK1 short isoform in macrophages. Furthermore, overexpression of DCLK1, including the short isoform, induces proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 (Luo et al. 2023). In contrast to these findings, our results demonstrate that TGF-β stimulation selectively induces the DCLK1-L in airway epithelial cells, whereas the short isoform shows minimal changes. This suggests that DCLK1-L may represent the functionally relevant isoform in the context of airway epithelial remodeling and EMT. Taken together, these findings highlight the context-dependent roles of DCLK1 isoforms and suggest that distinct upstream stimuli may differentially regulate DCLK1 isoform expression and function. Further studies are warranted to delineate the isoform-specific mechanisms underlying DCLK1-mediated epithelial dysfunction in asthma.

Murine models of allergic asthma are widely induced through OVA and HDM (Doras et al. 2018). The potent inflammatory effects of HDM are primarily attributable to Derp 1, a major HDM allergen, which activates protease-activated receptor 2 to trigger the release of epithelial alarmins, such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin and IL-33 (Xu et al. 2025, Kato et al. 2009). Epithelial alarmins stimulate group 2 innate lymphoid cells to produce IL-5 and IL-13, further amplifying eosinophilic inflammation and Th2 responses. Additionally, HDM activates TLR2 and TLR4, initiating proinflammatory signaling cascades (Xu et al. 2025, Kato et al. 2009). By contrast, OVA lacks the intrinsic capacity to stimulate TLR and requires repeated exposure to prime adaptive immune responses (Huang et al. 2018). Accordingly, OVA challenge induces less robust IL-5 secretion and lower MUC5AC expression in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid relative to HDM challenge (Xu et al. 2025). Nevertheless, both OVA and HDM elicit AHR and airway inflammation (DiGiovanni et al. 2009). In contrast, the HDM model more closely reflects human asthma pathophysiology, as it represents a clinically relevant aeroallergen and induces a more heterogeneous inflammatory response involving both innate and adaptive immune pathways beyond canonical T2 inflammation. Therefore, by employing both OVA and HDM models, we sought to determine whether DCLK1-mediated regulation is restricted to a specific inflammatory endotype or represents a broader mechanism across distinct asthma contexts. In this study, both OVA- and HDM-induced AHR and airway inflammation were lower in DCLK1f/f/Cre+ mice compared with in control mice. Furthermore, DCLK1f/f/Cre+ mice were protected against OVA- and HDM-induced epithelial barrier disruption, as evidenced by their attenuated E-cadherin loss and decreased fibronectin expression relative to control mice. Collectively, these findings indicate that DCLK1 deletion confers protection against OVA- and HDM-induced lung function impairment and airway remodeling.

Patient characteristics (Table 1) may represent potential confounders influencing epithelial state and DCLK1 expression. In this study, patients with severe asthma exhibited elevated total IgE and FeNO levels, along with reduced lung function, reflecting enhanced type 2 inflammatory responses and poorly controlled disease status. These inflammatory features may contribute to epithelial activation and remodeling, and thus potentially influence DCLK1 expression. In addition, prior studies have shown that DCLK1 expression in epithelial tissues is associated with specialized cell populations, such as tuft cells, suggesting a role in epithelial differentiation and function (PMID: 30478383). This raises the possibility that the increased DCLK1 expression observed in human airway epithelium may reflect altered epithelial composition or differentiation states in severe asthma.

This study has several limitations. First, we established a global model of DCLK1 knockout mice; thus, off-target effects may have been present. Although we performed complementary experiments using DCLK1 siRNA and a pharmacological inhibitor to evaluate the specific contribution of DCLK1 to airway epithelial barrier dysfunction, DCLK1 deletion in immune cells may nevertheless also confer anti-inflammatory effects, thereby contributing to the reduced AHR and airway inflammation observed in OVA- and HDM-induced asthma models. Second, human DCLK1 has both long (~ 80–82 kDa) and short (~ 45–50 kDa) isoforms. Studies have demonstrated that during neoplastic transformation, hypermethylation of the long DCLK1 isoform can shift expression toward the short isoform, which has been associated with a more invasive phenotype, suggesting the two isoforms maintain distinct biological functions (Roy et al. 2021). In one study, the short isoform of DCLK1 was induced by LPS in macrophages and directly interacted with the IKKβ/NF-κB pathway to mediate inflammatory responses (Luo et al. 2023). In the present study, we primarily observed TGF-β-induced expression of the DCLK1-L isoform in human bronchial epithelial cells. However, the relative roles of different isoforms in airway epithelial biology remain incompletely defined, warranting further investigation. Third, although both OVA and HDM are widely used allergens in asthma research, the mechanisms by which they induce asthma differ. HDM sensitization elicits a complex and clinically relevant immune response involving pronounced eosinophilia and Th1/Th2 imbalance, whereas OVA more strongly induces subepithelial fibrosis with comparatively less IL-5 and mucus hypersecretion (Doras et al. 2018). The present study focused on lung function impairment, airway barrier dysfunction, and inflammation; nevertheless, the distinct immunopathological mechanisms triggered by OVA and HDM should be considered in the interpretation of our findings’ translational relevance.

In conclusion, DCLK1 mediated TGF-β-induced airway EMT through NF-κB signaling, and DCLK1 deletion restored lung function impairment and inflammation (Fig. 8). These results provide novel insights that can support the development of management and mitigation strategies for epithelial barrier dysfunction and airway inflammation.
