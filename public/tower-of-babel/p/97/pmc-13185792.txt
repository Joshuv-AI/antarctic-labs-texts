Robotic Resection of Caudate Lobe Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Farman H. Fatah, Gazi B. Zibari, Andrew Crane, Anil Veluvolu, Gregory Wellman, Hosein Shokouh-Amiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Robotic liver resection is a valuable alternative to both open and laparoscopic techniques. We report a case of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) located in the caudate lobe, and the tumor was successfully resected with a robotic approach. When performed by experienced surgeons, robotic resection can be a technically sound and an oncological appropriate option for selected patients with complex hepatic tumors.Case Description:A 70-year-old man with a history of chronic hepatitis C infection, compensated cirrhosis, cholelithiasis, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hypertension was referred to our transplant and hepatobiliary center following an incidental liver lesion finding during routine surveillance imaging. He was asymptomatic and his family history was notable for HCC. Physical examination and laboratory investigations were unremarkable. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated a 4.6 cm hypoechoic mass in the caudate lobe, and cholelithiasis. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a 4.2 × 4.0 cm lesion in segment one with arterial enhancement and washout. Triphasic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) showed a 5 × 4.2 × 4 cm hypervascular mass, and esophagogastroduodenoscopy showed mild gastritis. A CT-guided needle biopsy from uninvolved right hepatic lobe revealed chronic hepatitis C and fibrosis. Metastatic workup was unremarkable. Robotic-assisted caudate lobectomy with cholecystectomy was performed with the da Vinci-5 robotic platform. The pathological report of the resected mass confirmed HCC with negative margins.Conclusion:This case highlights the safety of robotic resection for HCC in the caudate lobe. With proper patient selection and more clinical experience, it is becoming a widely accepted option.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary malignancy of liver, and it accounts for approximately 90% of all primary liver tumors.1,2 Globally, it ranks as the sixth most diagnosed tumor and the third leading cause of cancer-related death.3 It mostly affects middle-aged to older adults, with a median age of 62 at diagnosis, though about 15% of cases occur in younger adults aged 15–49 years.4 The overall prognosis remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of <20%.5

While HCC can present without cirrhosis in 10–15% of cases, it predominantly arises in the background of cirrhosis about 80–85% of cases.2,6 Globally, The most common causes of cirrhosis leading to HCC include hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection (responsible for 44% of cases) and chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection (responsible for 21% of cases), which is more common than HBV in western countries.2,7,8 Moreover, the other etiologies include alcohol consumption (responsible for 26% of cases), and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis/metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (NASH/MASH) (responsible for 10–20% of cases).7 Notably, the etiology distribution is recently trending from viral etiologies towards a more frequent nonviral causes, particularly NASH/MASH and alcohol-related liver diseases in developed nations due proper vaccination and better antiviral therapeutic treatments.8 Less common causes include hereditary hemochromatosis, autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, and aflatoxin exposure.8 The diagnosis of HCC usually follows a step-by-step approach guided by the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD). It starts with clinical evaluation, liver function tests, and checking α-fetoprotein (AFP) levels. Although high AFP level in high-risk patients may raise suspicion for HCC, however, it is not specific, and normal values do not rule it out.2,9 What makes HCC different from most of the other liver tumors is that a diagnosis can be made without a biopsy in the presence of the characteristic features on imaging.2,10 For nodules larger than 1 cm, a multiphasic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is performed.2,9,10 These scans typically show an arterial phase enhancement followed by washout in the portal or delayed phase, sometimes with a fibrous capsule on delayed imaging.2,9,11

MRI is generally more sensitive than CT scan in detecting these patterns.2 If a lesion does not show the typical features, a second imaging modality is usually performed.2 When both scans are inconclusive, biopsy is considered, though it carries risks like hemorrhage, tumor spread, or false negatives.2,12 In such cases, special stains may help confirm the diagnosis.13 For nodules smaller than 1 cm, current guidelines recommend close monitoring with follow-up imaging every 3–6 months.9

Treatment options for the HCC includes a broad range of modalities, such as surgical resection, liver transplantation, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), Yttrium-90 (Y-90), radiofrequency, microwave, cryoablation, histotripsy, external beam radiation therapy, systemic chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Surgical resection is typically reserved for patients with preserved liver function and a localized disease, which offers a 5-year survival rate of up to 70–80%.14 Patients who do not meet the criteria for the surgical resection, liver transplantation may be considered, with a 10-year survival rate of 50–60% and a recurrence rate of 10–20%.15

Many staging systems have been proposed over the years to guide management decisions. The AASLD endorses the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system, as it uniquely combines three independent predictors of survival: radiologic tumor burden, liver function, and patient performance status. This makes BCLC one of the comprehensive and widely used systems.2

Surgical resection remains one of the main curative options for HCC, particularly in patients with preserved liver function. Over the last two decades, the field of liver surgery has witnessed a major shift toward minimally invasive procedures, including laparoscopic liver resection (LLR) and, more recently, robotic liver resection (RLR). These techniques have been increasingly implemented due to their association with reduced blood loss, fewer complications, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery when compared to the traditional open surgery.16,17

The intuitive robotic platform offers 3-dimensional (3D)-magnified visualization, tremor infiltration, proper dexterity through wrist articulation, and greater surgeon control, particularly useful in anatomically challenging regions like the caudate lobe.18 Furthermore, the learning curve for robotic liver surgery is less steep than for laparoscopy, making it more accessible for surgeons who are new to minimally invasive hepatic procedures.19,20 Although RLR demands institutional resources and advanced training, it provides a proper flexibility and can be safely performed even in patients with underlying cirrhosis when performed by experienced teams.21–23

In this report, we present a rare case of a caudate lobe HCC in an early cirrhotic patient that was successfully resected with a robotic assisted procedure. Through this case and literature review, we aim to highlight the evolving role of robotic liver surgery in managing complex hepatic tumors and outline the considerations and challenges that are critical for a safe application of this approach.

Case Description

A 70-year-old male with a medical history of chronic hepatitis C infection, compensated cirrhosis, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hypertension was referred to our transplant and hepatobiliary center following the incidental discovery of a liver lesion during a routine surveillance imaging. The patient was diagnosed with hepatitis C in early 2022 and completed a three-month course of antiviral therapy, which achieved a sustained virologic response. He was diagnosed with cirrhosis in February 2022 and had been under regular gastroenterology follow-up, but he was lately lost follow-up.

At the time of presentation, he was asymptomatic. His body mass index (BMI) was 23.9 kg/m2, and physical examination was unremarkable. Family history was notable for numerous malignancies, including a father who died from gastrointestinal cancer at age 53, a sister who died from endometrial cancer at age 35, and a brother with HCC who underwent liver transplantation. Social history was negative for alcohol use, tobacco use, recreational drug use, occupational, or chemical exposures. He reported only occasional caffeine intake. His current medications included atorvastatin, losartan, metformin, and glimepiride. The abdominal ultrasound and CT scan (Figures 1A–D) revealed a 4.6-cm hypoechoic mass in the caudate lobe of the liver, with mild hepatic parenchymal coarsening and subtle surface irregularity. The study also demonstrated extensive cholelithiasis without biliary ductal dilatation. Doppler evaluation showed patent and normally directed portal and hepatic veins.

(A) Ultrasound illustrates a 4.6-cm caudate lobe mass. (B) Gallbladder with multiple echogenic foci. (C) Coronal CT view of gallstones. (D) Axial CT view of gallstones, consistent with extensive cholelithiasis.

A comprehensive laboratory evaluation was performed. AFP was within normal limit, and the synthetic liver function was preserved, including normal albumin, AST, ALT, ALP, PT, PTT, INR, and total bilirubin, consistent with Child-Pugh Class A cirrhosis (MELD score of 6). Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) revealed only mild gastritis. Cross-sectional imaging with MRI revealed a 4.2 × 4.0 cm lesion in segment I (caudate lobe) of the liver, and demonstrated heterogeneous T1 and T2 signal, moderate hepatic steatosis, and arterial enhancement with relative central washout and rim enhancement, the findings radiologically were consistent with HCC (Figures 2A and 2B).

(A) Coronal MRI depicts a caudate lobe lesion with heterogeneous signal and rim enhancement (49.6 mm cephalocaudal). (B) Axial MRI view demonstrates the lesion measuring 42 × 40 mm (anteroposterior × transverse).

Triphasic contrast-enhanced CT characterized the lesion as a hyper vascular mass, which measured 5 × 4.2 × 4 cm (cephalocaudal × anteroposterior × transverse), and revealed mild mass effect on the inferior vena cava (IVC) posteriorly and the main portal vein anteriorly (Figures 3A and 3B). CT-guided needle biopsy of the normal right hepatic lobe was performed. Histopathologic evaluation showed chronic hepatitis C with grade 2 portal and lobular inflammation, mild steatosis, and stage 2 fibrosis (Batts-Ludwig).

(A) Coronal contrast-enhanced CT image shows a caudate lobe mass in the cephalocaudal dimension (50 mm). (B) Axial view of the same lesion (42 × 40 mm) with rim enhancement and mild compression of the IVC and main portal vein.

As part of the metastatic workup, a nuclear medicine bone scan was performed using 20 mCi of technetium-99m-labeled methylene diphosphonate (MDP). The scan revealed no abnormal radiotracer uptake in either axial or appendicular skeleton, indicating the absence of osseous metastatic disease. Additionally, bilateral renal activity was preserved, and no extraskeletal tracer accumulation was observed. These findings, along with the remainder of the metastatic evaluation, confirmed there was no evidence of distant metastasis.

The case was reviewed at the Hepatopancreatobiliary Conference. Surgical resection was recommended based on the preserved liver function and the localized tumor without a vascular invasion. Because of the tumor’s deep location in the caudate lobe near the major vessels, a robotic approach was selected to improve the visualization and wrist articulation.

The patient was placed in the supine position on a tilt table and he was properly padded and sequentials applied to lower extremities. Then he was intubated and placed under general anesthesia. To optimize exposure of the caudate mass, a Nathanson retractor was placed in the right upper quadrant to elevate the lateral segment of the left hepatic lobe, and then a robotic standard cholecystectomy was performed. Indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging was utilized to identify the biliary anatomy, including the cystic duct and common bile duct.

Following cholecystectomy, the gastrohepatic ligament was dissected, and portahepatis lymphadenectomy was performed, which was negative for metastasis. Then, the peritoneum anterior to the infrahepatic IVC was incised, and dissection proceeded medially to allow elevation of the caudate lobe off the retrohepatic vena cava. Vessels supplying the mass, including draining veins and portal branches, were individually clipped and transected.

The mass was gradually mobilized from surrounding structures and adhesions. SynchroSeal was used for hepatic parenchymal transection, and hemostasis was achieved throughout the procedure. A single piece of surgical SNoW (Ethicon, LLC San Lorenzo, Puerto Rico) was placed over the anterior surface of the IVC, followed by placement of a Jackson-Pratt drain.

The total operative time was 91 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 60 mL. DISCUSSION

RLR is increasingly being implemented as a minimally invasive procedure and an alternative option to the traditional open liver resection (OLR) and the LLR, especially in the anatomically challenging liver segments such as caudate lobe (segment I). The caudate lobe is anatomically challenging due to its deep, posterior location and its closeness to the major vascular structures, including IVC, portal vein, and hepatic veins.17 Traditionally, tumors in this region were considered technically difficult to access laparoscopically and often reserved for open surgery. However, advances in the robotic systems, especially with the da Vinci-5 platform, have enabled surgeons to overcome these limitations and safely perform resections in such high-risk areas.

Our case report with cirrhosis and HCC in the caudate lobe exemplifies this shift. Despite the tumor’s difficult location that is surrounded by the IVC, portal, and hepatic structures, the robotic resection was performed uneventfully. This highlights the growing availability of RLR for tumors once considered inoperable or only approachable by open surgery. Expertise is an important factor of success in the robotic liver surgery, particularly when managing tumors in posterior regions.18 While RLR demands technical skill and familiarity with intra-abdominal navigation, the learning curve has been reported to be significantly less steep than LLR, which is estimated at approximately 30 cases for RLR compared to 60 cases for LLR, making it a more accessible option for experienced surgeons transitioning from the open or laparoscopic techniques.19,20 When comparing minimally invasive approaches, robotic surgery offers numerous technical advantages over standard laparoscopy.18 It provides a better navigation through 3D visualization, wristed instrument articulation, tremor filtration, and better ergonomics for the surgeon.18 These characteristics are particularly beneficial when dissecting around critical vascular structures, as in our case. As demonstrated in this report, the robotic approach resulted in a shorter operative time (91 minutes), minimal blood loss (60 mL), and no need for a Pringle maneuver, all of which showcase the efficiency and safety of the robotic platform in skilled hands.

Another notable benefit of minimally invasive procedures is reduced postoperative morbidity.23–25 Our patient tolerated the procedure well, resumed clear diet oral intake by the evening of the same operation day, and the patient was discharged by day two. These outcomes are consistent with published literature showing that RLR is associated with similar or even better hospital stays, postoperative pain control, and recovery compared to OLR and LLR.16,17

While concerns remain about the cost-effectiveness of robotic platforms due to equipment and maintenance costs, some studies suggest that these costs may be balanced by shorter hospitalizations, lower complication rates, and faster recovery times, especially in high-volume centers.22 Furthermore, the common surgical complications include bleeding, vascular injury, biliary leak, hematoma, parenchymal liver injury, and most importantly air embolism.22,25 Additionally, open conversion, which is a rare procedural mishaps, is an additional challenge associated with the robotic procedures. The factors leading to open conversion are the same technical and anatomical factors associated with the minimally invasive approaches, namely uncontrollable bleeding, vascular anomaly, poor operative field visualization, extensive adhesions from prior operations, and incomplete oncologic margin resection in addition to the posterior localization of the caudate lobe.19–23 We recommend to encircle both the porta hepatis and the infrahepatic IVC with vessel loops in addition to having vascular clamps readily available to ensure immediate access with proper vascular control. Additionally, the liver biopsy of the remnant liver showed a normal histology, which was a supportive finding to proceed with a robotic resection and avoided the requirement of open liver surgery and/or an invasive intervention such as liver transplantation.

Initially, the RLRs were limited to peripheral liver segments, however, the increasing experience has allowed surgeons to utilize the robotic platform for more complex hepatectomies, donor hepatectomies, and resections in previously “forbidden” zones such as segments I, VII, and VIII.37,38 As a result, the robotic resection should still be pursued cautiously in patients with a poor liver reserve and advanced tumor burden. In our case, the patient had compensated cirrhosis (Child-Pugh A) and a solitary localized lesion that had the ideal characteristics for a surgical intervention.

Importantly, the contraindications of robotic liver surgery are not always absolute, and the main limiting factors are institutional resources, surgeon expertise, and patient-specific anatomical considerations.39 Our case challenges the conventional limitations by demonstrating that even posteriorly located HCC in the caudate lobe can be safely resected robotically when performed by a skilled team. Furthermore, the short-term outcomes of RLR are comparable to those of LLR, with similar rates of intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complications, oncologic margins, and length of hospital stay.39,40

Finally, the adoption of robotic technology continues to expand, and it is driven by the patient demand for a less invasive surgery, better cosmesis, and the surgeon’s pursuit of better perioperative outcome. However, long-term oncologic outcomes and comparative studies remain limited, and more high-quality, multi-institutional prospective randomized clinical studies are needed to further validate the role of RLR, particularly in patients with centrally located tumors.41, 42

CONCLUSION

This case highlights the safety of the robotic resection for HCC in the caudate lobe, a region often considered anatomically challenging due to its closeness to major vascular structures. Despite the high-risk location, the tumor was successfully resected without vascular injury or open conversion, which highlights the importance of surgical expertise. RLR offers numerous key advantages over laparoscopic and open approaches, including enhanced visualization, wrist articulation, reduced blood loss, shorter operative time, and a lower conversion rate. With a lesser learning curve and proper patient selection, robotic surgery is gaining wider acceptance. However, further prospective randomized studies and long-term follow-up are needed to better define its role, especially in complex hepatobiliary cases. This case adds to the growing body of evidence, and it supports robotic resection as a safe and feasible option for tumors in anatomically challenging locations.
