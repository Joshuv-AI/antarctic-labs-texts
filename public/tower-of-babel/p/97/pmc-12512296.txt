Advancing quadruple helix theory for health systems innovation: evidence from Ageing@Coimbra
Authors: Alessandra Ricciardelli, Lorenzo Mercurio, Claudia Salvatore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocietal challenges in public health call for innovation to enhance health outcomes and equity. This study explores the application of the Quadruple Helix (QH) model—engaging academia, government, industry, and civil society—in driving healthcare innovation at the regional level.MethodsA qualitative case study approach was adopted, focusing on the Ageing@Coimbra Reference Site. Data sources included policy reports, secondary literature, and twelve semi-structured interviews with key stakeholders. A thematic analysis was conducted to examine tangible and intangible outputs and the role of local ecosystem dynamics.ResultsThe findings reveal that Ageing@Coimbra leveraged QH collaboration to generate both tangible outcomes (e.g., integrated care models, digital health tools) and intangible benefits (e.g., civic engagement, patient empowerment). The local ecosystem played a critical role in aligning multi-sectoral actors and enabling participatory governance.ConclusionsThe QH model emerges as a viable framework for advancing regional healthcare innovation. Ageing@Coimbra illustrates how local embeddedness and cross-sectoral trust can foster scalable, community-driven solutions. The study provides actionable insights for policymakers aiming to replicate similar strategies elsewhere.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13450-w.

Introduction

In the field of public health, demographic transformations—particularly population ageing—are exerting growing pressure on health systems, challenging their capacity to deliver equitable and sustainable care [1, 2]. These changes amplify existing health disparities and require innovative approaches that transcend traditional sectoral boundaries [3]. Health equality and equity, while distinct concepts, remain interrelated priorities: equality ensures fair access to healthcare services for all individuals, whereas equity acknowledges diverse needs and circumstances, advocating for tailored support to achieve comparable health outcomes [4–6].

Demographic transformation and population ageing are not only national but also global challenges, increasingly at the center of international health and policy debates. The World Health Organization has identified population ageing as one of the defining megatrends of the 21st century, calling for systemic innovation to ensure health systems remain resilient, inclusive, and sustainable (WHO, 2021). The United Nations Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030) further underscores the urgency of adopting participatory and multi-actor approaches, emphasizing that responses to ageing must go beyond healthcare to engage governments, academia, industry, and civil society in coordinated action. In this broader policy discourse, innovation frameworks such as the Quadruple Helix are gaining recognition as effective models for mobilizing diverse stakeholders, fostering social innovation, and aligning technological and organizational change with the lived experiences of older populations. International precedents, including European Reference Sites within the EIP on AHA, as well as cross-sectoral initiatives in Canada, Japan, and the Nordic countries, illustrate how QH-inspired collaborations can generate both tangible innovations—such as digital health solutions and integrated care models—and intangible outcomes, including strengthened governance, citizen empowerment, and enhanced social cohesion. Positioning the QH within this international landscape reinforces its theoretical and practical relevance, showing that it responds directly to the global call for transformative, collaborative strategies to address the complex challenges of ageing societies.

Addressing such complex challenges calls for novel frameworks capable of integrating diverse societal actors and knowledge systems. The Quadruple Helix (QH) model, as an evolution of the seminal Triple Helix framework [7, 8], extends traditional innovation paradigms by adding civil society as a fourth helix alongside academia, government, and industry [9–11]. This inclusion allows innovation processes to be more democratic, socially embedded, and responsive to local needs [12–15]. Within the QH framework, innovation is conceptualized as a co-created process where knowledge circulates among stakeholders, enabling multi-dimensional progress in economic, environmental, and social domains [16, 17].

Despite its conceptual appeal, there is still a paucity of empirical studies examining how QH dynamics operate in health systems and what types of benefits—tangible (e.g., technologies, services) and intangible (e.g., empowerment, governance inclusiveness)—they produce [18, 19]. Moreover, the enabling role of local ecosystems in supporting such collaborative innovation remains underexplored, particularly in regions facing demographic and socio-economic vulnerabilities [20]. This study aims to fill this gap by investigating how the QH model contributes to health system innovation and how local ecosystem dynamics facilitate its implementation.

The Ageing@Coimbra Reference Site, part of the European Innovation Partnership on Active and Healthy Ageing (EIP on AHA), provides an exemplary context for exploring these issues. As a pioneering QH-based initiative in Portugal, Ageing@Coimbra has engaged academia, government, industry, and civil society in co-designing innovative solutions to address ageing-related challenges. Its participatory governance model and cross-sectoral collaborations offer valuable insights into the potential of QH for regional healthcare innovation.

This study is guided by the following research questions:

RQ1

What are the benefits of adopting the QH model as an approach to trigger innovation in the health sector?

RQ2

What is the role of local ecosystems in implementing the QH as an approach that leads to innovation?

The remainder of this article is structured as follows: Sect. 2 presents the background of the study; Sect. 3 describes the methodology; Sect. 4 presents the results; Sect. 5 provides the discussion, conclusions and outlines limitations and directions for future research.

Background

The Quadruple Helix (QH) model extends the seminal Triple Helix framework by adding civil society to the traditional triad of university, industry and government, thus conceptualizing innovation as a co-created process that draws simultaneously on scientific knowledge, market forces, public policy and citizens’ lived experience [11, 12]. This fourth helix broadens the locus of problem-solving and embeds innovation more deeply in local culture and values, making the approach especially relevant for complex public-health challenges. Alternative lenses—such as the Learning Health System [8], Integrated Care [9] and Collective Impact [10] models—offer valuable insights into data feedback loops, service coordination and cross-sector alignment, yet they focus primarily on intra-system improvement or rely on a single backbone organisation. By contrast, the QH framework accommodates polycentric, distributed governance, mirroring the structure of the Ageing @ Coimbra initiative, where semi-autonomous actors work toward shared goals without a central authority.

While a range of theoretical lenses can illuminate innovation in health, the Quadruple Helix arguably provides the most fitting perspective for initiatives like Ageing @ Coimbra. Competing models—such as the Learning Health System, which emphasises continuous data-driven improvement, Integrated Care, which focuses on aligning providers to improve patient journeys, or Complex Adaptive Systems, which foreground emergent properties and interdependence—offer valuable analytical tools but remain largely intra-systemic or abstract. The Triple Helix tradition adds a strong emphasis on knowledge–economy linkages but omits the societal dimension that is critical in health innovation. By contrast, the QH framework explicitly integrates civil society as an active helix, acknowledging that citizens’ lived experiences and local values are not peripheral but central drivers of system change. This broader scope makes QH uniquely capable of capturing both the technical and the democratic aspects of health-system transformation, where legitimacy, trust, and co-ownership are as crucial as efficiency or coordination. In this sense, QH functions not as a substitute for other models but as a higher-order frame that embeds and extends their insights within a more participatory and territorially grounded logic of innovation.

Although some scholars have proposed a Quintuple Helix framework that integrates environmental and ecological considerations into innovation processes, our analysis deliberately focuses on the Quadruple Helix. This choice reflects both the aims of Ageing @ Coimbra and the scope of this study: while ecological sustainability is unquestionably important, the initiative under examination is primarily concerned with social and health-system innovation rather than environmental transitions. References to “ecosystems” in this paper are therefore used in the organizational and territorial sense—denoting interdependent actors, institutions and norms—rather than in the ecological sense. By clarifying this boundary, we maintain conceptual consistency while recognising the value of the Quintuple Helix for contexts where environmental dynamics are central drivers of innovation.

Historically, QH evolved out of Triple-Helix scholarship on university–industry–government dynamics and of Carayannis & Campbell’s “Mode 3” thesis, which emphasises plural knowledge-production logics and territorial embeddedness [13]. In this spiral system, knowledge circulates through four helices to advance social and economic progress [14, 15], while the interplay of multiple stakeholders generates micro-level collaboration dynamics within innovation ecosystems [16]. Recent policy documents highlight the need for open, co-creative mechanisms that harness this multi-actor potential; however, existing studies seldom move beyond conceptual discussion [17–20]. Empirical work remains fragmented—often limited to start-up ecosystems, technology transfer or specific business-model applications [21–26]—and rarely addresses the health sector, where only isolated evaluations of QH processes have been reported [14, 33–36].

In this study, governance is understood as the ongoing process of negotiated coordination among interdependent actors, rather than a fixed governmental hierarchy. We use local innovation ecosystem to denote the territorially bounded constellation of organisations, resources and social norms that shape how those actors collaborate. Co-creation refers to the joint design of solutions—where needs are discussed and prototypes conceived—whereas co-production denotes their joint delivery and implementation once a design has been agreed. Distinguishing these concepts clarifies both the analytical lens and the mechanisms examined in the subsequent sections.

The overlap between QH and Open Innovation is frequently noted: both value external knowledge flows and multi-stakeholder engagement [20, 27]. Yet QH adds an explicit democratic dimension by foregrounding citizen participation and public value, a feature that makes it particularly suitable for health-system transformation. Despite growing interest, robust evidence on the tangible (e.g., digital tools, integrated-care pathways) and intangible (e.g., trust, empowerment) benefits of QH collaborations in healthcare—and on the local ecosystem conditions that enable them—remains scarce [37–39]. By analysing Ageing @ Coimbra, this study aims to sharpen the theoretical foundation of QH, supply missing empirical data and clarify how territorial context shapes multi-actor innovation in health systems.

Methods

This study employs a single case-study design appropriate for investigating complex, context-dependent phenomena in emerging fields of inquiry [40–43]. We selected Ageing@Coimbra because it is the first European Innovation Partnership on Active and Healthy Ageing (EIP-AHA) Reference Site explicitly organised around a Quadruple Helix (QH) governance configuration and because its activities have generated peer-reviewed documentation of ageing- and cognitive-health–related innovations [7]. The initiative also exhibits a polycentric, citizen-anchored governance structure that aligns directly with our interest in how local ecosystems enable QH collaboration. Our methodological protocol followed established qualitative case-study standards, combining documentary review with semi-structured interviews in order to triangulate sources and capture diverse stakeholder perspectives. As researchers, we maintained an external but engaged role: none of the authors were formally affiliated with the Ageing@Coimbra consortium, yet we had prior exposure to its activities through European Reference Site networks. This position allowed for informed but independent observation, reducing risks of insider bias while ensuring familiarity with the institutional context.

Two of the authors were directly involved in the interview process. Access to Ageing@Coimbra was facilitated through a colleague in the European Reference Site Collaborative Network (RSCN), who introduced the research team to the consortium. This proximity supported contextual familiarity and facilitated participant recruitment, but also required careful attention to reflexivity. To mitigate potential bias, coding and analysis were undertaken collaboratively, with cross-checks between members more and less directly connected to the Reference Site.

The consortium operates under a memorandum of understanding endorsed by the Regional Health Authority, which grants it authority to pilot innovations within public primary-care units. Academia is represented by the University of Coimbra and the Pedro Nunes Institute, whose legal mandate allows direct knowledge-transfer contracts with SMEs. Industry partners—mostly digital-health start-ups clustered in the local incubator—benefit from fast-track procurement rules introduced by the 2019 Portuguese Innovation Decree, enabling them to test prototypes in public facilities for up to twelve months. Municipal governments contribute zoning powers and small-grant budgets earmarked for age-friendly infrastructure, while civil-society organisations (senior universities, patient associations) exercise agenda-setting influence through two seats on the consortium’s steering committee. Together, these levers create a polycentric but formalised governance environment that both empowers co-creation and ensures regulatory compliance.

Portugal’s institutional setting provides an especially fertile backdrop for analysing health-policy innovation. While the Serviço Nacional de Saúde (SNS) remains centrally financed, significant competencies for health promotion, primary care delivery, and ageing-related programmes have been delegated to regional health administrations and municipalities, creating opportunities for decentralised experimentation. Policy instruments such as the 2019 Innovation Decree and the National Programme for Healthy Ageing explicitly encourage pilot projects that involve universities, local governments, SMEs, and civil-society actors. This multi-level framework aligns with European Union priorities, notably the EIP-AHA Reference Site model, but Portugal has distinguished itself by embedding QH-inspired governance into statutory instruments rather than relying solely on soft coordination or project-based collaboration. Compared with more centralised systems (e.g., France) or those with stronger insurance-based pluralism (e.g., Germany, the Netherlands), Portugal represents a hybrid case where decentralised authority and targeted innovation policies converge. Against this backdrop, Ageing@Coimbra exemplifies how a territorially anchored, citizen-inclusive ecosystem can translate European policy ambitions into locally meaningful health-system innovations.

Data derive from two complementary sources: (i) documentary materials (policy reports, consortium records, and the published Frontiers in Medicine case description of Ageing@Coimbra) covering the period 2013–2025 [7]; and (ii) twelve semi-structured interviews conducted between March and April 2025 with stakeholders directly involved in the initiative. We used purposive, maximum-variation sampling to ensure that all four helices were represented. Interviews were completed with three participants from academia, three from government / public authorities, three from industry / technology or health-related enterprises, and three from civil-society organisations (community associations, patient / senior groups). The twelve interviewees represented a broad cross-section of the ecosystem. Academic participants included a senior gerontology researcher, a digital-health scientist, and a knowledge-transfer manager. Government/public authority representatives comprised two municipal health directors and a regional health-administration official. Industry voices came from two SME founders in the digital-health incubator and a product manager in a telemedicine start-up. Civil-society participants included leaders of senior universities, a patient-association chair, and a coordinator of community volunteers. This profile ensured that both strategic decision-makers and operational implementers were captured across all four helices. Eligibility required an active role in Ageing@Coimbra decision-making or programme implementation and the ability to comment on cross-sector collaboration. Recruitment proceeded until thematic saturation—defined a priori as the point at which two consecutive interviews no longer yielded new first-order codes relevant to our research questions—was observed after interview 10 and confirmed in interviews 11 and 12.

Interviews, lasting 45–75 min, were conducted in the participant’s preferred language (Portuguese or English) via secure video call or in person. A common guide covered seven domains: respondent role; organisational contribution to QH collaboration; perceived tangible and intangible benefits; collaboration challenges and inter-organisational dynamics; knowledge-sharing and governance practices; the role of the local ecosystem; and future perspectives for QH in healthcare innovation. With permission, all interviews were audio-recorded and professionally transcribed verbatim. Bilingual members of the research team checked each transcript against the audio for accuracy; where needed, Portuguese passages were translated into English and back-checked for meaning preservation. Participants were offered the opportunity to review and amend their transcript (member checking); minor clarifications were incorporated. The interview process was designed to facilitate open reflection. After presenting the information sheet and obtaining written consent, participants were encouraged to narrate both successes and tensions in their collaborations. Probing questions sought concrete examples of governance practices, co-creation challenges, and perceptions of ecosystem enablers. Field notes were taken to capture non-verbal cues and contextual observations, complementing the verbatim transcripts.

Analysis combined deductive sensitising concepts drawn from QH scholarship with inductive coding informed by constructivist grounded-theory principles [44–50]. First, two researchers independently read all transcripts and developed an initial open-coding frame anchored in the research questions (QH benefits; ecosystem role). Codes were iteratively compared, merged, and expanded to incorporate emergent categories (e.g., alignment challenges, distributed governance, empowerment). We then organised codes into seven higher-order thematic categories: (i) knowledge sharing, (ii) transformative collaboration, (iii) alignment challenges, (iv) patient-centred outcomes, (v) community engagement, (vi) distributed governance, and (vii) empowerment and inclusiveness. These categories were iteratively validated against both documentary evidence and the interview corpus, serving as the analytical scaffold for the Results section. By defining these clusters upfront, we make transparent how raw interview data were systematically connected to our conceptual claims. Documentary materials [7] were coded in the same NVivo project to support data triangulation and to trace temporal sequencing of initiatives referenced by interviewees. To enhance reliability, two coders double-coded four transcripts (~ 33% of the corpus); discrepancies were discussed until consensus and the codebook was revised accordingly. Given the small qualitative sample and our emphasis on interpretive depth, we did not calculate a formal inter-coder kappa; instead, agreement was established through negotiated resolution and full-team review of theme definitions.

Credibility was supported through triangulation of interview and documentary evidence, member-checking of transcripts, and maintenance of an audit trail documenting analytic decisions. Transferability is aided by providing thick contextual description in Sect. 4.1 and Supplementary Appendix A. Dependability and confirmability were strengthened by reflexive memos in which researchers recorded positionality, assumptions, and interactions with data throughout the study. The research involved professional stakeholders speaking in their institutional capacities and did not solicit personal health information or sensitive individual data; under the policies in effect at the authors’ institutions, such minimal-risk social-science interviewing was exempt from formal ethics-board review and therefore did not receive a protocol number. Nevertheless, we adhered to recognised ethical standards: all invitees received an information sheet describing the study purpose, voluntary participation, data anonymisation, and withdrawal rights; written informed consent was obtained prior to each interview; audio files and transcripts were pseudonymised and stored on encrypted drives in compliance with GDPR-aligned data-protection procedures.

Analysis of the twelve interviews and supporting documents yielded seven benefit themes: three tangible and four intangible (Table 1). Tangible benefits comprise (i) digital-health technologies such as the large-scale deployment of the MASK-rhinitis app, (ii) patient-centred service platforms co-designed by municipal health units and SMEs, and (iii) community health interventions—including the SIMPATHY polypharmacy toolkit—that have been replicated in six neighbouring municipalities. Intangible benefits include trust among stakeholders, increased health literacy, civic engagement, and distributed leadership. These outcomes emerge from a recurring three-stage cycle of agenda-setting, joint solution design and negotiated implementation.

Two cross-cutting mechanisms explain how the local ecosystem converted QH inputs into the benefits summarised above. First, deep territorial embeddedness aligned project priorities with the demographic and socio-cultural realities of central Portugal. As one civil-society leader observed, “We could tailor frailty screening to the rhythms of rural parishes—that wouldn’t fly in Lisbon” (R06-C). Second, a practice of structured joint agenda-setting reduced fragmentation across helices. Monthly “quad-labs” convened municipal officials, senior-association delegates, start-ups and university researchers; disagreements about data governance or procurement speed were resolved through weighted voting, allowing what one industry participant called “a slower but safer code base” (R08-I).

Contrary to the view of a frictionless partnership, interviewees described continuous power negotiation. A government official noted that differing timelines “sometimes made decision-making hard” (R08-G), while an SME complained of “limited voice when hospital lawyers enter the room” (R11-I). Yet these tensions proved generative: the final tele-monitoring protocol integrates both regulatory safeguards and agile development sprints, illustrating how conflict management rather than consensus per se drives innovation in a QH setting.

Four stakeholder groups—civil society, academia, industry and government—provide complementary inputs that converge in a central stage of collaboration and co-creation. This process generates two parallel outcome streams: on the left, tangible outputs such as digital-health technologies, patient-centred platforms and community health interventions; on the right, intangible outputs including trust among stakeholders, increased health literacy and civic engagement. Although presented sequentially, the model implies a virtuous feedback loop in which intangible gains reinforce subsequent rounds of collaboration, sustaining and amplifying the production of tangible solutions over time. These results demonstrate that Ageing@Coimbra does more than deliver isolated pilot projects; it sustains an iterative, feedback-rich innovation cycle in which intangible social capital amplifies the reach of tangible health solutions. The findings extend QH theory in three important ways. First, they empirically demonstrate how intangible enablers—trust, health literacy, civic engagement—function as causal amplifiers rather than incidental by-products, accelerating diffusion and adoption of innovations. Second, they show how polycentric governance practices such as weighted voting and negotiated compromise allow collaborative innovation to harness tensions productively, translating potential power asymmetries into balanced, regulatory-compliant solutions. Third, they highlight the role of territorial embeddedness as an active mechanism, where shared lived experience, dense local networks, and micro-resource mobilisation transform abstract collaboration into citizen-trusted health interventions. In this sense, the case contributes a process-based refinement of Quadruple-Helix theory, illustrating not just who participates in innovation, but how iterative feedback, negotiated governance, and place-based social capital together sustain measurable health-system change.

Discussion and conclusions

This case study shows how a Quadruple-Helix (QH) innovation cycle can operate in a mature health system: stakeholder inputs converge in a co-creation arena, generate complementary streams of tangible and intangible outcomes, and then feedback to reinforce the next round of collaboration. In doing so, the findings extend prior QH and collaborative-innovation research in three specific ways thereby refining the theoretical foundations of the model and offering clearer guidance for practice.

First, they refine the causal architecture of QH models. Earlier studies typically describe static quadruple partnerships [11, 12] or stress the importance of open knowledge flows [28, 29]. Our data instead highlight a dynamic, iterative loop in which intangible assets—trust, civic engagement and health literacy—act as amplifiers that accelerate the diffusion and uptake of tangible solutions. This echoes, but also operationalises, the “collective-impact feedback” theorised by Kania & Kramer [10] and the learning cycles discussed in integrated-care research [9] turning abstract propositions into observable mechanisms.

Second, the results demonstrate how polycentric, negotiated governance can manage power asymmetries inside collaborative innovation networks. Whereas Bozeman et al. [16] and Miller et al. [21] warn that university–industry–government relations often privilege institutional incumbents, our evidence shows that the inclusion of organised civil society and the use of weighted voting in “quad-labs” tempered dominance by any single helix—producing solutions that satisfied both regulatory and entrepreneurial logics. This adds empirical precision to the Mode-3 proposition that innovation emerges from plural—and sometimes conflicting—knowledge-production regimes [13], thus extending its application to health-system contexts.

Third, the study specifies the mechanisms of local embeddedness that make QH collaboration effective. Similar to the ecosystem-based successes reported for start-up districts [22] and non-profit alliances [37, 38], Coimbra’s experience confirms that place-based social capital is an enabling condition rather than a peripheral context variable. In practical terms, three mechanisms of local embeddedness proved decisive. First, shared lived experience of demographic challenges (e.g., rural isolation, frailty, low digital literacy) created a common problem frame across helices, which reduced misalignment in agenda-setting. Second, dense inter-organisational networks—cultivated over years through university–municipality projects and civil-society mobilisation—provided relational trust that lowered the transaction costs of co-creation. Third, territorially grounded resource mobilisation, such as municipal micro-grants and in-kind contributions from senior associations, allowed small-scale experimentation that could be quickly adapted to citizen feedback. Together, these mechanisms translated abstract collaboration into solutions that citizens recognised as legitimate and relevant, offering policymakers concrete levers for supporting place-based innovation. In short, the case confirms that place-based social capital is an enabling condition rather than a peripheral context variable.

While the Coimbra case demonstrates how QH collaboration can generate mutually reinforcing tangible and intangible outcomes, international experiences suggest that such success is far from guaranteed. For instance, evaluations of the European Innovation Partnership on Active and Healthy Ageing (EIP-AHA) highlight that several Reference Sites in Central and Eastern Europe struggled to institutionalise QH practices due to limited civic capacity and uneven engagement from SMEs, which resulted in fragmented pilot projects with little scaling potential [51]. In Canada, attempts to mobilise QH-inspired health-innovation clusters have faced barriers related to misaligned incentives between provincial health authorities and private-sector actors, limiting sustained knowledge transfer [52]. Similarly, comparative studies of Nordic countries, otherwise known for strong welfare infrastructures, show that QH collaboration can stall when industry priorities, such as rapid marketisation of e-health solutions, conflict with public-sector logics of equity and accountability [53]. Even within Western Europe, university–industry–government platforms have been criticised for reproducing incumbent power structures, with civil-society participation often tokenised rather than empowered [8].

Placing Coimbra against this international backdrop underscores both the opportunities and the vulnerabilities of QH-driven innovation: its capacity to generate place-based solutions depends heavily on pre-existing social capital, institutional alignment, and mechanisms of governance that ensure civil society’s role is substantive rather than symbolic.

Societally, the case shows that QH collaboration can narrow health-equity gaps in two ways: first, by embedding end-user knowledge directly in solution design, and second, by raising the collective capacity of citizens to navigate new technologies. These effects go beyond generic calls for leadership or “participatory governance”; they specify how power can be shared and which soft capabilities need deliberate investment.

Limitations and avenues for future research

The single-case design, while suited to theory elaboration [41, 42], limits statistical generalisability. Replication in other demographic and institutional contexts is needed to test the enabling conditions identified here—namely, shared commitment, cultural alignment with collaborative logics and a resilient regional support infrastructure. Longitudinal follow-up would clarify whether intangible gains continue to reinforce tangible adoption over a decade-long horizon. Comparative studies across multiple Reference Sites could also explore whether the conflict-management mechanisms observed in Coimbra travel intact or require local adaptation. In addition, reliance on stakeholder narratives may introduce biases: participants tended to emphasise positive achievements of the consortium, and while triangulation with documents partly mitigated this, some degree of selective recall or self-presentation cannot be ruled out. Future research could address this by incorporating independent observations, patient-level outcomes, or comparative perspectives from non-participating actors. Beyond methodological considerations, the case also underscores intrinsic limitations of collaborative innovation itself. Multi-helix initiatives remain highly context-dependent, drawing on place-based social capital and governance traditions that may not be present elsewhere. Even when power asymmetries are managed, they are not eliminated, as resource-rich actors can still exert disproportionate influence. Moreover, the durability of intangible enablers such as trust and civic engagement is uncertain over longer horizons, raising questions about whether early momentum can be sustained. Finally, mechanisms such as weighted voting or time-boxed negotiations, while effective in Coimbra, may require substantial adaptation in other institutional settings.

Overall, the study contributes a process-based refinement of Quadruple-Helix theory, demonstrates its practical feasibility in a complex health ecosystem and offers a concrete managerial playbook for regions seeking to move from ad-hoc pilots to self-reinforcing innovation cycles.
