Real‐world implementation of lecanemab in Alzheimer's disease: A Japanese cohort
Authors: Sho Shimohama, Shogyoku Bun, Ryo Shikimoto, Hiroi Tomioka, Toshiki Takayama, Yu Mimura, Hiroki Sasaguri, Koji Sekiguchi, Yoshinori Nishimoto, Yu Iwabuchi, Hajime Tabuchi, Jin Nakahara, Masaru Mimura, Daisuke Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONReal‐world evidence for lecanemab remains limited, particularly among Asian populations. We evaluated the feasibility, safety, biomarker profiles, and short‐term clinical outcomes of lecanemab in practice in Japan.METHODSWe screened 321 patients with early Alzheimer's disease (AD) and confirmed amyloid pathology using amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) or cerebrospinal fluid analysis. Plasma amyloid‐β42/40 was assessed when available. Among these, 102 initiated lecanemab and underwent structured magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) surveillance with 6 months of follow‐up.RESULTSMost treated patients had a Clinical Dementia Rating–Global (CDR‐Global) score of 0.5 (96.1%), and 6‐month treatment persistence exceeded 85%. Amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), predominantly mild and asymptomatic ARIA‐H, occurred in 11.4%, whereas no cases of isolated ARIA with effusion or edema (ARIA‐E) were observed. One macrohemorrhagic and two moderate–severe radiographic ARIA with hemorrhage or hemosiderin deposition (ARIA‐H) developed, which led to discontinuation of the treatment. Cognitive and functional measures remained generally stable over 6 months, with changes comparable to those reported in the phase 3 CLARITY‐AD trial. Plasma–PET discordance highlighted the complementary value of multimodal biomarker interpretation.DISCUSSIONLecanemab was feasible and generally well tolerated under structured MRI monitoring in routine Japanese practice.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities, biomarker‐based monitoring, Japan, lecanemab, real‐world evidence

BACKGROUND

Amyloid‐β (Aβ) accumulation is central to the pathophysiology of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and has long been considered as a critical therapeutic target. 1 , 2 , 3 Monoclonal antibodies against aggregated Aβ have recently conferred clinically meaningful effects in early AD, marking a new era of disease‐modifying therapy. 4 Among them, lecanemab selectively binds Aβ protofibrils, and significantly reduced amyloid burden and slowed cognitive decline in the CLARITY‐AD trial. 5 More recently, donanemab, which targets N‐terminal pyroglutamate Aβ, has also demonstrated clinical efficacy and received regulatory approval, 6 further underscoring the paradigm shift toward amyloid‐targeted treatments.

Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of amyloid‐targeted antibodies provide high‐quality, efficacy and safety data, their strict inclusion criteria and highly controlled conditions limit generalizability to routine clinical practice. In particular, real‐world implementation raises practical challenges related to patient selection, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) surveillance, and the management of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA). These are the most clinically relevant safety concerns associated with anti‐amyloid therapy. Therefore, real‐world evidence (RWE) may provide important complementary insights into the implementation of these therapies in routine clinical practice. Emerging RWE from Israel, China and the USA, indicates that lecanemab treatment is feasible in clinical settings and that its safety profile is broadly comparable to trial data. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 A real‐world study in Japan found that higher baseline cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) phosphorylated tau levels were associated with greater cognitive decline and a higher incidence of ARIA during lecanemab treatment, underscoring the potential role of fluid‐based biomarkers in patient stratification treatment monitoring. 11 However, differences in patient characteristics, including disease stage and clinical practice profiles, emphasize the need for additional real‐world data reflecting routine diagnostic and monitoring workflows in Japan. Notably, the first Asian cohort reported from China included patients with moderate‐to‐severe AD (Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR] score ≥ 2 and Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE] score < 22). That report differed from Japanese MRI guidelines in terms of monitoring intervals and healthcare system‐specific factors, including out‐of‐pocket medical care. 9 , 10 , 12 Furthermore, real‐world populations often have greater comorbidity burdens and demographic diversity than RCTs, underscoring the importance of contextual evaluation. 13

Despite these important contributions, RWE about lecanemab remains limited. This gap is particularly evident in Asian populations, where differences in genetic risk factors, comorbidity profiles, and healthcare systems might influence efficacy and safety. Pooled analyses of clinical trial participants suggested a lower incidence of ARIA between cohorts that were Asian and others who were not. 14 In parallel, biomarker frameworks are rapidly evolving, with the 2024 update to the National Institute of Alzheimer's and Aging Association (NIA–AA) formally incorporating blood‐based biomarkers, including phosphorylated tau, into the AT(N) classification. 15 While plasma Aβ42/40 tests were examined as a supplementary element of the diagnostic workflow, our primary focus was to evaluate how lecanemab is implemented and monitored within real‐world diagnostic pathways, rather than to conduct a formal validation of biomarker performance.

Accordingly, this study aimed to provide novel RWE from a Japanese memory clinic setting, focusing on the feasibility, safety, and diagnostic and monitoring aspects of lecanemab in routine practice. We systematically characterized treatment eligibility, deferral, safety monitoring, and biomarker profiles. The present findings offer regiospecific insights relevant to the diagnosis‐, assessment‐, and monitoring‐oriented implementation of amyloid‐targeted disease‐modifying therapies.

METHODS

Protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consent

This single‐center, retrospective observational study was conducted at Keio University Hospital, a memory clinic in Tokyo, Japan. The study was conducted between February 2024 and September 2025. All assessments and treatments were performed as part of routine clinical care. The Institutional Review Board at Keio University approved this study (No. 20170315), which was registered in the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000057200). The study also complied with the Ethical principles enshrined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective study design and use of an opt‐out procedure. Informed consent for lecanemab treatment was obtained as part of standard clinical practice.

Patients

This study was conducted within the context of routine clinical practice according to the Japanese appropriate use guidelines for lecanemab. 12 Participants with suspected MCI due to AD or mild AD dementia who were considered eligible for lecanemab under Japanese criteria underwent further assessment and were enrolled in this cohort.

Structured neuropsychological testing and MRI were performed in 320 of the 321 screened individuals; one individual was unable to undergo MRI because of claustrophobia. Initial clinical eligibility criteria for inclusion in the present study included a MMSE score ≥ 22 and CDR‐Global scores of 0.5 or 1. Imaging eligibility criteria included four or fewer cerebral microhemorrhages and no subarachnoid or intracerebral hemorrhage > 1 cm in diameter.

Amyloid PET was performed using approved tracers, including 1 8F‐florbetapir, 1 8F‐flutemetamol, and 1 8F‐florbetaben. 16 , 17 apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotyping was performed in consenting individuals. Under Japanese recommendations, APOE ε4 homozygotes are eligible for lecanemab and were included in this study. Patients who were amyloid‐negative, had contraindications, declined therapy, or were outside regulatory indications were excluded.

Of the seven patients treated with donanemab during the study period, five individuals with MMSE scores of 20–21 were excluded as they did not meet the eligibility criteria for lecanemab. Two additional patients who met lecanemab eligibility criteria elected to receive donanemab and were excluded (Figure 1).

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were feasibility, safety, and tolerability of lecanemab in routine clinical practice. Feasibility was defined as treatment that persisted for > 6 months. Safety outcomes comprised ARIA incidence, subtype, severity, and other adverse events. Tolerability was assessed as treatment interruption or discontinuation due to adverse events.

Secondary outcomes included exploratory within‐patient changes in MMSE and Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) and descriptive MRI findings over 6 months. Other methods are described in the Supplementary Methods.

RESULTS

Patient enrollment and overall study population

We initially screened 321 individuals with MCI due to AD or mild AD dementia suspected by an attending clinician for potential lecanemab treatment at our memory clinic. Among those who were willing to try lecanemab treatment and deemed suitable for further eligibility assessment, 102 started lecanemab therapy and were followed for up to 6 months. All patients who received at least one infusion were included in the analyses of safety and treatment continuity. At baseline, 31/99 patients (31.3%) in the treated cohort were still engaged in paid employment (actively employed) at the time of lecanemab initiation. Figure 1 shows the flow of patient enrollment and screening.

Screening outcomes

Among the 321 screened individuals, one could not undergo MRI because of claustrophobia. The remaining 320 (99.7%) were screened by MRI and underwent comprehensive neuropsychological tests including MMSE and CDR, before starting lecanemab treatment.

Among these 320 individuals, 154 (48.1%) were excluded after MRI and cognitive assessments. The primary reasons for ineligibility included a low clinical probability of AD based on findings from brain MRI and structured neuropsychological testing (11/320, 3.4%), alternative conditions comprising vascular dementia (n = 4), non‐AD degenerative diseases (n = 3), major depressive disorder (n = 1), folate deficiency‐related cognitive impairment (n = 1), attention disorder (n = 1), and drug addiction (n = 1). The MRI findings incompatible with treatment safety (34/320, 10.6%), more than four cerebral microbleeds (n = 16), hemosiderin deposition (n = 8), cerebral hemorrhage > 1 cm (n = 7), previous cortical or subcortical infarctions (n = 3); CDR‐Global scores of 0 or normal cognitive test results (45/320, 14.1%); MMSE score ≤ 21 (28/320, 8.8%); declined treatment after MRI and neuropsychological tests (32/320, 10.0%), concerns about potential adverse effects (n = 15), aversion to frequent infusions (n = 10), preference for lifestyle modification or non‐pharmacologic approaches (n = 2), financial concerns (n = 2), and insufficient understanding of the treatment process (n = 3). Four patients (4/320, 1.3%) were excluded for prioritized treatment for heart failure, interruption of outpatient follow‐up, admission to a long‐term care facility, or extensive ischemic changes on MRI.

Among 166 (51.7%) of 321 patients who underwent amyloid biomarker assessment, 165 underwent amyloid PET imaging as the primary confirmatory test, whereas one underwent CSF Aβ analysis because amyloid PET was not immediately available.

Among the 165 patients who underwent amyloid PET imaging, 54 (32.5%) were amyloid PET‐negative, whereas eight (4.8%) were amyloid PET‐positive but declined treatment after amyloid pathology testing. The primary reasons for treatment refusal among these PET‐positive patients were concerns about potential adverse effects (n = 5), including concerns regarding ARIA risk after APOE ε4/ε4 genotyping (n = 3), preference for non‐pharmacologic approaches such as lifestyle modification (n = 2), and family opposition combined with concern about the burden of frequent infusions (n = 1). In addition, the single patient who underwent CSF Aβ analysis showed amyloid positivity but also declined treatment because of the burden associated with frequent infusions. Ultimately, 102 (31.8%) of 321 patients started lecanemab treatment, and 12.7% discontinued therapy during the 6‐month follow‐up (Figure 1).

Baseline disease severity was predominantly mild, with most patients having a CDR‐Global score of 0.5 (98 [96.1%] of 102), which was consistent with the early symptomatic stage of AD. Among the 219 (68.2%) of 321 patients who did not proceed to treatment, only age and sex were systematically recorded. The mean age of this group was 75.2 ± 9.9 years, and 119 (54.3%) were female.

The overall discordance rate among 141 patients between plasma Aβ42/40 and PET visual interpretation was 20 (14.2%) (Table 2). Among 99 amyloid PET‐positive patients who started lecanemab treatment, seven (7.1%) had plasma Aβ42/40 ratios above the cutoff. Therefore, treatment decisions for these patients were based on PET findings, which are prioritized under the current insurance system. Among the discordant cases, 11/141 patients (7.8%) showed plasma‐positive but PET‐negative findings (Table 2). Such discordance is biologically plausible, as fluid‐based amyloid measures might detect early amyloid abnormalities before fibrillar deposition reaches the threshold for PET visual positivity. 17 Among the plasma‐positive/PET‐negative patients, two subsequently underwent CSF assessment. One patient, with an amyloid PET Centiloid value of 8.3, was included in the treatment cohort based on a positive CSF result (Table S1), whereas another patient, with a Centiloid value of ‐0.9, showed a negative CSF result.

Characteristics and APOE genotype

Patients who provided written, informed consent (96 [91.4%] of 105) were genotyped for APOE. These included three who were amyloid PET‐positive but declined treatment. Those who consented to genetic tests were informed of counseling with a certified clinical geneticist, but none requested it. Among the 96 patients who were genotyped for APOE, 57.3% were ε4 carriers (47.9% heterozygous and 9.4% homozygous), and 42.7% were not carriers. Among the homozygous carriers, two canceled because of ARIA concerns. Table 1 details the genotype distribution.

Overview of safety outcomes, including ARIA and infusion‐related reactions

Among patients evaluated within 6 months of treatment initiation, eight of 70 patients (11.4%) developed ARIA. The denominator for ARIA assessment included patients who underwent serial MRI surveillance during treatment, including individuals who developed ARIA before reaching the 6‐month follow‐up time point. Table 3 shows an overview of ARIA incidence, severity, and symptomatology. Most events comprised isolated ARIA‐H (10.0%), whereas combined ARIA with effusion or edema (ARIA‐E) + H occurred in 1.4%, but ARIA‐E was not isolated. Most ARIA was radiologically mild and asymptomatic and was detected during scheduled MRI surveillance, at predefined monitoring time points before five, seven, or 14 infusions. One macrohemorrhagic and two moderate‐severe radiographic ARIA‐H in 3 (37.5%) of eight patients led to treatment discontinuation, including one with symptoms that required temporary hospitalization. 18 One additional patient with mild ARIA‐H (2.5%) discontinued therapy because of advanced age. The remaining patients, including one with mild combined ARIA‐E + H, continued lecanemab under careful monitoring. Among seven of eight patients with ARIA, 3 (42.9%) were ε4 carriers (heterozygous n = 1; homozygous, n = 2).

Infusion‐related reactions occurred in 28 (27.5%) of 102 patients. The most frequently reported symptoms were fever (14.7%), rash (3.9%), fatigue (3.9%), headache (2.9%), and dizziness (2.0%) during or shortly after infusion. Most events were mild to moderate and were managed with temporary infusion interruption or symptomatic treatment. However, 2.0% of patients discontinued therapy because of persistent infusion reactions; however, no events were life‐threatening.

Treatment discontinuation

Most of the 13 (12.7%) of 102 patients who discontinued lecanemab were ARIA‐related and occurred close to the time of scheduled MRI surveillance. Discontinuations that were unrelated to treatment were distributed during follow‐up. The most common reasons were ARIA (30.8%), infusion‐related reactions (15.4%), and intercurrent medical illnesses requiring treatment (3.1%), including one with ischemic stroke and one who died of causes that were not associated with treatment. Some patients discontinued treatment due to financial burden or a perceived lack of cost‐effectiveness (23.1%), while others discontinued due to relocation to distant areas that prevented continued follow‐up at our center (7.7%). No discontinuations were attributed to progressive cognitive decline, and all affected patients remained under clinical follow‐up.

Longitudinal changes in cognitive and functional performance during 6 months

At the time of analysis, 23 patients had not yet reached the 6‐month follow‐up time point. Among the 66 patients who completed 6 months of treatment, the mean age was 75.7 ± 8.7 years, and 40 (60.6%) of 66 were female. Cognitive and functional measures remained largely stable during the study period. The CDR‐Global score increased in four (6.1%) patients from 0.5 to 1.0, one improved from 0.5 to 0, and the remainder did not change. The mean MMSE score modestly decreased from 25.0 ± 2.0 at baseline to 23.9 ± 3.4 at 6 months (p = 0.005), with a small but statistically significant increase in CDR‐SB from 1.56 ± 1.00 to 1.77 ± 1.11 (p = 0.026; Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

This study provides a real‐world evaluation of lecanemab therapy in Japan, offering an integrated overview of its feasibility, safety, and early clinical outcomes under routine care. We captured treatment implementation, discontinuation, and biomarker profiles relevant to diagnostic assessment and safety monitoring in Asia. Our findings complement recent real‐world investigations from Israel, the United States, and China, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 extending the global evidence base for amyloid‐targeted therapies.

Our findings align with recent Japanese RWE linking increased plasma phosphorylated tau levels to cognitive decline and ARIA. 11 Notably, the Japanese cohort in that study included a substantially higher proportion of patients with a CDR‐Global score of 1 (26.0%), compared to our cohort (3.9%). This suggests differences in disease stage at the start of treatment and association with a low ARIA‐E rate (1.4%).

Within this real‐world context, lecanemab was safely administered under structured monitoring. Among the 70 patients who continued MRI surveillance beyond 6 months, ARIA occurred in eight patients (11.4%), all within the first 6 months of treatment. Most patients had mild, asymptomatic ARIA‐H detected during scheduled MRI monitoring, and no life‐threatening events occurred. The frequency of ARIA‐E was very low (1.4%). Compared with the CLARITY‐AD phase 3 trial, our cohort included older patients with milder baseline clinical impairment, as reflected by lower baseline CDR‐SB scores (Table 1). 5 This suggests earlier treatment initiation in routine Japanese practice. Nevertheless, our overall safety profile was generally consistent with that reported in the Asian subgroup analysis of the CLARITY‐AD trial. 14 Recent real‐world data have suggested that patients with more advanced baseline cognitive impairment, particularly those with CDR‐Global 1, may have a substantially higher risk of symptomatic ARIA (approximately 15 times more) than those with CDR 0.5. 7 The predominance of patients with CDR‐Global 0.5 in our cohort may therefore have contributed to the relatively low incidence of ARIA observed in this study.

In the phase 3 CLARITY‐AD trial of lecanemab, ARIA‐E and ARIA‐H occurred in 12.6% and 17.3% of patients, respectively, with higher incidences among APOE ε4 carriers, particularly ε4 homozygotes. 5 An Asian subgroup analysis further confirmed this genotype‐dependent risk, showing numerically lower overall ARIA rates compared with Western cohorts, while maintaining a consistent effect of APOE ε4 on ARIA susceptibility 14 . Importantly, APOE ε4 homozygotes are currently eligible for lecanemab treatment under the Japanese appropriate use recommendations. In our cohort, individuals with the APOE ε4/ε4 genotype were included in both the screening and treatment processes, although three patients declined treatment after discussion of ARIA risk. In contrast, in our 6‐month real‐world series, ARIA incidence was similar between patients with and without ε4 (7.3% each), indicating that the APOE genotype alone is insufficient for ARIA risk stratification in routine clinical monitoring. ARIA risk is also influenced by age, cerebrovascular disease, hypertension, and antithrombotic exposure. 19 , 20

The limited use of APOE genotyping in Japan may partly reflect evidence suggesting that APOE ε4 has a less pronounced impact on ARIA risk in Asian populations than in non‐Asian populations, as well as the limited availability of clinical genetics specialists. Nevertheless, in our cohort, APOE genotyping was feasibly incorporated into treatment discussions in our cohort without the need for specialist genetic counseling, supporting the pragmatic use of genetic information within routine neurological workflows.

The mean plasma Aβ42/40 ratio in the treated cohort was 0.092 ± 0.0074, which was below the predefined cutoff for amyloid positivity (0.102). 16 This was broadly concordant with amyloid status confirmed by PET or CSF analysis. These findings support the clinical utility of chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay (CLEIA) ‐based plasma Aβ assays in real‐world diagnostic workflows. 16 , 17 , 21

In our cohort, 32.5% of patients who underwent amyloid PET evaluation were amyloid PET‐negative despite an initial clinical suspicion of MCI due to AD or mild AD dementia. This proportion is broadly consistent with findings from previous real‐world amyloid PET cohorts, including the IDEAS study in the United States and the AMYPAD‐DPMS study in Europe, which reported amyloid PET negativity rates ranging from 30 to 50% among patients evaluated for cognitive impairment. 22 , 23 These findings highlight the limitations of clinical assessment alone. Currently, only visual interpretation of amyloid PET is approved for determining amyloid positivity in Japan. However, recent RWE studies have revealed discrepancies of ∼ 14% between visual amyloid PET readings and centiloid scores (threshold < 24.4). 24 Several patients were blood‐based amyloid positive despite negative PET visual interpretation. Such disagreement is consistent with the notion that reduced plasma Aβ42/40 precedes fibrillar amyloid deposition detectable by PET. 25 , 26

We focused on the Aβ42/40 ratio as it is the sole blood‐based amyloid test approved in Japan; however, the biomarker landscape rapidly is evolving. In our previous study using the same HISCL® platform, the diagnostic performance of plasma Aβ42/40 for predicting amyloid PET positivity was comparable to that of plasma p‐tau217. 17 Notably, HISCL‐based plasma Aβ42/40 demonstrated high specificity and may detect Aβ accumulation at an earlier stage than the Aβ PET visual reading threshold. The 2024 update of the NIA–AA 14 formally incorporated plasma biomarkers into the AT(N) classification system, reflecting major advances in p‐tau217. Plasma p‐tau markers may provide complementary diagnostic information. 27 , 28 Although lecanemab is covered under the Japanese national health insurance system, age‐ and income‐dependent co‐payments, ranging from 10% to 30%, may still limit treatment continuation. Our study was limited to the first 6 months of treatment, when 92% of ARIA‐E occurs. 29 Nevertheless, our findings suggest that lecanemab can be safely administered in real‐world practice with structured monitoring. In biomarker‐positive observational cohorts such as ADNI and BioFINDER, gradual longitudinal worsening of MMSE and CDR‐SB has been reported. 30 The mean 6‐month increase in CDR‐SB observed in our cohort was broadly consistent with the expected range reported in biomarker‐positive MCI populations. 30 In contrast, the degree of MMSE decline in our cohort appeared somewhat greater than that reported in these observational studies. However, MMSE scores are susceptible to ceiling effects, educational background, language dysfunction, and the influence of outliers, and our study lacked an untreated comparator group. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Our findings are consistent with recent CLARITY‐AD safety updates and preliminary data from the open‐label extension, 30 with longer‐term analyses up to 48 months that should further clarify safety durability and late‐emerging ARIA events. 31

This study has some limitations, including its single‐center design and modest sample size, which may limit generalizability of the findings. First, this study focused primarily on eligibility rates and the early treatment phases. Future studies with larger cohorts and longer follow‐ups periods are needed to evaluate outcomes beyond 18 months. Second, biomarker assessment relied primarily on plasma Aβ42/40, with limited CSF data and without emerging plasma markers such as p‐tau 217. Third, although other amyloid‐targeting antibodies have been approved during the course of this study, we focused exclusively on lecanemab in this study.

Despite these limitations, our findings represent an early comprehensive RWE of lecanemab from Japan and our findings provide practical insights into early real‐world implementation of lecanemab. Collectively, our data support the structured integration of lecanemab into routine neurological care and contribute to the evolving international evidence base for amyloid‐targeted therapy.

Informed consent was obtained through hospital notice documents describing the study and publicly disclosed on the institutional website, and patients were provided with the opportunity to decline participation.
