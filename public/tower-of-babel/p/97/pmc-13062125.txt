Unraveling children’s mental rotation: insights from behavior and eye tracking
Authors: Hongan Wang, Xingcong Zhao, Xiaolin Zhao, Yutong Luo, Dongchuan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to examine mental rotation performance in children aged 5–15 years across varying rotational angles, and to elucidate the cognitive processing mechanisms and individual differences involved by integrating behavioral data, eye-movement metrics, and self-control capacity. Forty-one children participated in a mental rotation task featuring stimulus orientations of 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, and 300°. Outcome measures included accuracy, reaction time, fixation duration, fixation count, and mean pupil diameter, complemented by assessments from the Multidimensional Self-Control Scale. Data were analyzed using the Friedman test and Spearman’s correlation coefficients. Increasing rotation angles were associated with significant decreases in accuracy and increases in reaction time. Eye-tracking indicators—fixation duration, fixation count, and mean pupil diameter—showed consistent elevation, indicative of heightened cognitive load and adaptive changes in information processing strategies. Notably, impulse control exhibited negative correlations with fixation duration (R = − 0.35) and mean pupil diameter (R = − 0.31). The findings suggest that mental rotation performance is associated with not only spatial transformation skills but also cognitive control resources. Children exhibiting lower impulse control experienced greater cognitive load and reduced processing efficiency under demanding task conditions. Together, the evidence points toward an integrated view of mental rotation in children, linking spatial processing with self-regulatory capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40912-w.Keywords: Mental rotation, Eye-tracking, Pupil diameter, Self-controlSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Mental rotation, a fundamental aspect of spatial cognitive abilities, refers to the advanced mental operation whereby individuals manipulate two- or three-dimensional objects internally to evaluate their spatial alignment or congruence with other objects. Since the pioneering empirical investigations by Shepard and Metzler1, who first demonstrated this capacity through classic behavioral experiments, mental rotation has become a central paradigm in the study of spatial cognition. Subsequent research has elucidated that mental rotation involves complex spatial transformations and exhibits a pronounced angle-dependent effect1–3. This cognitive skill is critical for success in STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) education and supports everyday activities such as spatial navigation and block assembly4–6. Traditional research approaches have primarily relied on behavioral metrics, including accuracy and reaction time, to assess mental rotation proficiency. While these measures effectively capture overall task performance, they provide limited insight into the dynamic and continuous cognitive processes underlying task execution7.

Neuroscientific investigations have revealed that mental rotation depends on a distributed network of brain regions operating in an integrated fashion. Neuroimaging studies have identified the posterior parietal cortex, particularly the intraparietal sulcus, as a critical site for spatial representation transformations, with neural activation levels positively correlating with the magnitude of rotational angle8–10. Concurrently, the prefrontal cortex—especially the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex—has been implicated in cognitive control functions, including strategy selection and interference suppression during task performance11,12. Additional cortical areas, such as the primary visual cortex, premotor cortex, occipital lobe, and posterior insular cortex, also demonstrate varying degrees of involvement13. Collectively, these findings highlight mental rotation as a multifaceted cognitive operation requiring coordinated activation across multiple brain regions that support both fundamental spatial transformations and higher-order executive control. This reliance on prefrontal control regions underscores that successful mental rotation places significant demands on domain-general executive functions, such as maintaining task goals, inhibiting prepotent responses, and flexibly allocating attentional resources, in addition to core spatial transformation abilities14.

To capture the dynamics of these higher-order control processes in real time, researchers have increasingly turned to eye-tracking technology, which provides a novel methodological framework for examining real-time cognitive mechanisms15,16. Unlike conventional behavioral indices such as accuracy and reaction time, eye-movement data afford millisecond-level temporal resolution, thereby revealing dynamic shifts in attentional focus and cognitive resource allocation throughout task performance. The theoretical foundation of eye-tracking research rests on the eye-mind hypothesis, which posits a close correspondence between gaze behavior and underlying cognitive processes17. Empirical studies indicate that adults systematically modulate their eye-movement patterns in response to task complexity during mental rotation15,18. Critically, specific oculometrics have been linked to core cognitive processes in such tasks: fixation duration to the depth of spatial encoding/transformation, and pupil diameter to cognitive load and mental effort17,19. However, it remains an open question whether these eye-movement signatures of cognitive processing, predominantly characterized in adult populations, generalize to children. Critically, children’s still-developing executive functions (e.g., inhibitory control, working memory) and attentional systems may lead to cognitive engagement and load management patterns during demanding tasks like mental rotation that are not merely immature versions of adult patterns, but may be qualitatively different or more variable20. Therefore, investigating how children’s online cognitive processing, as indexed by eye movements, adapts to varying task demands within this developmental context is essential for building a developmentally sensitive account of spatial cognition.

Childhood represents a critical period for the development of spatial cognitive abilities, and understanding the real-time processes involved is key. Empirical evidence demonstrates that children as young as four to five years exhibit emerging cognitive offloading capabilities, adjusting their external behaviors in accordance with the complexity of mental rotation tasks21. Developmental cognitive neuroscience research further indicates that the maturation of mental rotation skills in children is reflected not only in improved behavioral performance but also in the progressive refinement of neural functional connectivity and the development of more efficient cognitive processes22. This developmental progression underscores the importance of studying not just outcomes but the dynamics of processing itself. Consequently, eye-tracking is ideally suited for this purpose, yet systematic investigations characterizing children’s own eye-movement patterns during mental rotation, and how these patterns relate to their developing cognitive control capacities, are currently lacking. Moreover, these insights hold practical implications for educational interventions aimed at enhancing spatial abilities, including domains such as mental arithmetic20.

The rotation angle constitutes the primary experimental variable in mental rotation tasks and directly influences the cognitive demands imposed by the task. According to the classical simulation theory of mental rotation, individuals mentally simulate the physical rotation of an object, with the complexity of this cognitive operation closely linked to the magnitude of the rotation angle1. A substantial body of empirical research has demonstrated that increases in rotation angle correspond to significant changes in behavioral performance, including decreased accuracy and prolonged reaction times23–25. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether these behavioral manifestations of the angle effect correspond to its influence on real-time cognitive processing as measured through continuous physiological indices like eye movements. Furthermore, the degree to which behavioral measures align with eye-movement characteristics warrants further investigation. Addressing these questions is essential for advancing understanding of the fundamental cognitive mechanisms underlying mental rotation. Drawing upon the simulation theory framework, the present study posits hypothesis 1 (H1): that increased rotation angle (and thus task difficulty) will be associated with not only systematic changes in both children’s behavioral performance (reduced accuracy, longer reaction time) but also with elevated levels of their eye-movement patterns, particularly those indicative of increased cognitive load and processing effort (e.g., longer fixation duration, larger pupil diameter). Crucially, this increased difficulty and cognitive load heightens the demand on top-down cognitive control for effective task management.

Recent developments in cognitive psychology have increasingly emphasized the importance of individual differences in the execution of such complex cognitive tasks, particularly in how individuals manage the control demands they impose. Core cognitive attributes, including executive function and attentional control, have been shown to be substantially correlated with individual performance under cognitive demands26,27. These variations are not only correlated with ultimate behavioral outcomes but are also reflected in the specific cognitive processing pathways and strategy selections employed during task engagement28. Specifically, self-regulatory aspects of executive function, such as the ability to maintain focus and inhibit impulsive responses, are theoretically relevant to mental rotation, as successfully manipulating a mental image requires sustained attention and resistance to premature decision-making, especially under high cognitive load.

A critical direction for research involves examining whether such self-regulatory capacities are reflected in behavioral responses and eye-movement patterns during mental rotation. However, systematic evidence linking specific facets of self-control to the online cognitive processes (as indexed by eye movements) during mental rotation in children is currently lacking. Grounded in theoretical perspectives on cognitive resource allocation, the present study advances an exploratory hypothesis regarding individual differences (H2): that children’s self-reported self-control capacities, particularly those executive facets pertinent to the task demands outlined above (e.g., impulse and attention control), will be associated with the efficiency of their online cognitive processing during the mental rotation task. Specifically, we will explore whether and how different dimensions of self-control (e.g., impulse control, attention control) correlate with behavioral performance and eye-movement metrics indicative of cognitive load and processing strategy (e.g., fixation duration, pupil diameter).

In summary, this study aims to systematically investigate the cognitive processing mechanisms underlying mental rotation ability in children by integrating behavioral assessments with eye-tracking technology. It examines children’s performance patterns across tasks of varying difficulty levels (H1) and explores how these online processing metrics associate with self-reported self-control capacities, with a particular interest in dimensions such as impulse control (H2). This multimodal approach is designed to capture both overt behavioral responses and covert cognitive processes during task execution, thereby addressing limitations inherent in reliance solely on traditional behavioral measures. The anticipated findings are expected to provide preliminary evidence on the link between self-regulation and spatial processing, and to offer an integrated, process-oriented perspective for understanding the mechanisms underpinning spatial cognitive development in children.

Methods

Subjects

The present study was carried out in Tianjin, China, involving the recruitment of 45 children through telephone contact. After data screening, four participants were excluded due to insufficient quality of eye-tracking data, based on the following predefined quantitative criteria: two due to excessive data loss (> 25% of trials with invalid gaze), one due to poor calibration accuracy (mean error > 1.0° visual angle), and one due to persistent tracking loss caused by excessive head motion, which resulted in < 75% valid trials. This yielded a final valid sample of 41 children (34 males, 7 females) aged between 5 and 15 years (mean age = 10.29 ± 2.25 years). All analyses were conducted on the combined sample, as the study was not designed or powered to detect sex differences, and the primary hypotheses concerned the within-subject effects of rotation angle and their correlations with self-control. Prior to participation, informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all children. To evaluate individual traits, the Chinese version of the Multidimensional Self-Control Scale29 was administered. This instrument comprises 29 items organized within a three-tier pyramid framework, assessing six dimensions: procrastination control, attention control, impulse control, emotion regulation, goal-directed behavior, and self-control strategies. The scale exhibited satisfactory reliability and validity, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.72 to 0.83, and was self-administered by the participants. Here, its scores are treated as exploratory measures of self-reported, trait-level self-regulation for investigating associations with objective task performance and eye-movement metrics, not as direct indices of online executive control processes. The mean scores (± standard deviation) for each dimension were as follows: 3.00 ± 0.49, 3.16 ± 0.45, 2.52 ± 0.93, 3.98 ± 0.81, 3.83 ± 0.87, and 4.01 ± 0.71, respectively. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Chongqing University of Education, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s) for the publication of identifying information and images in an online open-access publication.

Apparatus

The present study utilized the portable Tobii Pro Fusion eye tracker (Tobii Technology, Stockholm, Sweden) to capture eye movement data from children20. This device operates at a sampling frequency of 120 Hz, with an accuracy of 0.04°, a precision of 0.3°, an average latency below 13 ms, and a tracking recovery time of 250 ms. The experimental setup also incorporated a Logitech BRIO camera, offering a resolution of 640 × 480 pixels and a frame rate of 30 frames per second, alongside custom-designed mechanical keypads, each equipped with two directional keys positioned on the left and right sides. Visual stimuli were presented on an 18.5-inch Sculptor portable monitor, featuring a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, luminance of 250 cd/m2, a refresh rate of 100 Hz, and a response time of 5 ms. The experimental protocol was developed using Python version 3.11.4, with experimental control and eye-tracking data collection managed through PsychoPy (version 2023.2.3) and Tobii Pro Lab (version 1.232.52758), respectively.

Procedure

This study developed a modified version of the mental rotation test employing a left–right (L–R) synchronous presentation paradigm, as depicted in Fig. 1. During each trial, a standard stimulus was presented on the left side of the display, concurrently accompanied by either a rotated version of the stimulus—rotated by a predetermined angle—or its mirror image on the right side. The occurrence of rotated versus mirrored stimuli was randomized with equal probability (1:1 ratio). The rotation angles utilized included 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, and 300°. For consistency, clockwise rotations were assigned positive values ( +), whereas counterclockwise rotations were assigned negative values ( −). It is important to note that rotations of + 180° and − 180° represent the same spatial orientation, and similarly, 240° and 300° rotations can be equivalently expressed as − 120° and − 60°, respectively. The stimulus set consisted of five categories—digits, letters, arrows, pedestrians, and motorcycles. These stimuli were selected for their high familiarity to children and to sample both abstract symbols and simple embodied objects, thereby aiming to broadly assess mental rotation ability across diverse content types30. This specific combination constitutes a novel variation of the standard mental rotation paradigm, designed to enhance ecological validity. Critically, to prevent any systematic confounding effects from stimulus category, all stimuli were presented in a fully pseudorandomized sequence. Participants completed a total of 50 trials, derived from the combination of 5 rotation angles, 2 mirror conditions, and 5 stimulus categories. Children were instructed to determine, within a 10 s response window, whether the image on the right could be obtained by rotating the image on the left. Affirmative responses were indicated by pressing the left arrow key, while negative responses were indicated by pressing the right arrow key. Each trial was followed by a 1 s inter-trial interval displaying a central fixation cross.

Experimental paradigm for mental rotation. The highlighted region denotes the area of interest (AOI).

Data collection

The study was conducted in a controlled environment characterized by minimal noise and adequate lighting. Each child participant was seated approximately 65 cm from the stimulus display. The experimental session commenced with a nine-point calibration procedure to ensure accurate data collection. To facilitate understanding of the task requirements, all participants engaged in a brief practice session prior to the formal testing phase. During the experiment, children were instructed to maintain focus and respond by pressing directional keys as swiftly and accurately as possible. The experimental software automatically recorded these responses, coding correct and incorrect keypresses as 1 and 0, respectively. Concurrently, an eye-tracking device positioned beneath the screen continuously monitored eye movement trajectories throughout the mental rotation task. Participants were advised to minimize large head movements to ensure data integrity. The experimenter remained present beside each child during the session, pausing the procedure to address any questions and provide clarifications as needed. Upon completion, each participant was presented with a small gift as a gesture of appreciation.

Data analysis

Behavioral measures

Performance on the mental rotation task was evaluated using two classic indicators: accuracy and reaction time1. Accuracy was calculated for each rotation angle as the percentage of correct judgments (match vs. mirror). Higher accuracy reflects superior spatial transformation ability. Reaction time (RT) was defined as the interval from stimulus onset to a valid keypress (in seconds), with analysis restricted to correct trials to index processing speed. Longer RTs typically indicate increased cognitive demand or less efficient mental rotation.

Eye-tracking measures

To examine the dynamics of visual attention and online processing strategies, we analyzed gaze behavior directed at the rotated stimulus (the right-side figure), which was designated as the area of interest (AOI). The AOI was manually defined for each unique stimulus as a rectangular region, expanded by 0.5° of visual angle uniformly around its contour to compensate for minor calibration drift and ensure the capture of all task-relevant fixations (see Fig. 1). Within the AOI, fixations were identified using the I-VT filter (velocity threshold: 30°/s; minimum duration: 60 ms). Fixation duration was calculated as the total dwell time (seconds) within the AOI per trial. Longer durations signify greater sustained attentional engagement and deeper stimulus encoding (Rayner 1998)39. Fixation count was defined as the total number of discrete fixation events within the AOI per trial. A higher count often reflects a more piecemeal, sequential inspection strategy and increased task difficulty25.

Pupillometric measure

To capture cognitive load, we computed the mean pupil diameter for each trial19. This was calculated as the arithmetic mean pupil size (mm) of the dominant eye during stimulus presentation. Larger diameters are widely interpreted as reflecting greater mental effort and heightened cognitive resource allocation. Raw data were smoothed with a 5-sample median filter, and trials with > 25% invalid data were excluded.

Statistical analysis

This study utilized a within-subjects experimental design. Given that the data violated the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance (p’s < 0.05), the non-parametric Friedman test was employed to assess overall differences across the various rotation angle conditions (+ 60°, + 120°, +/− 180°, − 120°, − 60°) for the following dependent variables: cognitive-behavioral performance (accuracy, reaction time) and eye-movement metrics (fixation duration, fixation count, mean pupil diameter). To examine the robustness of these angle effects across the substantial developmental span of our sample (5–15 years), we also conducted supplementary Friedman tests within younger (≤ 10 years, n = 23) and older (> 10 years, n = 18) subgroups, formed by splitting the sample at the median age. In instances where significant effects were identified, subsequent post-hoc analyses were performed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with p-values adjusted according to the Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes were reported as r, interpreted according to the criteria proposed by Parker et al.31: small (0.1 ≤ r < 0.3), medium (0.3 ≤ r < 0.5), and large (r ≥ 0.5). Furthermore, Spearman’s rank-order correlation analyses were conducted to explore the relationships among all measured variables. To ensure that these relationships were not confounded by the substantial age range in our sample, we also report partial correlations controlling for chronological age for all key associations involving behavioral, eye-tracking, and self-control measures. All statistical procedures were carried out using the R software environment, with the significance threshold set at α = 0.05.

Results

Behavioural results

Analysis of the full sample revealed statistically significant effects of rotation angle on both accuracy and reaction time (accuracy: χ2 = 40.76, p < 1 × 10−4; reaction time: χ2 = 58.46, p < 1 × 10−4). To verify these effects were consistent across the developmental span, we examined younger and older subgroups separately. Both subgroups showed significant angle effects for accuracy (younger: χ2 = 36.4, p = < 1 × 10−4; older: χ2 = 15.4, p = 0.004) and reaction time (younger: χ2 = 34.0, p < 1 × 10−4; older: χ2 = 26.7, p < 1 × 10−4), and no significant age-group differences were found at any individual rotation angle (p’s > 0.05), indicating that the angle-dependent difficulty in mental rotation is a robust phenomenon across the studied age range. Taken together, the observed effect sizes ranged from medium to very large (r = 0.51–0.75 for significant comparisons), indicating that the cognitive demand imposed by increasing rotation angles translated into substantial behavioral consequences. For instance, the difference between the easiest (+ 60°: 71.50%) and most difficult (+ / − 180°: 59.00%) conditions corresponded to an accuracy drop of approximately 13 percentage points, and the reaction time increased from 1.76 s to 2.28 s—a difference of over half a second, underscoring the practically meaningful impact of angular disparity on performance.

Eye-movement results

Consistent with the behavioral results, the following eye-movement metrics exhibited robust and systematic modulation by rotation angle. Crucially, these patterns were not transient or variable but were reliably observed across both younger and older subgroups, indicating they reflect stable cognitive-physiological responses to task difficulty.

Fixation duration and fixation count

The pattern of results was characterized by medium-to-large effect sizes (r = 0.37–0.70 for significant comparisons) and, for several key contrasts, confidence intervals that excluded zero and indicated precise estimation (e.g., for the link between reaction time and fixation count, r = 0.62, 95% CI [0.38, 0.81]). This indicates that the adjustments in visual sampling strategy in response to task difficulty were not only systematic but also substantial in magnitude, with larger angles consistently requiring both longer total inspection times (e.g., an increase from 1.24 s at + 60° to 1.44 s at 180°, a difference of about 200 ms) and a greater number of discrete fixations.

Mean pupil diameter

The full sample showed significant variation in mean pupil diameter across rotation angles (χ2 = 62.75, p < 1 × 10−4). This index of cognitive load was similarly modulated by rotation angle in both younger (χ2 = 43.6, p < 1 × 10−4) and older (χ2 = 62.8, p < 1 × 10−4) subgroups, and no significant age-group differences were observed at any individual rotation angle (p’s > 0.05), indicating that the physiological response to mental rotation demands is robust across the studied age range. Subsequent post-hoc pairwise Wilcoxon tests demonstrated that the mean pupil diameter at the + 60° rotation (2.89 ± 0.32 mm) was significantly smaller than those observed at + 120° (2.92 ± 0.32 mm), +/− 180° (2.94 ± 0.33 mm), and − 120° (2.93 ± 0.32 mm) conditions (W = 24–127, p’s < 1 × 10−3, r = 0.61–0.82, adjusted). However, the difference between the + 60° and − 60° condition (2.90 ± 0.32 mm) was not statistically significant (W = 201, p = 0.051, r = 0.44, 95% CI [0.16, 0.70], adjusted). Additionally, the mean pupil diameter at + 120° was significantly greater than that at − 60° (W = 694, p = 0.001, r = 0.60, 95% CI [0.35, 0.78], adjusted), but did not differ significantly from the +/− 180° or − 120° conditions (W = 304 and 378, p’s > 0.05, r = 0.26 and 0.11, adjusted). The +/− 180° condition exhibited a significantly larger mean pupil diameter compared to the − 60° condition (W = 804, p < 1 × 10−4, r = 0.76, 95% CI [0.62, 0.85], adjusted), yet showed no significant difference relative to the − 120° condition (W = 541, p = 1.00, r = 0.22, 95% CI [0.02, 0.53], adjusted). Finally, the mean pupil diameter under the − 120° condition was significantly greater than that under the − 60° condition (W = 807, p < 1 × 10−4, r = 0.76, 95% CI [0.62, 0.85], adjusted), as illustrated in Fig. 4. It is worth noting that the mean pupil diameter showed greater inter-individual variability compared to the other metrics. This pattern is consistent with the nature of pupil diameter as a physiological measure, as it is influenced not only by cognitive task demands but also by intrinsic factors (e.g., baseline autonomic arousal) and subtle environmental factors. Critically, however, the significant statistical results indicate that the cognitive load effect elicited by increasing rotation angles is robust, with the systematic differences between conditions surpassing this inherent background variation.

Notably, the effect sizes for pupil diameter were among the largest observed in this study (r up to 0.82, as in the + 60° vs − 120° comparison), with correspondingly tight confidence intervals that reflected high precision of estimation (e.g., r = 0.76, 95% CI [0.62, 0.85] for +/− 180° vs − 60°). This underscores the high sensitivity and reliability of mean pupil diameter as a physiological index of cognitive load, with even the small absolute mean differences of 0.04–0.05 mm between low and high angles reflecting a very large and consistent systematic effect driven by task demands.

Correlation results

Spearman correlation analyses, supplemented by partial correlations controlling for the substantial age range, revealed several key associations between behavioral, eye-tracking, and self-control measures: (1) Reaction time was positively correlated with fixation count (R = 0.42, p = 0.006; partial R = 0.46, p = 0.002; Fig. 5A), indicating a link between slower responses and more frequent visual sampling. Notably, task accuracy did not show significant correlations with any of the eye-tracking metrics (p’s > 0.05), suggesting a dissociation between the speed and correctness of mental rotation in their relation to online gaze behavior; (2) fixation duration was strongly positively correlated with fixation count (R = 0.68, p < 1 × 10–4; partial R = 0.69, p < 1 × 10–4; Fig. 5B), confirming that longer inspection times comprised more discrete fixations; (3) Critically, fixation duration was negatively correlated with impulse control (R = − 0.35, p = 0.02; partial R = − 0.34, p = 0.03; Fig. 5C). This moderate effect size (accounting for approximately 12% of the shared variance, R2 = 0.12) suggests that children with higher self-reported impulse control required substantially shorter gaze durations to process the rotated object. This points to a more efficient visual encoding or maintenance process that is specifically linked to self-regulatory capacity, beyond the influence of age; (4) Similarly, mean pupil diameter showed a negative association with impulse control that approached conventional significance (R = − 0.31, p = 0.047; partial R = − 0.30, p = 0.059; Fig. 5D). This trend (explaining ~ 9% of variance, R2 = 0.09) implies that poorer impulse control tended to be associated with greater pupil diameter—a marker of higher cognitive load or arousal—during the task. Importantly, self-reported impulse control was not significantly correlated with either behavioral performance measure (accuracy or reaction time; all |R|< 0.16, p’s > 0.33). No other significant correlations were observed (p’s > 0.05). The robustness and meaningful magnitude of the association between impulse control and fixation duration underscore the significant link between self-regulation and visual processing efficiency during mental rotation.

Discussion

This study employed a comprehensive approach integrating behavioral assessments and eye-tracking technology to systematically examine mental rotation performance in children aged 5 to 15 across varying rotation angles, while also exploring its association with self-control capacity. The findings consistently revealed that: (1) task difficulty in mental rotation increased progressively with larger rotation angles, as evidenced by both diminished behavioral performance and adaptive modifications in eye-movement patterns; and (2) individual differences in self-reported impulse control were systematically linked to indicators of cognitive load and processing efficiency. These results support the two initial hypotheses and lend preliminary support to an integrative interpretive framework aimed at elucidating the cognitive mechanisms underlying mental rotation in children.

Firstly, the data are consistent with the simulation theory of mental rotation (H1). As rotation angles increased from 60° to 180°, children demonstrated marked declines in accuracy alongside prolonged reaction times, reflecting the classic angle-effect phenomenon23–25. This pattern is consistent with the view that children engage in a continuous, analog simulation process akin to physical rotation during mental rotation tasks. Crucially, eye-tracking metrics provided real-time, quantitative evidence of this internal cognitive process: with increasing angles, fixation duration and fixation count both increased, accompanied by an expansion in mean pupil diameter. These results align with findings reported by Bauer et al.19. Collectively, these indicators suggest that heightened task difficulty compels children to allocate greater visual attention (reflected in longer fixation durations), employ more frequent information-sampling (higher fixation counts), and endure elevated cognitive load (larger pupil diameters). The observed covariation between eye movements and behavioral performance effectively bridges external task outcomes with internal cognitive processing, confirming that escalating task demands systematically influence children’s cognitive strategies and load.

Beyond characterizing task difficulty, the study highlights a potential link, based on questionnaire data, between self-reported regulatory traits and mental rotation performance (H2). Correlational analyses (and partial correlations controlling for age) revealed significant negative associations between scores on the self-reported impulse control subscale and both fixation duration (explaining approximately 12% of variance, R2 ≈ 0.12) and pupil diameter (explaining approximately 9% of variance, R2 ≈ 0.09). Together, these correlations—accounting for a modest but non-trivial proportion of variance in online processing metrics—underscore that children who reported poorer impulse control tended to exhibit eye-movement indicators associated with higher cognitive load and less efficient visual sampling. This finding integrates traditional “cold” spatial cognition with “hot” self-regulatory processes as assessed via trait report. However, the reliance on self-report necessitates caution in interpreting these mechanisms. Based on the observed correlations, we speculate that self-reported deficits in impulse control may be linked to less efficient processing via two possible mechanisms: first, potentially diminished inhibitory control renders visual representations in working memory more susceptible to interference, necessitating more frequent refixations to maintain these representations (manifested as increased fixation duration). This speculative interpretation finds conceptual parallels in research on clinical populations. For example, Chamorro et al.32 have documented that children with ADHD (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder) demonstrate elevated error rates on inhibitory control tasks, including the anti-saccade task, characterized by more frequent involuntary regressions or erroneous saccades. Second, overcoming such interference and suppressing premature responses demands greater cognitive resource mobilization to sustain task goals (reflected in pupil diameter). For instance, Shirama et al.33 reported that individuals with ADHD exhibit attenuated stimulus-evoked phasic pupil diameter during cognitive tasks such as the auditory continuous performance task and visuospatial working memory task. Consequently, children reporting stronger impulse control showed eye-movement patterns consistent with more economical resource allocation, whereas those reporting weaker control showed patterns suggesting a higher physiological and cognitive cost. While our study did not assess clinical populations, these parallels not only highlight a valuable question for future research (whether cognitive control efficiency exists on a continuum) but also provide an empirical basis within typical development for exploring it.

An important nuance in these findings is that self-reported impulse control was associated with online processing indices but not directly with the behavioral outcomes of accuracy or reaction time. This dissociation suggests that impulse control may be more salient in modulating the efficiency and cognitive cost of the mental transformation, rather than directly determining its final correctness or speed. Thus, children with lower impulse control might achieve similar performance levels through compensatory, higher-effort processing strategies—a hidden cost precisely captured by eye-tracking but not reflected in standard behavioral scores.

Taken together, our results reveal two distinct but interconnected sources of influence on children’s mental rotation: the task-driven demands of spatial transformation (H1) and the trait-linked efficiency of cognitive control (H2). To synthesize these findings within a broader cognitive neuroscience context, we outline a dual-process framework for mental rotation. This framework integrates our behavioral and eye-tracking evidence with established neurocognitive literature; the neural correlates discussed are inferred from prior studies and are not directly tested here. The framework posits that successful performance depends on two functionally differentiable, yet interactive, subprocesses: (1) a spatial transformation process, responsible for the analog manipulation of mental representations, which is widely associated in the neuroimaging literature with activity in the posterior parietal cortex34; and (2) a state regulation process, responsible for top-down cognitive control, including attentional allocation and interference suppression, the neural substrates of which commonly involve the prefrontal cortex and its interaction with subcortical neuromodulatory systems such as the locus coeruleus–norepinephrine (LC-NE) system35,36. We hypothesize that the efficiency of the state regulation process is related to cognitive control traits, such as those indexed by self-report. The observed association between impulse control and fixation metrics (R2 ≈ 0.12) provides initial quantitative support for this link, indicating that self-regulatory capacity accounts for meaningful individual differences in the efficiency of the state regulation process during mental rotation.

Within this interpretive framework, rotation angle is viewed as predominantly influencing the demands on the spatial transformation process, whereas self-reported individual differences in impulse control are interpreted as being associated with variations in the efficiency of the state regulation process. When task difficulty increases (e.g., +/− 180° rotation) or self-reported regulatory capacity is lower, cognitive resource demands escalate, as indicated by pupil diameter. To preserve representational accuracy, individuals adopt compensatory external information-sampling strategies, evidenced by increased fixation behavior. This model integrates neurocognitive evidence with cognitive-behavioral performance, emphasizing that mental rotation is not solely a bottom-up spatial ability task but a complex process whose online efficiency, as measured by eye-tracking, is correlated with self-reported cognitive control resources.

The theoretical contributions and practical implications of this study are threefold. First, it transcends traditional single-metric paradigms by employing a multimodal methodology—combining behavioral measures, eye-tracking, and self-report questionnaires—to provide robust empirical evidence of the online cognitive processing underlying mental rotation in children. Second, it successfully bridges “cold” spatial cognition and “hot” self-regulatory capacities, by revealing a significant association between self-reported self-control and fundamental spatial cognitive processes, with self-control accounting for roughly 10% of the variance in real-time cognitive load indicators. Third, the proposed dual-process model offers a novel heuristic framework for understanding individual differences in mental rotation and could, pending future validation, inform the development of standardized assessment tools for children’s spatial cognitive abilities, as well as offering a methodological foundation for future research into objective markers of cognitive control, which may include studies of clinical populations (e.g., ADHD).

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. The generalizability of the findings is constrained by both the modest sample size and its composition. While the within-subject design provided robust tests of our primary hypotheses regarding rotation angles, the sample size may limit the statistical power to detect smaller or more complex effects. Critically, the notable gender imbalance (34 males, 7 females) means the identified cognitive patterns—particularly regarding the interplay of eye movements and impulse control—are most clearly representative of male children. Such an interpretation is prudent given established evidence of sex differences in spatial cognition37,38. Consequently, the study was not powered to examine sex differences, and future research with gender-balanced samples is explicitly needed to test whether the proposed dual-process model generalizes across genders. Additionally, the cross-sectional design inherently limits causal inference,the observed associations do not allow us to determine the direction of influence or rule out potential confounding factors. Thus, longitudinal designs are required to clarify whether impulse control capacity shapes the development of mental rotation efficiency, or vice versa. Second, individual differences were assessed via self-report, which captures subjective traits but cannot directly speak to online executive control processes during the task. Such self-reports are susceptible to biases (e.g., social desirability), potentially introducing measurement noise that may have attenuated the observed correlations. Therefore, our findings invite future validation using objective, task-concurrent measures of executive control (e.g., physiological indices or behavioral probes) to more directly test the “state regulation” component of our model. Relatedly, the specific combination of stimulus categories used, while enhancing ecological validity, constitutes a novel variation for which there is no single standardized protocol. Future work employing homogeneous stimuli could help isolate the core spatial transformation process from potential category-specific strategies. Furthermore, while the proposed dual-process framework integrates our behavioral and eye-tracking evidence with established neurocognitive literature, it currently lacks direct validation from neuroimaging modalities (e.g., fMRI/EEG) that could test its specific neuroanatomical mappings. Therefore, the neurocognitive components of the framework should be regarded as plausible, evidence-informed hypotheses that synthesize findings across levels of analysis, rather than as conclusions directly affirmed by the present data. Crucially, directly testing the model’s core postulate—that task difficulty and impulse control modulate the interplay between parietal and prefrontal networks—requires future research that combines this paradigm with concurrent neurophysiological measures.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the influence of rotation angles on mental rotation performance in children aged 5 to 15 by integrating behavioral assessments, eye-tracking metrics, and self-control questionnaires to elucidate the underlying cognitive mechanisms. The findings revealed that increasing rotation angles were associated with reduced accuracy and prolonged reaction times. Concurrently, eye-movement analyses demonstrated elevated cognitive load, as evidenced by longer fixation durations, higher fixation counts, and increased mean pupil diameters, reflecting more frequent information-sampling strategies. Notably, impulse control exhibited significant negative correlations with both fixation duration and mean pupil diameter, indicating that children with lower self-regulatory capacities experienced greater cognitive demands during the task. Drawing on these results, we propose a “dual-process model of mental rotation”, which posits that mental rotation involves not only parietal-mediated spatial transformations but also cognitive control processes governed by the prefrontal cortex. This research offers a novel theoretical framework and empirical support for understanding individual variability in the development of spatial cognition in childhood.
