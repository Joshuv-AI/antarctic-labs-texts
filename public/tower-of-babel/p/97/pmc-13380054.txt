Measuring the carbon footprint of Colchicine for Long COVID - a multicenter trial from India
Authors: Siaa Girotra, Shani S Thankachen, Niveditha Devasenapathy, Madhuri Dutta, Abhinav Bassi, Vivekanand Jha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionReducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from human activities is essential to combat climate change. The healthcare sector contributes to global emissions, and clinical trials play a crucial role in informing evidence-based healthcare practices. Attempts have been made to quantify emissions resulting from clinical trials to help devise effective mitigation strategies. We report carbon emissions from a multicenter clinical trial conducted in both community and hospital settings in India.MethodsWe audited the carbon emissions of a multicenter, placebo-controlled trial evaluating the efficacy of colchicine for Long COVID (CTRI/2021/11/038234) using the GHG Protocol Corporate Accounting and Reporting Standard and the Corporate Value Chain Standard. A three-step methodology was followed, including identification of relevant organisational stakeholders and setting the scope of the calculation, data collection by developing relevant study tools, and calculation of emissions using applicable emission factors.ResultsThe total emissions related to the trial were 12.38 tonnes of CO2 equivalents (CO2e) for the trial duration of three years. The top contributors to the emissions were the local commute of trial coordinators and research staff (71%, 8.73 tonnes CO2e), trial meetings (9%, 1.13 tonnes CO2e), patient investigations (10%, 1.27 tonnes CO2e) and the electricity consumption at the clinical trial unit (5%, 0.61 tonnes CO2e).ConclusionThe study demonstrated the feasibility of assessing the carbon footprint of a clinical trial conducted in India and provides a framework for future assessments and recommendations that can be considered during the planning and implementation of clinical trials.

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) declared climate change the biggest threat to global health in the 21st century, adversely impacting the lives and health of billions of people. Greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions resulting from various human activities are a significant contributor to climate change. In line with the commitments towards meeting the targets of Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3,7,9 and 13) and to uphold the Paris Agreement, efforts need to be made to minimize the carbon emissions of human activities. The impact of carbon emissions goes beyond the environment; they impact human health and wellbeing. The healthcare sector, whose mission is the protection and promotion of health, contributes approximately 5% of annual global emissions [1].

Clinical trials are crucial for generating evidence about the effectiveness and safety of health interventions, helping ensure that only effective and safe treatments enter medical practice while minimizing the risk of ineffective or harmful ones. However, they are resource-intensive and can generate large amounts of waste due to the processes involved in trial conduct. Despite the importance of questions answered through research, these activities must not exacerbate the problems they aim to solve. Decarbonizing clinical trials is increasingly becoming an essential component of improving trial efficiency. Hence, quantifying carbon emissions in clinical trials can help identify ways to reduce them without compromising the quality of trials.

The first attempt to quantify emissions associated with a clinical trial was made in 2007 by the Sustainable Trials Group, based in the United Kingdom using the GHG protocol [2]. They measured emissions of the international, multicentre CRASH trial studying the effectiveness of corticosteroids for head trauma [3]. This attempt provided a proof of concept, following which several trials conducted within the UK National Health System (NHS) were footprinted retrospectively [4]. In 2019, the National Institute for Health Research (NIHR) published guidelines for researchers to reduce emissions from health research without compromising research quality, validity, and reliability [5]. Since then, multiple attempts have been made to measure emissions from industry and academic trials [[6], [7], [8], [9]] and emissions for varied activity domains associated with trials were considered in these studies. The overall recommendations for sustainable trials focused on adopting an efficient study design, using existing research fully, and avoiding unnecessary data collection and monitoring [5,10,11]. However, these recommendations were based on evidence from high-income countries.

India is an important destination for global and national clinical trials. However, no carbon assessments have been done in clinical trials conducted in India and other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Carbon-footprint studies in LMICs are important because both the emission drivers and the practical solutions often differ from high-income settings, so imported solutions can miss the actual hotspots. The objective of this assessment was to quantify the emissions of a national multicenter double-blind, randomized controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of colchicine for Long COVID (CTRI/2021/11/038234), using the methodology outlined in international GHG reporting standards to identify emission hotspots. As the sponsor of this trial, we chose this study opportunistically as it was ongoing with sufficient documentation for a retrospective data collection. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first attempt to quantify the carbon emissions of a trial conducted outside of a high-income country context.

Methods

The Colchicine for Long COVID trial (Colchicine trial) randomised individuals with persistent post-COVID symptoms to receive either colchicine or placebo to assess the effect of colchicine on improving functional, constitutional, and mental symptoms. The trial was initiated in November 2021 with funding from the Indian Council of Medical Research. The George Institute for Global Health, India, was the trial sponsor. Details of the trial methodology have been previously published [12]. Briefly, 350 adults were enrolled across eight sites. Blood samples were drawn at screening to measure high sensitivity-C-Reactive Protein (hs-CRP) to ascertain eligibility. Participants were followed up until 52 weeks after randomization, at weeks 12, 26, and 52. Cardiac injury biomarkers and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were performed at baseline and at 26 weeks in 50 participants. Almost all the trial meetings for feasibility, initiation, training, and coordination were conducted online as the trial operated during the pandemic and immediate post-pandemic period. The trial medication was manufactured by Steadfast Medishield (New Delhi, India) and distributed to trial sites by the George Institute. The trial closeout was in November 2024. Emissions were estimated for the entire trial duration (Nov 2021-Nov 2024).

We used the GHG protocol, which is the global corporate standard for carbon footprint measurement and reporting [13], and a widely used accounting tool for quantifying and understanding GHG emissions for organizations. It provides a comprehensive framework to track emissions across the three emission scopes. We adopted an approach based on the GHG Protocol Corporate Accounting and Reporting Standard and the Corporate Value Chain (Scope 3) Standard [14]. We utilised the NIHR-funded detailed guidance and method to calculate the carbon footprint of a clinical trial to define the scope of activities and data collection for this study [7].

We followed a three-step methodology to calculate emissions. First, the key organizational stakeholders were identified, and operational boundaries for carbon assessment were defined. Next, data collection tools were developed to collect relevant data through discussions with the trial manager and data manager. This entailed reviewing trial-related documentation, utility bills, energy consumption bills, waste generation, and other relevant emission sources for each scope. Finally, emissions per scope were calculated using standardized emission factors [[15], [16], [17]]. All the trial activities carried out during the trial life cycle served as the operational boundary. The definition of the scopes can be found in the Supplementary File [13,14].

We mapped out trial activities at the Clinical Trial Unit (CTU) and clinical sites. Emissions associated with the manufacture of the trial medication, travel by trial participants to the clinical site for trials, and electricity consumption of the research activities at the clinical site were not considered due to data availability limitations. Data collection

Data were collected using data collection tools categorized into general, CTU-related, site-related, and employee commute. In consultation with the trial manager, all trial-related documentation, invoices and consumption bills were reviewed (Fig. 1). Activity data was collected using both the activity-based “bottom-up” and spend-based “top-down” approach.Fig. 1Study methodology.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Study methodology.

Data related to specific trial processes such as the number of times an investigation was conducted or the weight of investigational medicinal product (IMP) shipments and distance travelled were collected from trial documentations. For emissions due to trial meetings the number, duration, and mode of trial meetings were collected. However, for certain activities accurate primary data were not available and average values were used, for example, information on employee commute to CTU and average number of sheets printed using a printer cartridge. For activities for which consumption could not be estimated, we used the spend-based approach to ascertain the cost spent, for example on use of internet. The data source for each activity can be found in the Supplementary File.

Calculations

Since the research team at the CTU worked on various projects, only the time contribution to the Colchicine Trial in full-time equivalents (FTE) was considered for calculating electricity consumption and personnel commute, for the duration of the trial. To estimate emissions due to purchased electricity, it was assumed that all employees worked from the office due to the unavailability of the validated emission factor for homeworking. The average electricity consumption (in kWh) per FTE per year was calculated using the total electricity consumption of the office space and the employee headcount. We used the emission factor made available by the Central Electricity Authority (CEA) of India [16].

Employee local commute emissions were calculated through an institution-wide survey wherein the different modes of transport used in a single journey, the distance travelled using each mode, and the number of days employees travelled to work were collected. We found that an average employee worked two days/week from the office. Using this estimate, we calculated the average emissions per FTE per year.

All paper once used in the trial was considered as waste. Emissions resulting from the transportation of study materials were estimated based on the weight of the material and the distance travelled. Due to the lack of availability of India-specific emission factors, we referred to Department for Environment, Food, and Rural Affairs, UK (DEFRA) emission factors for each modality of transport [17]. For the other activities, we referred to emission factors mentioned in the NIHR detailed guidance document which has utilised emission factors from DEFRA and Ecoinvent 2.2. For activities for which we used the spend-based approach, we utilised emission factors from US Environmentally-Extended Input-Output (USEEIO)[15]. Each of the activity data elements were multiplied with the appropriate emission factors using MS Excel. The steps followed, and a detailed list of activities and their emission calculations are shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary File.

Analysis

The total emissions were categorized descriptively by scopes 1,2,3 and by type of activity. Furthermore, emissions were analysed by clinical trial unit and clinical site.

Results

The Colchicine trial research and operations team at the CTU comprised 15 members (including administrative staff), with an overall contribution of 2.8 FTE through the trial period. The total duration of the trial was 36 months. The trial screened 851 participants and recruited 350 participants from 8 sites. As part of the trial, 1600 blood investigations (Complete Blood Count and hs-CRP) were conducted. Additionally, for the sub-study, 88 biomarker tests and 62 MRI scans were conducted. Most of the trial monitoring was done remotely, with the project manager traveling only twice for in-person site monitoring visits from the CTU to the clinical trials sites. Medical equipment bought for the trial, including a spirometer, an oximeter, and a blood pressure measuring machine, was locally sourced by the sites. At each of the eight sites, a clinical research coordinator (1 FTE) managed the recruitment and follow-up.

The total emissions related to the trial were 12.38 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalents (CO2e) over the three-year trial duration. There were no scope 1 emissions associated with the trial since there were no institute-owned vehicles or manufacturing units. Scope 2 emissions (due to purchased electricity at the CTU) contributed to 4% of the total emissions, while scope 3 emissions were the largest contributor (96%, 11.87 tonnes CO2e) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Scope-wise emission in the Colchicine trial.Fig 2 dummy alt text

Scope-wise emission in the Colchicine trial.

The top contributors to the emissions in this trial were the home-to-office local commute of the site trial coordinators to the clinical sites and the employee commute to the CTU/coordinating Institute office (71%, 8.73 tonnes CO2e). Trial meetings contributed to 9% of the total emissions (1.13 tonnes CO2e). Additionally, business travel related to trial monitoring accounted for 90% of these emissions, despite most trial monitoring being conducted remotely, with only two site-based monitoring visits made during this trial. Emissions resulting from lab-based assessments for screening of 851 individuals and trial endpoint of 350 participants accounted for 10% of total emissions (1.27 tonnes CO2e), with the highest emissions attributed to cardiac MRI. Electricity emissions at the CTU contributed to 5% of total emissions (0.61 tonnes CO2e). Emissions due to the use of server space, medical equipment, and office supplies were minimal. Overall, clinical site-related activities contributed to more than half (53%) of the total emissions of the trial, with employee commute being the top contributor (4.78 tonnes CO2e). Discussion

We present a retrospective carbon audit of a multicentre clinical trial conducted in India, which used a remote monitoring approach. To our knowledge, this is the first attempt to assess the carbon footprint of a clinical trial being conducted in India. The total emissions from the trial were 12.38 tonnes CO2e. Since this is the first attempt to measure emissions of a trial conducted in India, we are unable to compare our estimates of trial-specific activities with other locally conducted trials. However, this is below the lower range of total emissions from international trials footprinted so far (17.6 tonnes CO2e to 2498 tonnes CO2e) reported in a recent systematic review [11].

Despite the publication of emissions from several trials [6,7,18,19] in recent years, emission estimates are not directly comparable across trials due to varying trial sizes and procedures. Emission estimates depend on a variety of trial characteristics such as the number of clinical sites, duration of trial, number of trial participants, transport of medication, trial related investigations and participant visits. Most carbon emissions reports of trials have been estimated using activity based operational data, with results reported under scopes 1,2 and 3, in alignment with the GHG protocol. However, a few studies have used life cycle assessment methods to quantify emissions associated with clinical trials. We utilised an activity-based approach to calculate carbon emissions arising from different activities in the clinical trial. Thus, due to the varied methodology employed in calculating emissions and the varying scope of activities considered [10,11], absolute amounts of carbon emissions may not be comparable across trials; however, relative comparisons of major contributors and hotspots can be drawn across studies.

The major carbon hotspots identified in this trial, such as employee commute, electricity usage at CTU, travel, and patient assessments, are in line with those reported previously [8]. Employee commute and electricity at CTU are hotspots in all trials due to the coordination requirements for multicentre trials. The electricity grid in India is even more carbon-intensive due to its reliance on coal for over 75% of the total power generation [20], in comparison to other HICs where renewable sources are used more commonly. Despite hybrid working policies, commute remains a major contributor due to the use of fossil fuel-dependent private modes of transport. In the Colchicine trial, meetings were conducted remotely due to the pandemic. However, even rare monitoring visits contributed to the emissions. This emphasises the need for less carbon-intensive monitoring methods, such as central data-driven monitoring and remote monitoring.

Over recent years, there has been an increasing emphasis on measuring the carbon footprint of clinical trials to devise strategies to lower their footprint [7,21]. The WHO has developed an operational framework for building climate-resilient and low-carbon health systems [22], which highlights the importance of conducting a baseline assessment of emissions. However, a recent cross-sectional survey conducted among representatives of academic research institutes who are members of the International Clinical Trials Centre Network in 15 countries found that half of the research institutes did not consider reducing emissions from their research projects, and the other half were unfamiliar with the concept. Moreover, the assessment of GHG emissions was not conducted by institutional review boards or other competent committees [23]. The lack of mandates from funding agencies and ethics bodies along with the low awareness of carbon accounting methods could attribute to research institutes not considering emissions of their research projects. Similarly, in India, carbon emissions are not considered when planning clinical trials, nor has an attempt been made to devise a strategy for measuring them. This emphasises the need to understand perceptions of researchers about the need for sustainable practices to reduce carbon emissions and understand awareness of regulatory bodies for the same.

We identified several challenges in quantifying emissions, which account for some of this study’s limitations. Firstly, the lack of region-specific emission factors for India led us to use estimates from the UK and the US as a proxy. This could have introduced biases, as emission factors vary significantly by country. Similarly, while assessing the carbon emissions of a trial conducted in South Africa, Siegfried et al identified the lack of regional emission factors [24]. They further noted that, when available, emission factors were higher for South Africa than those for the UK, underscoring the need for region-specific emission factors to ensure accurate estimations. However, the relative hotspots of emissions should remain the same. Secondly, data on emissions from the manufacturing of the IMP were unavailable because this is not currently a requirement in India. This is a crucial aspect, as India is one of the largest manufacturers of generic drugs in the world. Measuring the emissions and environmental footprint of these manufacturing units should become a regulatory requirement. Thirdly, as this was a retrospective exercise, we could not measure emissions from participants’ commutes to trial sites. This could have been avoided with prospective planning, allowing us to collect approximate distances from participants’ residences and the mode of travel at the time of screening. Finally, there could be underestimation of scope 2 emissions, especially at the clinical site as we could not isolate the electricity use for the trial. This is because most clinical sites in India generally do not have a designated research spaces and trial activities occur within routine care areas. Despite these limitations, our effort convincingly demonstrates the feasibility of assessing the carbon footprint of trials in India. It also provides a comprehensive framework that other researchers working in this domain can utilise. Moreover, this study aims to raise awareness in this domain and encourage the development and calculation of Indian-specific emission factors.

Carbon-footprint studies are needed in LMICs because the main emission drivers and the practical solutions differ from high-income settings, so imported solutions can miss emissions hotspots. Moreover, the standards of operation and emissions of same or similar technologies may be different. Without local data, credible targets and procurement rules cannot be developed. Given that LMICs that have contributed least to global emissions face major health harms from climate change, such evidence supports shared responsibility and financing. Finally, testing low-carbon trial designs in LMICs allows checking real trade-offs and to prevent carbon-intensive tasks being shifted to LMIC sites without measurement or reduction.

Based on the literature [5] and our experience of conducting trials, there are several ways trials can be made less carbon-intensive. First, wasteful research should be minimised by asking the relevant question and mandating a trial only when there is genuine uncertainty. While planning trials, researchers should ensure the use of sustainable trial designs. Only necessary trial outcome assessments (including specimen and radiological evaluations) must be carried out. There should be a strong rationale for storing specimens. Participant visits could be minimised in favour of telephonic follow-ups wherever possible. Furthermore, we should ensure that we collect only relevant data and data points aligned with the research question.

To reduce emissions during trial conduct, trials should adopt remote and risk-based monitoring, with investments in infrastructure wherever possible, to minimize travel-related emissions. Setting up a local supply chain for devices can also help reduce emissions associated with the long-distance transport of materials during trials. Devising strategies for optimisation of participant recruitment and retention can also contribute to reducing emissions by reducing the trial period and research waste. The use of paper may not directly lead to a significant reduction in carbon emissions. However, moving towards electronic documentation and the use of clinical trial management systems can reduce paper wastage and unnecessary communications. Furthermore, simple initiatives applied judiciously, such as sending fewer emails and keeping video off during calls, can help reduce emissions. Rationale for storage of paper and e-forms beyond the trial period can reduce server storage requirements. However, it is not known if the use of technology for the purpose of reducing carbon emissions could adversely affect the quality of trial. Further research is needed to measure the trade-offs in emissions in using technology versus traditional methods. Considering the findings from this trial and those of published trials, strategies for carbon reduction should focus on capacity building in this area. Strategies to increase awareness among researchers about carbon emissions should be adopted, and the development of a checklist for trialists to quantify carbon emissions for key activities in the planning stage might be helpful. At a national level, efforts to utilise green sources of energy, along with collective efforts to ensure reduced energy consumption and effective green waste management strategies, are required to make a significant impact on carbon emissions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the feasibility of measuring CO2e in a multicentre trial conducted in India. An important strength is hotspot clarity, enabling researchers and trial operations teams to target these hotspots to minimise emissions. It also shows that mostly remote trials still carry travel emissions, supporting a shift towards central and risk- based monitoring. Clinical trial units should make a conscious effort to identify hotspots of emissions and find ways to minimize CO2e without compromising trial quality. Future research should include embedding prospective carbon footprinting into protocols and development of India-specific emission factors.
