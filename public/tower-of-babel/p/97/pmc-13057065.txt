Ergothioneine promotes osteogenesis and angiogenesis through PI3K/AKT pathway and prevents glucocorticoid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head
Authors: Sun Xuecheng, Jin Gaoxin, Li Hang, Ma Xinlong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) has a high incidence worldwide, yet effective treatments remain lacking. Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are closely associated with ONFH development. Ergothioneine (EGT) is a potent antioxidant that can target various organs; however, its potential role in ONFH remains elusive. This study aimed to evaluate the protective effects of EGT against steroid-induced ONFH and to explore the underlying mechanisms involved. Murine bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were treated with EGT following dexamethasone induction. Subsequent experiments assessed cell viability and functional changes in each group. A steroid-induced rat model of ONFH was established, and the therapeutic efficacy of EGT was verified through imaging and histological analyses. Dexamethasone disrupted both the structure and function of mitochondria and induced apoptosis in BMMSCs and HUVECs. In contrast, EGT treatment activated the protein kinase B (AKT)/phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway under steroid stimulation, and the cellular damage was reversed. Moreover, in the steroid-induced ONFH rat model, EGT supplementation reduced bone structural destruction and the expression of degenerative biomarkers, along with maintaining angiogenic and osteogenic marker levels. EGT exerts a protective effect against steroid-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis by activating the AKT/PI3K-signaling pathway and maintaining osteogenesis and angiogenesis during ONFH development. Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a joint disorder that results from either chronic glucocorticoid (GC) use or femoral neck trauma1–4. During the early stages, ONFH is characterized by necrosis and cystic degeneration of the femoral head, whereas in advanced stages, collapse of the femoral head severely impairs hip joint function and reduces the quality of life of patients5–8. In recent years, the increased use of GCs has been associated with a higher ONFH incidence, which has become the most common form of nontraumatic femoral head necrosis. Although total hip arthroplasty remains an effective treatment for ONFH, the limited durability of artificial joints often necessitates young patients to undergo multiple invasive surgeries9–11. This warrants the need for novel, efficient treatments that can alleviate ONFH and promote quality of life in such patients. Additionally, the elusive specific pathogenesis of ONFH and limited treatment options further necessitate in-depth studies on ONFH pathogenesis for improving treatment outcomes12,13.

The onset and progression of GC-induced ONFH are thought to be strongly influenced by the effects of GCs on osteogenesis and angiogenesis. Reportedly, GCs exert complex regulatory impacts on skeletal cell metabolism. Excessive hormonal stimulation can induce oxidative stress and apoptosis in osteoblasts and vascular endothelial cells, thereby impairing bone formation and reducing blood supply. Consequently, high-dose GC exposure can lead to decreased bone quality, thereby contributing to the development of osteoporosis and osteonecrosis14. Ergothioneine (EGT), a sulphydryl compound naturally found in foods, exhibits potent antioxidant activity similar to that of glutathione15–18. It is synthesized by various microbes, including fungi and actinomycetes, has been deemed safe for human consumption, and exhibits a broad-ranging value in nutritional supplements and therapeutic antioxidants.

EGT possesses both antioxidant and mitochondrial-protective properties and has been shown to improve neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease19, Alzheimer’s disease20, and depression21, along with reducing the incidence and mortality associated with cardiovascular conditions. However, its function in bone metabolism remains poorly understood. Hence, this study aimed to investigate the effects of EGT on the osteogenic and angiogenic potential of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs) under oxidative stress conditions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate EGT’s role in bone metabolism. Additionally, we evaluated its therapeutic potential in GC-induced osteonecrosis. Through a combination of in vitro and in vivo experiments, the study aimed to elucidate a novel role for EGT in osteonecrosis, providing potential new therapeutic targets for treating ONFH22 (Scheme 1).

Methods

Acquisition of differentially expressed genes in SONFH

To obtain DEGs in SONFH relative to those from normal individuals, we conducted differential analysis on dataset GSE123568 from GEO. It included 30 peripheral serum samples from SONFH patients and 10 from healthy controls23,24. We obtained the DEGs and drew volcano and heat maps to display the results, with upregulated genes indicated in red and downregulated ones in blue in the dataset. The threshold was set to |log2FC| >0.5 and p value to < 0.0525.

Construction of a protein interaction network

To identify Ergothioneine’s drug targets, its 3D structure from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) was analyzed in Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swissadme.ch/), which yielded 24 targets with non-zero Probability. Another 162 targets were detected via PharmMapper (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/index.html). Merging and deduplicating these targets resulted in 185 unique genes. Intersecting these with 2,768 SONFH-related DEGs via a Venn diagram identified the candidate genes. To explore the interactions of the 29 key genes and their roles in biological and disease processes, a PPI network was constructed using STRING (www.string-db.org) with an interaction score of > 0.4, visualized in Cytoscape 3.8.2, producing a network of 18 nodes and 36 edges26,27.

GO functional enrichment and KEGG analysis

To explore the biological pathways and functions associated with the candidate genes, we conducted functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (encompassing biological process [BP], cellular component [CC], and molecular function [MF]) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. In the resulting plots, the point size reflects the number of enriched genes, with larger points indicating a higher gene count. The X-axis represents the gene ratio, where a larger value signifies a greater proportion of genes in the enriched pathway. The Y-axis lists the names of the enriched pathways.

Cell culture and cell-viability assays

BMMSC and HUVECs were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China. BMSCs were cultivated in Hyclone α-MEM medium enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA). HUVECs were maintained in ECM medium supplemented with 5% FBS and cultured within a humidified incubator maintained at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere28. In the experiment involving treatment with dexamethasone (DEX) (MCE, HY-14648) and EGT (MCE, HY-N1914), the cells were initially seeded in a 96-well plate at a concentration of 2500 cells/well. Once the cells had adhered, the existing medium was replaced with a fresh medium. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to EGT for 2 h before the introduction of DEX. After an additional 48-h of cultivation, cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Biosharp, China) as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. Finally, a microplate reader was used to determine the absorbance of each well at a wavelength of 450 nm. BMMSCs were divided into the following three groups: (A) negative-control group; (B) DEX (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), BMMSCs were cultured with 400 µM DEX for 48 h; (C) EGT + DEX, cells were pre-cultured with 100 µM EGT for 2 h and then incubated with DEX for another 48 h29–31.

Western blotting

Post-treatment, the protein was extracted from cells and subjected to separation through SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (WJ002; Epizyme, China). The membranes were processed with a blocking agent (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and incubated overnight with specific primary antibodies (Table S1) at 4 °C. After thorough washing, the membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-linked anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody at a 1:5000 dilution. The signals were detected using Thermo Scientific’s enhanced chemiluminescence system. The protein expression was quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA), and the signal was normalized to that of β-actin.

Real-time quantitative PCR

From the treated cells, total mRNA was isolated with the TRIzol reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology). The isolated total mRNA was then used as a template to synthesize complementary DNA by using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Vazyme, China). Real-time PCR was conducted by using an RNA PCR kit (Vazyme), with all procedures conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, as follows: 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s, for a total of 40 cycles. The PCR primer sequences used in the study are listed in Table S2.

Immunofluorescent staining

In this study, immunofluorescence analyses were performed following a previously described method. BMMSC and HUVECs were seeded on round coverslips in a 24-well culture plate at 5000 cells/well. After 24 h of different treatments, coverslips were incubated with primary antibodies and fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Table S3), with nuclei stained using DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology). The images were acquired using an IX51 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Reactive oxygen species quantification

In this study, the intracellular ROS levels were measured by using an ROS assay kit (S0033S; Beyotime Biotechnology) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. BMMSC and HUVECs were washed twice with sterile PBS, stained with 10 µM DCFDA, and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark with gentle mixing every 5 min. After three washes with the basal culture medium, the cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (IX51; Olympus).

MitoTracker assay

In this study, the mitochondrial function and membrane potential were assessed using MitoTracker (C1049B; Beyotime Biotechnology). The cells were incubated with culture medium containing 20 nM MitoTracker for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium, and the cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (IX51; Olympus).

JC-1 assay

In this study, the mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using a JC-1 assay kit (C2006; Beyotime Biotechnology). Following treatments in 24-well plates, BMMSC and HUVECs were incubated with the JC-1 staining solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. Each well was then washed twice with the JC-1 staining buffer. The fluorescence intensity was analyzed by using a fluorescence microscope (IX51; Olympus). The changes in the mitochondrial membrane potential were assessed based on the ratio of red-to-green fluorescence32.

Flow cytometry

In this study, apoptosis in treated BMMSC and HUVECs was assessed using the BD Pharmingen FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (BD Biosciences, USA). The cells were incubated with propidium iodide and annexin V-FITC for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Apoptosis was then quantified by using the CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA), and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Alkaline phosphatase staining

In this study, ALP staining was performed on BMMSC cells after 14 days of culture in osteogenic medium. The staining procedure involved incubating the cells with the BCIP/NBT working solution (C3206; Beyotime, China) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. A light microscope was used to capture the images, and the signal was quantified using ImageJ software.

Model setup and drug treatment

Twenty-five Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (male, age: 10 weeks, weight: 400 ± 50 g) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The rats were housed under controlled, identical specific pathogen-free (SPF) standard environmental conditions (23 ± 2 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and were allowed to move freely. The rats were assigned to the following 3 groups of 3 each: (1) negative-control, (2) model rats treated with vehicle (PBS), and (3) model rats treated with EGT.

A GC-induced ONFH model was constructed as described previously. Briefly, model rats were treated daily for 3 days via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of lipopolysaccharide (20 µg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich). Over the next 4 days and then once a week for the next 5 weeks, the rats were administered methylprednisolone (MPS; 60 mg/kg; Pfizer, USA). MPS was administered i.m. alternately into the left and right gluteus muscles. The negative-control rats were administered PBS in place of both lipopolysaccharide and MPS33. Ergothioneine (200 mg/kg; GL Biochem, China) was injected at the same time as MPS once per week i.g. in the manufactured animal models to serve as the treatment group. The animals were sacrificed after the respective treatments, and the femoral heads were collected34. The experimental animals were anesthetized via i.p. injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). Analgesia was also provided via a subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine (0.01–0.05 mg/kg), with the analgesic effect lasting for 8–12 h. The animals were then euthanized using carbon dioxide euthanasia. Femoral head samples were collected from each animal. The samples were immersed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses.

Micro-CT analysis

In this study, high-resolution micro-CT (Quantum GX2; PerkinElmer, Japan) was used to evaluate changes in the femoral head structure. The scanning settings were as follows: 15 μm isometric resolution, 70 kV X-ray energy, and 200 µA current. The weight-bearing region under the articular cortical bone was selected for analysis. After 3D reconstruction, parameters such as BMD, BV/TV, BS/BV, Tb.N, Tb.Th, and Tb.Sp were assessed. Moreover, coronal image analysis was performed to determine the area between the femoral head’s upper edge and the tangent circle, and the cortical changes were documented.

Histological and immunohistochemical staining

In this study, femoral head samples underwent a series of processing steps. They were first fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and then decalcified in 10% EDTA (G1105; Servicebio, China) for 4 weeks. Following this, the samples were embedded in paraffin, sliced into 6-µm sections, and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) to obtain a general view of the specimen and evaluate the trabecular structure.

For tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining, the paraffin sections were stained with a TRAP staining kit (G1050; Servicebio). Briefly, the paraffin sections were stained with tartrate buffer containing naphthol AS-BI phosphate and pararosaniline chloride at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark and then counterstained with hematoxylin. The section images were acquired by using an IX71-SIF microscope (Olympus).

Immunohistochemical staining was performed to define the expression of markers related to osteogenesis and the vasculature. Briefly, the sections were dewaxed and gradient hydrated to retrieve the antigens. Then, the samples were incubated with primary antibodies, including antibodies recognizing osteocalcin (ab133612), RANKL (ab52917), and CD31 (ab9498), and the corresponding secondary antibodies (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The chromogenic reaction was induced by using a DAB Kit (Beyotime). The images were gathered with an IX71-SIF microscope (Olympus). Positively stained cells and relevant areas were measured using ImageJ, and the analyses were performed by two independent observers.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least thrice. The Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to assess data normality, and Levene’s test was applied to evaluate the homogeneity of variance. Normally distributed data were presented as the mean ±standard deviation (SD), whereas the non-normally distributed data were presented as the median and interquartile range. Graphical comparisons between the groups displayed means with 95% confidence intervals. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired two-tailed t-test was applied when the parametric assumptions were met; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U-test was applied. For multiple groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was performed based on the experimental design, followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test whenever appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed P < 0.05. The results were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). The sample sizes for each experimental group are specified in the figure legends.

Results

Analysis of SONFH-associated DEGs

The volcano plot identified 2,768 significant DEGs, including 1,690 upregulated and 1,078 downregulated genes in SONFH samples compared with those in controls (Fig. 1A). The heatmap (Fig. 1B) illustrates the expression patterns of the top 10 upregulated and downregulated genes based on log2FC values, highlighting potential differences in oxidative stress status between normal and SONFH samples. Prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2), a key enzyme in inflammation and arachidonic acid metabolism, is often upregulated under oxidative stress conditions. In SONFH, PTGS2 upregulation enhanced inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress, leading to an accumulation of free radicals that further exacerbated cellular damage. Similarly, lectin, galactoside-binding, soluble, 2, associated with oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammation, may intensify oxidative injury by modulating apoptosis in SONFH. Its upregulation in SONFH likely promoted apoptosis and tissue necrosis. Collectively, these results indicate that oxidative stress is a major pathological hallmark of SONFH.

A Venn diagram analysis of the 2,768 DEGs and 185 druggable genes revealed 29 overlapping candidate genes (Fig. 1C). In the PPI network, nodes and edges indicated genes and regulatory interactions across subpathways, respectively. Node color corresponded to their degree, a measure reflecting connectivity, with protein kinase B (AKT)1 exhibiting the highest value (Fig. 1D). Subsequent GO (Fig. 1E) and KEGG pathway (Fig. 1F) analyses of these 29 genes revealed phosphoinositide 3‑kinase (PI3K) binding as a key pathway. These results suggest that EGT may exert therapeutic effects in SONFH by modulating the PI3K/AKT-signaling pathway.

EGT inhibits DEX-induced dysfunction of osteogenesis

Decreased osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells is a major contributing factor to ONFH development. Hence, the protective effect of EGT on osteogenic differentiation ability was assessed. Notably, BMMSCs were cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium, and expression of osteogenesis-related genes was examined at both mRNA and protein levels under various treatments. DEX markedly reduced type I collagen (COL1), bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2), and Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) levels (Fig. 2A–D), indicating that DEX interfered with the osteogenic differentiation capacity of the BMMSCs. Remarkably, EGT treatment restored the expression of these DEX-stimulated osteogenic markers at both mRNA and protein levels, suggesting its restorative ability for the impaired osteogenic ability of BMMSCs. These findings are consistent with the immunofluorescence results of BMP2 (Fig. 2E, F).

To further evaluate the protective effect of EGT on osteogenic capacity, ALP staining was performed. Notably, DEX markedly decreased endogenous ALP activity, whereas EGT addition restored ALP activity (Fig. 2G, H). Moreover, at the mRNA level, DEX downregulated COL1, OCN, osteopontin, RUNX2, and ALP levels, whereas EGT restored their expression (Fig. 2I). Altogether, these results suggest that EGT reverses DEX-induced impairment of cellular osteogenic differentiation in BMMSCs.

EGT inhibits DEX-induced apoptosis

GC-induced apoptosis can drive ONFH development. To examine if EGT can attenuate apoptosis, we assessed the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins, including cleaved-caspase-3 and B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2)-associated X protein (BAX), and the anti-apoptotic BCL2 protein in BMMSCs (Fig. 3A) and HUVECs (Fig. 4A). After 48 h of DEX exposure, western blotting revealed increased cleaved-caspase-3 and BAX levels, and decreased BCL2 expression. EGT treatment effectively reversed these DEX-induced changes in the expression of apoptosis-related proteins. Flow cytometry was performed to assess the apoptosis rate in BMMSCs, and DEX markedly increased the apoptosis rate compared to the control treatment. In contrast, EGT supplementation attenuated the pro-apoptotic effect of DEX in BMMSCs (Fig. 3B) and HUVECs (Fig. 4B). Fluorescence staining of different cells further showed that DEX-induced increase in cleaved-caspase3 was reversed under EGT treatment in BMMSCs (Fig. 3D, H). The tube formation assay of junctions, branching, and branch length was used to quantify the angiogenic potential of HUVECs35. Notably, DEX stimulation induced apoptosis and significantly disrupted vascular lumen formation, whereas EGT treatment largely restored the DEX-induced damage in vascular lumen formation, indicating that EGT can improve DEX-induced impairment of angiogenic capacity (Fig. 4F, K). Similarly, the wound scratch assay also supported this conclusion by showing enhanced cell migration and wound closure upon EGT treatment (Fig. 4G, L).

EGT mitigates DEX-induced mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondria are highly dynamic organelles that play essential roles in regulating energy metabolism, cellular proliferation, and cell death. Given that DEX induces oxidative stress and disrupts mitochondrial function and structure, along with the findings of the present study indicating that EGT mitigates DEX-induced cellular damage, we evaluated whether EGT can protect against DEX-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Intracellular ROS production in DEX-stimulated BMMSCs and HUVECs was measured, with and without EGT. DEX treatment markedly increased fluorescence intensity, indicating high ROS production, in both BMMSCs (Fig. 3C, G) and HUVECs (Fig. 4C, G). Interestingly, EGT could effectively reverse the increase in DEX-induced cellular ROS levels. These results suggest that EGT may alleviate the DEX-induced excessive oxidative stress state in both cell types.

Mitochondrial function was further evaluated through the JC-1 assay. In both BMMSCs (Fig. 3E, I) and HUVECs (Fig. 4E, J), DEX treatment considerably increased green fluorescence, indicating a harmful change in the mitochondrial membrane potential, whereas EGT treatment drastically reduced the DEX-induced accumulation of green fluorescence-emitting JC-1 monomers. The results of the MitoTracker assay further supported the EGT-mediated improvement of mitochondrial function in the presence of DEX. Notably, the mitochondrial morphology was more swollen and fragmented in the DEX-stimulated group, and EGT reversed this morphological alteration in mitochondria. Collectively, these data indicate that DEX causes mitochondrial disorganization in BMMSCs (Fig. 3E, I) and HUVECs (Fig. 4D, I), and EGT confers a protective effect to maintain mitochondrial structure and function in both cell types, thereby demonstrating therapeutic potential against ONFH.

EGT activates the PI3K/AKT pathway

AKT and PI3K pathways are critical to osteoblast function, and previous studies show that EGT can interact with the AKT and PI3K signaling pathways. Hence, we investigated the involvement of these associated factors in the protective effects of the EGT on mitigating DEX-induced effects on BMMSCs. Western blotting was performed to examine the phosphorylated forms of PI3K and AKT (p-PI3K and p-AKT, respectively) in BMMSCs (Fig. 5A). After 48 h of DEX exposure, phosphorylated PI3K and AKT levels notably decreased compared with those in the control, whereas EGT addition reversed this alteration (Fig. 5B, C). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5D, E) confirmed that EGT restored p-AKT and p-PI3K levels in the presence of DEX. These results collectively suggest that EGT activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, contributing to its regulatory roles in promoting cell survival and proliferation under DEX-induced stress.

Supplementation with EGT alleviates GC-induced ONFH models in rats

To evaluate the effects of EGT in vivo, a GC-induced ONFH rat model was established in this study, and EGT was administered through i.p injection (Fig. 6A). In the control, rats without GC exposure presented with a smooth and uniformly colored cartilage surface of the femoral head. Conversely, the cartilage surface in the weight-bearing area of the femoral head in rats with ONFH was abnormal in color, with a rough or collapsed surface. Treatment with EGT reduced the severity of these lesions, greatly relieving the general appearance of the femoral head (Fig. 6B).

To further evaluate the morphological changes, micro-CT scans of the femoral head samples were performed, with which cortical and cancellous bone could also be visualized. The sagittal, coronal, and cross-sectional views and the 3D reconstruction revealed severe trabecular bone loss and collapse in the weight-bearing area in the vehicle-treated rat models, whereas EGT treatment markedly alleviated this damage to the femoral head (Fig. 6C). Further Analysis of CT scans indicated that EGT treatment improved BMD, BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th compared with those in vehicle-treated rat ONFH models, indicating the protection of the bony structure in ONFH models (Fig. 6D–I).

Histological analysis further confirmed that EGT attenuated impaired bone formation. H&E staining revealed higher numbers of empty lacunae and a higher degree of trabecular collapse in PBS-treated rat ONFH model compared with those in negative control rats. In contrast, EGT treatment could mitigate these disordered ONFH developments (Fig. 7A, B). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry showed that EGT administration largely preserved the expression of osteoblast biomarker OCN compared with that in PBS-treated rats (Fig. 7C, D).

To further investigate the bone metabolism in the femoral head of the ONFH model, TRAP staining (Fig. 7E, F) and immunohistochemistry were performed for RANKL (Fig. 7G, H). The results revealed elevated RANKL levels in the subchondral bone and increased TRAP-positive osteoclasts, indicating enhanced osteoclast formation and activation, along with increased bone resorption, and EGT treatment inhibited this change. Because impaired angiogenesis is a characteristic morphological feature of ONFH, immunohistochemistry was performed to assess the expression of CD31, a marker of vascular differentiation. Notably, blood vessel formation was found to be diminished in ONFH rat models; however, EGT treatment retained angiogenic ability in the femoral head of the ONFH model (Fig. 7I, J).

Discussion

ONFH often results in hip dysfunction and severe disability, significantly reducing patients’ quality of life and imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems36. Previous research has identified impaired osteoblastogenesis, osteoclastogenesis, and angiogenesis as key cytological mechanisms driving the development of ONFH22,37,38. Bioinformatic analyses offer an effective approach to identifying disease-associated genes and elucidating their biological functions. In this study, we systematically integrated data from multiple public databases to identify ONFH-related genes. By intersecting ONFH-associated genes with the predicted molecular targets of the potential anti-ONFH drug EGT, we identified AKT as a critical node. GO analysis further revealed that the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway showed the strongest correlation with ONFH. Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations, as we did not experimentally validate the pathway’s role in ONFH, either through inhibitor assays or by knocking out key genes within the pathway. Despite these limitations, our bioinformatics and network pharmacology analyses establish a robust framework for understanding the potential regulatory influence of EGT on the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. By integrating current literature with identified molecular targets of EGT, our findings provide novel insights into the underlying mechanisms of ONFH and highlight EGT as a promising therapeutic candidate, warranting further experimental validation.

Our in vitro experiments demonstrated that EGT mitigates the inhibitory effect of DEX on osteoblast activity39. Specifically, EGT treatment enhanced the phosphorylation and consequently activated AKT and PI3K in DEX-stimulated BMMSCs. These findings suggest that EGT sustains the function of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to counteract the detrimental effects of DEX. Furthermore, EGT reduced caspase-3 activation through PI3K/AKT pathway stimulation, consistent with previous reports identifying similar mechanisms in potential therapeutic agents for ONFH40. Abnormal alterations in mitochondrial membrane permeability and morphology are frequently observed in various cell types following DEX exposure. In our study, EGT mitigated these mitochondrial abnormalities, suggesting its potential protective role against ONFH.

The lipopolysaccharide/MPS-induced rat model serves as a reliable preclinical model for GC-induced ONFH39,41. In this study, we successfully reproduced characteristic end-stage pathological features consistent with those reported in previous research. Micro-CT remains a key technique for the quantitative assessment of bone microstructure. Using this method, we observed that rats in the ONFH model developed a distinct cavity beneath the subchondral bone, accompanied by structural collapse of the femoral head. Compared with control rats, the ONFH model group exhibited decreased Tb.Th, Tb.N, BV/TV, and Tb.Sp, with an increased proportion of apoptotic cells. Notably, the femoral heads of rats treated with EGT, including those in the prevention group, displayed fewer empty osteocytic lacunae, reduced apoptosis, and improved trabecular architecture and bone tissue integrity41. Therefore, both micro-CT and histological analyses confirmed that EGT effectively protected the subchondral bone in the weight-bearing region of the ONFH rat model. These findings align with the observed protective effects of EGT on dexamethasone-challenged preosteoblasts in vitro.

A plausible explanation for these results is that EGT preserves the activity of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which is typically suppressed by GCs42. The AKT pathway is well recognized for its essential role in bone regeneration and the maintenance of skeletal homeostasis during ONFH progression43,44. In this study, GC administration inhibited PI3K/AKT pathway activity, whereas EGT treatment prevented this suppression and consequently attenuated ONFH development.

In summary, our findings suggest that EGT functions as an activator of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, counteracting steroid-induced suppression of this pathway and thereby mitigating the progression of ONFH. These results highlight EGT as a promising therapeutic candidate for the treatment of ONFH.

In recent years, advancements in synthesis technologies have reduced the production cost of EGT, facilitating an increase in related clinical research. A recent double-blind, placebo-controlled trial involving 14 elderly patients with dementia evaluated EGT supplementation (25 mg per capsule, three times weekly) over 1 year45. The results demonstrated that long-term EGT administration is safe and that participants receiving EGT exhibited moderate improvement in the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test. Although the study’s small sample size limits the generalizability of its conclusions, it provides valuable preliminary evidence for future investigations. Further studies are warranted to clarify the molecular targets, bioavailability, and metabolic pathways of orally administered EGT, as well as to define its optimal therapeutic concentration range for safe and effective clinical use.
