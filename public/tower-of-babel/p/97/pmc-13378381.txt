Sex differences in disease burden in China from 1990 to 2023 triangulating GBD and CHARLS
Authors: Taixiang Liu, Zichen Zhang, Wenwen Lv, Wei Zhou, Zheng Chen, Jinxin Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySex and gender shape health across the life course, but nationally comparable evidence on how fatal and non-fatal disease burden differs between females and males in China is needed for health planning. We analyzed Global Burden of Disease 2023 estimates for the 20 leading level-3 causes of age-standardized disability-adjusted life-year rates in China, compared patterns with global estimates, projected trends to 2030, and triangulated selected findings using CHARLS 2018. In 2023, males had greater burden from mortality-driven cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and injuries, especially stroke, ischemic heart disease, lung cancer, falls, and road injuries. Female disadvantage was concentrated in gynecological diseases, musculoskeletal disorders, dementia, depressive symptoms, functional limitation, and older-age fall burden. Smoking, alcohol use, occupational risks, and household air pollution contributed differently by sex. These findings support sex-responsive prevention, rehabilitation, and long-term care strategies in China.

Introduction

Sex and gender are fundamental modifiers of health, disease, and medicine across the life course.1 Sex refers to biological characteristics, including chromosomes, reproductive anatomy, and hormonal milieu, whereas gender refers to socially constructed roles, behaviors, identities, and power relations that shape exposures, health-related practices, occupational patterns, health-care access, and treatment pathways.2,3 These dimensions interact with age, socioeconomic position, and geography, contributing to divergent health trajectories between females and males.

Recent global burden analyses have shown that sex differences are not peripheral observations but central features of population health measurement. A systematic analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021 reported that females more often bear a higher burden from morbidity-dominant conditions, such as low back pain, depressive disorders, and headache disorders, whereas males more often bear a higher burden from mortality-driven conditions, including ischemic heart disease, respiratory and other cancers, and injuries.4 Recent GBD-based studies of musculoskeletal and headache disorders in Asian countries and spinal cord injury worldwide further show that updated burden estimation and projection analyses can reveal substantial heterogeneity by age, sex, and geography.5,6

Country-specific evidence remains essential because the magnitude, composition, and policy implications of sex differences are shaped by demographic transition, population aging, risk-factor exposure, health-system performance, and changing gender roles. China is undergoing rapid population aging, urbanization, epidemiological transition, and shifts in social and occupational roles.7,8 These transitions are occurring alongside the Healthy China 2030 strategy, a national medium-to long-term health plan that places greater emphasis on prevention, health promotion, and population-wide risk reduction.9,10 Although previous studies have described sex differences in selected conditions such as cancer, cardiovascular disease, and mental disorders in China,7,11,12 a systematic assessment of how the leading causes of health loss differ between females and males remains limited. Such evidence is important not only for documenting inequalities, but also for distinguishing mortality-dominant male disadvantage from morbidity-dominant female disadvantage in ways that may better inform prevention, service design, rehabilitation, and long-term care planning.

Against this background, we used GBD 2023 estimates to examine whether the leading causes of disease burden in China show reproducible sex-specific patterns in overall disability-adjusted life-year (DALY) burden, years of life lost (YLL)-years lived with disability (YLD) composition, life-course distribution, temporal change, and risk attribution. We hypothesized that males would bear a higher burden from mortality-driven cardiovascular, cancer, and injury causes, whereas females would bear a relatively greater burden from aging-related and musculoskeletal conditions dominated by non-fatal health loss. To strengthen geographic interpretation and external validity, we further compared China-specific patterns with global estimates and used the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) 2018 as an external individual-level triangulation dataset for selected morbidity and behavioral indicators.13 Because 2030 represents a major policy milestone in China’s national health strategy, we used short-term projections to 2030 to examine whether current sex-specific burden patterns are likely to persist by the Healthy China 2030 horizon, while interpreting these projections as trend-based extrapolations rather than policy-effect forecasts.9,10

Results

Female-specific or female-leaning burden was concentrated in gynecological diseases and selected morbidity-dominant conditions. Gynecological diseases contributed an ASDR of 631.57 per 100,000 in females. Point estimates also suggested higher female burden for Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, with a female excess of 178.25 per 100,000; however, the corresponding uncertainty interval crossed zero (95% UI−374.83 to 731.33), and this finding should therefore be interpreted as an imprecise female-leaning pattern rather than a clear female excess. Other musculoskeletal disorders also showed a female-leaning pattern, consistent with the broader concentration of morbidity-dominant burden in women (Figure 1B and Table S1).

YLL-YLD composition of sex differences

In males, YLLs accounted for 93.2% of DALYs for ischemic heart disease, 89.6% for stroke, and 98.8% for tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer. The corresponding proportions in females were 91.4%, 84.2%, and 98.7%, respectively. By contrast, female-specific or female-leaning conditions were more morbidity-dominant. In females, YLDs accounted for 99.7% of DALYs for gynecological diseases and 94.1% for other musculoskeletal disorders. Falls also showed a more disability-dominant pattern among females than males, with YLDs contributing 88.7% of female DALYs compared with 82.4% of male DALYs. Sex differences across the life course in China

Sex differences varied strongly across age groups, indicating that the burden gap between females and males was not static across the life course. In children and adolescents aged 0–19 years, males had higher DALY rates for neonatal disorders and road injuries, with relative male excesses of 29.5% and 50.9%, respectively. The male excess in road injuries was most pronounced in young adulthood. Among adults aged 20–44 years, the male DALY rate for road injuries was 231.6% higher than the female rate, indicating that injury-related sex differences emerged early and peaked during young adult life. For major cardiovascular and cancer causes, the male disadvantage widened substantially from midlife onward. Among adults aged 45–59 years, male DALY rates were 196.2% higher for ischemic heart disease, 129.1% higher for stroke, and 149.6% higher for tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer. In absolute terms, these gaps remained large in older adults; among those aged 60–74 years, the male-female difference reached 3,217.98 per 100,000 for ischemic heart disease and 4,650.96 per 100,000 for stroke (Figures 3, S1 and S2, and Table S2).

For falls, the male excess progressively attenuated with age and reversed by point estimate in the oldest age group. Among adults aged 90 years or older, females had a 13.1% higher fall-related DALY rate by point estimate, although the uncertainty interval crossed zero. Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias also showed a female-leaning pattern from middle age onward, but the age-specific estimates were imprecise and should be interpreted cautiously (Figures 3, S1 and S2, and Table S2).

Changes from 1990 to 2023 in China

Between 1990 and 2023, ASDRs declined for most of the 20 leading causes in China. The largest relative reductions in the both-sex population were observed for lower respiratory infections (−93.2%), neonatal disorders (−88.7%), self-harm (−78.1%), congenital birth defects (−76.6%), and hypertensive heart disease (−71.4%). Sex gaps narrowed markedly for several communicable, neonatal, and injury-related causes. The absolute male-female gap for lower respiratory infections declined from 1,101.83 per 100,000 in 1990 to 104.74 per 100,000 in 2023, while the gap for road injuries declined from 1,499.44 to 416.88 per 100,000. The male excess for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease also narrowed substantially, from 1,860.27 to 285.35 per 100,000 (Figure 4 and Table S2).

However, relative male disadvantage widened for several major non-communicable diseases. For ischemic heart disease, the relative male excess increased from 36.3% in 1990 to 70.2% in 2023. Corresponding increases were observed for stroke (26.7%–78.7%), tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer (111.1%–149.2%), colon and rectum cancer (29.9%–102.3%), and esophageal cancer (138.7%–357.7%). Female-leaning point estimates persisted for Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias and other musculoskeletal disorders, whereas diabetes mellitus shifted from a slight female excess in 1990 to a male excess by point estimate in 2023 (Figure 4 and Table S2).

Global comparison of leading disease burden

The global comparison showed that the broad direction of sex differences was similar to the China-specific pattern, but the leading cause composition differed. Globally, the three highest ASDRs in 2023 were neonatal disorders, ischemic heart disease, and stroke, with both-sex rates of 2,703.36, 2,128.58, and 1,737.13 per 100,000, respectively. In China, by contrast, the three highest ASDRs were stroke, ischemic heart disease, and falls (Figures 1C, 1D and 2B, and Table S5).

Among the global top 20 causes, male ASDRs were higher by point estimate for 16 causes, while female ASDRs were higher by point estimate for four causes, including Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, other musculoskeletal disorders, HIV/AIDS, and malaria. The largest male-female gaps globally were observed for ischemic heart disease (male 2,770.26 vs. female 1,534.86 per 100,000; difference 1,235.40), road injuries (1,347.95 vs. 494.86; difference 853.09), neonatal disorders (3,041.56 vs. 2,343.40; difference 698.16), stroke (2,028.33 vs. 1,471.17; difference 557.16), and tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer (724.32 vs. 308.32; difference 416.00) (Figure 2B and Table S5).

Female-leaning global patterns were observed for other musculoskeletal disorders, for which the female ASDR exceeded the male ASDR by 209.47 per 100,000, and Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, for which the female ASDR exceeded the male ASDR by 140.08 per 100,000 by point estimate. These global findings support the broader pattern that men carry greater fatal cardiometabolic, cancer, and injury burden, whereas women have greater burden from selected disability-dominant and aging-related conditions. Compared with the global pattern, China showed a more stroke-centered male excess, whereas the global pattern included a larger contribution from neonatal disorders and road injuries (Figures 2B and S5, and Tables S5, S6, S7, and S8).

From 1990 to 2023, global ASDRs declined substantially for diarrheal diseases, tuberculosis, lower respiratory infections, stroke, neonatal disorders, ischemic heart disease, road injuries, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. In contrast, globa1l ASDRs increased for diabetes mellitus, HIV/AIDS, other musculoskeletal disorders, and Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias. Thus, both China and the global pattern indicate a transition from infectious and early-life causes toward chronic, metabolic, injury-related, and aging-related causes, although the magnitude and ranking of these changes differ by location (Figure S6 and Tables S5, S6, S7, and S8).

Risk-factor contributions to sex differences

Marked sex differences were observed in risk-factor attribution. In China, smoking was the dominant contributor to male excess burden for several cancers and cardiopulmonary causes. The smoking-attributable fraction was 82.3% in males versus 27.0% in females for tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer, corresponding to a male excess of 55.3 percentage points. Similar male excesses in smoking-attributable fractions were observed for esophageal cancer (57.5% vs. 8.9%; difference 48.6 percentage points), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (66.6% vs. 19.8%; difference 46.7 percentage points), ischemic heart disease (38.2% vs. 8.0%; difference 30.1 percentage points), and stroke (30.1% vs. 5.4%; difference 24.6 percentage points) (Figures 5, S3 and S4).Figure 5Sex differences in risk factor attributable disease burden in China in 2023Plots show sex-specific population-attributable fractions for selected cause-risk pairs in China in 2023. Occupational risks also contributed disproportionately to male burden in China, particularly for falls and road injuries. The occupational-risk population-attributable fraction was 53.8% in males versus 29.5% in females for falls, a difference of 24.3 percentage points, and 60.0% versus 36.2% for road injuries, a difference of 23.8 percentage points. In contrast, household air pollution from solid fuels contributed relatively more to female burden for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (58.4% in females vs. 53.1% in males), lower respiratory infections (34.3% vs. 30.5%), stroke (34.3% vs. 31.4%), and tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer (32.4% vs. 28.0%). Selected metabolic risks also showed modest female excesses, including high body-mass index for hypertensive heart disease (22.9% in females vs. 20.8% in males) and ischemic heart disease (5.0% vs. 4.3%) (Figures 5, S3 and S4).

The global risk-factor comparison showed a similar structure. Globally, smoking showed the largest male excess in population-attributable fractions for several mortality-driven causes. The smoking-attributable fraction was 71.5% in males versus 31.0% in females for tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer, corresponding to a male-female difference of 40.6 percentage points. Similar male excesses were observed for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (47.6% vs. 13.4%; difference 34.2 percentage points), ischemic heart disease (27.0% vs. 5.0%; difference 22.0 percentage points), and stroke (20.7% vs. 3.2%; difference 17.5 percentage points) (Figure S7).

Occupational risks and alcohol use also contributed more to male-attributable burden globally. Occupational risks accounted for higher population-attributable fractions in males than females for road injuries (28.4% vs. 11.5%; difference 16.9 percentage points) and falls (20.8% vs. 8.6%; difference 12.2 percentage points). High alcohol use showed male excesses for cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases (32.3% vs. 15.2%; difference 17.2 percentage points) and road injuries (4.0% vs. 0.7%; difference 3.3 percentage points). By contrast, several risks showed higher attributable fractions in females globally. Low bone mineral density contributed more to falls in females than males (31.0% vs. 16.8%; female excess 14.2 percentage points). Household air pollution from solid fuels showed higher population-attributable fractions in females for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (19.4% vs. 14.8%; female excess 4.6 percentage points), lower respiratory infections (female excess 3.4 percentage points), and stroke (female excess 3.4 percentage points). High body-mass index and low physical activity also contributed more to diabetes burden in females than males, with female excesses of 5.1 and 3.2 percentage points, respectively (Figure S7).

BAPC projection and sensitivity analysis

BAPC projections suggested that the leading causes of disease burden would remain dominated by non-communicable diseases and injuries by 2030. In China, ischemic heart disease was projected to become the leading cause of ASDR by 2030, followed by stroke and falls. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer, Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, and diabetes mellitus were also projected to remain among the leading contributors to disease burden (Figures 1A and 1B).

The global BAPC sensitivity analysis suggested that the direction of major projected sex gaps was stable under alternative trajectory assumptions. Under the baseline BAPC projection, the largest projected male excesses in 2030 were observed for ischemic heart disease, road injuries, stroke, tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer, and neonatal disorders. The projected male-female ASDR gap was 1,638.63 per 100,000 for ischemic heart disease, 781.81 for road injuries, 544.90 for stroke, 489.73 for tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer, and 408.55 for neonatal disorders (Figure S8).

Across the alternative sensitivity scenarios, the direction of the major projected sex gaps was broadly consistent. The projected male excess for ischemic heart disease ranged from 1,144.31 to 2,305.71 per 100,000 across scenarios, and the corresponding ranges were 545.97–1,100.09 for road injuries, 380.52–766.72 for stroke, and 342.00–689.11 for tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer. Female-leaning projected gaps also remained directionally stable for Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias and other musculoskeletal disorders, with projected female excesses ranging from 292.70 to 589.76 per 100,000 and from 142.08 to 286.28 per 100,000, respectively (Figure S8).

External triangulation using CHARLS

External triangulation using CHARLS 2018 supported the main sex-specific patterns observed in the GBD analysis among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. The analytic sample included 19,542 adults aged 45 years or older, including 10,240 women and 9,302 men. Men had markedly higher prevalence of major behavioral risk factors. Current smoking was reported by 51.4% of men and 4.6% of women, corresponding to a male-female difference of 46.8 percentage points and an adjusted odds ratio of 24.09 (95% confidence interval [CI] 21.70 to 26.73) for men versus women. For selected chronic conditions related to male-predominant GBD burden, CHARLS showed directionally consistent patterns. Men had higher prevalence of chronic lung disease than women (17.5% vs. 13.8%; difference 3.7 percentage points; adjusted odds ratio 1.38, 95% CI 1.27 to 1.50), with the gap widening to 7.2 percentage points among adults aged 75 years or older. Men also had slightly higher prevalence of self-reported stroke than women (7.8% vs. 6.8%; adjusted odds ratio 1.12, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.26) (Figures 6A–6D).

By contrast, women had higher prevalence of non-fatal morbidity and functional impairment indicators. Arthritis/rheumatism was more common in women than men (44.3% vs. 33.4%; male-versus-female adjusted odds ratio 0.67, 95% CI 0.63 to 0.71), as were falls (22.3% vs. 16.9%; adjusted odds ratio 0.72, 95% CI 0.67 to 0.77), ADL limitation (22.4% vs. 14.9%; adjusted odds ratio 0.63, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.68), IADL limitation (28.9% vs. 18.7%; adjusted odds ratio 0.61, 95% CI 0.56 to 0.65), and depressive symptoms (43.5% vs. 29.6%; adjusted odds ratio 0.59, 95% CI 0.56 to 0.63). The female excess in arthritis/rheumatism, IADL limitation, ADL limitation, and falls widened with age, reaching differences of 14.1, 13.1, 9.8, and 8.2 percentage points, respectively, among adults aged 75 years or older (Figures 6A–6D).

Some CHARLS indicators differed in direction from the corresponding GBD burden patterns. Self-reported heart disease was more common in women than men in CHARLS (23.1% vs. 16.3%; adjusted odds ratio for men vs. women 0.57, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.62), whereas GBD estimates showed higher male burden for ischemic heart disease. Falls were also more frequently reported by women in CHARLS, whereas GBD estimates showed higher overall fall-related DALYs in males (Figures 6A–6D). These differences indicate that CHARLS should be interpreted as external triangulation of selected behavioral, morbidity, and functional patterns rather than as a direct validation of GBD-standard DALY estimates.

Discussion

This study combined GBD 2023 estimates, global comparison, risk-factor attribution, short-term projections, and CHARLS 2018 triangulation to characterize sex-specific disease burden in China. Three findings are most important. First, male excess burden was concentrated in mortality-driven cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and injuries. Second, female disadvantage was concentrated in morbidity-dominant, functional, musculoskeletal, and aging-related conditions. Third, the China-specific pattern was broadly consistent with global sex differences but showed distinctive cause rankings and risk-factor profiles relevant to Chinese health planning.

The male excess in stroke, ischemic heart disease, lung cancer, and injuries is consistent with the broader global pattern in which males carry a larger burden of mortality-driven conditions.4 In China, this pattern is especially important because stroke and ischemic heart disease ranked as the two leading causes by ASDR. The YLL-dominant composition of these causes indicates that the sex gap is driven largely by premature mortality rather than by non-fatal morbidity alone. This finding supports prioritizing earlier cardiovascular risk detection, tobacco cessation, occupational risk reduction, and injury prevention in men, particularly from young adulthood through middle and older age. The male excess in road injuries and selected injuries should also be interpreted as a gendered, modifiable exposure pattern rather than an immutable biological difference. Educational interventions at school and family levels, risk-behavior prevention among young men, alcohol-impaired driving control, and occupational safety measures may help reduce the early-life and young-adult male injury burden.14

The risk-factor findings indicate that sex differences in disease burden arise from multiple distinct pathways rather than from a single behavioral explanation. Smoking showed the most prominent male excess, especially for lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, ischemic heart disease, and stroke. This pattern is consistent with historically higher smoking prevalence among Chinese men and with gendered social norms that have shaped tobacco uptake and cessation.15,16 Alcohol use also contributed more to male-attributable burden, particularly for cirrhosis and injuries, but should be interpreted separately from smoking because alcohol-related harm reflects both social drinking patterns and biological differences in metabolism and organ susceptibility.17 Occupational risks represent a more structural form of gendered exposure. The higher male population-attributable fractions for road injuries and falls likely reflect men’s greater participation in transportation, construction, manufacturing, and other high-risk work environments. However, occupational exposure should not be treated as a fixed biological characteristic; it may change over time as labor-force participation, workplace safety regulation, and gender roles evolve. Delayed health-care seeking among men is also a gendered and potentially modifiable characteristic, suggesting that educational campaigns, workplace health promotion, and psychological support may help reduce preventable fatal burden.18

Female disadvantage was concentrated in conditions dominated by disability and functional impairment. Other musculoskeletal disorders, falls in later life, depressive symptoms, functional limitations, and dementia-related morbidity all point to a pattern in which women live longer but experience a greater non-fatal burden, consistent with the male-female health-survival paradox.19 Importantly, some GBD female-leaning estimates, particularly Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, had uncertainty intervals crossing zero and should not be presented as statistically clear sex differences. However, CHARLS provided external individual-level evidence that women had higher prevalence of arthritis/rheumatism, falls, ADL and IADL limitations, depressive symptoms, and slightly poorer executive or global cognitive performance. These findings support the broader interpretation that female disadvantage is concentrated in disability-dominant aging-related outcomes, even when individual GBD estimates are imprecise. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence on sex and gender differences in Alzheimer’s disease and with global estimates showing a high and growing burden of musculoskeletal disorders.20,21,22

The interpretation of female-leaning dementia and cognitive burden also requires biological and clinical nuance. Longer female life expectancy increases the number of women surviving to ages at which dementia is common, but survival alone is unlikely to fully explain sex-specific vulnerability. Emerging evidence suggests that cardiometabolic and liver-related pathways may contribute to cognitive decline and dementia risk. Liver fibrosis is increasingly recognized as a systemic condition linked not only to liver-related outcomes but also to cardiovascular and brain health. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that liver fibrosis was associated with increased risk of incident dementia, with risk increasing at more advanced fibrosis stages.23 In addition, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease may confer sex-specific cardiovascular risk, reinforcing the need to consider metabolic and liver-related pathways when interpreting sex differences in cardiovascular and cognitive outcomes.24 Therefore, female dementia-related burden may reflect the combined effects of longevity, vascular risk, metabolic dysfunction, liver fibrosis, and social determinants of diagnosis and care.

Female-attributable risks showed a different profile from male-attributable risks. Household air pollution from solid fuels contributed relatively more to female burden for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, and lower respiratory infections, consistent with greater exposure to cooking- and heating-related indoor pollution in many settings.25 Although China has made progress in clean-energy transition, residual exposure may remain concentrated in rural, older, or socioeconomically disadvantaged households, where women and children may be disproportionately exposed. Therefore, household air pollution should be framed not only as an environmental issue but also as a sex-responsive prevention priority for respiratory and vascular disease.26 Low bone mineral density contributed more to fall-related burden in females, highlighting the importance of osteoporosis prevention, fall-risk assessment, and rehabilitation in older women. Metabolic risks also require nuanced interpretation. They are not limited to obesity but include high body-mass index, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and liver-related metabolic dysfunction. These risks may interact with sex-specific biology, menopause-related metabolic changes, health-care use, and social determinants of diet and physical activity. Therefore, sex-responsive prevention should distinguish behavioral, occupational, environmental, skeletal, and metabolic pathways rather than grouping all modifiable risks into a single category.

The CHARLS triangulation strengthens the interpretation of the GBD findings by providing individual-level evidence for selected behavioral, morbidity, functional, and cognitive indicators.13 The most striking sex/gender contrasts in CHARLS were behavioral: men had 24-fold higher adjusted odds of current smoking and 7.7-fold higher adjusted odds of current drinking than women. Together with established comparative risk assessment evidence linking tobacco and alcohol exposure to cardiopulmonary disease, cancer, and injury burden, these findings support the interpretation that male-skewed behavioral risk exposure is an upstream contributor to the male excess in cardiopulmonary disease, cancer, and injury burden.27 The higher prevalence of chronic lung disease and stroke in men in CHARLS was also directionally consistent with the male-predominant respiratory and cerebrovascular DALY burden observed in GBD. Conversely, CHARLS showed a consistent female excess in non-fatal morbidity and disability-related indicators, including arthritis/rheumatism, falls, ADL and IADL limitations, depressive symptoms, and slightly poorer executive or global cognitive performance. These results support the interpretation that the female-leaning component of disease burden is concentrated in disability-dominant outcomes rather than premature mortality. CHARLS also helps explain two apparent discrepancies. The higher prevalence of self-reported heart disease among women in CHARLS may partly reflect differences in disease definition, survival, health-care contact, and symptom reporting, whereas male ischemic heart disease burden in GBD is largely YLL-driven. Similarly, higher fall occurrence in women in CHARLS and higher fall-related DALYs in men in GBD may reflect the distinction between event frequency and severity or fatality of fall-related injuries.

The BAPC projections should be interpreted conservatively. The 2030 horizon is policy-relevant because it aligns with Healthy China 2030, but the projection window is short and the models mainly assess whether recent patterns would persist under trend continuation. The deterministic sensitivity analyses showed that the direction of sex gaps was stable across alternative scenarios, but these scenarios were not intervention models. Therefore, the projection results should be viewed as evidence of short-term pattern stability rather than as forecasts of policy impact or long-term disease trajectories. Recent GBD-based projection studies of burn injury by total body surface area involvement and vertebral fractures illustrate how projection analyses can be used to identify future research needs and health-system planning priorities, especially for injury-related and musculoskeletal conditions in aging populations.28,29 In the present study, however, the projections were deliberately restricted to short-term trend-based extrapolation and were not designed to estimate the effect of specific interventions.

These findings suggest several priorities for sex-responsive health planning. For males, the combination of high fatal cardiovascular, cancer, and injury burden with high smoking, alcohol, and occupational risk attribution supports continued emphasis on tobacco control, cardiovascular risk detection, alcohol-related harm reduction, and occupational and road-safety interventions. For females, the concentration of non-fatal burden in musculoskeletal morbidity, falls, functional limitation, depressive symptoms, and aging-related conditions supports greater attention to rehabilitation, pain management, osteoporosis prevention, fall prevention, mental-health care, and long-term care. More broadly, because many observed differences reflect modifiable gendered exposures rather than fixed biological differences, monitoring systems and interventions should distinguish biological susceptibility from social, behavioral, occupational, and environmental pathways. Together, these findings support sex-responsive prevention, rehabilitation, and long-term care planning across the life course in China.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, GBD estimates are modeled from multiple data sources and may be affected by measurement error, data sparsity, cause misclassification, and uncertainty in non-fatal health loss estimation. Second, GBD 2023 reports estimates by female and male sex but does not directly measure gender identity, gender norms, or gender relations; therefore, social and behavioral explanations should be interpreted as plausible mechanisms rather than directly observed gender effects. Third, CHARLS provided individual-level external triangulation but was not designed to validate DALYs, YLLs, YLDs, or GBD-standard cause-specific burden. Fourth, the CHARLS analysis was restricted to adults aged 45 years or older, relied partly on self-reported conditions, and used unweighted estimates because the harmonized analytic dataset did not contain a general survey weight variable. Fifth, the 2030 projections were trend-based extrapolations rather than forecasts of policy effects and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study was a secondary analysis of publicly available population health estimates from GBD 2023 and individual-level survey data from CHARLS 2018. No animal experiments, cell lines, microscopy experiments, western blotting, protein structural analyses, or clinical trials were conducted. GBD 2023 provides estimates stratified by location, year, age group, and female or male sex. CHARLS 2018 included community-dwelling Chinese adults aged 45 years or older; the analytic sample used in this study included 19,542 participants, comprising 10,240 women and 9,302 men. Participant ethnicity was not analyzed because ethnicity was not available in the harmonized analytic variables used for this study. CHARLS data collection was approved by the Peking University Biomedical Ethics Review Committee for the main household survey (IRB00001052-11015), and all original CHARLS participants provided informed consent. Because this study used de-identified public or registered-access secondary data, no additional institutional review board approval was required for the present analysis.

Method details

Overview and data source

The primary analysis focused on China, and an additional global comparison was conducted to contextualize China-specific sex differences within broader international patterns. GBD 2023 provides internally comparable estimates of fatal and non-fatal health loss and risk-attributable burden across countries, territories, years, ages, and sexes.27 Because GBD classifies estimates as female and male and does not directly measure gender identity, gender norms, or gender relations, the quantitative analyses in this study concern sex-specific estimates. The term gender is used only when discussing potential social, behavioral, occupational, and structural mechanisms. GBD data sources and modeling procedures follow the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting framework.30

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) per 100,000 population. Secondary outcomes included age-standardized YLL and YLD rates, the proportional contribution of YLLs and YLDs to total DALYs, age-specific DALY rates, and risk-factor-attributable DALY rates and population-attributable fractions (PAFs). DALYs were defined as the sum of YLLs due to premature mortality and YLDs due to non-fatal health loss.31 YLL-YLD composition was used to distinguish mortality-dominant burden from morbidity-dominant burden.

Selection of causes

For the China-specific analysis, all level 3 causes in the GBD cause hierarchy were ranked according to their ASDRs in the combined population of China in 2023, and the 20 leading causes were selected for detailed analysis. This threshold was chosen to capture the major contributors to population health loss while maintaining interpretability across sex, age group, YLL-YLD composition, temporal change, risk attribution, projection, and global comparison analyses. Because the ranking was based on both-sex rates rather than sex-specific rankings, the analysis should be interpreted as an assessment of sex differences within the leading causes of overall disease burden rather than as a comprehensive inventory of all conditions with large sex disparities. For the global comparison, the descriptive analysis was repeated using global GBD 2023 estimates and the same level-3 cause hierarchy, ASDR metric, and male-minus-female difference definition.

Age groups

To assess variation across the life course, DALY rates were examined across six broad age groups: 0–19 years, 20–44 years, 45–59 years, 60–74 years, 75–89 years, and 90 years or older.

Risk factor analysis

Risk-factor attribution was assessed using the GBD comparative risk assessment framework.27 For each selected cause, sex-specific attributable DALY rates and PAFs were extracted for risk factors with established causal relationships and available GBD 2023 estimates. Behavioral, metabolic, environmental, and occupational risks were examined. PAF differences were calculated as male PAF minus female PAF; positive values indicate higher attributable fractions in males and negative values indicate higher attributable fractions in females. Because PAFs represent population-level counterfactual attribution, they were interpreted as population-attributable contributions rather than individual-level causal effects.

BAPC projection and sensitivity analysis

Exploratory Bayesian age-period-cohort projections to 2030 were conducted using integrated nested Laplace approximation.32 Separate models were fitted by cause and sex using annual age-specific DALY counts and corresponding population denominators. To examine the robustness of projected sex differences to alternative trajectory assumptions, deterministic sensitivity analyses applied four annual perturbations to the baseline BAPC projections: −2%, −5%, +2%, and +5% per year after 2023. These perturbations were not intended to represent empirically estimated intervention effects; they were used to assess whether the direction and relative ranking of projected sex gaps were sensitive to plausible improvements or deteriorations in future rates. Model fit was compared using the deviance information criterion, with lower values indicating a better balance between goodness of fit and model complexity.33 Because estimation was implemented using integrated nested Laplace approximation rather than Markov chain Monte Carlo simulation, posterior summaries were obtained from deterministic approximations and conventional MCMC trace-plot diagnostics were not required.

External triangulation using CHARLS

CHARLS 2018 was used as an external consistency check rather than a direct validation dataset, because CHARLS does not estimate DALYs, YLLs, YLDs, or GBD-standard cause-specific burden.13 Selected sex-specific patterns observed in the GBD analysis were compared with individual-level behavioral, morbidity, functional, and cognitive indicators in CHARLS. Indicators included smoking and drinking behaviors, chronic lung disease, stroke, self-reported heart disease, arthritis or rheumatism, falls, activities of daily living and instrumental activities of daily living limitations, depressive symptoms, and cognitive scores. Logistic regression models estimated adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for male versus female participants, adjusting for age, rurality, and education. Because the harmonized CHARLS dataset used in this analysis did not contain a general survey weight variable, estimates were unweighted.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For each cause, absolute sex differences were calculated as male rate minus female rate. Positive values indicate higher burden in males, whereas negative values indicate higher burden in females. Relative differences were calculated as the absolute difference divided by the female rate and expressed as a percentage. The same convention was applied to ASDRs, age-specific rates, YLLs, YLDs, risk-factor-attributable DALY rates, and PAFs. Uncertainty was summarized using the posterior draw distribution provided by GBD, and 95% uncertainty intervals were defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.27 A sex difference was described as clear only when the 95% uncertainty interval for the absolute difference excluded zero; otherwise, the finding was described as a point-estimate difference or an imprecisely estimated pattern. For CHARLS analyses, adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using logistic regression. No asterisk-based statistical significance notation is used in the figures. Where applicable, figure legends identify the statistical model, uncertainty or confidence interval, and number of analytic units. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2.
