Methadone Conversion Using a 3-Day Switch Strategy in Patients with Cancer on High-Dose Opioids: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Lei Lei, Qinfei Zhou, Xi Liu, Guanai Bao, Haiying Ding, Qunfang Ding, Liyan Gong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMethadone has shown effectiveness in pain control in patients with cancer who are intolerant to other opioids in China. However, the optimal strategy for methadone conversion from previous high doses of opioids in refractory cancer pain remains debatable. This study aimed to describe the efficacy and safety of a 3-day switch (3DS) strategy for methadone conversion in patients with refractory cancer pain on high doses of opioids.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 30-day medical records of 70 patients with refractory cancer pain who used a 3DS strategy for methadone conversion from previous high doses of opioids from July 2018 to December 2022. The 3DS strategy indicated that the methadone dose was increased by one third every day for 3 days. Data on the rate of successful conversion, the time to stable analgesia after conversion, the conversion efficiency, the corrected QT (QTc) interval, the actual conversion ratios, adverse events (AEs), and quality of life were analyzed.ResultsSeventy patients received 3DS methadone conversion and 64 patients were eligible for analysis. Fifty patients (78%) achieved stable analgesia, and the median time to stable analgesia was 8.14 ± 2.70 (range 6–14) days. The average dose of methadone was 77.94 ± 42.74 mg. The most common AEs (≥ 10%) included constipation, dry mouth, nausea, and cold sweats. The incidence of constipation was reduced post-methadone conversion, and a statistically significant but asymptomatic prolongation of the QTc interval was observed. Additionally, the actual conversion ratios were lower than Ayonrinde's recommended ratios.ConclusionsThe 3DS strategy for methadone conversion is applicable in Chinese patients with refractory cancer pain on high doses of opioids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40122-024-00651-0.

Introduction

Methadone has demonstrated effectiveness in treating refractory cancer pain [1, 2]. Methadone and morphine are both potent μ opioid receptor agonists. Unlike other strong opioids (morphine, fentanyl, hydromorphone), methadone has a noncompetitive antagonistic effect on N-methyl-d-aspartate receptors and may have incomplete cross-resistance with morphine. Therefore, methadone might be more effective for patients whose pain is poorly controlled by conventional opioids and is suitable for patients with refractory cancer pain. However, the proven therapeutic efficacy of methadone in cancer pain is hindered by a challenging pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic profile, considerable inter-patient variability, and increasing concern about the complexities of dosing. Moreover, there is no gold standard strategy for methadone conversion in patients with refractory cancer pain on high doses of opioids. An optimal dosing strategy for methadone conversion is urgently needed [3].

China is ranked as having the highest incidence of new cancer cases and deaths worldwide according to data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) released in 2020 [4]. Approximately 60–85% of Chinese individuals diagnosed with advanced malignant tumors experience moderate to severe pain [5, 6]. Patients with moderate to severe cancer pain need to receive opioid treatment, but only 16% of patients with cancer pain who require opioids for analgesia have been prescribed morphine, and morphine consumption accounts for only 2% of global opioid prescriptions [7].

Few studies have been reported on the application of methadone for cancer pain in China. In a previous retrospective study, we reported the potential advantages of the 3-day switch (3DS) strategy over the stop-and-go (SAG) approach in achieving successful conversion for Chinese patients with refractory cancer pain [8]. Here, we hypothesized that the 3-day switch strategy would be suitable for Chinese patients with cancer pain who need higher opioid doses and can safely and effectively switch from other opioids to methadone. We specifically reviewed the effectiveness and safety of the 3DS strategy focusing on patients with previous high doses of opioids. These preliminary clinical observations may pave the way for launching prospective and randomized trials to determine whether the 3DS strategy is superior to other methods in the context of Chinese patient care.

Methods

Study Design

We reviewed the 30-day medical records before and after the 3DS strategy for methadone conversion in 70 patients with refractory cancer pain who needed ≥ 300 mg oral morphine equivalent daily dose (OMEDD) [9], with refractory cancer pain defined as uncontrolled pain on any opioids rating an average score of > 4/11 on a numeric rating scale (NRS) [10] or with breakthrough pain (BTP) occurring > 3 times per day and/or intolerable opioid-related toxicity in patients [11].

Approval for this retrospective study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (No. IRB-2020–4). All procedures complied with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Informed consent was waived by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital due to the retrospective nature of this study.

Patients and Observational Indicators

The following data were collected from medical records: age, sex, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), primary tumor, pain mechanism, pretreatment opioids and their OMEDD, methadone dose, concomitant medications at the start of methadone administration, reason for methadone conversion, and hospitalization duration. Pain intensity was measured using the patient’s self-report on a numerical rating scale (NRS, 0–10). The rate of pain relief achieved, daily occurrences of BTP, and the use of rescue medications were collected. The time to stable analgesia was determined as the number of days from the day that methadone was started until the first day that patients reached stable analgesia. Stable analgesia was defined as a status in which patients had an average NRS score < 4 persisting for a minimum of 48 h, with rescue interventions required less than three times every 24 h and absence of dose-limiting adverse events (AEs) simultaneously. Methadone conversion efficiency referred to successful conversion, defined as patients who achieved stable analgesia. We calculated the percentage of cases with stable analgesia out of the total enrolled cases assessed. Conversion ratios indicated the actual ratio calculated between previous opioid dose and methadone dose at the time of stabilization. Symptoms associated with advanced cancer or opioid therapy including pain, tiredness, depression, and anxiety were assessed using the simplified Chinese version of the Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (C-ESAS) before conversion and on the day of discharge after conversion. The corrected QT (QTc) interval data collected via electrocardiogram on the day before conversion and on day 28 ± 3 days were also documented. All the AEs were collected and graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) 5.0. All the above data were retrieved from the inpatient or outpatient medical record system, including specific charts to assess pain and symptoms, and clinical notes from the patients’ diaries.

The study design was retrospective, and the key inclusion criteria were as follows: age 18–75 years; 30-day medical records before and after 3DS strategy for methadone conversion; and refractory cancer pain requiring ≥ 300 mg OMEDD, with refractory cancer pain defined as uncontrolled pain on any opioids rating an average NRS score of > 4/11 or with BTP occurring > 3 times per day and/or intolerable opioid-related toxicity in patients. Cases not meeting these criteria were excluded.

The primary outcomes included the efficiency of methadone conversion by the 3DS strategy and the time to stable analgesia. Methadone conversion efficiency was calculated as the percentage of cases with stable analgesia out of the total enrolled cases. Stable analgesia was defined as a status in which patients had an average NRS score < 4 persisting for a minimum of 48 h, with rescue interventions required less than three times every 24 h and the absence of dose-limiting AEs simultaneously. The time to stable analgesia was determined as the starting day of methadone conversion until the first day patients reached stable analgesia. Pain intensity was evaluated using the NRS, as rated by patients, and recorded as both the average and worst NRS scores within 24 h. In addition to pain intensity, the study collected data on daily occurrences of BTP and the use of rescue medications.

The secondary outcomes included the following: (1) assessment of pain relief, evaluated by NRS scores, the frequency of BTP episodes, and the number of rescue interventions required; (2) evaluation of methadone daily dose and the actual conversion ratio, where the methadone daily dose was defined as the dosage at which patients could maintain stability without necessitating adjustments or requiring a dose variance exceeding 20% without encountering dose-limiting AEs; (3) safety indicators, including (a) measurement of the QTc interval via electrocardiogram (ECG) before conversion (day −2 to day 0, i.e., baseline) and on day 28 ± 3 days, and (b) assessment of symptoms and AEs, where symptom intensity was assessed using the simplified Chinese version of the Edmonton Symptom Assessment System. Other AEs during conversion included respiratory depression, sedation, dry mouth, delirium, myoclonus, headache, constipation, and urinary retention. The presence of delirium, quantified as ≥ 7 on the Memorial Delirium Assessment Scale, served as an indicator of the intensity of delirium for assessing patients' cognitive status. Sedation was scored using the Chinese-modified Level of Sedation. The grade of AEs was determined according to the NCI-CTCAE 5.0.

3-Day Switch Strategy for Methadone Conversion

The implementation of the 3DS strategy for methadone conversion adopted in this study was as previously reported [12]. The prior opioid dosage was converted into methadone hydrochloride tablets (typically not exceeding 45 mg/day), guided by the conversion ratio recommended by Ayonrinde [13].

Methadone was continued orally, and dosage adjustments were made based on the patient's pain status. In cases where BTP occurred three or more times per day within 24 h, methadone dosage was incrementally increased by 30% every 3–5 days until achieving satisfactory pain relief. Management of BTP during and after the methadone conversion phase involved the use of morphine injections or tablets, with the dose being equivalent to 1/10 to 1/5 of the total oral opioids consumed in the preceding 24 h. For the management of BTP during the stabilization phase, we used rescue drugs including immediate-release morphine or hydromorphone, with the dose calculated as 5–20% of OMEDD.

Statistical Analysis

According to literature data and pre-experimental results [8, 14–16], the conversion efficiency of 3DS is about 80%. For the difference test, the single-sample proportion test was adopted, where a two-sided test was required with a significance level of α = 5% and power of 85% (β = 0.15). The estimated sample size was 48 cases by considering the natural survival rate in patients with advanced cancer and dropout rate of 20%; the final enrollment included 58 cases.

For continuous variables, a paired-sample t-test or repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to evaluate the distribution. Categorical variables were analyzed by Fisher's exact test and chi-squared test or multiple comparisons and are presented as numbers (percentages). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corporation), and OriginPro Software (version 8.5.1, OriginLab Corporation). All of the statistical tests were two-sided, with an α-significance level of 0.05.

Efficacy

Fifty out of 64 patients achieved stable analgesia, resulting in conversion efficiency of 78%. The mean time to stable analgesia for those patients was 8.14 ± 2.70 days, and 60% (30/50) of them achieved stable analgesia within the first 7 days. The average dose of methadone for those patients was 77.94 ± 42.74 mg (30–240 mg), and their mean hospitalized duration was 10.92 ± 5.77 days (7–26 days). Of note, the conversion efficiency in patients with OMEDD > 1000 mg was 78.57% (11/14), and their mean time to stable analgesia was 9.09 ± 3.14 days.

Both the average and worst pain scores decreased significantly after conversion, to 2.70 ± 1.11 points (p = 0.000) and 4.00 ± 1.02 points (p = 0.000), respectively, along with a reduced frequency of BTP at 2.47 ± 3.85 times (p = 0.000). A significant difference in these three indicators was observed (Table 2). Safety

The most common opioid-related adverse effects before conversion were constipation (100%), dry mouth (59.4%), dizziness (26.6%), and sweating (26.6%). Other AEs included dysuria (6.25%), somnolence (1.57%), and myoclonus (3.13%). No cases of headache, delirium, or blurred vision were observed. After conversion, the incidence of constipation gradually decreased, and it was significantly lower than in the pre-conversion period (p < 0.017). Additionally, the incidence of dizziness, drowsiness, sweating, and delirium appeared lower than before the conversion, but these differences were statistically nonsignificant (p > 0.017). Discussion

Methadone is recognized as an optimal choice for patients who are intolerant and/or have not responded to previous opioid treatments. Although both the SAG and 3DS strategies are recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network Guidelines for Adult Cancer Pain [17], the lack of evidence to support the superiority of one conversion method over another inspired us to conduct this trial. The 3DS strategy, involving a gradual reduction of opioids and an increase in methadone, offers an effective means of mitigating the rapid accumulation of methadone and reducing the incidence of AEs. A systemic review of 25 studies showed that 3DS had success rates of 68–93%, fewer dropouts because of AEs, and a shorter time to stable analgesia than other conversion strategies [18]. In our study, we mainly focus on whether 3DS can successfully convert methadone and the time to achieve stable analgesia in patients on high doses of opioids. The conversion efficiency reached 78%, the mean time to stable analgesia was 8 days, and the mean hospitalized duration was 10 days. Additionally, 60% of patients attained stable pain levels within the initial 7 days, highlighting the feasibility of implementing the 3DS strategy in Chinese patients with cancer on high-dose opioids.

The long half-life of methadone may affect the choice of conversion strategy, but our findings suggest that the potential better tolerance to opioids in patients on high-dose opioids could be balanced by the risks of the conversion strategy itself, such as the “gradual replacement” with 3DS. Moksnes and colleagues reported that methadone conversion could improve pain control and that 3DS had a higher conversion success rate than SAG in high-dose populations (94.7% vs. 62.5% in OMEDD = 1330 mg vs. 900 mg, respectively). Another study found that more patients dropped out of the SAG group when they compared the performance of SAG and 3DS in patients with OMEDD > 300 mg [14]. However, few studies have evaluated the time to stable analgesia by 3DS in methadone conversion [18]; evaluating the conversion efficacy of 3DS by the time to stable analgesia is worthwhile, especially in patients previously treated with high-dose opioids. Because methadone has a long and unpredictable elimination half-life, intensive monitoring should be performed for at least 5 to 7 days after methadone conversion to prevent related side effects such as sedation and respiratory depression. For elderly patients or patients who take a long time to reach a stable state, it may be necessary to prolong to 10–14 days [19]. Most of the failures in our study occurred in the first 7 days of methadone conversion, which may have been due to the complexity and variability of the disease in patients with advanced cancer and not related to the side effects of methadone. Moreover, the 3DS strategy is feasible and effective for patients on high-dose opioids, as reported [20]. Eleven out of 14 patients with OMEDD > 1000 mg achieved pain control, and the conversion efficiency reached 78.57%, slightly higher than the 78% in total patients; the time to stable analgesia for those patients was also marginally longer than for the total patient group (9.09 ± 3.14 vs 8.14 ± 2.7 days).

We adopted Ayonrinde’s method for the initial conversion ratio by taking into account the need for lower relative doses of methadone with escalating morphine equivalent doses [21]. However, the conversion ratios observed in our study were lower than the recommendations. One possible explanation for this phenomenon could be the uncertainty of the actual need for methadone when patients were intolerant and/or had poor responses to other opioids at the initial stage of enrollment, which was discussed in a previous study [22]. This discrepancy has been observed in another large cohort studies [15]. There is no doubt that methadone has a fair effect on refractory and complex cancer pain with insufficient analgesic effects from other strong opioids, even high-dose opioids.

Improvement in both pain relief and adverse reactions to previous opioids after methadone conversion could significantly improve the patient's quality of life. We listed the AEs in Table 3, primarily including constipation, nausea and vomiting, sweating, and dry mouth. After methadone conversion, most of those AEs were less severe than those associated with the previously used opioids (significant differences observed in nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and drowsiness), which is consistent with the findings of Cubero et al. [23]. No instances of death from any cause or severe sedation were observed in our study, aligning with the results of Moksnes et al. [22]. As shown in Table S2, the eight cases of failure in the first 7 days of 3DS conversion were basically related to the adverse reactions to methadone, including sedation, delirium, nausea, chest tightness, dizziness, and headache. The symptoms of those patients were relieved after the conversion was terminated. Substitution of morphine for methadone resulted in a significant reduction in constipation (p < 0.001) and dry mouth (p = 0.03) [23]. In addition to the sufficient analgesic effectiveness, the 3DS can also lower the risk of failure caused by adverse reactions during the prolonged conversion compared with SAG and other conversion strategies [18].

In this study, patients who switched to methadone had a statistically significant but asymptomatic prolongation of the QTc interval, and the maximum dosage of methadone in our study was 270 mg/day, but no severe arrhythmias were recorded. Those findings indicated that the 3DS strategy itself, rather than methadone alone, may not increase the risk of arrhythmias due to the purpose of this study. According to the US Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), 3.4% of methadone-induced adverse reactions were due to QTc prolongation [19]. There have been reports of sudden death with the use of methadone due to its QTc prolongation and TdP properties, particularly in higher dosages [12]. We hypothesize that methadone-induced arrhythmias may be more likely to occur in patients with deteriorating overall health. Asymptomatic QTc prolongation may occur in 28% of patients, which does not constitute a reason for discontinuation of treatment [24]. For patients with advanced malignant tumors, the worsening of their general health due to disease progression was likely the primary cause of atrial fibrillation rather than methadone overdose. Therefore, active ECG monitoring is essential for patients with advanced cancer who require long-term methadone treatment, particularly those experiencing a decline in their general health status [25].

Limitations

This was not a prospective and randomized trial design, and the conclusions could not change the clinical practice at the time due to the possibility of selection bias and confounding. However, pending the evidence for an optimal methadone conversion strategy, these preliminary clinical observations may pave the way for launching randomized trials to determine whether the 3DS strategy is superior to other methods in the context of Chinese patient care, especially in patients with refractory cancer pain previously on high doses of opioids. Additionally, enrollment of a sufficient number of eligible patients is very challenging, since opioids are generally underused in our country.

Conclusions

In summary, methadone conversion using the 3DS strategy is highly efficient, effective, and safe in Chinese patients with refractory cancer pain and receiving high opioid doses. Future prospective and randomized trials for the 3DS strategy compared with other conversion strategies are warranted.
