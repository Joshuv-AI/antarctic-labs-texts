Dental management of a 3-year-old child with congenital hyperinsulinism: A case report
Authors: Mansi Gandhy Baviskar, Shilpa Shetty Naik, Dhanashree A. Pawar, Aayushi J. Mehta, Rutuja Lakhotiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCongenital hyperinsulinism (CHI) is a rare genetic disorder causing hypoglycemia due to excessive insulin secretion. Managing dental care in children with CHI is challenging due to the risk of hypoglycemic episodes and the need for meticulous perioperative planning. This report discusses the dental management of a 3-year-old with CHI requiring treatment for multiple carious lesions, emphasizing the importance of multidisciplinary coordination and vigilant glucose monitoring for safe and effective care.

Introduction

Congenital Hyperinsulinism [CHI] is a pancreatic β-cell disorder causing severe hypoglycemia in neonates and children.[1] It presents with plasma glucose thresholds raised to 3.0 mmol/L in infants with suspected hyperinsulinism in the first 48 hours and under 45 mg/dL [2.5 mmol/L] thereafter.

CHI causes hypoglycemia, irritability, lethargy, frequent feeding, and sweating, with persistent hypoglycemia as its hallmark. The incidence ranges from 1 in 35,000–40,000 globally to 1 in 2,500 in consanguineous communities.[2] Hypoglycemia is classified as insulin- or non-insulin-mediated.[3]

Increased hunger leads to higher sucrose intake and caries risk. Pain and stress from untreated caries can trigger metabolic instability, worsening hypoglycemia.[4]

This paper discusses full-mouth rehabilitation of a 3-year-old with CHI under general anesthesia, highlighting precautions.

Case Report

A 3-year-old boy, informed consent was obtained from by his parents, visited the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry with a complaint of severe, lingering pain in the upper right back area of his jaw for 1 week and unable to eat. There was no notable medical, family, or psychosocial history. It was the child’s first dental visit, and his behavior was rated 2 on Frankl’s Behavior Rating Scale.

During anamnesis, the mother reported congenital hyperinsulinism with recurrent hypoglycemia diagnosed at birth, requiring mechanical ventilation and treatment with glucagon, octreotide, and diazoxide. The child was later transferred to Wadia Hospital, where doses were tapered. He is currently on 4.6 mg subcutaneous octreotide injections.

Intraoral examination revealed root stumps in teeth 52, 51, 61, and 62; deep occlusal caries involving the pulp in 54, 64, 74, and 84; pit and fissure caries in 55, 75, and 85; and smooth surface caries in 82, 81, 71, and 72 [Figure 1a–c].

Blood tests were normal, and an orthopantomogram was evaluated to plan treatment under general anesthesia [Figure 2].

The patient was admitted and evaluated by a pediatrician, pediatric endocrinologist, and ENT specialist. A pre-anesthetic evaluation was conducted before general anesthesia. To prevent hypoglycemia during NBM, 5% dextrose was administered intravenously, with blood glucose levels monitored hourly. As per the pediatric endocrinologist’s advice, if glucose levels dropped below 70 mg/dL, 10% dextrose was given. If a patient did not respond, 0.5 mg of intramuscular glucagon was to be administered. Parental consent was obtained before surgery. Intraoperatively, blood glucose levels were maintained between 70 and 120 mg/dL with hourly monitoring.

Treatment under general anesthesia included pulpectomy with teeth 54, 64, 74, and 84, followed by stainless-steel crown placement, and extraction of teeth 52, 51, 61, and 62 [Figure 3].

Intraoperatively, blood glucose levels were monitored every hour, ranging from 120 mg/dL to 170 mg/dL.

The child recovered uneventfully and was discharged the following day [Figure 5].

Parents received home-care instructions, including dietary counseling and brushing twice a day with fluoridated toothpaste, with focus on oral hygiene due to the inability to reduce sugar intake.

After, a 1-week initial recall, follow-ups were scheduled every 3 months.

Discussion

Congenital Hyperinsulinism is caused by mutations in multiple genes, of which mutation in the ABCC8 gene is the most common. The inheritance patterns vary and depend on the form. The “diffuse” form is more commonly autosomal recessive and less commonly autosomal dominant, while the “focal” form is more complex and often sporadic.[5] Inactivating mutations prevent the potassium channel from opening, leading to continuous insulin secretion. CHI can be transient, genetic, metabolic, or associated with other syndromes.[1] Treatment options include glucose infusion, particularly at presentation in the acute setting or during episodes of hypoglycemia that are not adequately managed with oral sugars or carbohydrates or in cases requiring hospitalization. Pharmacotherapy includes the use of diazoxide and opting for alternative therapies in diazoxide-unresponsive cases. Alternative therapies include octreotide, lanreotide, nifedipine, glucagon, and continuous subcutaneous dasiglucagon infusion, along with newer specific pharmacological therapies currently under research. Partial or subtotal pancreatectomy is advised in cases that are unresponsive to medical therapies. Avoidance of prolonged periods of fasting is an important part of management, involving frequent feeding.[1] Managing multiple dental procedures in one session minimizes stress and risk.

The parents reported giving a child a cornflour slurry with added sugar every morning, providing carbohydrates, moderate protein, and fat to reduce hypoglycemic episodes and improve fasting tolerance.[1] Frequent snacking and added sugars in slurry likely increased child’s susceptibility to dental caries.[6] At night, a child was given a glass of whole milk with added sugar. Frequent exposure to fermentable carbohydrates, like sucrose, is a major contributor to cariogenic biofilm formation and caries development.

Bedtime sugar consumption is linked to the development of caries in children. Dietary management includes frequent feeding, long-acting carbohydrates, abundant protein, fiber supplements, and fat emulsions to prevent hypoglycemia caused by rapid insulin release.[7] As sugar intake cannot be reduced, maintaining oral hygiene is crucial. Recommendations include choosing fresh fruits over dried or processed snacks, brushing twice daily for 2 minutes, and regular dental check-ups.

Conclusion

Managing dental pain and infection reduces stress-related hypoglycemia, improves the child’s quality of life, and prevents severe complications. Given the unique risks associated with CHI, careful preoperative glucose stabilization, intraoperative monitoring, and postoperative management are crucial to ensure a safe and successful outcome. Each child’s treatment plan should be personalized, considering their specific medical condition and long-term dental needs.
