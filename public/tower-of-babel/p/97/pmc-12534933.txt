Prevalence of exotoxin and type III secretion system genes in multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates from patients with burn injuries in Southern Iran
Authors: Seideh Pardis Dejman, Amir Emami, Neda Pirbonyeh, Amirreza Radfar, Fatemeh Javanmardi, Afagh Moattari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPseudomonas aeruginosa is a leading cause of burn wound infections, with pathogenicity driven by exotoxins and the Type III Secretion System (T3SS). Among its components, ExoA, ExoT, and the popB gene (T3SS translocon) are particularly associated with acute tissue damage and treatment failure.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study (March 2021–March 2022), 108 non-duplicate, drug-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates were collected from burn patients at Amir-Al-Momenin Hospital, Shiraz. PCR assays detected exoA, exoT, exoS, exoU, exoY, and T3SS (popB). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed according to CLSI 2022 guidelines, with MIC determination for colistin and imipenem. MDR was defined as resistance to ≥ 3 antibiotic classes.ResultsAmong the detected genes, exoA (81.5%) and exoT (77.8%) were the most prevalent, followed by exoY (68.5%) and T3SS (popB) (66.7%), while exoS (63.9%) and exoU (46.3%) were less frequent. Isolates carrying T3SS (popB) showed significantly higher resistance to multiple antibiotics compared to T3SS (popB) negative isolates (p < 0.05). MIC analysis demonstrated preserved susceptibility to colistin (MIC50 = 0.25 µg/mL, MIC90 = 0.5 µg/mL) but high imipenem resistance (MIC50 = 32 µg/mL, MIC90 = 64 µg/mL).ConclusionExoA and ExoT were the most prevalent exotoxin genes, while the T3SS (popB) gene, although slightly less frequent, showed a strong association with multidrug resistance. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of these virulence determinants, particularly popB, and support further investigation into T3SS-targeted therapies.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa(P. aeruginosa) is a major cause of hospital-acquired infections worldwide, particularly affecting immunocompromised patients and those in intensive care units [1, 2]. It is associated with a wide range of infections, including pneumonia, urinary tract infections, bacteremia, and peritonitis, contributing significantly to morbidity and mortality [2]. The growing global burden of P. aeruginosa infections highlights the need for a deeper understanding of its pathogenic mechanisms [3, 4].

Burn wounds create a favorable environment for P. aeruginosa colonization, due to moisture, nutrient availability, and impaired host defenses [5–7]. Infections in burn patients are a critical concern, often leading to delayed healing, systemic complications, and increased mortality. The emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) P. aeruginosa strains has further complicated management, as available therapeutic options are limited [8, 9].

The widespread use of last-resort antibiotics, such as colistin and imipenem, has become necessary in combating MDR strains; however, this approach may promote the selection of resistant bacteria, posing a challenge for infection control efforts. A comprehensive understanding of both resistance mechanisms and virulence factors is essential to inform effective treatment strategies and prevent further resistance development [10, 11].

Virulence in P. aeruginosa is mediated by diverse factors, including rhamnolipids, elastase, siderophores, and the Type III Secretion System (T3SS), which enables the direct injection of effector toxins into host cells [12–17]. The T3SS plays a critical role in immune evasion and tissue damage, delivering key exotoxins—ExoA, ExoS, ExoU, ExoT, and ExoY—that disrupt host cell signaling, inhibit protein synthesis, and impair epithelial barriers [18–21]. These virulence factors have been widely studied across various clinical settings, as they significantly contribute to the severity of P. aeruginosa infections [22–26].

Despite global studies on P. aeruginosa virulence genes, data from burn wound infections in southern Iran remain limited [27–29]. We focused on the exotoxin genes (exoA, exoS, exoU, exoT, exoY) and the T3SS structural gene popB because of their established roles in tissue damage, immune evasion, and adverse clinical outcomes, particularly in acute burn infections [30]. This study aimed to determine their prevalence in drug-resistant isolates and assess their association with antimicrobial resistance, providing insights to guide both local and global infection control strategies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted from March 2021 to March 2022 at Amir-Al-Momenin Hospital in southern Iran. The aim was to determine the prevalence of ExoA, ExoS, ExoT, ExoU, ExoY, and Type III Secretion System (T3SS) (targeted analysis of the popB gene segment gene) in drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates from patients with burn injuries. Based on estimated prevalence rates and statistical calculations, a total of 108 non-duplicate, drug-resistant isolates were included in the study.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1402.462). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians prior to sample collection. Patient anonymity and data confidentiality were maintained throughout the study.

Sample collection and bacterial identification

Clinical specimens were collected from burn patients, including sputum, blood, urine, wound exudate, tissue, and catheter tips. Samples were cultured on blood agar, MacConkey agar, and Sabouraud dextrose agar (Merck, Germany) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Colonies were assessed for morphology, pigment production, and Gram staining. Biochemical tests—including oxidase, catalase, triple sugar iron (TSI) slant, urea hydrolysis, Simmons citrate, and motility-indole-lysine (MIL)—were performed to confirm P. aeruginosa identification.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by the disk diffusion method according to CLSI 2022 guidelines for ciprofloxacin (CP, 5 µg), amikacin (AMK, 30 µg), ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg), gentamicin (GM, 30 µg), imipenem (IPM/IMP, 10 µg), meropenem (MRP, 10 µg), piperacillin–tazobactam (PIP-TAZ, 100/10 µg), trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (SXT, 1.25/23.75 µg), and nalidixic acid (AN, 30 µg). Colistin (C) susceptibility was assessed exclusively by broth microdilution, in accordance with CLSI/EUCAST recommendations, and MIC values were interpreted using the corresponding breakpoints (CLSI M100, 32nd edition; EUCAST v12.0).

Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, according to the international consensus criteria [31].

DNA extraction and molecular detection

Genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial kit (Beh Gene, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA samples were stored at −20 °C until further analysis. PCR assays were performed to detect exoS, exoU, exoT, exoY, exoA and T3SS (popB) gene using primers synthesized by Metabion (Germany). Data presentation and statistical analysis

Data were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons of resistance rates between popB-positive and popB-negative isolates were performed using the Chi-square test A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Graphical presentations were generated using Microsoft Excel 2019.

Results

Demographic characteristics and specimen distribution

A total of 108 drug-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates were obtained from burn patients at Amir-Al-Momenin Hospital. Of these, 79 (73.1%) were from male patients and 29 (26.9%) from female patients. The mean age of the patients was 37.6 ± 15.9 years (range: 2–80 years). The most common specimen type was wound exudate (33 isolates, 30.6%), followed by urine (22 isolates, 20.4%), tissue samples (22 isolates, 20.4%), Foley catheter tips (9 isolates, 8.3%), sputum (7 isolates, 6.5%), blood (6 isolates, 5.6%), throat swabs (6 isolates, 5.6%), and triple-lumen catheters (3 isolates, 2.8%) (Table 2).

Prevalence of virulence genes

PCR analysis revealed variable distribution of virulence genes among the 108 isolates. exoA was detected in 88 isolates (81.5%, 95% CI: 72.0–87.8%), exoT in 84 isolates (77.8%, 95% CI: 68.1–85.9%), exoU in 50 isolates (46.3%, 95% CI: 37.0–55.8%), exoS in 69 isolates (63.9%, 95% CI: 53.1–73.2%), exoY in 74 isolates (68.5%, 95% CI: 58.0–78.4%), and the T3SS structural gene popB in 72 isolates (66.7%, 95% CI: 57.2–75.4%). Antibiotic resistance patterns in T3SS (popB)-positive and T3SS (popB)-negative isolates

The data indicate a strong association between T3SS (popB) status and antimicrobial resistance across most tested drugs. In samples with positive T3SS (popB), resistance was markedly higher for several agents, including SXT, PIP, AN, AMK, MRP, CP, CAZ, IPM, and GM, with p-values frequently < 0.001, suggesting highly significant differences compared with T3SS (popB)-negative samples.

As shown in Fig. 1; Table 5, antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed a high prevalence of multidrug resistance among the isolates. Resistance rates were notably higher in popB-positive isolates compared to T3SS (popB)-negative isolates across multiple antibiotics, including ciprofloxacin (97.2% vs. 13.9%), amikacin (100% vs. 33.3%), ceftazidime (95.8% vs. 25.0%), gentamicin (98.6% vs. 25.0%), imipenem (98.6% vs. 30.6%), meropenem (100% vs. 33.3%), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (100% vs. 27.8%), piperacillin-tazobactam (100% vs. 38.9%) and nalidixic acid (95.8% vs. 16.7%). In contrast, Colistin showed minimal resistance in the popB -negative group (0% vs. 5.6%).

Discussion

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a globally recognized opportunistic pathogen associated with severe infections, particularly in immunocompromised patients and those with burn injuries. The ability of P. aeruginosa to persist in harsh environments, coupled with its intrinsic resistance mechanisms and diverse virulence factors, makes it a significant challenge in healthcare settings [34, 35]. Our study adds to this understanding by evaluating the prevalence of key exotoxin and Type III Secretion System T3SS (popB) in drug-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates from burn patients in southern Iran.

We found a high prevalence of the exoA (81.5%) and exoT (77.8%) genes among the isolates. This is consistent with previous studies, such as the meta-analysis by Javanmardi et al., which reported exoA and exoT prevalence rates of 78% and 83%, respectively (36). Similar findings have been reported by Nasirmoghadas et al. (100% for exoA) and Ghanbarzadeh Corehtash et al. (75% for exoA) in Iran [36, 37]. The consistent detection of exoA across diverse studies highlights its essential role in P. aeruginosa pathogenesis, given its ADP-ribosylating activity targeting elongation factor-2, which inhibits protein synthesis and promotes cell death [21].

In contrast, we observed a lower prevalence of exoS (36.1%) and exoU (46.3%) genes. These findings align with previous studies reporting variability in the distribution of these genes, such as Aghaei et al. (35.7%), Khoramrooz et al. (39.1%), and Finlayson and Brown (51%) [38–40]. Regional differences in patient populations, hospital settings, and antibiotic usage patterns likely contribute to this variability. Notably, studies have shown that exoU is more commonly associated with severe infections and ICU settings, as seen in reports by Zarei et al. (63.8%) and Winstanley et al. (59%) [41, 42]. Our relatively lower exoU prevalence may reflect differences in patient demographics and clinical practices in our burn unit.

The high prevalence of exoA, exoT, and T3SS (popB) in isolates from burn patients has important clinical implications. ExoA, a potent ADP-ribosylating toxin, disrupts protein synthesis and is associated with severe tissue necrosis and poor healing outcomes in acute infections [43]. ExoT, with dual ADP-ribosyltransferase and GTPase-activating protein activities, impairs epithelial repair and phagocytic clearance, thereby worsening infection persistence and patient prognosis [44]. Similarly, the presence of T3SS structural genes such as popB reflects a functional secretion system, enabling direct toxin injection into host cells and contributing to systemic complications [30]. Collectively, the high prevalence of these factors in burn patients highlights their role in driving unfavorable clinical outcomes, including delayed wound closure, invasive disease, and increased mortality risk, consistent with previous reports linking T3SS activity to severe P. aeruginosa infections [45].

Importantly, our study demonstrated that Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates carrying the T3SS (popB) gene exhibited markedly higher resistance rates across multiple antibiotics, including ciprofloxacin, amikacin, ceftazidime, gentamicin, imipenem, meropenem, piperacillin and tazobactam and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, compared to T3SS (popB) -negative isolates. These results are consistent with prior research suggesting that T3SS (popB) -positive strains may have a selective advantage under antibiotic pressure. Targeting T3SS has been proposed as a promising strategy to mitigate virulence without promoting resistance, highlighting the therapeutic potential of T3SS inhibitors [46].

The observed association between T3SS (popB) positivity and elevated antimicrobial resistance warrants closer consideration of potential underlying mechanisms. One explanation is that T3SS-positive strains are inherently more virulent, enabling them to persist under strong antibiotic pressure and survive in hostile host environments. In addition, genetic co-localization of T3SS determinants with resistance genes on mobile genetic elements, such as pathogenicity islands or plasmids, may facilitate their simultaneous acquisition and propagation. Another plausible mechanism is the contribution of T3SS activity to enhanced biofilm formation, which is known to markedly increase tolerance to antimicrobials [30, 47, 48]. These possibilities suggest that the interplay between virulence and resistance in P. aeruginosa is multifactorial and biologically coordinated, underscoring the importance of integrating both aspects into therapeutic and infection control strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal or temporal relationships between virulence gene carriage and clinical outcomes. Second, as a single-center study, the results may not be generalizable to other regions or healthcare settings. Third, clinical outcome data such as patient mortality, length of hospitalization, and treatment success were not available, limiting the ability to directly link virulence profiles with patient prognosis. Finally, PCR detection identifies the presence of genes but does not confirm their expression or functional activity. Future studies incorporating functional assays, multicenter designs, and clinical outcome analysis will be necessary to strengthen these findings and clarify the role of virulence determinants in multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa infections in burn patients.

Conclusion

This study showed that among burn wound isolates of P. aeruginosa, exoA and exoT were the most prevalent exotoxin genes, while the T3SS (popB) gene was detected in two-thirds of isolates. PopB-positive strains exhibited significantly higher resistance rates across multiple antibiotics, underscoring a potential link between T3SS-associated virulence and multidrug resistance. Although colistin retained activity, imipenem resistance was widespread. These findings provide region-specific insights into the molecular epidemiology of drug-resistant P. aeruginosa in burn patients and highlight the importance of infection control and stewardship measures, alongside further research on the interaction between virulence and resistance.
