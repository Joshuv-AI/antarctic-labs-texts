Predicting infected pancreatic necrosis in acute pancreatitis using machine learning models and feature selection
Authors: Li Xin, Ding Yixuan, Huang Bohan, Shen Yunheng, Lv Hairong, Cao Feng, Yu Tong, Li Fei, Fei Xiaolu, Li Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfected pancreatic necrosis (IPN) is a life-threatening complication of acute pancreatitis (AP), and its early prediction remains challenging. This study aimed to develop and externally validate interpretable machine learning models for individualized IPN risk prediction. A total of 728 patients with AP admitted to Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University, between 2017 and 2023 were retrospectively analyzed. Embedded feature selection was incorporated within model training using regularized linear and tree-based algorithms to enhance interpretability and prevent overfitting. Five machine learning algorithms and one neural network model were evaluated through nested cross-validation and an independent temporal external cohort consisting of 166 AP patients admitted to Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University, between 2022 and 2023. Model discrimination, precision–recall, and probability calibration were assessed, and model explainability was analyzed using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP). The Random Forest model achieved the best overall performance, achieving an external AUC of 0.764 (95% CI 0.696–0.830, ), precision of 0.893, recall of 0.604, and the lowest Brier score, indicating reliable probability calibration. SHAP analysis identified Fibrinogen, APACHE II score, D-dimer, IL-6, and C-reactive protein as key predictors associated with increased IPN risk, while higher Lymphocyte count, and Hematocrit were protective. These findings are consistent clinical pathophysiology. The interpretable Random Forest model demonstrated robust discrimination and calibration for IPN prediction, providing a transparent and data-driven framework for early risk stratification in acute pancreatitis. Prospective multicenter validation is warranted before clinical implementation.Keywords: Acute pancreatitis, Deep learning, Infected pancreatic necrosis, Machine learning, Prognosis predictionSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Gastroenterology, Medical research

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a prevalent acute digestive system disorder characterized by its high incidence and potential for severe outcomes. Globally, the incidence of AP ranges from 13 to 45 cases per 100,000 individuals annually, with a mortality rate of approximately 1–3% in mild cases and up to 20–30% in severe cases1,2.According to the modified Atlanta classification, AP is categorized into mild, moderate, and severe based on the presence of organ dysfunction and local complications, such as acute pancreatic pseudocysts, fluid collections, and infected pancreatic necrosis (IPN)3. Among these complications, IPN is particularly critical, occurring in 30–40% of patients with severe AP and associated with a mortality rate of 10–20%4,5. Therefore, early identification of high-risk IPN patients is crucial for timely intervention and improved prognosis.

In the clinical management of AP, various scoring systems and biomarkers have been developed to predict disease severity. Traditional scoring systems, such as the Ranson score6, Bedside Index for Severity in Acute Pancreatitis (BISAP)7, Modified CT Severity Index (MCTSI)8, and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II)9, integrate multiple clinical indicators to provide valuable assessment tools for clinicians. Additionally, simplified scoring systems like the Pancreatitis Activity Scoring System (PASS)10 and the Chinese Severe Acute Pancreatitis Scoring System (CSSS)11 have been introduced to enhance the convenience and timeliness of early prediction. However, these systems exhibit significant limitations in accurately predicting IPN, a severe complication of AP. They often lack precision in identifying high-risk patients and fail to account for the unique pathophysiology of IPN, resulting in suboptimal sensitivity and specificity. To address these limitations, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), as branches of artificial intelligence, have emerged as powerful tools for disease prediction.

ML and DL models can integrate diverse clinical variables and improve predictive accuracy compared to traditional methods12. In one study, logistic regression (LR), Fully connected neural network (FCNN), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost,XGB) were used to predict the risk of severe acute pancreatitis (SAP), demonstrating that ML models outperformed traditional scoring systems in risk stratification13. Recent advances show that ML with feature optimization and explainable AI (XAI) can achieve both high performance and transparency across clinical domains. In dengue diagnosis, Recursive Feature Elimination with Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) improved Random Forest (RF) performance while identifying key predictors14. Similarly, in maternal health, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)-optimized features with ensemble learning achieved high accuracy, with SHAP clarifying decision logic15.

Evidence from hepatology further supports this approach. Studies using Deep Feature Synthesis with tree ensembles achieved strong performance and provided interpretability through SHAP analysis16. Other work combined tree-based selection with ensemble models and XAI, though noting limitations like dataset bias and lack of external validation17. An Artificial Neural Network (ANN)-based framework optimized by LDA significantly improved accuracy while systematically integrating SHAP, Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), and Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE), emphasizing the need for rigorous validation18.

Although ML has been increasingly applied in predicting disease severity, previous studies on AP mainly focused on overall severity rather than IPN, and often lacked external validation or model interpretability. To address these gaps, this study developed and externally validated an interpretable ML framework for individualized IPN prediction. Multiple algorithms, including linear, tree-based, and neural network models, were systematically compared under nested cross-validation and temporal validation. Embedded feature selection methods (L1, Elastic Net, and tree-based importance) and SHAP interpretation were integrated to enhance model robustness and clinical explainability.

This approach combines methodological rigor and interpretability, distinguishing our work from previous studies and providing a reliable tool for early IPN risk assessment.

Method

Data collection and processing

This was a single-center retrospective study conducted at Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University, including 728 patients with AP admitted between December 2017 and December 2023. Patients included in this study were 18 years of age or older and were diagnosed with a first episode of AP according to the 2012 Atlanta Classification criteria. The classification criteria for AP refer to the Revised Atlanta Classification and Definitions by International Consensus3. Exclusion criteria included: (1) lack of early disease information, no abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan within one week of onset, or no serum marker tests within 24 hours of onset; (2) patients with recurrent AP; (3) patients with chronic pancreatitis, pancreatitis related to trauma or pregnancy; (4) patients with cancer; (5) patients under 18 years of age. After applying these criteria, 432 patients were classified into the IPN group, and 296 into the non-IPN group. A flow diagram of the selection process is presented in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Most predictor variables were collected within 24 hours of admission. Additional variables–such as comorbidities, MCTSI, and organ failure–were obtained at predefined time points but always before IPN diagnosis. Since IPN typically developed days after admission, all variables were available prior to outcome determination. Details are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

The primary outcome was IPN, defined according to the 2012 Revised Atlanta Classification and supported by the latest international consensus guidelines3,19. Diagnosis required evidence of pancreatic or peripancreatic necrosis with either (1) microbiological confirmation or (2) gas within the necrotic area on contrast-enhanced computed tomography. The diagnosis date was defined as the earliest time either criterion was met.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No.: XA Lin Yan Shen [KS2025] 002-001). All analyses were performed using anonymized data. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified records, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

In this study, we preprocessed the raw data to ensure its suitability for training and prediction with ML and FCNN. The main steps of preprocessing include binarization, one-hot encoding, normalization, and data distribution analysis.

Missing values were imputed using the median (for continuous variables) or mode (for categorical variables). The proportion of missing data for each variable is provided in Supplementary Table S4. To prevent data leakage, all preprocessing steps, including imputation and normalization, were fitted exclusively on the training folds during cross-validation and subsequently applied to the corresponding validation folds and the external test set. To address class imbalance, inverse class weighting was incorporated into the loss function, and stratified sampling was employed during cross-validation to ensure representative data splits. Categorical variables were one-hot encoded prior to model training, expanding several variables into multiple dummy features and resulting in a maximum of 33 input dimensions. Model development and validation

Six predictive models were developed: gradient boosting machine (GBM), XGB, RF, support vector machine (SVM), LR, and a FCNN. The data were temporally split, with samples from 2017 to 2021 used for model development, and 2022 to 2023 held out as an independent external test set for temporal validation. Nested cross-validation was applied within the training period: the outer 5-fold CV was used to estimate model performance, while the inner 3-fold CV was used for hyperparameter tuning and feature selection.

To mitigate data leakage, embedded feature selection was integrated directly into the model training process within a nested cross-validation framework applied exclusively to the training set (2017–2021). Within this framework, hyperparameters were optimized and feature subsets were determined independently inside each training fold.

The final model was subsequently constructed by applying the optimized training procedure to the entire training set, and its generalizability was evaluated through a single assessment on the held-out temporal test set (2022–2023).

All models were implemented using scikit-learn and XGBoost, with class weighting applied to address class imbalance. Hyperparameters were tuned using 5-fold inner cross-validation, optimizing the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC).

For linear models, embedded feature selection was performed via L1 or Elastic Net regularization in combination with SelectFromModel, retaining features with non-zero coefficients within each training fold to prevent information leakage. The FCNN employed L1 or Elastic Net regularization at the input layer to encourage sparsity, depending on the configuration.

For tree-based models (RF, GBM, and XGBoost), no explicit feature elimination was conducted; instead, features were implicitly weighted according to split gain or impurity reduction during training. Importantly, all feature selection or weighting processes were confined strictly to the training folds.

The FCNN architecture consisted of one or two hidden layers containing 64 to 128 neurons per layer, with activation functions chosen from ReLU and Tanh. We performed hyperparameter optimization through a grid search coupled with five-fold cross-validation. The search employed the Adam optimizer with learning rates ranging from 0.0005 to 0.001 and incorporated early stopping (patience=10). A summary of the hyperparameter search space and the resulting optimal configurations are provided in Table 2 and Supplementary Table S1, respectively.

Model performance was primarily assessed using ROC-AUC. Secondary metrics included accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and Brier score. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (95% CIs) for ROC-AUC, accuracy, and F1-score were estimated via 1000 bootstrap iterations. Calibration and precision–recall (PR) curves were plotted for visual assessment. The decision threshold for binary classification was determined by Youden’s Index, which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity on the ROC curve. Specifically, the threshold was selected at the point where the true positive rate and the true negative rate were maximized. Pairwise ROC-AUC differences between models were evaluated using a bootstrap-based DeLong-like test and visualized as heatmaps. The best-performing model was further interpreted using SHAP values to quantify feature contributions.

Result

Model performance and feature selection

All models were trained using embedded feature selection within a nested cross-validation framework, resulting in a spectrum of model complexities defined by the retention of 5 to 33 features. Tree-based ensembles–RF, GBM, and XGB–achieved the strongest external performance, with ROC-AUC values approaching 0.76. The FCNN with elastic-net regularization followed closely, achieving an ROC-AUC of 0.74, while linear models and the SVM delivered slightly lower results, with ROC-AUCs in the range of 0.68 to 0.73. The robust generalizability of all models was further confirmed by internal cross-validation, which yielded consistently high mean ROC-AUCs between 0.81 and 0.82.

As a conventional clinical benchmark, the APACHE II score was also evaluated independently on the external validation set. Its predictive performance was notably lower than that of most ML models, with a ROC-AUC of 0.596 and a PR-AUC of 0.695, highlighting the potential added value of multivariable ML approaches for IPN prediction.

Supplementary Fig. S1 shows the results of statistical comparison and calibration analysis. Feature interpretation of the best model

RF was selected due to its superior external ROC-AUC and well-calibrated performance. SHAP analysis revealed that higher levels of Fibrinogen, Prothrombin Time, APACHE II score, INR, and inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and C-reactive protein (CRP) were associated with an increased risk of IPN, as shown in Fig. 3. Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated multiple ML models for predicting IPN using a robust framework that integrated embedded feature selection and temporal external validation. A key finding was the superior and robust performance of tree-based ensemble methods, particularly RF. When trained on features selected by a tree-based selector, RF achieved optimal performance on the independent test set, exhibiting excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.764) and outstanding precision in identifying IPN cases (Average Precision = 0.854). Comparable performance was observed for other tree-based models, including XGBoost and GBM, which were evaluated under the same feature-selection paradigm. In contrast, models relying on L1- or Elastic Net–based feature selection, such as LR, SVM, and FCNN, showed more moderate performance. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of aligning model architecture with feature selection strategy and suggest that tree-based approaches are particularly effective in capturing the complex, non-linear relationships underlying IPN development.

Earlier prediction models for AP severity and IPN mainly relied on multivariable LR using clinician-selected variables13,20. Although these approaches are transparent and easy to interpret, they often fail to capture nonlinear and high-dimensional interactions in clinical data, which limits their discriminative power and external generalizability.

More recent research has shifted toward machine and deep learning approaches that integrate embedded feature selection, ensemble methods, and XAI to enhance predictive accuracy and interpretability21,22. Clinical studies across various diseases have demonstrated that combining optimized feature selection with XAI techniques–such as SHAP and LIME–can yield models that are both accurate and transparent, supporting more trustworthy decision-making in medical settings14,16,18.

Building on these methodological advances, our study demonstrates the added value of an integrated ML framework for IPN prediction in acute pancreatitis. Among all evaluated models, RF achieved the most favorable external performance, with superior calibration compared to linear and neural network–based approaches. To contextualize the added value of ML, we benchmarked model performance against the conventional APACHE II score. On the temporal test set, APACHE II showed only moderate discrimination (ROC-AUC = 0.596, PR-AUC = 0.695), whereas most ML models achieved ROC-AUC values exceeding 0.70. These results underscore the potential of multivariable learning-based approaches to enhance early IPN risk stratification beyond traditional clinical scoring systems.

Beyond discrimination, calibration is essential for clinical deployment because it determines whether predicted risks correspond to observed outcomes and supports threshold selection tailored to clinical priorities. All tree-based ensembles produced clinically reliable probability estimates, with the RF showing the lowest Brier score of 0.194, as reported in Supplementary Table S2. At the standard threshold, RF also provided high precision (0.893) with moderate recall (0.604), and decision curve analysis further supported its potential clinical utility. In practice, excessive false-positive predictions may lead to unnecessary imaging or antibiotic exposure, whereas false-negative predictions could delay timely intervention. Therefore, well-calibrated risk estimates and flexible threshold adjustment are critical to balancing sensitivity and specificity in real-world use.

Statistical comparisons via pairwise DeLong tests showed no statistically significant differences in discrimination among models within the same feature-selection group, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1a–c, with all . Nevertheless, RF maintained a modest numerical advantage over other tree-based ensembles and was further characterized by consistently favorable calibration. These results support selecting RF as the most clinically robust predictor, based on its practical consistency, reliable probability estimates, and interpretability, rather than relying solely on marginal statistical superiority in AUC.

To further enhance model interpretability, SHAP analysis was conducted on the best-performing RF model to quantify each feature’s contribution to IPN prediction23,24. The analysis revealed that systemic inflammatory, coagulation, and organ dysfunction markers were the dominant determinants of IPN, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Elevated Fibrinogen, Prothrombin Time, INR, CRP, Procalcitonin, Interleukin-6, and D-dimer were associated with increased predicted risk, reflecting the hyperinflammatory and procoagulant state characteristic of severe acute pancreatitis. Conversely, higher Lymphocyte count, and Hematocrit, along with lower Creatinine and MCTSI scores, were linked to reduced risk, suggesting preserved immune competence, adequate perfusion, and less extensive parenchymal necrosis. Established severity indices such as APACHE II and MCTSI also ranked among the most influential predictors, demonstrating strong concordance between model-derived and clinical understanding.

From a clinical standpoint, the SHAP-derived pattern closely mirrors the pathophysiological cascade leading from sterile necrosis to infection. Elevated coagulation and inflammatory markers indicate endothelial injury and microthrombosis, which compromise pancreatic perfusion and create a favorable environment for bacterial translocation. Meanwhile, lower Hematocrit values reflect hypovolemia and catabolic stress, often observed in patients who progress to IPN. Representative features such as MCTSI, Hematocrit, and D-dimer thus bridge radiologic severity, circulatory disturbance, and coagulation dysfunction–the three interdependent mechanisms underlying infected necrosis25,26. This coherence between model insights and biological mechanisms reinforces both the interpretability and credibility of the predictive framework.

The robustness and generalizability of the models were supported by consistent internal and external performance. During cross-validation, all models achieved mean AUC values between 0.81 and 0.82, with RF and XGB showing particularly stable performance at 0.816 ± 0.020 and 0.817 ± 0.011, respectively. Detailed cross-validation outcomes are provided in Supplementary Table S2. External validation revealed only a minor decline in discrimination, demonstrating good temporal transportability and model stability. These findings indicate that the proposed framework can generalize reliably to new patient populations, despite inherent heterogeneity in disease presentation and clinical practice. However, as a single-center retrospective study, this work remains limited by potential selection bias, residual confounding, and temporal drift. Future multicenter and prospective validation will be essential to confirm its reproducibility and ensure real-world applicability before clinical deployment.

Clinically, the proposed ML framework has the potential to support early risk stratification of IPN, enabling timely identification of high-risk patients and optimization of clinical decision-making. Such predictive tools could guide escalation of care, such as earlier transfer to intensive monitoring, targeted antibiotic therapy, or closer imaging surveillance in patients predicted to develop infection. By integrating routinely available laboratory and clinical parameters, the model is both interpretable and feasible for real-time application in tertiary and emergency care settings.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this study included a moderate sample size (n = 728) with 432 IPN events. As Xuanwu Hospital is a large tertiary referral center, the case mix may be enriched for more severe presentations, which could contribute to a relatively higher IPN proportion compared with primary or community settings. Second, although we implemented nested cross-validation, embedded feature selection, and an independent temporal validation cohort, the possibility of model optimism due to center-specific practice patterns cannot be completely excluded. Importantly, the consistent performance across internal cross-validation and temporal validation suggests that overfitting was mitigated to a substantial extent. Third, while temporal external validation was performed, validation across different institutions is still needed to confirm transportability under varying laboratory assays, clinical workflows, and patient populations. Prospective multicenter studies and, if necessary, model recalibration are warranted before broader clinical implementation.

Future work should prioritize validation in multicenter cohorts spanning different care settings, including both tertiary referral and non-referral hospitals, to further assess transportability and calibration stability across institutions. Model recalibration and, where appropriate, transfer learning approaches may help adapt predictions to evolving clinical standards, laboratory assays, and population characteristics over time. In addition, integrating multimodal data–such as radiological features and longitudinal trajectories of inflammatory markers–may further enhance predictive performance and provide deeper mechanistic insight. Finally, future studies should extend evaluation beyond discrimination and calibration to assess real-world clinical impact, including outcome-oriented and cost-sensitive analyses, to determine whether model-assisted decision-making improves patient outcomes compared with standard practice.

Conclusion

This study developed and evaluated an interpretable ML framework for early prediction of IPN in patients with acute pancreatitis, with IPN defined according to established diagnostic criteria. Predictors were restricted to routinely available variables collected early after admission and prior to IPN diagnosis to minimize information leakage, and embedded feature selection was integrated within a nested cross-validation framework. Using a temporal hold-out cohort for validation, the random forest model achieved the best overall performance, with an external AUC of 0.764, balanced precision and recall, and the lowest Brier score, indicating reliable probability calibration. SHAP analysis highlighted clinically plausible predictors, including fibrinogen, APACHE II score, D-dimer, IL-6, and CRP as risk-enhancing factors, whereas lymphocyte count and hematocrit were associated with reduced risk. These results support the potential of transparent, tree-based models for early, data-driven risk stratification in acute pancreatitis. Future work should prioritize prospective multicenter validation, calibration assessment and recalibration across institutions, and integration of multimodal data to enhance generalizability and real-world applicability.
