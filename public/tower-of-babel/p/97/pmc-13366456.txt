The Importance of Studying the Cognitive Neuroscience of Aging Across Cultures
Authors: Angela Gutchess, Yong Sheng Tan, James Qian, Joshua Oon Soo Goh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIt is now widely appreciated that culture can shape cognition and neural engagement. However, research investigating the effects of culture across the adult lifespan, with a focus on cognitive aging, is lacking. In this article, we consider why this integration of topics is useful and discuss the ways in which culture has been proposed to affect cognition and the cognitive neuroscience of aging. The literature thus far is reviewed from the perspectives of long‐term memory and decision‐making, which broadly encompass information processing in the brain and reflect a person's worldview and resulting actions. We argue for the importance of integrating aging into the study of cultural neuroscience, making the case from two different perspectives: the importance of studying cultural influences over the trajectory of the adult lifespan and the importance of understanding the cognitive neuroscience of aging across cultural groups. We discuss challenges and opportunities for future research, concluding that studying the cognitive neuroscience of aging across cultures has the potential to broaden understanding of the factors that contribute to successful cognitive aging as well as age‐related decline and pathology.Keywords: aging, cognitive neuroscience, culture and cognition, decision‐making, memory

Introduction

All human beings grow old, but no two people grow old in exactly the same way. This is particularly important for the effects of aging on the brain and its cognitive abilities. Many indicators of cognitive performance [1, 2], brain structure [3, 4], and brain functional responses [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10] show that differences between individuals expand rather than converge with increasing age. Such age‐related trends are indicative of cumulative differential intrinsic and extrinsic influences across persons that shape how one's mind and brain change over the lifespan. In this review, we consider one of the most obvious yet elusive of such influences over the lifespan—culture.

Every individual is deeply entrenched in at least one culture. Yet, the examination of cultural universals and variations across individuals and how they contribute to brain and cognition is only recently taking center stage. In our review, we integrate key concepts and findings across recent studies, primarily focusing on comparisons of Western and Eastern cultural groups that pertain to differences in cognitive strategies and the corresponding neural activity. We confine our scope as such because the juxtaposition between these two cultures has been the most researched in the field, based on purported differences in analytic versus holistic processing [11] and independent versus interdependent (or collective) self‐construals [12]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that culture is a much broader concept; in the next section we briefly consider definitions and approaches to defining culture.

Importantly, the main aim of this article is to examine the ways in which culture influences the cognitive brain as a function of age across the adult lifespan. To this end, we characterize the cultural neuroscience literature that is based on research with young adults before reviewing the small literature that integrates aging. Notably, cognitive neuroscience research comparing age effects across cultures is limited to a handful of studies with a focus on memory and decision‐making; we review all of these findings. Social, affective, and other domains of cognition are not reviewed here because the joint effects of culture and age have yet to be assessed using cognitive neuroscience methods. This review contributes by arguing for the integration of aging into cultural neuroscience from two complementary perspectives: the need to examine how cultural influences shape development across the adult lifespan and the need to understand how cognitive aging unfolds across different cultural contexts.

Defining Culture

The core definition of culture is based on delineating groups of people with shared experiences, an approach that spans the macro (e.g., countries or global regions) and micro (e.g., subregions; local organizations or institutions) levels. Much of the research has used country to operationalize culture, which can capture variation across a number of interconnected factors (e.g., norms, beliefs, cognitive styles, educational practices, and lifestyles). Most of these studies contrast people across continents or far‐flung regions of the globe (e.g., the United States vs. China, rather than the United States vs. Canada). This means that cultural comparisons can overlap with other factors, such as socioeconomic status, rural versus urban upbringing, and ethnicity. The literature on these topics has largely progressed separately from that on culture (see [13] for further discussion of the intersection of these topics). For example, studies comparing racial groups tend to focus on commonalities or differences in risk factors for diseases [14, 15]. Aside from a body of research on the effects of bilingualism on cognition and dementia diagnosis [16], there is little consideration of other cultural factors related to life experiences. Large‐scale studies are needed across cultures to disentangle the contributions of these different group‐related factors; granted, this is challenging given the expense and hardware constraints for cognitive neuroscience research.

Nevertheless, painting culture with these broad brushstrokes of interrelated factors that differ across countries or regions still permits specific inferences to be drawn about mechanisms. For example, some research balances the practical constraints of conducting empirical studies (e.g., accessing appropriate cultural samples in environments that support experimental testing) with a focus on a specific culture‐related dimension, such as language [17, 18], occupation [19], or agrarian practice [20]. One of the most common approaches is to define culture based on an individual's level of independent versus interdependent self‐construal, the extent to which one sees the self as a unique entity separate from others or inherently interconnected with and defined by others [12]. Individuals at the level of cultural group (e.g., Japanese vs. Americans) or even within a culture (e.g., more or less interdependent) can differ in the extent to which they display particular attitudes, behaviors, or brain markers consistent with these cultural values [21, 22] (although note that these effects are less commonly found for cognitive processes [23]). Extending this focus on specific cultural factors to study aging across cultures will be valuable for future research; at present, aging research largely defines cultural group based on nationality. In this article, we attempt to preserve the terms for cultural groups that are originally used in the cited studies, acknowledging inconsistency in how cultural groups are defined (e.g., Chinese Americans vs. Chinese vs. East Asians vs. Easterners).

Frameworks for Understanding Culture and Cognitive Neuroscience of Aging

At the outset of research on cognitive aging across cultures, Park et al. [24] established a model that laid the foundation for understanding how culture and aging jointly shape cognition. Their emphasis on the dynamic interplay of cultural factors throughout the lifespan is consistent with notions of developmental plasticity, culture‐behavioral‐brain loops, and culture–gene coevolution [25, 26, 27, 28]. Their influential model distinguishes between culture‐invariant cognitive tasks, those tapping general cognitive resources without culture‐specific strategies, and culture‐saturated tasks, those engaging culture‐specific knowledge or strategies. On culture‐invariant tasks, they argued, younger adults would perform similarly across cultures, and cognitive aging effects should be universal. That is, older adults would show similar declines across cultures, with no interaction of culture by age. In contrast, performance on culture‐saturated tasks would differ by culture and interact with age. Crucially, they argued that cultural differences observed in younger adults would either persist or diminish with aging, depending on whether the task relies on automatic or controlled cognitive processes (see Figure 1). For well‐practiced, automatic processes, cultural differences would persist or even magnify with age, because culturally specific experiences accumulate over a lifetime with relatively little age‐related impairment. In contrast, for effortful, controlled processes, cultural differences would diminish with age, as age‐related declines in cognitive resources would hinder the successful use of culturally specific strategies. Their foundational framework also provides clear predictions and methodological recommendations crucial for future cross‐cultural cognitive aging research.

The Park et al. model [24] proposes that cultural differences magnify with age for automatic processes but diminish for controlled processes. The revised model by Gutchess and Gilliam [25] suggests that it is more useful to think of the spectrum from top‐down processing to bottom‐up processing to account for cognitive performance. In this model, cultural differences are predicted to persist or amplify with age for tasks relying heavily on top‐down processing, whereas performance converges across cultures with age for tasks involve more bottom‐up processing.

Building upon this foundation, Park and Gutchess [26, 27] integrated a neuroscientific perspective into the original model that focused on behavior. They suggested that similarities in neural aging across cultures indicate universal, biology‐driven cognitive aging effects, whereas culturally distinct neural patterns highlight experience‐driven plasticity. Furthermore, they argued that neural aging can constrain the expression of culturally shaped neural pathways. With age‐related neural dedifferentiation (i.e., reduced functional specificity and broader recruitment of cortical regions), older adults may rely on more generalized—and often similar—neural networks to meet task demands, which can reduce the distinct, specialized activation patterns that differentiate younger cultural groups; thus, cultural differences in neural activation may attenuate or converge with age. At the same time, experiential plasticity could play a role in mitigating neurobiological constraints with age.

More recently, Gutchess and Gilliam [25] proposed a significant revision, motivated by new empirical evidence that challenged aspects of the earlier frameworks. Although the original Park et al. [24] model drew a binary distinction between automatic versus controlled processes, some findings indicated that this dichotomy was too simplistic. Certain tasks initially presumed to be automatic were found to still rely on cognitive resources or could be altered by task context. Gutchess and Gilliam responded by shifting the theoretical lens from a binary view to a continuum of processing demands. The revised model emphasizes the extent to which tasks engage top‐down versus bottom‐up processes, rather than strictly categorizing tasks as “automatic” or “controlled” (see Figure 1). Top‐down processing here refers to goal‐directed, knowledge‐driven cognition, which can be influenced by culture‐based strategies and motivations, whereas bottom‐up processing is driven by incoming sensory information with less opportunity for strategic modulation. According to this new model, cultural differences are most likely to persist, or even amplify, with age for tasks that heavily involve top‐down processes, because individuals can draw on a lifetime of culturally ingrained strategies and knowledge to support performance. In contrast, for tasks dominated by bottom‐up processing, age‐related neurocognitive declines are expected to overwhelm cultural influences, leading to convergence of performance across cultures in late adulthood.

Gutchess and Gilliam's revised framework also emphasizes task flexibility, recognizing that how individuals approach tasks can vary significantly depending on contextual factors and instructions, which help account for findings that posed challenges for the original model. Empirical support for this above notion might be seen in that older East Asians engage in less selective object processing in lateral occipital regions than older Westerners when allowed to use their default passive viewing strategies [28]. However, when instructed to use a more Western analytic style, that is, when top‐down processing was constrained, selective object processing responses in occipital regions were rescued in the older East Asians [29] (these two studies are reviewed in greater detail below). In addition, cultural differences in brain structure seen between younger East Asians and Westerners were evident between their older counterparts, but only for those with higher levels of cognitive performance; age‐related structural brain decline dominated those with lower levels of cognition [30]. In sum, the revised framework built upon the original model while incorporating a more nuanced understanding of cognitive control and strategy use. It underscores a more dynamic interaction between culture and aging, along a spectrum from bottom‐up to top‐down processing demands.

Building on Gutchess and Gilliam's 2022 revision, Cho and Gutchess [31] further expanded the model by adopting a lifespan developmental perspective. This approach considers how culture shapes cognition across childhood, adolescence, and adulthood, emphasizing cumulative and dynamic cultural influences throughout developmental stages. They propose that cultural impacts on cognition may follow nonlinear developmental trajectories, potentially intensifying during certain life stages while diminishing at others.

In connection to this, Goh et al. [32, 33, 34] have suggested a predictive coding perspective of cultural strategies for interacting with the environment over the lifespan. In this view, cultural biases are acquired as with any reinforcement learning regime in which the value of specific behavioral programs is modulated by environmental reward (or punishment) signals throughout development and life experiences. Behaviors that lead to favorable outcomes are fortified, stabilized, and made more default, whereas those that have negative results are dampened or deprioritized. That is, we form behavioral biases. Because we are embedded in our local life environment, we thus acquire our individual set of behavioral biases, which we internally predict (whether implicitly or explicitly) to be appropriate for interacting with our environment. Critically, an individual's predictions and ensuing behavioral biases might have some commonalities with other individuals with the same environmental background, from which culture group biases emerge. With experience over development and aging, predictions that are reinforced over one's lifetime that are not challenged become further automatized and require few neural resources to be engaged. By contrast, predictions that face constant need for updating or reframing remain susceptible to age‐related reductions in neurocomputational resources. In relation to the above top‐down/bottom‐up framework, predictions that are novel relative to one's cultural expectations are not automatic and thus require more top‐down control to process. In this case, cultural differences emerge to the extent that a given task involves such culturally novel neurocomputations. With continued exposure and reinforcement over time, such subjectively novel predictions become more automatic and thus default, requiring less top‐down control and licensing the default bottom‐up responses to take precedence. In this case then, cultural differences emerge to the extent that a given task dissociates culture‐specific default processing strategies.

In summary, theoretical and descriptive models of culture, aging, and cognition have progressively refined our understanding of how culture shapes cognitive aging. From early distinctions between culture‐invariant and culture‐saturated tasks to recent lifespan developmental and learning approaches, these models emphasize the importance of examining cognitive neuroscience of aging across cultures. Such approaches provide valuable insights into both universal and culturally specific aging processes, guiding future studies towards a more comprehensive understanding of cognitive aging in varied cultural contexts.

Contributions From a Cognitive Neuroscience Approach

Cognitive neuroscience can make critical contributions to the study of culture through its ability to reveal differences in processes that could result in the same behavioral response. For example, it has been posited that cultural differences could emerge through multiple routes [35], with variation across cultures stemming from (1) the recruitment of distinct strategies or cognitive processes to tackle a challenging cognitive task (e.g., whether one processes information in terms of categorical/taxonomic or relational information, or attends to objects or contexts), (2) differences in the content of the information brought to bear to the task (e.g., thinking of different exemplars of a category or an idea based on what information is familiar or primed for an individual), or (3) the differences in the relative difficulty of engaging a process across cultures (e.g., making relative vs. absolute judgments; using a well‐practiced vs. an unfamiliar strategy). Neural data may help to distinguish amongst these possibilities, considering differences across groups in the magnitude or timing of neural activity (e.g., a larger blood oxygenation level dependent [BOLD] signal or a slower event‐related potential [ERP] component for one group compared to the other) or in the brain regions or processing components engaged by the task (e.g., activity in regions associated with object vs. scene processing with functional magnetic resonance imaging [fMRI]; ERP components associated with attention vs. semantic processing). Although the limitations of reverse inference prevent one from making conclusive interpretations based on patterns of neural data [36], the nascent state of cognitive neuroscience research on culture means that findings can lead researchers in unexpected directions, informing the next round of hypotheses and experiments to target specific mechanisms that account for cultural differences. Strengthening the understanding of strategies and processes engaged by individuals from different cultures can refine the process of developing more precise cognitive tasks. Additionally, understanding cognitive changes with age, including risk for dementia or other age‐related cognitive disorders, benefits from culturally informed cognitive neuroscience methods because neural measures are potentially even more sensitive and tuned assays of cognitive changes beyond behavioral measures alone [37].

However, despite the growing number of studies investigating the influence of culture on cognition with aging, there has been virtually no research incorporating measures of brain structure and function. In this review, we define relevant studies as those that (1) investigate older adults (aged 60+), (2) directly compare cultural groups, and (3) include cognitive neuroscience methods that assess brain structure or function (e.g., MRI or fMRI; other methods such as electroencephalography [EEG] or transcranial magnetic stimulation [TMS] also would be relevant, but there are no cross‐cultural studies with older adults). We discuss all papers that fit these criteria and acknowledge the small number and the corresponding limited range of conceptual and theoretical perspectives. Underscoring this point, the only studies to date that directly compare younger and older adults across cultures with a cognitive neuroscience approach have been conducted by our research teams, including the labs in which we trained as well as the labs we now lead [28, 38, 39]. To situate findings in a broader context, behavioral and young adult research that relates to these aging studies is briefly reviewed.

Importantly, although the above historical conceptual overview deals with cultural differences in neurocognitive operations, we now bring these to bear on new directions. In the following sections, we briefly review cross‐cultural research in our recent topics of interest: memory and decision‐making. Reviewing this literature builds the foundation for our integration of a cognitive neuroscience of aging perspective with a focus on these domains. We close by discussing promising future directions and challenges for the field.

To characterize the types of methods and samples used in these studies (see [40] for a review of these issues), our samples for aging studies are collected from two countries, comparing Eastern (i.e., Singapore for earlier research; Taiwan more recently) and Western (i.e., the United States) samples. Our Eastern samples tend to be homogenous (e.g., Han Chinese), whereas our American samples have been majority white but include multiple ethnic and racial groups. That is, we sample for exposure to American culture without considering race or ethnicity, other than excluding Asian Americans who may have some exposure to Eastern cultural traditions. Study materials are developed collaboratively across sites, and materials are translated such that participants are tested in their native country in their native language. Neuropsychological tests, selected for appropriateness across cultures, are administered to ensure that samples are comparable on cognitive ability across cultures. These features are necessary for aging studies; due to the number of individuals from various cultures who move to pursue their education, studies of young adults may be able to be collected at a single site. However, it would be challenging to recruit well matched samples of older adults from a single site (e.g., potentially high variability in history of migration, language, socioeconomic status, and education).

Investigating the combination of value‐based decision‐making and memory offers advantages by comparing processes thought to reflect the operation of typical aging processes (frontostriatal) as opposed to Alzheimer's disease (temporoparietal) [41]. We predict culture and core values will shape performance and neural activity more for systems primarily impacted by normal aging processes compared to processes and regions impacted by dysfunctional aging, given the greater plasticity and flexibility available for a healthy brain. To test this idea, our line of research has investigated cognitive domains that we predict will be differently affected by normal versus pathological aging processes. Specifically, we assess how the interplay of aging and culture differentially affects decision‐making, mediated by frontostriatal systems, and memory for detailed information, mediated by medial temporal lobe (MTL) regions; see the model in Figure 2. In the following sections, we will detail evidence of how these processes are affected by aging and preliminary evidence for the contributions of culture.

Recent Considerations of Culture and Age Effects on Memory and Decisions

Memory

As the pioneering research with patient H.M. [42] shows, the critical role of the hippocampus and MTLs in long‐term memory has been appreciated. Subsequent research with cognitive neuroscience methods substantiated this; when memories are episodic and detailed, involving rich associations amongst an array of multisensory elements, these regions are strongly implicated in encoding information into memory [43, 44]. The hippocampus works in concert with the sensory regions associated with perceiving the information (e.g., visually or auditorily) as well as with prefrontal regions that contribute to the organization and strategic use of memory [45, 46]. Neural changes in medial temporal regions are associated with changes in memory with age [41], reflecting the importance of this region in memory for younger and older adults. In this section, we will characterize the research conducted thus far on memory across cultures, first discussing the larger behavioral literature before reviewing the small literature that integrates aging.

Memory Across Cultures

Cross‐cultural research conducted with young adults reveals differences in strategies and what information is prioritized in memory (for reviews, see Refs. [47, 48, 49]). For example, research on autobiographical memory in which participants recall events from their own lives shows cultural differences in what information is remembered and how that information is conveyed. Americans tend to focus on their own role in events, whereas Chinese focus on the role of others; Americans also tend to convey more emotional information than Chinese [50]. Similarly, in experimental tests of memory, Westerners remember more information related to the self, whereas Easterners remember more information related to others [51, 52]. In terms of strategy differences, Americans used taxonomic categories to organize information in memory more than Chinese [53]. Although use of a strategy, such as categorization, can support the effective and accurate recall of information from memory, strategies also can create vulnerabilities in memory. For example, Americans were more prone to commit memory errors for categorically related information than were Turks [54], illustrating the ways in which the use of a particular strategy can lead to errors in memory.

One cultural difference that has been well‐studied is that Americans’ memories tend to contain more detail than East Asians’ [23, 55, 56, 57, 58]. That is, although Americans and East Asians might both recognize that they have previously studied a picture of a bicycle, Americans are more likely to recognize the precise same item that was studied earlier, distinguishing it from similar items (e.g., correctly recognizing the photo of a bicycle that matches the color, shape, and orientation of the original, and rejecting lures that differ on some of those features). In some cases, these cultural differences are limited to recognizing the original item with a high level of detail, distinguishing it from similar lures [56], but in other studies [23], cultural differences emerge more generally, including distinguishing old items from entirely new and unrelated items. Both of these patterns could reflect the possibility that Americans have high‐fidelity, detailed representations available in memory that support accurate memory decisions. Our findings converge with findings of cultural differences in autobiographical memory, for which Americans include more details about specific events in their memories, whereas Easterners include more information about routine events [59, 60, 61]. The convergence in findings is striking given the vastly different methods. In the studies described in this section, the encoding episode (i.e., viewing pictures of objects) is equated across cultural groups, whereas the study of autobiographical memory is less controlled in that individuals are free to attend to various aspects of the original event as well as in choosing what information to convey from their memory.

In terms of integrating a cognitive neuroscience approach, we have probed the neural basis of cultural differences in memory specificity. However, results thus far do not clearly account for how Americans may form more detailed memories than East Asians. One study investigated encoding when participants first encountered information and potentially formed specific, detailed memories (i.e., recognizing an exact item as having been studied previously, rather than recognizing more generally that an item of that type—but not the identical exemplar—was previously studied). Cultural differences emerged in the engagement of some neural regions associated with memory and the formation of detailed visual memories: the fusiform, hippocampus, and parahippocampal gyrus [58]. Surprisingly, given their less detailed memories, the difference across conditions was larger for East Asians than Americans. Results were interpreted as potentially reflecting cultural differences in difficulty or the efficiency of encoding processes (e.g., whether more activation reflects more challenge in engaging these regions to encode detailed information).

When comparing cultures during retrieval, the stage of memory during which individuals recognize information as having been encountered previously, some neural differences emerged across cultures in response to specific memories [62]. Right fusiform activity was stronger for Americans who were better at distinguishing old items from similar lures in memory, whereas those Taiwanese who performed worse on these judgments engaged the region more (i.e., the relationship between right fusiform activity and memory performance was negative for Taiwanese). However, the most pronounced cultural differences emerged for the comparison of neural activity when correctly deciding that an item was “old” (studied previously) versus “new” (not presented previously); see the young adult data in Figure 3. Across a number of medial temporal and prefrontal regions (i.e., right hippocampus, left inferior frontal gyrus, and left middle frontal gyrus), Americans tended to engage the regions more for new items, whereas Taiwanese tended to engage the same regions more in response to old items. Again, the pattern of results does not speak directly to cultural differences in the amount of detail contained in memory. Instead, they may suggest cultural differences in the information processing priorities (i.e., orientation to novel vs. familiar information).

Despite several demonstrations of cultural differences in specific memory [23, 38, 62, 63], the underlying mechanism has remained elusive. In our recent work [31, 62], we predicted that cultural groups would differ in their tendency to pattern separate, which is forming a novel representation to new information, as opposed to pattern complete, which is applying existing representation to treat information as if it is old and familiar. However, we identified cultural differences in comparisons of old versus new information rather than in comparisons specific to pattern separation. Furthermore, cultural differences were not pronounced in the MTL regions thought to support pattern separation [64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70], but rather, in prefrontal regions.

Finding cultural differences for old versus new items [62] implicates other mechanisms that may account for cultural differences. At retrieval, Americans engage neural regions related to memory processes for new information more than to old information, whereas Taiwanese show the reverse pattern (old > new). These patterns may indicate a broader cultural difference in what information people find salient and are prone to attend to, with Americans exhibiting a novelty bias and East Asians preferentially processing familiar information. Whether or not these biases are limited to memory contexts or occur more broadly when processing information, perhaps reflecting cultural differences in the value of tradition, is an interesting question. Familiarity is a critical component in a host of behaviors in humans and nonhuman animals, including determining preferences and liking, as in the mere exposure effect [71], or in signaling safety, as a factor affecting ingestion [72]. The deep‐rooted nature of processes tied to familiarity makes it unlikely cultural groups show dramatic wide‐reaching differences but could indicate situational or contextual differences in proneness to attend to one of the signals over another.

Other research employing cognitive neuroscience methods has probed potential cultural differences in orientation to objects versus backgrounds. Although these studies did not directly assess memory processes, the findings are relevant in that differences across cultures in responses to qualities and aspects of information would have implications for what is remembered. These studies are motivated by findings that East Asians may attend to and remember background contextual information more than Westerners, who are more object‐oriented, processing and remembering information in a more context‐free manner [73, 74, 75]. Some early cognitive neuroscience research probed this question without directly testing memory processes. When viewing complex scenes (e.g., a focal object on a compatible background context) during an fMRI study, Americans activated regions associated with object processing more than East Asians [76]. Cultural differences did not emerge in regions associated with processing of backgrounds. Further research investigated neural activity in response to viewing background context information that was congruent (e.g., a squirrel in the forest) or incongruent (e.g., a squirrel in the kitchen) with the focal object. A study that used an fMR‐adaptation paradigm, which assessed the decrease in the BOLD response upon repeated presentation of stimuli, found that Chinese participants exhibited greater adaptation than Americans in bilateral lateral occipital complex, a region associated with object processing, to the viewing of incongruent scenes [77]. These results were interpreted as reflecting East Asians’ greater sensitivity to contextual information, which motivated them to have greater focus on subsequent presentations of the object when it was presented in an incongruent scene. No cultural differences or evidence for adaptation emerged in regions associated with background processing.

ERPs also have been applied to investigate cultural differences in object versus background context processing and the effect of incongruent context information. The first study found that compared to European Americans, Asian Americans exhibited a larger N400 response to incongruent than congruent trials [78]. Because the N400 indexes semantic incongruity, the results were interpreted as reflecting greater processing of contextual information by Asian Americans compared to European Americans. Although this study did not probe memory, subsequent studies did. Two studies [79, 80] employed memory tests in which for some trials, the background context was changed from what had been originally studied. Although both the N400 semantic incongruity response [80] and old/new effects [79], emerging in the 300–500 ms time window, distinguished previously studied intact items from those for which the background was changed at retrieval, these components did not reliably differ across cultures. Some circumscribed differences did emerge. For Masuda et al. [80], the relationship between the N400 response and memory accuracy differed across cultures, such that the N400 response was larger as memory accuracy increased for the Japanese sample, whereas the relationship tended to be more negative for the European Canadian sample (i.e., higher levels of memory accuracy were associated with a smaller N400). These findings were interpreted as reflecting the possibility that European Canadians experienced less interference from the inconsistent backgrounds than Japanese. In contrast, the takeaway from the Weigl et al. [79] study, which compared Chinese and German participants, emphasized the lack of cultural differences in object/background memory. This study had a strong emphasis on memory, including comparisons of old/new and source memory (i.e., were the object and background originally presented together or not?).

Memory Across Cultures With Age

Research conducted largely in Western samples establishes numerous ways in which long‐term memory changes with advancing age. Older adults tend to remember less information than younger adults, particularly when retrieval processes are effortful, such as demanding precision, the strategic use of cues, or the resolution of interference [81, 82, 83]. The tendency for memories to be less detailed and more prone to errors with age [84, 85] is associated with changes to the MTLs, reflecting changes as a function of age as well as early pathological changes associated with Alzheimer's disease [64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70].

In the few experimental studies comparing the effects of aging on memory across cultures, some show consistency across cultures. In those cases, age effects generalize across cultures, or cultural differences present in younger adults also emerge in comparisons of older adults. For example, the tendency for higher rates of memory errors with age, primarily documented in Western populations [84, 85], is found for both Turkish and American samples of older adults [86]. Cross‐cultural differences in the types of memory errors [54] extend to older adults [86], such that younger and older Americans both commit more categorical memory errors than their Turkish counterparts. Furthermore, findings of age‐related reductions in source memory [87] and item‐context binding [88] generalize across cultures.

In contrast to the patterns of similarity, there are some findings of ways in which cognitive aging processes unfold differently across cultures. For example, American older adults employ a clustering strategy in memory more than Chinese older adults [53], and Western advantages for categorical information may exaggerate memory differences with age across cultures [89]. Memory strategies focused on the self may be less effective for Taiwanese older adults compared to American older adults [90].

In contrast to the emerging literature investigating cultural influences on memory with age, there are very few studies that apply cognitive neuroscience methods to investigate memory with age. One early study did not directly investigate memory but compared neural responses to object and background information, which is relevant to memory (as discussed above in the young adult section). Comparing younger and older adults from the United States and Singapore found some evidence for universal effects of age on hippocampal activity during the binding of objects to scenes, with decreases in the response with age [28]. In terms of cultural differences, object processing activity was impacted by age more in Singaporeans than Americans. Using the fMR‐adaptation paradigm described above, Singaporean older adults did not show as much reduction as American older adults in activity in the lateral occipital complex when objects were repeated; young adults did not differ across cultures. These results converge with some of the prior studies with young adults to suggest reduced sensitivity to object information in an Eastern sample, although here the culture differences were limited to older adults. This finding could have implications for memory because it suggests that some aspects of complex environments (in this case, objects) could be systematically attended to less in one cultural group than another, resulting in poorer memory formation. This pattern could be exacerbated with age if older adults differ across cultures in the prioritization of object information, or as a result of cognitive changes that occur with age (e.g., reductions in the ability to form and retrieve vivid memories, or in the cognitive demands of attending to non‐preferred information). Interestingly, evaluating the same sample as in Goh et al. [28], Chee et al. [29] also found that object processing can be rescued in Singaporean older adults when instructed to maintain selective attention on focal objects. Nevertheless, the evidence for consistent reductions across cultures in the hippocampal response during binding suggests that overall declines in memory with age may occur across cultures, perhaps reflecting biological processes that are not as amenable to experiential changes.

To date, there is only one study to use cognitive neuroscience methods to investigate memory processes across age and culture groups [38]. That study built on research with young adults in which retrieval of identical, similar, and new pictures was compared across American and Taiwanese cultures [62]. Integrating samples of older adults, the results indicate ways in which memory is affected similarly by age in both cultures, as well as ways in which memory may be differently vulnerable with age across cultures. In behaviorally distinguishing old from new information in memory, older Taiwanese performed disproportionately worse than older Americans and compared to their young adult counterparts. Although Taiwanese performed worse than Americans on distinguishing same versus similar items in memory, the cultural differences were no larger for older than for younger adults. In comparisons of neural activity, cultural differences in the pattern of hippocampal activity initially identified in young adults [62] extend to older adults (see Figure 3, top). That is, Americans engage these regions more when recognizing new compared to old items, compared to Taiwanese. In contrast, the cultural differences in left inferior frontal gyrus are eliminated with age (see Figure 3, bottom). Although young Americans tended to engage the regions more for new items, Taiwanese tended to engage the same regions more in response to old items. For older adults, cultural differences in activity for old versus new items did not emerge. Converging with the investigation of binding object and background information during the viewing of pictures [28, 29, 91], changes to hippocampal activity may be a universal property of aging. However, the engagement of frontal regions, including left inferior frontal gyrus, may reflect processing priorities, with cultural differences persisting even into older adulthood. In terms of the memory processes investigated in this specific study, the results indicate there may be cultural differences in orientation to old versus new information.

Should cultural groups differ in their relative prioritization of old information versus novel information, as speculated in the previous section, this could lead to differences in the focus on encoding (novel focus) versus retrieval (old/familiar focus) processes and have implications for cognitive control demands with age. If Americans engage neural regions more at encoding, this could support the formation of detailed memories, particularly with age. In contrast, if Taiwanese do not initially harness these regions to support detailed memory formation, they may have more interference to resolve at retrieval. Cognitive control processes are invoked when there is interference from similar novel exemplars at retrieval, perhaps as a result of weaker encoding. Cognitive control and interference resolution could be disproportionately impacted by age and age‐related decline related to Alzheimer's disease and related pathologies, such as engaging control processes reactively rather than proactively [70, 92, 93, 94, 95, 96]. Age‐related impairments in cognitive control would be expected to impair Taiwanese older adults’ memory performance more than American older adults’ performance. Comparing across encoding and retrieval stages within the same individuals across cultures will be critical to understanding potential cultural differences in trade‐offs and compensation in processes.

In terms of how these data fit with models to account for cultural differences with age [25], distinct neural regions may map on to different types of processes. For example, frontal lobes (e.g., left inferior frontal gyrus) are implicated in strategic processes, indicative of top‐down cognitive processes. In contrast, although activity in the MTLs may reflect some strategic processes, their activity should reflect more bottom‐up processing than frontal regions. Interestingly, the pattern of cultural differences with age differs across these regions during memory processing, with cultural differences persisting in the hippocampus with age, whereas the activity in left inferior frontal gyrus converges across conditions with age [38]. That is, cultural differences are less pronounced in frontal regions for older adults, perhaps reflecting a limited ability to engage in culture‐specific strategies for memory retrieval with age. Of course, these results are based on only a single study, and much more research, using a variety of cognitively demanding situations, is needed.

Value‐Based Decision‐Making

Research on risky decision‐making demonstrates that, apart from apparent rewards, risk appetites, education levels, and socioeconomic status, differences in cultural background also contribute to a person's value‐based choices [97, 98, 99]. As reviewed below, studies on cultural differences in decision‐making from more psychological or cognitive perspectives are relatively few in number. There are more studies on age differences in value‐based decision‐making with neural findings, but these have also only slowly gained traction over the past two decades. Critically, we are aware of no study examining how cultural differences in the neural processing of value‐based decisions diverge or converge with age. A more complete picture of how the human brain computes value‐based decisions in which top‐down processes mitigate bottom‐up reactions should incorporate experiential and biological factors in cross‐cultural comparisons across age. The few findings on cultural and age differences in decision behaviors and neural processing are thus reviewed below. By drawing on these prior studies and our own past as well as ongoing work, we delineate the influences on decision‐making coming from culture, age, and their interaction. Importantly, we highlight discounting processing as a potential common neural mechanistic account for the observed findings to date and a useful framework for subsequent investigation in this area.

Cultural Differences in Decision Processing

We begin with the consideration that East Asians engage more holistic styles in their cognitive processes, whereas Westerners favor more analytical styles of thought [11, 76, 100]. This translates to a tendency for Westerners to display behavior that reflects how objects are processed as more independent from their contexts. Conversely, East Asians have a tendency to prioritize processing the relationships between objects and their contextual relationships so that more information is incorporated in parallel when making decisions, regardless of relevance to task requirements [101]. These broad notions of analytic‐holistic cognitive biases are manifest across different aspects of cultural differences on value‐based decisions and related brain processes as follows.

Culture‐related cognitive differences are evident in how and why decisions are made and who makes them at the organizational and societal levels [102]. One study comparing Chinese, Japanese, and American participants found that Japanese self‐reported greater indecisiveness when facing decision‐making scenarios compared to their Chinese and American counterparts [103]. This cultural difference extends into value endorsements, as Japanese were more favorable for indecisive behaviors compared to decisive behaviors—a completely reversed trend relative to that in Chinese and Americans [103]. Individuals from collectivistic cultures preferably seek advice from other people when encountering difficult problems and are more likely to take and give advice [104], as well as experience greater positive affect and agency when undertaking decisions that incorporate social norms and other people's input [105]. By contrast, individualistic cultures place greater emphasis on one's personal preference when making decisions [106].

Cultural cognitive differences are also seen in how Westerners emphasize the linearity of patterns, whereas East Asians emphasize the cyclical nature of phasic patterns [107]. In a simulated stock market exercise, European Canadians were more likely to sell stocks that are decreasing in value and to buy stocks that are increasing in value. In contrast, Chinese participants were more likely to buy stocks that are on a downward trend and sell those that are on the rise [108]. In a study on gamblers, those with a European Canadian background showed more susceptibility to the hot‐hand fallacy (i.e., that the current trend will continue), whereas Chinese were more prone to the gambler's fallacy (i.e., that the current trend is due for a change) [109]. Such cultural differences reflect underlying differences in cognitive beliefs about the nature of change that may extend from differences in cognitive styles [110]. Specifically, beliefs about the cyclical nature of events are supported by holistic thinking styles, as an emphasis on the interconnectedness of things facilitates reconciling contradictory states due to phasic precedence. By contrast, linear thinking is supported by analytic cognitive styles, with a focus on stable object attributes amidst dynamic contexts that promotes decisions reflecting expectancies of discriminative logical consistency with reduced weighting of apparent contradictions.

Cultural differences in value‐based decision processes are also evident in major life path choices. East Asian teenagers seek to achieve a balancing act between maximizing individual desires by choosing courses based on self‐interest whilst fulfilling social expectations by choosing courses based on preferences stemming from family members [111]. This ecological observation reflects the incorporation of a broader range of additional variables in adolescent East Asian decision‐making that might become habitual and require more extensive neural processing.

We note that cultural differences in value‐based decision‐making might primarily arise from differences in perception of risk rather than the attitude towards said perceived risk [112]. Specifically, when East Asian or Western participants perceive similar levels of risk, they make similar behavioral decisions. However, East Asians are more likely to perceive identical situations as less risky compared to Westerners. Granted, this cultural difference is restricted to financial domains, with East Asians being more willing to take more financial risk than Westerners but not when health or academic‐related risks are at stake [113]. One explanation for increased financial risk‐taking in East Asians may stem from a less fine‐grained use of probabilities amongst East Asians. A study comparing Asians from multiple countries (including Malaysia, Indonesia, and Hong Kong) and British participants observed that Asians exhibit a smaller range of probabilities in both numerical (i.e., percentages) and verbal (e.g., very likely, probable, and possible) expressions, indicating a more polarized view of risk (win or lose vs. different levels of probabilistic outcomes). This polarization is exemplified by the finding that Asian participants were more likely to overstate their accuracy in a test of uncertainty judgments [114, 115]. We caution, however, that Japanese participants, who are considered East Asians, tend to instead display underconfidence [115], which highlights the need for a more nuanced definition of cultural differences as pertaining to local social and societal contingencies rather than more ethno‐geographical boundaries.

Recently, our lab examined cultural differences in probabilistic value‐based decisions using the lottery choice task (LCT) in which young adult Taiwanese and Americans decided to accept or decline stakes with varying probabilities of winning (or losing) different magnitudes of points (Figure 4) [39]. We found that younger adult Taiwanese accepted more risky stakes than Americans, particularly when probabilities ranged from 50% to less than 100% chances of winning. Importantly, fMRI data acquired in our study afforded insight into the neurocognitive processes associated with the decision behaviors. Specifically, ventromedial prefrontal (vmPFC) activity was less distinctively modulated by probability in Taiwanese participants compared to Americans who particularly suppressed vmPFC activity in the above probability range during decision processing. Consistent with the above notions, these findings reflect a holistic approach in East Asians that viewed differential probabilities of winning across trials as winning probabilities nonetheless in the long‐term (cyclically) and thus do not require specific discrimination. By contrast, Westerners might analytically discriminate each stake as independent events and engage the vmPFC to more objectively rank different probabilistic outcomes [116] when making comparative choices. Overall, the above domain‐specific cultural differences in value‐based decisions delineate the need for a generalizable neurocognitive mechanism to explain the real observed systematic differences in human decision‐making biases across individuals associated with different culture groups.

Age Differences in Decision Processing and Cultural Mitigation

Importantly, evidence suggests that the above culture‐related differences in decision processing might not be stable over the lifespan of an individual but may dynamically change with age. Indeed, age effects on value‐based decisions are also evident that depend on a myriad of factors, including task difficulty, dependency on learning, or contextual frame, which implicates possible sociocultural influences as well. Moreover, although self‐reported financial risk‐taking declines with age [117], behavioral findings suggest differently when using objectively measured risk‐taking and age [118, 119]. For instance, older adults are more risk‐taking than younger adults in tasks that require implicitly learning probabilistic payoffs, such as the Iowa gambling task [118], an effect that diminishes when learning is not required or when relevant information is explicit [120] or salient [121]. The ability to learn or discover optimal decision strategies as opposed to more simply responding to explicit associations requires a fine balance between using fluid and crystallized intelligence [122]. As such, the above laboratory measures of learning‐based value‐related decisions are advantageous to younger adults because of more intact fluid intelligence relative to older adults [119, 123]. In contrast, the recall of previous experiences or more crystallized or automatized algorithms might have greater influence on choice behavior in older adults compared to younger adults [124]. This suggests that culturally distinct experiences might play a larger role in older than younger adult decisions. At present, we know of no systematic empirical data on culture‐related mitigation of age differences in objectively tested decision processing. Thus, in the following section, we consider studies that characterize known effects of age on decision behaviors and neural processing and synthesize possible ways in which cultural modulation might occur.

Cognitive neuroscience studies have investigated neural correlates underlying differences in younger and older adult decision behaviors. Samanez‐Larkin et al. [125] found lower striatal sensitivity to anticipated monetary losses in older than younger adults, with minimal age differences for anticipated monetary gains. Seaman et al. [126] reported greater lateral frontal and anterior cingulate responses to high‐risk rewards in older than younger adults. In our own work using the LCT, Su et al. [10] found that older adults accept more losing stakes than younger adults (Figure 5), an effect in our Taiwanese sample that was replicated in a German [121] and a separate Taiwanese [127] sample, delineating an invariant effect of age on mixed lottery decision behaviors between these two cultures. Importantly, this age difference in decision behavior was associated with ventral striatal (VS) sensitivity to probability of winning in younger adults [10], consistent with its coding of reward [128, 129], in contrast to a dramatic shift in value sensitivity instead to the MTL and frontal areas in older adults. Indeed, older adult dorsal medial prefrontal and bilateral lateral prefrontal areas increased responses as probability of losing increased, consistent with Seaman et al. [126], with medial temporal and vmPFC areas increasing activity with increasing win probability. We further found that older adult scalp ERPs during LCT were also coupled with a qualitative delay in processing time to a later component [127]. Thus, older adults recruit more frontal resources when considering (with greater acceptance) unfavorable risks, relative to younger adults. This indicates that the additional neural recruitment subserves regulatory processes, possibly to reconcile affective responses to the negative valence material. Overall, older brain choice processing reflects more complex decision‐making computations than in younger adults, with differential treatment of gains and losses. Such complex computations might include the engagement of compensatory neurocomputations to scaffold and append existing ones [130, 131], alternative neurocomputational algorithms that subsume existing ones, or inefficient neural operations due to declines in biological processes. These cases stand as operational entry points where culturally reinforced neurocomputational strategies can bias older adult decision processes.

Indeed, the findings above suggest that value computation in the older brain incorporates information beyond just objective or expected value, and a broader range of more subjective and heterogeneous processes might contribute, part of which might include one's cultural background. A clue to this is seen in how older adults demonstrate greater within‐subject general variability [132] than younger adults in the responses of the nucleus accumbens, a key region involved in signaling anticipated gains, during decision processing. In Su et al. [10], we noted a much wider range of risk‐taking decision thresholds between older than younger individuals, with younger adults being more veridical and having acceptance/decline decision thresholds set at an expected value = 0. Critically, in Goh et al. [9], we examined LCT processing in a large sample of American older adults and found substantial between‐subjects variability in risk‐taking and risk‐aversion across individuals, albeit they were more risk‐taking than risk‐neutral as a group of older adults. This heterogeneity in decision behaviors across older individuals was also seen in our previous LCT studies involving German and Taiwanese samples [10, 121, 127]. Importantly, Goh et al. [9] found that in frontal, striatal, and medial temporal regions, whereas risk‐averters increased responses to increasing mixed lottery expected values, risk‐takers increased responses to decreasing expected values instead, with more extreme risk preferences associated with poorer Mini‐Mental State Exam performance. These findings support the above notion that age‐related deficits in fluid cognitive abilities limit older adult capacities to integrate information for online decisions. This, in turn, acquiesces to affective and motivational neural processes, which have been crystallized or automatized, to have greater contribution in older adult decisions, unlike in younger adults who rely on more veridical online information. Tying back to the framework of culturally reinforced and automatized predictions, we thus expect East Asian and Western differences in decision processing would be augmented rather than diminished in older adult samples relative to their younger counterparts.

In tandem, there are several demonstrations across studies of neurobehavioral sensitivity towards positive but not negative valence information in older adults [133, 134, 135, 136, 137, 138, 139, 140]. This is consistent with the above neural findings of reduction in affective sensitivity or greater regulation of sensitivity to negative outcomes during decision‐making in older brains [10, 125], with greater acceptance of losing stakes due to a cognitive and affective blind spot for potential losses. Alternatively, older adults might accept risks because they particularly experience status quo as loss (i.e., “You miss shots you do not take”) [141]. In either case, we might expect age differences in loss‐related decisions to be present across cultures.

Ongoing work in our lab using the LCT paradigm again is being conducted precisely to elucidate the joint roles of culture and age in the neural processing of value‐based decisions from a cognitive neuroscience perspective [142]. Preliminary findings suggest a greater difference between Taiwanese and American older adult decision behaviors relative to their younger counterparts [142]. Moreover, older adults accept more losing stakes than their younger adults in both Taiwanese and American samples. Thus, such work can dissociate cultural modulation of age effects on decision behaviors from universal effects of age on decision processes that are invariant across cultures.

Age and Cultural Differences in Discounting

At this juncture, it is interesting to consider probability discounting of future gains and losses as a singular and general cognitive mechanism that could explain the age and culture differences in decision‐making observed. Probability discounting refers to the observation that gains become subjectively less valuable as the probability of obtaining said gains is perceived to decrease [143]. This is also applicable to the loss domain such that losses become subjectively less aversive as the probability of losing said amount is perceived to decrease. In the LCT, participants choose between certain status quo and uncertain reward at the risk of loss. Thus, the absence of gain and loss for the certain option in the LCT is a true risk‐free neutral option that bears no psychological incentive other than pure certainty. Therefore, participants have to arbitrate between the probabilistic values associated with winning or losing points and the value of the status quo. Risky decision‐making occurs when the probability of winning in winning stakes or when the probability of losing in losing stakes is subjectively underweighted. In this framing, older adults tend to discount the probability of winning under gains and losing under losses more, leading to greater risk and loss aversion, respectively.

Consistent with older adults recruiting a wider network of brain regions (presumably reflecting a greater range of computations apart from basic veridical operations) to guide LCT behavior, it is likely in these additional operations that older adults apply additional discounting weights on probability information. Moreover, less discounting of long‐term probabilities is consistent with greater consideration of other information sources as part of the holistic bias in younger East Asians. This explains the findings in Lee et al. [39], with linear changes in probability tracked more closely in the vmPFC in Americans but not Taiwanese. This suggests that younger Americans are more guided by immediate probability compared to younger Taiwanese. From an age and culture perspective, cultural differences in probability discounting are hypothesized to be modulated by age, the extent of which might reflect how extended experience with analytic‐holistic biases, changes in life goals, and changes in neural sensitivity to reward information interact towards the final value‐based decision. Overall, it is an attractive notion to consider discounting as a unifying framework to explain both experiential and biological factors associated with culture and age differences in decision processing.

Recommendations: The Case for Integrating Aging Into Cultural Neuroscience Research

Although about a decade ago, we were still validating the presence of detectable neural correlates of cultural influences on the brain based on known behavioral differences, now, empirical findings support real neurocomputational differences circumscribing how individuals from different culture groups process information. The question is how robust these cultural differences are given the effects of biological declines as well as extended cultural experiences that come with aging. At least two studies so far [28, 38] suggest that rather than age reducing cultural differences, cultural differences in the brain in younger adults are at least maintained in their older counterparts, particularly for those with higher cognitive performance [30]. In this section, we take a broader perspective to discuss why the consideration of culture is important for cognitive neuroscience of aging and, in parallel, why the consideration of aging is important for cultural neuroscience.

Cultural Neuroscience: Importance of Studying Aging

The study of culture can potentially reveal changes in the trajectory of aging, which can suggest directions for interventions. The study of aging across cultures has the potential to reveal qualitative differences (e.g., strategy shifts or vulnerabilities to different types of errors) as well as differences in the rate of cognitive decline (e.g., exhibiting impairments in decision‐making or memory at an earlier time point or declining faster than individuals from a different culture). Extending research on cultural influences on social neuroscience to older adults is a particularly intriguing future direction, given the rich cross‐cultural research in young adults [144, 145] and the potential for distinct trajectories with age for social, compared to cognitive, processes [5]. Cultural neuroscience has the potential to reveal risk and protective factors for mental and neurological disorders [146, 147] that may lead to different trajectories of cognitive decline across cultures for both healthy and dysfunctional aging. Cultural differences can then be targeted in future work as interventions to alter the course of cognitive aging, even within a culture. For example, findings that Westerners have more detailed memories than Easterners, including with age, could lead to further research to identify which factors and strategies support more detailed memory. Further research might also consider whether other aspects of memory apart from level of detail might be important to target in aging. These factors, in turn, could be used to inform the development of interventions, such as practicing the use of strategies, even those that may be less familiar or rarely used by a particular culture, to enhance memory or the quality of information available or the manner of its presentation for the purposes of making a decision. Likewise, it is necessary to determine which interventions or factors do not offer the same benefits across cultures. For example, age and education markers known to influence aging in Western samples may operate differently in other cultures that historically differed in access to education, life expectancy, or health span.

To understand aging trajectories, it will be necessary to adopt a longitudinal approach. Longitudinal approaches are important because they allow for more conclusive interpretation of effects as reflecting aging by comparing change within an individual over time, separating out static cohort or other differences across samples from processes that change over time with age. Concerns about cohort differences between younger and older adults in one culture may be exaggerated when comparing across cultures, particularly as globalization and access to media could mean that there is more convergence across cultures for young adults who have more shared experiences than for older adults, who may have fewer shared influences than young adults and likely grew up in more dramatically different cultural milieux than today's young adults. Thus, assessing trajectories of change is important to isolate changes as due to the process of aging within the same individual, without potential cohort or other factors contributing to patterns of differences.

Longitudinal measures also can be used to identify steeper trajectories of decline over time, making them useful markers of dysfunctional aging processes, indicating processes such as mild cognitive impairment. Research has revealed that short‐term changes in behavior or brain structure [1, 148, 149, 150, 151, 152] over a period of a few years (e.g., less than 5 years) can be detected and meaningfully related to cognitive outcomes, including predictions of dementia risk. Longitudinal measures reveal higher rates of cortical thinning than cross‐sectional methods [149], as well as brain functional and associated behavioral determinants [1, 148, 150], indicating that longitudinal methods may be quite sensitive to detect individual differences. Such an approach has yet to be adopted in the study of the cognitive neuroscience of aging across cultures. Should different styles of decision‐making or patterns of memory errors indicate pathological aging processes across cultures, such findings would inform the identification of early markers of age‐related dysfunction and suggest ways in which cultural background may inform the interpretation of specific patterns of change. Such an approach is not only important for global health challenges but also has public health relevance for the diverse population within a nation because it is possible that trajectories of aging and dysfunctional change may vary across ethnic groups.

Understanding the variability in trajectories with aging, even for healthy older adults, provides a baseline to understand pathology in global samples. Some diseases with the highest global burden are related to advanced age, such as Alzheimer's disease, and others, such as depression, post‐traumatic stress disorder, and alcohol and drug‐use disorders, impact memory and decision‐making [153]. The global burden in terms of total health lost due to Alzheimer's disease alone is 4.4 million disability‐adjusted life years in high‐income countries and 6.8 million in low and middle‐income countries [153, 154]. To adopt a global perspective to understand aging, it is necessary to form strong collaborations that integrate the perspective of local researchers (see [40, 155]). Addressing the global burden of disorders requires building on a foundation of understanding how culture systematically shapes mental processes in healthy individuals, providing a baseline with which to compare dysfunctional processes. Furthermore, establishing which aging processes are more or less affected by culture will illuminate which behavioral or neural markers of dysfunction are robust and are expected to generalize across cultures. Although culture is beginning to be considered in diagnosis and treatment of psychiatric disorders [156, 157, 158], this perspective is rare in the study of normal and dysfunctional cognitive aging.

Cognitive Neuroscience of Aging: The Importance of Studying Culture

The overall psychological literature is unlikely to be representative, as it has largely stemmed from researchers in Western nations (e.g., the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, and Western Europe) studying predominantly Western populations. The relative homogeneity of participant samples has received attention with the recognition that 96% of samples are from Western industrialized nations, countries that constitute merely 12% of the world's population [159, 160]. These numbers were based on analyses of the top journals in six behavioral subdisciplines of psychology, but the problem also characterizes human neuroimaging with estimates that 90% of participants are Westerners [161, 162]. In cognitive journals, only 7% of articles considered culture [13]. Although research has become less American‐centric in recent years, the field is still dominated by English‐speaking Westerners [163].

So too has the study of aging been predominantly a Western enterprise, with little research systematically comparing effects of aging across cultures. To illustrate this, a recent large‐scale study using a worldwide multi‐site sample evaluated signatures of age‐related pathology but had no comparisons across ethnic groups and reported zero Asians in their sample when race was known [164]. Cognitive aging is largely assumed to be universal, rather than explicitly considering the influence of cultural practices, lifestyles, and core values. The few studies thus far investigating aging samples across cultures largely identify cross‐cultural differences in cognition [53, 86, 88, 89]. The dearth of cognitive neuroscience research on aging across cultures is striking, with only two functional [28, 38] and one structural [30] published studies.

Moreover, representative samples are necessary to have accurate models. In order to understand how cognition function, writ large in a society, models must account for the diversity of the population. By understanding potential variability across cultural groups, it will be possible to develop models tailored to distinct groups. In terms of aging and age‐related pathology, representative, diverse samples are necessary to inform understanding of which cognitive changes with age and predictors of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias are universal and which are modifiable through sociocultural practices. Furthermore, cultural comparisons are important for the accurate use of cognitive assessments and diagnosis of clinical disorders across cultures [146, 156, 157, 165, 166].

Challenges and Future Directions

In this piece, we have reviewed models and existing studies that explore how culture and age can intersect to shape cognition and neural function. The limited data thus far, particularly with cognitive neuroscience methods, are a major limitation. In this section, we discuss other key factors that can be examined with a growth of research on this topic.

As mentioned in Section 1, the definition of culture is a difficult question. In this review from a cognitive neuroscience perspective, we have adopted a practical approach of primarily comparing behavioral and brain responses between groups of individuals with East Asian and Western backgrounds, reflecting the preponderance of research thus far. This approach is limited in many ways, as it can be seen as binarizing the complex concept of culture and perhaps seen as falsely casting two of the vast array of existing cultures as extremes. In part, this reflects the challenges in identifying an underlying factor or factors that can be used to operationalize culture in the cognitive literature. Although the social literature has had some success using self‐report measures or other assessments of self‐construal to investigate culture as an individual difference, within or between groups [167], the cognitive literature has largely not found such effects (see Ref. [17] for a discussion).

Another challenge to the conceptualization and study of culture is the need to capture the globalized nature of today's society, with migration and cross‐cultural connections for work, education, or even vacation being frequent occurrences. Even for individuals who do not leave their country of origin, exchange of ideas is common, including through the influence of media and social media. In many societies, people have unrestricted access to the populations across the globe. Such exchanges can mean that cultural shifts and exchanges can occur rapidly, in contrast to some early frameworks for the study of culture and cognition, which emphasized historic trends over centuries [11]. Recent research is beginning to incorporate ideas about acculturation and migration across cultures, which capture some aspects of globalization [168]. A budding cognitive literature includes studies examining changes in the use of memory strategies [169] and brain activity as individuals adjust to a new culture over time [170, 171]. For example, Chinese individuals who endorse values more representative of those of their host American culture benefit more from a self‐referencing strategy in memory (a strategy in line with American values) as they acculturate to their new setting over time [169]. The cognitive research has yet to investigate effects across the adult lifespan.

Despite the relative ease of working across the globe, there is still a great need to study diverse populations, including in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Illness and disease, including Alzheimer's, place a great burden, and early diagnosis and treatments, as well as cognitive interventions and trainings may be particularly impactful in these societies. In terms of research, logistics to support collaborations, including access to cognitive neuroscience methods, may be a challenge in such settings. However, the proliferation of new methods that are robust and portable, including fNIRS (functional near‐infrared spectroscopy) and simplified EEG systems (e.g., a few electrodes on a headband or portable mobile systems), may enable cognitive neuroscience research in far‐flung locations.

In addition to critically considering how culture is studied, we can turn the same lens on the study of aging. As reviewed in this article, the existing cognitive aging research across cultures compared younger and older adults. Adopting a truly lifespan perspective, which incorporates childhood and adolescence, is needed in order to connect early with late life development [31]. Such an approach would also increase understanding of factors at all ages that impact cognitive aging and vulnerability to age‐related diseases. Broadening the developmental scope also allows for better understanding and isolation of cohort effects.

Beyond investigating different developmental time periods, it is also beneficial to expand the timescale of study, reflecting the fact that cultural shifts and learning trajectories sometimes unfold over very long time periods, perhaps more than one generation. To illustrate the potential of such research, a recent study investigated how skills, knot‐tying in this case, were transmitted across generations [172]. The increased prefrontal activity in the later generations of learners was thought to reflect the increased cognitive demands in response to impoverished learning as the fidelity of information was lost across generations. Similarly, studying changes in behavior and attitudes over time may be important to understand views of aging. Societies are aging in many parts of the globe, and these demographic trends could lead to shifts in the way cultures view their older representatives, in positive (e.g., as the ones who have memories and neural representations for the past) or negative (e.g., seeing an older population as a financial burden) ways.

Taken together, these ideas highlight the potential insights to be gleaned by adopting a dynamic perspective. Such an approach would support the study of cultural effects over time as information is transmitted amongst individuals, as people age over time, or as societal shifts occur.

Conclusion

In this review, we present the initial cognitive neuroscience research on cognition with age; the few studies largely explore memory and decision‐making. We discuss why the combination of these two topics could be revealing due to predicted differences in the effects of aging as well as pathological changes (e.g., Alzheimer's disease) with age. Despite the limited scope of research, the results thus far have proven helpful for refining frameworks and gaining new insights into how cognitive aging may unfold similarly and differently across cultures.

Going forward, there are many questions to be answered through leveraging the complex and dynamic nature of culture and the rich variation in cultures that exist across the globe. In addition to having basic science potential, in revealing what is culturally universal about aging, the study of cognitive neuroscience across culture has translational potential. By broadening the scope of who is studied as well as the lifestyle practices and information processing strategies used naturalistically by different cultures, we have the potential to broaden understanding of the factors that contribute to successful cognitive aging as well as age‐related decline and pathology.
