Reinvestigation of a paternity case with three CODIS core STR inconsistencies using MPS-based multi‑marker analysis
Authors: Nana Wang, Weihong Chen, Junchao Chen, Zhiyong Liu, Hongyu Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA routine paternity test using 23 autosomal short tandem repeats (A-STRs) revealed three Mendelian inconsistencies at D21S11, D13S317, and D7S820 between the alleged father and child, yielding a combined paternity index (CPI) below the confirmation threshold (CPI > 10⁴) stipulated in the Chinese technical specifications. Extended capillary electrophoresis typing of 48 A-STRs and 17 Y-STRs eliminated further mismatches and raised the CPI above the standard. Subsequent massively parallel sequencing (MPS) of 54 A-STRs, 27 X-STRs, 48 Y-STRs, 132 identity-informative single nucleotide polymorphisms (iiSNPs), and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) provided both length- and sequence-based genotypes. The CPI reached 2.78 × 10²¹ (A-STRs) and 6.28 × 10¹⁷ (iiSNPs), strongly supporting the paternity. All other marker systems were consistent with the alleged relationships. The three observed incompatibilities were attributed to single-step paternal mutations. This case highlights the risk of misinterpreting multi-locus STR mismatches and demonstrates the value of MPS-based genotyping in resolving complex paternity evaluations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53806-8.Keywords: Parentage testing, Short tandem repeat (STR), Mutation, Massively parallel sequencing (MPS)Subject terms: Genetics, Medical research

Introduction

Short tandem repeats (STRs), including autosomal, X-chromosomal, and Y-chromosomal markers, are extensively used in forensic genetics for paternity testing and individual identification because of their high degree of polymorphism. Mutations can occur in all of these marker systems and may lead to apparent genetic inconsistencies in kinship analysis. In routine trio-based paternity testing, autosomal STRs (A-STRs) remain the primary marker system, and their locus-specific mutation rates are generally in the range of 10⁻⁴–10⁻³1–4. Although mutations at one or two STR loci are frequently observed in routine casework5–8, simultaneous mutations at three loci, particularly among the 13 core loci of the Combined DNA Index System (CODIS), are rare9,10. Such mutations at several loci may lead to the erroneous exclusion of true parentage, posing a significant challenge for paternity testing and often necessitating the introduction of additional genetic markers to resolve these inconsistencies. Typically, supplementary A-STRs, X-STRs, and Y-STRs are analyzed using capillary electrophoresis (CE) platforms10,11. In contrast, alternative markers such as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and mtDNA remain underutilized in forensic genetic laboratories.

In routine paternity testing, STR mutations are critical for evaluating and interpreting genetic inconsistencies. Current guidelines and national technical specifications (for example, ISFG recommendations) generally adopt likelihood-ratio–based criteria, in which a CPI ≥ 10⁴ is regarded as strong evidence supporting paternity, whereas a CPI ≤ 10⁻⁴ supports exclusion12,13. When one or two STR loci deviate from Mendelian inheritance, mutations can often be accommodated using appropriate mutation models and by increasing the number of loci tested. In contrast, simultaneous inconsistencies at three or more loci, especially within CODIS core markers, are exceedingly rare and are frequently treated as grounds for non-paternity according to some recommendations (the so-called “three-mutation rule”)14. Nevertheless, the rigid application of this rule may lead to the false exclusion of true parentage in rare yet biologically plausible multi-locus mutation scenarios.

To improve the robustness of identification, additional genetic markers and extended typing strategies have been employed. Routine resolution strategies involve the analysis of supplementary A-STRs, X-STRs, and Y-STRs on CE platforms to detect inconsistencies15–17. Beyond STRs, SNPs and mtDNA serve as valuable complementary markers18–22. SNPs offer the advantage of extremely low mutation rates (10− 8). The MPS enables the simultaneous interrogation of STRs, SNPs, and mtDNA within a single assay, offering both length- and sequence-level resolution23,24. To date, to the best of our knowledge, few reports have described the use of a combined A-STR and SNP panel for paternity testing on an MPS platform25, highlighting the substantial but still underexploited potential of advanced sequencing technologies for forensic applications.

In this study, we report a rare paternity case in which routine CE-based analysis of 23 A-STRs revealed apparent Mendelian inconsistencies at three CODIS core loci (D21S11, D13S317, and D7S820) between the alleged father (AF) and the child (C). Under conventional interpretation schemes, such a pattern is typically regarded as non-paternity. By progressively extending STR typing on the CE platform and subsequently applying an MPS-based all-in-one multiplex system that simultaneously targets A-STRs, X-STRs, Y-STRs, SNPs, and mtDNA, we resolved these inconsistencies and obtained strong probabilistic support for the paternity hypothesis. This case provides empirical insight into the mutation mechanisms underlying multi-locus STR inconsistencies and offers practical guidance for paternity testing in the future.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and STR genotyping

Peripheral blood samples were collected from the AF, alleged mother (AM), and child (C) on FTA cards. The research project and all experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. 008 [2016]), and all methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All samples were collected after obtaining informed consent. 1.2 mm diameter FTA cards were punched directly as an input template without DNA extraction. STR loci were amplified using the Goldeneye® DNA ID System 25 A Kit (Goldeneye Technology, Ltd., Beijing, China), Microreader™ 23sp ID System (Microread Genetics, Suzhou, China), Investigator® HDplex Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), and AmpFlSTR® Yfiler™ PCR Amplification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The amplified products were then separated and detected on an ABI 3500xL Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the electrophoresis data were analyzed using GeneMapper® ID Software v3.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

DNA genotyping based on MPS

One punched FTA card (1.2 mm×1.2 mm) was used as the input template for the AF, AM, and C. DNA libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy Signature Identification Library Prep Kit (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China), as recommended by the manufacturer26. Subsequently, 500 ng of the libraries were pooled for denaturation and single-chain cyclization to generate a mass of DNBs. Finally, the 8 ng digestion products were sequenced using the MGISEQ-2000RS (MGI Tech) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Data analysis

FASTQ files were processed using STRait Razor 3.0 software27. Genotyping reliability was evaluated based on the depth of coverage (DoC) and allele coverage ratio (ACR). The minimum DoC threshold was set at 100× for all STR loci except SE33 (≥ 50×), and the ACR threshold was set at 0.5 for all loci except SE33 (≥ 0.3) to ensure robust heterozygote detection and reduce the risk of allelic dropout. Loci or alleles that did not meet these thresholds were manually inspected or excluded. Allele nomenclature was standardized using an in-house VBA workbook, and the paternity index (PI) and CPI were calculated according to the recommendations of the International Society for Forensic Genetics (ISFG)28 and the revised Forensic STR Sequence Guide v529.

Results

Genotyping based on CE

The genotyping results and PI values for the AF, AM, and C are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Analysis of the three CODIS core loci (D21S11, D13S317, and D7S820) revealed Mendelian inheritance inconsistencies among 23 A-STRs between the AF and the C. The CPI values for the different trio combinations (AF–C, AM–C, and AF–AM–C) are shown in Supplementary Table 1. The results showed that, except for AM-C (CPI = 2.42 × 106), the CPI values of AF-C (CPI = 77.15) and the alleged parent-child (CPI = 7.53 × 103) did not meet the paternity testing standard (> 104), but the relationship could not be completely excluded (< 10− 4). To enhance the resolution, the total number of A-STR loci was increased to 48 using additional kits. No additional loci that exhibited Mendelian inconsistencies were detected. As summarized in Table 1, all recalculated CPI values met the identification criteria (CPI > 10⁴). Moreover, the genotypes of the 17 Y-STRs showed full consistency with paternal lineage inheritance (Supplementary Table 2), further supporting a shared patrilineal relationship.

Genotyping based on MPS

The MPS-based genotyping and sequencing depth are presented in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4, respectively. For 54 A-STRs, the lowest sequencing depths at the SE33 locus were 373× (AF), 150× (AM), and 161× (C). For the 27 X-STRs, the DXS10148 locus showed minimum depths of 821×, 209×, and 130× for the AF, AM, and C, respectively. For the 48 Y-STRs, the DYS389II locus had a minimum sequencing depth of 108× for the alleged father and 120× for the C. Sequencing depths for 132 iiSNPs, 53 aiSNPs, and 29 piSNPs exceeded 100× for all individuals, indicating high-quality sequencing data. The length-based genotypes from CE and MPS were consistent with overlapping A-STRs and Y-STRs (Supplementary Table 5). Meanwhile, the MPS-based genotypes showed that, except for three CODIS core loci (D7S820, D13S317, and D21S11), the remaining 51 A-STRs conformed to Mendelian inheritance. The CPI values for the alleged parents–child trio, calculated using 54 A-STRs and 132 iiSNPs, are presented in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 6. The CPI for the 54 A-STRs was 2.78 × 10²¹ with W = 0.99999999 (Table 1), while the CPI for the 132 iiSNPs was 6.28 × 10¹⁷ with W = 0.999999 (Supplementary Table 6). No mismatches were observed in the 27 X-STRs, 48 Y-STRs, 53 aiSNPs, or 29 piSNPs between the C and the alleged parents (Supplementary Table 3). Therefore, MPS-based results corroborated CE-based STR analyses, indicating that the genetic incompatibilities at D7S820, D13S317, and D21S11 were consistent with mutational events rather than true exclusions.

The sequence structures of the alleles at the three mutation loci are shown in Table 2, which were beneficial for inferring the source of the mutation. For D13S317, one allele of the C, “[TATC]7 AATC” (CE: 8), was identical to the corresponding maternal allele “[TATC]7 AATC” (CE: 8). The other allele of C, “[TATC]11” (CE: 11), differed from the two paternal alleles “[TATC]12” (CE: 12) and “[TATC]13” (CE: 13). According to the stepwise mutation model30,31, one allele of the C was inherited from the AM, whereas the other paternal allele is most parsimoniously explained by a one-repeat contraction from the AF allele “[TATC]12” (CE: 12) to “[TATC]11” (CE: 11), although multistep mutation cannot be completely excluded from consideration. Similarly, for D7S820, one allele of the C, “[TATC]12” (CE: 12), was identical to the maternal allele and was therefore assigned to the AM. The other allele, “[TATC]13” (CE: 13), is most likely of paternal origin. Given that the AF carried “[TATC]11” and “[TATC]14”, both a one-step contraction from “[TATC]14” and a two-step expansion from “[TATC]11” are theoretically possible; however, under the stepwise mutation model, the C’s paternal allele is most consistent with a one-step contraction from the AF allele “[TATC]14” (CE: 14). Compared with the other two mutation loci, the repeat unit of D21S11 was more complicated. The length-based CE genotype, the AF, AM, and C had the same allele 29; however, sequence analysis showed that the AF and AM had different sequences of allele 29. The repeat structure of the AF was “[TCTA]4 [TCTG]6 [TCTA]3 ta [TCTA]3 tca [TCTA]2 tccata [TCTA]11” (CE: 29). In contrast, the AM appeared homozygous for allele 29 based on CE typing, but sequence-based genotyping identified two different isoalleles (Table 2), indicating a heterozygous genotype with identical lengths but divergent repeat structures. One allele of the C, “[TCTA]6 [TCTG]5 [TCTA]3 ta [TCTA]3 tca [TCTA]2 tccata TCTA TCTT [TCTA]8” (CE: 29), was identical to one of the AM isoalleles and was therefore assigned to the AM. The other allele, “[TCTA]5 [TCTG]6 [TCTA]3 ta [TCTA]3 tca [TCTA]2 tccata [TCTA]12” (CE: 31), was inferred to be of paternal origin. Under the stepwise mutation model, this allele is most parsimoniously explained by a one-repeat contraction from the AF allele “[TCTA]5 [TCTG]6 [TCTA]3 ta [TCTA]3 tca [TCTA]2 tccata [TCTA]13” (CE: 32), although a multistep mutation cannot be completely excluded. Overall, the mutations observed at the three loci were inferred to have originated from the AF and were most consistent with one-step mutational events.

In addition to the nuclear genome markers, mtDNA haplotypes of the AM and C are presented in Supplementary Table 7. The AM and C shared identical mtDNA sequence profiles and were both assigned to haplogroup H2a2a, indicating a shared maternal lineage. Combined with the previous analyses of nuclear genetic markers, the mtDNA analysis further supported the parentage relationship between the AM and C.

Discussion

The mutation mechanism of STR is mainly caused by the strand-slippage replication model, with an average mutation rate of approximately 2 × 10[− 3 [32,33. It has been reported that more than 90% of STR mutations are one-step mutations, whereas two-step mutations constitute less than 10%, and multi-step mutations have been rarely reported, as consistently documented across multiple large-scale studies34–36. In paternity testing, STR mutations are a significant consideration and should be interpreted cautiously. When one or two loci exhibit Mendelian inconsistencies, the conventional approach is to incorporate additional STR loci for further evaluation12. However, reliance on a simple count-based exclusion rule may be problematic, because biologically plausible STR mutations can give rise to multiple Mendelian inconsistencies without excluding true paternity14. However, an analysis of 50,000 paternity cases in our laboratory revealed that all instances of paternity exclusion involved more than three loci showing Mendelian inconsistencies, with no exclusions based solely on three non-conforming loci. Therefore, excluding paternity based solely on the number of mutated loci is unreliable and should be approached cautiously.

In this case, three CODIS core loci (D13S317, D21S11, and D7S820) showed Mendelian inconsistencies between the alleged father and C. The PI calculated from the 23 A-STRs was inconsistent with the biological relationship, with a CPI of 77.15 for the AF-C pair and 7.53 × 10³ for the trio, both below the standard threshold of 10,000. As additional A-STR loci were typed on the CE platform, the CPI values for the various relationship combinations increased, and no further loci were found to violate the Mendelian inheritance. To improve the resolution and investigate potential sequence-based explanations for the observed discrepancies, MPS was subsequently performed using a panel that allowed the simultaneous detection of length- and sequence-based variations.

Compared with CE, MPS provides access to fragment-length and sequence-level polymorphisms37–39, which are particularly valuable in complex forensic cases. In the present case, although three loci exhibited apparent genetic incompatibilities that were ultimately attributed to paternal mutations, the combined evidence from extended STR typing and MPS-based analysis provided strong statistical support for paternity by indicating that the mismatched alleles were consistent with mutation events rather than true exclusions. A particularly notable finding in this case was the identification of isoalleles at the D21S11 locus in the AM. CE-based analysis classified the AM as homozygous (allele 29, 29); however, MPS-based sequencing revealed two structurally distinct alleles of identical length, demonstrating true heterozygosity that was undetectable by conventional length-based typing methods. This sequence-level resolution was critical for accurate allele tracing at the mutation locus; it allowed the maternally inherited allele in the C to be precisely identified, thereby confirming that the C’s allele 31 originated from the alleged father via a one-step reduction from allele 32. This finding further illustrates the added interpretive value of MPS in complex parentage cases, particularly when isoalleles or cryptic heterozygosity are present. Furthermore, the concurrent analysis of 53 aiSNPs and 29 piSNPs, although not the primary focus of this study, provided additional genetic corroboration. The absence of any discordance in these markers further supports the biological relationship between the C and the alleged parents, consistent with their expected Mendelian transmission patterns.

According to the 2001 guidelines of the German Association of Expert Witnesses for Paternity Testing, paternity may be conclusively excluded only when at least 12 mutually independent genetic markers distributed across a minimum of 10 different chromosomes are analyzed and three or more Mendelian inconsistencies are observed14. In the present case, three CODIS loci violated Mendelian inheritance, and all mutations were of paternal origin. According to this “three-mutation rule,” the case could have been interpreted as a non-paternity case. However, after increasing the number of STR loci analyzed on the CE platform and further incorporating the MPS method, both the CPI and W strongly supported paternity. Collectively, this case shows that a simple count-based “three-mutation rule” can lead to false exclusion when multiple biologically plausible one-step STR mutations occur. Therefore, observing three Mendelian inconsistencies (even at CODIS core loci) should trigger expanded STR typing and/or the incorporation of additional marker systems (e.g., SNPs or MPS-based sequencing), rather than being considered definitive evidence of non-paternity.

Conclusion

In this paternity case, mutations at three CODIS loci were observed in the initial panel of 23 A-STRs. However, the paternity between the AF and the C was ultimately supported after additional A-STR loci were analyzed using extended CE-based kits. Because the occurrence of simultaneous mutations at three loci is rare, paternity should not be confirmed or excluded solely based on a limited number of loci. To improve the robustness of the evaluation, both length- and sequence-based genotypes were obtained using the MPS platform. The MPS results were concordant with the CE profiles, supporting the biological relationship between the AF and the C and demonstrating that the mismatches reflected mutation events rather than true exclusions. These results illustrate that MPS can enhance the resolution and interpretive power in complex forensic paternity cases by providing both length- and sequence-level information across multiple genetic marker systems.
