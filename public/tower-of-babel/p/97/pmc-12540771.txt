Iron overload in CD11c+ myeloid cells exacerbates acetaminophen hepatotoxicity
Authors: Saisai Liu, Yohei Kanamori, Yudai Ohta, Shuran Li, Yanliang Liu, Hao Li, Mohamed Fathi Saleh, Akihiro Nita, Keiichi I Nakayama, Toshiro Moroishi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute liver injury often progresses to liver failure, with immune responses playing a critical role in regulating the inflammatory process. In this study, we aim to investigate the effects of iron on CD11c+ myeloid cells during acetaminophen-induced liver injury. Iron overload caused by F-box and leucine-rich repeat protein 5 (FBXL5) deficiency in CD11c+ cells exacerbates liver damage. CD11c+ myeloid cell-specific FBXL5-deficient mice exhibit higher serum transaminase levels, liver injury, and mortality than the controls. These phenotypes are associated with enhanced neutrophil infiltration and expression of interleukin (IL)-6, a pro-inflammatory cytokine. Mechanistically, iron overload in FBXL5-deficient cells increases IL-6 production by facilitating the recruitment of nuclear factor-κB to the Il-6 promoter. In vivo, IL-6 neutralization mitigates liver injury, confirming its role in disease progression. Our findings highlight the role of iron in immune responses and suggest that targeting iron may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for liver injury. Subject terms: Cell death, Interleukins

Introduction

Acute liver injury is caused by various factors, including drug toxicity, viral infections, environmental toxins, and autoimmune diseases. It is characterized by an initial inflammatory phase during which the immune cells infiltrate the liver to clear the pathogens and dead hepatocytes and promote tissue repair. However, in severe cases, this inflammatory response is dysregulated, impairing liver regeneration and leading to acute liver failure, a condition that often necessitates liver transplantation. Therefore, resolution of inflammation and restoration of liver function are necessary for good patient outcomes in acute liver injury1,2.

Immune response to acute liver injury is dynamic, involving the activation of several immune cells, including neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), and T cells. These cells are involved in both the inflammatory and resolutive phases of injury. In acetaminophen (APAP)-induced liver injury, neutrophils initially drive inflammation3 but later perform reparative roles as the injury resolves4. Natural killer T cells protect against liver injury, and natural killer T cell-deficient mice exhibit high susceptibility to APAP-induced liver damage5. γδ T cells play crucial roles in liver injury, and their depletion attenuates liver damage6, further highlighting the importance of specific immune cell subsets in the initiation and resolution of acute liver injury. In addition to these immune populations, CD11c+ myeloid cells, including both conventional DCs and subsets of macrophages such as Kupffer cells and monocyte-derived macrophages, modulate inflammatory responses during acute liver injury. Depletion of CD11c+ cells exacerbates APAP-induced liver injury7, and CD11c+ cells suppress hepatic ischemia–reperfusion injury8. Specifically, conventional type I DCs within the CD11c+ population protect against acute liver injury by producing interleukin (IL)-129. These reports suggest that CD11c+ myeloid cells protect against acute liver injury. However, the specific factors reprogramming intracellular signaling and phenotypic state of CD11c+ cells during acute liver injury remain ambiguous.

Metabolic reprogramming in response to microenvironmental cues plays a significant role in the phenotypic switching of immune cells and modulation of immune responses. Functional activity of CD11c+ cells is closely associated with cell metabolism10. Under resting conditions, conventional DCs primarily rely on fatty acid oxidation for oxidative phosphorylation. Inflammatory stimuli upregulate the expression levels of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase, which converts tryptophan to immunosuppressive kynurenine metabolites, thereby suppressing pro-inflammatory responses and promoting regulatory T cell proliferation11. Recent studies have also underscored the importance of iron metabolism in regulating immune cell function. During wound healing, iron accumulation inhibits macrophage transition to the M2 restorative phenotype, thereby impairing tissue repair12,13. Iron also epigenetically regulates the expression of the transcription factor, B-cell lymphoma 6 (BCL6), in T follicular helper cells, contributing to the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus14. However, the effects of iron metabolism on the functional roles of CD11c+ cells, particularly in acute liver inflammation, remain unclear.

Cellular iron homeostasis is primarily regulated by the iron-sensing ubiquitin ligase, F-box and leucine-rich repeat protein 5 (FBXL5), and RNA-binding protein, iron regulatory protein 2 (IRP2, also known as IREB2). IRP2 post-transcriptionally upregulates the expression levels of proteins involved in iron uptake and downregulates those of proteins involved in iron export, thereby increasing the cellular ferrous iron (labile iron) levels. FBXL5, a substrate recognition component of the SCF E3 ligase complex, recruits IRP2 for ubiquitination and degradation. As FBXL5 binds to iron and remains stable in its iron-bound form, its iron-sensing activity negatively regulates cellular iron levels by promoting IRP2 degradation15,16. FBXL5 deficiency results in the stabilization of IRP2 independent of cellular iron concentrations, causing cellular iron overload. FBXL5-null mice exhibit early embryonic lethality due to iron-induced oxidative stress, which can be prevented by the additional knockout (KO) of IRP217. Previous studies performed conditional FBXL5 deletion in specific cell types and demonstrated that FBXL5 deficiency increases the cellular iron levels, primarily due to the abnormal accumulation of IRP2 in vivo18–20.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the roles of iron in CD11c+ cells and examine its effects on immune responses during acute liver injury. FBXL5 deficiency in CD11c+ cells exacerbated acute liver injury progression. Moreover, iron promoted IL-6 levels in CD11c+ cells, thereby increasing the hepatic infiltration of neutrophils and promoting the inflammatory responses.

Results

Iron overload in CD11c+ myeloid cells exacerbates APAP-induced liver damage

To explore the roles of iron in CD11c+ myeloid cells during liver inflammation, we crossed Fbxl5F/F mice harboring floxed Fbxl5 alleles with Cd11c (also known as Itgax)-Cre transgenic mice (expressing Cre recombinase under the regulation of the Cd11c promoter). To verify FBXL5 deletion and its subsequent effect on cellular iron levels in CD11c+ myeloid cells, we isolated CD11c+ cells from the spleens of Cd11c-Cre/Fbxl5F/F (hereafter referred to as FBXL5ΔCD11c) and Fbxl5F/F (control) mice. Splenic CD11c+ myeloid cells from FBXL5ΔCD11c mice exhibited lower Fbxl5 mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 1A) and elevated ferrous iron levels (Supplementary Fig. 1B) compared to controls, confirming that FBXL5 depletion induces iron overload in CD11c+ myeloid cells. We next used FBXL5ΔCD11c and control mice to establish an APAP-induced acute liver injury model, characterized by hepatocellular injury and inflammation21 (Fig. 1a). To assess the effects of FBXL5 deficiency in CD11c+ myeloid cells on APAP-induced liver injury progression, we performed a time-course analysis of the serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels. ALT levels were elevated within 6 h of APAP administration (300 mg/kg) and remained elevated at 24 h. Compared to the control mice, FBXL5ΔCD11c mice showed a two-fold increase in ALT levels 6 h post-APAP injection (Fig. 1b). Histological analysis via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining confirmed the marked expansion of liver damaged area in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice compared to that in control mice after APAP treatment (Fig. 1c). Additionally, we evaluated the survival of these mice over 120-h after APAP treatment (Fig. 1d). While most control Fbxl5F/F mice survived, over half of FBXL5ΔCD11c mice died within 48 h when challenged with a higher dose of APAP (500 mg/kg) (Fig. 1e). Notably, Cd11c-Cre/Fbxl5+/+ exhibited a survival rate comparable to that of Fbxl5F/F control mice, suggesting that the severe phenotype observed in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice is due to FBXL5 depletion rather than a side effect of Cre recombinase expression. Taken together, these observations suggest that FBXL5 deficiency in CD11c+ myeloid cells exacerbates APAP-induced liver injury.

FBXL5 deficiency in CD11c+ myeloid cells promotes neutrophil infiltration during APAP-induced liver injury

We next investigated how FBXL5 deficiency in CD11c+ myeloid cells exacerbates APAP-induced liver injury. As a high dose of APAP (500 mg/kg) resulted in mortality in more than half of FBXL5ΔCD11c mice (Fig. 1e), subsequent experiments were conducted using a lower dose (300 mg/kg). Upon APAP treatment, the toxic APAP metabolite N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI) depletes glutathione, leading to lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic hepatocyte death21. Consistent with this, the lipid peroxidation marker 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) accumulated in both control (Fbxl5F/F) and FBXL5ΔCD11c mice after APAP treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Given that ferroptosis sensitivity increases with iron overload in hepatocytes, we assessed hepatic iron levels in control and FBXL5ΔCD11c mice, with or without APAP. Hepatic iron levels were comparable between the two genotypes and decreased following APAP treatment in both groups (Supplementary Fig. 2B), likely reflecting hepatocyte loss. These observations suggest that FBXL5 deficiency in CD11c+ myeloid cells does not alter total hepatic iron content or enhance hepatocellular ferroptosis induction under APAP challenge. We also assessed hepatocyte regeneration by quantifying Ki67+ proliferative cells 24 h post-APAP treatment. Although FBXL5ΔCD11c mice showed a trend toward reduced proliferating cells, the difference was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Together, these results suggest that the worsened liver injury observed in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice is not attributable to increased hepatocellular ferroptosis but is instead likely due to elevated ferrous iron levels within CD11c+ myeloid cells.

Inflammatory mediators released from damaged hepatocytes attract immune cells and trigger pro-inflammatory responses, further exacerbating liver injury and inflammation22. To investigate the immune response in this context, we examined CD45+ immune cell infiltration in liver sections from control and FBXL5ΔCD11c mice before and after APAP treatment. APAP induced CD45+ cell infiltration in both groups, with no significant difference between genotypes (Fig. 2a). As innate immune cells—including neutrophils, infiltrating macrophages, liver-resident macrophages (Kupffer cells), and DCs—are key mediators of acute liver injury23,24, we analyzed their populations by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 3A). The proportions of infiltrating macrophages (CD45+Ly6G−CD11bhighF4/80low), Kupffer cells (CD45+Ly6G−CD11blowF4/80high), and DCs (CD45+Ly6G−F4/80−CD11c+) were comparable between control and FBXL5ΔCD11c mice, regardless of APAP treatment (Fig. 2b). Notably, more than 95% of Kupffer cells expressed TIM4, a marker of embryonically-derived Kupffer cells25, indicating that repopulation by monocyte-derived macrophages (which are typically TIM4−) was minimal under these conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3B). In contrast, while neutrophil numbers were similar between groups before APAP treatment, neutrophil infiltration was significantly increased in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice compared to controls after APAP treatment (Fig. 2b). This enhanced neutrophil recruitment was further confirmed by Ly6G immunostaining (Fig. 2c). These results suggest that FBXL5 deficiency in CD11c+ myeloid cells promotes neutrophil infiltration and contributes to the progression of APAP-induced liver injury.

Gene expression profiling revealed pro-inflammatory responses in the liver of FBXL5ΔCD11c mice

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the exacerbation of liver inflammation in APAP-treated FBXL5ΔCD11c mice, we performed RNA sequencing of whole liver of the control and FBXL5ΔCD11c mice with or without APAP treatment for 6 h. Principal component analysis (Fig. 3a) and unbiased hierarchical clustering (Fig. 3b) revealed marked alterations in the liver transcriptomes of the control and FBXL5ΔCD11c mice following APAP treatment. As shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 3c), RNA sequencing identified 785 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with the levels of 296 genes upregulated (Supplementary Data 1) and 489 genes downregulated (Supplementary Data 2) in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice compared to those in control mice after APAP treatment. Pathway analysis indicated activation of multiple pro-inflammatory signaling pathways in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Notably, levels of several genes associated with inflammatory responses, including Il-6, were significantly upregulated in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice following APAP treatment (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 4B). Consistently, gene set enrichment analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of the IL-6 signaling pathway in APAP-treated FBXL5ΔCD11c livers (Fig. 3e). In line with elevated Il-6 mRNA expression, FBXL5∆CD11c mice exhibited higher serum IL-6 levels compared to control mice (Fig. 3f). As IL-6 is a potent neutrophil chemoattractant26–28, its elevated expression possibly contributed to enhanced neutrophil infiltration in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice. Collectively, these results suggest that IL-6-mediated inflammation exacerbates the liver injury in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice following APAP treatment.

IL-6 exacerbates APAP-induced liver injury in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice

To investigate the role of IL-6 in liver injury progression in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice, we administered an IL-6–neutralizing antibody prior to APAP challenge (Fig. 4a). Treatment with an isotype control antibody did not alter the phenotype of FBXL5ΔCD11c mice, which exhibited increased APAP-induced liver injury compared to control mice. However, IL-6-neutralizing antibody markedly reduced serum ALT levels in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice (Fig. 4a). Consistently, histological analysis with H&E staining revealed a reduced area of liver damage in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice treated with the IL-6-neutralizing antibody compared to those receiving the isotype control (Fig. 4b), with the extent of damage correlating with serum ALT levels (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis revealed a decrease in Ly6G+ neutrophils in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice pretreated with the IL-6-neutralizing antibody compared to the control group (Fig. 4d), indicating that elevated IL-6 expression contributes to enhanced neutrophil infiltration in these mice. Collectively, these results suggest that iron overload in CD11c+ myeloid cells exacerbates APAP-induced liver injury by promoting IL-6 production and subsequent neutrophil recruitment.

To determine whether FBXL5 deficiency directly affects IL-6 production in CD11c+ myeloid cells, we isolated CD11c+ cells from the livers of control and FBXL5ΔCD11c mice following APAP treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Hepatic CD11c+ cells from FBXL5ΔCD11c mice exhibited significantly higher Il-6 mRNA levels compared to those from control mice (Fig. 4e). In contrast, Il-6 mRNA levels in CD11c− cells did not differ significantly between the two genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 5B). These observations suggest that FBXL5 deficiency in CD11c+ myeloid cells increases IL-6 levels in these cells, thereby exacerbating liver injury in FBXL5ΔCD11c mice following APAP treatment.

Loss of FBXL5 promotes IL-6 levels in DCs

To explore the molecular mechanisms by which FBXL5 deficiency promotes IL-6 levels in CD11c+ myeloid cells, we conducted in vitro experiments by establishing an immortalized murine DC line, DC2.4, with FBXL5 deletion. FBXL5 depletion in DC2.4 cells was achieved using a lentiviral CRISPR-Cas9 system followed by puromycin selection. The resulting puromycin-resistant cells were used as a pooled population without single-cell cloning, and thus likely contained a small fraction of FBXL5-intact cells. Sufficient FBXL5 loss was confirmed via DNA sequencing (Fig. 5a) and immunoblotting analysis (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Figs. 6A and 7A). To assess the functional loss of FBXL5, we examined the response of the FBXL5–IRP2 axis to changes in cellular iron levels induced by ferric ammonium citrate (FAC) or the iron chelator, desferrioxamine (DFO), in wild-type (WT) and pooled FBXL5 KO cells. In WT cells, iron elevated the FBXL5 levels but decreased the IRP2 levels. In contrast, IRP2 was accumulated, and levels of its downstream target, ferritin, were downregulated in FBXL5 KO cells (Fig. 5b). These results confirmed the functional loss of FBXL5 in FBXL5 KO DC2.4 cells. To model the inflammatory conditions associated with liver injury, WT and FBXL5 KO cells were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a ligand for Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), to induce IL-6 expression in DC2.4 cells in vitro. Notably, Il-6 mRNA levels were significantly higher in FBXL5 KO cells than in WT cells within 6 h of LPS stimulation (Fig. 5C). In contrast, iron chelator 2,2’-bipyridine (BPY) almost completely suppressed the upregulation of Il-6 mRNA levels in FBXL5 KO cells (Fig. 5d). Therefore, iron overload increased IL-6 expression following TLR4 activation in FBXL5 KO cells. Overall, these results suggest that iron enhances IL-6 expression levels in DCs upon inflammatory stimulation.

FBXL5 deficiency promotes IL-6 levels by enhancing the recruitment of nuclear factor (NF)-κB to the Il-6 promoter

Next, we elucidated the effect of FBXL5 depletion on IL-6 induction downstream of DC activation. Upon TLR4 stimulation, IκB kinase (IKK; also known as IKBK) is phosphorylated and activated, leading to the phosphorylation and subsequent degradation of IκBα (also known as NFKBIA). This degradation releases NF-κB from the IκB complex, facilitating its translocation to the nucleus, where it binds to the promoter region of target genes, including Il-6, to activate their transcription29. To examine the effect of FBXL5 depletion on the NF-κB signaling pathway, we compared the time-course changes in the phosphorylation status and abundance of IKK and IκBα upon LPS stimulation in WT and FBXL5 KO DC2.4 cells. Immunoblotting analysis revealed no apparent differences in IκB phosphorylation and degradation between the WT and FBXL5 KO cells after LPS stimulation (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Figs. 8A and 9A). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining revealed comparable nuclear translocation of NF-κB in both WT and FBXL5 KO cells following LPS treatment (Fig. 6b). These data suggest that FBXL5 depletion does not affect the induction of NF-κB nuclear translocation following IKK-mediated IκB degradation upon TLR4 stimulation in DCs. We further investigated the effect of FBXL5 depletion downstream of NF-κB nuclear translocation. To assess the interaction of NF-κB with the Il-6 promoter region, we performed a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay. Without LPS stimulation, no difference was observed in the amount of NF-κB bound to the Il-6 promoter between the WT and FBXL5 KO cells. However, LPS stimulation significantly enhanced the recruitment of NF-κB to the Il-6 promoter in FBXL5 KO cells compared to that in WT cells (Fig. 6c). These findings suggest that iron facilitates IL-6 expression by enhancing the recruitment of NF-κB to the Il-6 promoter in DCs.

Discussion

Acute liver injury, a common clinical condition caused by various factors, often progresses to acute liver failure. Therefore, resolution of hepatic inflammation and restoration of tissue homeostasis are crucial for good clinical outcomes in patients with liver injury. In this study, we demonstrated that iron overload in CD11c+ myeloid cells exacerbated APAP-induced liver damage. Our findings highlight the vital roles of the cellular properties of CD11c+ myeloid cells, particularly iron metabolism, in the modulation of immune responses during acute liver injury.

CD11c+ myeloid cells play complex roles in liver inflammation. These cells, including conventional DCs and macrophages, are essential for the initiation and resolution of inflammation. Previous studies have highlighted the dynamic nature of immune cell activation during liver injury, with neutrophils and macrophages typically serving as early responders to tissue damage and DCs participating in both inflammatory responses and subsequent tissue repair processes. In our study, iron overload resulting from FBXL5 deficiency in CD11c+ myeloid cells drove these cells toward a more pro-inflammatory phenotype. Specifically, this was marked by increased IL-6 production, heightened neutrophil infiltration, and exacerbated liver injury, indicating that iron dysregulation in CD11c+ myeloid cells plays a pivotal role in the progression of acute liver injury. However, a limitation of our study is the lack of detailed characterization of the specific CD11c+ myeloid cell subsets involved in this process. Since CD11c expression can vary under inflammatory conditions and is expressed in certain macrophage subsets beyond conventional DCs, further investigation is needed to better understand the heterogeneity of CD11c+ cells in vivo. Future studies utilizing lineage tracing approaches—such as Cd11c-Cre/tdTomato reporter mice—combined with single-cell technologies (e.g., single-cell RNA sequencing) will be valuable for identifying the specific cell populations responsible for promoting liver injury under conditions of iron overload.

Recently, several studies have investigated the involvement of iron metabolism in the regulation of immune responses. Iron, an essential element for cellular respiration and DNA synthesis, functions as a key modulator of immune cell activity30. However, iron overload skews the immune responses toward chronic inflammation and impairs inflammation resolution, thereby causing tissue damage. Here, FBXL5 deficiency in CD11c+ myeloid cells caused iron accumulation, which enhanced the expression of IL-6, a pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in neutrophil recruitment and exacerbation of liver injury. These results are consistent with previous reports that dysregulated iron homeostasis drives inflammatory responses in various conditions, including autoimmune disorders31 and cancer32.

A notable aspect of our study was the identification of IL-6 as the central mediator of iron-induced inflammation in CD11c+ myeloid cells. IL-6 is a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine that plays critical roles in neutrophil recruitment and immune cell differentiation. In rheumatoid arthritis, activated DCs in synovial tissues secrete IL-6 in response to inflammatory stimuli, attracting neutrophils to the joint cavity, where they release inflammatory mediators, exacerbating the joint damage33. In bacterial pneumonia, mast cells produce IL-6 in response to infection, facilitating neutrophil recruitment to the site of pulmonary inflammation and pathogen clearance34. These reports highlight the key role of IL-6 in neutrophil recruitment. However, its action mechanisms in liver injury, particularly after drug toxicity, remain ambiguous. Our data showed that iron overload in CD11c+ myeloid cells induced IL-6 production, which enhanced neutrophil infiltration into the liver and exacerbated tissue damage. Our results emphasize the importance of the CD11c+ myeloid cell–neutrophil axis in liver injury, which may be a pro-inflammatory pathway contributing to disease progression. However, IL-6 also promotes liver regeneration35. Therefore, IL-6 effects are context-dependent, and it can exert both protective and detrimental effects depending on the severity and resolution of the inflammatory response.

This study also suggested the molecular mechanisms underlying iron-induced IL-6 expression in CD11c+ myeloid cells. Iron enhanced IL-6 levels by promoting the recruitment of NF-κB to the Il-6 promoter. Notably, this effect was independent of classical upstream signaling events, such as IκB degradation and NF-κB nuclear translocation, suggesting that iron influences the accessibility or binding affinity of NF-κB for its target promoter region. NF-κB cooperates with other transcription factors, such as activator protein-1 (AP-1) and CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein-β (C/EBPβ), to regulate IL-6 expression in response to inflammatory stimuli36. Therefore, iron possibly modulates the expression or activity of these cofactors, thereby influencing Il-6 transcription. Future studies should investigate the epigenetic landscape of the Il-6 locus, including histone modifications and chromatin remodeling, for more valuable insights into the mechanisms by which iron regulates gene expression at the chromatin level37. In addition, it is important to validate the in vitro mechanisms identified in this study within the context of acute liver injury in vivo. Considering the intricate interplay between NF-κB and other inflammatory pathways, the cross-talk between NF-κB and other metabolic pathways during iron overload should be explored to identify important therapeutic targets to alleviate inflammation in liver injury.

Our findings suggest potential avenues for the development of improved therapeutic strategies for acute liver injury. Modulation of the IL-6 pathway using neutralizing antibodies or small-molecule inhibitors is a promising approach to alleviate liver damage in patients with drug-induced liver injury. Additionally, it would be valuable to investigate whether restoring iron homeostasis in CD11c+ myeloid cells—such as through iron chelator administration or inhibition of key components of iron metabolism, including IRP2—can resolve inflammation and promote tissue repair. However, considering the complex roles of iron in immune functions and tissue repair, such therapies must be developed with caution to avoid the impairment of the essential reparative processes of the body. Additional studies are necessary to explore the therapeutic potential of iron modulation for liver diseases and better understand the long-term effects of such interventions on immune homeostasis and liver function.

In conclusion, this study provides insights into the roles of iron in the regulation of immune responses during acute liver injury. Our findings highlight the complex interplay between iron metabolism and immune functions, suggesting potential therapeutic avenues to modulate the inflammatory responses in liver diseases. Specifically, iron homeostasis and IL-6 signaling are potential therapeutic targets to mitigate acute liver injury and improve patient outcomes. Future research should focus on uncovering the precise molecular mechanisms through which iron influences immune cell function and tissue repair. This will be critical for the development of advanced targeted therapies for various liver diseases. Furthermore, recent studies emphasize the significant role of the immune landscape in the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases. Investigating how CD11c+ myeloid cell iron metabolism impacts the immune landscape in liver diseases, through comprehensive immunophenotyping, could yield deeper insights into the immune interactions driving liver pathology. Such research has the potential to pave the way for more personalized and effective treatments.

Methods

Mice

To generate Cd11c-Cre/Fbxl5F/F mice, Cd11c-Cre transgenic mice38 were obtained from the Jackson Lab (Stock Number 008068) and crossed with Fbxl5F/F mice17. All animal experiments were conducted using 7- to 10-week-old male mice. The mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. The mice were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation and euthanized by cervical dislocation, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. To induce acute liver injury, the mice were fasted for 16 h prior to the intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 300 or 500 mg/kg APAP (M1R4379; Nacalai Tesque) diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to 20 mg/mL. Food was provided immediately after the injection. In specific experiments, the mice were administered the anti-mouse IL-6 monoclonal antibody (100 μg; A2118; Selleck) or rat IgG1 isotype control monoclonal antibody (isotype control; 100 μg; A2119; Selleck) prior to APAP challenge. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All experiments were approved by and adhered to the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Kumamoto University and the Institute of Science, Tokyo.

Cell lines

DC2.4 mouse DCs were maintained in the Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium with 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (189-02145; Fujifilm) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (175012; Nichirei), L-glutamine (073-05391; Wako), MEM non-essential amino acids (2696374; Gibco), 2-mercaptoethanol (00424-1; Millipore), and penicillin–streptomycin solution (168-23191; Wako) under an atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The cells were plated in a 6-well plate at 2 × 105 cells/well and incubated for 12 h before the experiments, unless otherwise noted. The cells were pretreated with or without FAC (100 μM; RES20400-A7; Sigma-Aldrich), DFO (100 μM; D9533; Sigma-Aldrich), and BPY (200 μM; PAM4740; Wako) and stimulated with LPS (500 ng/mL; tlrl-eblps; InvivoGen) at the indicated time points.

Gene deletion using the clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeat (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) system

FBXL5 KO cells were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Single guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences were designed using the CRISPOR tool (http://crispor.tefor.net/)39. The sequence of FBXL5 KO was 5’-GATATCTTTAAGCTCCTCGT-3’. This guide sequence was inserted into the lentiCRISPR v2 Puro plasmid (Derived from Addgene plasmid #83480 by replacing the blasticidin S-resistance gene with a puromycin- resistance gene). To delete FBXL5 in DC2.4 cells, FBXL5-targeting lentiCRISPR v2 plasmids were co-transfected with the packaging plasmids, pLenti-P2A and pLenti-P2B (LV003; Cosmobio) into the HEK293T cells using PolyJet (SL100688; SignaGen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the lentiviral supernatant was supplemented with 5 µg/mL polybrene (TR-1003-G; MERCK) and used for infection. Forty-eight hours after infection, DC2.4 cells were selected with puromycin (5 µg/mL) in the culture medium for 3 days, and then used as FBXL5 KO (pooled) cells for experiments.

H&E staining

Liver tissues were fixed with 10% formalin neutral buffer solution (062-01661; Wako) overnight, immersed in 70% ethanol for 1 day at 4 °C, and embedded in paraffin using CT-Pro20 (GenoStaff). Then, the paraffin blocks were sectioned into 2-μm slices using a microtome (Leica RM2125), deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (8659; Sakura Finetek Japan), following the standard protocol. Finally, images were captured using the KEYENCE BZ-X800 instrument.

Immunofluorescence staining

For liver tissue staining, fresh liver tissues cut into small pieces were embedded using the OCT compound (45833; Sakura Finetek Japan), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and sectioned into 5-μm slices using a cryostat (CM1950; Leica). To evaluate immune cell infiltration, the sections were fixed with –30 °C methanol (139-01827; Wako) for 10 min. For CD45 immunostaining, the fixed sections were incubated with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA)/PBS for 30 min at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with the CD45-PE antibody (1:100; 12-0451-82; Invitrogen). For Ly6G immunostaining, the fixed sections were incubated with 10% goat serum with 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS at room temperature for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with the Ly6G-PE antibody (1:100; 551461; BD) diluted in the same solution. Then, the sections were treated with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:2000; D523; Dojindo Laboratories) in 1% BSA/PBS at room temperature for 1 h. To assess hepatic regeneration following 24 h APAP treatment, the sections were initially fixed in 10% formalin neutral buffer solution for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, samples underwent blocking through incubation with 5% goat serum with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min. The sections were incubated with Ki67 antibody (1:200; 9129; CST) in PBS supplemented with 1% BSA and 0.3% Triton X-100 overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa Fluor 546 (1:500; A-11035; Thermo) antibody and DAPI (1:2000; D523; Dojindo Laboratories) in 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS solution. Images were captured using KEYENCE BZ-X800.

For DC2.4 cell staining, 15-mm coverslips (4040911299; Matsunami) were pre-placed in a 24 well plate, and the cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well for overnight culture. The cells were treated with LPS for 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PAP2494; Wako) for 10 min. Then, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 30 min. The samples were incubated with the NF-κB p65 antibody (1:500; 8242; CST) for 3 h, followed by incubation with the Alexa Fluor 488 antibody (1:1000; A-11008; Invitrogen) and DAPI (1:1000; D523; Dojindo Laboratories) for 1 h in 1% BSA/PBS at room temperature. Images were acquired using the Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope.

Flow cytometric analysis

The mice were anesthetized, and their livers were first perfused with calcium and magnesium-free buffer (10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid [pH 7.4], 5.4 mM KCl, 120 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 5.6 mM glucose, and 5.0 mM NaHCO3) via the inferior vena cava for 3.5 min at a rate of 7 mL/min and then with the Hank’s balanced salt solution (5.4 mM KCl, 140 mM NaCl, 0.44 mM KH2PO4, 0.34 mM Na2HPO4, 1.3 mM CaCl2, 0.98 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 5.6 mM glucose, and 4.2 mM NaHCO3) containing 0.1% collagenase (032-22364; Wako) for 3.5 min. The livers were minced and further digested using Hank’s balanced salt solution supplemented with 0.1% collagenase for 30 min at 37 °C under agitation using the gentle MACS Octo Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec). The cell suspension was passed through a 70-µm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Subsequently, the suspension was centrifuged at 50 × g for 3 min to remove the sedimented hepatocytes and dead cells. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged, and the resulting cells were resuspended in an appropriate volume of PBS. Then, a 25% Percoll solution was prepared using 100% Percoll (10343286; Cytiva) and mixed with the cell suspension, which was gently layered over the 50% Percoll solution. After centrifugation, the cell populations in the 25 and 50% Percoll layers were carefully collected and washed twice with PBS to remove the residual Percoll, yielding an enriched population of liver immune cells. The mixture was further subjected to density gradient centrifugation at 1500 × g for 15 min. Then, the cell population at interphase was carefully collected and washed with PBS to remove the residual Percoll, resulting in an enriched population of liver immune cells. The cells were stained on ice using the LIVE/DEAD staining kit (1:2000; L34976; Invitrogen) for 15 min to distinguish between the live and dead cells, followed by a 30-min incubation with the anti-CD16/CD32 antibodies (1:200; 70-0161; Tonbo Biosciences) to block the Fc receptors. The cells were further incubated with the anti-CD45-PerCP-Cy5.5 (1:100; 550994; BD), anti-Ly6G-APC (1:200; 127613; Biolegend), anti-F4/80-AlexaFluor488 (1:200; 123119; Biolegend), anti-CD11b-eFluor450 (1:200; 48-0112-82; Invitrogen), anti-CD11c-PE/Cyanine7 (1:200; 117317; Biolegend), anti-CD45-PE/Cyanine7 (1:200; 147704; Biolegend), anti-TIM4-PE (1:200; B359567; Biolegend) antibodies on ice for 30 min. After washing and resuspending the samples in the FACS buffer, they were immediately tested using BD FACSVerse (BD Biosciences). Image processing and data analysis were performed using the FlowJo 10 software.

Mouse IL-6 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum IL-6 concentrations were quantified using the Mouse IL-6 Quantikine ELISA kit (M6000B-1; R&D Systems), adhering to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Cell sorting

Liver single-cell suspensions enriched with immune cells were prepared as described above. After blocking the Fc receptors, the cells were incubated with the anti-CD45-FITC (1:200; 553080; BD) and anti-CD11c-PE (1:200; 12-0114-81; eBioscience) antibodies. The samples were washed with the FACS buffer to remove the antibodies, followed by the addition of DAPI (1:5000; D523; Dojindo Laboratories) for 5 min. Then, CD11c+ cells were sorted using an automatic cell analysis separator (SH800S; Sony).

FerroOrange staining

Splenic CD11c+ cells (1.5 × 105 cells) were incubated with 200 μL of the 5 μmol/L FerroOrange (346-09531; DOJINDO) solution in a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube for 30 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, cells were centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min, resuspended in 200 μL HBSS, and then seeded into a 24-well plate. Intracellular iron levels were visualized using a KEYENCE BZ-X800.

Measurement of hepatic iron levels

Hepatic iron levels were measured as previously described40. Briefly, liver tissues were lysed in 400 μL of 50 mM NaOH, followed by the addition of 400 μL of 10 mM HCl and 400 μL of acid solution (700 mM HCl and 2.25% KMnO4). Samples were incubated at 60 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, 120 μL of iron detection reagent–composed of 6.5 mM ferrozine, 6.5 mM neocuproine, 2.5 M ammonium acetate, and 1 M sodium ascorbate–was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 3 min, the supernatant was collected and absorbance was measured at 550 nm.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells using ISOGEN (319-90211; Nippon Gene) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT kit (FSQ-301; Toyobo). The cDNA was diluted and used as a template for real-time PCR using the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Thunderbird Next SYBR (Toyobo, QPX-201), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The following primers were used for PCR: mouse Gapdh, 5′-GCCTGGAGAAACCTGCCAAGTATG-3′ and 5′-GAGTGGGAGTTGCTGTTGAAGTCG-3′; mouse Fbxl5, 5′-TCTTCCTCCTGAGGTAATGCTGTCC-3′ and 5′-CACAAAGATCCTGTTTTTGCCAGC-3′; mouse Il-6, 5′-CCAGAGATACAAAGAAATGATGG-3′ and 5′-ACTCCAGAAGACCAGAGGAAAT-3′. Gapdh mRNA reactions were performed on the same plate as the test mRNAs, and the results were normalized to the corresponding Gapdh mRNA levels.

Immunoblotting analysis

Equal amounts of protein were separated via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) under reducing conditions and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA or 5% skim milk, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The following antibodies were used for protein detection: anti-β-actin (1:2000; 12620; CST), anti-FBXL5 (1:2000; 672704; Biolegend), anti-IRP2 (1:2000; MABS2020-100UG; Millipore), anti-ferritin (1:2000; sc-376594; Santa Cruz), anti-phosphorylated IKK (1:2000; 2697; CST), anti-phosphorylated IκB (1:1000; 2859; CST), and anti-IκB (1:1000; 4814; CST) antibodies. Protein expression was detected using a chemiluminescent HRP substrate (WBKLS0500; Millipore), and images were acquired using the ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (17001401JA; Bio-Rad Laboratories).

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the liver tissues of Fbxl5F/F and CD11c-Cre/Fbxl5F/F mice, with or without APAP treatment using ISOGEN (319-90211; Nippon Gene). High-throughput sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system, generating 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were trimmed using Trim Galore (v.0.6.6) to remove the adapter sequences. HISAT2 (v.2.2.1) was used to align the mm10 reference genome. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 (v.1.36.0) based on an adjusted p-value threshold of <0.05 and log2 fold change >2. Pathway enrichment analyses of the upregulated genes against MSigDB Hallmark 2020 database were performed via Enrichr. Volcano plots were generated using the enhanced volcano package (v.1.20.0). Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler (v.4.10.1) with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 to identify the enriched gene sets.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The ChIP assay was performed as previously described41, with slight modifications. Briefly, DC2.4 WT and FBXL5 KO cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁷ cells in a 15-cm dish with or without LPS (500 ng/mL; tlrl-eblps; InvivoGen) for 1 h. The cells were fixed with 1/10 volume of 11% formaldehyde solution for 10 min at room temperature, followed by quenching with 1/10 volume of 1.5 M glycine. The fixed cells were washed multiple times with ice-cold PBS containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and lysed using the sodium dodecyl sulfate lysis buffer (0.2% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8]) containing protease inhibitors. Chromatin was fragmented via sonication (20 cycles of 30 s on/off) using a Bioruptor. The supernatant containing the chromatin fragments was collected via centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min. For immunoprecipitation, anti-rabbit M-280 Dynabead (20 μL/sample; 11203D; Invitrogen) was blocked with PBS containing 0.2% BSA, conjugated with normal rabbit IgG antibody (1 μg/sample; 2729; CST) or NF-κB p65 antibody (1 μg/sample; 8242; CST), and incubated with chromatin overnight at 4 °C. The beads were washed sequentially with low-salt (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8], 150 mM NaCl), high-salt (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8], 500 mM NaCl), LiCl (1% NP-40, 1% Sodium Deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8], 250 mM LiCl), and TE (1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8]) wash buffers to eliminate non-specific interactions. Immunoprecipitated chromatin was eluted with the ChIP direct elution buffer (0.5% SDS, 5 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8], 300 mM NaCl) and incubated at 65 °C for 4 h to reverse the crosslinks. RNase I and proteinase K treatments were performed to remove the RNA and proteins, respectively. DNA was purified via phenol–chloroform extraction, ethanol precipitation, and resuspension in the EB buffer. Finally, precipitated DNA was quantitated by qPCR analysis. All ChIP signals were normalized to the input (labeled as % of input on the vertical axis). Primers used to amplify the NF-κB binding site in mouse Il-6 promoter region were 5’-TGCTCATGCTTCTTAGGGCT-3’ and 5’-TGCACAATGTGACGTCGTTT-3’.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using the GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.). All statistical parameters and methods are described in the figure legends. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
