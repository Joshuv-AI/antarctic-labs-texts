A novel copper-based magnetic nano catalyst for the synthesis of hexahydroquinolines, aminocyanopyridines, and pyranopyrazoles
Authors: Arezo Monem, Davood Habibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A novel copper-based magnetic nanocatalyst, MNP@Im–Cu, was successfully synthesized via a six-step procedure. Its structural evolution, magnetic behavior, and thermal stability were systematically characterized using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), thermogravimetric analysis/differential thermal analysis (TGA/DTA), vibrating-sample magnetometry (VSM), inductively coupled plasma analysis (ICP), and X-ray diffraction (XRD). These analyses confirmed successful surface functionalization, uniform core–shell morphology, elemental homogeneity, magnetic properties, and thermal robustness. The catalytic efficiency of this multifunctional nanocatalyst was demonstrated in the one-pot, four-component synthesis of pharmaceutically relevant heterocycles: hexahydroquinolines (HHQs), 2-amino-3-cyanopyridines (ACPs), and pyranopyrazoles (PYPYs). Reactions proceeded under solvent-free conditions using readily available starting materials, affording the desired products in high to excellent yields with notably short reaction times. The catalyst exhibited excellent reusability and chemo-selectivity, underscoring its potential as a sustainable and efficient platform for multi-component heterocycle synthesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53404-8.

Introduction

Nitrogen-containing heterocycles are key scaffolds in medicinal chemistry due to their broad biological activities and extensive pharmaceutical applications1–3. Among these, dihydropyridine (DHP) frameworks have attracted considerable attention for their ability to generate chemical diversity and refine structure–activity relationships4–7. HHQs, a prominent subclass of DHPs, are typically synthesized via one-pot, four-component Hantzsch-type reactions involving dimedone, aromatic aldehydes, β-ketoesters, and ammonium acetate. Multicomponent reactions (MCRs), such as the Hantzsch reaction, represent a powerful synthetic strategy for constructing complex heterocyclic frameworks in a single operational step, offering high atom economy and structural diversity8,9.Various catalysts have been explored for this transformation, including K7[PW11CoO40]10, Fe3O4@(HSO4)211, BIL@ MNP12, La2O313, Bi(NO3)3·5H2O14, as well as catalyst-free conditions15, yet there remains a need for more efficient, sustainable, and recyclable systems.

Following HHQs, ACPs have emerged as versatile building blocks, enabling the construction of diverse heterocyclic frameworks. The cyano group facilitates reactions with mono- and bidentate nucleophiles, while the amino group promotes substitution and condensation reactions, making ACPs highly valuable in synthetic chemistry1,2. Several 2-amino-pyridines, such as piroxicam, sulfapyridine, tenoxicam, and tripelennamine, have been applied successfully in drug development3. Despite various catalytic systems reported, including Fe3O4 nanoparticles16, urea-based catalysts17, SrFe12O19 magnetic particles18, palladium hydroxide19, and copper(I) salts20, there is still room for improvement in catalytic efficiency, sustainability, and recyclability.

Finally, 4H-pyran-based heterocycles have gained attention for their pharmacological properties. PYPYs, derived from these scaffolds, exhibit antimicrobial, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and antitumor activities21–35. Numerous catalytic systems have been developed for PYPY synthesis, including Y3Fe5O1236, TEBA37, TSA38, Fe3O4@ SiO2@PTS-DABA39, NiFe2O4@SiO2@H14[NaP5W30O110]40, Fe3O4@HNTs–PEI41, and Fe3O4 @SiO2–EP–NH–HPA42.The strategic use of MCRs in pyrano-pyrazole synthesis has enabled rapid access to bioactive compounds with minimal purification steps, aligning well with green chemistry principles43.

Despite this diversity, a critical review of the literature indicates the need for a single, integrated catalytic system that combines high efficiency, magnetic recoverability, multi-functional cooperative groups, and copper-mediated reactivity to enable greener synthetic protocols. The MNP@Im-Cu catalyst was designed as a multifunctional, magnetically recoverable nano-composite integrating both organic and inorganic components in a hierarchical architecture. The Fe3O4 core provides strong magnetic properties, enabling easy recovery and reuse. The SiO2 coating enhances the structural stability and prevents core oxidation while offering a large surface area for further modification. The APTES layer introduces amino groups that serve as anchor sites for cyanuric chloride, which acts as an efficient cross-linker to covalently attach imidazole functionalities. These imidazole moieties coordinate with Cu(II) ions, generating Lewis acidic active sites that promote carbonyl activation in multicomponent condensation reactions. Such an integrated structure combines stability, catalytic activity, and recyclability in a single platform, demonstrating a rational and sustainable design approach.

To meet this challenge, we report the design and development of a novel multifunctional nanocatalyst, Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES@CC@Im@Cu, hereafter referred to as MNP@Im-Cu. This catalyst features a superparamagnetic Fe3O4 core, a stabilizing silica layer, a function-alized linker with cooperative amino and imidazole groups, and active copper centers.

In line with green chemistry principles, solvent-free synthetic protocols were adopted, offering reduced environmental impact, shorter reaction times, higher yields, and simplified work-up. MNP@Im-Cu proved to be an efficient and magnetically recyclable catalyst for the one-pot multicomponent synthesis of HHQs, ACPs, and PYPYs, demonstrating excellent chemo-selectivity, robustness, and reusability, and outperforming previously reported systems in terms of yield, recyclability, and reaction efficiency.

Experimental

General remarks

All reagents and solvents were purchased from Merck and used as received without further purification. The catalyst was thoroughly characterized using various analytical techniques: Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy for functional group identification, field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for morphological and microstructural analysis, energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy for elemental composition mapping, vibrating-sample magnetometry (VSM) for evaluating magnetic behavior, thermogravimetric/differential thermal analysis (TGA/DTA) for thermal stability assessment, inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis for metal content determination, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) for crystallographic structure and phase confirmation. The synthesized products were confirmed using standard techniques, including melting point determination, FT-IR spectroscopy, 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy, and mass spectrometry, ensuring their purity and structural integrity.

Synthesis of the MNP@Im-Cu catalyst

The MNP@Im-Cu nanocatalyst was synthesized via a six-step protocol involving magnetic core formation, surface functionalization, and metal coordination, as detailed below:

Step 1: synthesis of Fe3O4 magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs)

Fe3O4 nanoparticles were prepared using a co-precipitation method. FeCl3·6H2O (11.3 g) and FeCl2·4H2O (5.6 g) were dissolved in deionized water at 80 °C. Upon complete dissolution, 28 wt% NH4OH (25 mL) was added dropwise under vigorous stirring. The mixture was stirred for 30 min at room temperature, followed by 2 h at 80 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere. The resulting black colloidal suspension was magnetically separated, washed repeatedly with deionized water and acetone, and dried at 60 °C to yield Fe3O4 powder42.

Step 2: coating with silica (Fe3O4@SiO2)

Fe3O4 (1 g) was dispersed in an ethanol/water mixture (80:20 v/v) via ultrasonication for 15 min. Then, 28 wt% NH4OH (2 mL) and tetramethyl orthosilicate (TMOS, 2 mL) were added dropwise. The mixture was stirred at 50 °C for 2 h. The obtained Fe3O4@SiO2 particles were magnetically collected, washed thoroughly with water and ethanol, and dried overnight at 60 °C.

Step 3: amino functionalization (Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES)

Fe3O4@SiO2 (2 g) was dispersed in toluene via ultrasonication for 30 min. APTES (3-amino-propyltriethoxysilane, 2 mL) was added, and the mixture was refluxed at 110 °C for 24 h under nitrogen. The product was magnetically separated, washed with acetone, and dried at 60 °C.

Step 4: cyanuric chloride grafting (Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES@CC)

Cyanuric chloride (0.5 g) was dissolved in THF (30 mL) and stirred at 0 °C for 3 h under nitrogen. Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES (1 g) was added and ultrasonicated for 30 min, followed by stirring at room temperature for 24 h. The resulting product was magnetically separated, washed with ethanol and THF, and dried at 60 °C.

Step 5: imidazole functionalization (Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES@CC@Im)

Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES@CC (2 g) was dispersed in acetonitrile via ultrasonication for 15 min. Imidazole (2 g) was added, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The solid was magnetically separated, washed with a 1:1 acetonitrile/ethanol mixture, and dried at 60 °C.

Step 6: copper coordination (MNP@Im-Cu)

Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES@CC@Im (0.6 g) was dispersed in methanol (30 mL) via ultrasonication for 15 min. A solution of copper(II) acetate (1.4 g in 15 mL methanol) was added, and the mixture was refluxed at 60 °C for 24 h. The final MNP@Im-Cu nanocatalyst was magnetically separated, washed thoroughly with methanol, and dried at 60 °C to yield a brown powder.

General procedure for the synthesis of HHQs 8(a-o)

Dimedone (1 mmol, 0.140 g), Aldehyde (1 mmol), ethyl acetoacetate (1 mmol, 0.130 g, 0.12 mL), ammonium acetate (1 mmol, 0.077 g), and MNP@Im–Cu (0.05 g) were mixed and stirred at 70 °C. The reaction progress was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using n-hexane/ ethyl acetate (8:2, v/v) as the eluent. Upon completion, the mixture was cooled to room temperature, and boiling ethyl acetate (20 mL) was added. Owing to its insolubility in ethyl acetate, the catalyst was magnetically separated. Characterization of the catalyst

The structural, morphological, and physicochemical characteristics of the synthesized catalyst (MNP@Im-Cu) were examined using various analytical techniques. FT-IR spectroscopy was employed to identify functional groups and confirm surface modification. The morphology and particle size were analyzed by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Elemental composition and distribution were investigated by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Magnetic properties were measured by vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM), and thermal stability was evaluated by thermo-gravimetric/differential thermal analysis (TGA/DTA). Furthermore, inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis was performed to quantify the copper content in the catalyst, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) was utilized to determine the crystalline structure.

Characterization by FT-IR

The FT-IR spectra of each functionalization stage are presented in Fig. 1. In the spectrum of Fe₃O₄ (Fig. 1a), the characteristic absorption band at 575 cm−1 corresponds to the Fe–O stretching vibration, confirming the formation of magnetite nanoparticles. After coating with silica (Fig. 1b), a strong peak at 1083 cm−1 is attributed to the Si–O–Si stretching vibration of the silica shell, indicating successful deposition of SiO₂ on the magnetic core. The introduction of APTES onto the surface (Fig. 1c) is confirmed by a broad band around 3372 cm−1, corresponding to the N–H stretching vibrations of the amine groups, verifying the presence of surface-bound amino functionalities. These amino groups act as reactive anchoring sites that covalently bond with cyanuric chloride through nucleophilic substitution and subsequently coordinate with imidazole and Cu(II) species, ensuring stable functionalization and providing active sites for catalytic reactions. Following the grafting of cyanuric chloride (Fig. 1d), a new peak at 1607 cm−1, assigned to the C=N stretching vibration, confirms successful attachment of cyanuric chloride to the amino-functionalized surface. The subsequent reaction with imidazole (Fig. 1e) produces a peak at 1533 cm−1 corresponding to the conjugated C=N bond, verifying the incorporation of imidazole moieties. Finally, in the spectrum of MNP@Im-Cu (Fig. 1f), the appearance of a distinct band at 1585 cm−1 confirms the coordination of copper ions with the imidazole groups, completing the stepwise functionalization process. The progressive spectral changes observed at each stage collectively provide strong evidence for the successful surface modification and the formation of the MNP@Im-Cu nanocatalyst44,45.Fig. 1FT-IR spectra of (a): Fe3O4 (b): Fe3O4@SiO2 (c): Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES (d): Fe3O4@SiO2@ APTES@CC (e): Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES@CC@Im (f): MNP@Im-Cu.

Characterization by TGA

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed from 25 to 800 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere to evaluate the thermal stability and confirm successful stepwise surface functionalization of the MNP@Im-Cu catalyst. As seen in Fig. 8, the bare Fe3O4 (a) nanoparticles exhibit a weight loss of ~ 7%, mainly due to adsorbed moisture. Silica-coated Fe3O4@SiO2 (b) shows a higher weight loss of ~ 12%, attributed to the elimination of surface hydroxyl groups. Functionalization with APTES (c) results in a further increase to ~ 22%, confirming successful grafting of the organic layer. Interestingly, after cyanuric chloride (CC) coupling (d) and imidazole functionalization (e), the weight loss decreases to ~ 19% and ~ 15%, respectively. This trend reflects the formation of thermally stable planar heterocyclic ligands, which resist decomposition better than the previous amine layer. Finally, complexation with copper(II) ions to form MNP@Im-Cu (f) slightly increases weight loss to ~ 18%, likely due to minor moisture adsorption, surface rearrangement, or initial degradation of the metal–ligand complex, confirming successful copper coordination.Fig. 8TGA of (a) Fe3O4, (b) Fe3O4@SiO2, (c) Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES, (d) Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES@CC, (e) Fe3O4@SiO2@APTES@CC@Im, and (f) the final MNP@Im-Cu catalyst.

Characterization by XRD

As shown in Fig. 10, the powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the MNP@Im-Cu catalyst exhibits a series of well-defined peaks at 2θ values of 30.40, 35.75, 37.45, 44.65, 54.10, 57.45, 62.70, and 71.30°. These reflections correspond to the (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), (440), and (620) lattice planes, respectively, confirming the presence of a crystalline spinel-type Fe3O4 structure. The sharp and symmetrical peaks indicate that the magnetite core retains its crystallinity and structural integrity after successive surface functionalizations. The absence of additional diffraction peaks related to copper oxide or other crystalline impurities suggests that the Cu(II) species are highly dispersed and immobilized on the amorphous functional shell. Proposed mechanism

The proposed mechanism for the synthesis of hexahydroquinolines involves a multistep cascade catalyzed by MNP@Im–Cu. Initially, the aldehyde carbonyl is activated by the Lewis acidic Cu2+ centers, increasing its susceptibility to nucleophilic attack. Concurrently, the enol form of dimedone, also activated by the catalyst, reacts with the aldehyde to form the Knoevenagel intermediate (I). Ammonium acetate undergoes acid–base equilibrium to generate ammonia in situ, which then reacts with ethyl acetoacetate to yield the enamine intermediate (II). A subsequent 1,4-conjugate addition between intermediates (I) and (II), facilitated by surface acidic sites, produces intermediate (III). Finally, intramolecular cyclization and dehydration, both catalyzed by MNP@Im–Cu, afford the final hexahydroquinoline product (Scheme 5).Scheme 5The plausible proposed mechanism for the synthesis of HHQs by MNP@Im-Cu.

Hot filtration test

A hot filtration test was carried out to verify the heterogeneity of the MNP@Im-Cu catalyst using 6-amino-4-(4-chlorophenyl)-3-methyl-1-phenyl-1,4-dihydropyrano[2,3-c]pyrazole-5-carbonitrile (10c) as a model substrate. For this test, the reaction was stopped at 10 min, giving a 44% yield. The catalyst was then removed with a magnet, and the reaction mixture was allowed to continue under the same conditions for an additional 10 min. After purification, the yield increased only slightly to 46%, indicating that the reaction barely proceeded without the catalyst. These results confirm that the MNP@Im-Cu catalyst is truly heterogeneous and does not leach into the solution (Fig. 11).Fig. 11Heterogeneity Test of MNP@Im-Cu Catalyst via Hot Filtration.

Conclusion

In summary, the MNP@Im-Cu catalyst demonstrated outstanding catalytic performance in the synthesis of HHQs, ACPs, and PYPYs, achieving excellent isolated yields (up to 92%) within short reaction times (10–70 min, depending on the substrate). The presence of strong Lewis acidic Cu2+ sites on the catalyst surface effectively promoted multi-component condensation reactions under mild, solvent-free conditions. Structural characterization confirmed that the catalyst retained its core–shell morphology, high crystallinity, and surface functionalization even after several recycling runs. ICP-OES analysis indicated that approximately 91% of the active metals (Fe and Cu) were preserved after repeated use, consistent with the observed high recyclability. The developed catalyst offers several notable advantages, including simple magnetic separation, high thermal and chemical stability, rapid reaction rates, and minimal loss of activity across multiple cycles. These features collectively highlight the efficiency, robustness, and sustainability of MNP@Im-Cu as a green and reusable catalytic system. Overall, this study provides valuable insight into the design, characterization, and application of magnetically recoverable copper-based catalysts for environmentally benign organic transformations.
