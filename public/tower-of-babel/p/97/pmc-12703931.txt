Alternative methods for developmental toxicity testing: features, status and future directions
Authors: Inho Hwang, Eui-Bae Jeung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceThe developmental stage begins with implantation of a fertilized egg, followed by continuous cell division and differentiation to produce a complete organism. Developmental toxicity involves adverse effects that may be irreversible or undetectable until significant damage occurs, especially during early development, and must be assessed for drug and chemical safety. Animal tests face limitations such as cost, ethics, and species differences, prompting demand for alternatives. This review examines current alternative methods, institutional efforts, and future directions.ObservationsRecent advances have led to the use of various new approach methodologies to replace or complement animal testing. The embryoid body test now allows for faster and more reproducible assessment of early developmental toxicity, while organoids are being applied to evaluate organ-specific toxicities, such as neurodevelopmental and cardiac effects. Zebrafish offer validated endpoints for morphological and behavioral evaluation, enhancing their regulatory relevance. Predictive models, including those based on machine learning, are being integrated with in vitro data to strengthen mechanism-based analysis. These observations reflect a shift toward more human-relevant and efficient approaches in toxicity testing, which aligns with the central focus of this review. Regulatory bodies such as the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development are also actively promoting the acceptance and implementation of these methods.Conclusions and RelevanceAlternative methods provide ethical and rational approaches to developmental toxicity assessment. When integrated into regulatory science, they can contribute to early safety decisions in clinical development and public health. INTRODUCTION

The developmental stage of an organism encompasses the period during which a fertilized egg implants and progresses to an embryo and, subsequently, a fetus, involving continuous cell division and differentiation that ultimately gives rise to a complete organism. This stage is marked by rapid and diverse biological changes, rendering it highly unstable and susceptible to toxic insults [1]. Acute exposure to potent toxicants during this critical window can result in severe embryonic or fetal damage [2]. In contrast, chronic exposure to relatively low-level toxicants can result in their accumulation without noticeable symptoms in the mother, potentially leading to developmental malformations [3]. Although prenatal diagnostic techniques have advanced, enabling the detection of some abnormalities during pregnancy, they are not universally comprehensive [4]. Structural normalcy does not guarantee functional integrity, and subtle developmental impairments may go undetected. Most abnormalities identified during this period are difficult or impossible to treat [5]. Consequently, mitigating these potential risks mandates proactive and rigorous developmental toxicity assessments of materials used frequently in daily life, particularly those that come into direct contact with pregnant women or infants, such as baby bottles and tableware.

Animal testing remains the primary method for evaluating developmental toxicity because of the difficulty of reproducing complex physiological processes, including placental transfer, neural development, and hormonal regulation, using alternative models [6]. Experimental animals, most commonly mice and rats, have been selected for their ease of handling, availability, and low compound requirements [7]. Non-rodent species, such as rabbits, may be selected depending on the mechanism of action or research objectives [8].

The study design of nonclinical studies conducted for drug development must follow regulatory guidelines according to the intended use and characteristics of various drugs (Table 1). Although study designs vary according to the purpose, pregnancy and delivery are typically included in developmental toxicity assessments. Variables such as the pregnancy success rate, number of offspring, and sex distribution are unpredictable, which increases the number of animals required compared to general toxicity tests [9].

When studies cover an entire pregnancy, delivery, and postnatal growth, preparation time, study duration, and cost increase significantly [10]. Studies focusing on acute effects, such as embryo or fetal survival and malformations, have been conducted within the relatively short timeframe of gestation and delivery. In contrast, evaluations of the reproductive function, neurodevelopment, behavior, and multigenerational effects may require durations exceeding one year.

Growing global concern for animal welfare has led to increasing scrutiny of the ethical implications of animal testing. In many countries, ethics committees must approve animal experiments and follow the 3Rs principle (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) to minimize animal suffering and eliminate unnecessary testing [11]. Nevertheless, the movement to reduce or eliminate the use of mammalian animal testing is growing [12]. In the cosmetics industry, many countries have banned animal testing or prohibited the import of products tested on animals [13].

Regulatory agencies are also actively promoting policies aimed at reducing animal testing. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) encourages member countries to develop and adopt alternative test methods and continuously updates guidelines related to non-animal testing approaches [14]. In 2019, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) announced a plan to phase out toxicity testing on mammals by 2035 [15]. In January 2023, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) revised its regulations to no longer require animal testing in all stages of drug development before human trials [16].

Another major limitation of animal testing lies in its cost and time requirements. In addition to the expenses of acquiring and maintaining animals in controlled environments, significantly larger quantities of test substances are required than in vitro assays [17]. This increases the production and management costs, as well as the time and resources required for sample processing and data analysis. Considering these constraints, animal studies must be designed carefully and minimized, and appropriate alternative methods should be used to fill the gap.

Alternative testing methods evaluate efficacy or toxicity without relying on traditional animal experiments. These include in vitro assays using cultured cells, in silico analyses based on computer modeling, and ex vivo tests that use animal tissues while minimizing animal sacrifice [18]. In addition, tests using invertebrates such as Drosophila melanogaster or Caenorhabditis elegans, as well as fish-based assays that reduce the use of mammals, are also considered alternatives. These methods minimize animal use while offering reductions in time and cost compared to conventional animal studies [19].

The increasing awareness of the ethical and scientific limitations of animal testing has prompted international regulatory bodies, such as the U.S. EPA, the U.S. FDA, and the OECD, to revise policies and guidelines. These efforts focus on the development, validation, and regulatory adoption of new approach methodologies (NAMs) to establish a robust framework for their implementation [20].

Trends in the replacement of animal testing can be inferred from market forecasts to some extent. Although the market for animal testing has not shown a definitive decline because of the continued expansion of the pharmaceutical and biotechnology industries, long-term projections suggest relatively slower growth. The compound annual growth rate of animal testing was estimated to be 7.5% from 2026 to 2033, which is lower than the projected 10.97% for in vitro toxicity testing (2025–2034) and 11.9% for alternative methods in general (2024–2032) [21,22]. This trend likely reflects a gradual shift toward replacing animal experiments with appropriate alternatives, driven by cost, duration, and animal welfare concerns.

This review provides a comprehensive overview of developmental toxicity testing, encompassing its scientific rationale, recent advancements in alternative methods, and evolving institutional and regulatory responses. While many recent reviews have focused on specific test systems or guideline updates, this work offers an integrated perspective, aiming to deliver broader insights into current trends and future directions in the field.

METHODS

This narrative review was conducted to explore current trends, technological advances, and regulatory perspectives related to alternative methods for developmental toxicity testing. Literature was searched using major scientific databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Additional policy documents and regulatory guidance were referenced from official sources such as OECD iLibrary, European Chemicals Agency, and National Center for Biotechnology Information Bookshelf. Keywords used in the literature search included combinations of terms such as “developmental toxicity test,” “alternative methods,” “non-animal testing,” “in vitro assays,” “organoids,” “stem cell-based models,” and “data-driven predictive models.” Relevant articles were selected based on scientific credibility and thematic relevance to areas such as emerging technologies, regulatory adoption, human relevance, and application to developmental toxicity assessment. Given that this is a narrative review, no strict inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied. Instead, a flexible and integrative approach was used to ensure comprehensive coverage of key issues and recent developments in the field. This narrative review focused primarily on peer-reviewed literature and relevant regulatory documents published in English up to March 2025. Priority was given to studies published after 2010, while earlier seminal work was included where scientifically important. References were selected based on relevance and credibility rather than strict inclusion or exclusion criteria. We used a generative AI language model (ChatGPT, OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA) to assist with language polishing at the draft stage; all content was reviewed and verified by the authors before submission.

OBSERVATIONS

Alternative methods for developmental toxicity testing

Although definitions may vary depending on the perspective, developmental toxicology generally considers the developmental process to span from fertilization and implantation to birth, encompassing the transformation from a single cell to a fully formed embryo or fetus [23]. As development progresses, the growing complexity of inter-tissue interactions, including immune system maturation, hormonal regulation, and circulatory changes, renders comprehensive simulation increasingly difficult with current technologies [24]. As a result, alternative toxicity testing methods have been primarily developed and applied to the early stages of development, such as embryogenesis and organogenesis, where in vitro modeling is more feasible. Nevertheless, with ongoing technological advances, new alternative methods that can simulate the organ structure and function and the interactions between systems and environmental factors are being developed and applied gradually to developmental toxicity assessments. Non-mammalian in vivo models using invertebrates and fish have also been recognized as alternative approaches. These models retain the advantages of whole-organism testing while significantly reducing the use of mammals. In addition, various computer-based predictive models and tools have been developed using accumulated scientific data and chemical information. This study categorizes these alternative methods based on their purposes and formats and explores their characteristics and practical applications in developmental toxicity research (Table 2).

Developmental toxicity testing in early embryogenesis

Stem cells have significantly advanced alternative methods for assessing developmental toxicity during early pregnancy (Table 3). The embryonic stem cell test (EST), introduced in the late 1990s by Spielmann et al. [25], evaluates developmental toxicity by examining the differentiation of mouse embryonic stem cells into cardiomyocytes and cytotoxicity data from fibroblasts. Since its introduction, EST has been applied worldwide as a replacement or supplement to animal testing [18,25]. Although the OECD has not officially adopted the EST as a test guideline, the European Centre for the Validation of Alternative Methods (ECVAM) validated its utility. EST has since been a reference model for developing numerous subsequent assays in developmental toxicity testing [26].

Although EST offered a more straightforward and reproducible approach than traditional animal studies, the assay required approximately ten days to complete [25]. This relatively long duration for an in vitro test is a disadvantage, contributing to variability in reproducibility. Various efforts based on assessing toxicity through the proliferation and differentiation of mouse embryonic stem cells have been made to simplify the procedure and shorten the testing period. ZEBET (Center for Documentation and Evaluation of Alternative Methods to Animal Experiments), which originally developed EST, introduced fluorescence-activated cell sorting into the existing protocol to improve efficiency [27]. The Japan Center for the Validation of Alternative Methods developed a modified assay in which the hand1-luc reporter gene was introduced into stem cells, enabling the detection of cardiac differentiation more directly [28]. The embryoid body test (EBT) developed by Kang et al. [29] replaced the conventional cardiomyocyte differentiation evaluation of the EST with an assessment based on the area of embryoid bodies. After two validation rounds, several studies have reported the use of EBT for assessing developmental toxicity [30,31,32,33,34]. The EBT enables an evaluation within just four days and is expected to be utilized increasingly owing to its simplicity, higher reproducibility, and improved accuracy.

These assays, despite being in vitro tests, demonstrate excellent accuracy and predictive power in simulating developmental toxicity during actual embryogenesis. The EST, which was the first to be developed and is widely used, is reported to have an accuracy approaching 80% [35]. Notably, the EBT has shown approximately 90% accuracy and reproducibility, along with 80% predictivity, even with its shorter testing period and following two rounds of validation [18]. These results indicate that the EBT is highly comparable to conventional developmental toxicity tests and shows considerable potential for full replacement.

Recent efforts have focused on optimizing validated mouse stem cell-based developmental toxicity assays for high-throughput screening (HTS) to maximize the advantages of in vitro testing. In 2011, Suzuki et al. [36] reported a version of the EST integrated with HTS. Since then, various publications have discussed its applicability [36]. In contrast, the EBT was first reported as an HTS-compatible assay in 2024 and is expected to be applied widely based on its unique advantages [37]. Early developmental stages involve the embryonic phase before differentiation and growth into a complete organism. Therefore, it may be more effective in enhancing the strengths of currently available assays whose accuracy and reproducibility have been reasonably established rather than pursuing further refinement. In addition to traditional assessments of cell viability and differentiation, incorporating molecular markers, such as gene expression changes, could allow for more precise, rapid, and user-friendly testing.

Testing approaches focused on the development of specific body systems

As the embryo develops into a fetus, the organism becomes increasingly organized into distinct organ systems. Each system undergoes unique developmental processes influenced by specific regulatory and environmental factors. Therefore, modeling toxicity in these systems requires precisely designed assays that reflect organ-specific differentiation and function. Although the details vary according to the method, directed differentiation is a central component of organ-simulating assays. The basic concept of organ-specific developmental toxicity testing involves inducing stem cells to differentiate into a targeted lineage using specific signaling factors [38]. Differentiation is verified through the expression of lineage-specific markers, after which toxicity is assessed during the differentiation process or in the differentiated state. In many cases, the cells differentiate spontaneously or through guided conditions into three-dimensional structures that resemble organ-like features [39]. These models are evaluated for structural organization, marker expression, and functional activity.

Early lineage-specific models typically involve tagging stem cell differentiation markers with fluorescent or reporter genes to assess cytotoxicity and differentiation-related toxicity. Several studies have reported marker-based assays for various lineages, with validation through comparative testing against in vivo data (Table 4). The developmental neurotoxicity assay developed by Park et al. [40] is a notable example. This approach used 46c mouse embryonic stem cells in which the neural differentiation marker Sox1 was labeled with green fluorescent protein (GFP). The toxicity was evaluated by measuring the cell viability (IC50) and detecting the inhibition of three-dimensional sphere formation using the GFP signal intensity (ID50), from which a discrimination function was derived. Follow-up validation studies revealed more than 90% reproducibility and predictivity [41]. Therefore, assays based on stem cells and differentiation markers offer simplicity and reproducibility while maintaining high accuracy.

Representative differentiation markers by organ system

Organoids are advanced in vitro systems designed to closely simulate the structure and function of specific organs or tissues through guided differentiation. In 2009, Sato et al. first reported the development of intestinal organoids, marking a significant advance in the field [42]. Since then, organoid research has expanded rapidly across many biological and medical disciplines worldwide. Organoids are typically generated by inducing stem cells to differentiate in three-dimensional environments. This process often involves using extracellular matrix scaffolds, such as Matrigel, and specialized devices, including rotary bioreactors and organ-on-a-chip platforms [43]. These systems promote the self-organization of differentiated cells into structures that recapitulate the key features of native organs, both in form and function [38].

A major advantage of organoids is their ability to model human organ-specific characteristics with high fidelity, particularly when derived from induced pluripotent stem cells [44]. This enables precise assessments of toxicity and disease mechanisms. Nevertheless, the complexity of organoid systems presents challenges, including higher costs, longer preparation times, and potential limitations in reproducibility [45]. Although the application of organoids in developmental toxicity testing remains relatively underexplored, interest in this area continues to grow. Emerging studies have begun to focus on major organs, such as the brain and heart, suggesting that organoids may become valuable tools for evaluating developmental toxicity (Table 5).

Animal test-based alternative models

Despite the advances in biomimetic systems, fully replicating the complexity of living organisms remains a significant challenge. In vivo exposure involves multiple pathways and physiological barriers that are difficult to model using cell-based assays. Among these, behavioral assessments are particularly challenging because they lack viable alternatives and require whole-organism approaches. Although non-mammalian animal models involve living organisms and do not fully satisfy the criteria for alternative testing, they offer an important compromise [46]. These models utilize intact biological systems while significantly reducing the use of mammals, overcoming some limitations of cell-based assays. Non-mammalian models are generally easier to handle and maintain than mammalian species. They are readily available in large numbers, require shorter testing durations, and need smaller quantities of test substances. These characteristics make them attractive alternatives for evaluating developmental and systemic toxicity in a more physiologically relevant context.

Invertebrate-based assays

Invertebrates are classified biologically as animals, but some regulatory frameworks often categorize them as non-animal or alternative test methods. They have fewer ethical concerns compared to vertebrates [47]. These models offer several advantages, including short generation times, ease of mass cultivation, and minimal maintenance requirements [48]. Nevertheless, complete reliance on invertebrate models is limited by their genetic differences from vertebrates. Therefore, they are used primarily for early-phase screening.

C. elegans is a nematode approximately 1 mm in length, characterized by a fixed number of cells and well-defined developmental stages. The species shares approximately 60%–80% genome similarity to humans and matures from egg to adult in approximately three days, making it suitable for short-term toxicity testing [49]. This model allows assessments of reproduction, development, and behavior. Despite being a whole-animal model, C. elegans assays are rapid and convenient and have been used in studies of developmental toxicants such as methylmercury and bisphenol S [50,51]. Recently, research has integrated C. elegans with HTS systems to increase its utility [52]. Although no official testing guideline exists for C. elegans, the European Union Reference Laboratory-ECVAM has highlighted its potential for developmental and neurotoxicity testing. The organization has proposed 30 reporting criteria for using C. elegans in developmental and reproductive toxicity studies, supporting its broader adoption as an alternative model [53].

D. melanogaster (fruit fly) is another widely used invertebrate model commonly found in natural environments. This species has relatively complex organ systems, including brain, heart, and reproductive organs, and completes its life cycle from egg to adult in approximately ten days [54]. Its genome is well-characterized and highly amenable to genetic manipulation, making it a valuable model for studying the gene function and disease mechanisms. Drosophila is used frequently in developmental toxicity studies by exposing embryos, larvae, or pupae to various test compounds and analyzing the development of the target organs [55]. Functional endpoints include motor activity, behavior, memory and learning, lifespan, and reproductive capacity, with numerous related studies published. It is increasingly recognized as part of the NAMs for toxicological assessment [56].

Zebrafish model

The zebrafish (Danio rerio) is a small freshwater species native to South Asia, with adults measuring approximately 3–4 cm. Zebrafish exhibit high fecundity, making it easy to secure large numbers of specimens that are relatively straightforward to maintain in laboratory settings. Major organ systems form within 72 hours post-fertilization, and the transparency of embryos and larvae allows for direct observations of developmental processes [57].

Zebrafish share approximately 70% of their genes with humans, and their genome is highly amenable to genetic manipulation. Specifically, approximately 82% of human disease-related genes have homologs in zebrafish [58]. As a result, zebrafish are used widely in disease modeling, including cancer, metabolic and endocrine disorders, cardiovascular conditions, and neurodegenerative diseases [58]. Zebrafish are used frequently as models for studying neurodegeneration owing to the similar brain structure and associated genes and the feasibility of behavioral assessments.

Despite being vertebrates, zebrafish are well suited to HTS platforms, and several studies have demonstrated their utility in this context [59]. In developmental toxicity testing, the endpoints include embryo hatching and growth, cardiac function, yolk sac edema, and morphological abnormalities. These applications are formally recognized under the OECD Test Guideline 236 (Fish Embryo Acute Toxicity Test) [15]. Based on these attributes, the zebrafish is considered a key representative model within the framework of NAMs.

Data-driven predictive models

From the perspective of time and cost efficiency, an approach to testing that avoids physical experimentation is the most rational. In silico methods, which rely on predictive models developed from accumulated experimental data and biological logic, are attracting increasing attention for developmental toxicity assessments [60]. Although the performance and applicability of these models vary according to the type, they are currently used to guide study design and prioritization rather than serve as standalone replacements. Nevertheless, these methods are expected to play an increasingly central role considering their compatibility with HTS systems and substantial advantages in time and cost, particularly as artificial intelligence technology advances rapidly [61].

Quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR)

The QSAR approach is a representative in silico method. QSAR links the structural features of a test compound with its biological activity using mathematical modeling [62]. Based on the assumption that chemicals with similar structures exhibit similar properties, QSAR uses information on the molecular descriptors derived from chemical structures and toxicity data. These descriptors are processed through linear regression, machine learning (ML) algorithms, or other statistical techniques to predict the toxicological outcomes. Since the 2010s, several studies have applied QSAR to developmental toxicity. Regulatory bodies have actively supported this approach. The OECD issued a validation guideline report on QSAR in 2014 and has since developed and distributed the QSAR Toolbox [62]. The U.S. EPA incorporates QSAR models into its CompTox Chemicals Dashboard and the ToxCast/Tox21 programs, integrating them with large-scale in vitro datasets to improve toxicity predictions and chemical prioritization [63]. Many other regulatory agencies have also adopted QSAR models, recognizing them as core tools within the NAMs framework.

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling

Pharmacokinetics (PK) is one of the most challenging areas to replace in animal testing. Following exposure, a substance undergoes absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, all influencing its biological effects [64]. These processes are challenging to simulate using in vitro models, often providing limited auxiliary data such as plasma or metabolic stability. Even in vivo tests face limitations because of interspecies and interindividual variability.

PBPK modeling addresses these challenges by representing the body as interconnected compartments corresponding to organs or tissues [65]. Mathematical equations simulate the distribution of chemicals across these compartments over time, enabling a prediction of the systemic or organ-specific concentrations. In contrast, PBPK models can also be used in reverse to estimate the exposure levels based on the tissue concentrations, allowing an evaluation of exposure pathways and target organ distribution [66]. In the context of developmental toxicity, PBPK models have been used to predict the fetal blood concentrations and build neonatal-specific models [67]. The OECD and EPA promote the integration of PBPK models into the NAMs strategy, particularly through in vitro to in vivo extrapolation, where PBPK models are applied as correction tools [68]. Considering the lack of reliable in vitro alternatives for PK, PBPK modeling will likely become a critical component of future alternative testing strategies.

ML model

ML models are predictive tools that learn from various types of toxicity-related data to identify patterns and forecast toxic outcomes. Because ML is based on large datasets, the accumulation and quality of data are linked directly to the reliability and applicability of the model. With the recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly in deep learning based on multi-layer neural networks, ML has rapidly evolved as a powerful technology. In toxicology, ML can serve as a standalone predictive model and be integrated with traditional models such as QSAR to improve efficiency and reliability [69]. Although ML applications in developmental toxicity are limited, recent studies have begun to explore its integration with zebrafish and C. elegans-based assays [70,71]. ML is expected to enable more flexible and broadly applicable toxicity predictions as the technology evolves.

DISCUSSION

As demonstrated throughout this study, alternative testing methods have been applied to support or replace animal testing at various stages of developmental toxicity assessment (Fig. 1). With the advancement of alternative testing methods, the transition away from animal testing is steadily progressing. This shift is driven by a convergence of factors: the social imperative to reduce animal pain and suffering, the economic benefit of saving time and cost, and the industrial incentive to accelerate new drug development.

The growing population of companion animals has led to increased public awareness of animal ethics, and more consumers are taking interest in how products are developed. There has also been a rise in boycott movements against products developed through animal testing. However, consumers’ demands for stricter regulation on animal testing are not necessarily a burden on the pharmaceutical industry. Alternative methods can reduce time and cost in industry, particularly in the pharmaceutical sector. These methods also allow the simultaneous testing of multiple substances. A 2019 study reported that applying organ-on-a-chip technologies reduced R&D expenses by 10% to 26% [72]. This reduction translated to cost savings of between $169 million and $706 million per new drug. In a 2022 report by Morris et al. [73], 93% of respondents indicated that pharmacogenetic testing helped reduce costs when used in place of animal studies. These cost benefits can encourage broader and more diverse research efforts, supporting the growth of related industries.

Pharmaceutical companies and toxicologists are actively working to implement these transitions. By optimizing existing models for HTS, they aim to maximize testing efficiency. New technologies such as organoids and organ-on-a-chip are being expanded to model previously untestable organs, and ongoing advancements are enhancing simulation fidelity. Integrated models, such as organoid-on-a-chip, combine strengths of existing approaches to overcome limitations. Most importantly, the rapid evolution of artificial intelligence is enabling the integration of large-scale data and the development of highly refined predictive models. These innovations reduce both time and cost, minimize animal use, and enhance human relevance in toxicity prediction. A notable example is the co-development of the Predict-96 organ-on-a-chip platform by Pfizer and Draper [74]. Furthermore, more than 30 scientists from leading pharmaceutical companies such as GSK, Novartis, AstraZeneca, and Pfizer collaborated with regulatory and AI experts to establish five foundational pillars for ML-based toxicity prediction, reflecting the field’s strong commitment to collaborative innovation [75].

Consumers may also benefit. People may gain earlier and more affordable access to new substances and treatments if animal use decreases and research becomes faster and more human-relevant. Nevertheless, this approach has a fundamental condition. All test systems, including animal models and alternative methods, are substitutes for human data. Preclinical studies must collect sufficient information to ensure safety and efficacy before a substance is applied to people. Clinical trials are important for final evaluations, but their scope is limited. This is particularly true for sensitive populations such as pregnant women and fetuses. Despite safety improvements made after the thalidomide case, researchers must always prepare for the worst-case scenario when dealing with human health.

In this context, both acceleration and caution are equally important, and achieving this balance is the responsibility of regulatory agencies. These agencies play a pivotal role in connecting consumers and industry, and their decisions and actions are crucial to all stakeholders. With targeted support and guidance, new testing systems can grow rapidly, and the resulting drug safety outcomes will directly benefit consumers. Regulatory institutions like OECD and the FDA are now promoting the adoption of alternative methods through a cautious yet proactive approach. They are supporting the expansion of accepted methods through symposia, research funding, and regulatory updates, ensuring that alternative tests are used safely and effectively across a broad range of applications.

Currently, animal testing remains necessary. Market reports predict that the demand for animal testing may even increase until the early 2030s. Many alternative models exist, but most cannot fully replace animal studies. Some fields are still difficult to reproduce using current technologies, including drug metabolism and PK or placental transfer studies. In addition, current alternative methods face several limitations, such as insufficient long-term validation data, poor inter-laboratory reproducibility, and difficulty in simulating complex developmental environments like maternal–fetal interactions. Moreover, challenges remain in modeling immunological responses, capturing systemic toxicity, and translating in vitro results to in vivo relevance. National and international projects led by regulators must continue. These efforts will improve the precision of alternative models. Test strategies should match the stage of product development and the population involved. Regulatory policies should adapt continuously, making it possible to combine traditional and alternative methods depending on the situation.

The move toward alternative testing is already occurring. This shift is due to ethical concerns and is supported by science, industry needs, and regulatory readiness. If these forces continue to work together, alternative methods can reduce animal suffering while supporting research and industrial growth.
