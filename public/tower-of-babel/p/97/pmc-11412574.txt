Evaluating the Effects of Perinatal Exposures to BPSIP on Hepatic Cholesterol Metabolism in Female and Male Offspring ICR Mice
Authors: Qi Wang, Shulin Gao, Baoqiang Chen, Jiadi Zhao, Wenyong Li, Lijun Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:A broad suite of bisphenol S (BPS) derivatives as alternatives for BPS have been identified in various human biological samples, including 4-hydroxyphenyl 4-isopropoxyphenylsulfone (BPSIP) detected in human umbilical cord plasma and breast milk. However, very little is known about the health outcomes of prenatal BPS derivative exposure to offspring.Objectives:Our study aimed to investigate the response of hepatic cholesterol metabolism by sex in offspring of dams exposed to BPSIP.Methods:Pregnant ICR mice were exposed to 5μg/kg body weight (BW)/day of BPSIP, BPS, or E2 through drinking water from gestational day one until the pups were weaned. The concentration of BPSIP, BPS, or E2 in the plasma and liver of pups was determined by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Metabolic phenotypes were recorded, and histopathology was examined for liver impairment. Transcriptome analysis was employed to characterize the distribution and expression patterns of differentially expressed genes across sexes. The metabolic regulation was validated by quantitative real-time PCR, immunohistochemistry, and immunoblotting. The role of estrogen receptors (ERs) in mediating sex-dependent effects was investigated using animal models and liver organoids.Results:Pups of dams exposed to BPSIP showed a higher serum cholesterol level, and liver cholesterol levels were higher in females and lower in males than in the controls. BPSIP concentration in the male liver was 1.22±0.25 ng/g and 0.69±0.27 ng/g in the female liver. Histopathology analysis showed steatosis and lipid deposition in both male and female offspring. Transcriptome and gene expression analyses identified sex-specific differences in cholesterol biosynthesis, absorption, disposal, and efflux between pups of dams exposed to BPSIP and those in controls. In vivo, chromatin immunoprecipitation analysis revealed that the binding of ERα protein to key genes such as Hmgcr, Pcsk9, and Abcg5 was attenuated in BPSIP-exposed females compared to controls, while it was enhanced in males. In vitro, the liver organoid experiments demonstrated that restoration of differential expression induced by BPSIP in key genes, such as Hmgcr, Ldlr, and Cyp7a1, to levels comparable to the controls was only achieved when treated with a combination of ERα agonist and ERβ agonist.Discussion:Findings from this study suggest that perinatal exposure to BPSIP disrupted cholesterol metabolism in a sex-specific manner in a mouse model, in which ERα played a crucial role both in vivo and in vitro. Therefore, it is crucial to systematically evaluate BPS derivatives to protect maternal health during pregnancy and prevent the transmission of metabolic disorders across generations. https://doi.org/10.1289/EHP14643

Introduction

The use of bisphenol A (BPA) has been severely restricted due to proven adverse health effects,1,2 leading to the widespread utilization of bisphenol S (BPS) and BPS derivatives as alternative compounds in consumer goods.3,4 In a broad suite of BPS derivatives, 4-hydroxyphenyl 4-isopropoxyphenylsulfone (BPSIP) has been found to occur at an increasing frequency compared to BPA and BPS, suggesting that its prevalence might have been previously underestimated.5,6 A recent study revealed unexpectedly high levels of BPSIP in all 60 maternal plasma samples from pregnant women in South China, ranking second only to BPA but with concentrations approximately 10 times higher than those of BPS and bisphenol AF (BPAF).5 Subsequently, seven BPS derivatives were detected in human breast milk samples obtained from lactating women within the general population of southern China.7 Surprisingly, the primary derivative, BPSIP, was detected in all samples and showed comparable levels to those of BPS. This information highlights the unavoidable exposure of humans to this recently discovered derivative through placental transfer and breastfeeding during the perinatal period. Furthermore, recent studies have placed increasing emphasis on the detrimental impacts of bisphenols not only on parental exposure but also on offspring, particularly during perinatal exposure.8–10

The perinatal environment is pivotal in shaping offspring’s health and disease outcomes. It establishes a connection between early life environmental influences and the susceptibility to chronic noncommunicable diseases (NCDs).11 Recent epidemiological and animal studies have indicated a potential association between perinatal exposure to BPS and the occurrence and progression of various NCDs, with particular emphasis on maintaining cholesterol homeostasis.8,12 For instance, long-term exposure to BPS increased liver cholesterol levels in male zebrafish13 and resulted in increased levels of cholesterol in the blood of male rats.14 BPS perinatal exposure induced increased plasma cholesterol levels in C57BL6/J female and male offspring fed a high-fat diet.8 These results support the notion that the critical developmental period, particularly during prenatal years, represents a highly adaptable phase characterized by heightened sensitivity to environmental factors.15 Given the structural similarities between BPSIP and BPS (Figure 1A), our profound interest is sparked to investigate whether and how exposure to BPS derivatives in vivo induces an imbalance in cholesterol metabolism.

Sex has been found to play a distinct role in influencing the toxic effects of bisphenols. For instance, a multi-omics study demonstrated that exposure to low doses of BPA during the perinatal period resulted in sex-specific disruption of the gut microbiota–liver axis in offspring mice.16 The impact of BPS exposure on liver lipid metabolism in rats during pregnancy and lactation exhibited sex-dependent variations.17 Perinatal exposure of mice to BPS induces sex-dependent obesogenic multigenerational effects in offspring.8 Evidently, these sex-specific outcomes associated with bisphenol exposure were attributed to the disruption of metabolic homeostasis in the liver. Emerging evidence suggests that hepatic metabolism differs fundamentally between male and female mammals, highlighting the significant role played by sex in liver function and suggesting the potential for sex-specific metabolic responses to external pollutants.18,19 Currently, an emerging theme in the regulation of hepatic metabolism is the involvement of estrogens and associated receptors. Prior human20,21 and animal22,23 works have demonstrated that the liver is a significant target for estrogens, and the transcriptional activity of hepatic estrogen receptors (ERs) is strictly associated with liver metabolism. Therefore, our broader objective is to comprehend the mechanisms underlying sex bias in hepatic cholesterol metabolism responses induced by exposure to BPSIP.

In the present study, by exposing pregnant mice to low doses of BPSIP through drinking water, we evaluated a wide range of metabolic parameters, conducted liver transcriptome analysis, and examined transcriptional regulation and translational control of key genes in both sexes to assess the effects of BPSIP on hepatic cholesterol metabolism. As a result, this study aims to bridge existing knowledge gaps regarding BPS derivatives while garnering appropriate attention from the public.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

BPSIP [CAS No. 95235-30-6, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) purity ≥98%], BPS (CAS No. 80-09-1, 99%), 17β-estradiol (E2) (CAS No. 50-28-2, ≥98%), and anhydrous ethanol (CAS No. 64-17-5, ≥99.5%) were obtained from Macklin Co., Lt.

Animals and BPSIP Administration

All 8-wk-old female ICR mice were purchased from Anhui Medical University and kept under a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment with a temperature of 22 to 24°C and 50% relative humidity for the following time. Animals were kept in polypropylene cages with a 12-h light–dark cycle and given a standard water and pellet diet. Glass water bottles were used to prevent exposure to bisphenols in plastics. The growth and reproduction compound feed, purchased from Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering Co., Ltd., contained 200g of protein, 50g of crude fiber, 40g of fat, and 100g of water per kilogram. The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Fuyang Normal University (animal experiment license No. SYXK-wan2021-002), and all animals were handled and euthanized according to the guidelines provided by the committee.

Virgin female mice were paired with male mice (2:1 ratio) to initiate mating. On the second day after pairing, gestational day 1 (GD 1) was confirmed by observing vaginal sperm plugs in pregnant females. Thirty-two pregnant females were randomly divided into four groups (eight mice per group): control, BPSIP, BPS, and E2. To minimize potential mechanical damage and stress reactions caused by oral gavage,24 we administered the chemicals to all pregnant mice through their drinking water. The chemical was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol and added to the drinking water at a final concentration of 0.001%. The control group received the same amount of ethanol. Exposure started on GD 1 and continued until weaning, ∼40 days later. Pregnant mice were handled gently and outcomes were recorded by the same technician to reduce variation.

The experimental design scheme is shown in Figure 1B. All pups were weaned on postnatal day 21 (PD 21) and maintained on a regular diet until they were euthanatized with pentobarbital sodium on PD 70. Pups within a given group were separated by sex and then randomly mixed post-weaning to minimize potential litter effects; up to four mice per cage came from different dams, respectively. Furthermore, the ear tags on the mice enabled individual pups to be easily identified and traced back to their respective dams. The pups used in each experiment were selected randomly and ensured to be from different litters. Throughout the study, no chemical-containing drinking water was provided to the pups.

Due to the lack of safety standards for BPSIP consumption, the dosage was chosen based on previous information about BPA and its analogs. The US Environmental Protection Agency and Food Drug Administration published a standard human tolerable daily intake (TDI) of 50μg BPA/kg body weight (BW)/day.25 Following a reassessment of human exposure to BPA in various groups of the human population, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) subsequently revised the TDI of BPA to 4μg BPA/kg BW/day and reported that women of reproductive age exhibited dietary exposures (0.388μg/kg BW/day) comparable to those observed in men within the same age group.26 According to a simple practice guide for dose conversion between animals and humans,27 the human dose of 0.388μg/kg BW/day can be converted to mice by multiplying it by 12.3, resulting in a dose of 4.8μg/kg BW/day. Furthermore, after considering other confounding factors and variables, a dosage of ∼5μg/kg BW/day was selected.

Dynamic Experimental Design

A dynamic experimental approach was used to adjust chemical concentrations in pregnant mice drinking water every 5 days based on their body weight and water consumption (Figure 1C). The 40-d exposure was divided into eight intervals of 5 d each and represented as a line segment. Separation points were numbered sequentially as “n” (n=1–8). Mn (g), Vn (mL), Cn (μg/mL), and En (μg/kg BW/day) were utilized to define body weight, water consumption, chemical concentrations in drinking water, and actual oral exposure, respectively. Initially, the daily water consumption for each dam was estimated based on the average unadjusted daily intake of ICR mice (∼11mL/30g BW/day),28 denoted as V0 in Equation I at position 1 (see Figure 1C). The initial concentration of chemicals in drinking water (C0) was calculated using Equation I at position 2 (see Figure 1C) based on expected oral exposure (5μg/kg BW/day) and V0. Adjustments for chemical concentrations were determined using Equation II (see Figure 1C) based on the dams’ mean body weight and mean daily water consumption. Equation III (see Figure 1C) was used to calculate the amount of chemicals ingested per kilogram of body weight by dams. Dams consumed more chemicals when they drank more water while nursing and their weight loss during birth led to higher chemical exposure per kilogram of body weight. Adjusting the dose before birth ensured dams were exposed to 70% of the expected amount of chemicals in their drinking water. Overall, dams’ oral exposure to chemicals remained within expected levels throughout the exposure period (Figure 1G).

Biochemical Measurements

The pups were fasted for 14 h and allowed ad libitum access to water before blood collection. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C and stored at −80°C until biochemical analysis. The levels of serum total triglycerides (sTG), total cholesterol (sTC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (sLDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (sHDL-C), and serum total bile acid (sTBA) were quantified using a Siemens ADVIA 2400 fully automated biochemical analyzer at the Medical Laboratory Center of Fuyang People’s Hospital. Cholesterol oxidase phenol 4-aminoantipyrine peroxidase (CHOD-PAP) method, glycerol phosphate oxidase-p-aminophenazone (GPO-PAP) method, surfactant (SUR) method, catalase (CAT) method, and enzyme cycle (SOP-TBA) method were used to determine levels of sTG, sTC, sLDL-C, sHDL-C, and sTBA, respectively. The CHO kit, TG kit, LDL-C kit, HDL-C kit, and TBA kit (100020030, 100020090, 100020248, 100020238, 100020275; Biosino) were utilized for this purpose. Serum very-low-density lipoprotein (sVLDL) was calculated using the Friedewald formula: sVLDL (mmol/L)=sTG/2.2. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were employed to measure the levels of E2, liver TC (livTC), liver TG (livTG), and liver LDL-C (livLDL-C) (SY-ELA00427, M03531, M08262, M04071; ShuangYing).

Histopathological Analysis

Histological analysis was conducted following the previously described protocol.29 The liver samples from 10-wk-old mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 6-μm sections using a Leica RM2235 ccwUS paraffin slicer. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Oil red O (ORO), and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), respectively, and analyzed under an Olympus BX53F microscope. Macrovesicular steatosis: graded from 0 to 3 based on the percentage of hepatocytes involved (0=<5%; 1=5–33%; 2=33–66%; 3=>66%).30 Hepatocyte ballooning was limited to three categories (0=none, 1=few, or 2=many) based on the reproducible cut-off points as described previously.31 ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/software/imagej/; National Institutes of Health) was used to quantify lipid and glycogen depositions in six biological samples from each sex in each group. Eight microscopic fields were randomly photographed for statistical analysis.

Determination of BPSIP, BPS, and E2 Concentration

Offspring plasma and liver BPSIP, BPS, and E2 levels were detected by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) on a Triple Quad 5500 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex), an Exion UPLC system (AB Sciex), and chromatographic separation using X-Bridge BEH C18 (2.1×100mm, 2.5μm; Waters Corporation). The SCIEX Analyst software was utilized for both data acquisition and processing. Plasma or liver homogenization was mixed with 1M ammonium acetate and 100 units/L β-glucuronic acid polymerase. The mixed samples were incubated overnight at 37°C, then treated with saturated sodium chloride solution and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was extracted using solid-phase extraction cartridges (SPE cartridges, Oasis HLB, one cc, 30mg; Waters Corporation) to obtain organic matter. To activate the cartridges, 3mL of HPLC-grade methanol (A452-4; Fisher Scientific) was initially used, followed by 3mL of double-distilled water (ddH2O). A 2-mL solution, composed of acetonitrile (A955-4; Fisher Scientific) and water in a ratio of 25:75 (vol/vol) was used for washing after the sample passed through the cartridges. Finally, elution (two steps of 4mL) was performed using 8mL of methanol after drying the cartridges. Instrumental information can be found in Excel Table S26. Five to six biological replicates were measured for each sex in every group, with each sample obtained from a different dam.

RNA Sequencing and Data Analysis

Liver samples were obtained from offspring randomly selected from litters of different dams (n=3/group/sex). Total RNA was extracted using the MiRneasy Mini kit (QIAGEN; Catalog No. 74104) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific NanoDrop 1510 spectrophotometer, while RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Only RNA with an RNA integrity number (RIN) score higher than 7.0 were used in subsequent experiments. A total of 24 cDNA libraries were constructed from samples of both sexes. Libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep kit (Vazyme; Cat No. NRM604-02) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end 150-bp sequencing was performed on an Illumina Novaseq6000 system by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. First, the read quality was assessed using FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc).32 Raw reads were first processed using fastp version 0.20.1 (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) software to obtain clean reads. The filtered reads were mapped to the GRCm39 reference genome using HISAT2 version 2.1.0 (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml) software.33 Then, fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) of each gene were calculated, and the read counts of each gene were obtained by HTSeq-count version 0.11.2 (https://htseq.readthedocs.io/en/master/htseqcount.html) software.34,35

R version 3.2.0 (https://www.r-project.org) software was utilized to perform principal component analysis (PCA), aiming to assess the biological replication of samples. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DEseq2 version 1.22.2 (http://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html) software.36 q-Value <0.05 and foldchange (FC) >2 or <0.5 was set as the threshold for significant DEGs. The expression patterns of genes in different groups and samples were demonstrated by performing a hierarchical cluster analysis of DEGs using R. In addition, based on the hypergeometric distribution, Wiki Pathways enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed to screen the significantly enriched term using R. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) biological pathways associated with hepatic cholesterol metabolism.37 GSEA was performed using GSEA version 4.1.0 (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) software.37,38 Fisher’s exact test was applied to evaluate the significance of overlapping genes and pathways between DEG sets and tested whether the predefined gene set was enriched at the top or bottom of the ranking list. A gene set was considered enriched when the nominal p-value was <0.05 and the false discovery rate (FDR) q-value was <0.25. Bioinformatic analysis was performed using the OECloud tools at https://cloud.oebiotech.com/task/. The raw data we obtained from RNA-seq has been submitted to the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE232892 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov).

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

The RNA extraction and quantification process is detailed in the RNA sequencing description. MonScript RTIII Super Mix with dsDNase (MR05201; Monad) and MonAmp TaqMan quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) mix (MQ30101; Monad) were utilized for reverse transcription and qPCR, respectively. Reverse transcription was conducted using a Bio-Rad T100 thermal cycler system with the following program: 37°C for 2 min, 55°C for 15 min, 85°C for 5 min, and held at 4°C. For the qPCR, a Bio-Rad CFX Connect Real-Time PCR system was used, and the quantitative reaction was performed under the following conditions: 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was calculated from the threshold cycle (Ct) values within the log-linear phase, as 2−ΔΔCt,39 and normalized to the corresponding housekeeping gene of beta-actin (β-actin) expression (fold change=2−ΔCt sample/2−ΔCt control sample). All primer sequences for the measured genes (Excel Table S30) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co. Ltd. and purified via HPLC.

Western Blotting Analysis

Liver proteins were quickly extracted using ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (P0013C; Beyotime) with phenylmethanesulfonylfluoride (PMSF) (ST506; Beyotime) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer. Protein concentrations were measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (P0012S; Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein (20–30μg) were then analyzed on 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to 0.2μm PVDF membrane. The membranes were blocked with blocking buffer (P0023B; Beyotime) and then incubated overnight at 4°C on a shaker with primary polyclonal antibodies [β-actin, PCSK9, LDLR, ERβ, ABCG8, HMGCR (AA128, AF7692, AF1438, AE905, AF7119; Beyotime); IDOL, USP2 (D126668, D161636; Sangon); ERα (GB111352; Servicebio); SREBP2 (PAB47973; Bio-swamp); and ABCG5, NPC1L1 (DF8401, DF8449; Affinity)] diluted in primary antibody dilution buffer (P0023A; Beyotime) at a ratio of 1:1,000. The membranes were incubated with 1:2,000 dilutions of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (GB23303; Servicebio) for 2 h at room temperature. Immunolabeled bands were visualized with a BeyoECL Moon kit (P0018FS; Beyotime) and scanned using a ProteinSimple FluorChem M system. ImageJ software quantified the staining intensities of the protein bands and normalized them to β-actin.

IHC and IF

Fresh liver tissue was immersed in a 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by gradient dehydration using ethanol ranging from 50% to 100%. Subsequently, the tissue was treated with xylene for transparency and waxed with liquid paraffin at 62°C before being finally embedded. The tissue was sliced into 3-μm-thick sections and then subjected to immunohistochemistry (IHC) using the super vision (SV) two-step method with a specific primary antibody diluted in primary antibody dilution buffer at a ratio of 1:100 after blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin. Tissue sections were washed and incubated with an anti-rabbit IgG-HRP kit (SV0001; Boster) at 37°C for 45 min, followed by color reaction using a DAB chromogenic reagent kit (AR1022; Boster) and reverse staining with Harris and Mayer hematoxylin (AR0005; Boster). For immunofluorescence (IF) staining, the liver slices were blocked with 10% sheep serum (AR10009; Boster) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 30 min at 37°C before being incubated overnight at 4°C with a specific primary antibody diluted 1:80 in primary antibody dilution buffer. They were then incubated for 45 min with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (BA1142; Boster) diluted 1:100 at 37°C. The slices were counterstained with DAPI (AR1176; Boster) for 3 min at room temperature and cover slipped for imaging. Fluorescence signals of the slices were visualized and imaged using a Leica Aperio Versa 8 imaging system and analyzed by ImageJ software. Fluorescence-positive cells were counted using a threshold set at 10% of the average intensity in 8-bit grayscale images. Six replicate biological samples were taken from each sex in each group, with eight random microscopic fields photographed on each film for statistical analysis.

ChIP-qPCR Experiments

ChIP experiments were conducted using a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay kit (Bes5001; Bersinbio) following the manufacturer’s instructions. As described previously,40 ChIP procedure was carried out by crosslinking tissue in PBS containing a protease inhibitor, phosphatase inhibitor, and histone deacetylase inhibitor at 4°C and 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. After being washed with PBS, the samples were lysed using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) buffer. They were sonicated for 45 s to obtain chromatin fragments of 200–800 bp using a sonicator. The sonicated chromatin was clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C and then resuspended in ChIP dilution buffer. Aliquots of chromatin were incubated with ChIP-validated rabbit anti-ERα antibody (1:20, P03372; Zenbio) or anti-rabbit IgG antibody overnight at 4°C with mild agitation, then mixed with protein A/G beads at room temperature for 30 min. The immunocomplexes were washed with various buffers and then eluted with 0.1M NaHCO3 and 1% SDS at 65°C for 45 min. The crosslinking reaction was reversed by overnight incubation at 65°C. Purified ChIP-enriched fragments were amplified using qPCR with region-specific primers. The primer sequences synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and purified via HPLC are listed in Excel Table S31.

3D Liver Organoid Culture

The reagent setup and organoid culture followed established methods.41 In part one, duct cells were isolated by mincing the liver tissue into small pieces and digesting it with a collagenase digestion solution. The resulting mixture was then centrifuged at 800 rpm for 5 min at 8°C. The isolated duct cells were subsequently washed with a cold wash medium. In part two, duct cells were seeded in Matrigel matrix (35248; Corning) and cultured in isolation medium for 3 d (37°C, 5% CO2) before switching to liver expansion medium. The expansion medium was replaced every 3 to 4 d.

In part three, organoids were passaged either after isolation or following 5 to 7 d of culture. The fragmented organoids were then resuspended in cold basal medium and collected through centrifugation at 800 rpm for 10 min at 4°C before being seeded in a 24-well tissue culture plate. In part four, organoids were cultured in liver expansion conditions for 3 d before being transferred to the differentiation medium. The differentiation medium was replaced daily with fresh medium for up to 9 d. During the following 3 d, the medium was supplemented with 3μM dexamethasone. Excel Table S28 presents the details of the reagents utilized for cultivating the liver organoids.

During the final 3 d of in vitro differentiation of organoids, they were treated with either dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) or BPSIP. During administration, DMSO or BPSIP in the medium were updated with daily replacement of fresh differentiated medium. BPSIP were dissolved in DMSO at concentrations of 50 nM, 5μM, and 50μM (1.5μL/well; 0.5% final volume DMSO). PPT (an ERα agonist, B6735; APExBIO) and DPN (an ERβ agonist, B6763; APExBIO) were dissolved in DMSO for organoids culture at working concentrations of 5μM. The working concentration of each agonist, PPT and DPN, was set at 2.5μM when combined.

Statistical Analysis

The data and statistical analyses comply with the recommendations on experimental design and analysis in pharmacology.42 All data were provided as mean±standard error of the mean (SEM). The normality of data was determined by the Shapiro-Wilk or Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The F test was used to assess the homogeneity of variance between samples. Unless otherwise specified, when normally distributed and homogeneity of variance assumption was met, we used Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc Tukey’s test to determine statistical significance between two groups or more than two groups, respectively. Statistical analysis and generation of graphs were performed with GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software) and Origin Software 9.8 (OriginLab). The level of significance was set at p<0.05. The statistical parameters can be found in the figure legends.

Results

Records of Physical Parameters and Pregnancy Outcomes

Pregnant mice exposed to BPSIP, BPS, or E2 for 40 d, including pregnancy and through weaning of pups, did not significantly differ in weight or water intake compared to controls (Figure 1D,E). Chemical concentrations in drinking water were adjusted every 5 d based on body weight and water consumption until offspring were weaned (Figure 1F), with intake levels fluctuating around expected levels of 5μg/kg BW/day (Figure 1G). The oral exposure doses of BPSIP, BPS, and E2 were below set thresholds (Table S1). Moreover, gestational weight gain (Figure S1) and pregnancy outcomes (Table S2) were not significantly different in the BPSIP, BPS, or E2 groups compared to controls.

Assessment of Physical Parameters; Biochemical Parameters; and BPSIP, BPS, and E2 Levels

Starting at 6 wk of age, the body weight of female offspring of dams exposed to BPSIP, BPS, or E2 was consistently higher than that of the controls (Figure 2A). Male offspring of dams exposed to BPS were significantly heavier than controls before 4 wk of age, while offspring of BPSIP-exposed mice were significantly heavier after 6 wk of age (Figure 2B). In males, liver weight and liver organ index were significantly higher in the BPSIP or E2 exposed group, but not BPS, compared to controls (Figure 2C,D). Histopathological analysis revealed the presence of microvesicular steatosis in the liver tissues of both females (3.81%, <5%, Grade 0) and males (8.32%, 5–33%, Grade 1) treated with BPSIP, along with ballooning injury (Few, Category 1) observed in males (Figure 2E) but no obvious differences in histology between the BPS and control groups. The lipid and glycogen depositions in the liver are shown in Figure S2 through ORO and PAS staining, respectively. Offspring of dams showed no significant differences in plasma BPSIP, BPS, or E2 levels compared to controls (Figure 2F–H). Compared to the controls, there were no significant differences observed in liver BPS and E2 levels, while higher levels of BPSIP were measured in the liver of males (Figure 2I–K). Biochemical analysis showed that males and females of dams exposed to BPSIP had higher levels of sTG, sTC, sLDL-C, sVLDL-C, and lower levels of sHDL-C compared to controls (Figure 2H,I). Males exposed to E2 had higher serum cholesterol levels. BPSIP-exposed mice had higher livTG levels in both sexes and higher livTC levels in females. Interestingly, females treated with BPSIP had higher livTC levels compared to controls, while males had lower levels (Figure 2J,K). No significant differences were found in serum and liver biochemical parameters between the BPS treatment group and the control group. Furthermore, offspring of dams exposed to BPSIP exhibited higher liver enzyme activity compared to the controls, along with a lower albumin-to-globulin ratio (stemming from both higher globulin and lower albumin), suggesting impaired liver function (Figure S3). BPS and E2 exhibited more variable differences in liver enzymes and protein levels; however, BPS exhibited albumin-to-globulin ratio very similar to BPSIP (Figure S3).

The synthesis of bile acids is a major pathway for cholesterol disposal,45 with cholesterol 7 alpha-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) serving as the rate-limiting enzyme in this process. Exposure to BPSIP only resulted in a higher expression level of Cyp7a1 in males compared to the controls, while exposure to BPS led to a higher expression level of Cyp7a1 in both sexes (Figure 5I,J). Evaluation of the mRNA and Protein Expression of LDLR, PCSK9, and IDOL in LDLR-Mediated LDL-C Endocytosis

LDLR plays a crucial role in the uptake of cholesterol by peripheral cells, while both inducible degrader of the LDLR (IDOL) and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) independently regulate its stability.44 Females had higher Ldlr and Pcsk9 expression after BPSIP exposure, while males had lower levels (Figure 6A,B). The expression levels of Ldlr, Pcsk9, and Idol were higher in males exposed to E2 than in the controls. WB analysis revealed that exposure to BPSIP resulted in significantly lower LDLR expression levels in both sexes compared to the controls (Figure 6C,D). Females exposed to BPSIP had higher PCSK9 expression, while males had higher IDOL expression compared to controls. E2 exposure also led to higher expression levels of PCSK9 and IDOL in males. Immunohistology revealed that females exhibited higher expression levels of LDLR compared to the controls, while males displayed lower levels (Figure 6E–H). Furthermore, immunohistology showed that females exposed to BPSIP had higher PCSK9 expression, while males exposed to BPSIP had higher IDOL expression compared to controls (Figure 6I–L). To further confirm the rationality of regulating IDOL execution, we analyzed the expression of ubiquitin-specific peptidase 2 (USP2), a deubiquitylating enzyme that interacts with IDOL and mitigates its efficacy in promoting LDLR degradation. The results indicated that the expression of USP2 was lower in the offspring of dams exposed to BPSIP, BPS, or E2 compared to those of the controls (Figure S9). In addition, sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2 (SREBP2) serves as a master transcriptional regulator of hepatic cholesterol metabolism by controlling the expression of LDL endocytosis genes, such as Ldlr and Pcsk9.46,47 However, our findings suggest that there were no significant differences in the expression of SREBP2 in offspring from the BPSIP-, BPS-, and E2-treated groups compared to the control group, except for male offspring from dams treated with E2 (Figure S10).

Investigation of the Expression of ERs and Key Genes Related to Cholesterol Metabolism in Offspring of Dams and Organoids

The dynamic balance of cholesterol metabolism relies on a complex interplay between various biological processes. Table 1 presents the differences in biochemical parameters, mRNA expression, and protein expression related to cholesterol biosynthesis, efflux, disposal, absorption, and LDL uptake between male and female pups of dams exposed to BPSIP, BPS, or E2 compared to those of dams exposed to controls. To further investigate the underlying mechanisms responsible for sex-specific cholesterol response, we utilized both in vivo and in vitro models. Exposure to BPSIP led to lower E2 levels in females but not in males compared to the control group (Figure S7). qPCR analysis revealed that exposure to BPSIP or BPS led to significantly higher expression levels of ERα in females than controls. In comparison, males exhibited significantly lower expression levels of ERα (Figure 7A,B). The protein expression levels of ERα were significantly higher in females treated with BPSIP, BPS, or E2 compared to the controls. Conversely, males exhibited a significantly lower expression level of ERα due to exposure to BPS (Figure 7C,D). To assess the potential activation or repression of specific gene promoters affected by BPSIP exposure, we performed a ChIP assay using chromatin extracted from pups’ livers. ChIP analysis revealed that the binding of ERα protein to key genes associated with cholesterol metabolism was attenuated in BPSIP-exposed females compared to controls. By contrast, it was enhanced in males (Figure 7E,F).

The observed effects of BPSIP on cholesterol metabolism in animal models may be attributed to its systemic impact on various organs or tissues, including the liver. Therefore, due to the complexity of the in vivo environment, determining the intra-hepatic efficacy of BPSIP and its impact on offspring mice remains challenging. To distinguish between the systemic and intra-hepatic biological effects of BPSIP, bile duct cells derived from mice livers were expanded into a 3D hepatic organoid, which served as an in vitro model (Figure 7G). Organoids exposed to a concentration of 50μM BPSIP had significantly smaller organoid, as determined by mean organoid diameter and percentage of organoids that were 0–200μm in diameter, compared to controls (Figure S8). However, no significant differences were observed in the gene and protein expression levels of ERα at a concentration of 50 nM compared to controls (Figure S8). The expression level of ERα in liver organoids was significantly lower than that in the controls at a concentration of 5μM BPSIP (Figure 7H). The observed differences in protein expression levels of ERα provide further support for the mRNA findings (Figure 7I). Organoids exposed to BPSIP exhibited differences in the expression of key genes associated with cholesterol metabolism. Specifically, compared to the controls, there were higher expression levels of genes Hmgcr, Ldlr, and Cyp7a1, while lower expression levels of genes Pcsk9 and Abca1 were observed (Figure 7J). However, organoids exposed to BPSIP and the ERα agonist, PPT, did not differ in the expression of these key genes, except for Pcsk9, compared to the BPSIP-treated group (Figure 7K). The results indicated that exposure to BPSIP led to significantly lower mRNA and protein expression levels of ERβ compared to the controls (Figure 7L,M). Similarly, organoids exposed to BPSIP and an ERβ agonist demonstrated similar expression of key genes related to cholesterol metabolism as those exposed to BPSIP alone, except Abca1 (Figure 7N). Except for Srebp2, the differentiated expression of these key genes was reversed in organoids exposed to PPT and DPN (simultaneous activation of ERα and ERβ) compared to the BPSIP-treated groups (Figure 7O). Among them, Hmgcr, Ldlr, and Cyp7a1 exhibited similar expression levels as the controls, while Pcsk9 and Abca1 showed significantly higher expression levels compared to both the controls and BPSIP treatment group.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first examination of the developmental toxicology of BPSIP in animals, a derivative of BPS commonly found in pregnant women,5 fetal cord blood,5 and breast milk,7 indicating a potentially wider distribution than previously recognized. Through measuring cholesterol levels and performing histopathological analyses, we observed differences in cholesterol levels, lipid deposition, and steatosis in the livers of offspring mice of dams exposed to BPSIP compared to controls. Additionally, we found a sex-dependent effect of BPSIP exposure on body mass gain, liver organ index, liver cholesterol level, liver lipid, and glycogen deposition. By combining liver transcriptomics with gene transcription and translation validation, we can understand how exposure to BPSIP affects cholesterol processes. This potentially explained the sex-specific liver cholesterol level and lipid deposition observed in offspring following perinatal exposure. Moreover, the findings from a combination of an animal model and 3D liver organoids helped support the hypothesis that ERα exhibited sex-specific regulation of key genes involved in cholesterol metabolism in vivo, while ERβ-assisted ERα-mediated activity directly contributed to the differential expression of cholesterol metabolism genes associated with BPSIP exposure in vitro.

A previous study showed that BPS and BPA had similar placental transfer efficiencies, while BPSIP had a lower efficiency.5 However, unlike the high retention of BPS in the placenta, minimal retention of BPSIP was observed, suggesting that a majority of BPSIP that has passed through the placenta can eventually enter the umbilical cord plasma.5 Additionally, the sulfone group in BPSIP can more effectively establish electrostatic and hydrogen bond interactions with human serum albumins, potentially attributed to the pronounced hydrophobicity of BPSIP.48 Subsequent studies have also confirmed these findings, revealing that concentrations of BPSIP in human breast milk were comparable to those in umbilical cord plasma (ranging from 1.0 to 90.7 pg/mL).7 In addition, BPSIP exhibited lower clearance efficiency in liver cells and a higher number of metabolites than BPS.49 In our model, there was no significant difference in the plasma levels of BPSIP between the offspring exposed to BPSIP during the perinatal period and the control group, which may be attributed to rapid metabolism and excretion of BPSIP in the blood circulation. However, male offspring of dams exposed to BPSIP exhibited higher BPSIP levels in the liver compared to the controls, potentially attributed to differences in the absorption, transport, clearance, biocompatibility, and metabolites of BPSIP in mice. These findings not only provide further evidence supporting the idea that offspring can be exposed to BPPSIP through placental transport and breastfeeding but also suggest that differences in BPSIP concentration levels between female and male livers might contribute to the emergence of sex-dependent effects.

Currently, research on BPA is extensive, while studies on BPS are increasing. However, the impact of maternal perinatal exposure to BPS derivatives on the metabolic health of their descendants is only beginning to be addressed. Available data suggest that specific time windows were periods of heightened vulnerability, and maternal exposure to bisphenols leads to impaired metabolic homeostasis in the offspring.50,51 And, high levels of TC or TG, especially LDL-C, have long been recognized as major risk factors for the development of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).52 In our study, both males and females exposed to BPSIP had significantly higher levels of sTC, sTG, and sLDL-C compared to the controls. Further histopathological analysis revealed microvesicular steatosis in the liver of both females and males treated with BPSIP, particularly males who also exhibited balloon damage and abnormal deposition of lipids and glycogen. These findings suggested that exposure to BPSIP increased the steatosis of the liver in offspring, especially males, which might lead to greater susceptibility to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formally known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease.53,54 In addition, previous studies have reported that aberrant cholesterol synthesis has been recognized as an important and self-governing risk for developing ASCVD,55 which also has been implicated as one of the putative driving factors of MASLD.56 As a result, our animal models suggested that perinatal exposure to BPSIP disrupted the cholesterol homeostasis in the liver of offspring, possibly increasing their susceptibility to developing MASLD.

The liver has been recognized as a pivotal metabolic organ, and its functionality is profoundly influenced by sex.57 Emerging evidence indicates that a substantial proportion of hepatic genes in humans or mice exhibit sexually dimorphic expression patterns. For example, genetic analysis of 100 mouse strains identified key genes primarily involved in hepatic lipid metabolism that display sexual dimorphism58; transcriptomic profiling and biochemical analyses in rodents demonstrated sex-dependent expression of ∼10% of transcriptionally active genes in the liver.54 These findings emphasize the significant role played by sex in liver function.16 Our transcriptome analysis provides a comprehensive picture of changes in the liver in response to environmental exposures, including the up-regulation and down-regulation of biosynthesis and efflux-related regulatory genes. Based on this, the changes in liver genome-wide expression and their contribution to liver cholesterol metabolism imbalance and functional impairment in BPSIP-exposed mice were comprehensively evaluated. Several vital biological processes of hepatic cholesterol metabolism in our data, including biosynthesis, absorption, LDL endocytosis, efflux, and disposal, were identified with significant differences in mRNA expression between female and male offspring, suggesting sex-specific effects of BPSIP exposure on offspring.

Interference with any of the major phases of cholesterol homeostasis may lead to abnormal cholesterol levels.20,59 As shown in Figure 8, our study revealed that while the serum cholesterol levels of both female and male offspring in the BPSIP-treated group were significantly higher than those in the control group, these differences could be attributed to distinct factors. In females, the dominant factors leading to higher sTC and livTC levels than in the control group may be primarily attributed to increased expression of key genes involved in liver cholesterol production, as well as decreased expression of genes responsible for cholesterol efflux. However, in males, the higher sTC level compared to the controls may primarily result from an up-regulation of genes associated with cholesterol absorption in the intestines. In comparison, the lower livTC level resulted from the decreased expression of crucial genes involved in biosynthesis and the increased expression of genes associated with efflux and disposal. Through gene and protein expression analysis of LDLR, PCSK9, and IDOL, it was discovered that both females and males exhibited significantly higher levels of sLDL-C than controls due to distinct regulatory mechanisms. In females, PCSK9 showed a higher expression level compared to the controls, whereas in males, IDOL exhibited a higher expression level than that observed in the controls. It is important to note that while both PCSK9 and IDOL can independently induce the degradation of LDLR, they utilize different pathways to carry out this operation.44 In general, these observed sex-specific differences indicated that the impact of BPSIP on the expression of crucial genes involved in cholesterol metabolism varied depending on sex.

Moreover, in our experimental system, we introduced BPS and E2 as comparative controls to enhance the clarity of our research. Neither male nor female mice born to dams exposed to BPS demonstrated differences in serum and liver cholesterol levels, liver organ indexes, and lipid and glycogen deposition in the liver. Compared with BPSIP, the differences in cholesterol outcomes in mice born from dams exposed to BPS compared to those born to dams exposed to control were also minimal. This underscores the significant and intricate effects of BPSIP on cholesterol metabolism compared to BPS, despite their considerable structural similarities featuring two phenolic rings linked by sulfur. E2 was included in our experimental design because of its significant role in elucidating the disparities in the susceptibility to various metabolic diseases between sexes.61 Many aspects of these sex differences in disease risk are influenced by the multifaceted actions of estrogens in the body,20 particularly in the liver. This relationship has been observed in both human and animal models.62–65 In our study, female mice born to dams exposed to E2 did not exhibit differences in cholesterol homeostasis. Still, it should be noted that mice of dams that received E2 supplementation had higher expression of the rate-limiting enzyme CYP7A1 involved in bile acid synthesis45 and higher serum total bile acid levels compared to those of the control group. This finding aligns with the prevailing notion that estradiol promotes liver secretion of cholesterol into bile,20 a phenomenon that has been substantiated through animal studies.66,67 Given the diverse mechanisms through which estrogen exerts its biological effects in the liver, it is plausible to suggest that the lower levels of E2 in BPSIP-treated females might serve as one of the triggers for cholesterol imbalance in females. However, our research unveiled a significant sex-specific impact of E2 exposure on cholesterol homeostasis, with males experiencing more pronounced adverse effects than females. These effects encompass abnormal body mass gain, elevated liver organ indexes, increased cholesterol levels, abnormal lipid deposition in the liver, and hepatic microvesicular steatosis.

In the classical genomic mechanism of ER-mediated transcriptional gene regulation, ERs bind to genomic locations based on estrogen response elements (EREs) of the DNA-binding domain to mediate the biological effects of estrogen in the liver.60 Scores of liver genes in humans and mice have been reported to be involved in cholesterol metabolism in an ER-dependent manner.68,69 For example, a functional ERE has been identified within the promoter of the HMGCR gene.70 Another functional ERE in the SREBP2 gene promoter has been found to which estrogen could specifically bind and induce transcription.71 Additionally, although promoters of some liver regulatory genes do not contain classical EREs, observations show that their promoter activity is activated by alternative mechanisms after estrogen stimulation,72,73 such as LDLR.74 Currently, the majority of BPS derivatives have been demonstrated in zebrafish to have agonistic or antagonistic activity against ERα or ERβ, which can either mimic or block the action of endogenous estrogen.75–78 After binding ER-ligand–binding domains with varying energetics and conformations based on ER-selective specificity,76,79 BPS derivatives might induce or inhibit the transcription of regulatory genes involved in cholesterol metabolism through ER-mediated estrogen signaling pathways. Our study utilized ChIP-qPCR analysis to confirm the sexual dimorphism in the regulation of key cholesterol metabolism genes by ERα. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the role of ERα as a novel connection between BPSIP exposure and cholesterol homeostasis.

In animal models, bisphenols exert metabolic effects on various target tissues, involving multiple molecular pathways and gene regulatory networks. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the observed sex-specific expression of key genes in cholesterol metabolism induced by BPSIP is systemic or intra-hepatic. Until now, in vivo models, such as total body ER knockout models, have faced challenges distinguishing between systemic and intra-hepatic estrogen effects.19 However, in vitro models are subject to various limitations. Primary liver cells isolated from tissues tend to lose their phenotype rapidly when cultured outside a physiologically similar microenvironment.80,81 Cancer cell lines commonly used in research, which have been passaged for extended periods, often exhibit various genetic, epigenetic, and functional alterations.82 As an alternative approach, embryonic stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells have been differentiated into hepatocyte-like cells. However, genetic and epigenetic changes can occur during the derivation and reprogramming processes.83 Organoids maintain a high level of stability at the chromosome and structural levels, preserving many genetic and epigenetic features of their normal counterparts.84 The advantage of organoids lies in their composition of multiple cell types, replicating the complexity of the tissues from which they are derived.85 Estrogens exert their physiological effects through ERα and ERβ, these ERs might regulate target gene transcription via separate pathways. It has been reported that in some ERα-binding DNA regions, ERβ recruits ERα by direct or indirect interactions, e.g., by making the region available to ERα by affecting chromatin state.86 Our findings not only demonstrate a direct association between BPSIP exposure and mRNA expression differences of crucial genes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis, efflux, disposal, and LDL uptake but also support the involvement of ERα and ERβ in this intra-hepatic process.

Collectively, we explored correlations among cholesterol levels, liver gene expression patterns, and the dynamic equilibrium of cholesterol de novo biosynthesis, absorption, disposal, and efflux. With over 10 derivatives having already been detected in biological samples, it remains unclear whether these bisphenols, which bear a strong chemical resemblance to BPS, exhibit more intricate and pronounced biotoxic effects than BPS. Moreover, simultaneous exposure to multiple BPS derivatives might result in different outcomes due to unpredictable mixed interference effects, which contrasts with the effects of exposure to BPS alone.87 Therefore, further systematic evaluation of BPS derivatives is important to safeguard maternal health during pregnancy, thereby potentially preventing the detrimental cycle of nongenetic inter- and trans-generational transmission of metabolic disorders.

Conclusion

The developmental period of life, especially during the prenatal and early postnatal stages, is a highly adaptable phase that exhibits heightened sensitivity to environmental factors. Exposure to environmental chemicals during these critical stages can lead to alterations in physiological functions. In our mouse model, BPSIP, as an emerging alternative to BPS, exhibited a more pronounced adverse effect on cholesterol homeostasis in the offspring’s liver compared to BPS. Importantly, some effects differed by sex; we hypothesize that they could contribute to the onset of NCDs, such as MASLD, in adulthood. To our knowledge, no studies have been conducted to evaluate the potential toxicological effects of perinatal exposure to BPS derivatives on mouse offspring.
