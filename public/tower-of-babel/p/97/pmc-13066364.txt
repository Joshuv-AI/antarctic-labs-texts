Mitophagy-driven prognosis in pediatric acute myeloid leukemia: a new frontier
Authors: Rajiv Ranjan Kumar, Uttam Sharma, Akshi Shree, Shilpi Chaudhary, Shuvadeep Ganguly, Radhika Bakhshi, Archna Singh, Jayanth Kumar Palanichamy, Ranjit Kumar Sahoo, Atul Batra, Surender K Sharawat, Deepam Pushpam, Anita Chopra, Archana Sasi, Sameer Bakhshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitophagy is a selective form of autophagy that plays a crucial role in mitochondrial quality control. Mediators of the mitophagy pathway have been reported to contribute to tumor progression in multiple solid tumors. Limited data suggests that the overexpression of certain pathway components may predict for adverse survival outcomes in adult AML. However, the role of these mediators in pediatric AML has not been studied previously. We identified nine mitophagy-related genes (PINK1, PARKIN, SQSTM1, NDP52, OPTN, ULK1, MAP1LC3B, FUNDC1, and BNIP3) using the Reactome pathway database and Ensembl genome browser. Gene expression for each of the nine genes was quantified using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction on bone marrow mononuclear cells in a retrospective cohort of 90 children (aged ≤ 18 years) with AML and 30 controls. Differential expression of these genes between patients and controls and across different molecular subtypes was assessed using nonparametric statistical tests. For external validation, transcriptomic data from children and young adults (aged ≤ 39 years) with AML and control samples from the Beat AML cohort were also analyzed. Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier estimates, and log-rank tests were used to evaluate associations between gene expression (upper versus lower quartiles) and relapse-free and overall survival in our patient cohort. A total of 90 children with AML patients and 30 controls were included. All nine mitophagy-related genes were significantly overexpressed in AML compared with controls, which was independently confirmed in the Beat AML cohort. Upregulation of all nine genes was consistent across most subgroups examined. Among these genes, only FUNDC1 overexpression was significantly associated with inferior relapse-free survival (HR = 2.039, p = 0.044). Mitophagy-related genes are upregulated across pediatric AML subtypes. FUNDC1 expression overexpression is associated with inferior relapse-free survival (RFS). Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most common acute leukemia in adults and accounts for a substantial proportion of leukemia-related morbidity worldwide1. In pediatric populations, AML represents approximately 17% of all leukemia cases2. Although there have improvements in survival over the last few decades, approximately 30% of patients continue to relapse despite receiving best available therapies3. Thus, enhancing therapeutic strategies through a deeper understanding of disease biology is crucial for improving outcomes in pediatric AML.

Leukemic stem cells (LSCs) in AML have unique metabolic dependencies that distinguish them from healthy hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and depend on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to meet their energy requirements4. Consequently, LSCs also require mechanisms to prevent the accumulation of harmful reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can trigger mitochondrial dysfunction and cell death. Mitophagy is a form of selective autophagy that prevents mitochondrial ROS accumulation and consequent cellular dysfunction5. LSCs have been shows to be highly dependent on mitophagy to maintain quiescence and avoid differentiation6. Mitophagy is broadly organized into the ubiquitin-mediated and receptor-mediated pathways. The former plays a major role in coordinating mitophagy during cellular stress conditions and is mediated by the mitochondrial surface proteins SQSTM1, OPTN and NDP52 and PINK1, along with the E3 ligase, PARKIN. The latter pathway is crucial for constitutive metabolic regulation and differentiation, and is facilitated by the receptors BNIP3 and FUNDC1, among others5. Knockout of SQSTM1 and OPTN have each been shown to lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive ROS generation, which in turn inhibits leukemic cell proliferation7,8. Elevated transcript numbers of both of these genes have been seen to be associated with inferior overall survival in AML datasets, including predominantly adult patients with AML.

Adult AML blasts are more likely to possess age-related somatic mutations in mitochondrial and nuclear DNA as compared to pediatric AML, which is more likely to have canonical structural alterations. Thus, the landscape of mitochondrial phenotypic and functional characteristics is likely to be different in childhood AML. We and others have shown that mitochondrial DNA copy number is elevated in pediatric AML blasts, consistent with high dependence on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)9–11. Notably, high copy number in this patient subgroup is also associated with inferior survival. Furthermore, we have previously observed that mitochondrial encoded genes required for OXPHOS are highly expressed in adverse risk subgroups of pediatric AML and carry prognostic value12. Thus, it is likely that pediatric AML blasts may also be functionally reliant on mitophagy for maintenance and proliferation. We conducted this study to examine the level of expression and prognostic impact of nine major mitophagy mediators-PINK1, PARKIN, SQSTM1, NDP52, OPTN, ULK1, MAP1LC3B, FUNDC1, and BNIP3 in pediatric AML.

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment and clinical sample collection

This retrospective observational study included consecutive children and adolescents (≤ 18 years of age) with newly diagnosed AML and registered at the medical oncology outpatient clinic of Dr. B.R.A. Institute Rotary Cancer Hospital, All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi, between July 2016 and December 2019. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC approval no. AIIMSA1864/22.08.2024) and conducted in accordance with IEC regulations and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Bone marrow samples were collected from patients with AML included in the study and also from controls. Informed consent was obtained from caregivers before sample collection, and assent were taken from all children > 7 years of age. Patients who had mixed phenotypic acute leukemia and acute promyelocytic leukemia were excluded from the study. For the purpose of this study, 30 treatment-naïve patients with solid tumors (Ewing sarcoma and retinoblastoma) who did not have morphologic evidence of metastatic bone marrow involvement on routine marrow exams done for staging at baseline were included as controls. All collected samples were kept in TRIzol in a -80-degree freezer until use.

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, hematologic parameters at baseline, clinical features at presentation, cytogenetic and molecular grouping and European Leukemia Net (ELN) 2017 risk group, were recorded for the patients13. All patients received intensive induction therapy care (with either 3 + 7 or ADE) followed by three cycles of high dose cytarabine consolidation (18 g/m2 per cycle)14. An additional cycle of intensive induction was administered in case remission was not achieved after one cycle. An allogeneic transplant was not performed at first remission as per institutional practice. Patients were tracked for relapse and survival status. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from registration at our center to the last follow-up or death. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from registration to relapse, death or last follow-up, whichever is earlier.

RNA Isolation, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

We used the Reactome pathway database and the Ensembl genome browser to identify and curate genes involved in the regulation of mitophagy. A total of nine genes were identified-namely: PINK1, PARKIN, SQSTM1, NDP52, OPTN, ULK1, MAP1LC3B, FUNDC1, and BNIP3. The expression of these genes was evaluated in pediatric AML samples using quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). Mononuclear cells were isolated from bone marrow aspirates by Ficoll-Hypaque (Sigma Diagnostics) density gradient centrifugation. Total RNA was subsequently extracted TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA concentration and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. The master mix containing random hexamer primers and oligo dT, buffer and reverse transcriptase enzyme was added to the RNA samples for cDNA synthesis. Reverse transcription was performed at 42 °C for 30 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 2 min. cDNA quality was assessed by amplification of the 18S rRNA housekeeping gene in all samples. The resulting cDNA was diluted 1:4 for subsequent applications. Primers for mitophagy markers were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool and synthesized by Eurofins Chemical Co (Bangalore, India). Details of the primer sequences used are provided in Table 1 ref.15. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the Light Cycler 480 II system (Roche, USA) with SYBR Green-based fluorescence chemistry. Relative gene expression levels of the nine pre-specified genes were calculated using the comparative Ct method (ΔCt method). Target gene Ct values were normalized to 18S rRNA (ΔCt = Ct_target-Ct_18S) and relative expression was calculated as 2^-ΔCt. For visualization, log10-transformed (2^-ΔCt) values were plotted as scatter plots. For external validation, we analyzed publicly available bulk trancriptomic datasets from the Beat AML database (https://datacatalog.ccdi.cancer.gov/dataset/Vizome-BEAT AML2.0) and compared the expression of the nine mitophagy-related genes in patients with AML with that in control samples (healthy pooled CD34 + and the bone marrow of healthy individuals). For this analysis, only patients aged ≤ 39 years were included in the study cohort.

Comparison of gene expression profile between patient and control groups

Gene expression levels expressed in the form of log10-transformed 2^-ΔCt were compared between patients in the AML cohort and controls in the retrospective database. Comparisons were also performed between patients in five AML subgroups (RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion, CBFB::MYH11 fusion, NPM1-mutated, FLT3-ITD mutated and complex karyotype) versus the control group. In parallel, for the Beat AML cohort, differential gene expression was assessed by comparing normalized RNA-seq expression counts between control and AML samples. Analyses were performed on log2-transformed normalized expression values were further extended to evaluate differences across AML molecular subtypes.

Prognostic value of elevated expression of mitophagy-related genes in patients with AML

For each of the nine genes, patients were stratified based on gene expression distribution within each cohort. In the retrospective cohort, log10-transformed 2^-ΔCt values were used, whereas in the Beat AML cohort, log2-transformed normalized expression values were analyzed. Patients in the upper quartile of expression were classified as high expressers, and those in the lower quartile were classified as low expressers. The association between gene expression level (high versus low) and survival (RFS and OS) were computed for the selected genes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0 (La Jolla, California, USA) and Stata version 19.0 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA). The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the expression of mitophagy-related genes between pediatric AML samples and controls in both the retrospective and Beat AML cohorts. Additional pairwise comparisons were performed between each AML molecular subgroup and control samples using separate Mann–Whitney U tests. To account for multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. Kaplan–Meier method curves were used for survival analyses, and log-rank tests were used to compare survival between high and low mitophagy gene expression groups. Data for follow-up were censored on 16 January 2026. Statistical significance was denoted using the following symbols: **** for p-values < 0.0001, *** for p-values < 0.001, ** for p-values < 0. 01 * for p-values < 0.05, and “ns” for non-significant results (p-values ≥ 0.05).

Prognostic value of overexpression of mitophagy-related genes

None of the nine mitophagy-related genes were seen to have a significant association with OS [Fig. 3A–I]. FUNDC1 overexpression was significantly associated with inferior RFS (HR = 2.039, 1.01- 4.08, p = 0.044). No associations were observed between the remaining eight genes and RFS [Fig. 4A–I]. We additionally sought to identify the prognostic value of FUNDC1 within AML patients harboring the RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion in our cohort. However, no association was found between FUNDC1 overexpression and inferior RFS in this subgroup (Fig. S3).

Discussion

In this study, we observed that genes encoding major mediators of mitophagy were significantly overexpressed both in children with AML in our cohort as well as among pediatric/young patients with AML in a publicly available dataset. Knockout of several genes that were evaluated in our study-PINK1, OPTN, NDP52, BNIP3 and SQSTM1-has been reported to lead to impaired mitophagy in AML cell-line based studies, highlighting their role in mitochondrial quality control7,8,16. We observed that the upregulation of these mediators at the trancriptome level was consistent across the ubiquitin and receptor-mediated mitophagy pathways and the core autophagy machinery. Developmental programming of mitophagy has been demonstrated in embryonic hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, which exhibit relatively high mitochondrial mass and OXPHOS17,18. Herein, mitophagy protects these cells from excessive ROS accumulation that occurs due to high metabolic demand. Thus, it is possible that the broad upregulation of mitophagy observed in our cohort may reflect age-related variations in blast mitochondrial programming superimposed upon leukemic transformation.

Our patient cohort comprised exclusively pediatric AML, of which around 40% harbored RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusions. Notably, this subgroup demonstrated relative overexpression across all nine mitophagy-related genes evaluated. Dependence of t(8;21)-driven AML on regulators of mitochondrial homeostasis has previously been reported in the context of epigenetic modulation19. BNIP3 and FUNDC1 were not upregulated in our patient cohort in the other subgroups studied. However, the smaller numbers of the patients in these subgroups may have limited our ability to derive meaningful conclusions. A prior TCGA analysis of adult AML noted SQSTM1 upregulation to be higher in AML harboring NPM1 mutations than those who were NPM1-wild type; however, no differences were observed between those with and without favorable cytogenetics or FLT3-ITD alterations8. In contrast, our cohort and the Beat AML analysis showed SQSTM1 upregulation across all molecular profiles studied. This suggests that enhanced mitophagy may be a unifying biological feature that transcends genomic heterogeneity in pediatric AML.

In our study, we observed that FUNDC1 gene overexpression was associated with inferior relapse-free survival. FUNDC1 has been reported to contribute to tumor progression in the context of colorectal20–22, cervical and breast cancer in cell-line-based studies. Accordingly, overexpression of this gene has also been seen to be associated with adverse survival outcomes in breast and cervical cancer21,22. Aside from its role in mitophagy, wherein significant functional redundancy exists, the pro-tumorigenic role of FUNDC1 may also be related to its involvement in mitochondrial reprogramming and maintenance of cellular plasticity23. Prior TCGA-based analyses examining the prognostic value of mitophagy-related genes in adult AML have identified SQSTM1 and OPTN overexpression to be associated with inferior overall survival7,8. On the other hand, overexpression of MAP1LC3B and BNIP3 have been reported to be associated with superior survival outcomes24. Differences in age and molecular subgroup distributions may explain the heterogeneity in the prognostic value of different mitophagy-related genes. AML in older age groups is enriched in myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)-related (MR) mutations, which harbor distinct biological characteristics25. Mutations in the splicing factor, SRSF2, are known to alter splicing of mitochondrial mRNA, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and activation of the ubiquitin-mediated wing of the mitophagy pathway26. Furthermore, the prognostic impact of mitophagy-related gene expression may also vary depending on the therapeutic context. Mitophagy has been shown to confer resistance to BH3 mimetics, and autophagy inhibition has been shown to be associated with enhanced sensitivity to cytarabine and to FLT3 inhibitors in studies using cell-lines and primary AML cells27–29. All the patients included in our retrospective cohort were treated using intensive cytarabine and daunorubicin-based chemotherapy regimens, allowing evaluation of prognostic rather than predictive impact of mitophagy-related genes. Notably, in our cohort, on subgroup analysis, FUNDC1 did not impact RFS in the patient subgroup harboring a RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion. Analyses using larger cohorts of specific AML subgroups may allow us to identify specific groups for which mitophagy-related gene expression may provide additional prognostic value.

To our knowledge, this is the first analysis of mitophagy-related gene expression and its prognostic relevance in pediatric AML. The study includes a cohort who received homogenous treatment, enabling clear interpretation of prognostic implications. Although the study was retrospective in nature and performed at a single center, the results are strengthened by the external validation of our gene expression profile findings with those recorded through analysis of an independent public dataset of pediatric and young adult AML. However, our study has a few limitations. Due to sample size limitations, only univariable survival modelling is reported, and multivariable analysis including other clinical characteristics was not performed. The study relies on gene expression profiles to quantify the level of activation of mitophagy and does not validate its findings at a proteomic or functional level.

In conclusion, our study shows that there is broad transcriptional upregulation of genes across different mitophagy pathways in pediatric AML. The consistent overexpression of mitophagy mediators across subtypes suggests that age-related differences in mitochondrial metabolism may contribute to inter-patient heterogeneity, beyond somatic genetic alterations within leukemic blasts. Overexpression of FUNDC1 was associated with inferior survival outcomes in our patient cohort. Future studies focussing on identifying therapeutic vulnerabilities within mitophagy pathways in leukemic blasts may uncover novel targets and expand the treatment armamentarium for pediatric AML.
