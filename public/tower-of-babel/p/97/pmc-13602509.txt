Sacroiliac joint abscess and osteomyelitis associated with wet cupping therapy: a case report
Authors: Bahar Kandemir, Rukiyye Bulut, Pınar Belviranlı Keskin, Esma Kepenek Kurt, İbrahim Erayman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWet cupping therapy (Hijama) is a widely practiced procedure in traditional medicine. When performed under non-sterile conditions, it can lead to severe infections. In this report, we present a case of sacroiliac abscess and osteomyelitis that developed following cupping therapy.Case presentationA 35-year-old male patient with no known systemic disease presented with a two-month history of lower back and right leg pain. Introduction

Cupping therapy is a traditional practice commonly used for pain relief, involving the use of specially designed cups, particularly in East Asia and the Middle East [1]. The origins of this procedure date back to the time of Herodotus and Hippocrates. In ancient times, it was widely employed for the treatment of various conditions, including back pain, gynecological complaints, and disorders of the extremities [2]. The proposed mechanism of action is thought to involve the extravasation of inflammatory mediators through negative pressure and stimulation of the autonomic nervous system; however, this mechanism has not yet been fully elucidated [3]. Cupping therapy is generally considered safe, although it has not been officially approved for the treatment of any medical condition. Nevertheless, when performed under non-sterile conditions, various complications, including life-threatening infections, may occur. In this report, we present a case of sacroiliac abscess that developed following wet cupping therapy.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old male with no known systemic comorbidities presented with a two-month history of persistent lower back and right leg pain. The pain was constant, exacerbated by movement, and resulted in significant gait impairment. There was no history of fever, trauma, or previous surgical intervention. Prior to admission, the patient had received various analgesic treatments for a preliminary diagnosis of lumbar disc herniation at external centers, without symptomatic relief. His medical history was negative for the consumption of unpasteurized dairy products, animal husbandry, alcohol or tobacco use, and the use of steroids or intravenous drugs. On physical examination, the patient was conscious and cooperative. While muscle strength in all extremities was intact and peripheral pulses were palpable, deep tendon reflexes were decreased in the right Achilles and right lower limb. Movement was severely restricted due to pain. Admission laboratory tests revealed a marked systemic inflammatory response: C-reactive protein (CRP) 303 mg/L (0–5), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 89 mm/hour (0–20), and a leukocyte count of 10,690/µL (79% neutrophils). Other markers included procalcitonin 0.3 µg/L (0-0.05), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 103 U/L (0–41), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 42 U/L (0–42). Common infectious etiologies were ruled out by a negative Brucella immunocapture test, and a 0 mm tuberculin skin test. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the pelvis provided a detailed characterization of a deep-seated, multifocal inflammatory process centered at the right sacroiliac joint. T2-weighted sequences demonstrated diffuse high signal intensity in the right sacrum and iliac bone, consistent with significant bone marrow edema and intense subchondral osteitis adjacent to the joint surfaces. Post-contrast T1-weighted images revealed a 30 × 18 mm, irregularly contoured lesion within the right sacroiliac joint, exhibiting peripheral contrast enhancement and internal liquefaction consistent with an abscess (Fig. 1). The inflammatory process extended anteriorly beyond the joint space into the iliopsoas muscle, and intramedullary abscesses within the bone structure. Upon detailed questioning, it was revealed that the patient had undergone wet cupping (Hijama) therapy approximately two months prior, performed by an unlicensed practitioner in a non-clinical setting. The procedure was applied to the right sacroiliac region. The patient reported the onset of right hip pain one week post-procedure, which progressed to walking difficulty within two weeks. Due to the small size and the multifocal, sequestered nature of the intramedullary and joint-space abscesses, surgical drainage was not performed. Empirical therapy was initiated with intravenous (IV) ceftriaxone (2 × 1 g) and teicoplanin (loading dose 2 × 400 mg, maintenance dose 1 × 400 mg). Microbiological investigation of two separate sets of blood cultures identified the pathogen as Methicillin-Sensitive Staphylococcus epidermidis. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing confirmed susceptibility to oxacillin, teicoplanin, vancomycin, ciprofloxacin, fusidic acid, and clindamycin, while resistance was noted to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Following the isolation of the pathogen, ceftriaxone was discontinued and therapy was maintained with teicoplanin. By the 15th day of IV treatment, the patient showed significant clinical and laboratory improvement: CRP 21 mg/L, leukocyte count 6,150/µL, and ESR 71 mm/hour. Following the resolution of fever and initiation of mobilization, the patient was discharged to complete a total of six months of antibiotic therapy, transitioned to oral moxifloxacin. At the end of treatment, CRP was 2 mg/L and ESR was 3 mm/hour. A control MRI confirmed full radiological recovery with no residual abscess or pathological contrast enhancement (Fig. 2). During a 15-month follow-up period, the patient remained asymptomatic with laboratory values within normal limits and returned to full physical activity.

Discussion and conclusions

Cupping therapy and bloodletting are among the oldest medical practices, utilized historically in both Western and Eastern medicine. Traditionally, wet cupping (Hijama) was believed to prevent disease by removing “bad blood” from the body [2, 4]. While dry cupping maintains skin integrity, wet cupping involves creating negative pressure followed by superficial skin incisions to draw blood [1, 2]. This technique poses significant infectious risks when performed by non-medical personnel. The use of non-sterile needles can lead to abscesses, wound infections, and the transmission of blood-borne pathogens such as hepatitis B, C, and human immune deficiency virus (HIV) [3, 5]. The involvement of an unlicensed individual in this case highlights a significant public health risk associated with unregulated traditional practices. Spontaneous sacroiliac abscesses are typically associated with risk factors like IV drug use, diabetes mellitus, or chronic immunosuppression [4, 5]. As our patient lacked these comorbidities, the infection was considered a direct complication of cupping therapy.

MRI played a pivotal role in the diagnostic workup. While initial evaluations with radiography and ultrasound were inconclusive, the superior sensitivity of MRI in detecting early bone marrow changes and deep-seated inflammatory processes was crucial. Early imaging facilitated the prompt initiation of empirical therapy, thereby preventing complications such as irreversible neurological deficits, sepsis, and mortality [5, 6]. In some cases, drainage of the abscess may be required as part of the treatment [5]. Regarding management, conservative therapy was prioritized over surgical intervention. The multifocal distribution of intramedullary and iliopsoas involvement suggested that invasive drainage would be technically challenging and potentially incomplete. In the absence of neurological deficits, a prolonged, targeted antimicrobial strategy was deemed the safest clinical path. While the literature reports a mean treatment duration of 11 weeks for similar infections, we intentionally extended the regimen to 24 weeks [5]. This cautious approach was necessitated by the presence of sacral and iliac osteomyelitis and the lack of surgical debridement, aiming to ensure definitive eradication and prevent chronic relapse. The 15-month relapse-free follow-up confirms the long-term durability of this strategy. What distinguishes this case from the existing literature is the isolation of S. epidermidis as the causative agent in a completely immunocompetent host without prosthetic hardware or prior spinal surgery. Furthermore, our successful management with a 24-week conservative antimicrobial regimen demonstrates that prolonged, targeted therapy can ensure definitive eradication in complex, multi-site involvements where surgical drainage is technically restrictive.

In conclusion, practitioners of cupping therapy must adhere to safety standards, including proper hand hygiene, disinfection of the application area, use of single-use needles, and sterilized medical equipment. It is especially important to recognize that wet cupping therapy may lead to potentially serious complications. Symptoms such as back pain and gait disturbances following cupping therapy should prompt consideration of musculoskeletal infections and related complications, which must be diagnosed early.
