Prevalence of malaria infection and associated factors among children under five years attending health facilities in the Nandom Municipality, Upper West Region, Ghana
Authors: Stephen Boadu, Cyril Kwami Azornu, Wisdom Axame, Emmanuel Osei Bonsu, Rukaya Wumnaya, Sebastian Yidana, Williams Azumah Abanga, Lydia Sarpomaa Asante, Michael Rockson Adjei, Chrysantus Kubio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOver the years, malaria has remained a significant public health concern, particularly among children under five, with sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bearing the greatest burden due to its high morbidity and mortality rates. Despite the increasing number of cases among this vulnerable age group in Nandom Municipality, there is a paucity of information on the prevalence and associated factors of malaria for planning and evaluation of elimination strategies. This study, therefore, investigated the prevalence of malaria parasite infection and its predictive factors among children under 5 years in the Nandom Municipality.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted in six health facilities in the Nandom Municipality in July 2024. Children with febrile illness and their caregivers were systematically sampled and interviewed. Both malaria Rapid Diagnostic Test (RDT) and microscopy examinations were conducted on each eligible child. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were done, using malaria test positivity through microscopy as the outcome variable, to identify factors associated with under-five malaria at a significance level of 0.05. Results were presented in tables and graphs.ResultsOf the total, 404 children with febrile illness enrolled in the study, those who tested positive for malaria were 125 (30.9%; CI 26.6‒35.6), through RDT and 105 (26.0% CI 21.9‒30.5) for microscopy. Children from the Gengenkpe sub-municipality were 4.58 times more likely to test positive for malaria compared to those in the Baseble sub-municipality (aOR: 4.58; 95% CI 1.58‒13.27). Children who did not sleep under insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs) the night before the survey were 2.01 times more likely to test positive for malaria compared to those who slept in ITNs (aOR: 2.01, 95% CI 1.02‒3.93). Children whose caregivers travelled over thirty minutes to a local pharmacy shop to access antimalarial medications had greater odds of malaria compared to those who used less travel time (aOR: 2.19, 95% CI 1.08‒4.45).ConclusionMalaria prevalence among under-five children attending healthcare facilities in Nandom Municipality was 26%, which is high compared to the 2024 Ghana National Malaria Elimination Programme target of 9.7%. The prevalence is associated with the sub-district of residence, ITN usage and travel time to the local pharmacy shop. Continuous education on ITN usage is needed among caregivers on malaria during community durbars, outreach sessions, and media advertisements to reduce the burden of under-five malaria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05584-3.

Background

Malaria still poses a serious threat to life and the risk of infection is heightened, particularly among infants and children under 5 years [1]. Malaria prevention involves measures like vector control and chemoprophylaxis. Adequate treatment can halt the progression of mild cases [1]. However, when untreated, malaria can swiftly deteriorate to severe illness and, in certain cases, result in death within 24 h [2, 3] Among the Plasmodium parasite species responsible for causing malaria in humans, Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax stand out as the most menacing. Plasmodium falciparum is the most virulent malaria parasite and is highly prevalent across the African continent [3].

The global malaria epidemiology is evolving, with increasing recognition of the influence of various megatrends on the disease. In 2023, an estimated 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 malaria-related deaths occurred worldwide, with 95% and 94% of the morbidity and mortality burden reported in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). Of the malaria-related deaths reported in SSA, 76% occurred among children under 5 years [4] Factors like escalating deforestation, rapid economic growth, urbanization, and the effects of climate change are anticipated to alter transmission patterns. Moreover, these trends underscore the urgency to optimize the coverage of interventions to address these shifting dynamics and mitigate the escalating impact of malaria [5].

It has been stipulated that all suspected malaria cases require confirmation through parasite detection methods like quality-assured microscopy or antigen-detecting rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) [6]. Both public and private healthcare sectors are expected to have the necessary equipment and resources to confirm the diagnosis before administering antimalarial treatment. This ensures accurate identification of malaria cases, enabling appropriate and targeted treatment for confirmed infections while preventing unnecessary use of antimalarial drugs in cases where malaria is not present [7].

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the deployment of three primary vector control interventions for combating malaria. Mainly, the use of Insecticide-Treated bed Nets (ITNs), Seasonal indoor residual spraying of insecticide (IRS) and Larviciding. These interventions have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing malaria transmission by targeting the vectors, primarily mosquitoes, responsible for spreading the disease. Both strategies play vital roles in malaria prevention efforts worldwide [6]. However, in controlling malaria in small communities with limited resources, such as the lack of routine distribution of ITNs and IRS services. Larval Source Management has proven to be efficient in reducing the mosquito population and subsequently the risk of malaria transmission in the community [8]. Malaria chemoprevention is also implemented in the Nandom Municipality for children under 5 years of age by administering sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine and amodiaquine (SPAQ) during the start of the rainy season, following WHO recommendations in 2012 [9]. All under-5-year malaria cases are treated using the approved artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) regimen. Additionally, Intermittent Preventive Treatment (IPTp) for pregnant women is implemented in the municipality to prevent malaria in pregnancy and intrauterine malaria transmission [5]. Yearly IRS and routine distribution of ITN for pregnant women and mothers are also implemented [5, 10]. Despite all these efforts, the Upper West Region of Ghana had the second highest prevalence of (13.4%) under-five malaria according to microscopy results [11]. Nandom Municipal, being part of the 11 Districts in the Upper West Region, is also a malaria-endemic area; nevertheless, there is a paucity of literature on the burden and determinants of malaria infections among children under 5 years of age in Nandom. This study provides a comprehensive understanding of malaria infection in children under 5 years of age, considering both individual and institutional factors, to better inform targeted interventions and improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and population

A cross-sectional study was conducted among caregivers and their paired children, residents of Nandom Municipality in the Upper West Region, Ghana, from 1st July to 31 st July 2024. A mother or legal guardian with a child under 5 years of age who presented with febrile symptoms, was considered a permanent resident in Nandom for a minimum of 6 months and expressed willingness to take part in the study during their visit to the health facility, was included in the study. However, among the eligible, those who declined to be interviewed were excluded and children who presented with febrile symptoms and were diagnosed with comorbidities were excluded from the study. Participation in the study was voluntary and respondents were not offered financial or in-kind incentives.

Study area

The Nandom Municipal area is situated in the northwestern part of the Upper West Region of Ghana, spanning from latitude 50° 18W–500° 10 W and longitude of 10° 20N–20° 25N. It shares borders with the Lambussie District to the East, Lawra Municipal to the south, and Burkina Faso to the north and west. According to the 2021 Population and Housing Census, the total population of the municipality was 51,328, comprising 25,577 males (49.8%) and 25,751 females (50.2%), with a growth rate of 2.1%. Notably, there are approximately 10,266 children under the age of 5 years [12].

The municipality has one primary hospital (St. Theresa’s Hospital), one polyclinic, four health centres, and three private clinics. The municipality currently has 42 demarcated Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) zones, out of which 20 have compounds. Only the St Theresa’s Hospital, Nandom Health Centre and Ko Polyclinic have laboratory capacity [13]. However, due to logistical constraints at the time of the study, Ko Polyclinic was unable to conduct blood film microscopy for malaria. The malaria rapid diagnostic test (RDT) was carried out at all service delivery points, including the private facilities and the 20 CHPS compounds. All community health officers had been trained on how to conduct quality-assured RDTs using malaria diagnostic test kits. The St. Theresa’s Hospital has five Laboratory Scientists and seven Laboratory Technicians. The hospital laboratory is equipped with six light microscopes and adequate reagents for blood film (BF) microscopy examination. Nandom Health Centre has one laboratory technician and one light microscope with adequate reagents for BF microscopy examination.

Sample size and sampling of participants

The estimated sample size of 404 participants under the age of 5 years was calculated based on an assumed prevalence of 50%, a 95% confidence level (Zα/2 = 1.96), a 5% margin of error (d), and an adjustment for a 5% non-response rate, using the following formula for sample size determination. The sample size was calculated from the following Cochran formula [14]. Systematic sampling was employed to enroll participants in the study. The health facilities selected for this study were purposively chosen. The primary hospital, which serves as the only referral facility and the largest health service provider in the study area, was purposively selected. The other five health facilities were selected based on malaria case load reported among children under 5 years in 2023. Thus, in each sub-municipality, the health facility with the highest malaria cases was purposively selected for this study. The sample was proportionately assigned to each health facility based on the total malaria cases under 5 years recorded in 2023. This was done by dividing the malaria under 5 years case load in the respective health facility by the total recorded malaria cases (recorded by the six health facilities in 2023) multiplied by the sample size (404).

Data collection

Five nurses were trained to build capacity and ensure optimal adherence to the study protocol for data collection and blood sampling for microscopy. The principal investigator also trained the two data collectors on how to administer the questionnaire using an electronic-based data collection tool (KoboCollect) to the respondents. The study tool was piloted in a community under Yirpelle CHPS to validate and finalize it before being used in the study area. The questionnaire was administered to the 404 eligible participants, whether male or female, present at the study facility site, who gave consent. The data collection team members were trained to be able to translate essential study information into both English and Dagaare, the indigenous language. This enabled the team to inform participants of relevant information to enhance informed consent. To increase the diversity of the sample, at the beginning of the data collection at each site, every third successive eligible child under 5 years who presented febrile symptoms was enrolled until the facility sample quota was exhausted. Children with febrile illness and their caregivers were systematically sampled and interviewed. Both RDT and microscopy examinations (the gold standard in this study) were conducted, using standard procedures, on each eligible child.

Study variables

The dependent variable was testing positive for malaria microscopy and the independent variables were sociodemographic factors (child: age, sex, and weight. guardian/mother: age, sex, educational level of caregiver, occupation, health-seeking behaviour. Institutional and malaria service utilization factors were distance to the health facility, affordability of treatment, ITN ownership, ITN usage and IRS patronage.

Data analysis

The collected data from the electronic-based tool were downloaded, transferred, and analyzed using Stata Version 17 software (Stata Corp, College Station). Descriptive statistics were performed to generate frequencies and proportions. Subsequently, logistic regression was done, using malaria test positivity through microscopy as the outcome variable, to determine factors related to malaria test positivity. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent factors associated with the test outcomes while accounting for confounding variables. This analysis estimated odds ratios and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. A p-value of 0.05 was deemed statistically significant in determining associations between independent variables and malaria test positivity.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study population

A total of 404 children with paired caregivers participated in the study. The sociodemographic characteristics of caregiver and their paired children have been presented in Table 1. The results revealed that most caregivers, 75% (303/404), were in the age group, 20 to 29 years, with a mean age of 26.5 years and a standard deviation of 3.7 years. Among the sub-municipals of residence of the caregivers, a little below half (41.8%, 169/404) were staying in the Nandom sub-municipality. With regards to religious affiliation and marital status, 76.2% (308/404) of the respondents were Christians, followed by Muslims, 23.8% (96/404). A greater number (70.1%, 283/404) of the caregivers were married. Most (41.8%, 169/404) of the respondents had a minimum of secondary school education, followed by tertiary education, 29.2% (118/404). A little over one-third (35.9%, 145/404) of the respondents were unemployed and, 25.0% (101/404) were public sector employees. More than half (52.5%, 212/404) of the respondents had one child under 5 years. Most (54.7%, 221/404) children enrolled in the study were in the age group of 24–59 months. Most (62.1%, 251/404) of the sampled children were male. Institutional characteristics and malaria service utilization among children under 5 years of age in Nandom Municipality

The result revealed that 38.4% (155/404) of children under 5 years sleep under ITNs throughout the year and 35.4% (143/404) use ITNs only during the rainy season. From the study, 69.6% (281/404) of the respondents had easy access to transport from home to the health facility. Socio-demographic predictors of malaria parasite infection among children under 5 years

The risk of malaria infection was twice as high among caregivers with children aged 24–59 months compared to those with children 0–11 months of age (cOR: 2.22, 95% CI 1.21, 4.08; p = 0.010). From the adjusted regression analysis, children from the Gengenkpe sub-municipality were 4.58 times more likely to test positive for malaria compared to those in the Baseble sub-municipality (aOR: 4.58, 95% CI 1.58‒13.27; p = 0.005)., However, the age of children was not significantly associated with malaria infection after the adjusted regression analysis (aOR: 1.96, 95% CI 0.91‒4.22; p = 0.084) (Table 3). Association between institutional and malaria service utilization factors and malaria parasite infection among children under 5 years

Caregivers whose children did not sleep under ITN the night before the survey were 2.69 times as likely to test positive for malaria compared to those who used ITN (cOR: 2.69, 95% CI 1.48–4.86; p = 0.001). Subsequently, caregivers whose travel time to the nearest community pharmacy shop was greater than thirty minutes were 2.59 times at a greater risk of malaria than those who used less time (cOR: 2.59, 95% CI 1.54‒4.35; p < 0.000). From the multivariate regression analysis, caregivers whose children did not sleep under ITNs the night before the survey were 2.01 times as likely to test positive for malaria (aOR: 2.01, 95% CI 1.02‒3.93; p = 0.042), compared to those who slept under ITNs the previous night before the survey. Children of caregivers who walked for more than thirty minutes to access treatment at the nearest community pharmacy shop were 2.19 times as likely to test positive for malaria compared to those who used less travel time (aOR: 2.19, 95% CI 1.08‒4.45; p = 0.028) (Table 4). Discussion

From this study, the overall prevalence of malaria among children visiting health institutions in the Nandom Municipality was 30.9% for mRDT and 26% for microscopy. This study’s malaria prevalence is similar to the 2022 Ghana Demographic Health Survey report, where the Upper West Region had a prevalence of 30.2% for mRDT among children under 5 years [11]. However, the prevalence of malaria under 5 years by microscopy was higher than the GDHS report in the Upper West region. The National Malaria Elimination Programme Strategic Plan 2024 −2028 has a positivity target of 9.7 for the year 2024 [15]. Conversely, in this study, a prevalence of 26% is higher than the 2024 positivity target. This study’s microscopy results are comparable to a study conducted in the Arba Minch “Zuria” district of South Ethiopia, where they recorded a 22.1% prevalence [16]. Also, the Ziquala District, Northeast Ethiopia, shares a similar overall prevalence of 24.6% [17]. Orish et al. [18] also recorded a 21.1% prevalence of confirmed malaria among children under five based on laboratory tests in the Western Region of Ghana. In contrast, the prevalence in the current study shows a lower prevalence in comparison to studies in Jasikan, Ghana, 61.5% [19] Malawi 37% [20], Sierra Leone, 52.67% with RDT and 40.05% with microscopy [21], and in Ghana, Ho, 59.6% overall prevalence of malaria [22].

This study was conducted in the major malaria transmission season in the region, which spans from June to October and the first cycle of Seasonal Malaria Chemoprevention for children under 5 years had already taken place in June and Indoor Residual Spray was also conducted before the start of the major transmission season [23]. The high prevalence of malaria in this study area suggests that there is still a gap in the elimination strategy of the National Malaria Elimination Programme in Nandom Municipal.

In this study, the sub-municipality of residence was significantly associated with malaria among children under 5 years. Children from the Gengenkpe sub-municipality had more than fourfold increase in odds of testing positive for malaria compared to children in the Baseble sub-municipality. This might be due to the availability of conducive vector-breeding conditions in the Gengenkpe sub-municipality, which is considered a rural area compared to Baseble, which is peri-urban. The practice of backyard farming in rural communities is associated with the pooling of rainwater on leaves of plants/crops and ridges (beds) on farms, providing suitable breeding places for mosquitoes [24]. This could be linked to the greater risk in Gengenkpe sub-municipality (a rural area) compared to urban areas, where backyard farming activities are not common.

Rural areas have more vegetation, providing mosquitoes with shelter and breeding sites, and often have more stagnant water sources, such as irrigation dams, which enable mosquito breeding [25]. Rural areas also have warmer temperatures and higher humidity, ideal for mosquito survival than peri-urban communities [26]. Furthermore, findings in this study were consistent with findings in studies in Zambia, Burkina Faso, and Rwanda. The study also found that children residing in rural areas had higher odds of malaria compared to those in urban areas [27–29]. Contrary to the observations of this study, findings from Ethiopia were contradictory and indicated that the residence of children was not significantly associated with malaria among children under five [17]. This may be due to study design, study period (season of high malaria transmission where data was collected) and type of malaria testing method.

The study found that most caregivers use mosquito nets regularly throughout the year, with others doing the same during rainy seasons. This observation could have resulted from the data collection period when the rainy season had just begun, which might have influenced the high usage of ITN among caregivers. Further analysis revealed that children who did not sleep under ITNs the night before the survey had more than twofold increase in odds of malaria than those who used ITNs. This emphasizes the essential need for the use of ITNs among caregivers with children under 5 years to offer protection against mosquito bites. Contrary to the study in Ghana by Afoakwah et al. [30] which found no significant association between ITN usage and malaria. In this study, there was no clear differentiation between ITNs and long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs). This study simply enquired if the caregiver uses an ITN, but the respondent may not be well informed about the difference.

The study confirmed findings consistent with other literature that children who sleep under mosquito nets are often protected against malaria [31–34, 37]. The observation confirms the WHO findings that consistent usage of ITN provides optimal protection for mothers and children [10]. Similar studies in Ethiopia and Nigeria reported findings consistent with this study’s findings [16, 35, 36]. This might have been due to the frequent ITN distribution to pregnant mothers and a repeated supply at 18 months when the child is due for the measles second dose vaccination. Also, the reason for the high usage could be a result of the freely distributed ITN to caregivers since 1998 [37]. However, the current finding was in contrast with a study done in Hohoe, Ghana, where no evidence was found among children who sleep under ITN compared to those who do not [38].

In this study, children of caregivers who walked more than thirty minutes to the nearest community pharmacy shop had higher odds of testing positive for malaria. Similar findings were observed in other African countries, such as Uganda, West Ethiopia, and Southeast Nigeria, which also unravelled findings that are in line with the current research [39–41]. The reason for this finding could have been that caregivers who spend considerable time and money travelling to the nearest community pharmacy shop often self-medicate or treat their children themselves. Longer walking time leads to delays in seeking medical attention at the health facility when self-medication fails, which might lead to severe forms of malaria in children. More so, further analysis indicated that caregivers who take more than thirty minutes to arrive at the nearest community pharmacy shop have more than twofold increase in risk of malaria than those who use less time.

Limitations

Healthcare accessibility is a complex interplay of factors, and solely relying on participant-reported assessments of distance to health facilities might present challenges in accurately gauging true accessibility. Therefore, this study’s findings may not be universally applicable due to its sample being limited to individuals seeking treatment at health facilities. Similarly, while the use of LLINs is crucial in malaria prevention, conclusions based solely on the previous night’s use might not capture consistent usage patterns. The study’s cross-sectional design restricts the identification of long-term trends. The use of febrile children seeking care at health facilities to determine the prevalence of malaria may have underestimated the actual burden of malaria infection in children under five. Community-based studies may be needed to help estimate the true magnitude of malaria among children under 5 years. Furthermore, relying on facility-based interviews might influence participants’ responses, potentially skewing data due to social desirability bias or altered perceptions in a healthcare setting. Therefore, a comprehensive approach that combines various data collection methods and considers potential biases was essential to ensure a more accurate understanding of healthcare access, disease prevalence, and preventive measures in public health assessments.

Conclusion

Malaria prevalence among under-five children attending healthcare facilities in Nandom Municipality was high and was associated with the sub-district of residence, ITN usage and travel time to local chemical shops. This study highlights the urgent need to alleviate the malaria burden among children under five in Nandom Municipal. The National Malaria Elimination Programme should maintain the distribution of ITNs to pregnant women and children under 5 years to enable them to continue all year-round usage of ITNs. When delivering health education to mothers and caregivers, healthcare workers in the Nandom Municipal and its partners should stress the importance of all children, both younger and older, sleeping under mosquito nets each night. The Municipal Health Directorate should provide or strengthen health outreach services such as home visits and community health screening to minimize the distance people must travel to receive care. Further community-based studies are needed to help examine socio-cultural and environmental determinants of malaria infection in the Nandom Municipality.
