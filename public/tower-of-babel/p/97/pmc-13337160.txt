Longitudinal Changes in the Impact of Socioeconomic Status on Graft Failure in Kidney Transplantation
Authors: Rikako Oki, Saleh Al-Juburi, Ingrid Rocha, Susan Wright, Atsushi Nishimagi, Luckshi Rajendran, Emily Kerby, Dean Kim, Atsushi Yoshida, Marwan Abouljoud, Rohini Prashar, Shunji Nagai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

It is well known that kidney transplantation (KT) is the most effective treatment option for patients with advanced CKD or ESKD, offering lower mortality and dramatic improvements in quality of life and health status, compared with chronic dialysis treatment.1 One-year graft survival (GS) now surpasses 90% in the United States, along with the advancements in immunosuppressive protocols, surgical techniques, and post-transplantation care improvements.2,3 The improvements in long-term survival have also been observed, showing that median survival for deceased donor kidney transplantation (DDKT) increased from 8.2 years in the era 1995–1999 to an estimated 11.7 years in the era 2014–2017.4 This improvement in GS was particularly notable among recipients with diabetes or hypertension compared with those with other causes of kidney disease and older recipients compared with younger recipients.

However, socioeconomic disparities in transplantation outcomes still exist despite medical progress. Socioeconomic factors encompass various aspects, including income level, employment status, level of education, cultural and discriminatory factors, and accessibility to health care. Goldfarb-Rumyantzev et al. reported that college and post-college education were associated with better graft and patient survival (PS) in the whole population.3 In addition, having private insurance was significantly related to better GS. Compared with KT recipients working full time at the time of transplantation, those not working by choice had an association with decreased GS time.5 Even if the transplant surgery itself goes smoothly, long-term management afterward is essential for keeping organ function. Geographic location also contributes to disparities in transplant outcomes. As one of the other indicators for socioeconomic factors, area deprivation index (ADI) has been widely used to reflect neighborhood-level socioeconomic status, categorized by zone improvement plan code,6 which helps us understand the impact of a patient's community resources on health care accessibility and outcomes in transplantation field.7–10 However, there are few studies examining the effect of ADI in KT.

It is not clearly understood whether socioeconomic factors affect transplant outcomes differently depending on the post-transplantation period. We hypothesized that effect of socioeconomic status on patient outcomes might change depending on the period post-transplantation. Assessment for the substantial variability in socioeconomic effect on GS in KT could have important implications for long-term care. Thus, we evaluated how the effect of socioeconomic status on patient outcomes varies over different periods after KT by using Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) dataset.

Methods

Patients and Data Collection

This was a retrospective cohort study using the OPTN database. We included all adult (older than 18 years) single-organ KT recipients from 2003 to 2013 with the last follow-up as of March 2024. Recipient characteristics included age, sex, race, kidney disease etiology, history of diabetes, body mass index (BMI), dialysis at transplantation, serum creatinine at transplantation, number of HLA mismatches, history of delayed graft function (DGF), and socioeconomic factors. Donor characteristics included age, sex, race, BMI, type of donor (deceased or living), relationship to the donor, donation after circulatory death, and kidney donor profile index. Primary kidney disease etiology was reviewed and divided into five groups, diabetic nephropathy, nephrosclerosis, GN, polycystic kidney disease, and others. This study used the OPTN dataset provided by the United Network for Organ Sharing. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Henry Ford Hospital (17536). All methods of research procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Definition of Socioeconomic Factors

Socioeconomic factors encompassed patient education level, employment status at transplantation, ADI, and insurance status. Patient education level, employment status at transplantation, five-digit ZIP codes of patients' homes, and insurance status were obtained from the OPTN database as well. Employment status was recorded as whether the recipient was working for income at the time of transplantation.

ADI is an open-access dataset managed by the University of Wisconsin School of Medicine and Public Health6 (https://www.neighborhoodatlas.medicine.wisc.edu/). ADI is based on a measure created by the Health Resources and Services Administration, which enables rankings of neighborhoods by socioeconomic disadvantage within a region of interest. It incorporates factors related to the theoretical domains of income, education, employment, and housing quality. National ADI data are ranked from 1 to 100, with higher values indicating a lower socioeconomic status. We used the 2020 ADI, which was derived from the 2020 American Community Survey data representing a 5-year period from 2016 to 2020. Then, ADI was linked to our database at the ZIP code level. The median ADI score of all nine-digit ZIP codes within the same five-digit prefix was used, as only five-digit ZIP codes were available in the OPTN dataset. Patients were categorized into four groups based on quartiles of ADI of their residential ZIP code: least deprived (first ADI quartile; 0–25), less deprived (second ADI quartile; 26–47), moderately deprived (third ADI quartile; 48–70), and most deprived (fourth ADI quartile; 71–100) areas.

Since the definition of ADI incorporates information on income and education based on the ZIP code of the patient's residence, it should be considered separately from the employment and education status provided in the OPTN dataset. Therefore, we first examined the impact of socioeconomic factors defined as patients' employment, education level, and insurance status. Subsequently, in the subset of the cohort with available ADI data (excluding patients with missing ADI data), we examined the effect of socioeconomic factors defined as ADI and insurance status.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was graft failure, a composite of the earlier of either graft loss or death. To evaluate the longitudinal effect of socioeconomic status over time, the period of post-transplantation was divided into three categories and the outcome of each group is as follows; group 1: 1-year graft failure in all patients, group 2: 5-year graft failure in 1 year-graft survivors, and group 3: 10-year graft failure in 5-year graft survivors. The longitudinal effect of socioeconomic status on patient death over time was also evaluated (Figure 1).

Chart showing flow of the study. KT, kidney transplantation.

The effect of socioeconomic factors on graft failure in each group was evaluated, stratified by DDKT and living donor kidney transplantation (LDKT). As previously described, we first examined the impact of socioeconomic factors, defined as patients' employment status, educational level, and insurance status. Subsequently, in the subgroup of the cohort for which ADI data were available, we assessed the effect of socioeconomic factors defined as ADI and insurance status. Thus, we examined temporal changes in the effect of socioeconomic factors on graft failure through above two approaches. The risks were adjusted by recipient factors (age, sex, race, BMI, diabetes, history of dialysis, history of KT, the number of HLA mismatches, DGF), and donor factors (sex, race, kidney donor profile index) in DDKT group. Similarly, in LDKT group, the risks were adjusted by recipient factors (age, sex, race, BMI, diabetes, history of dialysis, history of KT, the number of HLA mismatches, DGF) and donor factors (age, sex, race, BMI).

Cluster Analysis

We conducted a two-step cluster analysis to identify distinct social profiles based on ADI quartile, insurance type, employment status at transplantation, and education level. The model automatically identified two clusters based on the Bayesian Information Criterion. The silhouette measure of cohesion and separation was 0.4, indicating a fair model fit. These clusters were then used in Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models for graft failure or patient death in each group.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using software (SPSS, Version 27.0.1; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous data were expressed as mean±SD or median (interquartile range). We used standardized differences to assess the balance of baseline characteristics between the DDKT group and the LDKT group. A standardized difference >0.1 was considered indicative of meaningful imbalance.11 The Student t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare continuous variables. The chi-square test or Fisher exact test was used to compare the categorical variables. The Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test were used to compare differences in GS between groups defined by ADI quartiles. Multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to examine significant factors associated with graft failure or patient death. P values < 0.05 were inferred as significant.

Analyses excluded patients with missing covariate data. Given the substantial missingness for employment status, a sensitivity analysis was performed excluding employment status from the multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Subgroup analyses using ADI excluded patients with missing ADI data.

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics and the comparison between the LDKT group and the DDKT group. Notably, the rate of DGF was much higher in the DDKT group (4.1% versus 25.1%; standardized difference, 0.636). Regarding socioeconomic factors, recipients of LDKT had higher rates of employment at transplantation and a greater proportion of private insurance coverage compared with recipients of DDKT.

We then compared background characteristics across ADI quartiles separately for the LDKT and the DDKT group in subgroup with available ADI (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). In both LDKT and DDKT, recipients in the highest ADI group (ADI 71–100, most deprived) had lower rates of employment at transplantation, lower rates of private insurance coverage, and a higher proportion of individuals with an education level of high school or lower. The distribution of recipient race varied across ADI quartiles.

Impact of Socioeconomic Factors on Graft Failure or Patient Death

Multivariable Cox hazard models revealed that unemployment status at transplantation was independently associated with graft failure in all groups in both DDKT and LDKT (Table 2). Compared with those with private insurance, patients with Medicare were consistently associated with graft failure across nearly all post-transplantation periods in both DDKT and LDKT. Although a lower education level (high school or lower, reference; post-college graduate degree) was not the risk factor in the early post-transplantation period (group 1), it increased the risk of graft failure in the later periods for both DDKT and LDKT.

The effect of socioeconomic factors on patient death showed a similar trend to that on graft failure. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models demonstrated that being unemployed at the time of transplantation was independently associated with patient death across all groups in both DDKT and LDKT (Table 3). Patients with Medicare were consistently associated with a higher risk of patient death throughout almost the entire post-transplantation follow-up for both DDKT and LDKT. Although having a lower education level (high school or below) was not a significant risk factor in the early post-transplantation period (group 1), it was associated with an increased risk of mortality in the later period (group 3) for both DDKT and LDKT.

Subgroup Analysis of the Impact of ADI on Graft Failure or Patient Death

In the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test for DDKT, no significant difference in GS by ADI was observed in the early period (group 1). However, in the mid and late post-transplantation period (group 2 and 3), patients residing in the most deprived areas showed worse outcome (Figure 2). By contrast, for LDKT, patients in the most deprived areas were consistently associated with worse GS across all post-transplantation periods, from early to late (Figure 3).

Cluster Analysis

We performed a cluster analysis to identify underlying sociodemographic patterns among recipients. The two-step method was used, incorporating categorical variables such as ADI quartile, insurance type, employment status at transplantation, and education level. Cluster 1 consisted of individuals who were less likely to live in high-ADI areas, had a lower proportion of Medicare insurance, a lower proportion of individuals with a high school education or less, and a lower rate of unemployment. Cluster 2 consisted of recipients who lived in areas with higher ADI, were predominantly covered by Medicare, had a lower educational level, and were more likely to be unemployed at the time of transplantation (Supplemental Table 3). Based on these characteristics, cluster 1 was characterized as a socioeconomically less deprived group, whereas cluster 2 represented a more socioeconomically deprived group.

We then conducted multivariable Cox regression models examining graft failure across each post-transplantation period. Compared with cluster 1, cluster 2 was consistently associated with a higher risk of graft failure throughout all phases, from early to late post-transplantation periods, in both DDKT and LDKT. Notably, in LDKT, the adjusted hazard ratios were higher than those observed in DDKT (Table 6). Multivariable Cox regression analyses for patient death demonstrated a similar pattern, with cluster 2 showing consistently a higher risk of patient death across all phases in both DDKT and LDKT. Importantly, the association was stronger in LDKT, where the adjusted hazard ratios exceeded those observed in DDKT (Table 7).

Discussion

In this study, we assessed variations in the effects of socioeconomic status on graft failure and patient death after KT using the OPTN dataset. We explored the influence of socioeconomic status on long-term post-transplant outcomes. Because ADI and individual-level socioeconomic variables conceptually overlap, we first analyzed them separately to clarify the independent associations of each factor with outcomes. In addition, by performing cluster analysis that incorporated both ADI and individual-level variables, we further explored whether distinct patient profiles emerged from their combined patterns. We found significant associations between post-KT outcomes and individual socioeconomic factors, including employment status, education level, and insurance status. Particularly, a lower education level was not the risk factor in the early post-transplantation period, it increased the risk of graft failure in the later periods for both DDKT and LDKT. Analyses using ADI revealed that post-transplantation outcomes were worse among recipients from highly deprived areas. Cluster analysis found that the socioeconomically disadvantaged group was consistently associated with worse outcomes from early to late post-transplantation periods in both DDKT and LDKT. However, this adverse association was more pronounced in LDKT.

Previous research, which assessed the impact of socioeconomic disadvantage in KT at either the area or individual scale, has largely emphasized outcomes related to graft function or mortality.12–16 To date, few studies have examined the effect of socioeconomic factors on transplantation outcomes by stratifying the post-transplantation period into early, mid-term, and late phases. Our study additionally evaluated socioeconomic influences from both the individual and area levels. These aspects represented a novel approach to evaluating the longitudinal influence of socioeconomic status on post-transplantation prognosis.

Employment status at the time of transplantation has been reported to have a strong and independent association with GS or PS.5,17 Unemployment would suggest financial disadvantage, which has been recognized as one of the factors associated with worse transplantation outcomes. Similarly, it has been reported that recipients with higher education levels demonstrate a clear advantage in terms of both graft and overall recipient outcomes.3,18,19 However, it is noteworthy that the effect of education level was not evident in early outcomes and became increasingly pronounced with longer follow-up durations, particularly beyond 1 and 5 years post-transplant. Among KT recipients who did not adhere to their medication and follow-up care, 91% experienced graft loss or died.20 Potential risk factors for noncompliant behavior after transplantation include socioeconomic issues, occupational status, educational status, and poor social support.21 Medication compliance was not evaluated in the present study, and thus, it was not possible to definitively determine whether differences in compliance existed according to education level. However, while comprehensive patient education is typically provided immediately after KT, it is conceivable that such educational efforts may diminish during the mid-term and long-term post-transplantation periods. To improve very long-term outcomes in later periods, consideration of the patients' education level in follow-up care might be necessary.

As previously mentioned, ADI incorporates a range of distinct indicators that reflect household conditions within a specific geographic area, and it captures financial hardship with more detailed measures.6 To date, numerous studies have investigated the association between various indicators of socioeconomic status and post-transplantation outcomes.22–24 Our findings underscore the importance of area-level socioeconomic factors in both DDKT and LDKT. A study published in 2010 using OPTN data examined area-level factors by applying an index of neighborhood socioeconomic status derived from patients' ZIP codes.13 The study found that patients residing in less deprived neighborhoods experienced lower mortality following DDKT.13 It is also known that socioeconomically advantaged individuals not only have better access to LDKT but also experience lower post-transplant mortality.13

Several recent studies have examined the impact of socioeconomic factors on KT by incorporating both individual- and area-level indicators, highlighting their overlapping yet distinct roles in shaping access to and outcomes of transplantation.25–27 In our study, while individual education level showed different impacts on outcomes between the early and late post-transplantation periods, ADI, which also incorporates education level remained a consistent risk factor across all periods. This finding indicates that although individual-level and area-level socioeconomic factors can overlap, it remains essential in clinical practice to consider each patient's own education level when providing care.

We performed a cluster analysis to identify patterns based on individual socioeconomic factors and ADI and subsequently examined their associations with graft failure or patient death. The socioeconomically disadvantaged cluster was significantly associated with worse graft failure or patient death in both DDKT and LDKT from the early to late post-transplantation periods. This detrimental effect, however, was more substantial in LDKT than in DDKT. There may be a difference in the magnitude of the effect of socioeconomic factors between LDKT and DDKT. In general, LDKT has consistently demonstrated better GS and PS than DDKT.4 As the incidence of DGF is substantially lower in LDKT, the difference in GS between LDKT and DDKT is particularly pronounced. Given the lower possibility of graft failure due to clinical complications, the relative impact of socioeconomic factors may be more prominent in LDKT. In addition, LDKT typically involves active decision making and planning by the recipient, whereas DDKT is unpredictable. This process requires substantial recipient engagement, including scheduling of surgery and adjustments to work and daily life. Consequently, post-transplantation outcomes in LDKT may be more sensitive to differences in socioeconomic status. Higher community-level socioeconomic risk has been linked to worse pre-transplantation outcomes and increased waitlist removal due to health decline.28 Health outcomes are strongly affected by socioeconomic status, which determines access to medical services, compliance with medications, and affordability of drugs.26

There are limitations in our study. First, this is a retrospective study using OPTN data. Thus, there exists the missing data in socioeconomic factors. For example, the employment status at transplantation is missing for 30% of the dataset. We compared patient characteristics between those with available employment information and those with missing data. Aside from a higher proportion of LDKT in the group with available employment status, no meaningful differences were observed between the two groups. This suggests that the missing employment data are unlikely to have materially affected our findings (Supplemental Table 4). Furthermore, we performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding employment status from the multivariable Cox proportional hazards models (Supplemental Tables 5 and 6). Consistent with the main analysis, patients with Medicare exhibited higher risk of graft failure or mortality than those with private insurance across all post-transplantation periods in both DDKT and LDKT. Similarly, while a lower educational level was not a risk factor in the early post-transplantation period, it was associated with an increased risk of worse outcomes in the later periods for both DDKT and LDKT. These consistent patterns reinforce the robustness of our findings. Second, as the OPTN data do not have nine-digit ZIP codes, five-digit ZIP codes were linked to the median ADI score for the neighborhoods within the same five-digit prefix, which may fail to capture socioeconomic disparities within adjacent neighborhoods in the same five-digit ZIP code area. Some previous studies have also used ADI linked to five-digit ZIP codes.29–31 ADI was evaluated in quartiles rather than as a continuous variable to mitigate discrepancies between patient's actual nine-digit ZIP codes and the median values of the five-digit ZIP codes. Even if nine-digit ZIP codes were available, ADI is based on neighborhood or Census block-group data, and therefore shares the same limitations as Census and American Community Survey data, including incomplete coverage in some areas.6 Third, since patients may move after KT, ADI only based on the ZIP code at the time of KT may not fully reflect its impact on mid-term and long-term outcomes. Furthermore, our study used the 2020 ADI, which covered a five-year period from 2016 to 2020. Since demographic factors change over time, these differences in data collection periods might partly explain the discrepancies. This study also includes employment status and insurance status at the time of transplantation; however, these factors may change over time, and post-transplant data on these variables were not available. In addition, information on retirement or student status is not available in the OPTN database. Recipients were classified based solely on the presence or absence of paid employment at the time of transplantation, and further distinctions could not be made. Therefore, these data should be interpreted as indicative of general trends. Since the mechanisms contributing to sociocultural and sociodemographic disparities in graft failure or patient death are likely multifactorial, future investigations must explore the temporal dynamics of these associations and assess the validity.

Despite the limitations, the novelty of our study lies in assessing longitudinal changes in the impact of socioeconomic status on post-KT outcomes. The effect of individual-level factors on graft failure or patient death varied over time. Individuals from highly deprived areas experienced worse outcomes across all post-transplantation phases. These observations underscore the critical influence of socioeconomic factors on transplantation success. To improve very long-term outcomes in the later post-transplantation period, follow-up care may need to account for both individual-level and area-level socioeconomic factors.
