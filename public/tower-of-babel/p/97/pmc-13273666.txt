ABSTRACT
Key Points•HLA-ESP-1C single-chain trimer protects gene-edited (MHC-I/II/TCR/CD54/CD58 deficient) human allogeneic CAR T cells from lysis by NK cells.•UCART19 (gene-edited, HLA-ESP-1C) cells control Raji lymphoma in NSG mice engrafted with allogeneic NK cells.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells have shown immense promise for the treatment of hematological malignancies.1, 2, 3 However, the current CAR T-cell manufacturing process has several disadvantages. First, CAR T-cell manufacturing relies on autologous T cells from patients who have previously received therapies that may lead to lymphopenia and T-cell dysfunction.4, 5, 6 Second, complex logistics, manufacturing, and release of a CAR T-cell product can take up to 4 to 6 weeks, resulting in substantial delays in treatment and ultimately making this line of therapy inaccessible for patients in need of immediate intervention.7

A proposed strategy to overcome these limitations is to develop off-the-shelf allogeneic CAR T cells using healthy donor T cells.8, 9, 10 To implement this strategy, histocompatibility barriers must be addressed to mitigate graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) as well as host-versus-graft disease (HVGD). Inactivation of the endogenous αβ T-cell receptor/CD3 complex (αβTCR) would prevent GVHD, whereas deletion of major histocompatibility complex (MHC)–I and -II molecules would circumvent HVGD. However, ablation of MHC-I poses a risk of inducing the natural killer (NK) cell missing-self response.11,12

HLA-E is a nonclassical MHC-Ib molecule.13,14 Like classical MHC-Ia molecules (HLA-A, -B, and -C), HLA-E is presented on the surface of cells as a trimolecular complex composed of an HLA-E heavy chain, β2-microglobulin (β2m), and peptide.15,16 The HLA-E locus is known to be well conserved compared with the highly polymorphic HLA-A, -B, and -C loci.17,18 Of the multiple known HLA-E alleles, only 2 (HLA-E∗01:01 and HLA-E∗01:03) are functional in humans. These alleles differ by a single amino acid at position 107 (arginine in HLA-E∗01:01 and glycine in HLA-E∗01:03).19 This single amino acid difference results in higher cell surface expression of HLA-E∗01:03 complexes as well as higher binding affinity for many peptides.20,21 HLA-E preferentially binds nonamers derived from the signal peptide (SP) of classical MHC-Ia molecules and, among the 16 distinct SP variants, favors those SP with defined anchor residues, such as methionine at position 2 and leucine at position 9.15,22 In addition, pathogen-derived peptides can also be presented by HLA-E for recognition by αβTCR CD8+ cells.23, 24, 25 The interaction between HLA-E and NKG2A/CD94 or NKG2C/CD94 depends on the SP sequence and results in either NK cell inhibition or activation, respectively.26, 27, 28, 29

NK cell function depends on combinatorial signals received through both activating and inhibitory receptors. Natural killer group 2D (NKG2D), lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1), and DNAX accessory molecule-1 (DNAM-1) are known to be major activating NK cell receptors, often working synergistically.30, 31, 32 NKG2D binds MHC class I polypeptide-related sequence A (MICA) and MHC class I polypeptide-related sequence B (MICB), which can be upregulated on the cell surface under conditions of cellular stress, such as DNA damage.33,34 LFA-1 has been shown to function as an important adhesion molecule, interacting with CD54 early in immune synapse formation during NK cell lytic activation.35 LFA-1 is also known to recruit DNAM-1 (CD226) to the synapse, facilitating interaction with CD112 and CD155, resulting in an amplified lytic response.36 Recent studies have also suggested a role for CD2-CD58 interaction in NK cell activation, where CD2 is often upregulated specifically on the surface of adaptive NKG2C+ NK cells.37, 38, 39 To prevent clearance of allogeneic CAR T cells by the NK cell missing-self response, we hypothesized that enhanced expression of HLA-E, along with ablation of additional NK ligands, would be required.

Here, we report the successful development of HLA-E–expressing, allogeneic/gene-edited (CD3ε/MHC-I/MHC-II/ CD54/CD58null) T cells (designated as universal allogeneic CAR T [UCART] cells) that circumvent key histocompatibility barriers and the missing-self response of NK cells. We used a single-chain trimer (ScT) platform40 to identify an amino acid peptide sequence for HLA-E presentation (designated as HLA-ESP-1C) that resulted in significant inhibition of NKG2A+ cells with negligible activation of NKG2C+ cells. We further demonstrate that genetic ablation of CD54 and CD58 by adenine base editing, in combination with overexpression of HLA-ESP-1C, allows engineered T cells to resist heterogeneous NK cell populations, confirming the importance of these adhesion molecules in NK cell signaling. Finally, using a UCART19/Raji model, we demonstrate the efficacy of UCART cells to promote tumor rejection while evading allogeneic NK cell elimination.

Methods

Primary cells

CART19 and scFvCD19 UCART (UCART19) cells were generated from normal donor T lymphocytes by seeding 1 × 106 cells per mL in Optimizer media supplemented with 5% human AB serum and then activated with anti-CD3/28 beads (Dynabeads, ThermoFisher) as previously described.41 The following day, cells were transduced with lentivirus (LV; multiplicity of infection, 5) and then 4 days later, gene edited as described in supplemental Methods. LV-transduced or LV-transduced/gene-edited CART19 cells were expanded for 6 to 7 days and cryopreserved. NK cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 5% human AB serum and activated using irradiated LCL 721.221 cells in the presence of interleukin (IL)-21 and IL-2 for 11 days, as described in supplemental Methods.42

LV constructs

HLA-E ScT constructs were made using the third-generation pTRPE-eGFP-T2A-mCherry backbone, as described in supplemental Methods. DNA oligo primers were designed to encode each nonamer peptide in place of enhanced Green Fluorescence Protein (eGFP) to create 10 HLA-E ScT for the final version to encode plasmid translational research program expression vector backbone (pTRPE)-(peptide)-linker-β2m-linker-HLA-E∗01:03. The HLA-E ScT constructs were codon optimized to make them resistant to Cas9 protein riboprobes targeting endogenous β2m.

Functional assays

Details related to real-time cytotoxicity analysis, 51Cr release assays, NK cell flow-based assay, mixed leukocyte (or lymphocyte) reaction assay, and flow cytometry are provided in supplemental Methods.

Animal model

NSG and NSG-huIL-15 mice (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME) were housed under pathogen-free conditions. The Raji xenograft model was established by IV tail injections of 1 × 105 cells expressing click beetle red luciferase for bioluminescence imaging (BLI). On day 7 after tumor inoculation, mice were treated with 3 × 106 T cells, either gene-edited CART19 T cells or gene-edited UCART19 T cells. Negative control mice received no treatment, and positive control mice received 3 × 106 CART19 cells. BLI was used to monitor tumor growth following intraperitoneal injections of 3 mg D-luciferin/150 μL phosphate-buffered saline per mouse (∼120 mg/kg), and imaged after 10 minutes using an In vivo imaging system (IVIS) Spectrum imaging system, as previously described.41 Data were analyzed using Living Image version 4.5.2 software (PerkinElmer). All animal procedures were performed according to the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocol (804226).

Detailed methods are provided in supplemental Materials.

Results

HLA-E expression on K562 cells inhibits NK cell activity

To identify peptides presented by HLA-E that result in significant inhibition of NK cell function, an HLA-E single-chain dimer (ScD) encoding HLA-E∗01:03 heavy chain and human β2m was constructed and used as a template for generating HLA-E ScT constructs, with each presenting a distinct SP or pathogen-derived amino acid peptide sequence (Figure 1A). DNA oligo primers were customized to encode each peptide on the HLA-E ScD template, creating 10 distinct HLA-E ScT constructs (Figure 1B). These candidate peptides were chosen based on their reported high binding affinity for HLA-E∗01:03 and the interaction of the corresponding peptide-HLA complex with NKG2A.16,22,23,25,43 Among these candidates, 6 represented HLA-I SP variants recently described by Lin et al.44 In initial experiments, K562 cells were used as recipient cells for HLA-E constructs and as a surrogate model for allogeneic T cells because of the absence of an αβTCR and MHC-I/-II on its cell surface. Because K562 cells do not endogenously express HLA-E, to achieve cell surface expression we encoded a tripartite HLA heavy chain, β2m, and peptide as ScT (Figure 1C). K562 cells were transduced with HLA-E ScT constructs, sorted for purity based on HLA-E expression, and tested as targets in a 4-hour 51Cr release assay with expanded bulk NK effector cells. K562 cells expressing an HLA-E ScT construct presenting the peptide sequence VMAPRTLIL (HLA-I SP variant SP-1C),43 resulted in the most significant resistance to NK cell lysis (Figure 1D). K562 cells expressing HLA-E ScT constructs presenting peptides derived from SP of HLA-A∗02:01 (SP-2A variant) or HLA-G (SP-G variant) as well as virally derived peptides from cytomegalovirus (CMV)-Towne (also SP-1A variant) and CMV-AF1 (also SP-F variant) resulted in moderate inhibition of NK cell lysis. Next, we sought to determine whether HLA-ESP-1C expression could confer long-term (days vs hours) protection against NK cell lysis. Real-time cell (eSight) assays were performed by culturing K562 target cells with NK cells for up to 3 days. Images taken over 3 days revealed that HLA-ESP-1C K562 cells resisted NK cell attack and persisted in culture compared with untransduced (UTD) K562 cells, which were eliminated by 24 hours (supplemental Figure 2A). NK cell populations with higher percentages of NKG2A−C+ cells (donor NK561) were inhibited to a lesser degree, resulting in partial clearance of HLA-ESP-1C K562 target cells. (supplemental Figure 2B). This observation was not surprising, as NKG2A−C+ NK cells are known to be activated by HLA-E under most conditions.44,45 Based on these results, we further investigated the functional consequences of HLA-ESP-1C expression on targets using defined NK cell populations.

Characterization of the NKG2A/C phenotype of NK cells

Expanded bulk NK cells from 5 normal donors were phenotyped for expression of NKG2A and NKG2C. Two of the 5 donors, NK517 and NK518, had significant NKG2A+ populations with ∼75% NKG2A+C− and ∼13% NKG2A+C+ (Figure 2A). Donors NK510 and NK516 had significant NKG2A+ populations (∼50%-60%) as well as a distinct NKG2A−C− population (∼13%-19%). Donor NK485 presented a substantial NKG2A−C+ population, representing ∼20% of total NK cells. The degree of NK inhibition observed in 4-hour 51Cr release assays among these donors correlated with the NKG2A/C population composition, where HLA-ESP-1C–expressing targets were less susceptible to lysis by NK populations enriched in NKG2A+C− or NKG2A+C+ populations (NK517 N518; Figure 2B). In contrast, HLA-ESP-1C-expressing targets were more susceptible to lysis by NK populations enriched in NKG2A−C− or NKG2A−C+ cells (NK516, NK510, and NK485; Figure 2B). Expression of HLA-ESP-1C confers resistance to NKG2A+ NK cell lytic activity

To dissect the ability of HLA-ESP-1C to inhibit or activate various NK cell populations, a flow cytometry–based assay was used to measure CD107a expression as a direct readout of NK activation/degranulation in coculture experiments with K562 target cells. Two NK donors, NK517 and NK561, were chosen as they presented disparate frequencies of NKG2A−C+ cells, these being 2.3% and 20.8%, respectively (Figure 2C). Activation of NKG2A+C− and NKG2A+C+ cells was completely abrogated in both donors by HLA-ESP-1C expression, a finding consistent with its inhibitory effect on NKG2A+ populations (Figure 2C). In donor NK561, NKG2A−C+ cells showed modestly increased activation toward HLA-ESP-1C–expressing targets compared with UTD targets. In contrast, HLA-ESP-1C expression on K562 had a neutral effect on NKG2A−C− cell activity because CD107a expression was similar to that observed in UTD cells (Figure 2C). A similar analysis was performed in 3 additional NK donors, observing a consistent inhibition of NKG2A+C− and NKG2A+C+ but not NKG2A−C− and NKG2A−C+ populations, upon expression of HLA-ESP-1C on target cells (Figure 2D).

Given the distinct resistance conferred by HLA-ESP-1C to NKG2A/C populations, we next examined HLA-ESP-1C–expressing target cells for sensitivity to lytic activity against purified NKG2A/C NK populations (Figure 3A). In 4-hour 51Cr release assays, HLA-ESP-1C completely inhibited the lytic activity of both NKG2A+C− and NKG2A+C+ NK cell populations (Figure 3B). NKG2A−C+ NK cell lysis of HLA-ESP-1C targets was equivalent to lysis of UTD cells (Figure 3B). HLA-ESP-1C protects MHC-I–deficient Raji cells in vivo

Next, we examined the in vivo protective effect of HLA-ESP-1C expression against NK cell recognition in a humanized murine model. Raji (Click Beetle Green luciferase and GFP−) cells were gene edited to ablate human β2m expression (MHC-I−) and transduced with HLA-ESP-1C ScT LV (MHC-I–/HLA-ESP-1C; supplemental Figure 5A). The MHC-I− Raji cells were sensitive to NK cell lysis, whereas MHC-I−/HLA-ESP-1C Raji cells were resistant to NK cell lysis, thus confirming the protective effect of HLA-ESP-1C for MHC-I–deficient cells (supplemental Figure 5B). Next, the engineered Raji tumor cell lines were studied in NSG mice engrafted with NK cells. Mice were injected with Raji tumor cells, and 2 × 107 expanded bulk NK cells (supplemented with IL-15) were adoptively transferred 1, 4, and 6 days after tumor injection (supplemental Figure 5C). Mice were imaged for up to 25 days, with total flux measured to determine tumor growth. Although MHC-I− Raji cells were cleared by day 6 after NK cell administration, MHC-I−/HLA-ESP-1C Raji cells persisted at levels comparable to (unedited) parental MHC-I–expressing Raji cells (supplemental Figure 5D). These results demonstrate the in vivo protective effect of HLA-ESP-1C expression against NK activity.

Genetic ablation of selected NK cell ligands to examine their role in NK cell activation

As HLA-ESP-1C expression does not inhibit NKG2A−C− or NKG2A−C+ populations, we wanted to identify additional NK ligands that may contribute to NK cell activation.30, 31, 32 NKG2D ligands (MICA, MICB, and UL16-binding protein 2 [ULBP-2]), DNAM-1 ligands (CD112 and CD155), LFA-1 ligand (CD54), and CD2 ligand (CD58) were targeted for ablation in K562 cells (supplemental Figure 6A,C). Sorted, single gene-edited/HLA-ESP-1C K562 cell lines were phenotyped and tested as targets in 4-hour 51Cr release assays (supplemental Figure 6B,D). Single gene edits in HLA-ESP-1C K562 lines did not reduce NK cell recognition relative to HLA-ESP-1C K562 cells (supplemental Figure 6).

Since single ligand ablation had no effect on NK cell lytic activity, multiplex gene editing on HLA-ESP-1C K562 cells was undertaken by sequential editing of MICA and MICB (2 knockout [KO]), CD112 (3KO), and CD155 (4KO; Figure 4A-B). Additionally, recent findings have highlighted the importance of CD2 in NK cell activation, suggesting that CD58 ablation may prevent NK cell signaling because of improper immune synapse formation. Similarly, deletion of CD54, another important ligand for immune synapse formation, has been reported to abrogate NK activation. In 4-hour 51Cr release assays, we found that lysis of HLA-ESP-1C 2KO, 3KO, and 4KO K562 cells by bulk NK cells was similar to that of HLA-ESP-1C targets (Figure 4C). Interestingly, CD58 deficiency (HLA-ESP-1C 5KO) decreased NK cell recognition, and most importantly, combined ablation of CD58 and CD54 (HLA-ESP-1C 6KO) resulted in an additive effect with near-complete inhibition of NK lytic activity (Figure 4C). Similar results were observed in CD107a degranulation assays (Figure 4D). Importantly, analysis of various NKG2A/C populations demonstrated that HLA-ESP-1C 6KO K562 cells were resistant not only to NKG2A+ populations but also to NKG2A−C+ and NKG2A−C− NK cell populations (Figure 4E). As further confirmation, K562 CD54/CD58 2KO (double edit only) target cells expressing HLA-E SP-1C (supplemental Figure 6E) showed significant resistance to bulk NK cells from multiple (n = 8) independent donors (supplemental Figure 6F).

Taken together, these results confirm that CD58 and CD54 expression on target cells is critical for NK cell activation, most likely because of their requirement for immune synapse formation. Thus, genetic ablation of CD54 and CD58, along with an expression of HLA-ESP-1C on target cells appears to effectively inhibit recognition by heterogenous NK cell populations.

No proliferation of unrelated responder T cells was observed with UCART193KO T cells or HLA-matched autologous stimulator cells, whereas significant proliferation was observed with HLA-mismatched stimulator cells (Figure 5D). Furthermore, allo-primed cytotoxic T cells could not lyse UCART195KO target cells in 4-hour 51 Cr release assays (supplemental Figure 8). The absence of alloreactivity to UCART193KO T cells was observed with multiple stimulators and responder pairs (Figure 5D). These results confirm that ablation of MHC-I/-II in UCART cells abrogates allogeneic T-cell responses.

Finally, we examined the resistance of HLA-ESP-1C+ (UCART193KO or UCART195KO) or HLA-ESP-1C− (CART193KO or CART195KO) populations to NK cell lysis. In 4-hour 51Cr release assays, bulk expanded NK cells from donors of varying NKG2A/C phenotype (Figure 5E, top) were cocultured with either CART193KO, CART195KO, UCART193KO, or UCART195KO T cells. We found that UCART193KO and CART195KO T cells exhibited similar resistance to NK lysis (Figure 5E, bottom). Interestingly, UCART195KO T cells exhibited the highest protection from NK cell lytic activity, regardless of increased numbers of NKG2A−C+ cells (NK602; Figure 5E, lower). Taken together, these results confirmed findings previously observed in the K562 surrogate model and demonstrated the protective, additive effect that HLA-ESP-1C expression along with CD54/CD58 ablation confers to allogeneic T cells against the missing-self response of NK cells.

In vitro and in vivo functionality of UCART cells

To ensure that gene editing did not compromise UCART cell potency, an eSight imaging assay was conducted to compare CART19 vs UCART193KO cell activity against Raji tumor cells. Both UCART193KO and CART19 cells killed Raji cells with similar kinetics and efficiency at varying effector-to-target ratios (supplemental Figure 9A). Next, titrated UCART193KO cell concentrations were tested in vivo for rejection of Raji cells implanted in NSG-huIL-15 transgenic mice. Raji tumor cells were injected in mice 7 days before adoptive transfer of increasing doses (0.3 × 106, 1 × 106, or 3 × 106) of UCART193KO cells (supplemental Figure 9B). Mice that received 3 × 106 UCART193KO cells completely cleared tumor 2 weeks after T-cell injection, whereas mice that received lower T-cell doses had an initial BLI response but ultimately succumbed to disease by day 60 (supplemental Figure 9C). Furthermore, mice showed no signs of GVHD throughout the 60-day period. These results demonstrate the in vivo tumor rejection capacity of UCART193KO T cells.

Discussion

The need for an off-the-shelf allogeneic CAR T-cell therapy has become apparent, as autologous CAR T-cell manufacturing has notable limitations.49 Here, we demonstrate an engineering strategy to produce UCART cells that evade recognition by allogeneic host T cells and NK cells. Enforced HLA-ESP-1C expression on gene-edited UCART19 cells was sufficient to circumvent the missing-self NK cell response. Our survey of 10 SPs provides strong evidence that the SP-1C (VMAPRTLIL) sequence presented in the context of HLA-E∗01:03 confers resistance to allogeneic NK lysis. The interaction of NKG2A with HLA-ESP-1C resulted in complete inhibition of NKG2A+ cells while minimizing NKG2C+ NK activation, as assessed in a series of functional assays. Near-complete inhibition of NK cell–mediated killing was achieved by combining HLA-ESP-1C expression with gene ablation of CD58 and CD54 on TCR/CD3null/MHC-Inull/IInull CART19 (UCART195KO) cells. Thus, multiple genetic modifications were required to evade the host response to allogeneic donor T and NK cells.

It is well established that ablation of endogenous αβTCR and MHC-I/MHC-II in donor hematopoietic cells is required to avoid GVHD and HVGD, respectively.9,10 The absence of MHC-I on allogeneic T cells induces the missing-self response of NK cells, and although potential solutions have been suggested, most have not been rigorously tested in humanized mouse tumor models. Given the established role of HLA-E in antagonizing NK cell–mediated killing of target cells, improving HLA-E expression was a rational starting point for generating UCART cells that evade the NK cell missing-self response. In several prior studies that proposed HLA-E as an NK inhibitory receptor for allogeneic CAR T-cell generation, the HLA-G SP was used47,50, 51, 52; however, our analysis indicated that the SP-G sequence (VMAPRTLFL) is suboptimal (supplemental Figure 1). Li et al53 used the HLA-E ScD in CRISPR-cas9 edited triple knockout (TKO) CAR-T cells; however, the edited T cells were functionally compromised, consistent with recent evidence from our group implicating double-strand DNA breaks caused by Cas9.41 Guo et al54 studied both HLA-E and HLA-G ScD in CRISPR-Cas9–edited double knockout (DKO) (β2m/TRAC) CAR T cells, providing partial protection against NK cell lysis.

The effectiveness of the proposed UCART design may be limited by the presence of NKG2A−C+ NK cells, which are less inhibited by HLA-ESP-1C and may show lytic activity against UCART cells. Under certain conditions, minor activation of NKG2A−C+ NK cells by HLA-ESP-1C is noted. In individuals with large populations of NKG2A−C+ NK cells driven by CMV viremia, this finding may limit the persistence of UCART cells. Because human CMV is geographically widespread, some investigators have taken a different approach to circumvent the expansion of NKG2C+ NK cells. Our experience shows that abrogation of NKG2D ligands (ie, MICA/B and ULBPs) and DNAM-1 ligands (ie, CD112 and CD155) on target cells has minimal effect on NK cell recognition. A recent report shows that the genetic ablation of CD54 and CD58 on allogeneic CAR T cells interferes with NK cell recognition.52 Our results confirm and extend this important observation by demonstrating that HLA-ESP-1C expression complements CD54 and CD58 ablation, resulting in inhibition of heterogeneous (including NKG2A−C− and NKG2C+A−) NK cell populations.

Recent work in off-the-shelf cell therapies has yielded several innovations that could potentially complement our findings to improve allogeneic CAR T-cell persistence in the host. The alloimmune defense receptor strategy using 4-1BB ligand is an active approach to engage and destroy nascent allo-activated 4-1BB+ NK and T cells and was effectively deployed in humanized tumor mouse models as proof of concept.55 Expression of HIV-1 negative factor (NEF) as an immune-evasion decoy allowed CART19 cells to resist allogeneic T and NK cells in peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)–engrafted NSG mice and clear NALM-6 leukemia; however, this strategy was effective for CART19/CD28ζ cells but less so with CART19/41BBζ cells because of the inability to phosphorylate Pak2.56

The effectiveness of HLA-ESP-1C in protecting UCART cells from NK cell attack and allowing tumor control was tested using allogeneic NK cells in a humanized murine model. By engrafting large numbers of defined NK populations, we could directly test resistance to NK activity because of the missing-self response. Previous studies have used PBMC-engrafted mice and thus the actual number and subset composition of NK cells present in these PBMC populations were not specified.57

In summary, our report provides an in-depth assessment and identification of an optimal HLA-E ScT entity that inhibits the NK cell “missing-self” response. In functional assays, HLA-E∗01:03 presenting the SP-1C inhibits bulk NK cells as well as the NKG2A+C− and NKG2A+C+ subsets. In addition, this study confirms and expands on the importance of ablating specific adhesion ligands, CD54 and CD58, which complement HLA-ESP-1C and aid in inhibiting activation of a diverse repertoire of NK populations. Finally, depletion of residual unedited mature T cells (by negative selection of the final product) will be required to eliminate the risk of GVHD to comply with current Good Manufacturing Practice standards.

Taken together, these findings suggest an updated approach for the generation of universal cell therapies that will allow the development of, and broader access to, allogeneic off-the-shelf T-cell therapies.
