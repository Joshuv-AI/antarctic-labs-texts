Prevalence of gastric cancer and its associated mortality among patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy at a tertiary level hospital in Tanzania
Authors: Ewaldo Vitus Komba, Mario Nyakiha, Tuzo Lyuu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDescriptive data on gastric cancer (GC) from African countries are limited. We describe the prevalence, clinicopathological features, short-term outcomes and factors associated with gastric cancer at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH), which is a tertiary-level facility in Tanzania.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled consenting patients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent diagnostic upper endoscopy at MNH from September 2023 to February 2024. The participants were followed up for 90 days to determine whether they died or developed gastrointestinal bleeding or gastric outlet obstruction. Sociodemographic and clinicopathological data were collected through a questionnaire and analysed via SPSS software. The prevalence of gastric cancer and short-term outcomes are expressed as proportions. Logistic regression analysis was used to test the significance of the associations between predictor variables and gastric cancer. A p value of < 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsA total of 870 patients were included, 459 (52.8%) of whom were male. The mean (± SD) age of the participants was 53.3 (± 17.4) years. GC was observed in 77 (8.9%) patients, with a male to female ratio of 0.83:1 and a mean age of 59.4 (± 10.6) years. The symptoms associated with GC were upper abdominal pain in 71(92.2%), weight loss 39(50.6%), abdominal fullness 24(31.2%), and vomiting 23(29.9%). H. pylori antibody testing was positive in 63 (81.8%) GC patients. The antrum was the most common location for GC in 51 (66.2%) patients, with ulcerative morphology in 47 (61.0%) patients. Histologically, 72 patients (96.1%) had adenocarcinomas. H. pylori infection (aOR 4.711; 2.493–8.902; P < 0.001), and cigarette smoking (aOR 2.477; 1.453–4.221; P = 0.001) were independently associated with GC. The 90-day mortality rate was 45.5% (35/77). Within the 90-day follow-up period, 88.3% (68/77) of the patients received palliative care, and 9.1% (7/77) were eligible for curative treatment.ConclusionsThe prevalence of gastric cancer among patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy at Muhimbili National Hospital is high. Most patients have advanced disease at presentation, and the 90-day mortality rate is high. Cigarette smoking and H. pylori infection were independently associated with GC in this population.

Background

The gastrointestinal (GI) cancers are responsible for more cancers and more deaths from cancer than any other cancers. [1] The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates a 45% increase in cancer deaths between 2007 and 2030 [2].

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, with an estimated 968,350 new cases and 659,853 deaths in 2022 [3]. The incidence of gastric cancer varies significantly across different regions worldwide [4, 5]. While the incidence of gastric cancer is high in countries such as China, Japan and Korea, it remains relatively low in most parts of Europe, North America and Africa [6] Within the African continent, there have been regional variations. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 data, Northern, Southern and Eastern African nations have higher incidence rates of gastric cancer than Central and Western African nations do [3].

The epidemiological features of GC have been extensively studied in Japan and the Western world; [7–9] however, only a few reports have emerged from less developed countries [6, 10–12]. There is a lack of comprehensive descriptive data on GC from African countries, where both cancer registries and data on risk factor prevalence are limited. Although the overall incidence of GC has declined, an increasing number of cases are being reported among individuals under 50 years of age, particularly in regions with historically low incidence [8, 9]. There are no clear reasons to explain this. However, the available global literature has come up with some possible explanations differing from one geographical region to the other. For example, Li et al., in their systematic trend analysis, noted that despite an overall declining trend in the burden of gastric cancer among young individuals in Africa, some low-income countries such as Lesotho and Zimbabwe still exhibit an increase in both incidence and mortality rates in younger individuals. [13] The authors attributed this to the low universal health coverage in these populations [14] and lack of screening for gastric cancer and H. pylori infection. For North America and Europe, the observed increase in incidence rate for young-onset gastric cancer was attributed partly to the increasing use of screening and surveillance procedures among younger individuals, with the detection of more cancers at earlier stages. Indeed, multiple guidelines from the North America and Europe have been established since 2015. [13, 15, 16]

The primary risk factor for gastric cancer is Helicobacter pylori infection, with approximately 90% of noncardia gastric cases associated with the bacterium. Globally, H. pylori infection affects a large portion of the population. In developing countries, 70%–90% of the population is infected with H. pylori, mostly during childhood, whereas the prevalence is lower in developed countries, ranging between 30% and 40% [17, 18]. People of Japanese and Korean ancestry have a relatively high incidence of gastric cancer in the United States, suggesting that cultural and dietary factors may play a significant role [19, 20]. While a high prevalence of H. pylori has been reported in South American and African countries, Africa paradoxically has a lower incidence of gastric cancer, a phenomenon referred to as the “African enigma”, which warrants further investigation [6]. One possible explanation lies in the geographical variation of H. pylori strains; populations where although the overall incidence of GC has declined, an increasing number of cases are being reported among individuals under 50 years of age, particularly in regions with historically low incidence rates, the Eastern Asian type CagA strain predominates and has a higher incidence of GC than populations where the Western type CagA strain is more common [21].

Moreover, the consumption of salted and smoked foods is known to increase the risk of gastric cancer and likely plays an interactive role in the development of GC.

Survival rates are highly variable and depend largely on the stage at which surgical intervention is initiated. The average 5-year survival rate is 31% in the United States and 26% in Europe [5]. However, studies in developing countries have reported significantly lower short-term and five-year survival. Mabula et al. recorded a GC 5-year survival rate of 6.9% [6].

While the incidence of GC is decreasing in the Western world, possibly due to socioeconomic development and associated health improvements, it is important to gain insights into the situation in other regions, such as East Africa, particularly Tanzania.

The aim of this study was to describe the prevalence, clinicopathological features, short-term outcomes and factors associated with GC among patients attending the endoscopy unit at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH).

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

From September 2023 to February 2024, we conducted a hospital-based descriptive cross-sectional study with a three-month follow-up component at the endoscopy unit of Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH), a tertiary hospital, in Tanzania. The hospital has a state-of-the-art endoscopy unit that performs both upper and lower diagnostic endoscopies, as well as therapeutic procedures such as polypectomies, band ligation therapy, injection sclerotherapy, esophageal stenting for the palliation of esophageal cancers and Argon plasma coagulation (APC) for gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding lesions. The endoscopy unit is well staffed with 15 endoscopists, 15 endoscopy nurses and 5 supporting staff. Annually, the unit performs an average of 5760 diagnostic upper endoscopies, 1536 diagnostic colonoscopies and 960 interventional procedures.

We included all adult patients aged 18 years or older who underwent diagnostic esophagogastroduodenoscopy (OGD) at the MNH endoscopy unit for any indication during the study period. Patients who underwent upper endoscopy for a specific therapeutic procedure after prior disease confirmation (e.g., band ligation for esophageal varices) were excluded.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was determined on the basis of the finite population of patients who underwent upper endoscopy at MNH. For a prevalence study, the following formula was used: n = Z2 P (100 − P)/ε2, where n = minimum sample size needed; Z = standard deviation set at 1.96 (CI of 95%) and P = prevalence of primary outcome: 52% (from a Rwandan study), since our population was finite, we adjusted the sample size using the following formula: N = n/(1+ ((n-1)/population)), where N = adjusted sample size for a finite population; n = calculated sample size for a nonfinite population: the finite population of patients undergoing upper endoscopy at MNH for a 6-month study period. Therefore, the minimum sample required was 866 participants.

Participant recruitment, interviews and endoscopic procedures

Participants were consecutively recruited into the study. Upon providing consent, they underwent interviews and anthropometric measurements. Weight was measured via a weighing scale without shoes and recorded to the nearest 0.5 kg, and height taken with a stadiometer was recorded to the nearest 0.5 cm. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and categorized as follows: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25–29.9 kg/m2) or obese (> 30 kg/m2). Using a structured questionnaire, the study recorded sociodemographic data (age, sex, level of education, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and dietary habits), clinical and laboratory data (GI symptoms and signs, body weight, height, H. pylori status, and comorbidities, including systemic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and HIV status), endoscopic procedures, and histological findings. After completing the questionnaire, 3–5 mL of venous blood was aseptically collected by trained laboratory personnel for H. pylori antibody testing.

Patients were instructed to fast from the morning of the procedure day but were allowed to drink clear fluids (e.g., water) up to one hour before the procedure. Endoscopic examinations were conducted via EVIS EXERA III video endoscopy (Olympus Medical Systems) by on-duty endoscopists without sedation. Suspicious gastritis lesions, including ulcerations and polypoid appearances, were carefully examined via conventional white light imaging (C-WLI). Biopsies of suspicious lesions were collected, fixed in formalin, and sent to the MNH Pathology Department for histological processing. Suspicious gastric cancers were macroscopically classified via the Borrmann classification system into five types as follows: type I (polypoid/fungating) tumors, type II (superficial spreading) tumors, type III (ulcerating) tumors, type IV (diffusely infiltrating/linitis plastica) tumors and type V (unclassified) tumors [22].

Pathological diagnosis and other laboratory tests

Histological diagnoses were made via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. An on-duty pathologist examined the samples and performed a histological diagnosis of GC on the basis of both the WHO and Japanese classifications. The histological classification followed Laurens’ classification (1965) into three types: intestinal diffuse and mixed [23–25]. Evidence of prior H. pylori infection was assessed via a rapid antibody test. Serum samples were tested for anti-H. pylori antibodies using a commercially available serological test strip (HEXAGON H. PYLORI, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Compared with ELISA, the test kit has a sensitivity of 97% and specificity of 95% (HEXAGON H. PYLORI, Germany). The different H. Pylori strains were not determined in this study.

Study outcomes

Short-term outcomes, including death, gastric outlet obstruction (GOO), and treatment with curative or palliative care, were assessed over a three-month period through routine monthly follow-up or emergency hospital visits. Patients were referred to the appropriate department on the basis of their clinical complaints. Patients with upper GI bleeding or GOO were routed via the MNH Emergency Medicine Department (EMD) to the endoscopy unit for endoscopic diagnostic confirmation. Patients considered for curative or palliative interventions were evaluated by a tumor board composed of specialists in medical and surgical gastroenterology and oncology, who made final management decisions.

Statistical analysis

The data collected were analysed via SPSS computer software version 23.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data are summarized in the form of proportions and frequency tables for categorical variables. Student’s t test was used to test for significant associations between the predictor and outcome variables among the continuous variables. The prevalence of gastric cancer was expressed as the number of patients who had positive biopsy findings for gastric cancer out of the total number of patients who underwent upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy. Short-term outcomes were expressed as proportions of gastric cancer patients who died, developed complications (GOO, vomiting blood) or underwent interventions (palliative or curative intent care). Endoscopic and histological findings are expressed as percentages and absolute numbers. Logistic regression analysis was used to test the significance of the associations between predictor variables and gastric cancer. Variables with a univariate p value < 0.2 were included in the multivariate analysis model (binary logistic regression analysis). Significance was defined as a P value of < 0.05.

Prevalence of gastric cancer among patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy at MNH

Table 2 shows the upper GI endoscopic diagnoses of the participants. The proportion of patients who were found to have gastric neoplasms was 81 (9.3%). Among the 81 patients who had gastric neoplasms, 77 (95.1%) were histologically confirmed to have gastric cancer. Therefore, the true, histologically confirmed, prevalence of gastric cancer was 8.9% (77/870). The four patients who tested negative for gastric cancer on histology were diagnosed with nonspecific chronic gastritis (not shown in Table 2). These patients received treatment for gastritis and underwent follow-up endoscopy. Although females had a relatively greater proportion of patients with gastric cancer than males did (10.2% vs. 7.6%), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.179).

The mean ± SD age of the 77 participants was 59.4 ± 10.6 years, with an age range of 27–97 years. Men were relatively younger than women were (mean age 57.4 ± 10.9 years versus 61.0 ± 10.2 years, respectively). The male-to-female ratio was 0.83:1 (35 males vs. 42 females). The most common presenting symptoms reported by patients were upper abdominal pain 71(92.2%), weight loss 39(50.6%), abdominal fullness 24(31.2%), and vomiting 23(29.9%). These symptoms were similar in both female and male patients. Among the comorbidities, hypertension was more common in patients with gastric cancer 21 (27.3%). More than ¾ of the gastric cancer patients had serological evidence of H. pylori infection 63 (81.8%). The most common anatomical site for gastric cancer was the antrum, harbouring approximately 51 (66.2%) of all the cancers, the majority of which were ulcerative in morphology (47 (61.0%)), followed by fungating/polypoid tumors (22 (28.6%)). Almost all the gastric cancers (74 (96.1%)) were adenocarcinomas.

Factors associated with gastric cancer

In the univariate logistic regression analysis, age group 40–59 years (cOR 12.15, 95% CI 3.71–39.79), ≥ 60 years (cOR 7.10; 95% CI 2.16–23.48), marriage (cOR 0.79, 95% CI 0.43–1.47), having comorbidities (cOR 2.00; 95% CI 1.21–3.31), hypertension 2.97 (1.72–5.13), positive H. pylori antibodies (cOR 3.97; 95% CI 2.181–7.177) and smoking (cOR 3.97; 95% CI 1.670–4.298) were associated with gastric cancer, all with p values < 0.01. According to multivariate analysis, patients with H. pylori-positive antibodies had more than 4-fold greater odds of having gastric cancer than H. pylori-negative patients did after controlling for other factors, aOR 4.71; 95% CI 2.493–8.902; p < 0.001). Discussion

In this study, we aimed to determine the prevalence of gastric cancer and its associated 90-day mortality among patients who underwent diagnostic upper gastrointestinal endoscopy at Muhimbili National Hospital. The prevalence of histologically confirmed gastric cancer (GC) was 8.9%. GC was most common in middle-aged individuals, with a mean age of 59.4 years (SD 10.6), and affected more females than males, with a male-to-female ratio of 0.83:1. Abdominal pain was the most common presenting symptom, followed by weight loss, abdominal fullness, and vomiting. Over 80% of patients with GC had positive serology for H. pylori, indicating evidence of infection. The anatomical location of GC is predominantly in the antrum and is mostly ulcerative in nature. Over 90% of the GC cases were adenocarcinomas.

By the end of the 90-day follow-up period, nearly 50% of the GC patients had died. Those who died were mainly patients who presented with advanced disease, including symptoms such as significant weight loss, persistent vomiting, and gastric outlet obstruction. These individuals were also more likely to have large, ulcerative tumors located in the distal stomach and had limited access to curative treatment options, receiving primarily palliative care.

The prevalence of gastric cancer in the present study is alarmingly high and among the highest reported in East Africa and globally. This figure exceeds those reported in previous similar studies conducted in India (2.4%) [24], Kenya (3.67%) [2], Iran (2.4%) [26],, Poland (5.4%) [26], the USA (2.2%) [26], and Spain (4.1%) [27]. On the other hand, the prevalence reported in the present study is much lower than that reported in some Asian studies. In Japan, the prevalence of gastric cancer among patients undergoing OGD reached 14.7%, whereas in China, it was 12.3%. [26] All these studies were conducted in referral tertiary healthcare institutions with a high volume of patients.

The relatively high prevalence of GC observed in the current study aligns with the high seroprevalence (81.8%) of H. pylori infection among GC patients. H. pylori infection is a well-established major risk factor for GC. In fact, in the present study, H. pylori infection was identified as an independent predictor of gastric cancer via multivariate analysis. Similarly, in their systematic review and meta-analysis, Shirani M et al. reported that large amounts of GCs in developed countries were associated with H. pylori [28]. These findings underscore the urgent need for improved screening, early detection, and targeted H. pylori interventions to manage and reduce the burden of gastric cancer in Tanzania.

Furthermore, the rate of cigarette smoking in the current study population was high at 30.8%, and smoking was also found to be an independent predictor of gastric cancer. Tobacco use has been implicated in the increasing incidence of cardia gastric cancer in developed countries. It is estimated that 11% of global stomach cancers and 17% of cases in Europe are attributable to smoking [5]. Therefore, smoking cessation should be one of the key strategies used to curb the increasing prevalence of gastric cancer.

Gastric cancer affects mostly older people, with the average age of people when they are diagnosed with gastric cancer being 68 years [29]. In our study, GC affected mostly the middle-aged group (40–59 years), with a tendency toward a younger age, contrary to expectations. However, our findings are consistent with those of an Indonesian study in which the most commonly affected age group was 50–60 years, comprising nearly half of [30] gastric cancer patients. With respect to global data, Li Y et al. [13] reported a marked difference in the trends of gastric cancer incidence and mortality at the regional and national levels. While the reasons for this remain unclear, it is suggested that the geographical disparities in the incidence of gastric cancer across populations may stem from factors such as altitude, host genetics, genotypes, the prevalence of H. pylori infection and dietary habits [31].

We observed a female dominance in the prevalence of GC in our study population, with a male to female ratio of 0.8:1. This is contrary to most global statistics of GC, where in many countries, rates are 2 to 3 times higher in men than in women [2, 6, 29]. On the other hand, our findings are in agreement with those of the Indonesian [30] and Rwandan studies [23], in which more than half of the gastric cancer patients were female. A possible explanation for the higher prevalence of GC in females in our study population is the relatively small number of gastric cancer patients in the current study (77 patients) compared with other studies, which included more patients. Moreover, the higher rates of healthcare-seeking behaviour among women and the post-menopausal status of most of female participants could partly explain this observation. Indeed literature indicates that women generally demonstrate higher rates of healthcare-seeking behaviour and utilisation compared to men across all health concerns [32] and that oestrogens have protective effect against gastric cancer in premenopausal women. [33] Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up times could yield different findings.

Consistent with findings in previous studies [1, 4, 6, 7, 34], we found that the antrum was the most frequent site for gastric cancer. In addition, previous reports investigating the location of gastric cancers resected by ESD or surgery have shown that the antrum and lesser curvature of the stomach are predominantly affected [7, 35]. The predominance of gastric cancer in this location can be explained in part by the high prevalence of Helicobacter pylori infection reported in the present study. Helicobacter pylori-induced atrophic changes usually advance from the antrum to the corpus along the lesser curvature, predominantly affecting these areas [35].

Within the 90-day follow-up period, a significant portion of patients in the current study experienced complications and required interventions. Nearly half of the patients had died by the end of this period, indicating high mortality. This underscores the aggressive nature of gastric cancer and the late presentation of patients with GC to healthcare facilities. In the present study, gastric cancer patients most commonly presented with symptoms indicative of advanced disease at diagnosis, namely, upper abdominal pain, weight loss, abdominal fullness, and vomiting. Similar symptom patterns were reported in previous studies on GC, where more than half of the patients were diagnosed with advanced-stage disease (stage III or IV) [11, 36]. These symptoms suggest obstructive lesions in the distal stomach, which is consistent with our findings where the most frequent anatomical tumor location was the antrum/pyloric region. A comparable short-term outcome was noted in an Rwandan study, where the three-month survival rate of GC patients was 52% [37].

Notably, within the 90-day follow-up period, the majority of participants (88.7%) received palliative care, reflecting the limited treatment options available due to the advanced stage of the cancer. Only a small proportion (9.1%) were deemed eligible for curative treatment, highlighting the challenges in managing advanced gastric cancer. These findings are consistent with those from Rwanda, where 19.9% of GC patients received curative treatment [37], further underscoring the late stage at diagnosis. Previous studies conducted in Tanzania looked at the reasons why cancer patients presented in late-stage disease. [38, 39] Generally, there are barriers related either to the patient or to the health care system. These include lower educational attainment, increased travel time, lack of cancer knowledge, and fear of cancer and cancer treatments. [39] Moreover, socio-economic disadvantage: those with low income and non-professional occupations experienced longer delays before diagnosis. [38]

The predominant macroscopic appearance (Borrmann’s classification) of gastric cancer in the present study was the ulcerative type, followed by fungating/exophytic/polypoid lesions. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies [6]. However, other reports have revealed a predominance of exophytic tumors [4]. This variation may be attributed to the fact that the macroscopic morphology of gastric cancer partly depends on the degree of differentiation of the tumor. Well-differentiated, gland-forming tumours tend to present as exophytic or ulcerated lesions, whereas poorly differentiated tumours, composed mainly of discohesive cells, tend to be diffusely infiltrative [1, 4]. Hence, it appears that patients in both studies had predominantly well-differentiated, gland-forming tumours.

In terms of histologic type, adenocarcinoma was the most commonly diagnosed malignancy in the present study and was observed in more than 90% of patients with GC. However, most of the histopathological reports do not classify gastric cancer into its subtypes according to either Lauren’s classification (intestinal, diffuse or indeterminate) or the 2010 WHO classification, which categorizes GC into four histological patterns: tubular, papillary, mucinous, poorly cohesive (including signet ring cell carcinoma), and several uncommon variants [40]. Nevertheless, these findings are consistent with previous reports worldwide [2, 4–6, 25, 36, 37, 41, 42], where adenocarcinoma is the most prevalent gastric malignancy. For example, in Europe and the United States, adenocarcinoma remains the predominant histological type of gastric cancer, constituting approximately 90–95% of all cases.

Study limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between GC and associated factors, as it does not allow the determination of temporal sequences or causality. Second, although the study’s large sample size enhances generalizability, the findings may have limited applicability beyond Muhimbili National Hospital or Tanzania. Given that the study population was highly selected from the endoscopy unit of a national referral hospital, the results may not reflect the true magnitude of gastric cancer in the general Tanzanian population. Third, variations in healthcare systems, socioeconomic factors, and healthcare-seeking behaviours between Tanzania and other regions may hinder the comparability of these findings with international literature. Fourth, the short follow-up period precludes the ability to provide five-year outcome data for the patients. Fifth, there were two patients who were diagnosed with Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs). We acknowledge that this diagnosis is difficult to reach based only on endoscopic mucosal biopsies due to submucosal nature of these tumors, despite the fact that these patients presented with upper GI bleeding and biopsies were taken from the ulcer margins on these tumors. So, we may not be 100% confident with this diagnosis.

Conclusions

Our findings revealed a high prevalence of gastric cancer among patients who underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and presented with advanced disease and high 90-day mortality. Cigarette smoking and H. pylori infection were independently associated with gastric cancer. We therefore recommend that when alarming features and/or chronic symptoms are present, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy be done. Biopsy of the pathological lesions and histopathological confirmation should be done to rule out upper gastrointestinal cancers. Further studies with long follow-up times to prospectively determine the five-year survival rate after initial interventions/therapy will also be valuable.
