Zebrafish Xenograft Model for Predicting Cisplatin Efficacy in Muscle‐Invasive Bladder Cancer
Authors: Yusuke Sugino, Xin Bao, Sho Sekito, Shiori Miyachi, Takumi Kageyama, Takeshi Sasaki, Kouhei Nishikawa, Toshio Tanaka, Manabu Kato, Yasuhito Shimada, Keishi Takano, Raku Son, Liqing Zang, Kenji Nakayama, Masatoshi Watanabe, Yasuhiro Murakawa, Takahiro Inoue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe standard treatment for muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) is cisplatin (CDDP)‐based neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) followed by radical cystectomy. However, only about 40% of patients respond to NAC, and tools to predict individual responses remain limited. The zebrafish patient‐derived xenograft (zPDX) model offers a rapid and cost‐effective platform for functional drug testing, but its application to MIBC has not yet been established. In this study, we developed a zPDX model optimized for evaluating CDDP sensitivity using clinical bladder cancer specimens. This model was validated through three steps: (1) evaluation of CDDP response in zPDXs derived from cell lines, (2) comparison of drug responses between mouse PDX (mPDX) and zPDX models, and (3) correlation of zPDX responses with clinical outcomes. The robustness of step 1 was demonstrated through complementary assays, including temperature optimization, in vivo platinum quantification, fluorescent dye validation, Cap Analysis of Gene Expression (CAGE), and whole‐mount immunofluorescence. An image‐based platform for quantifying drug response by measuring fluorescent area was established and applied in steps 2 and 3. Step 2, using mPDXs, provided essential technical validation before applying the protocol to clinical samples. In step 3, patient‐derived tumors were transplanted into zebrafish, allowing successful prediction of CDDP efficacy. Despite the small patient cohort, this study provides fundamental evidence supporting zPDX as a clinically relevant and experimentally validated tool for functional assessment of CDDP sensitivity in bladder cancer.Keywords: bladder cancer, cisplatin, PDX, xenograft, zebrafish

Introduction

Bladder cancer is a common malignancy worldwide and is classified into non–muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) [1]. While NMIBC is typically managed with local therapies and carries a relatively favorable prognosis, MIBC is associated with a higher risk of metastasis and mortality [1, 2]. Cisplatin (CDDP)‐based chemotherapy is the systemic standard for advanced urothelial carcinoma [3]. In MIBC, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) improves pathologic complete response rates and overall survival by facilitating local control and the eradication of micrometastases [4, 5]. However, the objective response rate to NAC remains limited to approximately 40%, and delaying definitive surgery in non‐responders may lead to worse outcomes [6, 7]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for predictive biomarkers capable of prospectively identifying CDDP sensitivity.

Recent advances in molecular profiling have identified several candidate genomic predictors of response to CDDP [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Nonetheless, due to the complexity of tumor heterogeneity and the limitations of genetic data alone in capturing therapeutic responsiveness, clinical application remains challenging [13]. Patient‐derived xenograft (PDX) models, which preserve the histological and genetic features of the original tumors, have emerged as promising tools for preclinical drug testing [14]. However, conventional mouse PDX (mPDX) models are time‐ and resource‐intensive, rendering them impractical for real‐time clinical decision‐making [14].

The zebrafish patient‐derived xenograft (zPDX) model has emerged as a potential alternative, offering advantages such as a short generation time, low cost, and rapid in vivo analysis [15]. While zPDX models have shown promise across various tumor types and have already been investigated for BCG response in NMIBC, their utility in advanced bladder cancer remains largely unexplored [16].

In this study, we aimed to develop and validate a zPDX model for evaluating CDDP sensitivity in MIBC using a three‐step approach (Figure 1): (1) establishment of a robust, image‐based efficacy evaluation system using bladder cancer cell lines, (2) translational validation of zPDX findings against established mPDX models, and (3) application of this platform to patient‐derived bladder tumor samples to assess the correlation between zPDX responses and clinical outcomes. This work provides both technical and conceptual foundations for implementing zPDX models as a personalized treatment strategy in bladder cancer.

Establishment of a bladder cancer zPDX protocol through a three‐step comparative approach. Step 1: In vitro vs. zebrafish PDX (zPDX) drug sensitivity. Step 2: Mouse PDX (mPDX) vs. zPDX derived from the same patient tumor. Step 3: zPDX drug response vs. actual clinical response in the patient.

Material and Methods

UMUC3 and T24 bladder cancer cell lines and their CDDP‐resistant variants were used. Drug sensitivity was tested with WST‐8 assays in vitro. For zPDX, albino embryos at 2 days post‐fertilization (dpf) were injected into the duct of Cuvier (DoC) with cancer cells or patient‐derived tissues. CDDP was administered, the maximum tolerated dose was determined, and platinum levels were quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS). Tumor burden was assessed by fluorescence imaging (CellVoyager CQ1) using DsRed2 or CytoTell Ultra Green (CTUG).

Gene expression was analyzed by Cap Analysis of Gene Expression (CAGE) sequencing of RNA from in vitro cultures, mPDX, and zebrafish xenografts. Whole‐mount immunostaining for Ki‐67 and cleaved caspase‐3 assessed proliferation and apoptosis. mPDX models were established by implanting patient tumors into immunodeficient mice, and therapeutic response to CDDP was compared with controls. Tumor‐derived cells were also transplanted into zebrafish for parallel efficacy testing.

Statistics were performed using EZR version 1.54 [17]. Statistical analyses used Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, with significance at p < 0.05.

Detailed protocols are provided in Data S1.

Results

Step 1: Development of a zPDX Model Using Bladder Cancer Cell Lines

Determination of an Optimal Incubation Temperature for Zebrafish Xenograft Models of Bladder Cancer

The optimal rearing temperature for zebrafish is 28°C–28.5°C; however, human cancer cells cannot proliferate at this temperature [18]. Thus, zebrafish xenograft models are typically maintained at 31°C–37°C [18]. To establish a reliable model for bladder cancer drug sensitivity testing, we evaluated the appropriate incubation temperature.

We first assessed CDDP sensitivity in two human bladder cancer cell lines, UMUC3 and T24, using WST‐8 assays. Cells were treated with CDDP at 37°C for 72 h, yielding IC50 values of 1.98 μM (UMUC3) and 3.13 μM (T24). CDDP‐resistant cell lines, UMUC3CR and T24CR, showed IC50s of 3.97 and 5.68 μM at 37°C, respectively (Figure 2A,B and Figure S1).

CDDP sensitivity of bladder cancer cell lines in vitro. (A) A cell proliferation assay (WST‐8 assay) was performed using the CDDP‐sensitive bladder cancer cell line UMUC3‐DsRed2 (hereafter referred to as UMUC3) and the CDDP‐resistant cell line UMUC3‐DsRed2‐CR (hereafter UMUC3CR). Each experiment included five wells per CDDP concentration and was conducted with three technical replicates. Results are presented as mean ± SD. (B) IC50 values of UMUC3CR and UMUC3 were determined after 72 h of CDDP exposure. A calibration curve was generated from the average of three technical replicates, and IC50 was calculated accordingly. Replicate data are provided in Figure S1.

To examine temperature effects on viability, WST‐8 assays were performed at 32°C, 35°C, and 37°C. UMUC3 proliferation decreased at 32°C and 35°C versus 37°C (Figure S1), but CDDP IC50 values at these temperatures—2.33, 2.53, and 1.98 μM—showed no significant differences (Figure 2B).

These findings support the feasibility of conducting CDDP sensitivity assays at 35°C in the zebrafish xenograft model.

Effects of CDDP on Zebrafish

We tested intravascular CDDP administration in zebrafish, reflecting the standard route in humans. To determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD), various CDDP concentrations were injected into the DoC of 2‐dpf zebrafish. Concentrations above 500 μM led to < 60% survival at 3 days post‐injection (dpi) (Figure 3A). Based on this, 400 μM was set as the MTD, and efficacy tests were performed at or below this level.

Effects of CDDP injection on zebrafish embryos/larvae. (a) Zebrafish embryos at 2‐dpf were injected into the duct of Cuvier (DoC) with 0–3333 μM CDDP. Mean survival rates (%) were recorded at 0–3‐dpi (n = 20 per concentration, triplicate; mean ± SD). (b) Zebrafish injected with 400 μM CDDP at 2‐dpf were collected at 0–3‐dpi, freeze‐dried (n = 100 per time point), and analyzed for platinum (Pt) content using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS). Wet weight was measured (n = 10 per time point), and Pt concentration (μg/g wet tissue) per individual was estimated based on the mean wet weight.

To assess temporal changes in CDDP levels, the absolute platinum (Pt) content (ng) was measured by ICP‐MS [19]. Pt concentrations declined over time, mirroring blood kinetics in human CDDP administration [20](Figure 3B).

To evaluate drug response, UMUC3‐DsRed2—a bladder cancer cell line stably expressing red fluorescent protein—was transplanted into zebrafish. Representative images at 1 and 2 dpi are shown in Figure 4B,C. Cells were injected into the DoC, a common site for modeling hematogenous metastasis [21, 22]. Injected cells entered the circulation, disseminated systemically, and some localized to the caudal hematopoietic tissue [21, 22].

Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal image cytometer (CellVoyager CQ1; Yokogawa Electric Corporation, Japan), and analyzed with CQ1 software to quantify individual fluorescence areas as an index of tumor burden. Total fluorescence area was semi‐automatically measured with identical threshold settings across groups. Time‐dependent changes in relative fluorescence area after CDDP injection were analyzed in the same individual, revealing that the CDDP‐sensitive UMUC3‐DsRed2 exhibited a dose‐dependent decrease in fluorescence, whereas the CDDP‐resistant UMUC3CR maintained fluorescence signals, indicating in vivo resistance (Figure 4D). Results were consistent between in vitro and zebrafish models, demonstrating reproducibility (Figure S2).

Fluorescent Labeling of Cancer Cells for zPDX Analysis

This study aimed to establish a zPDX model by transplanting transurethrally collected bladder cancer tissue into zebrafish and evaluating CDDP efficacy. A key requirement is effective fluorescent labeling of cancer cells for quantitative imaging and analysis.

Fluorophores used for labeling can be broadly divided into two categories [23]. Endogenous reporters, that is, fluorescent protein expression via gene transfer, are widely used in vitro due to stable signals. However, gene transfer is often cytotoxic, may have low efficiency depending on cell type, and requires extended ex vivo manipulation to generate stable clones. In UMUC3, stable expression was achieved by chemical transfection, but this was difficult for fragile patient‐derived cells.

The other category is exogenous probes. We focused on CytoTell Ultra Green (CTUG), a cytoplasmic dye with strong fluorescence retention [24]. As CTUG's quantitative performance in zebrafish had not been reported, we evaluated it in vitro and in vivo.

To evaluate the quantitative properties of CTUG in vitro, UMUC3‐DsRed2 cells were stained with CTUG and subjected to serial dilution in 96‐well plates. After 48 h, fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal fluorescence microscope, and red (DsRed2) and green (CTUG) fluorescence areas were measured. Cell viability was assessed by WST‐8 assays, with absorbance at 450 nm plotted (Figure 5A and Figure S3). CTUG and DsRed2 showed comparable quantitative performance in vitro, without significant cytotoxicity at optimal concentrations.

Quantitative performance of DsRed2 and CytoTell UltraGreen (CTUG). (A) In vitro comparison of DsRed2 and CTUG fluorescence using dual‐fluorescent UMUC3‐DsRed2 cells stained with CTUG. Fluorescence area was plotted on the y‐axis, and WST‐8 absorbance at 450 nm, reflecting cell number, was plotted on the x‐axis. The graph shows a representative result from three independent experiments; replicates are shown in Figure S3a,b. (B) Dual‐fluorescent UMUC3‐DsRed2–implanted zebrafish larvae at 1‐dpi. Red fluorescence (excitation: 561 nm, emission: 617/73 nm) represents DsRed2, and green fluorescence (excitation: 488 nm, emission: 525/50 nm) represents CTUG. Yellow areas indicate overlap of the two signals. Scale bar = 1000 μm. (C) Relative fluorescence area by DsRed2 and CTUG in zebrafish transplanted with dual‐labeled UMUC3‐DsRed2 (box plots). CTUG effectively quantified the therapeutic effect of CDDP, comparable to DsRed2. Replicates are provided in Figure S3d,e. Statistical significance was determined by the Mann–Whitney U test vs. control. **p < 0.01.

Next, CTUG performance in zPDX was assessed using dual‐labeled cells (DsRed2 and CTUG) (Figure 5B). Like other cytoplasmic dyes, CTUG can transfer to macrophages upon cell lysis. However, signal from debris was excluded by applying a < 10 μm size filter during image analysis. Interestingly, CTUG was metabolized and excreted via the digestive tract, similar to carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) [21] (Figure S3). These properties enabled exclusion of extracellular fluorescence and tracking of viable cancer cells post‐transplantation.

Comparison of CTUG‐ and DsRed2‐based assessments in zebrafish showed strong consistency between control and CDDP‐treated groups. Both methods demonstrated significant fluorescence reduction with CDDP (Figure 5C), a trend reproducible across experiments (Figure S3), supporting CTUG as a reliable tool for zPDX analysis.

Validation With Additional Bladder Cancer Cell Lines

To further validate CTUG‐based labeling, T24 and its CDDP‐resistant counterpart, T24CR, were labeled with CTUG and transplanted into zebrafish. The zPDX model confirmed CDDP efficacy in T24 but not in T24CR (Figure S4).

Molecular Characteristics of Zebrafish Xenografts

Before Step 2, we performed molecular analyses using two complementary approaches to clarify the cellular behavior and molecular background of human bladder cancer cells in zebrafish.

Gene Expression Analysis via RNA Sequencing

To compare gene expression between in vitro and in vivo models, RNA was extracted from UMUC3‐DsRed2 cells in the logarithmic growth phase (in vitro) and from UMUC3‐DsRed2 cells transplanted into subcutaneous tissue of nude mice or the DoC in zebrafish embryos (in vivo). RNA sequencing using the CAGE was performed to analyze differentially expressed genes between in vitro and in vivo conditions. (Figure S5) [25].

In the zebrafish model, 1%–4% of RNA was human‐derived (Figure 6A), indicating transcriptional activity of human cancer cells within the zebrafish microenvironment. Overall, 1277 genes were differentially expressed in vivo (zebrafish and mouse) vs. in vitro (Figure 6B). Of these, 113 were related to stress responses, including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, oxidative stress, immune stress, mechanical stress, and acidic pH (Table S3). These findings suggest that cancer cells in zebrafish encounter microenvironmental stress similar to that in mouse xenografts.

CAGE‐analysis comparing human gene expression in xenograft models vs. in vitro. (A) Percentage of human‐derived genes in the cell line, mouse xenograft model, and zebrafish xenograft model. A higher level of contamination with zebrafish genes was observed in the zebrafish model, but human‐derived reads were detectable in all models. In vitro and the mouse model, RNA was extracted from three technical and biological replicates, respectively. In the zebrafish model, RNA was extracted in quadruplicate (Rep1: n = 22, Rep2: n = 41, Rep3: n = 20, and Rep4: n = 23). (B) Human‐derived genes that showed common expression variation in zebrafish xenograft and mouse xenograft models compared with gene expression in vitro are presented (FDR < 0.01). Genes in the upper right are significantly higher in expression in vivo than in vitro, and those in the lower left are significantly reduced in expression in vivo than in vitro. CAGE, cap analysis of gene expression; FDR, false discovery rate.

Immunostaining for Proliferation and Apoptosis Markers

As a second approach, whole‐mount immunostaining was performed. Zebrafish embryos were fixed at 2 dpi and analyzed in control and CDDP‐treated groups [26]. Ki67 and cleaved caspase‐3 served as markers for proliferation and apoptosis, respectively.

The control group showed higher Ki67 expression, indicating active proliferation, while cleaved caspase‐3 was elevated in the CDDP‐treated group, consistent with CDDP‐induced apoptosis (Figure S6). These results demonstrate that transplanted cancer cells proliferate and respond to CDDP in zebrafish, supporting the model's utility for evaluating tumor growth and therapeutic effect.

Summary of Step 1

In summary, CDDP sensitivity in bladder cancer cell lines was highly consistent between in vitro assays and the zPDX model, confirming the reproducibility of our approach. The zPDX model also reflected the molecular background observed in vitro.

Step 2: Validation of CDDP Sensitivity Across mPDX and zPDX Models

Establishment of mPDX Models and Evaluation of CDDP Sensitivity

Patient‐derived bladder cancer tissues obtained via transurethral resection were fragmented and subcutaneously transplanted into the backs of 4–6‐week‐old immunodeficient mice (NOD/ShiJic‐scid/Jcl). About 30% of tumors engrafted, and 5‐week‐old nude mice (BALB/cAJcl‐nu/nu) were used for passaging. Four stably passaged and cryopreserved mPDX models—MieUC1, MieUC5, MieUC7, and MieUC9—were established. Patient backgrounds are summarized in Table S1. mPDX tissues retained the histological morphology of the original tumors (Figure S7).

To assess CDDP efficacy, mice bearing MieUC1, MieUC5, MieUC7, or MieUC9 received intraperitoneal CDDP (4 mg/kg on days 1, 8, and 15; n = 8 ± 1 per group) [27]. MieUC1 and MieUC7 responded to CDDP, while MieUC5 and MieUC9 were resistant (Figure 7). Body weight did not differ significantly between groups (Figure S8).

Statistical significance was determined by Mann–Whitney U test rvs. control. **p < 0.01; n.s., not significant. (B, D, F, H) Relative fluorescence area (2‐dpi/1‐dpi) between the CDDP treatment group and the control group when MieUCx were transplanted into zebrafish after tissue dissociation (box plots). CDDP Sensitivities in mPDX Models Were Reproduced in zPDX Models

Successful transplantation of patient‐derived tissues into zebrafish required optimized dissociation and fluorescent labeling. Inadequate dissociation clogged injection needles, while over‐dissociation reduced viability. Higher CTUG concentrations improved signal retention, but could harm cells. Protocols were refined through repeated trials with mPDX models (Doc S1).

In zPDX experiments (Figure 7B,D,F,H and Figure S9), CDDP showed efficacy in MieUC1 and MieUC7, but not in MieUC5 and MieUC9, mirroring mPDX results.

Summary of Step 2

mPDX and zPDX models showed high concordance in predicting CDDP response.

Step 3: zPDX Predicts Clinical Response to CDDP in Patients With Bladder Cancer

Patient‐derived tissues from six cases were transplanted into zebrafish (Table S2), and CDDP efficacy was successfully assessed at 2 dpi in all, confirming the robustness of the system. Among these, two patients (Pt#4 and Pt#6) received CDDP‐based chemotherapy.

In Pt#4, zPDX accurately predicted CDDP sensitivity (Figure 8A). This patient had invasive urothelial carcinoma with plasmacytoid and lipid‐cell features and underwent radical cystectomy without NAC. Postoperative imaging revealed residual pararectal lymph node metastases, which disappeared after two cycles of gemcitabine and CDDP, achieving a complete response per RECIST version 1.1 (Figure 8B).

Correlation between zPDX evaluation using patient‐derived bladder cancer tissues and clinical outcomes. (A, C) Bladder cancer tissues obtained from Pt#4 and Pt#6 (Table S2) were transplanted into zebrafish to test CDDP efficacy. zPDX predicted sensitivity to CDDP in Pt#4 and resistance to CDDP in Pt#6. (B) Pararectal lymph node metastasis disappeared following gemcitabine‐CDDP therapy in Pt#4. (D) Almost no therapeutic effect was observed in bladder cancer tissues post‐NAC with gemcitabine‐CDDP in Pt#6. Scale bar: 50 μm. Statistical significance was determined by Mann–Whitney U test v.s. control. **p < 0.01; n.s., not significant.

In contrast, zPDX predicted CDDP resistance in Pt#6 (Figure 8C). This patient received NAC followed by cystectomy, but histopathology revealed predominantly viable tumor cells, indicating minimal effect (Figure 8D). In both cases, zPDX predictions matched clinical outcomes, supporting zPDX as a reliable tool for predicting CDDP efficacy.

Summary of Step 3

zPDX accurately predicted CDDP efficacy using patient‐derived samples, marking an initial step toward clinical application.

Discussion

Should all CDDP‐eligible patients with MIBC receive NAC? We developed a zPDX protocol that enables the evaluation of multiple individuals within a significantly shorter timeframe than other models and investigated its potential as a preclinical avatar model. Through a three‐step validation combining in vitro studies, mouse models, and clinical data, we demonstrated the robustness of zPDX. The zPDX model exhibited drug sensitivity comparable to that of other models, suggesting its potential as a predictive tool complementary to mPDX.

Invasive bladder cancer progresses rapidly, posing significant challenges in developing preclinical models that provide clinical benefits within the necessary timeframe. Patients with MIBC typically initiate CDDP‐based NAC within 2–3 weeks after transurethral resection for histopathological diagnosis. Although NAC achieves a pathological and complete response in 40.8% and 22.8%, respectively, it represents overtreatment for non‐responders [6]. Consequently, a rapid and reliable predictive model is essential to ensure timely cystectomy. While mouse and organoid/spheroid models have been developed for urothelial cancer, their use within this restricted timeframe remains challenging [28, 29]. Our findings highlight zebrafish as a viable option for clinical application [15].

Zebrafish offer several advantages over other animal models for studying cancer in vivo, making them an increasingly popular choice in cancer research [15, 30, 31, 32, 33]. One key advantage is the transparency of embryos and larvae, enabling real‐time visualization of tumor growth and metastasis using fluorescence microscopy [34]. While lipophilic membrane‐labeling dyes such as DiI are commonly used in zebrafish xenograft models, their quantification remains unreliable due to dye transfer to phagocytes, potentially generating false‐positive signals resembling micrometastases [35, 36]. Our initial attempts to label bladder cancer cells with DiD faced similar challenges, as distinguishing viable cells from phagocytes was difficult in confocal fluorescence images. We therefore tested cytoplasmic labeling dyes, which provide more uniform staining and are better suited for in vivo single‐cell tracking [21, 23]. Among them, CTUG, an optimized alternative to CFSE with reduced cytotoxicity and improved sensitivity [24], demonstrated stable fluorescence in vitro and in vivo for at least 3 days. Notably, CTUG has a unique characteristic in zebrafish: when released extracellularly, it is excreted through the digestive tract, minimizing background fluorescence (Figure 5 and Figure S3). Additionally, when phagocytosed, CTUG appears as smaller fragments, making it distinguishable from viable cells. By leveraging these advantages, our study is the first to validate CTUG's quantitative accuracy using dual‐fluorescent protein‐labeled cells in both zebrafish and in vitro models. These findings establish CTUG as an ideal fluorescent marker for zPDX, enabling reliable tumor tracking in zebrafish xenograft research.

Several injection sites have been considered for zebrafish xenotransplantation. While yolk sac transplantation is technically simple, we found that post‐transplantation imaging lacked clarity, making threshold determination for image analysis difficult. This issue may arise from the three‐dimensional distribution of cancer cells within the thick yolk sac and fluorescence interference from adjacent image slices [15]. Additionally, the yolk sac's protein‐ and lipid‐rich composition differs from the human tumor microenvironment, potentially compromising cancer cell survival [37]. Given these limitations, the DoC was selected as the primary transplantation site (Figure S10). In this study, a single researcher performed DoC injections with a success rate exceeding 90%. Compared with the perivitelline space, which has been commonly used in recent studies but presents technical challenges, DoC transplantation yielded higher success and sufficient numbers for robust statistical analysis.

Several studies have transplanted patient‐derived tumor tissues into zebrafish to assess drug efficacy [38, 39]. However, the limited availability of fresh patient samples makes them unsuitable for repeated experiments during protocol optimization. To overcome this, we employed a three‐step validation approach integrating in vitro studies, mouse models, and patient clinical data, enabling systematic troubleshooting and establishing a reliable protocol for handling patient‐derived samples. A similar strategy, using mPDX as an intermediary validation model for zPDX, was reported by Ali et al. in non‐small cell lung cancer and is recommended for developing zebrafish xenograft models [40].

Despite their advantages, zebrafish models have certain limitations that must be addressed [31]. Their breeding and maintenance require specialized expertise and dedicated facilities, and mastering microinjection techniques remains a technical barrier. Stepwise proficiency in transplantation, tissue dissociation, fluorescent labeling, and image analysis is essential for ensuring reproducibility. Additionally, our study was also limited by the number of patient samples, highlighting the need for future validation in larger cohorts. Establishing zPDX as a preclinical model for bladder cancer, it is crucial to evaluate drug responses and long‐term prognoses in a broader patient population. Beyond assessing concordance with clinical outcomes, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying zPDX responses is essential [41]. Cancer‐associated fibroblasts have been shown to enhance metastasis when co‐transplanted with cancer cells in zebrafish [42], and the zPDX system has been used to study tumor–microenvironment interactions [41]. Future research should validate zPDX in the context of the tumor microenvironment and cancer immunology.

Furthermore, this study mainly focused on CDDP as a representative cytotoxic agent. Although the zebrafish PDX system may potentially be applicable to other therapeutic modalities such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, antibody–drug conjugates, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors, it is unrealistic to expect that all drugs currently used in urothelial carcinoma can be tested in this system. This represents a limitation of our current approach. In addition, the difference in IC50 values between parental and cisplatin‐resistant sublines was relatively modest (approximately two‐fold). Although we believe that even such differences may reflect clinically relevant resistance to some extent, this should also be recognized as a limitation of the present study.

The evolving landscape of bladder cancer treatment, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, antibody‐drug conjugates, and FGFR inhibitors, represents a major advancement [43, 44, 45]. However, these therapies are significantly more expensive than conventional platinum‐based chemotherapy and present distinct adverse effects, highlighting the need for personalized treatment strategies. Predicting CDDP efficacy is crucial for optimizing bladder cancer management and has significant clinical implications. Moreover, the methodology established in this study extends beyond bladder cancer, offering a versatile platform for evaluating drug efficacy across various cancer types. Our zebrafish xenograft model holds promise as a preclinical avatar to guide clinical decision‐making and advance precision oncology.
