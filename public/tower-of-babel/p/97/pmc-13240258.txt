Clinical and Genetic Characterization of Inherited NPRL3 Splice Variants in Two Patients With Epilepsy
Authors: Shouxing Wang, Jihua Wu, Conglei Song, Yuanzhi He, Zhongli Zhao, Bin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground NPRL3‐related epilepsy (NRE) is a GATORopathy characterized by focal seizures, with or without focal cortical dysplasia. Although NPRL3 variants have been associated with syndromes such as familial focal epilepsy with variable foci and sleep‐related hypermotor epilepsy, the genotype–phenotype correlations in NRE remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the clinical and genetic features of NRE in two Chinese families, together with a review of the genotype–phenotype spectrum.MethodsTwo pediatric Chinese patients with NRE were identified through whole‐exome sequencing (WES). Clinical, neuroimaging, laboratory, and genetic data were analyzed. A review of reported NRE cases was conducted.ResultsInherited NPRL3 splice variants (NM_001077350.3: c.924 + 1G>A and c.925–1G>C) were identified in both probands using WES; both were inherited from their asymptomatic mothers. The patients presented with seizures starting at 1–2 years of age. Proband 1 experienced focal and focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (FBTCS), while Proband 2 had focal seizures. Neuroimaging findings were normal. Patient 1 achieved seizure control with sodium valproate, oxcarbazepine, and lacosamide, while Patient 2 responded to oxcarbazepine and levetiracetam. A literature review of 41 articles identified 94 NPRL3 variants in 84 pedigrees and 23 cohort cases with epilepsy, revealing an estimated penetrance of 76% (162/214). Loss‐of‐function variants predominated (83%), while splice variants were relatively rare (13%). No clear genotype–phenotype correlations were observed, even in cases with identical NPRL3 variants.ConclusionThe findings of the study highlight the genetic and clinical heterogeneity of NRE, which is characterized by incomplete penetrance and phenotypic variability. This underscores the importance of tailored antiepileptic management and the consideration of NPRL3 variants as causes of familial and sporadic epilepsy.Keywords: epilepsy, genotype–phenotype correlation, NPRL3, whole‐exome sequencing

Introduction

The GTPase‐activating protein activity toward Rags 1 (GATOR1) complex, comprising DEPDC5, NPRL2, and NPRL3, is a key negative regulator of mTOR signaling, involved in essential cellular processes such as protein synthesis, metabolism, synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and growth (Ivanova and Shen 2024). Loss‐of‐function variants in GATOR1 components lead to constitutive mTOR activation, contributing to abnormal neuronal morphology, cortical lamination defects, and increased cell size (Bar‐Peled et al. 2013). These variants are associated with familial focal epilepsy with variable foci (FFEVF, OMIM #617118), an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by focal seizures with varying degrees of cortical involvement, as well as other focal epilepsy syndromes, including cases without detectable brain abnormalities (Riney et al. 2022). While DEPDC5 is the most commonly affected GATOR1 gene, associated with approximately 8% of FFEVF cases, variants in NPRL2 and NPRL3 have also been linked with FFEVF and related conditions (Baldassari et al. 2019). Notably, more than half of individuals with GATOR1‐related epilepsy are resistant to traditional anti‐seizure medications (ASMs), highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches.

Nitrogen permease regulator‐like 3 (NPRL3), located on chromosome 16p13.3, encodes a component of the GATOR1 complex and is essential for development (Nabavi Nouri et al. 2024). Dysregulation of NPRL3 function leads to mTOR hyperactivation, disrupting neuronal development and contributing to seizures; enlarged neurons and aberrant mTOR signaling are observed in brain tissues in NPRL3‐related focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) (Iffland et al. 2022). Studies in animal models have confirmed this role. Deletion of NPRL3 in the mouse dorsal telencephalon results in spontaneous seizures, dysmorphic neurons, and increased mTOR signaling, while NPRL3 knockdown in neuronal cell lines is associated with abnormal cell morphology and disrupted mTOR localization in neural precursors (Iffland II et al. 2018). Most NPRL3 variants result in truncated proteins, disrupting the GATOR1 complex and inducing hyperactivation of mTOR. These variants are associated with several clinical phenotypes, including familial and sporadic focal epilepsy with or without FCD (Du et al. 2023). Focal seizures associated with NPRL3 variants can arise in frontal, temporal, parietal, or occipital cortical regions of the brain, often associated with conditions such as sleep‐related hypermotor epilepsy (SHE) and benign epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes (BECT) (Dainelli et al. 2023). However, the relationship between genotype and phenotype in NPRL3‐related epilepsy (NRE) remains poorly understood.

There is limited research on NRE, particularly in Chinese populations. In this study, whole‐exome sequencing (WES) was used to investigate the molecular basis of epilepsy in two Chinese patients. This led to the identification of splice variants in NPRL3, enabling the diagnosis of FFEVF. The genotype–phenotype association was also explored to enhance understanding of NRE and provide a foundation for improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Considerations

The legal guardians of the participants provided written informed consent and permission to publish medical information and images. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Board at Anhui Provincial Children's Hospital and was conducted under the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (EYLL‐2023‐024).

Whole‐Exome Sequencing

DNA was isolated from 2 mL of peripheral blood collected from the probands and their parents using the Blood Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Cwbio Biotechnology, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. The DNA concentration was determined using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Exome sequencing was conducted using the IDT xGen Exome Research Panel v2.0 (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA), with sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), achieving an average 100× depth of coverage. Paired‐end reads (150 bp) were sequenced.

Bioinformatics Analysis

The sequencing data were processed using FASTQ software (Illumina) for the removal of low‐quality reads. The remaining high‐quality sequences were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) using BWA v0.7.17. Variants, including single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels, were detected using GATK v4.1.8.1, which also aided in the removal of duplicates, realignments around indels, and recalibration of the base quality scores. The generated VCF files were integrated using a family‐based strategy. The variants were annotated using ANNOVAR, with reference to allele frequencies from the dbSNP, 1000 Genomes Project, ExAC, and gnomAD databases. The potential impact of variants was evaluated using tools such as MutationTaster, SIFT, PolyPhen‐2, CADD, Revel, PROVEAN, and M‐CAP. Splice‐site analysis was conducted using SpliceAI and MaxEntScan, while conservation scores were determined using GERP+, phyloP, and phastCons. Each sample was analyzed for potentially harmful variants, such as nonsense, splice‐site, frameshift, in‐frame, and non‐synonymous changes, based on their predicted effects. Variants were further analyzed and interpreted using disease‐gene databases, including HGMD, ClinVar, and OMIM, as well as by literature searches. Variant pathogenicity was assessed according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines (Richards et al. 2015). Finally, the authenticity of the identified variants was verified through Sanger sequencing; the primer sequences as well as further details can be provided upon request.

Results

Clinical Presentation

Proband 1: A 7‐year‐old boy presented to Anhui Provincial Children's Hospital in April 2024 with a chronic history of recurrent unprovoked seizures for more than 4 years. His seizures manifested in two distinct forms: (1) Focal seizures: These episodes were associated with localized symptoms, including shouting, blinking, twitching of the corner of the mouth, clenching of both fists, and unresponsiveness to calls. Each episode lasted approximately 10 s and resolved spontaneously with normal mental response. (2) Focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (FBTCS): The symptoms included deviation of the eyes to one side, blinking, slanting of the mouth corner to the right, cyanosis of the lips, frothing at the mouth, and strong tetany of the limbs. These episodes did not respond to external stimuli, lasted 1–4 h, and required sedative intervention for resolution. The postictal symptoms included transient fever without vomiting or diarrhea. Neurological examination revealed no significant abnormalities. The intermittent seizures were treated sequentially with sodium valproate, oxcarbazepine, and lacosamide, resulting in the cessation of seizures. The patient was born full‐term via spontaneous vaginal delivery (G3P3) and showed normal development, including fine motor skills, gross motor skills, language, and cognition, comparable to that of peers. While there was no consanguinity in the family, both his grandmother and aunt had a suspected history of epilepsy. His parents and two sisters were healthy and had no history of serious illnesses (Figure 1A). The results of laboratory investigations, including metabolic screening, blood ammonia, pyruvic acid, lactic acid, and tests for liver and kidney function, were unremarkable. Cerebral MRI showed no abnormal signals in the brain parenchyma. Video electroencephalogram (VEEG) demonstrated background activity at 9 Hz, with medium to high‐amplitude irregular slow waves apparent mostly in the bilateral frontopolar, frontal, and temporal regions during interictal phases, which were more pronounced on the right side. Sporadic or clustered discharges were also observed (Figure 2A).

Proband 2: A 1‐year and 6‐month‐old girl presented to Anhui Provincial Children's Hospital in September 2023 after experiencing recurrent seizures for over one month. The seizures, which occurred without identifiable triggers, were focal and characterized by sudden dropping of the head, unresponsiveness to calls, deviation of both eyes to one side, eyelid fluttering, and clenching of both fists. Each episode lasted from several seconds to half a minute and resolved spontaneously with a normal mental response. The seizures occurred every 3–4 days, ranging from a single episode per day to clusters over two days with 1–2 episodes each day. No fever, vomiting, or diarrhea was reported before or after the seizures. Neurological examination revealed no significant abnormalities. The seizures were controlled with sequential administration of oxcarbazepine and levetiracetam. The last seizure occurred in April 2024 with no further episodes since. The patient was born at 38 weeks and 5 days via cesarean section (G1P1) and had no history of asphyxia or hypoxia (Figure 1B). There was no family history of epilepsy. The child's motor, language, and cognitive development was comparable to that of her peers. Cerebral MRI findings were normal. However, VEEG revealed background activity at 5–6 Hz with high to extremely high‐amplitude sharp waves and sharp‐slow waves predominantly in the right parietal, occipital, and posterior temporal regions during wakefulness and sleep. These discharges were almost continuous and were more pronounced during sleep. Additionally, sharp waves of medium to high amplitude were observed sporadically in the left frontal region, more frequently during sleep (Figure 2B). Laboratory investigations were unremarkable except for a single post‐ictal lactate elevation of 4.0 mmol/L in Proband 2, with no other metabolic abnormalities.

Identification of Variants

Analysis of the WES results identified pathogenic variants in the NPRL3 associated with epilepsy. Proband 1 and his mother were found to carry a heterozygous splicing variant, NM_001077350.3: c.924 + 1G>A, located in intron 9 of NPRL3. Similarly, Proband 2 and her mother were identified as carriers of another heterozygous splicing variant, NM_001077350.3: c.925−1G>C, also in intron 9 of the same gene (Figure 3). Unfortunately, genetic testing was not possible for Proband 1's grandmother and aunt, who had suspected epilepsy, due to the unavailability of their sample. Both variants disrupt canonical splice sites, resulting in predicted aberrant splicing potentially leading to exon skipping, frameshift variants, and the introduction of premature termination codons (PTCs). These events may cause protein truncation degradation via nonsense‐mediated decay (NMD) (PVS1). We emphasize that these are in silico predictions requiring experimental validation (e.g., via minigene assays or patient RNA analysis). The c.924 + 1G>A variant is predicted to disrupt the canonical splice donor site in intron 9, leading to aberrant splicing. This defect could result in the skipping of exon 9, causing a deletion of 157 bp and inducing a frameshift variant. Activation of a cryptic splice donor site could potentially lead to a deletion of 110 bp. The c.925−1G>C variant is likely to disrupt the canonical splice acceptor site in intron 9, also causing aberrant splicing. Potential outcomes include skipping of exon 10, resulting in a deletion of 107 bp and a frameshift variant, or activation of an alternative splice acceptor site, leading in a 9‐bp deletion (Figure 4). Comprehensive in silico analysis demonstrated near‐complete disruption of canonical splicing by both NPRL3 variants. SpliceAI and Pangolin heatmaps revealed profound splice‐site impairment (c.924 + 1G>A: Δdonor = 0.99; c.925−1G>C: Δacceptor = 0.99). MaxEntScan confirmed deleterious impacts through significant score reductions (c.924 + 1G>A: 8.73 → 0.54 [↓94%]; c.925−1G>C: 10.03 → 1.97 [↓80%]) (Figure 4). The c.924 + 1G>A variant was identified in gnomAD v4.1.0 with an allele count of 1/1,613,110. The c.925−1G>C variant was absent from major population databases, including dbSNP, the 1000 Genomes Project, and gnomAD (PM2_Supporting). Based on ACMG criteria, these variants were classified as likely pathogenic (PVS1 + PM2_Supporting). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of familial focal epilepsy with variable foci (FFEVF) in both cases. WES did not identify any additional pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in known epilepsy‐associated genes.

Literature Review

Searches using the term “NPRL3” in the PubMed and HGMD Professional databases up to December 2024 identified 41 publications. These included 166 patients from 84 pedigrees with NRE and 23 additional cases from epilepsy cohorts (Table 1, Table S1). These individuals carried 94 unique NPRL3 variants, encompassing SNVs and gross deletions distributed throughout the gene (Table S2), with a penetrance of 76% (162/214) and significant clinical variability. Penetrance was calculated by dividing the number of reported symptomatic individuals (n = 162) by the total number of at‐risk individuals (symptomatic and asymptomatic carriers) from the 84 pedigrees (n = 214). The age of onset was recorded in 128 cases and ranged from birth to 51 years, with considerable variations seen in carriers of the same variant, emphasizing the importance of considering NPRL3 variants across all ages and asymptomatic individuals. Focal seizures were the most common presentation (57/166, 34.3%), while associated syndromes included SHE (20 cases), frontal lobe epilepsy (17 cases), temporal lobe epilepsy (10 cases), tonic–clonic seizures (25 cases), and febrile seizures/febrile seizures plus (FS+, 6 cases). The results of brain MRI, available for 111 patients, indicated abnormalities in 42 cases (39%), with postoperative confirmation of FCD in 25 patients (FCD IIa: 15 cases; FCD IIb: 3 cases). Neurodevelopmental delay was reported in 25 individuals, and EEG findings were abnormal in 86 of 97 patients (89%). Drug‐resistant epilepsy was noted in 53% (62/112) of cases, and 34 patients underwent surgery for epilepsy. No clear genotype–phenotype correlation was identified, even among individuals with identical variants. The variant types included gross (28.7%), frameshift (27.6%), nonsense (14.9%), missense (13.8%), splice‐site (12.8%), and in‐frame (2.2%) variants, with 15 cases involving NPRL3 splice variants, as reported in this study (Table 2, Figure 3C). The age distribution did not differ significantly across variant types (Table 1). No variant hotspots were identified, with only seven recurrent variants reported across families.

Discussion

This study identified two NPRL3 splice variants (NM_001077350.3: c.924 + 1G>A and c.925−1G>C) in unrelated Chinese patients with epilepsy via WES. Both were inherited from asymptomatic mothers, demonstrating the incomplete penetrance characteristic of NRE and other GATORopathies (Dainelli et al. 2023). The distinct clinical presentations—Proband 1 with focal and generalized seizures versus Proband 2 with features consistent with FFEVF—highlight the phenotypic spectrum. Misdiagnoses are common in China due to limited genetic access. WES is recommended for suspected familial epilepsy to enable accurate and early diagnosis.

FFEVF, an autosomal dominant syndrome caused by NPRL3 variants (Ricos et al. 2016; Sim et al. 2016; Weckhuysen et al. 2016), exhibits marked heterogeneity. Seizures originate from diverse cortical regions with onset from birth to adulthood (Table 1). Our patients' features align with this known heterogeneity. Structural abnormalities like FCD are reported in some NRE cases, yet many, including both probands here, lack detectable MRI lesions, suggesting neurofunctional disruptions may underlie seizures rather than overt structural malformations in a subset. A ‘two‐hit’ hypothesis, involving modulation of germline NPRL3 variant effects by somatic variants or environmental factors during brain development, has been proposed to explain this variability and incomplete penetrance (Li et al. 2024). This model has significant implications for genetic counseling. Asymptomatic carriers, like the mothers in our study, may still harbor somatic variants in brain tissue that are undetectable in blood, contributing to the phenotypic variability. Advanced neuroimaging (e.g., 7T MRI) or, if clinically indicated, deep sequencing of surgical brain tissue could potentially identify such second hits, though these remain primarily research tools at present. EEG findings showed focal epileptiform discharges, consistent with our probands' EEGs (right frontal/mid‐temporal irregular slow/spike‐waves in P1; right parietal/posterior temporal high‐amplitude spikes in P2). Notably, most NRE patients, including ours, exhibit normal cognitive development (Dainelli et al. 2023). It is important to acknowledge the limitations in establishing the FFEVF diagnosis for Proband 1. A limitation: in Family 1, the grandmother and aunt with suspected epilepsy could not be tested, preventing definitive segregation analysis and underscoring the challenge of diagnosing FFEVF in small families with incomplete testing.

The neuropathological correlate of GATOR1 variants is increasingly well‐characterized. Unlike the migration deficits seen in MTOR‐related FCD type IIb, GATOR1‐related FCD type IIa often exhibits a distinct vacuolizing phenotype associated with autophagy alterations, including p62‐positive aggregates within autophagosomes (Honke et al. 2023). Furthermore, secondary lysosomal dysfunction and lipofuscin accumulation have been observed in dysmorphic neurons of FCDIIa, as recently reported in a patient with a novel NPRL3 splice variant (Garbelli et al. 2026). These findings highlight that disrupted mTORC1 regulation by GATOR1 leads to a specific histopathological signature involving autophagy‐lysosomal pathway dysfunction, which may underlie the epileptogenicity even in the absence of gross MRI‐detectable lesions.

The epilepsy phenotype associated with GATOR1 complex variants (NPRL3, DEPDC5, NPRL2) is generally not subunit‐specific due to the functional interdependence (Zhang et al. 2023). Variants are predominantly loss‐of‐function (LoF), disrupting GATOR1‐mediated mTORC1 inhibition (Krochmalnek et al. 2023). Haploinsufficiency via nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay (NMD) is a primary mechanism (Yin et al. 2023). However, some premature termination codon (PTC)‐containing mRNAs may escape NMD, producing truncated proteins that could exert dominant‐negative effects or gain‐of‐function. Critically, direct functional validation of our splice variants (c.924 + 1G>A, c.925−1G>C) is needed—the splicing outcomes in Figure 4 are computational predictions. Future minigene assays or RNA analysis from patient‐derived cells will confirm aberrant splicing and assess effects on NPRL3 expression and mTOR pathway activity. Currently, only 13 distinct NPRL3 splice variants have been reported, associated with a wide seizure spectrum, variable EEG/MRI findings, and early onset (Table 2). For instance, the NPRL3 c.1137dup variant evades NMD (Wang, Liu, et al. 2023), offering a plausible mechanism for phenotypic variability. Here, Proband 1 (c.924 + 1G>A) lacked MRI abnormalities, contrasting with a prior report linking this variant to FCD IIb (Straka et al. 2023). Proband 2 (c.925−1G>C) presented mildly despite the variant being reported across ages (McKnight et al. 2022; Truty et al. 2019). The rarity of c.924 + 1G>A in gnomAD, its occurrence in asymptomatic carriers, and the observed phenotypes support likely pathogenicity. Splice variants might evade NMD and generate truncated proteins disrupting GATOR1 assembly via dominant‐negative interactions (Du et al. 2023), potentially explaining severe phenotypes. Additionally, epigenetic modifiers regulating mTORC1 feedback could further modulate expressivity (Ivanova and Shen 2024), underscoring the need for individual variant characterization.

Therapeutic responses in NRE vary. Sodium channel blockers (carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine) are often effective first‐line treatments, but ~50% of patients develop drug‐resistant epilepsy (Zhang et al. 2023). For these, epilepsy surgery is highly effective when a well‐defined epileptogenic zone and MRI‐detectable lesion (e.g., FCD) are present; comprehensive presurgical evaluation (PET, ictal SPECT, intracranial EEG) is essential (Wang, Yu, et al. 2023). Our patients responded well to tailored regimens: Proband 1 achieved control with valproate, oxcarbazepine, and lacosamide; Proband 2 responded to oxcarbazepine and levetiracetam. For drug‐resistant epilepsy without a resectable lesion, mTOR inhibitors (e.g., everolimus) represent a promising targeted therapy given the shared downstream mechanism with DEPDC5 and TSC. Future research should elucidate biomarkers for drug resistance and surgical candidacy, and explore mTOR inhibitors, particularly for patients without resectable lesions.

In conclusion, this study expands the phenotypic and genotypic spectrum of NRE and reinforces the critical role of genetic testing. The identification of NPRL3 splice variants c.924 + 1G>A and c.925−1G>C, despite challenges posed by incomplete penetrance, contributes to understanding this complex disorder. Future work must prioritize functional validation and explore the roles of epigenetic factors, somatic variants, and genetic modifiers to enhance diagnostic precision and treatment strategies.
