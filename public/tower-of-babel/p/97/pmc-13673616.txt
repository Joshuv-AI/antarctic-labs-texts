Rhizobacterial functional assemblies modulate accumulation of phthalates in choysum (Brassica parachinensis L.)
Authors: Dan-Yang Cao, Rui-Ting Wu, Huan Du, Mo-Lin Li, Jin-Cheng Ye, Lei Xiang, Nai-Xian Feng, Bai-Lin Liu, Yan-Wen Li, Quan-Ying Cai, Qing X. Li, Ce-Hui Mo, Hai-Ming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRhizomicrobiomes exhibit dynamic adaptability in their structural and functional compositions in response to environmental stressors, playing pivotal roles in modulating host plant tolerance and growth. Nevertheless, the responses of rhizomicrobiomes to pollution stress remain poorly understood. In this study, we investigate how di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), a widespread soil contaminant, alters the rhizomicrobiomes of two choysum (Brassica parachinensis L.) varieties and how these stress-regulated microbial communities contribute to the distinct response patterns between the low-accumulation variety (LAV) and the high-accumulation variety (HAV).ResultsThe two choysum varieties exhibited differential recruitment of bacterial taxa in response to DEHP. The DEHP-sensitive LAV variety preferentially recruited DEHP-degrading bacteria, adopting an “avoidance-like” pattern to minimize DEHP accumulation. In contrast, the DEHP-tolerant HAV variety recruited plant growth-promoting bacteria, prioritizing growth regardless of DEHP accumulation. Synthetic microbial communities (SynComs) constructed from variety-specific isolates, along with corresponding cross-inoculation experiments, confirmed preferential colonization within host plants and the resulting phenotypic outcomes. These findings suggest that variety-specific rhizomicrobiome assembly confers distinct patterns in response to DEHP contamination, offering a potential mitigation approach to enhance food safety.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates variety-specific response patterns to soil organic pollutant (DEHP) from the perspective of rhizosphere microbial assembly, and establishes a direct link between DEHP accumulation phenotypes and functionally divergent rhizobacterial assemblies validated by SynCom experiments. Introduction

Extensive use of phthalates (PAEs)-containing products such as plastic films, pesticides, and fertilizers has led to ubiquitous distribution of PAEs in agricultural soils [1]. Currently, PAEs have become the second most abundant environmental pollutants worldwide, and the richest organic pollutant in agricultural soils, raising great concerns on agri-environmental security and sustainable crop production [2]. Numerous investigations on PAE residues in Chinese agricultural soils show that the total PAE contents commonly exceed 100 mg/kg, with di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) being the predominant PAE species [2, 3]. As a typical endocrine-disrupting chemical and the priority pollutant, DEHP has various adverse effects on human health, starting from birth defects to developmental disorders, deadly diseases like cancer and even immunological disorders [4]. PAEs including DEHP in agricultural soils tend to be taken up by and bioaccumulated in various crops, thus posing an enormous threat to food safety and human health [2]. It is essential to minimize PAE accumulation in crops to ensure the safety of agricultural products.

Screening crop varieties accumulating specific pollutants at levels low enough for food safety is considered a promising approach to ensure human health in commonly contaminated agricultural soil [5–7]. A number of pollutant low-accumulating varieties (LAVs) for cereals and vegetables have been identified to minimize toxic pollutant content in crops, and related mechanisms have also been advanced to the molecular level based on physiological and biochemical studies [5–10]. Although the accumulation traits of plants for the specific pollutants are often considered to be controlled by physiological and genetic bases [11–13], rhizomicrobiome is also found to be an important factor influencing plant growth and pollutant bioaccumulation [14–16]. Rhizomicrobiome plays key roles in controlling the bioavailability, uptake, and transformation processes of heavy metals [14, 17, 18]. Rhizomicrobiomes influence plant survival and growth via plant-soil feedbacks, thereby shaping plant performance and evolution under abiotic stress [17, 19]. Various plants differ in their rhizosphere microbial communities and their response to particular microbial species [14, 16, 17], but the related mechanisms remain unclear, necessitating in-depth research.

Plants are capable of assembling beneficial rhizomicrobiomes through a “cry for help” mechanism in response to biotic and/or abiotic stresses [20]. This phenomenon is originally described in plant defense against pathogens, and it is also found to be part of the adaptation strategy to ensure the “holobiont” (plant and its co-evolved microbiome) health in contaminated environments [21]. The specific rhizomicrobiome assembly is dependent not only on the microbes that are most directly affected by plant introduction, but also on the pollutants and their levels [22, 23]. The plants growing in polychlorinated biphenyl (PCB) contaminated soils modify their root exudates in a “cry for help” strategy to recruit PCB-degrading microbes in the rhizosphere due to the phytotoxic effects [24]. Notably, the keystone taxa within the rhizomicrobiome, like the above pollutant-degrading microbes, can exert a significant influence on the structure and function of microbiome through mutualistic biotic interactions with the host, independent of their abundance [25]. In crop production, the important roles of keystone taxa in host productivity and health have been confirmed [26]. For example, a 4-decade fertilization experiment provides solid evidence that the keystone taxa are essential for supporting plant production and soil functions [27]. Therefore, identifying the keystone taxa is crucial for further understanding the interaction process between rhizosphere microbes and plants, which is also a key step for constructing synthetic communities (SynComs) to manipulate the stably associated microbiomes for improving plant growth and performance [28]. However, the current understanding of microbial keystone species influencing plant performance in response to soil pollution is still in its infancy. Besides, previous rhizosphere microbiome studies were focused mainly on the taxonomic characterization [29, 30], but hardly on the functional traits involved in host performance.

In this study, we aimed to explore the rhizobacterial communities of the two choysum varieties differing DEHP accumulation (LAV and HAV) under DEHP stress [5, 11, 31, 32], and to reveal the functions of key rhizobacteria in regulating plant growth and DEHP accumulation. We clearly propose three core hypotheses: (1) the two choysum varieties recruit distinct rhizobacterial communities in response to DEHP stress; (2) the differentially recruited rhizobacteria perform distinct functions (DEHP degradation vs. plant growth promotion), supporting two contrasting response patterns for the two varieties; (3) the variety-specific rhizobacterial assembly contributes to shaping plant DEHP tolerance and accumulation phenotypes. To test these hypotheses, we analyzed rhizobacterial community structure and carbon metabolism patterns, identified key functional taxa, constructed variety-specific SynComs, and performed cross-inoculation experiments to verify their functions. Our results reveal variety-specific rhizobacterial functional assemblies in response to DEHP pollution stress, and elucidate the formation mechanism of LAV at the perspective of core rhizosphere microbiome, suggesting that the variety-driven rhizobacterial selection appears to confer a distinct response pattern on plants for better adaptation to soil pollution stress.

Results

Plant biomass and DEHP concentration

Compared with the control, shoot and root biomasses of both varieties changed little under 20 mg/kg DEHP treatment (except for the root biomass of LAV), but decreased significantly (P = 0.016, Cohen’s d = 1.08) under 100 mg/kg DEHP treatment (Fig. 1A). Notably, the biomasses of LAV were significantly lower (P = 0.013, Cohen’s d = 1.12) than those of HAV when exposed to DEHP, indicating that LAV was more sensitive to DEHP stress. DEHP concentrations in both the varieties increased significantly with increasing soil DEHP levels (P < 0.001, Fig. 1B), with 2.4~6.4-fold lower in LAV than in HAV (P = 0.004, Cohen’s d = 1.27). This was consistent with our previous report (3.7~6.8-fold) [5], suggesting stable phenotype variations in DEHP accumulation between the choysum varieties. Unexpectedly, DEHP residues in the rhizosphere soils of LAV were also significantly lower than those of HAV. Notably, DEHP residues in rhizosphere soils of both LAV and HAV were significantly lower than those of non-rhizosphere soils and unplanted soils, showing a great rhizosphere DEHP degradation by rhizosphere microorganisms driven by plant. Carbon source utilization patterns of rhizomicrobiome

Figure 2A shows the carbon metabolic activities (AWCDs) of rhizomicrobiome of two choysum varieties exposed to different levels of DEHP. There were obvious differences in the dynamic changes of the AWCD values between the two varieties, especially under DEHP exposure. After FDR correction, the AWCD dynamic values of LAV were significantly greater than those of HAV (q < 0.05), indicating that the rhizomicrobiome of LAV harbored higher metabolic activity toward soil carbon sources. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation of rhizobacterial communities between the two varieties under DEHP exposure. PERMANOVA analysis based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity confirmed that choysum variety significantly influenced community structure (R2 = 0.41, P = 0.001), explaining a substantial proportion of the observed variation. Specifically, PCA ordination illustrated that HAV had three groups located in the direction of PCA1, and LAV had three groups positioned in the negative direction of the PCA1 axis (Fig. 2B and Table S3), also suggesting significant differences in the utilization of carbon sources between the two varieties. Furthermore, scatterplot analysis revealed that microbial metabolism underpinning the utilization ability of most available carbon substrates was considerably higher in LAV than in HAV (Fig. 2 C). Figure 2Dsummarizes the relative utilization ratio of some complex carbon sources by rhizosphere microbes of the two varieties. After exposure to DEHP, there were greater percentages of aromatic compounds (phenolic acids) and polymers metabolism in LAV rhizosphere than in HAV rhizosphere, suggesting a more obvious enhancement of specific metabolic potentials in the former. Variation in rhizobacterial community diversity

The rhizobacterial α-diversity of two choysum varieties exposed to DEHP was estimated using the indices of observed species (ASVs), Shannon, and Simpson (1-D) (Fig. S3A). There were no significant differences (q > 0.05) in the ASVs among all soil samples, indicating that soil bacterial richness changed little under choysum planting and DEHP stress. The indices of Shannon and Simpson (1-D) decreased more significantly in choysum-planting soil than in unplanted soil (q = 0.018 and q = 0.024, respectively). Notably, the LAV had significantly lower indices of Shannon and Simpson (1-D) than the HAV when exposed to DEHP, suggesting its rhizobacterial species richness and evenness reduced more greatly. That is, the rhizobacterial responses to DEHP stress were different between two choysum varieties. Especially, the LAV rhizobacterial community showed substantial shifts in composition in response to DEHP. Consistently, a PCA plot showed clearly separated rhizobacterial communities between choysum varieties when exposed to DEHP, which explained 79.64% (65.36% and 14.28%) of the overall variation (Fig. S3B). This suggested that the rhizobacterial communities of the two choysum varieties presented different degrees of aggregation under DEHP exposure. Overall, these results indicated that the two varieties form distinct rhizobacterial assembly patterns under DEHP stress, which lay the foundation for their different functional strategies.

Differences in rhizobacterial assembly between the two choysum varieties

The dominant phyla in all the soil samples were Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, and Acidobacteria, covering over 85% of the bacteria (Fig. 3A). The two varieties of choysum differed in the assembly of rhizosphere taxa at the level of phylum in response to DEHP (Table S4). Figure 3B shows differences in the relative abundances of dominant rhizobacterial genera between the two choysum varieties (relative abundance > 1%). The relative abundances of Sphingomonas (Actinobacteria), Pseudomonas (Proteobacteria), Flavobacterium (Proteobacteria), Gordonia (Actinobacteria), and Rhodococcus (Actinobacteria) were greater in LAV than in HAV, while the relative abundances of Massilia (Proteobacteria), Bacillus (Firmicutes), Enterobacter (Proteobacteria), Streptomyces (Actinobacteria), and Rhodanobacter (Proteobacteria) were lower in LAV than in HAV. Furthermore, the differences in taxa abundance in the rhizosphere of two choysum varieties were identified using the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe, LDA score > 3.0), explaining the recruitment of rhizobacterial communities in terms of the relative abundances (Fig. 3C). In general, there were significant differences in the assembly of rhizobacterial taxa at the level of genus between the two choysum varieties exposed to DEHP, with variety-specific rhizosphere bacterial taxa regardless of DEHP levels. There were two specific taxa Sphingomonas and Pseudomonas significantly enriched in the LAV rhizosphere, and four specific taxa Streptomyces, Bacillus, Massilia, and Rhodopseudomonas significantly enriched in the HAV rhizosphere. The ternary plots also showed that the numbers of specific ASVs differed significantly between the LAV and HAV (Fig. 3D). Construction of the variety-specific rhizobacterial SynCom

To reveal the function of the rhizobacterial microbiome, the variety-specific rhizobacterial strains as determined by the ASV data mentioned above were isolated. About 500 bacterial isolates were obtained from the rhizospheres of the two varieties under DEHP stress by a culture-dependent method. The V3–V4 region of each isolate based on amplicon sequencing of the full-length 16S rRNA gene was mapped back to the ASV enriched in the rhizosphere soil of LAV or HAV. In addition, the strains were selected from different genera so as to promote community diversity and guarantee strain identification in the final community. No noticeable antagonistic effects were observed between isolates when the five strains in the SynLAV or SynHAV were compared in pairs, exhibiting close coexistence within the same SynCom (Fig. S4). Ultimately, two groups of core rhizobacteria with ten representative strains were selected to construct simplified SynLAV and SynHAV, respectively (Table S2). Functional characteristics differed significantly between the two SynComs. As expected, the SynLAV presented the potentials mainly in DEHP degradation but hardly in plant growth promotion, while the SynHAV presented the opposite potentials.

Consequences of SynCom inoculation in two choysum varieties

The rhizosphere cross-inoculation experiment for the two choysum varieties under DEHP stress was conducted to examine the consequences of SynLAV and SynHAV inoculation (Fig. 4). The rhizosphere colonization of the SynCom strains was measured after 2 weeks of inoculation. With the V3-V4 region of fully sequenced 16S rRNA gene as the expected sequence, the SynCom strains could be matched to ASVs from the mentioned-above rhizosphere community analyses, serving as an indicator for strain presence. The ASVs with > 99% sequence identity to any of the SynCom strains were designated as targeted ASVs. The results showed that nine targeted ASVs were tracked among the ten SynCom strains. For the SynLAV inoculation, there were three targeted ASVs (Pseudomonas sp. L29, Sphingomonas sp. L75, and Flavobacterium sp. L11) detected in the rhizosphere soil of LAV, but no targeted ASVs detected in the rhizosphere soil of HAV (Fig. 4A). Instead, for the SynHAV inoculation, there were five targeted ASVs (Bacillus sp. H297, Enterobacter sp. H20, Massilia sp. H156, Rhodanobacter sp. H323, and Streptomyces sp. H96) detected in the rhizosphere soil of HAV, and two strains (Bacillus sp. H297 and Massilia sp. The two choysum varieties inoculated with SynHAV increased biomass more significantly than SynLAV, particularly in the HAV, due to more effective rhizosphere colonization and plant growth promotion function (Fig. 4A). Notably, inoculation of the two varieties (especially the LAV) with SynLAV significantly reduced rhizosphere soil DEHP residues and plant DEHP accumulation in comparison to uninoculated controls. Conversely, rhizosphere soil DEHP residues decreased insignificantly and plant DEHP accumulation even increased significantly when inoculated with SynHAV (Fig. 4B). Overall, these results implied that variety-specific SynComs were able to successfully colonize their original host rhizospheres, greatly affecting plant biomass and DEHP accumulation. In addition, OLS regression models showed variety-specific linear relationships between the abundance of colonized SynComs and the plant responses to DEHP (i.e., biomass, soil DEHP residue and plant DEHP accumulation) (Fig. 5B and C). The relative abundances of SynLAV were significantly negatively correlated with the plant biomass, soil DEHP residue, and plant DEHP accumulation (Fig. 5B), while relative abundances of SynHAV were significantly positively correlated with the plant biomass and DEHP accumulation (Fig. 5C), further confirming the roles of recruited function-specific rhizobacteria in contributing to the two varieties in response to DEHP stress. Collectively, these results demonstrate that variety-specific rhizobacterial assemblies significantly influence plant growth performance and DEHP accumulation phenotypes, thereby substantiating our hypotheses.

Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated that the variety-specific PAE accumulation in choysum is associated with plant tolerance to PAE stress. The PAE-sensitive LAV minimizes PAE accumulation to avoid phytotoxicity [5, 11], while the PAE-tolerance HAV permits more PAE accumulation [11, 31, 32]. The underlying mechanisms of distinct tolerance to PAEs and resulting accumulation have been investigated at plant physiological and molecular levels. Here, we tried to address this question from the viewpoint of rhizosphere microbiome assembly and functions.

Variety-specific DEHP accumulation associated with plant tolerance and rhizosphere soil DEHP concentration

There were significant differences (P < 0.001) in root and shoot biomass between the two choysum varieties exposed to DEHP. The biomass of both varieties decreased significantly under high DEHP treatment due to DEHP-induced phytotoxicity at physiological and molecular levels [11, 31, 32]. The HAV plants presented a stronger vitality than the LAV under high DEHP stress (Fig. 1A), being consistent with previous results that HAV harbors higher DEHP tolerance [11]. Accordingly, the HAV could accumulate more DEHP from polluted soils (Fig. 1B). As a consequence of evolution to adapt environmental stresses, the plants with high pollutant accumulation capacity (especially for hyperaccumulators) typically have greater resistance to pollution stress [33]. Generally, the tolerance mechanism involves a variety of processes, such as enhancing detoxification and metabolism, compartmentalizing in vacuoles, and improving physiological performances, which are helpful to promote plant growth [34]. Our previous results demonstrated that the HAV had more effective regulation in physiology and detoxification in response to DEHP stress, accounting for its greater DEHP tolerance and resulting accumulation [31, 32]. That is, the stronger DEHP tolerance is an essential precondition for more DEHP accumulation in HAV.

By contrast, the LAV with weaker tolerance minimized DEHP accumulation in plants by reducing DEHP residue in rhizosphere soils. As expected, the residual DEHP in rhizosphere soils was significantly lower in LAV than in HAV (Fig. 1B). Plant rhizosphere, a hotspot of microbes, preferentially recruits the specialized microbial community for organic pollutant removal and plant health in response to pollution stress [35]. Studies have demonstrated that the rhizosphere effects driven by root exudates promote the enrichment of DEHP-degrading bacteria, thereby accelerating rhizosphere DEHP removal [35, 36]. Thus, we can deduce that the lower DEHP residue in rhizosphere soils of LAV might be attributed to more effective enrichment of DEHP-degrading bacteria, consequentially minimizing DEHP accumulation in plant. To gain insight into the rhizomicrobiome assembly that may influence DEHP accumulation in choysum, we compared the variety-specific rhizosphere bacterial communities between the two choysum varieties (LAV and HAV), respectively.

Variety-specific rhizobacterial communities involved in DEHP degradation or plant growth promotion

Rhizomicrobiome is very important for the general health of plants, either for supplying them with nutrients and water or for helping them survive under adverse stressful conditions [20]. Numerous studies have indicated that microbial communities dwelling in the plant rhizosphere are host-specific species, and their community structures and metabolic capabilities are often influenced by the physiological status of the host plants [37]. As expected, our results showed that the two choysum varieties developed distinct rhizosphere microbial community structures and carbon-source utilization patterns in response to DEHP stress (Fig. 2 and Fig. S3). This divergence could be attributed to the differential preference of rhizobacteria for root exudates and their ability to utilize DEHP as a carbon source [38]. Given that DEHP is a complex aromatic compound, we speculate that choysum may selectively recruit specific rhizobacteria to enhance the degradation of this recalcitrant carbon substrate. Besides, rhizobacterial diversity is influenced by the type of organic residue, particularly its decomposability, as recalcitrant compounds tend to select for specific taxa involved in degradation [39]. Therefore, we propose that the higher metabolic activity (especially toward complex carbon sources) and lower species richness/evenness in observed LAV might result from the dominance of functionally specialized rhizobacteria (e.g., DEHP degraders), which outcompeted other taxa and reduced overall diversity. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the Biolog EcoPlate assay primarily captures the potential metabolic capacity of the culturable fraction, rather than serving as definitive evidence of in situ degradation function. In contrast, HAV maintained higher rhizobacterial diversity despite reduced carbon metabolic activity under DEHP stress. This could benefit plant growth by enhancing rhizosphere nutrient availability and stress resilience [40].

Consistently, the differences in recruited rhizobacterial community compositions between the two choysum varieties were also observed at the ASV level (Fig. 3 A and B), suggesting that the choysum roots exerted variety-specific discrimination on the soil microbiome in response to DEHP. A unique bacterial community was enriched in the LAV rhizosphere, comprising several key taxa from Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas, Flavobacterium, Gordonia, and Rhodococcus, which were frequently identified as the dominant genera in PAE-contaminated environments [41, 42]. Sphingomonas was the most important discriminant genus in LAV rhizosphere exposed to DEHP (Fig. 3B), and many bacterial strains belonging to Sphingomonas showed efficient degradation capacities of DEHP [16, 43, 44]. A microbial consortium (CM9) isolated from farmland soil, containing the primary genera such as Sphingomonas, Rhodococcus, and Pseudomonas, could completely degrade 1000 mg/L DEHP within 72 h [43]. By contrast, the HAV rhizosphere specifically recruited some taxa involved in plant growth promotion, such as Bacillus, Streptomyces, Massilia, Enterobacter, and Rhodanobacter [45, 46]. Bacillus and Streptomyces, as a common group of microbes colonizing plant rhizospheres, were the most important discriminant genera in HAV rhizosphere exposed to DEHP (Fig. 3B). Many members of the two genera can improve plant tolerance to abiotic stress by regulating phytohormone level, Ca2+ influx, and callose deposition, and directly stimulate plant growth by IAA production, phosphate dissolution, nitrogen fixation, etc. [47, 48]. Overall, we can conclude that variety-specific rhizosphere taxa recruited by LAV are associated with DEHP degradation, whereas those by HAV are associated with plant-growth promotion. Consequently, the variety-specific rhizomicrobiome appears to be a key factor contributing to the differential accumulation of DEHP in the two choysum varieties.

Variety-specific rhizosphere keystone species mediating plant growth and DEHP accumulation

It is well documented that the construction of SynCom with lab-selected bacteria provides a potential to improve plant performance and stands a better chance to survive and function in a non-sterile environment [49]. Various design approaches of SynComs have been recently developed in numerous studies [50, 51]. Here, we successfully constructed two simplified SynComs (SynLAV and SynHAV) based on a strategy for tailoring the design of functional SynComs, and the rationale was to identify key functional traits, reduce the consortia complexity, and prioritize the most relevant microbial interactions [51]. Functional assessments showed that the SynLAV focused on high-efficient DEHP degradation and the SynHAV focused on obvious plant growth promotion, representing the variety-specific function of rhizobacterial community as concluded above, even though the SynComs contained only a few representatives of rhizobacteria. Similarly, many functionally representative rhizobacteria have already been used to construct the simplified SynComs relevant for rhizomicrobiome-associated host phenotypes [52–55]. Thus, it is the optimal combination of various functional strains rather than a greater number of strains that should be emphasized when constructing a SynCom. Nevertheless, it is important to note that our simplified SynComs could represent only a fraction of the native rhizosphere community and might not fully recapitulate the ecological complexity and dynamic interactions inherent to natural soil systems.

To explore the consequences of SynComs (SynLAV and SynHAV) for plant growth and DEHP accumulation, we performed the rhizosphere cross-inoculation experiments. As expected, the two choysum varieties showed a distinct preference for their indigenous rhizobacteria, and demonstrated the important role of variety-driven rhizobacterial selection in plant growth and DEHP accumulation (Figs. 4 and 5). Considering the variety-specific carbon source utilization patterns of rhizobacterial communities (Fig. 2), the different preference of rhizobacterial colonization might arise due to the variation in carbon compounds exuded by the two choysum varieties. This has been backed up by many studies involved in the plant-mediated “cry for help” to the rhizomicrobiome, showing that plants are capable of releasing specific root exudates to recruit beneficial rhizosphere microbes against adverse conditions [21, 56]. Thus, the observed variety-specific rhizobacterial colonization patterns in response to DEHP are likely mediated by differential root exudation profiles between the two choysum varieties, as evidenced by chemotaxis assay results (Fig. 5A and Fig. S5). However, considering no metabolomic profiling of root exudates performed in this study, the proposed link between specific exudates and bacterial recruitment remains inferential. Future research is required to validate this mechanistic relationship definitively. On the other hand, plants generally tend to recruit some microbes to perform specific functions due to intense competition in the presence of abundant strains. Many studies have found that only a few bacterial taxa enriched in stress-affected roots are associated with stress resistance, which could be a strategy employed by plants to adapt themselves to environmental stress [48–50]. The successful colonization of the recruited DEHP-degrading strains in LAV or PGP strains in HAV rhizosphere endowed the choysum varieties with corresponding functions (Figs. 4 and 5). As a result, there were significant differences in biomass and DEHP accumulation between the two choysum varieties due to different functions performed by the selected rhizosphere strains. The variety-specific rhizobacteria selection appears to confer adaptive advantages for the two choysum varieties in response to DEHP stress with a preference for their beneficial rhizobacteria, revealing a link between variety-driven rhizosphere selection and plant health.

Collectively, the SynCom construction and cross-inoculation experiments provided evidence that the SynComs constructed from the functional microbiome modulate host plant growth and DEHP accumulation, confirming a variety-dependent link between the choysum response to DEHP stress and the composition of recruited rhizobacteria. It is known that a plant’s ability to resist environmental adverse conditions relies either on maximizing its resistance to damage (tolerance strategy), or on minimizing the stresses encountered by the plant (avoidance strategy) [57]. In this study, we borrow the terms “avoidance-like” and “tolerance-like” to describe contrasting patterns of plant-microbiome associations under DEHP stress rather than definitive evolutionary strategies. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the two choysum varieties have developed distinct patterns in response to DEHP stress through a “cry for help”: the LAV with low tolerance recruits DEHP-degrading bacteria, minimizing DEHP accumulation, while the HAV with high tolerance recruits plant growth-promoting bacteria, permitting more DEHP accumulation. Consistent with our hypotheses, the divergent rhizobacterial assemblies lead to specific functional outcomes: LAV recruits DEHP degradation bacteria for reducing DEHP accumulation, while HAV recruits beneficial bacteria for maintaining growth. Additionally, these microbially mediated processes likely function synergistically with plant physiological mechanisms, including root uptake dynamics and internal detoxification, to collectively shape the observed DEHP accumulation patterns. Considering no significant difference in rhizobacterial assembly found between the two varieties in the absence of DEHP exposure (data not shown), it is obvious that DEHP pollution stress induces succession of rhizobacterial communities, especially in the LAV rhizosphere. Thus, the rhizobacteria shaping observed here has real consequences for plant health: plants recruit specific microbes for coping with pollution stress and avoiding phytotoxicity. Overall, our study reveals a clear association between organic pollutant (DEHP) accumulation phenotypes and functionally distinct rhizobacterial assemblies in choysum, which was further verified by SynCom inoculation. The novel findings provide a foundation for constructing an artificial core rhizomicrobiome to breed microbiome-assisted crop varieties, promoting crop growth and/or minimizing pollutant accumulation. Nevertheless, further investigations are required to identify key mediators in the interaction between plants and rhizomicrobiome, and to explore the roles of key mediators in mediating the recruitment and functioning of rhizomicrobiome. Materials and methods

Design of pot experiments

The experimental treatments were set up according to a randomized complete two-factor design, including three DEHP levels (0, 20, and 100 mg/kg) and two choysum varieties (LAV and HAV) with additional unplanted treatment. To facilitate clear separation and accurate collection of rhizosphere soil, two 300-mesh polyester root bags (10 cm height × 2 cm diameter) were placed in each pot (20 cm × 10 cm × 12 cm). These root bags allow the passage of water, cations, and anions but restrict root growth and soil particle movement, thereby cleanly separating rhizosphere soil from bulk (non-rhizosphere) soil [58]. Each root bag was filled with 0.25 kg of soil, and the space outside the root bag was filled with 1.5 kg of soil. After seed germination in vermiculite for 2 weeks, the uniform seedlings of the two choysum varieties with 4–5 leaves were transplanted into the root bags filled with the treated soils. Each treatment was performed with five replicates. All the pots were randomly arranged in a glass-enclosed greenhouse under natural day-night condition, maintaining a temperature ranging from 25 to 32 °C and a relative humidity of 60%. After 45 days of cultivation, plant and soil samples were collected from each pot. The experimental setup and plant growth stages are illustrated in Fig. S1. The soil preparation, choysum varieties, and sampling methods used for the pot experiments are detailed in Supplementary Text S1.

Extraction and determination of DEHP in plant and soil

The ultrasonic-assisted sample extraction, silica gel column cleanup, and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS, Shimadzu QP2010 Plus, Japan) determination of DEHP were conducted according to the method previously described [5]. To ensure the determination quality, solvent blank, spiked blank, spiked matrix, and sample duplicate were routinely analyzed together with the plant or soil samples, and suitable standard controls. Surrogate standard (nitrobenzene-d5) as a recovery indicator was added to each sample to monitor the procedures of extraction and analysis. The detection limit of DEHP in samples was 1.6 μg/kg. The surrogate recoveries in soil and plant samples ranged from 91.2% to 96.7% and 88.5% to 104.2%, respectively.

Biolog Ecoplate™ assay and Illumina sequencing of rhizomicrobiome

The Biolog-Ecoplates (Biolog, Inc., USA) were used to assess community-level carbon utilization profiles of rhizomicrobiome in each rhizosphere soil sample. The experimental procedure is detailed in Supplementary Text S2.

DNA was extracted from the rhizosphere soil samples and the double-ended sequencing was performed with an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). Sequencing results were analyzed with DADA2 R package. An amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) approach was used because ASVs as the standard unit of marker-gene analysis were more precise, reusable, and reproducible than operational taxonomic units [59]. All bacterial beta- and alpha-diversity metrics were calculated with QIIME2. The Illumina sequencing and bioinformatics analyses such as diversity analysis, PCA, Circos mapping, linear discriminant analysis of effect sizes (LEfSe), and ternary enrichment are detailed in Supplementary Text S3.

Isolation and functional characterization of rhizosphere bacteria

Roots with firmly adhering soil were suspended in sterile PBS after removing the clumps of soil loosely adhering to the roots. Then the rhizosphere soil suspensions were serially diluted and plated on R2A, TSA, and LB agar media (Table S1). Plates were incubated at 28 °C and colony appearance was monitored daily until visible colony formation. A single colony was randomly selected and streaked on R2A agar plates. The cultivable bacteria were taxonomically identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing using universal primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATC(A/C)TGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R(5′-TACGG(C/T)TACCTTGTT ACGACTT-3′) [60]. All bacterial isolates were cryopreserved as an aqueous 20% glycerol suspension at − 80 °C.

Representative isolates from rhizosphere soils of the two choysum varieties were selected for functional characterization, including DEHP degradation capacity and plant growth-promoting (PGP) activities. For DEHP degradation, each isolate was inoculated into mineral salt medium (MSM) containing 100 mg/L DEHP as the sole carbon and energy source, and incubated at 28 °C and 180 rpm for 72 h. Residual DEHP was extracted and quantified using GC-MS to calculate degradation efficiency [61]. For PGP trait validation, five key functions were evaluated using standardized media and corresponding reference methods (Table S1): phosphate solubilization (NBRIP medium), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production (King’s B medium), siderophore production (CAS medium), nitrogen fixation (Ashby medium), and potassium solubilization (potassium-solubilizing medium). All functional assays were performed in triplicate and analyzed following standard protocols with supporting references [62–65].

Construction of synthetic communities and their functional evaluation

The simplified SynComs of LAV (named SynLAV) and HAV (named SynHAV) were designed by selecting representative rhizobacterial isolates, respectively. Before constructing the SynComs, a pairwise antagonism assay was employed to examine interactions among candidate isolates according to previously reported method with slight modification [66]. Briefly, the OD600 of each candidate isolate grown in LB liquid medium was adjusted to 1.0. Then on LB agar plates, 0.5 μL suspension of each isolate was inoculated next to one other isolate at distances of 0.6 cm or 1.2 cm. Pairwise interactions were visually observed after 2–4 days of incubation by colony growth, and the candidate isolates without inhibiting effects on one another were selected. As shown in Table S2, the selected five members of SynLAV harbored DEHP degradation rates ranging from 22 to 54% and negligible PGP activity, while the selected SynHAV members presented DEHP degradation rates ranging from < 1% to 14% and potent multi-PGP activity. Subsequently, the suspension of each selected isolate was adjusted with PBS to an OD600 of 0.5, and then the SynLAV or SynHAV were obtained by mixing the isolates in equal ratios. The DEHP degradation performances of the two SynComs were evaluated with the abovementioned degradation test, and their PGP potentials were evaluated by analyzing growth of inoculated choysum [67].

Cross-inoculation experiment and validation of chemotactic preference

Surface-sterilized seeds of the two choysum varieties LAV and HAV were germinated in vermiculite for 2 weeks and then transferred into pots with soils contaminated by 100 mg/kg DEHP. Each SynCom suspension was diluted to a final OD600 of 0.2 (approximately 105 CFU/mL) with PBS for inoculation. As shown in Fig. S2, the seedlings of LAV or HAV were inoculated with 5 mL of each SynCom inoculum, respectively. PBS served as the uninoculated negative control, while each treatment was in triplicate. The pot experiment was conducted in a glass-enclosed greenhouse as described above. After 10 days of cultivation, plant and rhizosphere soil samples were collected from each pot. The fresh plant weight was recorded, and DEHP concentrations in plants and rhizosphere soils were determined as described above. Besides, the 16S rRNA sequencing of rhizobacterial communities was conducted to examine the colonization of inoculated SynComs. Differential abundance analysis of rhizobacterial communities between inoculation treatments was performed using the DESeq2 package in R (v3.6.1) to identify significantly enriched taxa, with a log₂ fold change (log₂FC) threshold of ± 1 and an adjusted P-value cutoff of 0.05. The results were visualized using volcano plots.

To assess the chemotactic preference of SynLAV or SynHAV toward the root exudates of their respective host varieties, a standard capillary chemotaxis assay was conducted according to our previously published protocol with minor modifications [68]. Prior to this, the root exudates of the two choysum varieties were collected via hydroponic cultivation according to our previously established protocol [69]. Briefly, a 100-µL aliquot of bacterial suspension (OD600 of 0.5 in 0.05 M MOPS buffer, pH 7.4) was loaded into a 200-µL pipette tip. A needle capillary connected to a 1 mL syringe was used to draw 100 µL of filter-sterilized root exudates (from LAV or HAV). The capillary assembly was then inserted into the pipette tip to a depth of 3 mm to ensure full contact with the bacterial suspension. After incubation at room temperature for 45 min, the liquid within the capillary was collected, serially diluted to 10⁻2, and spread onto LB agar plates. Colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted after incubation at 28 °C for 24 h to evaluate chemotactic responses. A control treatment containing only MOPS buffer (without root exudates) was included in parallel. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reliability.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted in R. Normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) were assessed prior to parametric testing. Group comparisons employed one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post hoc tests; non-parametric alternatives (Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s test) were used when assumptions were violated. Exact P values are reported (except P < 0.001). To control for multiple testing, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied where appropriate; adjusted P values (q values) < 0.05 were considered significant. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d. Microbial community structure was analyzed via PERMANOVA (Bray–Curtis dissimilarity), reporting R2. Linear relationships were evaluated using OLS regression, with model assumptions checked via residual normality, Breusch–Pagan, and VIF. LEfSe analysis combined Kruskal–Wallis tests with LDA, interpreted alongside FDR-adjusted significance.
