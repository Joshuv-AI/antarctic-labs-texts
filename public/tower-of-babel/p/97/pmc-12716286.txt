Unmet needs of women with epilepsy living in rural low‐ and middle‐income countries: The experience of the Bolivian Chaco
Authors: Alessandra Nicoletti, Gianluca Latino, Calogero Edoardo Cicero, Anna Barbiero, Chiara Aguzzoli, Bianca Ribelli, Valentina Petrini, Maria Micieli, Sandra Padilla, Estela Vilte, Francesco Cosmi, Elizabeth Blanca Crespo Gomez, Alessandro Bartoloni, Loretta Giuliano

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEpilepsy affects approximately 70 million people worldwide, with 80% living in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), where many people with epilepsy (PWE) go undiagnosed or untreated. Over the last few decades, many efforts have been made worldwide to reduce the treatment gap (TG) in LMICs. However, while Western countries emphasize personalized antiseizure medications (ASM) management in Women with Epilepsy (WwE), little data are available on this topic in LMICs. Our study aimed to assess healthcare standards regarding epilepsy in women from a rural area of Bolivia.MethodsWe interviewed a sample of WwE living in rural communities of the Bolivian Chaco, previously identified by community health workers (CHWs) and/or general practitioners (GPs) and who had been prescribed at least one ASM. A previously validated questionnaire was used addressing epilepsy management and maternal health.ResultsWe interviewed 54 women aged 7–64 years. Among them, 18 (33.3%) had experienced at least one pregnancy. Of these seven (38.9%) continued ASM treatment during pregnancy, but only two received specific counseling. One woman spontaneously discontinued treatment during the pregnancy. There were 15 pregnancies while on treatment, with ASM dosage adjusted in only two cases. No major congenital malformations were reported in the offspring.SignificanceThis pilot study explored the reproductive health challenges faced by WwE in rural Bolivia. The lack of data in LMICs highlights the need for awareness campaigns to ensure an adequate management of WwE in these areas.Plain Language SummaryWomen with epilepsy face additional challenges related to their reproductive and social life. INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy affects approximately 70 million people worldwide, with 80% residing in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), where a significant proportion of people with epilepsy (PWE) remain undiagnosed or untreated. 1 , 2 In 2022, the Intersectoral Global Action Plan (IGAP) for Epilepsy and other Neurological Disorders was approved 3 marking the latest development in efforts that began with the Global Campaign Against Epilepsy in 2002. 4 This initiative aimed to reduce the treatment gap (TG), alleviate the economic burden, and address the stigma associated with epilepsy through various regional programs. 5 These initiatives have relied on the involvement of primary health workers in regions lacking specialists alongside community‐based approaches. Over the past decades, numerous activities have been conducted worldwide in LMIC to pursue these goals 6 with different pilot projects implemented in Africa and Asia—such as in Ghana, Mozambique, and Myanmar—demonstrating a significant reduction in the TG through community‐based care models. 7

In the Chaco region, Plurinational State of Bolivia, the estimated prevalence of lifetime epilepsy is 12.3/1000 (11.1/1000 for active epilepsy) and 7.2/1000 for Epilepsy Associated with Tonic Clonic Seizures (EATCS). 8 , 9 , 10 Over the past decades, our team has undertaken several activities, many under the aegis of the global campaign, to improve epilepsy knowledge and facilitate the accurate diagnosis and treatment of PWE in this region. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 These efforts have contributed to reducing the epilepsy TG from approximately 90% to 45% by 2016. 8 , 13 This reduction has led to an increase in the number of Women with Epilepsy (WwE) receiving diagnosis and treatment. In Western countries, there is significant emphasis on personalized antiseizure medications (ASM) management for WwE. However, there is a lack of data on this issue in LMICs. The management of WwE poses some specific challenges due to concerns related to reproductive and social life. Reproductive health factors such as sexual function, fertility, pregnancy, labor, lactation, birth control, and menopause require specialized health care, which is likely insufficient in rural LMIC settings.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the standard of healthcare provided to WwE in a rural area of Bolivia.

METHODS

Study area and population

This pilot study was conducted in a rural area of the Bolivian Chaco. The Plurinational State of Bolivia is a low‐income country where nearly 4 million people live below the “poverty line,” particularly in rural areas. 20 The southeast region of Bolivia is part of the “Gran Chaco,” a subtropical area which also includes Argentina and Paraguay. The ethnic group living in the study area is predominantly composed of native Guaraní people who live in poor dwellings located in rural communities, often reachable only by rural roads, without access to basic infrastructure like running water or electricity. Their local economy is based on agriculture and animal husbandry.

The study was conducted between October and November 2023 in three municipalities (Gutierrez, Eiti, and Camiri) of the Cordillera province, Department of Santa Cruz, Bolivia. We interviewed a sample of WwE previously identified by the community health workers (CHWs) and/or general practitioners (GPs) working in the study areas, who were present in the communities at the time of the survey and for whom at least one ASM has been previously prescribed. A standardized questionnaire with questions related to epilepsy management and maternal health was used (Data S1‐ S2).

Statistical analysis

Data were collected in an ad‐hoc created dataset. Data cleaning was performed before the data analysis considering both range and consistency checks. Qualitative variables were described as percentages and quantitative variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The study was developed according to STROBE guidelines for observational studies (Data S3).

Ethics

The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Bolivian Neurological Society (IRB).

Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and for minors, consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians.

RESULTS

Of 91 WwE residing in 24 different rural villages in the Cordillera Province, we interviewed 54 WwE who were present in the communities between October and November 2023. At the time of the interview, their mean age was 26.0 ± 13.6 years, ranging from 7 to 64 years. Specifically, eight women (14.8%) were under 13 years old, 11 (20.4%) were between 13 and 18 years, 31 (57.4%) were between 19 and 50 years and only four women (7.4%) were over 50 years old. Among the 54 women, only one (1.8%) was married and four (7.4%) were cohabitating with a partner, while the majority, 46 (85.2%) were single. In terms of education, 11 women (20.4%) had completed primary school, and the majority (29, 53.7%) had completed secondary school. Nine women (16.7%) were illiterate and only five (9.2%) had attended high school. Sixteen women (30.2%) were still students, while the majority (22, 41.5%) were housewives. Eleven women (20.7%) were inactive. Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Epilepsy features

Among the interviewed WwE, the majority (32, 82.05%) experienced tonic–clonic seizures, either as generalized‐onset tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS; 29, 74.4%) or focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (3, 7.7%). Four women (10.3%) had only focal seizures and two (5.1%) reported absence seizures. Forty‐six women (85.2%) experienced at least one seizure in the past 5 years classifying them as having “active epilepsy.” Among the eight women classified as “having inactive epilepsy” five were not taking ASMs.

The average age at epilepsy onset was 10.4 ± 9.5 years with a range from 0 to 50 years. At the time of the interview, 39 women (72.2%) were on ASMs, though 19 of them (48.7%) did not adhere to treatment regularly. Distance from the hospital and the cost of ASMs were the most common reasons for non‐adherence. Of the 15 WwE not currently taking ASMs, five had “inactive epilepsy”, one woman stopped ASMs due to the absence of seizures in the last year, one had a recent epilepsy diagnosis and had not been prescribed ASM treatment, one discontinued the treatment following a medical consultation, and the remaining seven chose to stop treatment independently.

The most commonly prescribed ASM was carbamazepine (CBZ) taken by 22 women with an average daily dose of 381.8 ± 186.8 mg/day (range: 200–800 mg), followed by phenobarbital (PB) prescribed to 20 women with an average dose of 136.1 ± 44.7 (range:100–200 mg). Additionally, five women were on phenytoin (PHT), seven on valproate (VPA), one on lamotrigine (LTG), and one on levetiracetam (LEV). According to 62.7% of the women, their treatment was effective in controlling seizures. Epilepsy characteristics are summarized in Table 2.

Reproductive health data

Among the 54 interviewed WwE, 18 (33.3%) had experienced at least one pregnancy totaling 62 pregnancies. However, only seven of these women (38.9%) had been diagnosed with epilepsy and were on ASMs prior to their pregnancy. Of the eight WwE with “not active epilepsy” three had at least one pregnancy. Of these two had one pregnancy not exposed to ASMs; one woman, who was classified as “not active epilepsy” at the time of the interview, had six pregnancies on phenytoin 300 mg/day, while her epilepsy was still active.

All WwE received specific counseling from a neurologist at the time of their first ASM prescription including information on actions to take in the event of pregnancy. Despite this, only two (28.6%) of the seven women received additional counseling at the time of the pregnancy (Figure 1, Table 3).

Of the total number of 62 pregnancies, six (9.7%) occurred before the onset of epilepsy. Among the 56 pregnancies that occurred after epilepsy onset, only 15 (26.8%) happened while the women were on ASMs treatment (seven women). In three cases the ASM dose was reduced during pregnancy and one woman independently discontinued her ASMs during pregnancy. However, for 11 pregnancies the neurologist was not consulted and the ASMs regimen was not modified. Notably one woman had six pregnancies while taking 300 mg of PHT daily, another had three pregnancies while on 300 mg of CBZ daily and a third had two pregnancies while on 150 mg of PB daily.

In most cases (11; 73.3%), deliveries took place in the hospital. Breastfeeding was reported by only four women (accounting for six pregnancies). All women reported normal development for their children (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In this pilot study, we explored the reproductive health challenges in a sample of WwE, living in the rural Bolivian Chaco. Among the 54 interviewed WwE, only 18 had experienced at least one pregnancy, and only seven of them while taking ASMs. Although all WwE received specific information from the neurologist at the time of the first ASM prescription about the actions to be undertaken in case of pregnancy, only two women received additional counseling during pregnancy, which led to a reduction of their ASM dosage. Despite this, almost all deliveries took place in hospitals and none of the women reported miscarriages, major congenital malformations and/or neurodevelopmental disorders in their children. It is noteworthy that all the women in the study were taking first‐generation ASMs (CBZ, PB, and PHT), which are often the only available treatments in this setting and are considered as first line drugs. However, these drugs are well known to be linked to negative reproductive outcomes. In fact carbamazepine and phenytoin are among the most commonly used ASMs in LMIC and monotherapy is the most common treatment regimen, as confirmed by a recent review 21 A possible explanation for the absence of negative reproductive outcomes in this population could be the use of a monotherapy regimen and the low dosages of these drugs. It is well established that the risk and the type of major congenital malformation (MCM) depend on the type of ASMs used and, in some cases, the dosage used, with an increase in malformation rates with increasing dose at the time of conception for almost all drugs. 22 , 23 Nonetheless, we cannot rule out that early miscarriage or mild developmental disorders during infancy may have gone unrecognized or undiagnosed in this rural setting as interviews were conducted directly with WwE, without the support of clinical charts or physician consultation. Despite the lack of negative reproductive outcomes, urgent action is needed at government level to introduce safer drugs such as levetiracetam as first‐line ASMs in LMIC. 3 , 24 , 25

In line with a previous study, 16 we observed a frequent non‐adherence to ASMs treatment among WwE. Nearly 50% of the women on ASMs reported irregular use. The distance to hospital and the cost of ASMs were cited as the most common reasons for incomplete adherence. A recent study on ASM affordability in LMIC found that affordability of ASMs is poor and may be acting as a barrier to accessing treatment for epilepsy. Moreover, additional healthcare expenses, such as physician consultation fees and transportation must also be considered. 26

Furthermore, many women discontinued treatment without consulting a physician and two of them reported stopping ASMs due to pregnancy. Given that only 15 of 62 pregnancies occurred during ASM treatment, it is plausible that the number of women who discontinued treatment due to unplanned pregnancies was higher. The literature shows a high frequency of unplanned pregnancies among WwE in LMIC, probably due to the lack of preconception counseling, with approximately 61.7% of pregnancies being unplanned in urban areas of Bolivia, as found in a previous study. 27 Unplanned pregnancies may lead to the sudden withdrawal of ASMs when women become aware of their pregnancy, increasing the risk of seizure‐related complications, such as status epilepticus and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) 28 or adverse fetal outcomes. 29 , 30

Another noteworthy finding is the potential stigmatization of WwE in this area. In our sample among the 54 WwE interviewed, only 18 (33.3%) had experienced at least one pregnancy and the majority were single. Even excluding WwE under the age of 18, the percentage of women who had at least one pregnancy, as well as those who were married, was significantly lower compared to national data. In 2016 the Istituto National de Estadística 31 reported that only 7% of women aged 25–49 years in rural Bolivia were without children, while in our sample this figure was of about 70%. Although further analytic studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis, stigmatization of WwE may explain these findings. This observation is also supported by a previous study conducted by our group in the rural Chaco which assessed stigma before and after a community‐based epilepsy awareness campaign. A significant percentage of participants believed that PWE could not attend school (25.5%) or university (22.7%), get married (18.2%), or have children (17.1%). 17 Future anthropological qualitative studies are needed to explore cultural beliefs and stigmatization of WwE.

To the best of our knowledge, there are limited data on the management of epilepsy in women living in rural LMIC. Two literature reviews reported higher perinatal death 32 and greater stigmatization among WwE in LMIC. 33 In addition, two prospective studies conducted in Senegal and in Nigeria, 34 , 35 among 91 pregnant WwE treated with first‐line ASMs, found that 16.2% had miscarriages, 41.9% had premature births, and 4.1% experienced MCMs, while no data were found concerning preconception counseling or post‐natal outcomes. 33 Similarly, a study from Brazil found that cesarean deliveries and miscarriages were more frequent in WwE. 36 A case–control study was conducted in West China, with over 50% of participants from rural areas found that 23.69% of WwE had adverse reproductive outcomes with factors such as seizure frequency, status epilepticus, ASM type, use of valproate, and lack of folic acid supplementation being associated with a higher risk of negative outcomes. 37 To our knowledge, only one study has assessed the health challenges of WwE in Latin American countries, specifically in Argentina in 2011 38 while a review performed in 2019 highlighted the scarcity of studies on this topic in sub–Saharan Africa. 33

The paucity of reproductive health data in WwE in LMIC highlights the need for awareness campaigns to ensure proper management of WwE in these settings. Thanks to the effort of the Global Campaign Against Epilepsy and the IGAP, several successful initiatives have been implemented in different regions to reduce the epilepsy TG and, consequently, many WwE are now receiving ASMs. However, our study raises significant concerns about the management of WwE in rural LMIC. Despite the coverage of issues related to WwE in epilepsy educational programs, and awareness campaigns aimed at reducing the TG in the rural Chaco, 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 and despite the information provided by the neurologist during initial ASM prescription, most WwE did not received preconception counseling to manage ASMs during pregnancy.

To improve knowledge and awareness at the community level in this rural setting, continuous educational activities must be performed, with a focus on preconception planning including contraception, pregnancy, and breastfeeding. This is especially important in areas where GPs often work only briefly before moving to urban areas, leading to a high turnover rate. These findings underscore the urgency of advocating for governments to promote continuous and large‐scale activities aimed at improving epilepsy care at a national level.

There are some limitations to this study. First, we interviewed only WwE who were present in their communities at the time of the survey, which may have introduced a selection bias. Nevertheless, we were able to enroll about 60% of the whole population of WwE, which can be considered a quite representative sample. Additionally, as a pilot study, the sample size is small and does not allow for generalization. Moreover, as previously underlined, the data obtained by the interviews were not completed by the review of medical records. This, together with the presence of missing answers to a significant number of questions, could have affected the reproducibility of our data. Moreover, we cannot rule out the possibility of conscious concealment for some of the given answers, which could explain the low rate of negative outcomes reported.

One of the goals of the IGAP on epilepsy and other neurological disorders for 2022–2031 is to reduce the stigma, impact, and burden of neurological disorders including their associated mortality, morbidity, and disability. In LMIC, the primary step toward achieving these goals is reducing the TG. However, further efforts are needed to ensure a high standard of care for WwE in rural LMIC areas.

Despite the limitations, our study highlighted important unmet needs of women with epilepsy living in rural areas of LMICs: the presence of stigma, lack of safer ASMs, limited counseling, and frequent nonadherence represent important critical issues that need to be urgently addressed.
