Successful pregnancy in a patient with active Crohn's disease, chronic intestinal failure, and long‐term parenteral nutrition receiving upadacitinib: A case report
Authors: Diana Coman, Katarzyna Orlicka, Dane Christina Daoud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractData on pregnancy in patients with Crohn's disease receiving Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, with concomitant short bowel syndrome and chronic intestinal failure requiring long‐term parenteral support, are extremely limited. We report the case of a patient with refractory Crohn's disease, short bowel syndrome–associated chronic intestinal failure, and long‐term home parenteral nutrition who achieved a successful full‐term pregnancy while receiving upadacitinib. The 34‐year‐old woman had a history of severe, treatment‐resistant ileocolonic Crohn's disease and multiple intestinal resections, leaving 145 cm of small bowel and resulting in high‐output stoma and chronic intestinal failure. After inadequate response to multiple biologics, upadacitinib was initiated with clinical and endoscopic improvement. The patient became pregnant unintentionally while receiving upadacitinib 45 mg daily. Through intensive multidisciplinary management, she achieved clinical remission, stable parenteral support needs, and adequate weight gain and delivered a healthy infant at term. This case illustrates that with close multidisciplinary follow‐up, successful pregnancy is possible in patients with chronic intestinal failure and active Crohn's disease receiving parenteral support, even with concurrent exposure to upadacitinib. Further data are needed to assess the safety of JAK inhibitors in pregnancy and to guide care in this complex patient population.Keywords: chronic intestinal failure, Crohn's disease, home parenteral nutrition, Janus kinase inhibitor, short bowel syndrome, upadacitinib in pregnancy

INTRODUCTION

Chronic intestinal failure is a condition in which the intestine is unable to absorb sufficient nutrients and fluids to sustain health and requires parenteral support. The most common cause of chronic intestinal failure is short bowel syndrome, which often results from extensive intestinal resection. 1 Upadacitinib is a selective Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) inhibitor approved for the treatment of moderately to severely active Crohn's disease (Table 1). There are currently very limited data on the effects of upadacitinib during pregnancy and almost no data on pregnancy outcomes in patients with chronic intestinal failure.

CASE PRESENTATION

We report the case of a patient with refractory Crohn's disease and chronic intestinal failure due to short bowel syndrome requiring long‐term parenteral support who successfully completed a full‐term pregnancy while being treated with upadacitinib.

The patient is a 34‐year‐old woman with severe stricturing and fistulizing ileocolonic Crohn's disease diagnosed at age 10 years, who had failed numerous treatment options, including azathioprine, 6‐mercaptopurine, methotrexate (2001–2006), and infliximab (2006–2007), as well as multiple courses of corticosteroid therapy. In 2007, she underwent ileocecal resection because of disease progression.

She subsequently received adalimumab, certolizumab, and ustekinumab, but inflammation remained uncontrolled. In 2015, she underwent proctocolectomy and ileal resection with terminal ileostomy and 185 cm of remaining small bowel.

A flare in June 2018 prompted initiation of vedolizumab, but despite treatment, she required a 40‐cm ileal resection and new stoma for peristomal fistula and active disease in June 2020, resulting in end jejunostomy and 145 cm of remaining bowel.

Chronic intestinal failure

Unfortunately, following the last resection, the patient's short bowel syndrome has led to chronic intestinal failure with a high‐output stoma (3 L/day). She experienced severe malnutrition, as defined by the GLIM criteria, and from cachexia with the lowest recorded weight of 35 kg (BMI 13) (calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared). She developed complications, including dehydration, electrolytes imbalance such as severe hypomagnesemia, iron deficiency anemia, and osteopenia. The psychological impact of her situation was so profound, that at one point, she requested medical assistance in dying. Long‐term parenteral support was therefore initiated in August 2022, with self‐administered home parenteral nutrition delivered 3 times per week through a single‐lumen tunneled central venous catheter (Hickman type). Each infusion bag provided 1326 kcal including 60 g of amino acids, 190 g of dextrose, and 44 g of lipids (SMOFlipid 20%) for a total volume of 1750 ml. This regimen supplied an average of 16 kcal/kg/day and 21 ml/kg/day of fluid. High stoma output was managed by the intestinal failure unit with antimotility medication and dietary modifications. Her average daily intake was of 1800 kcal and 85–95 g of protein. She was supported by our dietitian and followed a short bowel syndrome diet, including oral fluid restriction, use of a homemade oral rehydration solution, reduced intake of simple sugars, and eating small, frequent meals. These interventions, combined with multidisciplinary care, reduced stoma output to 2 L/day, allowed parenteral nutrition discontinuation and transition to hydration, and resulted in 20‐kg weight gain, bringing her weight to 55 kg (BMI 20). The patient continued to receive infusions 3 days per week, now consisting of an individualized hydroelectrolytic solution tailored to fluid and electrolyte imbalances. It included essential electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium, phosphorus, cloruride, acetate), intravenous multivitamins, and trace elements (copper, selenium, and zinc). In 2023, a preteduglutide assessment was planned but ultimately not pursued because of active Crohn's disease.

Active Crohn's disease and pregnancy

Active Crohn's disease was suspected when the patient developed abdominal pain. Investigations later confirmed persistent active ulcerative jejunitis despite treatment with vedolizumab and subsequently risankizumab. A joint decision was made to initiate upadacitinib 45 mg daily in November 2023. The dose was decreased to 30 mg daily after 3 months. March 2024 ileoscopy demonstrated endoscopic improvement with some residual ulcers (simple endoscopic score—Crohn's disease score: 5 out of 56). This indicated mild endoscopic disease. Upadacitinib was escalated back to 45 mg daily in April 2024 because of recurrence of symptoms, likely caused by malabsorption related to short bowel syndrome, leading to clinical remission.

The patient became pregnant unintentionally while receiving upadacitinib 45 mg daily in May 2024. During this pregnancy, a multidisciplinary approach was implemented involving her obstetrician, the intestinal rehabilitation team, the inflammatory bowel disease team, the internal medicine specialist in obstetrical medicine for high‐risk pregnancies, and the anesthesia team. Follow‐up was provided every 2 weeks by the parenteral nutrition team, then weekly starting at the 20th week. Given that the patient had been successfully weaned from parenteral nutrition but was still receiving hydroelectrolytic support cyclically 3 days per week, regular and frequent obstetric ultrasounds were conducted to determine whether adjustments to parenteral energy provision were necessary in the event of fetal growth restriction or maternal weight loss. Despite her complicated medical history, active inflammation, and chronic intestinal failure, her parenteral support requirements remained stable throughout the pregnancy. She remained on hydration support 3 days per week and did not require resumption of parenteral nutrition or any adjustments to trace elements or electrolytes. Additionally, the patient received routine maternal folate supplementation and her weight went from 53 kg (BMI 19.7) to 64.7 kg (BMI 24). She remained asymptomatic overall, experiencing only one episode of intestinal obstruction around 30 weeks, which required 24 h of hospitalization. Glycemic control remained stable throughout the pregnancy, and routine screening for gestational diabetes was unremarkable. She successfully carried the pregnancy to full‐term and delivered a healthy baby via scheduled cesarean delivery at 38 weeks and 3 days of gestation, with a 3.45 kg birthweight and an Apgar score of 9‐9‐9, in January 2025.

DISCUSSION

This case highlights a rare instance of a patient with severe Crohn's disease and chronic intestinal failure secondary to short bowel syndrome, receiving long‐term parenteral support, who achieved a full‐term pregnancy while being treated with upadacitinib.

Animal studies suggest that high doses of JAK inhibitors may negatively impact fetal bone growth and weight during intrauterine development. The manufacturer recommends against the use of upadacitinib during pregnancy because of insufficient human data and potential risks. 2 However, some recent studies suggest reassuring pregnancy outcomes. 3

In a 2024 study by Mahadevan and al, pregnancy outcomes for patients treated with upadacitinib were assessed, identifying 80 pregnancies from clinical trials and 48 postmarketing cases from AbbVie's safety database. No apparent teratogenic effects were noted with first‐trimester exposure. However, no definitive conclusions could me made about pregnancy outcomes. 3 Case reports describe a healthy birth at 39 weeks after upadacitinib treatment for atopic dermatitis and a 35‐week vaginal delivery after preterm membrane rupture in a patient treated for ulcerative colitis with normal fetal growth and no congenital abnormalities. 4 , 5

Uncontrolled inflammatory bowel disease complicates the course of pregnancy with risks for both fetal and maternal outcomes. For instance, a study by Hoffmann et al analyzing 50 live births found that adverse pregnancy outcomes occurred in 28% of cases, including small for gestational age in 6%, low birthweight in 18%, and preterm birth in 20%. 6 Other studies have consistently shown the association between disease activity and adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as preterm birth, low birthweight, small for gestational age, stillbirth, and spontaneous abortion. 7 , 8 In patients with refractory Crohn's disease for whom disease control is only achievable with upadacitinib, the risks of uncontrolled inflammatory bowel disease need to be balanced against the theoretical risks associated with upadacitinib exposure.

Moreover, chronic intestinal failure presents a critical challenge during pregnancy because of the persistent malabsorption and constant instability of energy, hydration, and electrolyte balance. It poses risks to both maternal and fetal outcomes. This is a highly underresearched area, with scarce data available on the subject, as chronic intestinal failure is a rare and orphan condition. Most of the reported cases are presented as case reports. 9 Nonetheless, a long‐term retrospective study in France reviewed 21 pregnancies from 1984 to 2014 of mothers with chronic intestinal failure requiring home parenteral nutrition and found maternal complications in 67% of cases; half of the complications were related to the underlying disease and about a third were related to complications of the home parenteral nutrition. The authors concluded that pregnancy is possible in patients receiving home parenteral nutrition but requires close multidisciplinary follow‐up because of the high risk of complications. 10

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates the challenge of managing pregnancy in patients with severe Crohn's disease and chronic intestinal failure secondary to short bowel syndrome requiring long‐term parenteral support. Our experience and the current literature, still scarce, guide us toward a multidisciplinary and closely monitored follow‐up of patients with chronic intestinal failure receiving parenteral support. This includes monitoring nutrition needs to ensure adequate fetal growth, as well as managing the infectious and metabolic risks associated with chronic intestinal failure and long‐term parenteral support. Upadacitinib exposure theoretically carries risks, and active disease presents considerable risks, but our patient successfully carried a full‐term pregnancy and delivered a healthy infant. This case adds to the growing body of literature suggesting that JAK inhibitors may not be inherently teratogenic, though further studies are needed to establish their safety in pregnancy.
