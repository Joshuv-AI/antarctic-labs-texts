Aortic Root Thrombosis With Multivessel Coronary Embolism Following Left Ventricular Assist Device Implantation
Authors: Radhay Parikh, Chloe Kharsa, Rody G. Bou Chaaya, Gal Sella, Mangesh Kritya, Karl Abou Zeid, Mahwash Kassi, Neal Kleiman, Alpesh R. Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAortic root thrombosis is uncommon but potentially catastrophic in patients supported with continuous-flow left ventricular assist devices (LVADs).Case SummaryWe describe a 28-year-old woman presenting with syncope 1 month after HeartMate 3 LVAD implantation. Electrocardiography demonstrated a myocardial infarction. Echocardiography revealed severe biventricular dysfunction, persistent aortic valve closure, and normal LVAD inflow velocities. The international normalized ratio was 1.4. Coronary angiography identified a mobile aortic root thrombus with multivessel coronary embolization. Given prohibitive embolic risk, intensive medical therapy was pursued, leading to clinical stabilization.DiscussionEarly recognition and multidisciplinary management are critical when invasive strategies carry high risk.Take-Home MessagesAortic root thrombosis is a rare but life-threatening complication in patients supported with continuous-flow LVADs. Acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction in patients with LVAD should prompt urgent evaluation for coronary embolism rather than atherosclerotic disease, especially in young patients without traditional risk factors. Management of aortic root thrombus with multivessel coronary embolization requires individualized, multidisciplinary decision-making.

History of Presentation and Past Medical History

A 28-year-old woman presented to the emergency department after a witnessed syncopal episode occurring approximately 1 month after implantation of a HeartMate 3 (HM3) continuous-flow left ventricular assist device (LVAD) (Abbott). The syncopal event was sudden in onset, associated with brief loss of consciousness, and followed by spontaneous recovery without focal neurologic deficits.

The LVAD implantation had been performed as destination therapy in the setting of advanced heart failure after percutaneous closure of a patent foramen ovale. Her postoperative course was complicated by post-cardiotomy shock requiring temporary mechanical circulatory support, acute right ventricular failure necessitating inotropic and vasopressor support, a transient seizure episode without neurologic sequelae, and marked transaminase elevation consistent with congestive hepatopathy. She eventually stabilized and was discharged on guideline-directed medical therapy and warfarin anticoagulation.

Her past medical history was notable for stage D nonischemic cardiomyopathy with severely reduced left ventricular systolic function and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. She had no known history of coronary artery disease or hypercoagulable disorders.

Investigations

On presentation, given the syncopal episode and recent LVAD implantation, an emergent noncontrast computed tomography scan of the head was performed and demonstrated no evidence of acute intracranial hemorrhage or ischemic stroke. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed a subtherapeutic international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.4.

Electrocardiography demonstrated new ST-segment elevations in the inferior leads (II, III, and aVF) with reciprocal ST-segment depressions in the lateral leads, consistent with an acute inferior wall ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (Figure 1). Laboratory testing demonstrated marked myocardial injury, with significantly elevated cardiac troponin levels and creatine phosphokinase. Liver function tests revealed worsening transaminase elevation compared with baseline, attributed to acute hepatic congestion in the setting of right ventricular dysfunction secondary to inferior myocardial infarction.

Management

Urgent transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severely depressed left ventricular systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction <20%). The right ventricle was markedly dilated and severely dysfunctional. The aortic valve remained persistently closed throughout the cardiac cycle, with no evidence of intermittent opening. LVAD inflow cannula velocities were within normal limits, making inflow cannula thrombosis unlikely. No pericardial effusion or intracardiac shunt was identified (Video 1).

Given the electrocardiographic findings consistent with acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, the patient was taken emergently to the cardiac catheterization laboratory for further evaluation.

Procedure

Coronary angiography revealed a large, mobile thrombus within the aortic root, accompanied by extensive multivessel coronary thromboembolism. There was complete occlusion of the mid right coronary artery, occlusion of the mid left anterior descending artery with angiographic evidence of distal thrombus migration, and a smaller thrombus in a distal obtuse marginal branch. Contrast injections demonstrated absence of antegrade flow in the affected vessels, consistent with embolic rather than atherosclerotic occlusion (Video 2).

During angiography, the aortic valve failed to open during multiple cardiac cycles, confirming severe aortic root stasis. These findings were consistent with a diagnosis of aortic root thrombosis (ART) with subsequent coronary embolization.

Right heart catheterization demonstrated preserved hemodynamics, with normal biventricular filling pressures (mean right atrial pressure: 6 mm Hg; mean pulmonary capillary wedge pressure: 3 mm Hg) and a preserved cardiac index of 2.6 L/min/m2.

After these findings, a multidisciplinary heart team—including advanced heart failure specialists, interventional cardiologists, and cardiothoracic surgeons—was convened. Given the preserved LVAD-mediated systemic perfusion, the high risk of further embolization with mechanical manipulation, and the extensive thrombotic burden, percutaneous coronary intervention or surgical thrombectomy was deferred. A conservative, aggressive medical management strategy was selected as the safest therapeutic approach.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit and treated with intensive antithrombotic therapy, including intravenous unfractionated heparin, a glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor, and oral warfarin. Parenteral agents were discontinued once a therapeutic INR target of 2.5 to 3.5 was achieved. She stabilized clinically without recurrent ischemic symptoms, arrhythmias, or neurologic events.

She was discharged home after 10 days on a stable warfarin regimen. She remains clinically stable 2 years later without recurrent thromboembolic events, neurologic complications, or heart failure hospitalizations.

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare but severe complication of continuous-flow LVAD support: ART with extensive multivessel coronary embolization in a HM3 recipient. Although the device incorporates design features intended to reduce thrombogenicity—including a fully magnetically levitated rotor and artificial pulse technology1—ART remains a clinically relevant complication.2 Recent observational data suggest ART may occur in up to 10% of HM3 recipients, most commonly within the first several months after implantation, and is associated with increased risks of stroke, myocardial infarction, mortality, and need for aortic valve intervention.3

Anticoagulation status played a pivotal role in this patient's presentation. A subtherapeutic INR of 1.4 was identified at admission and likely served as the immediate precipitant for this thrombotic catastrophe. Inadequate anticoagulation remains one of the most consistent risk factors for thrombotic complications in LVAD recipients.4 The 2024 International Society for Heart and Lund Transplantation consensus statement continues to endorse vitamin K antagonists as the cornerstone of antithrombotic therapy, with a recommended target INR of 2.0 to 3.0 for most HM3 patients.5,6

Although direct oral anticoagulants have generated interest due to their favorable bleeding profiles in other cardiovascular populations, robust evidence supporting their safety and efficacy in HM3 recipients remains lacking. Similarly, findings from the ARIES-HM3 (aspirin and hemocompatibility events with a left ventricular assist device in advanced heart failure) trial suggest that aspirin may be safely omitted in selected HM3 patients to reduce bleeding risk without increasing thromboembolic events;7 however, these results may not be generalizable to patients with established ART or heightened thrombotic risk. This case underscores the profound clinical consequences that may result from even brief deviations from therapeutic anticoagulation.

The diagnosis of ART with coronary embolization was established through a systematic multimodality imaging approach, highlighting the complementary roles of invasive and noninvasive imaging in evaluating complex LVAD-related complications.

Management of ART complicated by multivessel coronary embolization remains challenging and is not guided by standardized recommendations. Therapeutic options include intensified anticoagulation, thrombolysis, surgical thrombectomy, coronary intervention, or heart transplantation, each associated with substantial risks.8 In this case, preserved LVAD-mediated systemic perfusion allowed for a conservative approach.

Collectively, this case emphasizes that despite advances in LVAD technology, ART remains a clinically significant and potentially catastrophic complication. Vigilant surveillance of aortic valve opening, strict adherence to anticoagulation protocols, and early multidisciplinary collaboration are essential to mitigate risk and optimize outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Conclusions

ART with coronary embolization is a rare but life-threatening complication in patients supported with continuous-flow LVADs. In selected patients with preserved LVAD-mediated systemic perfusion, aggressive medical management may represent a viable therapeutic strategy when invasive intervention carries prohibitive risk. Early recognition, careful imaging assessment, and multidisciplinary decision-making are essential to optimize outcomes in this complex clinical scenario.
