Collective exerkines mediate physical activity mitigating new-onset heart failure risk in older adults with prediabetes or diabetes: a community-based longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Weike Liu, Luxiang Shang, Peilin Li, Yanli Yao, Haipeng Yin, Hua Zhang, Zhendong Liu, Yuqi Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence regarding how collective exerkines mediate the association between physical activity (PA) and heart failure (HF) risk in older adults with prediabetes and diabetes remains limited.MethodsThis prospective cohort study enrolled 5,886 older adults with prediabetes or diabetes from community dwellings in Shandong, China, between 2008 and 2011. PA metabolic equivalent task (MET) scores and plasma concentrations of ten exerkines, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and interleukin (IL)−6, were evaluated. The new-onset of HF during follow-up served as the outcome.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 11.1 years, 615 participants developed HF, with an incidence rate of 9.59 per 1000 person-years. A U-shaped dose-response association emerged between PA MET scores and HF risk. Compared to light PA, moderate and vigorous PA reduced HF risk by 57.0% (HR: 0.43; 95% CI: 0.33–0.56) and 36.0% (HR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.49–0.83), respectively. A quartile increase across all examined exerkines was associated with a 32% decreased HF risk (HR: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.63–0.73). Among these exerkines, BDNF was the most significant negative contributor, while IL-6 emerged as the predominant positive contributor to HF risk. Principal component analysis revealed IL-6 and BDNF as the primary contributors to component 1, explaining 42.87% of the data variance. Population attributable risk analysis indicated that low BDNF levels carried the highest attributable risk percentage at 18.6%, followed by elevated IL-6 levels at 12.6%. Recreational-leisure PA MET scores demonstrated a significant positive correlation with BDNF and a significant negative correlation with IL-6. In contrast, correlations between occupational PA MET scores and both BDNF and IL−6 were not statistically significant. The collective exerkines mediated 67.83% and 20.48% of the associations of the PA MET scores and hyperglycemic status with HF risk, respectively (Padjustment <0.001).ConclusionsCollective exerkine activity plays a crucial mediating role in the relationship between PA and HF risk among older adults managing prediabetes or diabetes. Cultivating optimal physical activity habits is paramount for preventing HF onset in this vulnerable group. This includes moderately increasing recreational-leisure pursuits, strategically reducing occupational and household activities, enhancing protective exerkines like BDNF, and suppressing harmful ones such as IL-6.Trial registration Retrospectively registered number: ChiCTR-EOC-17013598.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03170-8.Keywords: Diabetes, Exerkine, Heart failure, Physical activity, Prediabetes

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) stands as a leading cause of mortality, morbidity, and poor quality of life, affecting roughly 64 million individuals globally and imposing a substantial burden on both patients and the global healthcare system worldwide [1–3]. Hyperglycemic status, encompassing prediabetes and diabetes, emerge as major contributors, dramatically heightening the hazard for HF onset and progression [4–8]. Diabetes significantly escalates HF risk by more than 2-fold [8], while prediabetes independently elevates HF risk by 12%, even without progression to diabetes, compared to euglycemia [6]. Despite remarkable progress in pharmaceuticals, HF attributable to hyperglycemia remains persistently elevated [4, 5, 9].

Physical activity (PA) is universally acknowledged as the most cost-effective approach for achieving optimal physical fitness, including the prevention of hyperglycemia-attributable conditions [10, 11]. PA elicits multiple beneficial adaptive responses via enhancing muscle mass and strength, lowering body fat, and optimizing metabolic fitness [12–16]. For HF, PA stands as the recommended fundamental strategy for both prevention and treatment [17–19]. Regularly performing moderate- and/or vigorous-intensity PA is associated with a 33% to 66% lower HF risk [17, 18]. However, which PA patterns are optimal for older individuals with prediabetes or diabetes, and the underlying mechanisms through which PA confers these protective benefits, remain elusive.

In recent years, research interest in exerkines has exploded with remarkable intensity. Exerkines, first coined by Safdar et al. in 2016 [20], encompass a diverse spectrum of hormones, cytokines, nucleic acids, and metabolites dynamically released into circulation by tissues—including skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, the brain, heart, and liver—in response to PA [16, 20–22]. Crucially, only biomolecules regulated by PA qualify as exerkines. To date, hundreds of distinct exerkines, such as interleukin (IL)−6, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and adiponectin, among others, have been identified and harnessed as exercise mimetics to combat chronic conditions [16, 20–22]. The family of exerkines continues to expand, with new members continually emerging.

Each specific exerkine wields unique—often diametrically opposed—biological effects on metabolic cardiovascular diseases [16, 23–25]. A prospective cohort study, followed-up 5,672 subjects over a median 7.2 years, demonstrated that elevated IL−6 levels were independently associated with a 57% increased risk of cardiovascular disease. This robust association persisted even after adjustment for high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) [23]. Conversely, Framingham Heart Study data demonstrated that each standard deviation (SD) increase in serum BDNF level corresponded to a significant 12% reduction in cardiovascular disease risk and a 13% lower mortality risk [24]. Furthermore, given their inherent complexity and the synchronous secretion, exerkines function through intricate mixtures and interplay rather than acting in isolation [16, 20–22, 26]. Additionally, distinct patterns of PA, such as chronic and acute—trigger markedly different exerkine secretions from various tissues [21]. Therefore, their potential co-effect on metabolic cardiovascular diseases risk, and which factor plays a greater role, demand meticulous scrutiny.

This study primarily aimed to elucidate the potential mechanisms by which exerkines, acting as a whole, underlie the link between PA and the risk of prediabetes and diabetes-attributable HF in older adults, based on a longitudinal community cohort design. Furthermore, it aimed to explore optimal PA regimens and elucidate the potential contributions of distinct exerkines in enabling tailored and precise prevention, treatment, and management strategies for HF.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study adopts an observational, longitudinal cohort design. Between August 2008 and November 2011, a total of 5,886 individuals aged 60 and older, with prediabetes and diabetes, were eligible and enrolled from community dwellings in the Shandong area of China (Registration number: ChiCTR-EOC-17013598) [2, 7, 27–29]. Prediabetes is characterized by a fasting plasma glucose (FPG) level between 100 and 125 mg/dL and/or a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level of 5.7–6.4%, without a diagnosis of diabetes and not taking glucose-lowering medication. Diabetes is defined by a FPG level ≥ 126 mg/dL or a HbA1c level ≥ 6.5%, a previous diagnosis of diabetes, or the use of glucose-lowering medication. Participants with diabetes were further categorized into two distinct groups: those with well-controlled blood glucose levels (FPG < 126 mg/dL and HbA1c < 6.5%), and those exhibiting poorly-controlled levels (FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) in this study. The exclusion criteria for participants in this study were as follows: individuals with euglycemia (FPG < 100 mg/dL, HbA1c < 5.7%, no diagnosis of diabetes, and not taking glucose-lowering medication) [30–33], heart failure, myocardial infarction, stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, malignancy, dementia, psychosis, acute and chronic liver dysfunction, acute and chronic renal dysfunction and dialysis treatment, thyroid and parathyroid disease, Cushing’s syndrome, connective tissue diseases, autoimmune diseases, acute and chronic infectious diseases, alcohol and drug abuse, and those unwilling to provide informed consent.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Basic Medicine, Shandong Academy of Medical Sciences (Approved number: 2008-03-06). This study was conducted in strict compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Physical activity assessment

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to assess the duration and frequency of PA in various domains: occupational, transport-related, household, and recreational-leisure PA [34]. Weekly PA was converted into metabolic equivalent task (MET) values, using the formula: PA (MET-mins/week) = MET × weekly frequency × duration of each PA session. The activity was categorized as light (< 600 MET-mins/week), moderate (600 to < 3000 MET-mins/week), and vigorous (≥ 3000 MET-mins/week).

Assessment of plasma exerkines

Fasting plasma were collected from all participants and were subsequently stored at -80 ℃ until further analysis. The plasma concentrations of adiponectin, BDNF, fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15), IL-6, IL-7, IL-10, IL-15, irisin, and transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFβ1) were quantified using sensitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Specifically, kits for adiponectin, BDNF, FGF21, IL-6, irisin, and TGFβ1 were supplied by Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Kits for GDF-15, IL-10, and IL-15 were provided by ACROBiosystems (Beijing, China), while the IL-7 kit was sourced from R&D Systems (R&D Systems China Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Covariates

Covariates were collected and used for adjustment and stratification in the analysis models. Self-report questionnaires were utilized to collect information on age, sex, smoking habits, dietary intake, education level, history of hypertension and dyslipidemia, and history of antihypertensive and anti-dyslipidemia medication use. A food frequency questionnaire, a 149-item instrument validated through 24-hour recalls and multi-week weighted dietary records, was used to assess participants’ dietary intake. Dietary intake patterns were assessed through the alternative Healthy Eating Index 2010 (AHEI-2010), a comprehensive measure calculated using 11 key dietary elements (Supplementary Table 1) [35]. Waist circumference (WC) was measured using inelastic tape at the midpoint between the iliac crest and the lower rib margins. Abdominal obesity was defined as WC ≥ 85 cm for females and ≥ 90 cm for males [29]. The body mass index (BMI) is calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by the square of body height (m). Blood pressure and heart rate were assessed using a digital blood pressure monitor (HEM−701, OMRON Healthcare Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan). Plasma concentrations of total cholesterol (TCHO), triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), and FPG were detected using an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7600, Tokyo, Japan). HbA1c levels were detected using ion-exchange high-performance liquid chromatography. NT-proBNP plasma concentrations were quantified using a recombinant NT-proBNP assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), an important indicator of insulin resistance associated with HF [7, 27], was derived from the following formular: TyG = ln [Triglycerides (mg/dL)×FPG (mg/dL)/2] [7, 27]. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using serum creatinine as described by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration according to age, sex, and ethnicity. Anemia was defined as hemoglobin level below 120 g/L for females and 130 g/L for males. HsCRP level was evaluated using an ELISA kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Outcomes of interest

This study’s follow-up period concluded in October 2022. The primary outcome of interest in this study was the time until the initial onset of HF, whether occurring during hospitalization or specialized outpatient visits, following plasma exerkines assessment. HF was identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision code I50. The HF outcomes were adjudicated by a committee based on a comprehensive review of medical records, encompassing clinical signs and symptoms, NT-proBNP levels, echocardiogram recordings, and medication usage.

Statistical analyses

The baseline characteristics of the participants were presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR; 25th to 75th percentile) for continuous variables, with the distribution’s normality determined using the Kolmogorov Smirnov test, and as number (percentage) for categorical variables. The differences in continuous variables were analyzed using an independent samples t-test or a Mann-Whitney U test between the two groups. One-way analysis of variance and Kruskal-Wallis test with Bonferoni test was performed across three groups depending on the normality of their distribution. Categorical variables were analyzed using a chi-square test.

The restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses (‘rms’, ‘ggplot2’, and ‘survival’ packages in R version 4.5.1) were conducted to investigate the dose-response associations between PA MET scores, exerkine levels and HF risk. In this study, four knots were selected for use as they yielded the lowest Akaike Information Criterion value following an evaluation of knots ranging from three to six. The inflection points of PA and exerkines were determined based on the patterns of the RCS analyses. The Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to determine the difference in HF risk between groups classified by the levels of PA and exerkines. The hazard ratio (HR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was assessed using the Cox regression model.

A multivariable Quantile-G-Computation (QGC) model (‘qgcomp’, ‘ggplot2’, and ‘splines’ packages in R version 4.5.1) was employed to assess the collective effect of exerkines. Within the model, exerkine measurements were subjected to linear transformation utilizing the polynomial model: y = x2 − 2x + 1, when non-linear association with HF risk were observed. Each exerkine level was categorized into quartile and incorporated into the linear model. The collective effect was quantified using a HR with 95% CI, representing the alteration in HF risk associated with a one-quartile increase in all exerkines. The covariates adjustment for in the models were as follows: Model 1 for none; Model 2 for age, sex, smoking, education level, AHEI-2010, WC, BMI, blood pressures, heart rate, plasma levels of HbA1c, TG, HDL-c, and LDL-c, history of hypertension and dyslipidemia, history of antihypertensive and anti-dyslipidemic medication use, eGFR, and hemoglobin level; and Model 3 for confounders in Model 2 plus hsCRP, NT-proBNP levels and PA MET scores.

A multiple linear regression analysis was carried out to evaluate the correlations between PA MET scores and exerkine levels, after converting them to z-scores (mean = 0, SD = 1), with beta (β) coefficients serving as the correlation metric. The population attributable risk percentage (PARP) for HF risk was calculated across exerkine categories, which were categorized based on their respective inflection points identified from RCS analyses. The PARP was determined using the formula: PARp = 1-[(1-S0(t))/(1-S(t))], where S0(t) is the counterfactual survival function and S(t) is the factual survival function [7, 27]. A principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to assess the significance of the examined exerkines in the incidence of HF.

Sensitivity analyses were initially conducted to assess the robustness of the association by incorporating HF confounders, encompassing education level, AHEI-2010, hemoglobin level, eGFR, and hsCRP. AHEI-2010, which assesses diet quality, is associated with incident cardiovascular disease [36]. Furthermore, anemia, eGFR, and hsCRP are strongly linked to HF risk [1, 4, 37]. Subsequently, the initial onset of HF within the first year after plasma exerkines assessment was excluded, and Fine-Gray model was conducted accounting for competing risk of unknown causes death, to limit the bias. Stratified analyses were conducted to explore potential heterogeneity across factors including PA status (light/moderate/vigorous), sex (male/female), diabetes status (prediabetes/diabetes with well-controlled glucose/diabetes with poorly-controlled glucose), abdominal obesity (no/yes), hypertension (no/yes), and anemia (no/yes). The E-value was calculated to rigorously evaluate the robustness of the findings against unaccounted confounders, thereby mitigating potential bias in the association between exerkines and HF risk [38]. A small E-value indicates that little unaccounted confounding is necessary to explain the observed association. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was evaluated, where a VIF below 5 signifies no significant collinearity among covariates, thereby preventing multicollinearity. Finally, we also explored the role of collective exerkines in the association of hyperglycemic status and PA MET scores with HF risk using a mediation analysis model (Hayes process procedure for SPSS version 4.1).

R software (version 4.5.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, http://www.R-project.org) and SPSS (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) were used to perform all statistical analyses. Graphs were generated using R software version 4.5.1 and GraphPad Prism (version 9.1.0, GraphPad Prism Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 5,886 participants, aged 68.57 ± 6.32 years, were included in this study. Among them, 3,123 (53.1%) were female, 4,264 (72.4%) had prediabetes, 1,191 (20.2%) had well-controlled glucose, and 431 (7.3%) had poorly-controlled glucose. Participants with lower education levels, lower AHEI-2010, hypertension, diabetes with well- or poorly-controlled glucose, anemia, elevated WC, higher BMI, increased FPG and HbA1c, raised NT-pro-BNP levels, higher TyG, and heightened hsCRP, alongside lower proportions of transportation-related and recreation/leisure-related PA METs and higher household-related PA METs, were more likely to develop HF. Outcomes

Over a median follow-up of 11.1 (IQR: 10.3–11.8) years, 615 participants developed HF, corresponding to an incidence of 9.59 (95% CI: 8.84–10.34) per 1000 person-years.

Association between PA MET scores and HF risk, and correlations with exerkine levels and hyperglycemic status

RCS analysis revealed a U-shaped, non-linear dose-response association between PA MET scores, treated as a continuous variable, and HF risk, with an inflection point identified at 2357.5 MET scores (P for nonlinear < 0.001; Fig. 2A). When participants were classified into three groups, moderate PA was associated with a remarkable 57.0% reduction in HF risk compared to light PA (HR: 0.43; 95% CI: 0.33–0.56; Padjustment <0.001) after adjustment for confounders. Vigorous PA demonstrated a 36.0% lower HF risk (HR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.49–0.83; Padjustment = 0.001; Fig. 2B). For each PA domain, U-shaped associations emerged between occupational, household, and transport-related PA and HF risk (P for nonlinear < 0.001). Notably, concerning occupational and household PA, HF risk significantly increased as activity levels climbed (Padjustmen <0.001). Conversely, recreational-leisure PA exhibited an L-shaped association with HF risk (P for nonlinear < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 1).

After standardized z-score transformation, multilinear regression analysis revealed that PA MET scores exhibited a significant positive correlation with BDNF, adiponectin, TGFβ1, and IL-10 levels (Padjustment <0.001). Conversely, it showed significant negative correlations with IL-6, GDF-15, IL-15, irisin, FGF21, and IL-7 levels (Padjustment <0.001; Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table 2). Corrleations between standard MET scores of four PA domains and exerkines identified using multlinear regression analyses were detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Recreational-leisure PA MET scores demonstrated a significant positive correlation with BDNF and a significant negative correlation with IL-6. In contrast, correlations between occupational PA MET scores and both BDNF and IL−6 were not significant.

The differences in FPG and HbA1c across the PA status were showed in Supplementary Fig. 2. Compared to light PA, the levels of FPG and HbA1c were significantly lower in moderate and vigorous PA (all P < 0.001). PA MET scores were significant negative correlated to FPG (β: -0.03, 95% CI: -0.05–-0.01; Padjustment = 0.002) and HbA1c (β: -0.03, 95% CI: -0.04–-0.02; Padjustment <0.001). Among diabetes patients, those engaging in moderate PA exhibited the highest rate of well-controlled glucose at 75.6%, significantly higher than those engaging in light (27.9%) or vigorous (69.4%) PA (P < 0.001).

Association between specific exerkines and HF risk

After adjustment for confounders, a one-SD increase in BDNF, adiponectin, TGFβ1, and IL-10 concentrations was significantly associated with a markedly reduced risk of HF. Conversely, one-SD increments in IL-6, GDF-15, IL-15, irisin, FGF21, and IL-7 were significantly associated with an increased risk of HF (Fig. 3A).

Association between collective exerkines and HF risk

The multivariable QGC model revealed that a quartile increase across all ten measured exerkine levels was significantly associated with a reduced HF risk after adjustment for confounders (HR: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.63–0.73, Padjustment <0.001; Fig. 4A). BDNF, TGFβ1, adiponectin, and IL-10 were identified as negative predictors of HF risk (sum of negative coefficients = -0.58). Among these, BDNF emerged as the strongest negative predictor (scaled effect size = 0.49). The scaled effect sizes were 0.20, 0.17, 0.14 for TGFβ1, adiponectin, and IL-10, respectively. Conversely, IL-6, irisin, IL-7, IL-15, FGF21, and GDF-15 were identified as positive contributors to HF risk (sum of positive coefficients = 0.41), with IL-6 standing out as the most potent positive contributor (scaled effect size = 0.47; Fig. 4B). The scaled effect sizes were 0.15, 0.15, 0.13, 0.09, and 0.02 for irisin, IL-7, IL-15, FGF21, and GDF-15, respectively. Other sensitivity analysis

After excluding new-onset HF within the first year following exerkine assessment and treating deaths from unknown causes as competing risk events, the associations between PA status, hyperglycemic status, and collective exerkine levels with HF risk remained robust (Supplementary Table 6). The E-value for each exerkine was assessed in this study (Supplementary Table 7). The results revealed a low likelihood of confounding influence from unaccounted factors. We evaluated the VIF values of all covariates included in Model 3. The values for TCHO and TyG both exceeded five and were consequently excluded to ensure model stability. The retained covariates demonstrated low multicollinearity and high statistical independence, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent regression analysis (Supplementary Table 8).

Discussion

In this extensive, community-based, longitudinal cohort study, our findings revealed that exerkines, acting as a whole, robustly mediated the association between PA and HF risk, alongside the link between hyperglycemic status and HF risk. Among measured exerkines, BDNF served as the most significant negative contributor, while IL-6 emerged as the most potent positive contributor to HF risk. After rigorous adjustment confounders and stratified analysis, these findings remained robust and consistent. Moreover, PA MET scores exhibited a distinctive U-shaped, non-linear dose-response association with HF risk. Among PA domains, distinct U-shaped associations appeared between occupational and household PA and HF risk, while a clear L-shaped association was observed between recreational-leisure PA and HF risk.

Promoting PA stands as a universally recognized and exceptionally cost-effective strategy, vital for combating conventional cardiovascular risk factors and significantly reducing the risk of chronic conditions and mortality [10, 11, 17, 39–41]. Accumulating at least 150 min per week of moderate-intensity PA or 75 min per week of vigorous-intensity PA is crucial for adults with type 2 diabetes [40]. The Australian Longitudinal Study on Women’s Health [39] showed that women maintaining increasing or high PA trajectories experienced significantly lower risks of diabetes, obesity, and physical disability compared to those with consistently low activity levels. A prospective UK Biobank cohort study [17] revealed that moderate and vigorous PA slashed HF risk by 63% and 66%, respectively, compared to no moderate-to-vigorous PA. However, a reverse J-shaped dose-response association emerged between vigorous PA and HF with a potentially lower risk reduction above 150 min/week. Our study observed a U-shaped dose-response association between PA MET scores and HF risk, with an inflection point at 2357.5 MET scores, in older adults with prediabetes and diabetes. The lowest HF risk emerged in participants engaging in moderate PA, not vigorous PA, although both groups exhibited significantly lower HF risk compared with those performing light PA. This difference may be primarily attributable to moderate-intensity PA fostering more effective than high-intensity PA in promoting the secretion of protective exerkinins. These findings indicate that moderate-intensity PA likely represents the optimal pattern for minimizing HF risk in this specific population.

For specific exerkines, for example, the results of the RCS models in this study demonstrated a linear dose-response association of BDNF with HF risk and a non-linear dose-response association of IL-6. Each one-SD increment in BDNF and IL-6 was associated with a 31.0% decrease and a 34.0% increase in HF risk, respectively. In the general population, every 1 ln pg/mL increase in IL-6 has been independently associate with a 28% increase rise in HF risk [25]. Whereas, BDNF serves a critical protective role in preserving the structural and functional integrity of the heart [41, 42]. A meta-analysis [43] encompassing 11 studies and 1,420 HF patients revealed that BDNF levels were significantly lower in HF patients than in healthy individuals. In this study, the QGC model was also used to precisely delineate the specific direction and magnitude of each exerkine’s contribution within mixtures through positive or negative weighting [44]. The results further identified BDNF as the most significant protective factor against HF risk, while IL-6 emerged as the most prominent risk factor. However, regarding PARPs, diminished BDNF levels and heightened IL-6 contribute a mere 31.2%. A substantial 54.4% is attributable to a diverse array of other exerkines. These findings highlight that exerkines, beyond their inherent complexity and synchronous release in response to PA, function through intricate mixtures rather than in isolation [16, 20–22].

The significant advantage of the QGC model lay in its emphasis on total exerkine production while adeptly navigating the intricate complexity of these bioactive molecules as dynamic, interactive mixtures [44]. We employed this model to assess the combined influence of the ten exerkines. The results demonstrated that HF risk decreased by 32% for each quartile increase across the ten exerkines following adjustment for NT-proBNP levels and pertinent confounders. The PCA results further highlighted the role of exerkines collectively modulating HF risk. It suggests an independent protective effect of exerkines, acting collectively, on HF.

The mediation analysis demonstrated that collective exerkines mediated 67.83% of PA’s protective effect against HF. This mediation effect also significantly explained 20.48% of the association between hyperglycemia and HF risk. PA triggers a cascade of profoundly beneficial adaptive responses, exerting far-reaching positive effects on skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, heart, brain, liver, and the immune system, underpinning essential whole-body health benefits [12–16]. Exerkines, released in response to physical activity, act as pivotal biosignaling molecules that mediate these crucial adaptations. The potential mechanisms underlying this mediation encompass the intricate modulation of glucose uptake, mitochondrial biogenesis, cellular bioenergetics, redox biology, inflammatory responses, angiogenesis, and muscle hypertrophy [14–16, 21]. The mediating role of exerkines in the association between hyperglycemia status and HF risk may stem from hyperglycemia’s intricate links to mitochondrial biogenesis, redox biology, and inflammatory responses [45, 46].

It is well-established that PA involves the harmonious coordination of diverse tissues, organs, and systems across the entire body. This intricate interplay induces the release of a cascade of hormones, cytokines, nucleic acids, and metabolites. In this study, we measured ten key exerkines, selected for their well-established roles in cardiometabolic adaptation to PA. These crucial molecules originate from distinct tissues: BDNF primarily emanates from the brain; IL-6, IL-7, IL-10, and irisin derive from muscle; and adiponectin and FGF21 originate from adipose tissue [14, 15, 20, 21]. Assessing these distinct exerkines may significantly elucidate the synergistic response mechanisms of different tissues to PA.

Among four PA domains, we uncovered pronounced U-shaped associations linking both occupational and household PA to HF risk. HF risk climbed significantly as activity levels increased. While transport-related PA also exhibited a U-shaped association with HF risk, the risk was significantly reduced at high activity levels compared to low levels, as defined by its inflection point. Regarding recreational-leisure PA, a stark L-shaped association emerged clearly with HF risk. These findings indicate that reducing occupational and household activities, particularly occupational tasks, while maintaining appropriate levels of recreational-leisure and transportation activities—especially those focused on recreation and leisure—may prove beneficial for older adults with prediabetes and diabetes seeking to mitigate HF risk. The primary cause likely stems from recreational-leisure and transportation activities stimulating the release of protective exerkines like BDNF, whereas occupation and household activities exert minimal impact on harmful factors such as IL-6.

Moreover, this study investigated the interplay among sex, obesity type, and hyperglycemic status concerning the collective predictive capacity of exerkines. These interplay indicate that physiological and metabolic contexts are significantly affected by collective exerkines. For instance, the higher predictive value in females might be related to differences in sex hormones, adipose tissue distribution, and metabolic regulation, which could be affected by exerkines production and function [47, 48]. In participants with abdominal obesity, the exerkine profile may modify insulin resistance and inflammatory levels which are associated with the increased adipose tissue [49, 50]. Similarly, individuals with diabetes, even when blood glucose was well-controlled, may exhibit altered insulin signaling pathways influenced by collective exerkines [51, 52]. Therefore, our data demonstrate the crucial need to promote optimal PA pattern, thereby boosting protective exerkine levels, to significantly low the incidence of HF in individuals battling abdominal obesity and diabetes, especially pronounced in those struggling with poorly controlled glucose.

Evidence has demonstrated that the production and function of exerkines exhibit differential responses to acute and chronic PA [21]. Acute PA is associated with the maintenance of metabolic homeostasis and the balance of acute inflammation [53], whereas chronic PA leads to long-term metabolic adaptations, balance of exerkines production and function, and a reduction in inflammation [54]. For example, acute PA dramatic surge in circulating IL-6 levels, released from contracting skeletal muscle [55, 56]. Conversely, chronic PA consistently reduces the circulating IL-6 concentrations [21, 55]. In this study, we collected baseline blood samples from participants after an overnight fast of at least eight hours. The detected exerkines levels served as a reliable indicator of chronic PA exposure. Moreover, multiomics-based studies demonstrate the feasibility of using baseline exerkine signatures to predict individual metabolic and cardiorespiratory responses to exercise [50, 57]. Therefore, our findings may primarily apply to individuals with established chronic PA patterns, such as appropriate levels of recreational-leisure activities, and might not be generalizable to those experiencing acute PA. Furthermore, exerkines may emerge as promising novel therapeutic targets for the precise prevention, treatment, and management of prediabetes and diabetes in elderly patients.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of this study lies in employing a multivariable QGC model to comprehensively evaluate the effect of collective exerkines. Our study reinforced and expanded the evidence regarding the role of exerkines as a whole in the pathogenesis of HF, particularly in older adults with prediabetes and diabetes. Previously, this evidence was scarce. Secondly, stratifying diabetes cases into well-controlled and poorly-controlled glucose categories, combined with meticulous adjustment for multiple potential confounders, strengthened the robustness of the main findings. Thirdly, we incorporated education level and AHEI-2010 as adjusted confounders to account for the potential influence of these socioeconomic factors and dietary pattern choices on the link between PA and HF risk. Finally, the considerable sample size, prospective cohort study design, and long-term follow-up facilitated robust longitudinal analysis.

This study also has several limitations. Firstly, apart from the investigated exerkines, others remained unexamined, which potentially introduce a significant bias into our results, although the E-values of the measured ten exerkines were low. Secondly, as previously noted, the exerkine levels measured in this study primarily reflect chronic PA exposure; research into acute exposure merits further investigation. Thirdly, we assessed PA using the IPAQ in the study. This questionnaire only covers PA in the past week and relies on self-report. This may introduce recall bias, leading to inaccurate results in the study. Fourthly, the analysis did not account for dynamic changes in collective exerkine levels, which might more precisely capture their association with HF risk. Fifthly, this study did not evaluate muscle quality, strength, or alterations in fat distribution, potentially restricting our depth of understanding regarding the link between collective exerkines and HF risk [2, 27, 58]. Additionally, although diabetes and prediabetes were determined using either FPG or HbA1c, alongside a history of diabetes and glucose-lowering medication, the 2-hour post-load plasma glucose value, recommended by the American Diabetes Organization and World Health Organization guidelines [59, 60], was not considered in this study, which may introduce bias into the findings. Participants were predominantly recruited from China’s Shandong region, which may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

This longitudinal cohort study reinforces and expands evidence concerning the roles of PA and specific collective exerkines in influencing HF risk among older adults with prediabetes and diabetes. Fostering optimal PA habits is paramount. This includes moderately increasing recreational-leisure pursuits, reducing occupational activities, boosting protective exercise factors like BDNF, and lowering harmful exercise factors such as IL-6. Implementing these strategies may significantly reduce the risk of HF within this vulnerable group. These insights may guide targeted interventions, such as structured chronic aerobic or resistance exercise, and advance exerkine-based therapies—particularly precise therapies for individuals unsuitable for exercise—for both preventing and managing HF. However, uncovering the precise link between exerkines and HF risk necessitates further investigation across diverse ethnicities, euglycemic individuals, populations younger than 60 years, and other exerkines not yet studied.
