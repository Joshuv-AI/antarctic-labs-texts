Postoperative outcomes of reconstituting versus fenestrating subtotal cholecystectomy: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Behnam Reza Makhsosi, Pourya Ghobadi, Amir Tarighinia, Tara Mazaheri, Leila Solouki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLaparoscopic cholecystectomy remains the gold-standard treatment for symptomatic gallbladder disease; however, in situations where safe dissection is not achievable, subtotal cholecystectomy has emerged as a reliable alternative to minimize operative risk. Given the technical differences between the reconstituting and fenestrating approaches and the inconsistent evidence regarding their postoperative outcomes, this systematic review and meta-analysis was designed to provide a comprehensive comparison of these two surgical techniques. The present study incorporates more recently published studies and provides an updated synthesis of the available evidence.Materials and methodsThis study was conducted as a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, SID, Magiran, and IranMedex using relevant MeSH terms and keywords. The literature search was conducted up to December 2025. Eligible studies were systematically analyzed using random-effects meta-analysis; risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and results were visualized using the ROBVIS tool.ResultsThirteen studies involving 950 patients (reconstituting: n = 324; fenestrating: n = 626) were included. Reconstituting subtotal cholecystectomy was associated with a significantly lower incidence of postoperative bile leak compared with the fenestrating technique (OR = 0.29, 95% CI 0.16–0.55; P = 0.0002; I² =34%). No statistically significant differences were observed for postoperative ERCP (OR = 0.43, 95% CI 0.17–1.07; P = 0.071; I² = 64.7%) or reoperation rates (OR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.19–1.61; P = 0.276; I² = 0%).ConclusionBased on low-certainty GRADE evidence, this meta-analysis indicates that reconstituting subtotal cholecystectomy is associated with a significantly lower risk of postoperative bile leak compared with the fenestrating technique, with low-to-moderate heterogeneity. Although the overall direction of effect for postoperative ERCP and reoperation also favored the reconstituting technique, these differences did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting limited statistical power and heterogeneity; these results should be considered hypothesis-generating. Overall, the reconstituting technique appears to offer a safer postoperative profile, though confirmation by prospective studies is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-04039-z.Keywords: Subtotal cholecystectomy, Bile leak, Reconstituting, Fenestrating, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is widely recognized as the standard treatment for symptomatic gallbladder disease and is associated with favorable short- and long-term outcomes in most patients [1]. Nevertheless, a subset of cases—commonly referred to as the “difficult gallbladder”—continues to present substantial technical challenges even for experienced surgeons [2]. Severe acute or chronic inflammation, extensive fibrosis, dense adhesions, distorted anatomy within Calot’s triangle, Mirizzi syndrome, or prior interventions may obscure critical biliary structures and significantly increase the risk of iatrogenic bile duct injury [3]. Such injuries remain among the most serious complications of biliary surgery, carrying considerable morbidity, long-term sequelae, diminished quality of life, and increased healthcare costs [4].

To reduce these risks, bailout strategies have gained increasing acceptance when safe identification of the cystic duct and cystic artery cannot be achieved. Among these approaches, subtotal cholecystectomy has emerged as a well-established alternative that allows surgeons to avoid hazardous dissection in high-risk anatomical regions while maintaining operative safety [5]. By deliberately leaving a portion of the gallbladder wall in situ, this technique provides effective source control and symptom relief [6]. Over the past two decades, particularly with advancements in laparoscopic techniques, the utilization of subtotal cholecystectomy has increased substantially and is frequently favored over conversion to open surgery [7].

Despite its widespread adoption, subtotal cholecystectomy is not a uniform procedure and encompasses distinct technical variations that may influence postoperative outcomes. The two principal techniques are reconstituting and fenestrating subtotal cholecystectomy [4]. Although both methods rely on partial removal of the gallbladder, they differ fundamentally in the management of the gallbladder remnant and the cystic duct [8]. These technical distinctions are believed to underlie differences in both early and late postoperative complications. As a result, the choice between the two approaches is often driven by surgeon preference rather than definitive evidence [9].

In reconstituting subtotal cholecystectomy, following excision of the anterior gallbladder wall and evacuation of gallstones, the remaining gallbladder remnant—typically at the level of the infundibulum or neck—is closed using sutures or stapling devices, thereby creating a sealed biliary pouch [10]. This approach is intended to reduce early postoperative bile leakage. In contrast, fenestrating subtotal cholecystectomy involves leaving the gallbladder remnant open, usually without closure of the cystic duct, allowing bile to drain freely [11]. In many cases, mucosal ablation of the remaining gallbladder wall is performed, and a surgical drain is placed to control bile output [12].

These technical differences have important clinical implications. Reconstituting subtotal cholecystectomy has generally been associated with lower rates of early bile leakage, a complication that may necessitate prolonged drainage, endoscopic intervention, or reoperation [13]. However, the presence of a closed gallbladder remnant may predispose patients to long-term complications such as recurrent stone formation, remnant cholecystitis, or the eventual need for completion cholecystectomy [14]. Conversely, fenestrating subtotal cholecystectomy eliminates the closed biliary reservoir and may therefore reduce late biliary complications, but this benefit appears to come at the cost of a higher incidence of early bile leakage and an increased need for postoperative interventions, including endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) [15].

The available literature has reported inconsistent findings regarding the comparative outcomes of these two techniques. Rates of bile leakage, reintervention, readmission, and late biliary complications vary widely across studies [16]. This heterogeneity is largely attributable to differences in study design, patient populations, disease severity, surgeon expertise, and the lack of standardized definitions for key outcomes [17]. Furthermore, most published studies are retrospective in nature, limiting the strength of causal inferences and increasing susceptibility to selection bias [18].

In 2024, Motter and colleagues published a review comparing outcomes between reconstituting and fenestrating subtotal cholecystectomy, providing valuable insight into postoperative bile leakage and the need for additional interventions [19]. Motter et al. reported a pooled OR of ~ 0.30 (95% CI 0.13–0.71) favoring the reconstituting technique; the present analysis yields a similar estimate (OR = 0.29, 95% CI 0.16–0.55) with a more homogeneous, laparoscopic-only dataset. Despite its contribution, that analysis was constrained by incomplete capture of more recently published data and underrepresentation of longer-term biliary outcomes [20]. Moreover, the expanding volume of real-world evidence from high-volume surgical centers, along with ongoing refinements in perioperative and endoscopic management, underscores the need for an updated and more comprehensive synthesis of the available data [21, 22].

Given the persistent challenges associated with the management of the difficult gallbladder, ongoing uncertainty regarding the optimal subtotal cholecystectomy technique, and the growing body of published evidence in recent years, a contemporary reassessment of comparative outcomes is clinically warranted. Therefore, the present study was designed as a systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively compare the clinical outcomes of reconstituting and fenestrating subtotal cholecystectomy. While previous meta-analyses (e.g., Motter et al., 2024) have compared these two techniques using broader inclusion criteria, the present study applies more restrictive eligibility criteria — requiring explicit anatomical definition of reconstituting versus fenestrating techniques and excluding studies with mixed surgical approaches — to reduce clinical heterogeneity. Consequently, our analysis includes fewer studies but provides a more homogeneous comparison of the two techniques, complemented by a detailed assessment of publication bias, sensitivity analysis, and schematic illustration of surgical anatomy

Material and methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. A study protocol was developed a priori. Although prospective registration in PROSPERO was not feasible due to internet access restrictions in our country and time limitations, we obtained ethics approval from Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (IR.KUMS.MED.REC.1403.131) before data extraction to ensure adherence to a predefined methodology. The internal protocol is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they reported comparative clinical outcomes following reconstituting versus fenestrating subtotal cholecystectomy. No restriction on study design was applied during the selection process; however, all included studies were retrospective in nature. Case reports, review articles, editorials, conference abstracts, and studies with insufficient or non-comparable outcome data were excluded from the analysis.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across international and national databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, SID, Magiran, and IranMedex. Relevant keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) related to subtotal cholecystectomy, reconstituting technique, fenestrating technique, and postoperative outcomes were combined using Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”). The search process was independently performed by two authors, and any discrepancies in study selection were resolved through discussion with a third author (Table 1). No language restrictions were applied. The full search strategies for all databases provided in supplementary file 2.

Data extraction

Data were systematically extracted from each included study using a predefined standardized form. For every article, information was collected on study characteristics (author, year, country, and study design), patient demographics (age, sex, ASA grade), surgical context (indication for surgery, proportion of emergency procedures, and conversion to open surgery), and the type of subtotal cholecystectomy performed (fenestrating or reconstituting). Postoperative outcomes of interest included bile leak, need for postoperative ERCP, and reoperation rates, each recorded separately for reconstituting and fenestrating groups as event counts with corresponding denominators. Only laparoscopic cases were included, and patients converted to open surgery were excluded to ensure comparability across studies. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). The results were subsequently visualized using the ROBVIS tool, which provides a structured and transparent graphical display of bias domains for illustrative purposes only across studies. In addition, the certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) approach, which rates the overall quality of evidence based on study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Statistical analysis

Dichotomous outcomes were synthesized using effect estimates expressed as odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals, while continuous variables were pooled as weighted mean differences. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran’s Q test and quantified with the I² statistic, with statistical significance defined as a p value < 0.10 or an I² value exceeding 25%. We adopted this conservative, sensitivity-oriented threshold (rather than the conventional I² > 50%) to detect even modest levels of heterogeneity that might influence interpretation; this choice means I² = 34% is classified as low-to-moderate rather than acceptable. Heterogeneity was described as low (I² ≤ 25%), low-to-moderate (25–50%), or substantial (> 50%). All meta-analytical computations were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) software, version 5.4. To evaluate the robustness of the results, sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding individual studies from the overall analysis. A random-effects model was used due to anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot inspection and Egger’s test for outcomes including at least 10 studies.

Results

A total of 811 records were identified, of which 675 remained after duplicate removal. Following title and abstract screening, 411 records were excluded. A total of 264 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 252 were excluded. Finally, 12 comparative studies were included in the systematic review (Table 2). Of these, 11 studies were included in the quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) of the primary outcome, postoperative bile leak. One study was excluded from the quantitative synthesis because of incomplete outcome data for the primary outcome (Table 3; Fig. 1).

The included studies were conducted across a wide range of countries, including Japan, South Korea, the Netherlands, Turkey, Australia, Canada, the United Kingdom, the United States, Norway, China, and Saudi Arabia, reflecting a broad international experience. The publication years of the included studies ranged from 2008 to 2025. Notably, the majority of studies were published during the 2020s, indicating a growing research interest in subtotal cholecystectomy over the past decade (Table 2).

Across the included studies, the mean or median age of patients generally ranged from the late 50s to early 70s years, with a variable male predominance, with the proportion of male patients ranging approximately from 42% to 86%. The primary indication for subtotal cholecystectomy was acute cholecystitis, including severe, gangrenous, perforated, and acute-on-chronic forms, while other indications such as symptomatic gallstones, choledocholithiasis, biliary colic, cholangitis, and pancreatitis were reported less frequently (Table 2).

This meta-analysis included 11 comparative studies encompassing a total of 763 patients (note: Lunevicius 2021 is included in Table 3 for transparency but was excluded from the pooled analysis because it included non-laparoscopic procedures, violating the laparoscopic-only inclusion criterion), of whom 275 underwent the reconstituting technique and 488 underwent the fenestrating technique. For postoperative ERCP, seven studies comprising 691 patients were analyzed. For reoperation, five studies comprising 468 patients were analyzed. Using a Mantel–Haenszel random-effects model, the pooled analysis demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of postoperative bile leak in the reconstituting group compared with the fenestrating group (pooled OR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.16–0.55; Z = 3.78, P = 0.0002). Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (τ² = 0.30; χ² = 15.67, df = 10, P = 0.11; I²= 33–34%), indicating low-to-moderate heterogeneity consistent with the included studies (Fig. 2- Table 3). Note: Fasting 2023 met eligibility criteria but did not report any of the three pre-specified outcomes in extractable form and was therefore excluded from all quantitative analyses. Funnel plot analysis for bile leak did not suggest significant asymmetry, andEgger’s test was not statistically significant (p = 0.093), though with only 11 studies this test has limited statistical power to detect asymmetry. The funnel plot is provided as Supplementary Fig. 1. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the primary outcome. Sequential exclusion of each individual study did not materially alter the pooled estimate, with odds ratios ranging from 0.25 to 0.35 across all iterations (Supplementary Fig. 2). In no iteration did the 95% confidence interval cross 1, indicating that no single study unduly influenced the overall result.

Discussion

The findings of this updated systematic review and meta-analysis, which incorporates more recently published studies and applies more restrictive inclusion criteria than prior analyses, suggest that the reconstituting technique is associated with a lower incidence of postoperative bile leakage compared with the fenestrating technique, based on low-certainty observational evidence. While the overall trends for other key outcomes, including postoperative ERCP and reoperation rates, also favored the reconstituting technique, these differences did not reach statistical significance. These results suggest that the choice of surgical technique exerts a meaningful influence on certain complications, although its impact is not uniform across all postoperative parameters and is shaped by multiple clinical and technical factors.

The most clinically important finding of this analysis was the significantly lower incidence of postoperative bile leak among patients treated with the reconstituting technique. Bile leak remains one of the most frequent and challenging complications following subtotal cholecystectomy and is associated with secondary issues such as intra-abdominal abscess, sepsis, prolonged hospitalization, and the need for additional interventions [36]. The disparity between the two techniques can largely be explained by their intrinsic anatomical and technical differences. In the reconstituting technique, the gallbladder remnant is completely or partially closed, creating a sealed or semi-sealed cavity that allows for better control of bile drainage [37]. Conversely, the fenestrating technique intentionally leaves the gallbladder stump open, permitting free bile flow into the operative bed or drain—an environment that predisposes to prolonged leakage, especially when intraductal pressure is elevated [38].

Furthermore, patients undergoing subtotal cholecystectomy often present with severe inflammation, fibrosis, and distorted biliary anatomy, all of which complicate precise cystic duct identification and ligation. Under such conditions, fenestrating techniques may inadvertently leave small tracts of bile leakage unaddressed, whereas the reconstituting technique seeks to structurally restore the biliary continuity, thus minimizing the risk of persistent drainage [39]. This mechanistic distinction likely accounts for the substantially lower rate of bile leak observed in the reconstituting group and underscores the clinical importance of this surgical strategy in reducing postoperative morbidity and resource utilization [40].

Regarding postoperative ERCP, although its incidence was numerically lower in patients undergoing the reconstituting technique, the difference did not reach statistical significance. We acknowledge that the lack of statistical significance for ERCP and reoperation is partly due to limited statistical power, which itself results from our intentionally restrictive inclusion criteria (laparoscopic‑only studies with explicit anatomical definitions). This trade‑off between clinical homogeneity and sample size was a deliberate design choice to reduce heterogeneity, but it inevitably reduces power for secondary outcomes. This outcome likely reflects the multifactorial nature of ERCP indications. Endoscopic intervention after subtotal cholecystectomy is not dictated solely by bile leak but also by factors such as residual common bile duct stones, transient biliary obstruction, elevated liver function tests, and center-specific clinical policies [41]. Therefore, even when bile leakage is less frequent, the need for ERCP may persist for other diagnostic or therapeutic reasons, obscuring the true procedural difference between groups [42].

Institutional variation further contributes to this inconsistency. Some centers adopt an early or even prophylactic ERCP policy in the presence of minimal symptoms or laboratory abnormalities, whereas others adhere to a conservative, symptom-guided approach. These differences in practice patterns, together with variability in endoscopic expertise and postoperative monitoring, likely explain the moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity observed among studies and the lack of statistical significance despite a directional trend favoring the reconstituting technique [29, 43]. However, subgroup or meta-regression analyses were not feasible due to the limited number of studies and incomplete reporting of relevant variables. Similarly, reoperation rates were lower in the reconstituting group, though this difference was not statistically significant. Reoperation after subtotal cholecystectomy typically represents a last resort in the management of severe or persistent complications—such as uncontrolled bile leak, bleeding, or intra-abdominal sepsis—that have failed less invasive management [4]. With the advent and widespread availability of minimally invasive techniques such as ERCP, percutaneous drainage, and image-guided interventions, many such complications are now managed non-surgically. As a result, absolute reoperation rates have declined, limiting the statistical power to detect meaningful differences between surgical techniques [34].

Moreover, the decision to perform a reoperation is highly dependent on individualized clinical judgment, patient comorbidity, and institutional thresholds for intervention [38]. These subjective elements add variability to the literature and may obscure subtle yet clinically relevant differences between techniques. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance for reoperation should not necessarily be interpreted as equivalence; rather, it reflects the overall rarity of the event and variability in clinical decision-making processes [9].

Taken together, based on low-certainty observational evidence, the findings suggest that the reconstituting technique confers an advantage in minimizing bile leakage, while its impact on other postoperative outcomes — such as ERCP and reoperation — remains inconclusive, as these differences did not achieve statistical significance. From a clinical standpoint, this emphasizes the importance of tailoring the surgical approach to patient-specific factors and intraoperative circumstances [35]. In cases characterized by severe inflammation, extensive fibrosis, or distorted anatomy, the reconstituting technique may offer more reliable closure of the biliary remnant with potentially fewer early postoperative complications, based on the current low-certainty evidence; however, these observations require confirmation from prospective studies [1].

Nevertheless, these results must be interpreted in light of certain limitations. Most included studies were observational, with inherent selection bias and variations in surgical expertise. Definitions of outcomes, follow-up durations, and management protocols differed among studies, contributing to heterogeneity. Moreover, details regarding drain placement, intraoperative cholangiography, and reconstruction technique were often inconsistently reported, which may influence the generalizability of the findings. Despite these limitations, the relatively large pooled sample size and the consistent direction of effect across studies strengthen the reliability of the conclusions, particularly regarding bile leak reduction [43, 44].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, all included studies were observational, with inherent selection and confounding bias. Second, the review was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO, although an internal protocol was followed. Third, our strict inclusion criteria — while reducing heterogeneity — resulted in a smaller sample size than some prior meta‑analyses. Fourth, heterogeneity for the ERCP outcome (I² = 64.7%) could not be fully explored due to limited data. Fifth, outcome definitions (e.g., bile leak, ERCP indication) varied across studies. Sixth, indications for postoperative ERCP were not uniformly reported or defined across studies, limiting the comparability of this outcome. Seventh, publication bias assessment via Egger’s test and funnel plot analysis was performed with only 11 studies, which provides limited statistical power to reliably detect asymmetry; these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, the low event rate for reoperation limits statistical power.

Based on the present findings, well-designed randomized controlled trials with standardized outcome definitions and adequate follow-up are warranted to further clarify the comparative effectiveness of reconstituting versus fenestrating subtotal cholecystectomy. Future studies should also account for disease severity, anatomical complexity, surgeon expertise, and postoperative management protocols to better identify sources of heterogeneity. From a clinical perspective, the results of this meta-analysis support the preferential use of the reconstituting technique, particularly in patients at higher risk of postoperative bile leak, while emphasizing that the final surgical approach should remain individualized and guided by intraoperative findings and surgical judgment.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, based on low‑certainty evidence (GRADE), demonstrates that, in subtotal cholecystectomy, the reconstituting technique may be associated with a statistically significant reduction in postoperative bile leak, an effect that remained robust despite low to moderate heterogeneity, supporting the reliability of this finding. In contrast, although the overall direction of effect for postoperative ERCP and reoperation favored the reconstituting technique, these differences did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting moderate to substantial heterogeneity and wide confidence intervals. Collectively, these results suggest that the reconstituting technique appears to be associated with a reduced incidence of one of the most clinically relevant complications of subtotal cholecystectomy, based on low-certainty observational evidence, while its impact on other postoperative outcomes appears to be influenced by multiple clinical, technical, and institutional factors.

In conclusion, this meta‑analysis reinforces that the reconstituting technique significantly reduces bile leak compared with the fenestrating technique, a finding that persists even with more homogeneous inclusion criteria than previously applied. While our study does not replace larger meta‑analyses, it provides complementary, methodologically transparent evidence supporting the reconstituting technique as a preferred bailout strategy in selected patients.
