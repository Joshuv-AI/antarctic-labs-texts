Male partner involvement in ANC to increase institutional delivery and postnatal care utilization in developing countries: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fitiwi Tinsae Baykemagn, Feseha Berhane, Gebremedhin Gebrezgiabher, Gebremedhin Berhe Gebregergs, Hagos Aregay, Micheale Hagos Debesay, Yisak Gebregziabher, Hadush Asmelash, Tadele Hagos, Dawit Tezera, Desta Hadgu, Gebretsadik Berhe, Gebreamlak Gidey, Abadi Kidanemariam, Yemane Berhane Tesfau, Nicky Milner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMale partner involvement in antenatal care (ANC) is defined as a woman’s husband actively participating in prenatal care-related activities and processes during ANC visit. Lack of male partner involvement affects preparation for health facility delivery and postnatal care utilization; this leads to increased maternal mortality. This association is inconsistent from study to study. However, there is limited evidence about the pooled (quantitative) impact of male partner involvement in ANC. This study aimed to assess the relationship between male partner involvement in ANC and both institutional delivery and postnatal care utilization.MethodsThe data were searched from different electronic databases like PubMed/Medline, Embase, POPLINE, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar. We included observational studies and randomized trials that reported male partner involvement in ANC and its association with institutional delivery and postnatal care utilization. Qualitative studies, systematic reviews, case reports, and studies done in developed countries were excluded. The study populations were women who had given birth and their male partners. Two authors independently assessed the quality appraisal using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist. If there were disputes between them, they were solved by the third author. Data were extracted by two independent authors using a pre-prepared Excel spreadsheet and finally analyzed with Stata 14 and Review Manager 5.3. We used a random-effect model to show the pooled result. Subgroup analysis and publication bias were also performed, using a funnel plot and Begg’s test.ResultsA total of 2206 articles were retrieved from data base, and nineteen articles were included in the meta-analysis. The pooled result showed that there is a positive relationship between husband involvement in ANC and both institutional delivery and postnatal care utilization [OR: 2.2, CI: 95% 1.73–2.80, I²=94% at P < 0.001] and [OR: 2.81, CI: 95% 1.56–5.07, I²=91% at P < 0.001], respectively. Based on the Egger’s test, publication bias was non-significant (P = 0.62). In the subgroup analysis using the categories of study designs, countries’ income level, and study sample size, the level of heterogeneities was 95%, 91%, and 97%, respectively.ConclusionHusband involvement during ANC has a positive effect on institutional delivery and postnatal care utilization. In developing countries, governments and stakeholders need to exert efforts on the male partner involvement approach of ANC utilization. Background

Antenatal care (ANC) is care provided by skilled healthcare professionals to pregnant women to ensure the best health conditions for both mother and fetus during pregnancy [1]. For the purpose of this study, male partner involvement in ANC is operationally defined as the husband accompanying his wife to a health institution for at least one ANC visit during the most recent pregnancy. Approximately 303,000 women died worldwide in 2015 with complications of pregnancy and childbirth [2, 3]; of those 99% of deaths occurred in developing countries [4, 5]. These countries face unique barriers, including socio-cultural factors, limited access to skilled care, and inadequate infrastructure that influence health-seeking behaviors [6]. For instance, only 50% of women in low-income countries delivered in a health facility, compared to over 90% in high-income countries [7, 8]. Fast decision-making and proper care during childbirth could have controlled around 90% of maternal mortality [9]. In developing countries, institutional delivery could reduce 29% of neonatal mortality and also have the potential to reduce maternal morbidity and mortality [10, 11]. Consequently, as institutional delivery increases, stillbirth and perinatal mortality decrease [12].

The use of postnatal care (PNC), which is essential for the health of both mothers and newborns, is very low in developing countries. PNC coverage is almost universal in developed countries, where 94% of women receive care from qualified medical professionals. On the other hand, PNC utilization is low in developing nations, especially in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where rates as low as 36% are used [13]. In developing countries, male partner involvement in ANC is significantly associated with maternal service utilization, including PNC, particularly in developing countries where men often control household decisions [14].

Male partner involvement in ANC is increasingly recognized as a strategy to address these disparities, as it encourages participatory decision-making and enhances access to maternal health services [15]. Pregnant women and their husbands participating in birth preparedness and complication readiness plans are one of the strategies to decrease maternal mortality related to childbirth complications [16]. Thus, it is used to avoid medical-seeking delays [16–18] and increase institutional deliveries as well as PNC service utilization [14]. Encouraging male partners to attend ANC is noted as an important entry point for attracting men to support women’s health from pregnancy throughout the postpartum period [14, 19, 20]. During ANC, it increases a couple’s communication on birth preparedness and postnatal care utilization [21, 22]. It also connects men to the health care system by increasing their knowledge of reproductive health information, including prenatal and postnatal care practices [23, 24]. Male partners normally serve as caretakers of their wives and determine the place of delivery, but until now they are rarely encouraged to be involved in ANC visits [25].

Husband engagement in ANC is related to increasing women’s knowledge and ability to identify maternal and neonatal danger signs [26], supporting reducing the woman’s work burden, and making financial and transportation arrangements for delivery [27, 28] to improve maternal and newborn outcomes [29]. Despite these benefits, the literature indicates inconsistent findings. Such as men accompanying their wives to ANC, which affects women’s autonomy through poor medical decision-making power [30, 31], while other literature reports male involvement in ANC has no effect on maternal health services utilization, including health facility delivery and PNC services utilization [32].

In light of these inconsistent results, we conducted this meta-analysis study. And it will provide a clue for researchers, programmers, and policymakers in developing countries. This study aims to assess the relationship between male partner involvement in ANC and both institutional delivery and postpartum care utilization in developing countries.

Methods

Protocol and registration

In this study, we used the preferred reporting item for systematic review and meta-analysis (PRISMA) checklist protocol [33] (See Supplementary File 1) and it was registered in the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO) and its registration number is CRD42019147770.

Information sources and search strategy

The literature search was initially conducted by the principal investigator (FTB), and then author (YB) also searched independently using the same search terms. We used different database sources like PubMed/Medline, Embase, ScienceDirect, Popline, and Google scholar for retrieving literature on April 21, 2019 and also articles search was updated on December 12, 2024. We also retrieved other relevant studies from Google Scholar and direct Google for additional not found in those database sources. We have used a limited publication period from 2009 to 2024 to maintain the updated information and data management efficiency.

We used Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) the following searching key terms in the mentioned database: (Husbands involvement OR Male partner involvement OR Spouse involvement OR Husbands accompany) AND (prenatal care OR antenatal care OR ANC) AND (institutional delivery OR facility delivery OR postnatal care OR postpartum care OR PNC). The outputs of the search were exported to EndNote software X8 and duplication was removed with it.

The study selection process, such as screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts, was performed independently by two authors. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion; if unresolved, a third reviewer was consulted.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We used a PICO/PECO (population, intervention/exposure, comparison, and outcomes) framework to determine the eligibility criteria of the studies (See Table 1).

We included observational studies (cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort study design) and trial studies designs in this review in order to gather a wide variety of data pertinent to the research question. The exclusion criteria were articles difficult to access the full text, not written in the English language, studies that didn’t report specific exposure and outcomes result, qualitative studies, and studies didn’t conduct in developing countries, as defined by the World Bank [34].

Data extraction and data collection

Data were extracted by authors (FT, YB, GBG, GG, SD, and GG) using an Excel spreadsheet independently. The data extraction format contains the characters of the primary author, publication year, country of study, study participant, sample size, response rate, the total number of women who delivered in a health facility and postnatal care used by the mother whose husband accompanied to a health institution for at least one visit during the most recent pregnancy time to receive ANC and counterparts, birth preparedness and complication readiness component, health care seeking decision-maker, crude or adjusted ORs with 95% CI, association male involvement and institutional delivery and/or postnatal care practice.

We retrieved a total of 2206 articles relevant to the reviewed topics from data base. After retrieving the studies from different databases export the output to EndNote X8 software and then excluded duplications of articles using EndNote software. And also, we excluded articles with irrelevant title, without full text, not reporting the desired outcome, and qualitative study method. After initial inspection of the article titles and removal of those unrelated and duplicated, 196 articles were screened for full text. Of those 53, articles were selected for further appraisal (see Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram showing databases used for search, screened, eligibility, and included studies

Quality assessment

All studies that met eligibility criteria were examined its quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist (See Supplementary File 2). For each paper was independently assessed by two team members. Disagreements between two evaluators were settled through discussion or by third reviewer. Studies those score half and above (≥ 50%) were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis.

Data synthesis and analysis

After studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria, we described the systematic review based on the outcome categories (institutional delivery and postnatal care (PNC) utilization). Then we carried out statistical analysis using the Review Manager software (Rev Man 5.3) and STATA 14. In the analysis first, we used a fixed-effect analysis model for combining the result data; However, after considerable heterogeneity was detected, a random effect analysis model was employed to estimate the pooled relationship. The forest plot was used to show the overall pooled relationship.

We assessed the heterogeneity and publication bias using I2 statistics and Egger’s test respectively. The heterogeneity (I2) statistical test was declared at 25%, 50%, and 75% as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity respectively [35], and a p-value less than 0.05. We also further examined the studies in sub-group analyses to identify the sources of high levels of statistical heterogeneity. The Egger’s test publication bias was declared statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

After initial critical observation of the article titles and removal of those unrelated and duplicated, 196 articles were screened for full text. Of those 53, articles were selected for further appraisal. This resulted in 27 studies which perform a systematic review, with only 19 having sufficient data for meta-analysis.

In the descriptive part of this systematic review, we included 27 studies [16, 18, 21–25, 27, 31, 35–52] for describing the relationship between husband involvement in ANC and institutional delivery. Five of those studies [18, 39–42] were also used to describe the relationship between husband involvement in ANC and postnatal care service utilization. Finally, for meta-analysis 19 articles were included for evaluating the relationship between male partner involvement during ANC and institutional delivery. And also, three articles were included for evaluating the relationship between husband involvement in ANC and PNC service utilization. The study areas of the selected articles were: six from Ethiopia [22, 38, 41, 43, 48, 50] four from Nepal [18, 28, 52, 53], three from Bangladesh [26, 37, 54]; two each from India [44, 49], Uganda [17, 45], Kenya [36, 42], and Indonesia [46, 51]; and one article each from Malawi [55], Ghana [56], Myanmar [40], Tanzania [32], Afghanistan [47], and Zambia [39].

In terms of study design the selected articles were: twenty-one cross-sectional, four cohorts, and two articles are experimental studies. Study participants of the selected articles were the mother (n = 14), a male partner (n = 8), and both partners (n = 5). The publication years of the included studies were since 2008. The selected articles were seventeen from low-income countries and ten from low-middle income countries (LMIC) (See Supplementary File 3).

The majority of included articles [17, 26, 32, 36, 38, 39, 41, 42, 45, 47–51, 54] were indicated that there was a positive association of male partner involvement in ANC during the last recent wife pregnancy and institutional delivery practice of women as compared with women who visited ANC alone. But in a study [18, 43, 46] husband involvement in ANC indicated that there was no association between male partner involvement in ANC and the institutional delivery use as compared with women who visited ANC alone.

Meta-analysis in husband Involvement during ANC to facility delivery utilization

A total of 19 studies with 47,624 sample sizes were included to estimate the association of male partner involvement in ANC and institutional delivery utilization. The result of the meta-analysis revealed that there was a positive association between mothers who had visited with their male partners during ANC visits and were 2.2 times more likely to utilize institutional delivery (95% CI 1.73, 2.80) as compared to mothers who visited ANC alone. Due to this sizable heterogeneity, a random-effects meta-analysis model was used to quantify the association of male partner involvement in ANC and institutional delivery utilization (Fig. 2).

Publication bias

We estimated the number of unpublished studies with null results that would be required to make meta-analysis findings non-significant in order to evaluate the possible influence of publication bias using the File Drawer Analysis method. File Drawer Analysis yielded a result of -9, indicating the high significance and robustness of this meta-analysis. The value itself, however, is not interpretable; rather, it relies on Egger’s test.

The analysis of publication bias using Egger’s test revealed that the symmetrically distributed studies in the funnel plot graph, as well as the P-value was, none significant at P = 0.616, (95% CI: -7.0, 11.4). Thus, there was no evidence of publication bias (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis

First, we decided on retrieving the kinds of literature that should be published in peer-review journals. The sensitivity test was done in Rem-man software excluding studies one by one. But we didn’t find any sizable change in the result. On the other way, we also tested using the Influence analysis (metaninf) test all the studies included in a meta-analysis, the estimated value was within the confidence interval. That means there was no sensitive effect from one result to the other studies (Fig. 4).

Meta-analysis in male partner involvement during ANC to PNC utilization

In this meta-analysis, three articles [18, 39, 41] were included, and that showed there was a positive association between male partner involvement during ANC and postnatal service utilization. From these included articles, the pooled result of male partner involvement during ANC to PNC utilization was 2.81 times higher than those women who visited ANC alone (95% CI:1.56, 5.07) But the level of heterogeneity in this meta-analysis was high (I2 = 91%) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This study provides evidence on the effect of male partner attendance at ANC with their pregnant partners on institutional delivery and/or postnatal care utilization in developing countries. The findings show that male attendants at ANC with their pregnant partners significantly increased utilization of institutional delivery and postnatal check-up attendance.

The overall pooled effect of male partner accompany in ANC to institutional delivery was OR = 2.05 (95%CI: 1.63, 2.59) as compared with women whose husbands didn’t accompany them to antenatal care. In this study, the level of heterogeneity was high in institutional delivery (I2 = 94%) at a P-value of less than 0.001. The finding of this systematic review and meta-analysis was consistent with other studies focusing on the husband’s attendance at ANC performed to have more impact on women’s utilization of maternal health services, relating to skilled birth attendants. This could be due to male partners supporting shared decision-making and encouraging their wives to attend skilled delivery care, and they arrange transport access during labor [57, 58], and health care providers encourage men to take responsibility [58].

A male partner accompanied to ANC by his wife has an opportunity to discuss maternal health. Comparatively, a high proportion of women who discussed their health with their husbands have made birth preparedness as compared with their counterparts [59]. The male partner who had received facility-based health education/counseling was more likely to offer assistance during labor including taking the woman to a health institution, arranging for someone to take her, and arranging transport to the health institution as compared with not received counseling partner [29].

On the other hand, the included three studies didn’t have an association between male involvement in ANC and institutional delivery; this might be due to, the negative attitude of healthcare providers to husband’s involvement in maternity care unite, which could encourage marginalized maternal care for the duty of women only. The negative attitude of care providers in ANC was an obstacle for men participating in their wives’ utilization of health facility delivery [55]. In another study, health care providers didn’t allow the husband in with his wife due to crowding and lack of privacy in ANC, therefore, discouraging any efforts towards husband participation in skill delivery care [60].

Waiting a long time in health facilities during ANC visits with a lack of waiting room for the husband is also another means to decrease the utilization of institutional delivery. When clients were disrespected by their healthcare providers, they wouldn’t want to return to such health service providers. Dissatisfaction for the first time could demotivate women and their husbands from using a continuum of care [56].

The overall meta-analysis result of male partner involvement in ANC with their wives was 2.81 times more likely to receive PNC service (95% CI: 1.56, 5.07) as compared to women who didn’t accompany their wives during ANC visits. This might be due to, ANC being a crucial entry point into formal health care services, and the effective programs offered through ANC increase the impact and effectiveness of care during childbirth and PNC [61]. Involving husbands in health education programs enhances communication between partners and promotes health-seeking behavior [59].

Male involvement in education and counseling is important to use a maternal continuum of care. Therefore, it is recommended that public health organizations design policies and behavior change strategies that influence the involvement of male partners in ANC to improve institutional delivery and postnatal care service utilization. Health managers should put effort into designing programs that target the wider inclusion of male partners in ANC consultations to support the existing encouraging platform and increase reproductive health care utilization.

Limitations of this study

Our search was limited to articles published in the English language only. Furthermore, due to resource constraints that made it impossible to incorporate papers with inaccessible full texts without contacting the related authors, some pertinent studies might not have been included. To reduce this potential bias, future reviews should focus on contacting authors using different communication methods to minimize this potential bias. The presence of high statistical heterogeneity in sub-group analysis was considered a weakness of this systematic review and meta-analysis. This can weaken the robustness of the review’s conclusion.

Conclusion

Male partner involvement during ANC increases the utilization of institutional delivery and postnatal care by women living in LMIC. The overall heterogeneity of male partner involvement in ANC to increase institutional delivery and postnatal care utilization was high. For researchers, limited RCT has been conducted to examine the effect of male partner involvement during ANC on institutional delivery utilization. For increasing the quality of evidence, additional studies, including RCT, are required in the future. In developing countries, governments and other stakeholders need to exert efforts on the male partner collaborative approach of ANC service utilization to increase institutional delivery and postnatal care service use.
