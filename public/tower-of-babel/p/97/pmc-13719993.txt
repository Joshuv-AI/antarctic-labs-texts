Identification of phillyrin as a PPAR-γ ligand through network pharmacology and molecular modeling
Authors: Ling Huang, Zhizheng Fang, Rongchun Han, Xiaohui Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsulin resistance is a core pathological hallmark of metabolic syndromes like type 2 diabetes mellitus. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) is a classical therapeutic target for improving insulin sensitivity, yet the clinical utility of its synthetic agonists faces diverse adverse reactions. We previously showed that phillyrin, an important component of Forsythia suspensa, could improve insulin resistance in obesity. However, its direct molecular targets remain not fully understood. Herein, we adopted a combined in silico and experimental approach to determine whether phillyrin could function as a ligand of PPAR-γ. We implemented a multi-scale strategy integrating network pharmacology, molecular modeling, with experimental verification. Network pharmacology analysis was employed to predict system-level targets and pathways associated with phillyrin. Subsequently, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using computational chemistry methods to characterize the binding mode, dynamic stability, and binding free energy (MM-GBSA) of phillyrin within the PPAR-γ ligand-binding domain. Finally, key computational predictions were experimentally validated in insulin-resistant 3T3-L1 adipocytes and in a high-fat diet-induced murine model of insulin resistance.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Insulin resistance represents a fundamental pathological hallmark of multiple metabolic disorders, including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), obesity, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease1,2. Despite decades of investigation, insulin resistance remains a highly complex, multifactorial condition involving impaired glucose and lipid handling in key insulin-sensitive tissues like adipose tissue, muscle, and liver. This dysfunction is usually coupled with chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and disrupted signaling cascades3,4. Its multifactorial nature limits the efficacy of single-target therapies and underscores the need for systems-level approaches to decipher the underlying regulatory networks. The conventional approaches typically focus on modulating a single enzyme or receptor, yet they often fail to address the interconnected nature of metabolic inflammation and signaling crosstalk.

To effectively address this complexity in insulin resistance, targeting key nodal regulators within the metabolic network is essential. PPAR-γ is a well-validated central transcriptional regulator of adipogenesis, lipid metabolism, glucose homeostasis, and inflammation5,6. Its impaired activity is a critical event in the development of insulin resistance7. Consequently, synthetic PPAR-γ agonists, notably thiazolidinediones (TZDs), have been widely used as insulin sensitizers in T2DM8. However, the long-term clinical application of these agents is significantly restricted by serious adverse effects, such as weight gain, fluid retention, and elevated cardiovascular risk8,9. These limitations highlight an urgent need for safer PPAR-γ-targeting strategies that retain therapeutic benefits10.

Natural products from medicinal plants represent a promising reservoir for identifying novel PPAR-γ modulators. They are characterized by extensive structural diversity, generally favorable safety profiles, and inherent multi-target potential11. Traditional Chinese medicine has long been used to manage metabolic disorders and serves as a rich repository of phytochemicals with insulin-sensitizing and anti-inflammatory properties12. Among these, phillyrin has garnered increasing attention. This major lignan glycoside, isolated from Forsythia suspensa (Lian Qiao), shows anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and metabolically beneficial effects in preclinical models13. Nevertheless, its precise molecular targets, particularly its potential interaction with the central regulator PPAR-γ, remain unclear. Thus, whether phillyrin could exert its effects by directly modulating PPAR-γ signaling or not constitutes a critical mechanistic gap.

Therefore, we adopted a multidisciplinary strategy integrating network pharmacology, molecular modeling, and experimental evidence. Firstly, a network pharmacology approach was applied to identify potential targets of phillyrin associated with insulin resistance at the systems level. Secondly, molecular docking and dynamics simulations were employed to characterize its binding mode, stability, and energetic profile within the PPAR-γ ligand-binding domain (LBG)14,15. Lastly, these computational predictions were experimentally tested through in vitro and in vivo assays evaluating phillyrin’s effects on PPAR-γ under insulin-resistant conditions16,17. Through this multi-tiered strategy, this study aims to establish whether phillyrin acts as a natural PPAR-γ ligand and to elucidate its mechanistic role in alleviating insulin resistance.

Results

Identification of potential targets of phillyrin against insulin resistance

Following absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME)-based screening using the oral bioavailability (OB ≥ 30%) and drug-likeness (DL ≥0.18) criteria, phillyrin was identified as a candidate bioactive compound with favorable pharmacokinetic properties. Its potential molecular targets were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction, TargetNet, and DrugBank. After normalization and removal of duplicate entries, 580 non-redundant putative targets were obtained. Insulin resistance-associated targets were collected from multiple public databases, including GeneCards, OMIM, and MalaCards. Following data integration and relevance score filtering, 5107 insulin resistance-related targets were retrieved, among which 2553 were classified as highly relevant. Intersection analysis between the predicted phillyrin targets and insulin resistance-associated targets identified 218 overlapping genes. These shared targets were considered candidate targets potentially mediating the effects of phillyrin on insulin resistance. Notably, PPAR-γ was among the overlapping targets (Fig. 1).

PPI network construction and analysis

The STRING database was employed to generate a PPI network for the 218 overlapping targets, applying a stringent confidence filter (combined score ≥0.7). A PPI network was generated, consisting of 218 nodes and 528 edges. The network exhibited a highly significant PPI enrichment p value (<1.0 × 10⁻¹⁶), supporting extensive interactions among the targets. Module detection using the MCODE plugin identified nine densely interconnected functional modules within the PPI network. Module 1 (Score = 22.692, 27 nodes) was the core signaling module, containing AKT1, EGFR, MTOR, SRC, and PIK3CA, mainly associated with insulin signaling and PI3K-AKT pathway. Module 2 (Score = 18.93, 44 nodes) contained PPAR-γ, TNF, MAPK8, JAK1, and other key regulators, which were closely linked to lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and insulin resistance-related pathways. Module 3 (Score = 7.143, 8 nodes) was involved in fatty acid and triglyceride metabolism. Module 4 (Score = 5.704, 28 nodes) participated in cell adhesion, migration, and immune-metabolic crosstalk. Module 5 (Score = 4.286, 8 nodes) was associated with neuroendocrine signal transduction. Module 6 (Score = 4, 4 nodes) was related to ion channel activity and membrane transport. Module 7 (Score = 3.789, 20 nodes) regulated steroid hormone metabolism and inflammation. Module 8 (Score = 3, 3 nodes) participated in steroid hormone biosynthesis. Module 9 (Score = 3, 3 nodes) contributed to glucose and lipid sensing and metabolism (Fig. 2A). Additionally, the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape was applied to screen core hub genes using the maximal clique centrality (MCC) algorithm. Top 8 hub genes were pinpointed, included AKT1, EGFR, TNF, SRC, ESR1, CASP3, PPAR-γ, and BCL2 (Fig. 2B).

GO functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed for the 218 overlapping targets using the Metascape platform. Significant enrichment was observed across all three GO categories: BP, CC, and MF. Within the BP category, the intersecting targets were mainly enriched in processes related to oxidative stress response, lipid metabolic process, glucose homeostasis, hormone response, and regulation of inflammatory responses. Enriched CC terms primarily included membrane rafts, transcriptional regulatory complexes, and nuclear receptor-associated complexes. MF enrichment analysis highlighted nuclear receptor activity, ligand-activated transcription factor activity, and kinase regulator activity (Fig. 3A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified 20 significantly enriched pathways (P < 0.01), including the PPAR signaling pathway, insulin signaling pathway, AMPK signaling pathway, lipid and atherosclerosis pathway, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 3B). Several core genes, including PPAR-γ, AKT1, MAPK1, and TNF, were present across multiple enriched pathways.

Docking of rosiglitazone and phillyrin with PPAR-γ

Re-docking of rosiglitazone (PDB ID: 5YCP) into its binding pocket yielded a near-perfect superimposition with the original crystallographic conformation (Supplementary Fig. 1). The RMSD value between the redocked rosiglitazone and the protein-prepared crystallographic rosiglitazone was 0.2574 Å. This docking result showed the high stability of the native ligand–protein interaction. The docking score of rosiglitazone was −10.34 kcal/mol. In Fig. 4A, B, thiazolidinedione of rosiglitazone formed hydrogen bonds with Ser289, protonated His323, protonated His449 and Tyr473, respectively. His323, His449, and Tyr473 were considered as a conserved feature for PPAR-γ agonist recognition18. Rosiglitazone establishes aromatic hydrogen bonding interactions with Phe282 and Ser289. Residues like Phe282, Leu330, and Met348 (hydrophobic, labeled in green) pack against the rosiglitazone’s nonpolar aromatic/aliphatic regions, boosting binding affinity via van der Waals forces.

A Protein-prepared crystallographic rosiglitazone (green) in the LBD of PPAR-γ (PDB ID: 5YCP). C Binding pose of phillyrin (white). B, D Two-dimensional interaction diagrams of rosiglitazone (B) and phillyrin (D). Hydrogen bonds are shown as pink dashed lines.

As for phillyrin, the docking score was -15.59 kcal/mol. In Fig. 4C, D, hydroxyl groups of phillyrin formed hydrogen bonds with Phe282, Ser289, and Tyr473. Protonated His449 formed an aromatic hydrogen bond with phillyrin. Other hydrophobic residues like Phe282, Ile326, Tyr327, Leu330, Met348, and Leu353 packed against the phillyrin’s nonpolar regions. During protein preparation, all water molecules beyond 2 Å from the ligand were removed except HOH642. These water-mediated hydrogen bonds occurred between Ser342 and the heterocyclic ring of phillyrin. These docking results suggested phillyrin was a potential PPAR-γ ligand.

MD simulations of rosiglitazone and phillyrin

MD simulations of the rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ and phillyrin-PPAR-γ complexes were independently performed three times for each ligand. Representative trajectories are presented in the main text, while the remaining simulations are provided in supplementary information (Supplementary Figs. 2–7 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). RMSD analysis was conducted to evaluate the conformational stability of the protein backbone and ligand during the 100 ns simulations, whereas RMSF analysis was used to assess residue-level flexibility.

For the rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ complex, the system RMSD gradually converged and reached a stable range after ~12 ns (Fig. 5A). The RMSD values of the protein backbone fluctuated between 2.0 and 2.8 Å, while the ligand RMSD relative to its initial conformation ranged from 0.3 to 0.8 Å. RMSF analysis revealed relatively higher fluctuations in several loop regions, including residues 237–248, 259–277, 393–404, and 425–431, with the highest RMSF value observed for Lys240 (3.74 Å). RMSF values for other residues in contact with rosiglitazone were below 3.3 Å (Fig. 5B).

For the phillyrin-PPAR-γ complex, RMSD stabilization was observed after ~20 ns (Fig. 5C). The RMSD values of the protein backbone ranged from 2.0 to 2.7 Å, whereas the ligand RMSD ranged from 0.7 to 1.4 Å. RMSF analysis indicated elevated flexibility in loop regions spanning residues 259–277, 331–338, 425–431, and 457–470, with Asp462 exhibiting the highest RMSF value (3.09 Å). RMSF values of other residues interacting with phillyrin remained below 2.2 Å (Fig. 5D).

MD simulations were also performed for the phillyrin-PPAR-γ and rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ complexes using GROMACS. Analysis of RMSD and RMSF profiles indicated that the protein backbone and ligand conformations were stable in both complexes (Supplementary Figs. 8A, B and 9A, B). We further evaluated the Rg. For the rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ complex (Supplementary Fig. 8C), the Rg fluctuated between 1.93 and 1.99 nm, exhibiting a slight initial decrease and become almost constant from 60 ns. For the phillyrin-PPAR-γ complex (Supplementary Fig. 9C), the Rg fluctuated between 1.93 and 1.98 nm. Similar to the rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ complex, it increased gradually starting at 60 ns and plateaued for the rest of the simulation. These observations confirm that both complexes maintain favorable structural stability, which is consistent with our previous MD simulation results.

The SASA was analyzed for the phillyrin-PPAR-γ and rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ complexes. For the rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ complex (Supplementary Fig. 8D), SASA fluctuated between 138 and 154 nm2, with minor fluctuations observed throughout the simulation. For the phillyrin-PPAR-γ complex (Supplementary Fig. 9D), SASA ranged from 136 to 150 nm2 and exhibited smaller fluctuations than the rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ complex. These stable SASA profiles confirmed that the protein maintained a consistent solvent-exposed surface area.

Water-mediated interactions were mainly observed between the pyridine moiety of rosiglitazone and Ser342. Additional hydrophobic interactions were identified at residues Phe264, Ile326, Leu330, Ile341, and Met364 surrounding the ligand.

Binding free energy analysis

MM-GBSA calculations were performed to estimate the binding free energies of the phillyrin-PPAR-γ and rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ complexes. As summarized in Table 1, both ligands exhibited negative binding free energy values, indicating favorable interactions with PPAR-γ. For rosiglitazone, the docking binding free energy was −76.78 kcal/mol, and the average MM-GBSA binding free energy calculated over the MD trajectory was −71.46 kcal/mol. Binding free energy values derived from trajectory snapshots extracted at 10 ns intervals are listed in Table 2. A decrease in binding free energy magnitude was observed after 60 ns along the simulation trajectory. For phillyrin, the docking binding free energy was −94.92 kcal/mol, and the average MM-GBSA binding free energy across the MD trajectory was −97.15 kcal/mol. Temporal variations in binding free energy were observed throughout the simulation (Table 2), which may reflect dynamic adjustments at the binding site. Notably, the more negative average binding energy of phillyrin suggests a potentially stronger thermodynamic affinity for PPAR-γ compared to rosiglitazone.

Regulation of PPAR-γ expression by phillyrin in vivo and in vitro

Western blot analysis was conducted on epididymal adipose tissue of HFD-fed mice to evaluate phillyrin’s effect on PPAR-γ expression under insulin resistance. As shown in Fig. 7A, PPAR-γ protein expression was markedly reduced in HFD mice compared with NC mice. Treatment with phillyrin significantly increased PPAR-γ protein level in HFD mice. Similarly, administration of rosiglitazone resulted in an increase in PPAR-γ protein expression. Notably, combined treatment with phillyrin and rosiglitazone produced a greater increase in PPAR-γ protein levels than either treatment alone.

Consistent with the in vivo results, qRT-PCR analysis revealed significant alterations in Pparg mRNA expression in differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocytes under different treatment conditions. Compared with control cells, insulin-resistant conditions reduced Pparg mRNA levels in adipocytes. Treatment with phillyrin significantly increased Pparg mRNA expression relative to the model group. Discussion

In this study, we employed an integrated computational and experimental framework to investigate the potential of phillyrin, a lignan glycoside from Forsythia suspensa, as a modulator of PPAR-γ in the context of insulin resistance. Our findings demonstrate that while phillyrin exhibits robust thermodynamic favorability and a clean safety profile, its pharmacological efficacy is constrained by physicochemical limitations inherent to its natural product structure. This duality provides a nuanced understanding of how phillyrin interacts with the PPAR-γ receptor and why it behaves differently from classical synthetic agonists like rosiglitazone.

Our molecular docking and MD simulations confirm that phillyrin stably occupies the PPAR-γ LBD. The MM-GBSA and MM-PBSA calculations revealed a more negative binding free energy for phillyrin compared to rosiglitazone, suggesting a strong theoretical affinity driven by extensive hydrogen bonding and water-mediated interactions with residues such as Ser289, His449, and Tyr473. Notably, the retention of the crystallographic water molecule (HOH642) during our preparation protocol proved critical, as it bridged the interaction between the glycosidic moiety of phillyrin and Ser342. This highlights the importance of precise methodological transparency in computational biology, as emphasized during the revision process. The stable RMSD and Rg profiles further confirmed that phillyrin binding does not destabilize the AF-2 helix, a prerequisite for receptor activation.

Despite these favorable in silico metrics, the experimental data present a more tempered view of phillyrin’s efficacy. While phillyrin significantly upregulated PPAR-γ expression in HFD-fed mice and 3T3-L1 adipocytes, the magnitude of this effect did not vastly exceed that of rosiglitazone, despite the seemingly superior docking scores. We attribute this discrepancy to a fundamental pharmacokinetic barrier rooted in the compound’s physicochemical properties. As detailed in our ADME analysis (Table 4), phillyrin violates Lipinski’s rule of five with respect to MW (>500 g/mol) and HBA (>10). The presence of the bulky, polar glucose moiety results in a high TPSA and a logP value indicative of poor lipophilicity. Consequently, the causal relationship is clear: the same structural features that enable high-affinity hydrogen bonding within the polar PPAR-γ binding pocket simultaneously impede passive diffusion across the hydrophobic lipid bilayer of the cell membrane. This creates a kinetic bottleneck; less phillyrin likely reaches the intracellular PPAR-γ target compared to the highly lipophilic rosiglitazone.

Conversely, this very limitation in membrane permeability appears to be the cornerstone of phillyrin’s favorable safety profile. The ADME predictions indicate that phillyrin is not a substrate or inhibitor of major CYP450 isoforms (CYP2C9, CYP3A4, etc.), unlike rosiglitazone, which is extensively metabolized by these enzymes and is associated with drug-drug interactions and hepatotoxicity concerns. Furthermore, the lower intracellular exposure likely prevents the supraphysiological activation of PPAR-γ that drives the adverse effects of TZDs (weight gain, fluid retention, bone loss). Therefore, phillyrin may function as a gentle or partial agonist, normalizing deficient PPAR-γ signaling in insulin-resistant tissues without triggering the exaggerated transcriptional programs responsible for toxicity. This trade-off, moderate efficacy in exchange for enhanced safety, is a recurring theme in natural product pharmacology and represents a strategic advantage for long-term metabolic management.

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged to ensure scientific rigor and guide future investigation. (1) Membrane permeability uncertainty: while we logically infer that poor permeability limits efficacy, we did not directly measure intracellular concentrations of phillyrin using LC-MS. It remains possible that active transport mechanisms partially compensate for poor passive diffusion. Future studies employing cellular thermal shift assays or fluorescently labeled phillyrin are required to quantify precise target engagement in cellulo. (2) Bioavailability and metabolism: the low predicted oral bioavailability raises the question of whether the in vivo effects observed in mice are mediated by the parent compound phillyrin or by its deglycosylated metabolite (phillygenin) produced by the gut microbiota. The current study design cannot distinguish between systemic phillyrin activity and local gut-mediated effects.

To conclude, this study successfully identified phillyrin as a natural PPAR-γ ligand through a rigorous integration of network pharmacology, molecular modeling, and experimental validation. We have established that while the intrinsic polarity of phillyrin’s glycosidic structure imposes a limitation on cellular entry and peak efficacy, it simultaneously confers a distinct safety advantage by avoiding CYP450 interactions and minimizing over-activation toxicity. Our findings refine the understanding of phillyrin’s mechanism of action, positioning it not as a potent TZD replacement, but as a safer, moderate modulator suitable for adjuvant therapy or functional food development in the management of insulin resistance.

Methods

In silico characterization and target identification

Phillyrin, a lignan glycoside derived from Forsythia suspensa, was selected for this study. Its pharmacokinetic profile was obtained from multiple Traditional Chinese Medicine databases, including the TCMSP and HERB 2.0. To evaluate the drug-like properties, key ADME parameters were analyzed. Phillyrin met the standard screening criteria for oral bioavailability (OB ≥ 30%) and drug-likeness (DL ≥0.18). The chemical structure and SMILES string of phillyrin were obtained from PubChem, and the SMILES notation was subsequently submitted to SwissTargetPrediction, TargetNet, and DrugBank for target prediction. All predicted targets were then standardized to official gene symbols using UniProt and deduplicated. In parallel, targets associated with insulin resistance were systematically compiled from multiple public databases, including GeneCards, OMIM, MalaCards, using the keyword “insulin resistance”. Retrieved gene entries were standardized and deduplicated, yielding a non-redundant set of 5107 unique targets. This disease target set was then intersected with the predicted targets of phillyrin, resulting in 218 overlapping genes. These shared targets were considered potential key mediators and formed the foundation for subsequent network construction and enrichment analysis.

Construction and analysis of the protein–protein interaction (PPI) Network

To elucidate the systemic relationships among phillyrin’s candidate targets in insulin resistance, a PPI network was constructed using the 218 shared targets. Interactions were retrieved from the STRING database, restricted to Homo sapiens with a high-confidence threshold (combined score ≥0.7)19. The network was visualized and analyzed in Cytoscape (v3.10.4)20. Topological properties (degree, betweenness, and closeness centrality) were calculated using the NetworkAnalyzer plugin21. Core hub genes were identified via the CytoHubba plugin employing the maximal clique centrality (MCC) algorithm, with the top ten nodes selected22. Densely interconnected modules were detected using the MCODE plugin (settings: node score cutoff = 0.2, K-core = 2, max depth = 100)23. The hub genes and key module members were subsequently subjected to functional enrichment analysis.

Functional enrichment analysis

To characterize the biological functions and pathways linked to the 218 overlapping targets, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed. GO analysis covered three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). KEGG pathway analysis was conducted in parallel to identify signaling cascades significantly linked to insulin resistance. All analyses were carried out using the Metascape platform. GO terms and KEGG pathways with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and strong enrichment scores were considered statistically significant and selected for further biological interpretation.

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of PPAR-γ in complex with rosiglitazone (PDB ID: 5YCP) was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB)24, and a resolution of 2.00 Å was utilized in this study. The high-resolution crystal structure was used to provide precise spatial information, which was crucial for molecular docking and dynamics simulations. The selected structure was prepared using the protein preparation wizard on Maestro of the Schrödinger suite (Release 2021-2), modeling suite25. Briefly, the protein structure was prepared by adding hydrogen atoms and optimizing the hydrogen bonding network. Water molecules more than 2 Å away from the ligand were removed from the crystal structure, and only one water molecule HOH642 was retained in the final structure. The optimized potentials for liquid simulations 4 (OPLS4) force field was used for energy minimization of the crystal structure26. The 3D structures of phillyrin and rosiglitazone were generated using the LigPrep module in Maestro and subsequently optimized and minimized using the OPLS4 force field. Possible ionization states were generated at a pH of 7.0 ± 2.0. The parameter of “determine chiralities from 3D structure” was applied to ligands. The cognate ligand position in the crystal structure was selected as the binding site. The grid box was centered on the rosiglitazone binding site in the crystal structure, with XYZ coordinates: 22.56, −22.74, and −1.55.

For docking, the induced-fit docking (IFD) method in the Schrödinger suite was applied to this study27–29. An initial soft-potential docking of the ligands was performed on the rigid receptor with van der Waal radii scaling of 0.5. Residues within 5 Å of the ligand were optimized, which mainly included the energy minimization of the side chains and the ligand itself. Complexes within 30.0 kcal/mol of the minimum energy structure were redocked with van der Waals radii scaling of 1.0 for the receptor and 0.8 for the ligand. The Glide extra precision (XP) setting was selected to enhance accuracy and identify the most promising ligand30. For further evaluation, the ligands were selected based on the docking score, binding energy, and analysis of the binding pose of the ligand–protein complex.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the Desmond module within the Schrödinger suite (Release 2021-2) to assess the stability and dynamic behavior of the phillyrin-PPAR-γ complex and the rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ complex31. The initial structure was derived from the molecular docking pose with the highest score. The system was prepared by solvating the complex in an orthorhombic TIP4P (Transferable Intermolecular Potential with 4 Points) water box with a 10 Å buffer distance27, adding counterions to neutralize the charge, and applying the OPLS4 force field. Energy minimization was conducted using the steepest descent method. Subsequently, a 100 ns production MD simulation was performed in the NPT ensemble at 310 K and 1.01325 bar, with a time step of 2 fs. Trajectory frames were saved every 100 ps. To ensure robustness, three independent replicates were conducted using different random seeds. Post-simulation analyses, including calculations of the root mean square deviation (RMSD) and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), as well as protein–ligand interaction profiling, were executed with the built-in tools in Desmond and visualized in Maestro.

For the analysis of the radius of gyration (Rg) and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), MD simulations were performed on the phillyrin-PPAR-γ and rosiglitazone-PPAR-γ complex using GROMACS 2025.432. The Amber99SB-ILDN force field33 was employed to describe the protein, while the small molecule was parameterized using the general AMBER force field 2 (GAFF2)34,35. Ligand topological parameters were generated with ACPYPE36, and atomic partial charges were assigned using the AM1-BCC scheme37 as implemented in AmberTools38. The protein–ligand complex was solvated in a truncated dodecahedral box containing TIP3P water molecules39, with a minimum solute-box edge distance of 1.0 nm. To ensure overall charge neutrality and to reproduce physiological ionic strength, NaCl was added to a final concentration of 0.15 mol/L.

The solvated system was first subjected to energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm until the maximum force converged to below 100 kJ/mol/nm, with a maximum of 50,000 steps. This was followed by sequential equilibration under the NVT ensemble for 50 ps and the NPT ensemble for 100 ps. During equilibration, the temperature was maintained at 298.15 K using the V-rescale thermostat40, while the pressure was maintained at 1 bar using the C-rescale barostat41. For nonbonded interactions, a cutoff of 10 Å was applied to van der Waals interactions, and long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method42,43. All covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm44, allowing an integration time step of 2 fs. Following equilibration, a 100 ns MD simulation was performed, and trajectory coordinates were recorded every 10 ps. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three spatial dimensions throughout the simulation.

Binding free energy calculation

Calculation of the protein–ligand binding free energies was conducted via the MM/GBSA assay in Schrödinger’s Prime module (2021-2)45,46, with the results serving to quantify ligand binding affinities47,48. In this work, G_complex, G_receptor, and G_ligand represent the Gibbs free energies of the ligand–protein complex, the target protein alone, and the unbound ligand, respectively. Accordingly, the binding free energy (ΔG_bind) for both docked poses and simulation trajectories was derived from the equation: ΔG_bind = G_complex − G_receptor − G_ligand. The OPLS4 force field and VSGB solvation model were employed in the calculations49. As for MD simulation, the snapshots every 10 ns were extracted to calculate the binding free energy.

Molecular mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) analysis was conducted to quantitatively evaluate the energetic contributions to receptor-ligand interactions and to identify residues potentially critical for binding. MM-PBSA calculations were performed using the “g_mmpbsa” toolkit50 based on the 100 ns molecular dynamics trajectory. To investigate the binding characteristics at different simulation stages, representative snapshots were extracted at 10 ns intervals from 0 to 100 ns, resulting in a total of 11 structures. Each snapshot was analyzed independently to estimate the binding energy at the corresponding stage of the simulation. Before analysis, water molecules and ions were removed, and periodic boundary conditions were corrected to preserve the structural integrity of the receptor-ligand complex. Trajectory preprocessing was performed using the “gmx trjconv” utility in GROMACS32. As entropy calculations are not supported by g_mmpbsa, the reported values represent binding energies rather than absolute binding free energies.

In principle, the binding free energy (ΔG_bind) can be expressed as the sum of the molecular mechanics energy contribution (ΔE_MM), the solvation free energy contribution (ΔG_sol), and the entropic term (-TΔS), as shown in Equation (1):

ΔG_bind = ΔE_MM + ΔG_sol − TΔS = ΔE_vdW + ΔE_elec + ΔG_polar + ΔG_nonpolar − TΔS. (1).

Here, ΔE_MM includes the van der Waals (ΔE_vdW) and electrostatic (ΔE_elec) interaction energies, whereas ΔG_sol comprises the polar (ΔG_polar) and nonpolar (ΔG_nonpolar) solvation contributions. In the present study, the entropic term was not included because the “g_mmpbsa” toolkit50 does not currently support entropy calculations. Therefore, the resulting values are more appropriately referred to as binding energies (ΔE_bind) rather than rigorous binding free energies. The polar solvation energy was calculated using the integrated APBS package51, whereas the nonpolar solvation energy was estimated based on the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) model.

Drug‑likeness predictions

ADME profiling, which refers to the assessment of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, was employed to study the disposition of the potential compound within an organism. In this study, SwissADME online servers (http://www.swissadme.ch/) was selected to check the pharmacokinetic properties and drug-likeness of phillyrin and rosiglitazone52. Key pharmacokinetic parameters were evaluated, including gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeation, P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrate potential, cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme inhibition, and skin permeability (Log Kp). Compliance with Lipinski’s rule of five was also assessed based on molecular weight, calculated octanol-water partition coefficient (Log P), count of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, and count of rotatable bonds.

Animal study

Male C57BL/6J mice, around 8 weeks old, were used in this study were obtained from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Mice were housed in a pathogen-free facility, under standard housing conditions (five animals per cage, temperature 22–26 °C, relative humidity 45–55%, 12 h light/12 h dark cycle). Based on previous reports demonstrating insulin-sensitizing effects of rosiglitazone in diet-induced obese mouse models53,54, and our prior findings showing that phillyrin at 25 mg/kg significantly improved insulin sensitivity in obese mice55, appropriate doses were selected for the present study. After a week of acclimatization, mice were randomly allocated (6 mice per group) into five experimental groups: normal control (NC), high-fat diet (HFD), phillyrin (phil, 25 mg/kg), rosiglitazone (rosi, 0.6 mg/kg), and the combined phillyrin + rosiglitazone (phil + rosi, 25 mg/kg + 0.6 mg/kg) group, with 6 mice per group. The NC group was assigned to a standard chow diet. The HFD, phillyrin, rosiglitazone, and combination groups were all fed a 60% high-fat diet (Product No. 241126F, Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd.). Water was provided ad libitum to all groups. Phillyrin and rosiglitazone maleate were suspended in 1% carboxymethyl cellulose sodium (CMC-Na) solution and administered orally once daily. Control groups received an equivalent volume of vehicle. Drug administration was continued for 12 weeks. Upon completion of the treatment regimen, animals were deeply anesthetized using an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg) and subsequently sacrificed. For subsequent molecular and biochemical analyses, epididymal white adipose tissue samples were rapidly excised, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and maintained at −80 °C. All animal use followed the ARRIVE guidelines and the UK Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986 and associated guidelines, EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments. All animal use was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine (AHUCM-mouse-2025001).

Western blot analysis

Epididymal white adipose tissue samples were homogenized on ice in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Total protein concentrations were quantified using a standard protein quantification assay. We separated equal protein loads by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) using 15% polyacrylamide gels. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes employing a wet transfer system. After transfer, membranes were subjected to blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk prepared in TBST (45 min, 25 °C). This was followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies, which were diluted in TBST containing 3% BSA. Following primary antibody incubation, the membranes were rinsed three times with TBST. Subsequently, they were probed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 40 min at 25 °C. Immunoreactive protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent and captured using an appropriate imaging system. Primary antibodies consisted of anti-PPAR-γ and anti-β-actin.

3T3-L1 cell culture, differentiation, and treatment

3T3-L1 preadipocytes (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC; Cat. No. CRL-3242) were cultured under standard conditions. Cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Routine monitoring of cell growth and morphology was performed using an inverted microscope. At approximately 90% confluence, cells were switched to an adipogenic induction cocktail. This induction medium I consisted of high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1.5 μg/mL insulin, 1 μM dexamethasone, and 500 μM IBMX, and was applied for 3 days. Following this initial induction, cells were maintained in medium II (high-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS, 1% antibiotics, and 1.5 μg/mL insulin), which was refreshed every 48 h. Following 6 days of differentiation, mature adipocytes were identified by their characteristic rounded morphology and abundant intracellular lipid droplets. Fully differentiated adipocytes were then randomly allocated to four treatment groups: normal control (vehicle), phillyrin (1 μM), rosiglitazone (100 μM), and combined treatment with phillyrin (1 μM) and rosiglitazone (100 μM).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

We extracted total RNA from treated 3T3-L1 adipocytes. This was carried out with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To synthesize first-strand complementary DNA (cDNA), total RNA (1 μg) was first treated to eliminate residual genomic DNA, followed by reverse transcription using the SPARKscript II RT Plus Kit with gDNA Eraser (Vazyme, China). SYBR Green-based quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) and the corresponding Master Mix (Vazyme, China). The mRNA expression level of Pparg (encoding PPAR-γ) was quantified using a validated primer pair (Shanghai Shenggong Biological Engineering Co., Ltd.): forward 5′-GGAAGACCACTCGCATTCCT-3′ and reverse 5′-GGCAGCAGTGTGGTATGGAC-3′. All qRT-PCR reactions were performed in triplicate with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) as the internal reference. Relative expression levels were determined via the 2⁻ΔΔCt method and are expressed as fold changes compared to the vehicle-treated control group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons among multiple groups were made by one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. All analyses were carried out in GraphPad Prism 8.0, with quantitative results expressed as mean ± SEM. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance is denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.
