Profile of GAS6 gene single nucleotide polymorphisms [GAS6 c.834+7G>A (rs8191974), and GAS6 c.1332C>T (rs1803628)] among a cohort of Egyptian women with preeclampsia: Relation to disease risk and severity
Authors: Mostafa Mohamed Khodry, Sawsan M.A. Abuhamdah, Mohammed H. Hassan, Abd El-Naser Abd El-Gaber Ali, Asmaa Ahmed Sayed Khames, Mohammad A.M. Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study represents the investigation of the potential link between single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the growth arrest-specific 6 (GAS6) gene and preeclampsia (PE) in Egyptian women. Few existing research have explored this association in other populations, prompting this investigation into the relationship between variants of GAS6 SNPs and both the probability and severity of PE. 200 pregnant Egyptian women participated in the study; 100 were normotensive, 50 had mild PE, and 50 had severe PE. RFLP-PCR was used to genotype GAS6 SNPs [NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A (rs8191974) and NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T (rs1803628)] in patients and controls (mean ages 30.33 ± 7.17 and 27.43 ± 5.14 years, respectively). Key findings included a significantly higher frequency of AA genotype (29 % vs. 7 %; P < 0.001) and A allele (56 % vs. 30 %; P < 0.001, OR = 0.337, 95 % CI = 0.223–0.508) of NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A in PE patients compared to controls. In contrast, the CC genotype (27 % vs. 4 %; P < 0.001) and wild C allele (59.5 % vs. 49.5 %; P = 0.045, OR = 1.499, 95 % CI = 1.009–2.226) of NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T were also significantly more frequent in PE patients. Within PE cases, severe cases showed a higher frequency of the CC genotype of NM_000820.3:c.1332C > T (38 % vs. 16 % in mild cases; P = 0.009) and C allele (68 % vs. 51 %; P = 0.014, OR = 2.042, 95 % CI = 1.149–3.627). These results indicate an association between PE risk and the A allele and AA genotype of NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A variants. Within this cohort of Egyptian women, PE development and severity were significantly linked to the presence of the CC genotype and C allele of the NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T variants.

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE), a multisystem disease associated with pregnancy that has an uncertain etiology, is frequently observed after gestational diabetes mellitus and is typically linked to placental insufficiency1. Intravascular inflammation, syncytiotrophoblast stress, and endothelial cell activation are some of its shared pathophysiological characteristics. Proteinuria and hypertension have historically been necessary for diagnosis; however, new guidelines suggest that if multisystem involvement is apparent, a diagnosis can be made even in the absence of proteinuria2. Circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines rise and activate endothelial cells in PE, resulting in an overabundance of systemic inflammation3.

By interacting with the tyrosine kinases of the TAM receptor family (Tyro-3/Axl/Mer), growth arrest-specific 6 (GAS6), a vitamin K-dependent protein, mediates several biological processes, such as inflammation and reversible growth arrest. According to recent reviews, GAS6′s pro-atherogenic and anti-angiogenic characteristics, as well as its function in platelet activation and thrombus stabilization, may make it a unique atherothrombotic risk factor[4], [5], [6]. One or more functionally linked genes may have clustered single nucleotide polymorphisms, which could be the cause of PE7. Among these is the gene for GAS6, which is located on chromosome 13q348.

Although the exact pathogenesis of PE is still unknown, this study presents the first analysis of the relationship between GAS6 gene polymorphisms—specifically (NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A (rs8191974) and NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T (rs1803628)—and the risk and severity of PE within an Egyptian cohort.

Patients and Methods

Methodology and participant Selection

This case-control study, in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Qena University Hospitals, South Valley University, Egypt, included 200 pregnant women. Participants were stratified into two groups: 100 women with preeclampsia (PE) (50 with mild and 50 with severe PE) and 100 healthy pregnant controls, randomly selected. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, South Valley University, Egypt (Ethical approval code: SVU-MED-OBG024-4–25-1–7), and the study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

The diagnostic criteria for preeclampsia (PE) comprised the presence of both Elevated blood pressure, defined as a systolic measurement exceeding 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic measurement exceeding 90 mmHg, recorded on two separate occasions at least six hours apart after 20 weeks of gestation and before the commencement of labor and proteinuria, defined as > 0.3 g/24 h or > 2 + by dipstick urinalysis. Severe PE was diagnosed when systolic blood pressure exceeded 160 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure exceeded 110 mmHg on two occasions at least six hours apart, accompanied by proteinuria > 5 g/24 h or > 3 + by dipstick on at least two occasions9. Participants were included if they were pregnant, had a gestational age greater than 20 weeks, and met the defined criteria for PE. Exclusion criteria included pre-existing conditions such as diabetes, chronic hypertension, bronchial asthma, autoimmune diseases, chronic liver and kidney diseases, and multifetal pregnancies.

Statistical power analysis using Epi Info, with a 95 % confidence level and 80 % power (type I error set at 0.05), indicated a required sample size of 100 cases and 100 controls.

Data collection

Blood pressure was measured (following ten minutes of rest, blood pressure was recorded on the right arm while the participants were in the supine position using a conventional sphygmomanometer with the proper cuff size). Information regarding maternal age at delivery, gestational age at delivery, parity, and neonatal outcomes was collected.

Blood samples and routine laboratory workup

Aseptically collected venous blood (7 mL) from each participant was divided into three aliquots for analysis.•Two milliliters were collected in EDTA tubes for complete blood count (CBC) and subsequently stored at −80 °C for genetic analysis of the studied SNPs.•Two milliliters were collected in citrated tubes, and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min, and the separated citrated plasma was used for prothrombin time (PT) and fibrinogen assays.•Three milliliters were collected in plain tubes, allowed to clot for one hour at 37 °C, centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min, and the separated serum was used for various biochemical assays.

Using an automated cell counter (Cell-Dyne Ruby, Abbott Diagnostics) CBC was conducted, with a particular focus on hemoglobin and platelet count.

The coagulation profile was assessed by measuring prothrombin time (PT) using an automated coagulation analyzer (Sysmex CS-1600, Kobe, Japan). Plasma fibrinogen levels were evaluated using the Human Fibrinogen Antigen ELISA Kit, and microplate ELISA reader (EMR-500, USA).

Colorimetric assays were performed using a Chem-7 spectrophotometer (Erba Diagnostics Mannheim GmbH, Germany), and were used to measure liver function tests (alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total protein levels, and serum albumin), renal function tests (urea and creatinine), 24-hour urine proteins, random blood glucose, and albumin-to-creatinine (A/C) ratio (mg albumin/gm creatinine). Qualitative albumin assays in urine samples from all participants were measured using urinary dipsticks. Additionally, plasma fibrinogen was measured with fluorescent immunoassay (FIA), with fluorescence readings taken at 485 nm for excitation and 525 nm for emission.

DNA extraction and genetic assays of NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A (rs8191974) and NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T (rs1803628) SNPs

After genomic DNA extraction from whole EDTA blood samples using a total DNA extraction kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Inc., Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, genotyping of NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A (rs8191974) and NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T (rs1803628) SNPs were performed using RFLP-PCR technique. NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A was analyzed using the forward and reverse primers 5′-TTCCCTCAAGAAAGAGCCCG-3′ and 5′-TCTCATCCCAAACCTCCACA-3′, while NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T was analyzed using 5′-GCCCCGTTTCTGCCGTAGAGAG-3′ and 5′-CTTGCTTTCAGCGTATGCC-3′7. To amplify the target SNPs, 12.5 μl of the PCR master mix solution (catalog No. 25028, iNtRON Biotechnology, Korea) was mixed with 1 μl of the extracted DNA, 1 μl of forward primer, 1 μl of reverse primer, and 8.5 μl of nuclease-free water. The PCR reactions were conducted using a Biometra thermal cycler (Germany, serial number 2603204). The PCR condition utilized for genotyping was as follow: mixture was heated to 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of amplification, which included denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 61 °C for 30 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 30 s. The final extension took five minutes at 72 °C. The PCR amplification product for rs8191974 SNP was 481 bp and for rs1803628 SNP was 358 bp (Fig. 1). The PCR amplification products were digested using restriction enzymes. AlwN1 (catalog No. R0514S, supplied by New England Biolabs) was used for the detection of GAS6 c.834 + 7G > A, and NlaIII (catalog No.R0125S, supplied by New England Biolabs) was used for the detection of GAS6 c.1332C > T. One μl of the enzyme, 5 μl of enzyme buffer (1X), 1 μg of PCR products were completed to 50 μl of nuclease-free water, then incubated for 15 min hour at 37 °C. Then heat inactivation was performed by incubation of AlwN1 containing mixture at 65 °C for 20 min and incubation of NlaIII containing mixture at 80 °C for 20 min. The digested products were seen under a UV transilluminator after being resolved on a 2 % agarose gel electrophoresis with ethidium bromide. For use in determining the band size of the PCR products, a 50 bp DNA ladder (catalog No. 10416014, Invitrogen Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) was added alongside the PCR products. Data analysis and interpretation

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous data were presented as mean ± SD (parametric) or median and interquartile range (non-parametric), after assessing normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Nominal data (percentages) were compared using the Chi-square test. Group comparisons were performed using independent t-tests (parametric, two groups), ANOVA (parametric, >2 groups), Mann-Whitney U test (non-parametric, two groups), or Kruskal-Wallis H test (non-parametric, >2 groups). Correlations were assessed using Spearman's rho. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium was assessed for all studied SNPs. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

While the association between GAS6 SNPs and PE has been explored in other populations, this association has not been established in Egyptian women. This study therefore aimed to determine the potential correlation between PE incidence and severity and the NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A (rs8191974) and NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T (rs1803628) SNPs in this population.

In the present study, patients exhibited a lower gestational age, with systolic and diastolic blood pressures elevated compared to controls. Notably, primiparous women were more common among patients, whereas multiparous women were more prevalent in controls. In the analysis of PE, both severe and mild cases demonstrated significantly lower gestational ages and higher blood pressures than controls, with a lower prevalence of multiparous women in severe PE. These findings align with those of Kasraeian et al.10, who investigated serum biomarkers for detecting PE severity. While no significant age difference was found between mild and severe PE, severe cases were significantly associated with lower gestational age. Similarly, Akram and Al Defer11 reported no significant age difference between cases and controls but found significantly higher systolic and diastolic blood pressures in the case group.

In terms of laboratory data, our study found that patients had lower platelet counts, higher urinary proteins, elevated INR, A/C ratio, SGOT, SGPT, creatinine, and plasma fibrinogen levels, alongside lower plasma total proteins. Severe PE cases exhibited even lower platelet counts and higher levels of urinary proteins, A/C ratio, liver enzymes, creatinine, and fibrinogen compared to mild cases and controls, alongside reduced total protein and increased 24-hour urinary protein levels. This is consistent with Rajoria and Code12, who prospectively studied the relationship between deranged liver and renal function tests and PE severity. Women with mild PE had stable liver function (mean SGOT 60.76 ± 22.63 U/L; mean SGPT 62.47 ± 22.36 U/L), while those with severe PE had significantly higher levels (mean SGOT 120.76 ± 22.63 U/L; mean SGPT 128.47 ± 22.36 U/L). Although the present study found lower blood fibrinogen in cases (P = 0.0012), Akram and Al Defer11 also reported significantly lower serum platelet levels (P < 0.05) and higher SGOT and SGPT levels (P < 0.05) in their case group. The role of proteinuria in PE was examined by Dong et al.13, who discovered that in severe cases, a significantly smaller number of patients had proteinuria levels < 0.3 g/L than those with proteinuria > 0.3 g/L. However, when proteinuria levels were higher than 0.3 g/L, no change was seen. Groups with proteinuria > 0.3 g/L had a significantly increased frequency of severe PE. Although there was no correlation between proteinuria levels and the time from PE onset to delivery, patients with proteinuria > 3 g/L experienced an earlier onset. Patients with proteinuria > 3 g/L had considerably lower birth weights, and those with proteinuria > 5 g/L had a significantly higher risk of fetal growth restriction or stillbirth. According to this research, proteinuria levels are associated with the severity and prognosis of severe preeclampsia, although they are not necessary for the diagnosis.

Our findings of poorer birth outcomes and increased newborn care needs in patients with PE are consistent with previous research. We observed fewer full-term births, more preterm births, and a greater need for incubation in patients compared to controls. Guida et al.14 also reported a significant incidence of premature births (45.6 %) and a high cesarean delivery rate (63.4 %) in severe PE. Khader et al.15, studying Jordanian women, found significantly higher rates of low birth weight deliveries (32.5 % vs. 8.3 %), prematurity (30.8 % vs. 7 %), and neonatal mortality (81 vs. 12 per 1000 live births) in women with PE.

The results of our investigation showed a substantial correlation between the AA genotype and the A allele of the NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A SNP with the incidence of PE. There was no discernible difference in the genotypes and allele frequencies of the NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A SNP between mild and severe PE cases. However, there was a significant correlation between the occurrence and severity of PE and the NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T SNP's C allele and CC genotype. Only two studies examining the potential association between GAS6 SNPs and PE risk and severity were found in the literature. Ye et al.7, who investigated the relationship between GAS6 gene polymorphisms and preeclampsia risk in a Chinese population, found notable variations in the NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T genotypes and alleles frequencies, which partially supported our findings. They discovered that the + 1332 TT genotype imparted a risk of developing severe preeclampsia (OR = 0.597, 95 % CI 0.416–0.855; P = 0.01) and was protective against PE (odds ratio 0.271, 95 % CI 0.077–0.953; P = 0.03). The NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A genotypes and alleles frequencies, however, did not change. The polymorphism, plasma GAS6 levels, and PE in Turkish women were examined by Ozakpinar et al.5, who found that PE patients had considerably lower plasma GAS6 levels. According to their findings, the GG genotype was the most common and had a 3.5-fold increased risk of preeclampsia in comparison to the AA genotype. PE patients were less likely to carry the A allele (OR = 2.118, 95 % CI = 1.330–3.371, p < 0.001). Different sample sizes, inclusion criteria, and genetic variances by ethnicity and geography could all account for these discrepancies.

Conclusion

Our thorough investigation of the relationship between preeclampsia risk and growth arrest-specific 6 protein gene polymorphisms in Egyptian women found that the genotype and allele frequencies of patients and controls differed significantly. The NM_000820.4:c.834+7G>A polymorphism revealed that patients with PE had higher frequencies of the AA genotype and A allele and a lower percentage of the GG genotype and G allele. PE cases were more likely to have the CC genotype and the C allele of the NM_000820.4:c.1332C>T polymorphism, especially the severe variety. This indicates a potential genetic predisposition to preeclampsia risk and severity among Egyptian women Clinical and laboratory comparisons revealed distinct biomarker profiles, including altered platelet counts, elevated SGOT and SGPT levels, higher A/C ratios, and variations in birth outcomes and neonatal care needs. These findings offer valuable insights into the genetic and clinical aspects of preeclampsia, suggesting further research into genetic risk factors and therapeutic targets.

Study’s limitations

The generalizability of these findings is limited by the relatively small sample size, lack of Sanger sequencing of some random samples from each genotype, and single-center design of this study. Furthermore, the lack of plasma GAS6 measurements prevented correlation with the genetic profile of the studied SNPs.
