Development of an adapted glossary of implementation strategies for supporting behavioral health program uptake in faith-based communities
Authors: Thomas J. Waltz, David Schlundt, Sharon Jones, Jemal Gishe, Leah Alexander, Neely Williams, Omaràn D. Lee, Kristin Clarkson, Jialong Zhen, Rebecca Selove
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFormal compilations of implementation strategies like the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC; 1) are relevant for planning and evaluating implementation initiatives. The relationship between these strategies and the particulars of a specific initiative is not always clear, particularly when implementation occurs outside of organized healthcare settings. The present study reports on the process used to develop an adapted glossary of ERIC strategies for implementing evidence-based health promotion programs in African American churches.MethodsA glossary adaptation team composed of academics and community representatives met twice a month for six months to adapt implementation strategy definitions to fit the context of the present project. Collaborative discussions were held until consensus was reached for each strategy. This project-adapted glossary was then subjected to coding to document the types of changes that occurred during the adaptation process.ResultsThe glossary adaptation team collectively dedicated 99.5 person-hours to the meetings to obtain consensus for the strategies. The final strategy glossary retained 64 strategies relevant to the project, with 84.4% of strategies involving some level of adaptation. Background

Despite the availability of established implementation strategy compilations such as the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) compilation [1] and the Cochrane Effective Practice and Organization of Care (EPOC) taxonomy [2], researchers continue to face challenges in translating these broad categories into actionable, measurable strategies. The lack of specificity in how strategies are defined and operationalized often obscures the mechanisms by which they influence outcomes, making it difficult to compare findings across studies or replicate effective approaches. To address this, researchers are encouraged to specify the relevant actor, action, action target, temporality, dose, implementation outcome affected, and use case justification [3]. Yet such specifications tend to occur at the project level and vary substantially across local contexts, limiting generalizability. Consequently, tailoring—while widely endorsed—remains inconsistently applied and poorly understood as a process for linking contextual determinants to specific strategies. This work aims to clarify that gap by examining how decisions to adapt implementation strategy definitions are made and documented during pre-implementation for health promotion programs in African American churches, and by exploring ways to make these processes more transparent, systematic, and reproducible across implementation settings.

Implementation strategies can only be tailored to address the determinants identified for a specific project if the strategies are recognizable to the team coordinating the initiative. Adapting the content of implementation strategy definitions for this purpose occurs at an earlier step in the implementation process than strategy tailoring. For example, Cook and colleagues [4] utilized a seven-step process to adapt ERIC for the education sector. The resulting School Implementation Strategies, Translating ERIC Resources (SISTER) strategy compilation was then used as a resource for supporting subsequent implementation initiatives in the education sector. Evidence suggests that relevant stakeholders found these adapted strategies to be more important and feasible for use in school settings than the original ERIC language [5].

Balis and colleagues [6] took a bottom-up approach after encountering difficulties adapting ERIC strategy definitions for community-based settings (see [7, 8]). Using an emic, qualitative design grounded in the Reach Efficacy-Adoption Implementation Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework [9], they identified locally relevant strategies reported by community researchers and practitioners. This process yielded the Implementation Strategies Applied in Communities (ISAC) compilation [6]. An effort was made to relate ISAC strategies with ERIC strategies, however, outside experts were not utilized to help make these connections.

Both Cook et al. [4] and Balis et al. [6] developed approaches to create adapted implementation strategy compilations that they viewed as being relevant for broad application in their relevant areas (i.e., school-based implementation and community-based implementation, respectively). While there is value in having examples of sector-adapted implementation strategy compilations, project-level adaptations represent opportunities to ensure implementation strategies are recognizable at a more local level. The present study describes a process used for developing project-level adaptations of the ERIC strategy compilation for a project providing technical assistance to African American churches implementing evidence-based behavioral health promotion programs.

Context for this case example

Engaging Partners in Caring Communities (EPICC) is a community-engaged research (CEnR) project designed to build the capacity of churches serving African American communities in Nashville, Tennessee to implement evidence-based health promotion programs by providing technical assistance for congregation-based program delivery. These churches were offered a menu of six programs that were identified by the EPICC research team as addressing chronic disease disparities among African Americans, and meeting criteria offered in the literature for minimizing barriers to implementation. The programs and the process that led to their identification have been described previously [10]. EPICC was designed to provide technical assistance (TA) to churches to enhance their capacity to deliver the programs supported by EPICC as well as other evidence-based programs in the future.

Churches that agreed to participate in EPICC completed an implementation capacity survey developed for this project, building on others’ work to identify facilitators and barriers to implementation of health promotion programs in churches (e.g., [11–13]) using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [14]. The EPICC Project staff transformed results of the survey into two lists referred to as strengths (facilitators) and opportunities for growth (barriers) as part of delivering the results of the survey back to each church. The opportunities for growth were entered into a version of the CFIR-ERIC Tool [15] designed for EPICC which provided an approach for prioritizing ERIC strategies that technical assistants could use to address the barriers that were identified in the EPICC survey responses.

This technical assistance focuses on providing a tailored set of recommendations for supporting health program implementation, supporting local capacity for implementation, and evaluating implementation outcomes. The present work represents early efforts in the EPICC project to adapt implementation strategies to this setting so they could be meaningfully incorporated into the project’s TA processes.

The objective of this study was to illustrate the process used to develop an adapted glossary of ERIC strategies for implementing evidence-based health promotion programs in Black churches. Previous research has noted the value of reporting implementation strategies using language that can be more easily understood by the stakeholders participating in the implementation evaluation [16].

This glossary is being used in conjunction with the CFIR-ERIC Matching Tool [15] as part of a more comprehensive TA and implementation evaluation processes.

Methods

A team-based consensus process was used to review and adapt the ERIC strategies into a glossary of implementation strategies to be used to support evidence-based health promotion programs in African American serving churches in Nashville Tennessee. Team meetings occurred between December 2021 and May 2022. Transcripts from the team meetings were used to document the team members present and meeting duration. Team meetings occurred over Zoom.

The glossary was built from an existing compilation of strategies (ERIC; 1) and was supplemented by two strategies and several friendly edits from Perry et al. [17]. Seventy-five strategies were reviewed in total. Additional file 6 from Powell et al. [1] served as the primary source material for the group adaptation exercise and its content was organized by thematic cluster [18]. A shared online document was used to record adapted definitions for the project as well as ancillary considerations that were identified during the discussion of each strategy. Implementation strategy definitions were adapted in real-time during the collaborative discussions until consensus was reached. Strategy labels were adapted as appropriate to reflect the adapted definitions. A relevant summary of the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications to Evidence-based Implementation Strategies (FRAME-IS; [19]) is provided in Additional file 1.

Glossary adaptation team

The glossary adaptation team included 10 contributors, nine of whom were members of the EPICC Core Research Team that was developed to manage the overall project. These individuals were informed of the purpose and scope of the project and updated on project activities during weekly team meetings. Six were EPICC academic partners with backgrounds in behavioral health, healthcare, public health, and quantitative and qualitative research methods (inclusive of the PI’s). Two members were representatives from the Congregational Health and Education Network (CHEN), a faith-based non-profit organization dedicated to reducing health disparities in Nashville’s African American community and a key partner for the EPICC project. Two members were consultants to EPICC. One consultant was a Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) community advocate who is also pastor, with personal relationships with leaders of churches that were being recruited to participate in EPICC. The final member of the team was a consultant who is an academic implementation scientist.

Glossary adaptation team meeting structure

Additional file 6 from Powell et al. [1] was placed in a GoogleDoc where the first three pages had a hyperlinked index to all of the individual implementation strategies, organized by conceptual cluster, alphabetized within cluster. The clusters were in the following order: use evaluative and iterative strategies, provide interactive assistance, adapt and tailor to context, develop stakeholder interrelationships, train and educate stakeholders, support clinicians, engage consumers, utilize financial strategies, and change infrastructure.

Each strategy entry in the document had three boxes. The first was the original ERIC label and definition for the strategy. The second was labeled EPICC notes. This is where the team proposed label/definition revisions and amendments to ancillary materials. The consensus definition was placed at the top of the material in the EPICC notes box. The third contained the ancillary materials from Additional file 6. These reflected notes that the experts who participated in the development of the ERIC compilation had made during that process. This material was included because it may provide contextual information to help with the adaptation process.

All team members had access to the document and were able to review it before team meetings. Team members could leave notes in the EPICC notes box or use the Comment feature to create a margin note in advance of the meeting. During the team meetings, we began with the first item in the first conceptual cluster and then moved down the list. The first question the group addressed was whether the strategy was appropriate for the EPICC project. Preliminary adaptive language for the strategy was discussed to facilitate this determination. When a strategy was determined to be potentially appropriate for EPICC, preliminary drafts of adapted definitions were transcribed. All members were able to participate in transcription in real time. Rich discussions of the key features of ERIC implementation strategies and the structural and cultural aspects of implementing behavioral health programs in congregations occurred. The team would deliberate the proposed adapted definitions before determining a best synthesized adaptation. Having an academic team member with extensive qualitative research expertise greatly facilitated the development of a clearly worded synthesized adapted definition. Faith community representatives on the team supported culturally competent adaptations for the setting of the project. Core research team members also brought considerations regarding how adapted strategies would be utilized in technical assistance activities and documented as part of the implementation evaluation. The implementation consultant helped maintain conceptual similarity with the original definition and their familiarity with other definitions was useful when discussions for one strategy definition diverged and began addressing issues best captured by a separate strategy.

While the team worked through the glossary in the order described above, we commonly revisited already adapted definitions to update language reflecting emerging group consensus regarding best practices for characterizing relevant aspects of the EPICC project and its constituencies. Dedicated meetings with our CHEN community partner team members were also held to ensure their perspectives were adequately represented.

Team time dedicated to adapting strategy definitions

Members of the glossary adaptation team met on 12 occasions, twice a month for six months. There were 10 members on the glossary adaptation team and an average of 5.6 attended each meeting (range 2 to 8). While the CHEN representatives often had conflicting obligations with the adaptation team meetings, most were present in other EPICC team meetings. Two dedicated meetings were held specifically to gather CHEN representative expertise on adapted strategies and improve consistency across all strategies in the glossary. One of these dedicated meetings was 60 min in duration and the other was 90 min. All other meetings were 90 min in duration, resulting in a total of 99.5 person-hours dedicated to the adaptation team meetings.

Glossary analysis team

The team coding the adapted glossary included the implementation science consultant from the glossary tailoring team (TW). He was also a member of the team that published the ERIC implementation strategy compilation. The second coder (JZ) was a first-year doctoral student in health services psychology with interests in implementation science.

Glossary analysis codebook

The analysis team coded the adapted glossary to determine whether the adaptation process produced changes in any of the six dimensions of strategy specification according to Proctor et al. [3]. Each strategy was also characterized by the level of change that occurred (i.e., deep, surface, none, or deleted; [4]) and whether a label change occurred. Finally, the coding reflected whether the adapted strategy reflected community-forward language, that implementation occurs in a congregation context, and a qualitative summary of the common elements retained from the original definition to the adapted definition. The Codebook is provided as Additional file 2.

Glossary coding procedure

The glossary analysis team members coded each strategy independently. Coding proceeded across multiple rounds, each round involved one or more clusters of ERIC strategies [18] with early rounds involving single clusters and later rounds involving multiple clusters. After each round of coding the analysis team met to identify discrepancies in coding, discuss those differences, and make updates to the codebook to improve coding procedures.

Glossary coding process

Each strategy was coded along 11 dimensions. The first round of coding involved the 9 strategies retained in the use evaluative and iterative strategies cluster. There were 18 disagreements (18%) and the reconciliation meeting resulted in two updates to the codebook. A reconciliation log was maintained for every point of disagreement to document the consensus-based decision-making and to serve as a supplement to the codebook for subsequent rounds of coding. The second round of coding included strategies from the provide interactive assistance and adapt and tailor to the context clusters. There were 12 disagreements (7%) and the reconciliation meeting resulted in one update to the codebook. Overall, 12.8% of the codes needed to be reconciled during the reliability/discrepancy meetings. In 68.9% of the cases, the code of the senior expert (TW) was retained and in 31.1% of the cases, the code of the student (JZ) was retained.

Table 2 also notes that 14.1% of strategies were adapted to reflect community forward language. Community forward language involves terminology identified as culturally appropriate for use with congregation community partners by the CHEN community members participating in the glossary adaptation team. Several examples were identified and highlighted in the codebook. “Gathering experiences” was the preferred alternative to “collecting data” to emphasize that it is a process done with (versus to) congregational partners. The identification of “challenges and strengths” was preferred to “barriers and facilitators” as reflecting clearer language and preserving community agency. Speaking of “covenants’ was identified as an appropriate term for discussing “commitments” in a congregation context. And finally, “celebrate” was identified as the preferred means of characterizing social-based incentives for engaging in relevant health programming rather than “incentivize.”

Discussion

The adaptation of strategy definitions to a project is iterative and involves a substantial amount of group meeting time. It took 99.5 person-hours to develop a glossary where representatives of multiple project-related constituencies could experience shared meaning. The glossary adaptation team experienced the process as a creative endeavor that challenged them to carefully consider the various ways health promotion program implementation could be strategically supported in faith-based communities. Community partners’ (pastor/community advocate and two CHEN community representatives) participation in the process was essential for adapting strategies to reflect the strengths and challenges of implementing health programs in faith-based communities and for determining which strategies were a poor fit. They provided guidance for adapting language to more consistently honor the culture and tradition of the African American church. The community forward langauge examples such as “gathering experiences”, “covenants”, and “celebrate” have direct implications for the EPICC TA team regarding how they collaborate with the projects participating congregations. These partners’ expertise transformed the team’s understanding of how specific strategies fit (or not) within EPICC. An example of a strategy with deep-level changes was the ERIC strategy identify early adopters which is defined as identify early adopters at the local site to learn from their experiences with the practice innovation [1]. The adaptation team discussions noted that community-based programs are not expected to always have relevant experts in their congregations and that when relevant experts are present, they would not necessarily have direct experience with the specific evidence-based program targeted for implementation. However, the spirit of the strategy involves utilizing local expertise and our community partners noted that congregations often rely on the expertise of their members in support of community activities. As a result of this discussion, we adapted this strategy’s label to identify local expertise and adapted the definition to: within each congregation engage congregation members who have existing knowledge, experience, and expertise in health promotion. This adaptation reflects that informal support networks involving individuals with relevant expertise are often present in community settings. This is coded as a “deep” change because the roles and structures in community-based settings are different from organized healthcare settings. Nevertheless, an early adopter and an individual with local expertise can perform similar functions for an implementation initiative. Overall, the glossary adaptation team served as a cultural exchange for the academic partners, community partners, and consultants.

The present results are similar to those observed in Cook et al.’s SISTER project ([4]; compare their Table 10 with our Table 1). Most strategy adaptations were surface-level changes (75% and 72%, respectively. The SISTER adapted strategy compilation was for an entire implementation sector (school-based implementation) while the adapted EPICC strategy compilation is for a specific community-based project. For a project like EPICC that involves the implementation of a faith community’s choice among five evidence-based health programs, the adapted glossary of implementation strategies needs to be general enough to apply to different programs and faith communities of varying sizes and organizational complexity. Eleven of the ERIC strategies were eliminated from the glossary as impractical or irrelevant to EPICC. SISTER, as a sector-focused project, was more conservative and eliminated only five. The majority of implementation strategies could be adapted directly to the context of congregation-based health promotion programs with surface-level changes. Only 8 (12.5%) of the strategies retained for the glossary did not involve any adaptation of their content. Similarly, the SISTER project had 11 strategies that did not involve any changes from ERIC. Across both projects definition adaptation provided value to the teams in creating shared understanding. The adapted glossaries for both projects serve as crosswalks between adapted strategies and the original ERIC strategies. This enables the EPICC team (and SISTER researchers) to communicate evaluations of the project in terms that facilitate its inclusion in future meta-analyses of ERIC implementation strategies applied in community-based settings.

The coding of the adapted glossary highlighted specific challenges that accompany the adaptation of strategy definitions during the planning phase of a project. As with the original ERIC strategy completion many of the specific actors, actions, action targets, timing, dose, and outcomes are not specified or are stated in general terms. While this is a deliberate feature of implementation strategy definitions, it impacts interrater reliability and necessitates the use of coder reconciliation meetings. It is only at the summative evaluation phase that all of the dimensions of the Proctor [3] reporting framework are available. However, having a project-adapted implementation strategy glossary is anticipated to improve the team’s ability to identify and document the full range of implementation strategies employed during the project and not just the signature strategy of the project (i.e. technical assistance). This reduces the ambiguity other researchers have noted in identifying the relevance of ERIC strategies to community-based implementation projects [6–8].

Limitations

While we obtained valuable input from community members, only two community representatives were involved and both were experienced in community advocacy and partnerships with academic institutions. A broader and more inclusive group of community representatives from congregations with existing and well-experienced health ministry teams may have had different input and perspectives. The resulting glossary is specifically adapted to the EPICC project and the process and outcomes may be very different for other kinds of community-engaged research and future researchers should consider undergoing a similar process to develop adapted strategies that are specific to their project. The adaptations made in the EPICC strategy glossary may serve as a useful point of reference for other teams considering the types of adaptations they may want to make to the strategy definitions to obtain a better contextual fit for their projects.

The changes made in the EPICC project strategy glossary were partly a function of the broad expertise of the academic team which included experience with implementation science, health services research, community-engaged participatory research, health disparities, and qualitative and quantitative methods. A different team may have produced a different set of adaptations. However, the glossary development process and detailed evaluation of the changes presented in this paper will help guide other teams who embark upon adapting the ERIC strategies for specific implementation projects. We recommend that those interested in developing strategy glossaries that apply more broadly to consider the seven-step process used by Cook and colleagues [4, 20].

Conclusion

The strategy definition adaptation process represents a real-time investment from a project team and benefits from existing relationships among community and academic partners. The community member perspectives were essential to negotiating a shared language. The strategy adaptation process builds trust and has transformational effects on the team members and the team’s culture.

Developing adapted implementation strategy glossaries prior to the active implementation phase of a project represents an opportunity for making implementation science research products more relevant to implementation practitioners by connecting the specifics of a project to the broader implementation literature. This process can also help the implementation team identify a wider range of potentially relevant implementation strategies that may be employed to support the project. While it would be premature to identify an exhaustive list of relevant expertise that should be represented in an implementation strategy glossary adaptation team, we believe future teams will find the following types of representatives useful. There should be at least one implementation scientist with interest in supporting the connection between the project’s adapted strategies and published strategy compilations like ERIC or EPOC that may be used to structure future meta-analyses of strategies used across similar projects. There should be at least one representative of the implementation evaluation team who will be responsible for documenting the implementation strategies used during the project. There should be at least one representative for the constituents directly responsible for implementing the initiative. If there are multiple types of constituents, then careful consideration should be given regarding the breadth of representation on the team, even if some representatives have limited time to review and comment on the adapted strategies. There should be at least one representative for the constituents providing implementation support to those directly enacting implementation. Similarly, if there are multiple types of these support-level constituents, then careful consideration should be given regarding the breadth of representation on the team. Finally, synthesizing the discussions about adapting strategies strongly benefits from having an experienced qualitative research expert. We found team members with experience soliciting, listening for, and documenting shared understanding essential for efficiently establishing satisfactory consensus.
