Improved quantum long short-term memory with successive variational mode decomposition for solar irradiance prediction
Authors: Snigdha Sarangi, Pradipta Kishore Dash, Ranjeeta Bisoi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Short-term solar irradiance prediction is critical for efficient power scheduling, the design of solar-integrated energy management systems, and maintaining grid stability. However, the inherent variability of solar energy introduces fluctuations in power output, and posing challenges for reliable grid operation. Leveraging recent advancements in quantum computing, the current work proposes a hybrid quantum-driven neural network for solar irradiance prediction, combining successive variational mode decomposition (SVMD) with an improved quantum long short-term memory (IQLSTM) network. To enhance circuit expressibility, a robust variational quantum circuit (RVQC) incorporating controlled-X (CRX) gates is embedded within the quantum long short-term memory (QLSTM) architecture. In the RVQC, a circular topology with two-way connections is employed to optimize qubit interconnections and strengthen entanglement. The proposed model is evaluated using two solar irradiance datasets of Hyderabad (2019) and Bengaluru (2019, 2018), obtained from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). To assess the effectiveness of the proposed circuit, evaluations are carried out under varying weather conditions and across multiple forecasting horizons, and results are compared against classical LSTM and other quantum-based networks like quantum gate recurrent units (QGRUs), quantum support vector machine (QSVM) and QLSTM. Furthermore, the model is evaluated under noisy quantum environments to approximate realistic Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) hardware conditions, enabling the assessment of its robustness under practical quantum computing constraints. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed model achieves superior predictive accuracy and robustness compared with existing classical and quantum-based approaches.

Introduction

Solar energy is one of the most widely utilized forms of renewable energy and has established a strong presence in the competitive power market in recent years. Power grids have experienced significant growth in the integration of this energy source, as it is recognized the most eco-friendly and widely available form of renewable energy1. Some key benefits of integrating solar energy into power grids include reducing greenhouse gas emissions, decreasing power losses and operational costs, enabling direct charging of DC-powered devices like laptops, electric vehicles, and mobile phones without the need for converters, and improving overall power quality2. Despite these advantages, its high penetration into the power grid can lead to critical power balance problems, primarily due to its intermittent and fluctuating nature3. Hence, accurately predicting solar energy is essential to support its growing importance in the modernization of the energy sector. From this perspective solar irradiance is regarded as the most vital and influential factor in defining the performance of solar units, especially for estimating the potential amount of power that can be generated in a specific area4. So, numerous studies have been conducted in recent years on solar irradiance prediction (SIP), with a brief overview presented in the following section.

Machine learning models are capable of capturing and modeling nonlinear and multifaceted relationships using solar irradiance data, meteorological variables, or a combination of both. The autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model has proven to be effective for high-resolution SIP over a 24-hour period5. On the other hand, advanced machine learning techniques like artificial neural networks (ANN) have demonstrated strong potential in predicting solar irradiance over both short-term as well as medium-term timeframes6. Besides ARIMA and ANN, the support vector machine (SVM) approach has also proven effective in predicting photovoltaic (PV) output. In7 SVM is utilized as a classification technique, categorizing days into cloudy, foggy, sunny, and rainy to train the model. In the context of regression-based forecasting, SVM outperformed ANN at different seasons8. To offer deeper insight into the forecasting outcomes, various techniques such as extreme learning machine (ELM), random vector functional link neural network (RVFLN), genetic algorithms (GA), hidden Markov model (HMM), naive bayes, gaussian process (GP), k-means clustering, and decision trees (DT) have been utilized9–12. Further deep learning techniques build upon machine learning methods for prediction. With their complex and intricate network structures, deep learning models possess powerful nonlinear mapping abilities, which enhance their performance. The three most widely used deep learning techniques for predicting solar irradiance are deep belief networks (DBN), convolutional neural networks (CNN), and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks13,14. Furthermore, recent research has focused on hybrid and chaos-enhanced metaheuristic optimization techniques to improve convergence efficiency, search stability, and the overall effectiveness of energy system optimization frameworks15. These methods are designed to address common drawbacks of traditional optimization approaches, including premature convergence and insufficient exploration of the search space. For example, chaos-integrated variants of gorilla troops optimization have been introduced to enhance population diversity and achieve a better balance between exploration and exploitation throughout the optimization process16. Likewise, hybrid algorithms that integrate moth–flame optimization with particle swarm optimization have demonstrated improved convergence characteristics and solution quality when applied to complex optimization tasks in power transmission and distribution systems17. Additionally, several other advanced optimization strategies have been proposed to facilitate efficient renewable energy integration and to enhance the operational performance of modern power distribution networks18.

Recent advances in quantum computing have drawn growing interest, as studies have revealed unprecedented advantages over classical approaches. This has, in particular, sparked a surge of interest in quantum machine learning (QML). Several studies have leveraged quantum technologies to enhance machine learning algorithms, offering valuable references for future research in QML. With the advent of noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ)19 devices, the variational quantum circuit (VQC) system20,21 has emerged as a leading contender for developing shallow quantum algorithms that can outperform classical computers. In22, the author investigated the use of VQCs in deep reinforcement learning, employing low-depth circuits to restructure various components of the model, including experience replay and target networks. Ref23. noted a gap between quantum computing and real-world applications, emphasizing the need to identify suitable domains that can demonstrate quantum computing’s potential for problem-solving. Addressing these limitations could enhance prediction accuracy by integrating a deep learning model with a VQC. As research advances, models such as quantum support vector machine (QSVM)24, quantum gated recurrent units (QGRUs)25, and quantum convolutional neural networks (QCNN)26 have successively emerged. But among all the classical networks, LSTM has the capacity to capture long-term dependencies, efficiently process sequential data, and alleviate the vanishing gradient problem, making it a strong candidate for various sequential data processing tasks in machine learning. Motivated by these strengths, the researcher proposed a model that integrates LSTM with a QNN and develops quantum long short-term memory (QLSTM)27. Expanding the application of LSTM, QLSTM has been refined through a series of enhancements and has been implemented it widely across areas including speech recognition28, sentiment analysis29, and others. In30, a quantum-based hybrid neural network for time series prediction is employed, which introduces a novel architecture that combines deep LSTM layers with VQCs. In a similar manner, a resilient hybrid Quantum LSTM-based model is applied for short-term wind speed prediction31, and a comprehensive QLSTM cell implemented on quantum hardware is proposed in32, with the objective of improving computational speed and scalability in quantum neural networks. Additionally, advanced layers incorporating enhanced VQCs alongside LSTM have been employed to improve the efficiency of QLSTM models across various applications33–35.

To reduce prediction errors, numerous researchers have introduced a range of data decomposition techniques. The most frequently used decomposition methods for SIP are empirical mode decomposition (EMD) and ensemble empirical mode decomposition (EEMD)36. VMD is a substitute decomposition method for EMD and EEMD in SIP and has shown promising results37. However, the effectiveness of VMD relies on key parameters like the number of parameters that need to be optimized. Selecting incorrect parameters can lead to issues such as mode mixing or mode splitting in the resulting decomposed components. To resolve these problems, successive VMD (SVMD) was introduced, enabling automatic and iterative selection of parameters38. Hence, SVMD offers a more effective option for decomposing nonlinear data like solar irradiance.

From the preceding analysis, this study introduces a customized ensemble SVMD and Improved quantum LSTM (IQLSTM) framework that incorporates a robust VQC into the LSTM structure to enhance SIP. According to our understanding, this work is one of the earliest attempts to integrate a robust VQC with the QLSTM algorithm for SIP. In this circuit, classical data is encoded into quantum states using angle embedding, chosen for its low complexity and high computational efficiency39. This encoding approach also optimizes qubit utilization by reducing the total number of qubits required for encoding. Unlike conventional CNOT gates, controlled rotation gates such as CRX, CRY, and CRZ offer a wider coverage of the Hilbert space, allowing for a more comprehensive representation of solution spaces. In our VQC model, CRX gates are used for their enhanced entangling strength and greater expressibility relative to CRY and CRZ gates40. Additionally, the proposed VQC circuit has a circular and two-way connection topology, while taking into account the spatial arrangement of control and target qubits. Collectively, these configurations enhance the traditional QLSTM architecture, delivering improved expressibility, reduced training overhead, and greater accuracy in time-series forecasting tasks. The overall performance and robustness of the proposed model are systematically assessed and examined.

The entanglement strategy, defined by the placement of two-qubit gates and the pattern of qubit connectivity, has a significant influence on the representational power and optimization performance of a variational quantum circuit. In the figure RVQC is a circular two-way connected VQC, which incorporates controlled-X gates and adds an extra pair of columns consisting of rotation gates i.e. Ry and Rz, organized in a circular manner for qubit-pair interactions. Parameter optimization

Like traditional machine learning algorithms, the IQLSTML is trained to handle tasks based on data-driven approaches. From a mathematical perspective, the learning process consists of minimizing the loss function L (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document}), which represents the objective function. In this study, a gradient-based optimization technique is employed to iteratively adjust the parameters of the RVQC. The approach utilizes the geometrical characteristics of the parameter space to facilitate faster and more stable optimization convergence. The parameters are repeatedly refined by moving in the direction that most effectively minimizes the loss function, typically formulated as32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{j}\leftarrow\:\:{\theta\:}_{j}-\eta\:{\nabla\:}_{{\theta\:}_{j}}L\left(\theta\:\right)$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\nabla\:}_{\theta\:\:}$$\end{document} denotes the gradient operator in terms of quantum parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\eta\:\:$$\end{document} represents the learning rate. So, RVQC optimization is carried out by computing gradients through the parameter-shift rule. For an output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:f\left(x,\:\theta\:\right)\:$$\end{document} defined in terms of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} and input features x, the gradient of the variational quantum circuit is computed as33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\nabla\:}_{\theta\:}f\left(x,\:\theta\:\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[f\left(\left(x,\:\theta\:\right)+\:\frac{\pi\:}{2}\right),\:f\left(\left(x,\:\theta\:\right)-\:\frac{\pi\:}{2}\right)\right]$$\end{document}

According to the parameter-shift rule, the parameter-wise gradient in specific quantum circuits can be obtained by evaluating the circuit at two shifted parameter values and taking the difference of the resulting outputs. In every training epoch, a mini-batch of input–output pairs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:x,y$$\end{document}) is drawn from the dataset and passed to the IQLSTM. The predicted output (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y$$\end{document}out) is then compared with the original input to compute the loss function L. Subsequently, gradients are reset to 0, and the learnable parameters θ are optimized via backpropagation based on the computed loss. This iterative process continues across epochs until convergence is achieved. The structured sequence of steps for IQLSTM is written in Algorithm 1.

Modelling process of the proposed approach

This section provides details on the initial hyperparameter settings and outlines the procedures of the proposed model. After data normalization, the SVMD algorithm is initially applied to decompose the original solar-irradiance data series into its underlying intrinsic modes. One of the key parameters in SVMD is α, which defines the frequency range of the intrinsic modes. If α is set too high, it can produce incorrect modes that are actually just noise, or it might cause issues with convergence. Conversely, if α is too low, it can lead to mode mixing, where multiple distinct modes are merged into one. As a result, selecting an appropriate value for α requires a careful balance. To eliminate the difficulty of selecting an appropriate α value, the parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upalpha\:}}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upalpha\:}}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}}$$\end{document} are predefined. Moreover, the optimal varies significantly depending on the type of signal being decomposed. Therefore, a heuristic approach inspired by SVMD is proposed. During the iteration for extracting modes at the Lth stage, α is gradually increased in an exponential manner from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upalpha\:}}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upalpha\:}}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}}$$\end{document} using a logarithmic progression. The logarithmic variation preserves computational efficiency while providing effective coverage of a large parameter space. This effectively serves as a process of identifying the most dominant modes in the residual signal, progressing from a coarse to a fine resolution. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{1\:}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{2\:}$$\end{document} serve as the convergence thresholds for the inner and outer loops, respectively. When the normalized difference between subsequent updates meets a particular threshold, the inner iteration is terminated as c1 < \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{1\:}$$\end{document} providing that the extracted modes converge. When the total energy of all extracted modes meets the criteria c2 < \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{2\:}$$\end{document}, the outer iteration is terminated (SVMD flowchart, Fig. 1). The algorithm stops searching when the total energy of all Lth scale modes falls below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{2}$$\end{document}, indicating that the remaining modes can be considered as noise. In the final step, all extracted modes are arranged in ascending order based on their center frequencies. The analyzed dataset is characterized by significant nonlinearity, intermittent patterns, and multi-scale temporal variations. Accordingly, the minimum and maximum values of the parameter α are selected as 10 and 20,000 to ensure effective representation of these dynamics38,39. The convergence thresholds are empirically determined to be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{\epsilon\:}_{1\:}=\:{10}^{6}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{2\:}=\:{10}^{4},$$\end{document} which are frequently used in iterative decomposition techniques for achieving an equilibrium between computational efficiency and numerical stability.

After applying the SVMD method, the data sequence is decomposed into multiple intrinsic mode function (IMF) components. These components are then predicted using IQLSTM, and the final forecast is derived by combining the predictions of all individual components. The proposed quantum model uses 6 VQCs with 4 qubits and a circuit depth of 2, incorporating three rotation gates within the variational layer, along with 2 additional parameters for final scaling. The hidden state is configured with a dimensionality of 4. The learning rate is set to 0.002 for smooth updating of the parameters. Therefore, the number of parameters in IQLSTM is 146. To prevent overfitting, all models including the suggested IQLSTM will be trained over 50 epochs using early stopping. The mean squared error (MSE) loss function is computed and assessed at the end of each epoch. Finally, early stopping is triggered if the loss improvement remains below 10− 5 for five consecutive epochs. For comparison, experiments are also conducted with other specialized quantum models, including classical LSTM, QLSTM, QSVM, and QGRUs. The overall workflow is shown in Fig. 4. Furthermore, all the quantum-inspired models are trained using the same set of hyperparameters as mentioned in the baseline LSTM model to ensure a fair and consistent comparison. PyTorch and PennyLane are used to implement the suggested IQLSTM and other networks. The classical component of the network has been built using the PyTorch library and the quantum component has been implemented through the PennyLane framework. Initially, the IQLSTM model is trained and simulated under ideal conditions, assuming noise free quantum operations, assuming noise-free quantum operations. To approximate realistic quantum hardware conditions, additional experiments are conducted in PennyLane, where quantum noise is integrated into the circuit, resulting in a noisy IQLSTM model. These noise models emulate imperfections present in practical quantum processors. The quantum noise is simulated using built-in noise models in PennyLane to emulate realistic NISQ hardware conditions, as accessing real quantum devices is limited by hardware availability and queue constraints. The SIP approach was executed on a Python environment, utilizing a 64-bit system equipped with an Intel Core i5-9300 processor (2.4 GHz) and 8 GB of RAM and having MATLAB R2020b.

The effect of varying qubit counts on the predictive capability of IQLSTM is also examined. Despite the theoretical advantage of additional qubits in enhancing circuit expressiveness and forecasting ability, the observed gains are marginal. As shown in Fig. 13, a higher qubit count fails to produce notable improvements in results. From the figure, it is evident that with 4 qubits the proposed circuit achieves the highest accuracy and lowest loss, whereas at 8 qubits the model exhibits higher loss and reduced accuracy, followed by the cases of 5 and 10 qubits. This decline may be explained by the barren plateau phenomenon, a widely recognized issue in VQC. As the number of qubits grows, both circuit depth and parameter count expand, making the optimization landscape progressively flatter and leading to vanishing gradients47. As a result, the optimization process does not converge effectively, resulting in suboptimal model performance. This suggests that the number of qubits must be tuned properly to balance the expressibility of the circuit with the trainability of the quantum model. Hence, in this circuit the number of qubits is chosen as 4.

Ablation analysis of proposed model

To comprehensively assess the effectiveness of the proposed SVMD-IQLSTM framework, ablation experiments were performed by systematically removing or replacing individual components of the model. These experiments were conducted to evaluate the influence of different components, including SVMD, RVQC, and the CRX gate, on solar irradiance prediction performance. The January dataset obtained from Data Frame 1 was used for 30-minute and 1-hour ahead forecasting experiments, while the full-year dataset from Data Frame 2 was employed for 2-hour and 5-hour ahead forecasting of Dataset 1, serving as reference cases. The experimental findings show that integrating SVMD as a preprocessing step enhances the representation of solar irradiance data by decomposing the highly non-stationary time series into multiple intrinsic modes with distinct spectral properties. This multi-scale decomposition separates noise components from meaningful temporal patterns, allowing the recurrent prediction model to capture more informative and stable features. Consequently, the SVMD-LSTM model yields lower prediction errors than the baseline LSTM, and a similar improvement trend is observed for the SVMD-QLSTM, QLSTM, IQLSTM, and SVMD-IQLSTM models. Additionally, the effect of different entanglement gates was examined by comparing the parameterized CRX gate with the conventional CNOT gate. The CRX gate provides tunable rotational parameters that enable adaptive control of qubit entanglement during training. This flexibility enhances the expressive capability of the quantum circuit and improves feature extraction compared to fixed entanglement operations such as CNOT and reduces the error. Moreover, integrating a QLSTM architecture with the RVQC improves the model’s nonlinear representation capability. By leveraging quantum superposition and entanglement, the circuit maps input features into a higher-dimensional Hilbert space, enabling better capture of complex temporal dependencies in solar irradiance data which reduces the error. Overall, the SVMD-IQLSTM model achieves the lowest prediction errors in terms of RMSE and MAE across all forecasting horizons (30 min, 1 h, 2 h, and 5 h) as shown in the Table 10. These results indicate that the integration of SVMD, RVQC, and the CRX gate effectively enhances the accuracy and robustness of SIP.

Theoretical analysis of quantum advantage

The incorporation of variational quantum circuits (VQCs) into classical neural network architectures has recently attracted considerable attention as a potential approach for improving model expressiveness and enhancing feature representation in complex learning tasks. In the proposed framework, the IQLSTM model integrates a RVQC to strengthen the representation of nonlinear temporal dynamics inherent in solar irradiance data. The potential advantage of the quantum component arises from several properties of quantum computation, including high-dimensional Hilbert space representations, quantum entanglement, and enhanced circuit expressibility.

When classical input features are encoded into quantum states, the data are projected into a higher-dimensional feature space, enabling the quantum circuit to model complex nonlinear relationships that may be difficult for classical neural networks to capture efficiently. This capability is particularly beneficial for solar irradiance prediction, where the data often exhibit strong nonlinearity and non-stationary characteristics due to atmospheric variations.

Another factor contributing to the enhanced representational capability of the proposed architecture is the incorporation of controlled rotation gates, specifically CRX gates, within the RVQC structure. These gates introduce entanglement between qubits, enabling the circuit to learn correlations among encoded input features and improving its ability to capture complex dependencies in time-series data. Furthermore, the expressibility of the quantum circuit is enhanced by increasing the circuit depth through multiple layers of parameterized rotation and entanglement operations. The expressibility of the circuit is evaluated using the KLD between the probability distribution generated by the circuit and the Haar-random distribution, where lower KLD values indicate higher expressibility and improved coverage of the quantum state space.

Scalability and practical deployment

Validating the scalability of proposed model across different renewable energy domains, such as wind and hydropower forecasting, as well as in various industrial forecasting applications, will help demonstrate their adaptability, versatility, and robustness. The preprocessing stage based on SVMD decomposes the original time-series data into a finite set of intrinsic modes, which can be processed independently. This characteristic improves computational efficiency and facilitates scalability when dealing with large datasets or extended forecasting horizons. Moreover, the prediction component built upon QLSTM incorporates a compact variational quantum circuit with a limited number of qubits, thereby maintaining manageable computational complexity. The integration of a RVQC with parameterized entanglement using the CRX gate further enhances feature representation while avoiding the need for deep quantum circuits, making the model more compatible with current NISQ-era quantum devices. In practical deployment, the framework can operate within a hybrid quantum–classical architecture where the quantum circuit is executed on quantum hardware or simulators, while the remaining computations are handled by classical processors. This hybrid configuration enables scalable model training and inference while ensuring reliable performance for real-time solar irradiance prediction.

The model utilizes four qubits with a shallow circuit depth of two layers, which limits the number of quantum gate operations and reduces overall computational overhead. The architecture also contains a relatively small number of trainable parameters (146), enabling efficient optimization while requiring minimal quantum resources. Such a lightweight design is well suited for implementation on current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum devices, where hardware limitations such as decoherence, gate errors, and restricted coherence time constrain the execution of deep quantum circuits. In quantum processors, qubits preserve their quantum states only for a finite duration, referred to as coherence time. Deep circuits involving many sequential gate operations increase execution time and lead to the accumulation of noise and gate imperfections. By maintaining a shallow circuit structure, the proposed architecture reduces the number of quantum operations and shortens circuit execution time, thereby mitigating the effects of decoherence and improving the reliability of computations on NISQ hardware.

Although the proposed model yields encouraging results in theoretical evaluations, several inherent challenges remain in its practical implementation. The realization of the quantum machine learning model is limited by the high cost and constrained accessibility of existing quantum hardware platforms. Current quantum computing infrastructures require ultra-low-temperature environments to maintain qubit coherence, resulting in significant reliance on costly cryogenic cooling mechanisms. Moreover, quantum chip fabrication is characterized by exorbitant costs and stringent precision requirements, thereby constraining the scalability and accessibility of quantum hardware.

Conclusion and future scope

Conclusion

High predictive accuracy and efficiency not only enhance the practical applicability of the QLSTM model but also substantially improve its reliability and effectiveness in complex scenarios. In this study, an enhanced computing framework based on the QLSTM model (IQLSTM), integrated with a successive VMD technique was proposed for solar irradiance prediction. Real-world datasets from Hyderabad (2019) and Bengaluru (2019, 2018) with various predicting horizons and weather conditions were used to validate the model. The experimental results demonstrated significant gains in prediction performance, evidenced by higher R values and lower RMSE, MAPE, and MAE scores across multiple solar irradiance datasets. The analysis further indicates that the SVMD algorithm effectively reduces data volatility and redundancy, enabling superior decomposition of the sample data and thereby improving the predictive accuracy of the model. The IQLSTM model employs angle embedding along with variational quantum circuits (VQCs) featuring circularly entangled controlled-X (CRX) gates in a bidirectional configuration. This design allows for efficient handling of complex data while reducing computational overhead. The primary aim of this study is to design a resource-efficient variational quantum circuit by analysing the trade-off between circuit expressibility and complexity across different topologies and gate configurations, strategically replacing certain conventional neural network components, and implementing the QLSTM model within a hybridized quantum–classical architecture for efficient SIP. Several experiments were conducted to optimize the number of qubits, select appropriate gate types, and design the embedding scheme, all aimed at maximizing the accuracy of the proposed quantum circuit. The results indicate that the IQLSTM model consistently outperforms classical LSTM and other quantum models such as QLSTM, QSVM, and QGRUs, by attaining reduced error rates and improved accuracy across training and forecasting phases. Moreover, the model maintains stable performance under noisy quantum environments, highlighting its robustness and suitability for practical NISQ-era implementations. Overall, the suggested SVMD-IQLSTM architecture offers a scalable and dependable short-term solar irradiance forecasting solution that promotes effective energy management and grid stability. Further the non-parametric Friedman test with Dunn-Sidak procedure confirms the significant difference of the proposed method from the other considered methods. The reported results obtained from the test identify the proposed method to be the superior method among all methods.

Future scope

Although this study includes noise-aware simulations to approximate realistic quantum hardware conditions, further investigation is still required. Future work will focus on evaluating the proposed model using more detailed hardware-calibrated noise models and experiments on real quantum processors to better understand the effects of decoherence, gate errors, and measurement noise. Additionally, advanced error-mitigation strategies and improved hybrid quantum–classical architectures could be explored to further enhance prediction performance on Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum devices.
