The protein phosphatase 6 subunit SAPS3 modulates lifespan by regulating metabolism
Authors: Ying Yang, Da Eun Kang, Qi Fan, Eric A. Hanse, Hosung Bae, Rocio A. Barahona, Xiyu Shen, Bryan I. Ruiz, Shelly H. Lee, Katherine R. Pasterczyk, Nainpriya Babbar, Katherine G. Waldvogel, Roberto Tinoco, Kim N. Green, Qin Yang, Cholsoon Jang, Mei Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aging is characterized by disruptions in metabolic homeostasis, yet the mechanisms that regulate these metabolic changes remain poorly understood. We show that the serine/threonine–protein phosphatase 6 (PP6) regulatory subunit 3, SAPS3, is a critical regulator of metabolism during aging. SAPS3 deletion significantly extends lifespan in mice and counteracts age-related impairments in metabolic health. SAPS3 deficiency improves the effects of aging on the affective behaviors, cognition, and motor functions in aged mice. We find that SAPS3 expression is increased during aging to inhibit adenosine monophosphate–activated kinase (AMPK) activity. Deletion of SAPS3 leads to AMPK activation and reverses cellular senescence and aging-induced metabolic alterations. Using in vivo U-13C6-D-glucose tracing and metabolomic analysis, we find that SAPS3 deficiency restores metabolic homeostasis with increased glycolysis, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and decreased fatty acid synthesis in aged mice. These findings highlight a critical role of the SAPS3/PP6 phosphatase complex in aging and suggest that strategies targeting SAPS3 may promote longevity and healthy aging.

INTRODUCTION

Aging is the gradual deterioration of physiological functions over time. With increasing longevity, the population of older individuals is growing substantially, facing challenges such as hearing loss, cataracts, refractive errors, back and neck pain, osteoarthritis, diabetes, depression, and dementia. Consequently, there is an increased focus on promoting healthy aging (1, 2), leading to efforts to identify factors that contribute to well-being and alleviate the effects of aging (3, 4).

Metabolism plays a crucial role in the aging process, with a growing body of evidence highlighting that metabolic homeostasis deteriorates with age (5–7). As organisms age, various physiological and molecular changes occur that affect metabolism. For instance, aging is often associated with increased insulin resistance, which disrupts glucose homeostasis, leading to higher blood sugar levels and an increased risk of metabolic diseases (8). Aging is also associated with changes in the distribution and function of adipose tissue (9). For example, there is an increase in visceral fat, which is more metabolically active and can release proinflammatory cytokines, contributing to metabolic dysfunction (10). In addition, cellular pathways that sense and respond to nutrients become dysregulated with aging. Overactivity of these pathways can lead to anabolic resistance and impaired autophagy (11). Despite the complexity of these interactions, the primary molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways underlying the control of metabolic homeostasis during aging remain elusive.

Adenosine monophosphate–activated kinase (AMPK), an evolutionarily conserved energy sensor, has been previously established as the key regulator in managing aging-related metabolic homeostasis (12). Energy deficiency leads to adenosine triphosphate depletion, which activates AMPK signaling. This activation stimulates catabolic processes that maintain energy homeostasis by turning off energy-consuming reactions, including protein and lipid synthesis (13–15). Substantial evidence underscores that AMPK, along with its homologs sucrose nonfermenting 1 in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and 5′-AMP-activated protein kinase catalytic subunit alpha-2 (AAK-2) in Caenorhabditis elegans, is a critical regulator of the aging process. These molecules have been proven to extend lifespan in a variety of models, including yeast (16), C. elegans (17–19), and Drosophila (20, 21). Moreover, AMPK-mediated signaling has been shown to enhance lifespan in mice (22, 23). Notably, research across various models suggests that the responsiveness of AMPK signaling declines with aging (24–26). Therefore, AMPK is a crucial target involved in the regulation of metabolic homeostasis, enhanced stress resistance, improved health span, and extended lifespan through the integrated signaling network. Targeting AMPK activation has the potential to mitigate age-related diseases, such as metformin (23) and rapamycin (27). However, potential mechanisms suppressing AMPK activation over aging remain a critical question.

Despite a growing body of evidence connecting protein phosphatases to aging, their precise roles in the aging process and the molecular mechanisms by which they influence aging are largely unknown. We previously demonstrated that protein phosphatase 6 (PP6) regulatory subunit 3, SAPS3, is a negative regulator of AMPK that responds to metabolic stress induced by overnutrition (28). SAPS3 is one of three PP6 regulatory subunits and plays a critical role in determining PP6 substrate specificity (29, 30). We reported that SAPS3 recruits the PP6 catalytic subunit to dephosphorylate and deactivate AMPK upon metabolic stress, consequently affecting AMPK-controlled pathways (28). Given the critical role of AMPK in the regulation of age-related metabolism, whether SAPS3 plays a major role in aging is unknown. In this study, we demonstrate that the deletion of SAPS3 in mice significantly extends lifespan and promotes metabolic health over aging. SAPS3 deficiency also reduces aging-associated physiological decline and cognitive deficits and attenuates cellular senescence. We further investigated the mechanism of SAPS3 in aging-associated phenotypes and found that SAPS3 is up-regulated in both aged male and female mice and the loss of SAPS3 increases AMPK activity during aging and restores metabolic homeostasis. Therefore, our results indicate that targeting SAPS3 may promote metabolic health and longevity, making it a promising approach for preventing and treating age-related diseases.

RESULTS

Deletion of SAPS3 extends lifespan in both male and female mice

Aging disrupts metabolic homeostasis, leading to a progressive decline in both cellular and organismal function. AMPK is a central regulator of energy homeostasis and has been implicated in the control of lifespan across multiple organisms. Increased AMPK activity promotes cellular processes linked to longevity, and previous studies have shown that SAPS3 negatively regulates AMPK activation (28). This suggests that SAPS3 might act as a physiological inhibitor of AMPK during aging. Here, to explore whether SAPS3-regulated metabolism affects lifespan, we assessed two cohorts of SAPS3 knockout (KO) and wild-type (WT) mice, involving 162 mice (80 males and 82 females). The first cohort comprised male mice, where the log-rank test analysis showed significant differences in the survival curves between WT and KO mice. Compared to male WT littermates, male KO mice showed a 13% increase in median lifespan and a 17.7% increase in mean lifespan (log-rank test, χ2 = 7.037, df = 1, and P = 0.008) (Fig. 1A). In the second cohort of female mice, similar significant differences in survival curves were observed through log-rank test analysis. Relative to WT littermates, female KO mice exhibited a 12% increase in median lifespan and a 17.4% increase in mean lifespan (log-rank test, χ2 = 11.63, df = 1, and P = 0.001) (Fig. 1B). Moreover, the maximum lifespan (mean lifespan of the oldest 10% of a cohort to die) (31) increased 20% (P = 0.038) in males and 23% (P = 0.009) in females with SAPS3 KO compared to WT mice.

Deletion of SAPS3 extends the lifespan in both male and female mice.

Kaplan-Meier curves of male (A) and female (B) WT and KO mice (male mice, n = 40 per group; female mice, WT group: n = 40 and KO group: n = 42 mice; log-rank test).

These results suggest that SAPS3 deficiency leads to a significant extension of median lifespan in both sexes. In addition, the maximum lifespan was also increased for both male and female SAPS3 KO mice compared to their WT counterparts, indicating that the deletion of SAPS3 contributes to enhanced longevity.

SAPS3 deficiency promotes metabolic health during aging

Aging is known to promote disruption of glucose metabolism (6, 32). Thus, we investigated whether SAPS3 deletion affects aging-related glucose metabolism. Consistent with our previous study showing that KO of SAPS3 had no significant effects on mice under balanced conditions (28), there were no differences in the body weight between SAPS3 KO and WT young mice. In middle age, male mice still exhibit no significant difference between SAPS3 KO and WT groups; however, female mice began to show differences, with SAPS3 KO females maintaining a body weight similar to young mice. After 20 months of age, SAPS3 KO mice had a significant reduction in age-related body weight gain in both males and females (Fig. 2A). Notably, both WT and KO mice demonstrated similar food intake levels across ages (fig. S1A).

Next, we analyzed fat mass and lean mass composition in male and female WT and SAPS3 KO mice. While there were no significant differences when the mice were young, both male and female SAPS3 KO mice showed reduced fat mass compared to WT mice starting from middle age, which becomes more significantly pronounced in older mice. In addition, while male KO mice showed a significant increase in lean mass at an older age, female KO mice demonstrated significantly higher lean mass than WT mice beginning in middle age and continuing into old age (Fig. 2B). We measured physiological outputs in these mice and found that aged SAPS3 KO mice showed significantly lower serum triglyceride levels than WT, consistent with reduced adiposity (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, aged KO mice had reduced fasting blood glucose levels compared to WT mice (Fig. 2D). Analysis of glucose homeostasis through glucose tolerance test (GTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT) revealed compromised glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity in aged WT male mice. In contrast, aged SAPS3 KO mice demonstrated more efficient glucose clearance and improved insulin sensitivity compared to their WT controls (Fig. 2E and fig. S1B). Metabolic cage analyses showed that both oxygen consumption (VO2) and carbon dioxide production (VCO2) were significantly elevated in aged KO mice compared to their age-matched WT counterparts (Fig. 2, F and G), indicating higher rates of cellular respiration and increased energy expenditure in KO mice. These results suggest that SAPS3 deletion in aged mice promotes a metabolic phenotype similar to that of young mice.

SAPS3-deficient mice exhibit improvements in behavior, cognition, and motor function

Aging is characterized by increased anxiety, memory decline, and motor impairments (33). In addition to assessing metabolic health, we conducted a parallel study to investigate whether SAPS3 KO might have broader impacts on healthy aging, particularly in relation to neurological behavior. SAPS3 KO mice were compared to WT mice using a battery of tasks, including the open field (OF) and elevated plus maze (EPM) (assessing motor function and anxiety), new object recognition (NOR) (assessing cortical learning and memory), rotarod and wheel running (assessing motor function), and Y-maze (assessing spatial memory).

In all tests, young SAPS3 KO mice showed no difference compared to WT mice, indicating that the KO of SAPS3 does not affect behavior in young mice. However, in the OF, old WT mice displayed significantly decreased travel distance and time compared to young mice, along with increased nonmovement time. The deletion of SAPS3 reversed this anxiety behavior in aged mice (Fig. 3A) with no difference in average speed (fig. S2A). In the EPM test, aged KO mice spent more time in the open arm, indicative of reduced anxiety, while WT mice spent more time in the closed arm (Fig. 3B). KO mice also spent significantly more time exploring a new object compared to WT mice (Fig. 3C). While all groups showed increased average time on the beam over time, demonstrating learning ability, old WT mice had the shortest time spent on the beam, across the five trials. On day 5, aged SAPS3 KO mice showed a time similar to that of young mice (Fig. 3D). Young WT and SAPS3 KO mice exhibited equivalent levels of wheel running activity. In addition, old SAPS3 KO mice showed significantly enhanced wheel running activity compared to WT mice (Fig. 3E). The Y-maze test demonstrated that SAPS3 KO mice had a higher curiosity for exploration and longer distances traveled compared to WT mice (Fig. 3F). As a control, total entries and speeds in the maze did not differ significantly among the groups (fig. S2, B to D). These results suggest that lifelong loss of SAPS3 improves anxiety, mobility, and cognitive impairments associated with aging.

SAPS3 deficiency attenuates the senescent phenotype

To assess the impact of SAPS3 deletion at a cellular level, we used mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells from WT and SAPS3 KO mice and assessed for cell proliferation and β-galactosidase (β-Gal) expression via senescence-associated β-Gal (SA-β-Gal) staining, which serves as a marker for cellular senescence (34). KO cells showed slightly higher proliferative capacity compared to WT cells (Fig. 4A), and KO MEFs showed significantly lower SA-β-Gal activity compared to WT primary MEFs, suggesting that deletion of SAPS3 delays cellular senescence (Fig. 4B).

(B) Representative images of SA-β-Gal staining in WT (passage 12) and KO MEFs (passage 14). Scale bar, 400 μm. The percentage of stained cells is shown. Another well-established marker of cellular senescence is increased expression of the cell cycle inhibitor p21, which leads to irreversible growth arrest (35). We found no significant difference in p21 mRNA expression in the liver in young mice, whereas aged WT mice exhibited increased hepatic p21 mRNA levels in both males and females. In contrast, aged SAPS3 KO males and females maintained low levels of p21 mRNA, similar to those observed in young mice (Fig. 4C). Consistently, p21 protein levels were elevated in liver samples from aged WT mice but not in SAPS3 KO mice (Fig. 4D). Together, aged SAPS3 KO mice exhibited a substantial suppression of p21 expression, suggesting that SAPS3 inhibits senescence in aged cells and tissues.

SAPS3 expression increases during aging and inhibits AMPK activity

To determine the mechanisms by which SAPS3 deficiency influences aging phenotypes, we compared SAPS3 expression levels in young and old mice. We observed an age-related increase in SAPS3 expression at both the mRNA (Fig. 5A) and protein levels (Fig. 5B), while SAPS1 and SAPS2, the other members of the PP6 regulatory subunits, did not exhibit these changes. Aged mice showed reduced phosphorylated AMPK (p-AMPK) and downstream phosphorylated acetyl–coenzyme A (CoA) carboxylase (p-ACC) levels, consistent with previous reports (26), suggesting that increased SAPS3 during aging facilitates AMPK deactivation (Fig. 5B).

Given that SAPS3 recruits PP6 catalytic subunit (PP6C) and activates PP6, we evaluated PP6C activity in liver samples from young and old mice. While PP6 activity did not differ between WT and KO mice in young cohorts, aging induced an increase in PP6 phosphatase activity in WT mice, which was significantly suppressed in aged KO mice (fig. S3A). Next, we investigated whether increased SAPS3 expression and PP6 activity contribute to AMPK suppression in aged mice. In young mice, we found that KO of SAPS3 had no effect on AMPK phosphorylation, consistent with our previous finding that SAPS3 is specifically responsive to stress conditions in regulating AMPK. We found that SAPS3 KO reactivated AMPK in liver samples from aged mice compared to their WT counterparts (Fig. 5C and fig. S3B), but not in the brain, muscle, or spleen (fig. S3, C to E). While SAPS3 expression and AMPK phosphorylation show modest changes during aging in the lung, p-AMPK and p-ACC were increased in aged KO mice compared to WT mice (fig. S3F), suggesting that the SAPS3-AMPK axis may not be a primary driver of natural aging but that forced deletion of SAPS3 can activate AMPK signaling and may contribute to enhanced metabolic homeostasis and lifespan extension in the lung. We further examined AMPK-regulated signaling pathways in the liver and found that aged KO mice showed a significant increase in phosphorylated Raptor (p-Raptor) (36), a downstream target of AMPK. Unexpectedly, we did not observe an inhibition of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, including phosphorylated mTOR (p-mTOR), phosphorylated p70 ribosomal S6 kinase (p-p70S6K), and phosphorylated eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E–binding protein (p-4E-BP1) in aged KO mice relative to WT mice, suggesting that SAPS3-AMPK–regulated aging phenotypes may operate independently of the mTOR pathway. Moreover, we did not find an increase in autophagy pathways in aged KO mice compared to WT mice, including phosphorylation of Unc-51-like autophagy-activating kinases 1 (p-ULK1) (37), and the level of microtubule-associated protein light chain 3 (LC3-II). In addition, we also assessed the activity of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) kinase (MEK) pathway in livers and found no significant changes between SAPS3 KO and WT mice (Fig. 5C).

To further investigate whether AMPK activation is a major mediator for the phenotypes we found in SAPS3 KO cells, we performed AMPKα1/α2 knockdown using short hairpin RNA (shRNA) in both WT and KO MEFs. The efficiency of AMPK knockdown was confirmed by immunoblotting (Fig. 5D). SA-β-Gal staining showed that AMPK knockdown reversed the protective effect of SAPS3 deficiency on cellular senescence (Fig. 5E). These results demonstrate up-regulation of SAPS3 expression in aged mice, and KO of SAPS3 mitigates age-associated declines by maintaining higher levels of p-AMPK.

SAPS3 deficiency reverses aging-induced metabolic alterations in serum

Aging leads to systemic changes in metabolism that alter serum metabolites and contribute to various age-associated conditions. To further assess how SAPS3 deletion and AMPK activation influence metabolic status over aging, we conducted liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis using serum samples from young WT and SAPS3 KO and old WT and SAPS3 KO mice. Aging induced significant changes in serum metabolites in WT mice (Fig. 6A). The analysis of metabolic pathways during aging revealed substantial changes in lipid-related metabolic pathways, such as arachidonic acid, linoleic acid, and sphingolipid metabolism (Fig. 6B). Further, SAPS3 KO did not significantly alter metabolites in young mice, consistent with previous phenotypic data (Figs. 2 and 3) and AMPK status (Fig. 5D). In contrast, deletion of SAPS3 induced substantial changes to metabolites in old SAPS3 KO mice compared to WT mice, with many of the alterations observed in aged WT mice being reversed by loss of SAPS3 (Fig. 6C). Among the 52 metabolites significantly altered in aged WT mice, 28 metabolites were up-regulated, and 24 were down-regulated compared to young mice. In the up-regulated metabolites, 20 (72%) were completely reversed, and 4 (14%) were partially reversed. In the down-regulated metabolites, 15 (63%) and 6 (25%) were completely or partially reversed, respectively. Overall, we identified 45 (87%) of 52 metabolites that were altered by aging that were reversed by SAPS3 deletion, suggesting that SAPS3 is a gatekeeper for the metabolic changes caused by aging (Fig. 6D).

We also identified key biomarkers associated with aging and age-related diseases. For instance, cyclic phosphatidic acid (CPA; 18:0/0:0), associated with cancers (38) and chronic diseases (39), was significantly decreased in KO mice (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, we observed a reversal of hyodeoxycholic acid in aged SAPS3 KO mice, which is associated with longevity (Fig. 6E) (40). Together, our findings indicate that SAPS3 KO mice exhibit a healthier metabolic profile compared to WT mice as they age, suggesting a protective role of SAPS3 deficiency in reducing age-related metabolic changes.

SAPS3 deficiency maintains metabolic homeostasis in the liver in aged mice

Liver metabolism plays a crucial role in the aging process (41). Age-related alterations in liver metabolism contribute to various aspects of the aging phenotype. As SAPS3 deletion stimulates AMPK activation, which enhances glycolysis and inhibits lipid synthesis in the liver, we examined metabolic pathways during aging in the livers of WT and KO mice using gas chromatography–MS (GC-MS) (Fig. 7A). We observed a decrease in glycolytic intermediates in aged WT mice compared to SAPS3 KO (Fig. 7B), consistent with less efficient glycolysis observed in aged mice (42). Glycolysis is closely linked to mitochondrial functions, as pyruvate generated from glycolysis enters the mitochondria for further metabolism through the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. The TCA cycle was significantly decreased in aged WT mice, exhibiting no change observed in young WT and KO mice. In contrast, old KO mice showed restored levels of TCA cycle intermediates, such as α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), fumarate, and malate (Fig. 7C).

Emerging evidence suggests that fatty acid metabolism plays an important role in aging-induced stress responses (43). During aging, lipid enzyme expression undergoes notable changes and is associated with different longevity pathways. We found that SAPS3 KO mice showed significantly lower levels of palmitate, stearate, oleate, and linoleate in the liver (Fig. 7D and fig. S4).

Having established that aged KO mice show a higher clearance of glucose (Fig. 2F), we directly measured the contribution of glucose to glycolysis and TCA cycle by providing young and old WT and KO mice with 13C6-glucose via continuous infusion for 2 hours and measuring the labeling of downstream metabolites in serum (Fig. 7E). The fraction of 13C6-glucose in plasma across different groups indicated no significant difference in infusion (Fig. 7F).

We observed significantly lower levels of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PG), pyruvate, and lactate (m + 3) derived from 13C6-glucose in old WT mice, suggesting a reduced contribution of glucose to glycolysis during the aging process. Young KO mice showed no difference in labeled glycolytic intermediates compared to WT mice. In contrast, old KO mice had higher levels of 3-PG, pyruvate, and lactate (m + 3), similar to young mice and significantly higher than old WT mice, indicating a preserved contribution of glucose to glycolysis in aged KO mice (Fig. 7G). α-KG (m + 2), fumarate (m + 2), and malate (m + 2) showed similar levels in young WT and KO mice. However, in aged WT mice, these three metabolites were lower than in young mice, and KO mice could reverse this decrease in TCA cycle intermediates (Fig. 7H). Thus, these data suggest that the deletion of SAPS3 reverses the altered liver metabolism under aging-induced metabolic stress.

DISCUSSION

Aging is increasingly recognized as a form of metabolic stress that our bodies encounter over time (44). As we age, cellular and organ function undergo changes influenced by environmental factors, genetic modifications, and lifestyle choices. This cumulative metabolic stress affects various physiological processes and contributes to age-related conditions. Understanding the mechanism underlying aging is crucial for promoting healthy aging and improving quality of life (45, 46). On the basis of our previous studies demonstrating that SAPS3 is a regulator of metabolic stress and plays a critical role in managing cellular responses to metabolic challenges, we hypothesized that SAPS3 deficiency would promote lifespan extension and enhance metabolic health during aging. We determined the effect of SAPS3 deletion using whole-body KO C57BL/6J mice compared to WT mice. Our results showed that SAPS3 deficiency increased the lifespan of male and female mice by 13 and 12%, respectively (Fig. 1). Furthermore, old SAPS3-deficient mice maintained a metabolic homeostasis close to that of the young mice.

We previously reported that SAPS3 is a negative regulator of AMPK and that most of the SAPS3 KO induced metabolic phenotypes can be reversed by AMPK inhibition, suggesting that the aging phenotype observed in SAPS3 KO mice is largely mediated by AMPK activation. Consistent with our investigation that SAPS3 protein levels increase during aging, a progressive decline in AMPK activity with age has been previously reported (47), and strategies that lead to AMPK activation can extend the lifespan (26). AMPK controls the aging progression via different mechanisms. For example, in aged tissues, elevated p21 levels are closely associated with functional decline and cellular senescence. Previous studies have shown that AMPK activation reduces p21 expression in various cell types, potentially through the modulation of p53 signaling and autophagy pathways (48–50). By suppressing p21 expression, AMPK activation may protect cells from age-related cellular damage, which can be beneficial in delaying or reversing senescence. Our data demonstrate that although SAPS3 KO MEFs exhibit delayed senescence compared to WT controls, they are not immortalized and eventually undergo senescence. AMPK inhibition was able to reverse the reduced senescence phenotype in SAPS3 KO MEFs, indicating that SAPS3 may influence senescence through an AMPK-dependent pathway.

AMPK activation has been reported to facilitate longevity through the regulation of autophagy (20), stem cell maintenance, and mitochondrial biogenesis (51). Our data show that SAPS3 KO leads to increased levels of p-AMPK in aged mice. We found that p-Raptor is significantly elevated in the livers of SAPS3 KO mice, suggesting that the Raptor-regulated pathway may contribute to the SAPS3 KO–induced longevity. AMPK is known to suppress the mTOR pathway, and mTOR inhibition has been associated with the promotion of longevity (52, 53). However, we did not observe decreased mTOR pathway activity in SAPS3 KO mice, suggesting an mTOR-independent mechanism for promoting healthy aging through SAPS3. A study using short-lived killifish also suggested that AMPK-related healthy aging occurs through an mTOR-independent mechanism (54). We found that SAPS3 deletion did not lead to changes in the p-ULK1 or LC3-II levels in old mice, suggesting that SAPS3 deletion–induced longevity may not be related to autophagy. In addition, beyond AMPK, SAPS3 has been implicated in the dephosphorylation of MEK (55), yet in our study, the effects of SAPS3 KO were independent of the MEK pathway (Fig. 5C). Future work will be needed to elucidate the major pathways that can mediate SAPS3 KO–induced healthy aging.

In our study, we used SAPS3 constitutive KO mice to examine the effects of its deletion on aging-induced changes. Aging is a systemic and complex process that can be controlled by multiple organs. While no single organ solely regulates the aging process, our data support that liver plays a major role in SAPS3-mediated metabolic regulation (28). Consistently, liver plays a critical role in aging due to its central functions in metabolism, detoxification, and energy homeostasis (44). However, deletion of SAPS3 may affect metabolism in various organs, contributing to the aging phenotype. Skeletal muscle is a key factor in age-related frailty and sarcopenia (56, 57). In our behavioral experiments, we observed that aged KO mice were more active than WT mice on rotarod (Fig. 3D) and wheel running tests (Fig. 3E), suggesting that SAPS3 deletion in muscle may affect aging phenotypes. Structural and physiological changes in the brain often lead to behavioral impairments and an increased risk of neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders (58). However, our data show that the SAPS3-AMPK axis is not affected in the brain, muscle, or spleen in old mice, suggesting that SAPS3 KO–induced aging phenotypes may not arise directly in all organs. Unexpectedly, we found increased SAPS3 levels in the lungs of old mice, and SAPS3 KO led to elevated p-AMPK levels, indicating that the lung may also contribute to SAPS3 KO–regulated healthy aging.

In summary, our data suggest that SAPS3 primarily functions under metabolic stress conditions induced by aging, a critical factor in metabolic perturbations. Thus, SAPS3 deficiency acts as a guardian to maintain metabolic homeostasis. These findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the biological response to aging and show the potential of inhibiting SAPS3 function for the development of AMPK activators to treat metabolic syndromes and delay aging.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

The main objective of this study was to investigate the effect of SAPS3 deletion in mice on lifespan and metabolic health during aging. We used a genetic SAPS3 KO mouse model. To determine whether SAPS3-regulated metabolism influences lifespan, two cohorts of SAPS3 KO and WT mice were set up for lifespan testing. To evaluate whether SAPS3 deletion reduces aging-associated physiological decline, we measured body weight and fat composition and conducted GTT, ITT, and metabolic cage studies. For health assessment, a battery of behavioral tasks was performed. To explore the mechanisms behind these phenotypes, we used senescence assay and immunoblotting to evaluate AMPK activity, mTOR pathway, autophagy, and MEK pathway in multiple organs. Furthermore, to examine the effects of SAPS3 deletion and AMPK activation on metabolism, we performed LC-MS and GC-MS analyses. The sample sizes are explicitly stated in the figure legends.

Cell culture and transfection

SAPS3 KO and WT MEFs were generated from SAPS3 KO and WT C57BL/6 mice according to previously established protocols (28). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (10-017-CV, Corning) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (100-106, GeminiBio), penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml) (516106, Sigma-Aldrich).

Lentiviral particles were produced in 6-cm dishes by cotransfecting 293T cells with 2.5 μg of shRNA or control vector, 2.5 μg of pCMV-VSV-G, and 3.2 μg of pCMV-dR8.2 dvpr using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Invitrogen). TRC Lentiviral Mouse Prkaa1 shRNA (RMM4534-EG105787) and Prkaa2 shRNA (RMM4534-EG108079) were purchased from Horizon Discovery. Viral supernatants were collected at 36 and 48 hours posttransfection. Cells were infected with the virus supplemented with polybrene (10 μg/ml).

Animals and animal care

All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the approved protocols by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of California, Irvine (AUP-23-110), ensuring compliance with ethical standards with animal research. The WT and SAPS3 KO C57BL/6 mice were bred from Ppp6r3+/− mice. All mice were housed in a controlled environment with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle and provided ad libitum access to water and diet. Weekly body weight measurements were recorded, and body composition, including fat and lean mass, was assessed in conscious animals using a whole-body composition analyzer (EchoMRI). At the end of the experiments, mice underwent a 4-hour fasting period before being euthanized via CO2 inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation.

Glucose tolerance and insulin tolerance

Blood glucose levels were measured using an automated blood glucose reader (Accu-Chek, Roche). GTTs were conducted on mice following an overnight fast. Blood samples were collected immediately before and 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after intraperitoneal injection of glucose (1 g/kg of body weight). For ITTs, mice were fasted for 3 hours before being injected with insulin (0.5 U/kg of body weight; 91077C, Sigma-Aldrich). Blood glucose levels were assessed at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min postinjection.

Metabolic cage experiment

Energy expenditure was measured using the TSE PhenoMaster System. Mice were acclimated to the metabolic cages for 2 days before data collection. VCO2 and VO2 were recorded every 20 min over a period of 3 to 4 days. The levels were normalized to body weight.

Cell proliferation

Cell proliferation was measured using the MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] assay (M2128, Sigma-Aldrich) following the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 1000 cells per well were seeded in 100 μl of culture medium in 96-well plate. At the indicated time point, the culture medium was replaced with medium containing MTT (0.5 mg/ml). The plate was incubated at 37°C for 2 hours to allow for formazan crystal formation. Subsequently, the MTT solution was removed, and 100 μl of dimethyl sulfoxide was added to solubilize the crystals. The absorbance was measured using a plate reader (Accuris SmartReader 96) at 562 nm.

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, followed by reverse transcription reaction using the qScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (95047-100, Quanta Biosciences). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted in a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (95072, Quanta Biosciences). The PCR primers used were as follows. The PCR cycling parameters included an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s and annealing at 55°C for 30 s. Immunoblotting

Tissues were homogenized on ice in lysis buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 5 mM sodium fluoride, 5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 250 mM mannitol, and 1% (v/v) Triton X-100] supplemented with protease inhibitor complex (04693159001, Roche) and phosphatase inhibitors (1862495, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lysates were then boiled with NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (1771559, Invitrogen) containing 5% β-mercaptoethanol (M3148, Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min. Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto precast NuPAGE bis-tris gels (NP0321BOX, Life Technologies) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membrane (1620115, Bio-Rad) or polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (1620177, Bio-Rad). Immunoblotting was conducted with the following antibodies: anti–p-AMPK (ab133448, Abcam; 1:1000), anti-AMPK (5831, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti-ACC (3662, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti–p-ACC (3661, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti–β-Actin (A1978, Sigma-Aldrich; 1:5000), anti-SAPS3 (A300-971A, Bethyl; 1:1000), anti-p21 (ab188224, Abcam; 1:500), anti-p53 (32532, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti–p-p44/42 MAPK (4370, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti–p-MEK1/2 (9154, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti–p-p90RSK (11989, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti–p-Raptor (2083, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti–p-mTOR (5536, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti-mTOR (2983, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti–p-p70S6K (9234, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), anti–p-4E-BP1 (2855, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000), and anti–p-ULK1 (5869, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1000). The quantification of blot results was analyzed by ImageJ.

Triglyceride measurement

Protocol was followed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ab65336, Abcam). Briefly, standard dilutions or samples (adjusted to a volume of 50 μl with triglyceride assay buffer, with 5 μl of sample used) were loaded into plates. Subsequently, 50 μl of the reaction mixture was added to each standard, sample, and background control well. The plate was then mixed and incubated at room temperature for 60 min and protected from light. Following incubation, absorbance was measured using a plate reader at 570 nm.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry

Serum samples (5 μl) were extracted with 300 μl of ice-cold acetonitrile:methanol:water (40:40:20) solution. Following vortexing and centrifugation at 16,000g for 10 min at 4°C, 70 μl of supernatant was loaded into MS vials. Metabolites were analyzed by quadrupole-orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive Plus, Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) coupled to hydrophilic interaction chromatography via electrospray ionization. LC separation was on an Xbridge BEH amide column (2.1 mm by 150 mm, 2.5-μm particle size, 130-Å pore size; Waters) at 25°C using a gradient of solvent A (5% acetonitrile in water with 20 mM ammonium acetate and 20 mM ammonium hydroxide) and solvent B (100% acetonitrile). The flow rate was 150 μl/min. The LC gradient was as follows: 0 min, 90% B; 2 min, 90% B; 3 min, 75% B; 7 min, 75% B; 8 min, 70% B; 9 min, 70% B; 10 min, 50% B; 12 min, 50% B; 13 min, 25% B; 14 min, 20% B; 15 min, 20% B; 16 min, 0% B; 20.5 min, 0% B; 21 min, 90% B; 25 min, 90% B. Autosampler temperature was set at 4°C, and the injection volume of the sample was 3 μl. MS data were acquired in negative and positive ion modes with a full-scan mode from a mass/charge ratio of 70 to 830 with 140,000 resolution. Data were analyzed using the Compound Discoverer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) and MAVEN software. Metabolites were confirmed by authentic standards based on retention time, precursor ion, and tandem mass spectrum generated in-house and Human Metabolome Database. MetaboAnalyst was used to analyze substantially changed metabolites and generate the heatmap and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis (www.metaboanalyst.ca/).

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry

A total of 3 to 10 mg of tissue samples were cut on dry ice and submerged in precooled 80% methanol in high-performance LC–grade water. Homogenization was performed using a Precellys 24 homogenizer with a Precellys ceramic kit. For polar metabolites, norvaline was added as an internal standard to the samples. Following centrifugation at 17,000g at 4°C for 10 min, the supernatant was transferred to a new tube and dried using a speed vacuum. Subsequently, 50 μl of methoxyamine hydrochloride (10 mg/ml in pyridine; 226904, Sigma-Aldrich) was added and the mixture was incubated at room temperature overnight. This was followed by the addition of 100 μl of N-tert-Butyldimethylsilyl-N-methyltrifluoroacetamide (394882, Sigma-Aldrich), and the samples were incubated at 70°C for 1 hour. The processed samples were then transferred to GC vials and analyzed using Agilent 7820 A chromatography coupled with an Agilent 5977B mass spectrometer. For nonpolar metabolites, an equal volume of chloroform and 10 μl of 10 mM d31-C16:0 as an internal standard were added to the samples. After vortexing and centrifugation at 14,000g for 5 min, the chloroform phase was dried. Subsequently, the samples were derivatized to form fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) by adding 500 μl of 2% H2SO4 in methanol at 50°C for 2 hours, followed by addition of 100 μl of saline and 500 μl of hexane. After extraction, the hexane layer was dried and dissolved in 100 μl of hexane. These samples were analyzed using a select FAME column.

Behavioral assays

Noldus EthoVision software was used to track, record, and analyze animal behavior. The behavioral paradigms were conducted in the following sequence (59). Equipment was thoroughly cleaned before and in between every trial.

Elevated plus maze

In successive order, each mouse was placed in the center of an EPM, facing an open arm. Each mouse was tested for a duration of 5 min to evaluate anxiety levels. The total count of entries into open and closed arms, along with the duration spent in each arm, was calculated.

Open field

Mice were placed in a white box (33.7 cm in length by 27.3 cm in width by 21.6 cm in height) for a 5-min period to evaluate anxiety levels. The time spent in the center versus the perimeter of the arena was recorded, along with measurements of distance traveled and average speed. The arena used for OF was also used for novel object tasks.

New objective recognition

Mice were allowed to freely explore two identical small glass beakers. Twenty-four hours later, one of the familiar glass beakers was replaced with a plastic building block, and the mice’s behavior was recorded for 3 min. The amount of time spent investigating the novel object was calculated using a discrimination index. This index was obtained by subtracting the time spent investigating the old object from the time spent investigating the novel object and then dividing by the total exploration time.

Y-maze

In the symmetric Y-maze with arms labeled A, B, and C, mice were positioned facing the back wall of an arm (alternating the starting arm for each test animal, so that each arm is used the same number of times throughout testing to avoid bias). Each arm measures 40 cm in length, 8 cm in width, and 20 cm in height. Mice were then given 5 min to freely explore the maze. Alternation, known as a triad, is defined as entry into all three arms during consecutive choices (i.e., ABC, CAB, or BCA, but not BAB). Spontaneous alteration was quantified by counting the number of times the mouse entered each of the three arms of the maze consecutively and dividing this by the maximum number of possible alterations.

Rotarod

Motor performance and motor learning were assessed using an accelerating rotarod with six lanes (Ugo Basile). Each mouse was weighed and positioned on the rotarod beam, which accelerated from 8 to 40 rpm over a maximum duration of 5 min. The latency to fall was automatically recorded via a sensor on the rotarod apparatus. Five trials were conducted per mouse on 5 consecutive days.

Wheel-running activity

Total wheel-running activity counts of mice were performed by placing one mouse in an individual cage with a running wheel (10000987, Fisher Scientific) and housed in the animal room for 3 consecutive days. The activity counts were recorded every day.

Tracing with 13C6-glucose

Mice were fasted for 6 hours before infusion. Infusion of 13C6-glucose (CLM-1396-PK, Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) was administered through tail vein catheters over a 2-hour period (60). The infusion rate for 13C6-glucose was set at 30 mg/kg per minute. Throughout the entire infusion, mice were maintained under 2% isoflurane anesthesia on warm pads. Tissues were dissected within 3 to 5 min following the completion of the infusion.

Senescence assay

Protocol was followed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (KAA002, Sigma-Aldrich). Briefly, the cell medium was removed, and cells were rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline. Cells were then fixed with 0.5 ml of fixative solution for 10 to 15 min at room temperature. Next, 0.5 ml of the staining solution mix was added to each well. The plate was covered and incubated at 37°C overnight. Last, the cells were observed under a microscope for the development of a blue color.

PP6 activity

Five micrograms of anti-PP6C antibody and 25 μg of Protein A agarose beads (16-125D, Invitrogen) were added to 300 μg of tissue lysate. The volume was adjusted to 500 μl with pNPP Ser/Thr assay buffer, and the mixture was incubated for 2 hours at 4°C with constant rotation. After that, the beads were washed three times with 700 μl of tris-buffered saline, followed by one wash with 500 μl of pNPP Ser/Thr assay buffer. Sixty microliters of diluted phosphopeptide (final reaction concentration will be 750 μM) and 20 μl of Ser/Thr assay buffer were added to the tube and incubated for 10 min at room temperature in a shaking incubator. After incubation, samples were briefly centrifuged. Twenty-five microliters of supernatant was transferred into each well of the ½ volume of microtiter plate. After 100 μl of malachite green phosphate detection solution was added into each well, the color was developed for 10 min at room temperature. The plate was measured using a plate reader (Accuris SmartReader 96) at 650 nm.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times, yielding consistent outcomes. Results are presented as means, with error bars representing SD, unless specified. Statistical significance between means was determined using either the unpaired two-tailed t test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA with Tukey or Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001), and calculations were performed using Microsoft Excel, GraphPad Prism (v 9) software, or R version 4.3.3 with the environment RStudio version 2024.9.0.
