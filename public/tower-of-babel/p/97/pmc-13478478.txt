miR-9394b from Lactobacillus paracasei extracellular vesicles targets iNOS to reprogram host NO-ornithine metabolism and ameliorate DSS-induced murine colitis
Authors: Honghao Ding, Heng Zhang, Diying Li, Zhiqi Hu, Guoqi Xiang, Qingqing Nie, Qibing Liu, Yunxiang Liang, Shuai Wang, Shiyu Tao, Yingjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a growing global health burden, creating an urgent need for new therapies. While extracellular vesicles (EVs) from probiotic bacteria show therapeutic potential, their molecular mechanisms are largely unknown. This study reveals that Lactobacillus paracasei (LPC) and its EVs (LPC-EVs) alleviate Dextran Sulfate Sodium Salt (DSS)-induced colitis in mice through a multi-faceted mechanism involving partial reshaping of the gut microbiota, suppression of TLR4/NF-κB signaling, and reinforcement of tight junctions. We found that miR-9394b carried by LPC-EVs plays a central role in this effect. In MODE-K cells, miR-9394b directly targets iNOS, shifting arginine metabolism away from nitric oxide (NO) production toward ornithine-associated metabolism. The increased ornithine served as a downstream mediator linking epithelial metabolic remodeling to macrophage polarization. Collectively, these findings support a model in which bacterial EVs mediate the transfer of miR-9394b to coordinate a metabolic-immune response, highlighting a potential avenue for miRNA-guided intervention in experimental colitis.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic and often refractory intestinal disorder whose global incidence has continued to rise over the last twenty years, with shifting epidemiological trends1. Hallmark clinical features include rectal bleeding, gut microbial dysbiosis, loss of mucosal immune homeostasis, and intestinal tissue injury2. Probiotic supplementation represents a promising therapeutic avenue for UC3,4. Evidence shows that Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG alleviates murine colitis by promoting IL-10 production in Ly6C⁺ monocytes and remodeling gut microbiota to enhance anti-inflammatory activity5. Additional strains, including Lactobacillus reuteri and Lactobacillus plantarum AR17-1, also confer protection in Dextran Sulfate Sodium Salt (DSS)-induced colitis by tempering pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and strengthening intestinal barrier function6,7. While these studies highlight the potential of specific lactic acid bacteria (LAB) in colitis management, the efficacy of individual probiotic strains is often inconsistent, and the identity of their active components and modes of action remain largely unknown.

In recent years, research focus has shifted from live probiotics to their functional components, among which bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) have garnered significant attention for their role in mediating bacteria–host crosstalk8,9. BEVs are nanoscale phospholipid bilayer structures (20–400 nm) secreted by probiotics, carrying diverse bioactive cargo, including proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites. They exhibit remarkable stability in the gastrointestinal environment, resist enzymatic degradation, and are efficiently internalized by host cells10–12. As an important functional component of probiotics, BEVs inherit multiple beneficial properties from their parental strains, offering promising opportunities for nanotherapeutic interventions in UC13–16. For instance, EVs derived from Lactobacillus paracasei (LPC) have been shown to mitigate LPS- and DSS-induced intestinal inflammation by modulating cytokine expression and activating the endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway17. Although the biological effects of probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles have been increasingly reported, the key bioactive cargo mediating these effects remains to be clearly defined, and their in vivo targets and molecular mechanisms are still incompletely understood. We further hypothesize that EV-induced metabolic remodeling of the host represents an important component of their protective activity, particularly the metabolic pathways closely linked to epithelial repair and barrier restoration, which remain underexplored.

To this end, this study set out to systematically investigate the mechanism by which Lactobacillus paracasei and its EVs alleviate colitis. We first screened four infant-derived Lactobacillus strains and identified LPC as the most potent. We then isolated its extracellular vesicles (LPC-EVs) and validated their efficacy. By integrating in vivo functional assays with non-targeted metabolomics and miRNA sequencing, we aimed to uncover the fundamental pathways through which LPC-EVs confer protection. This approach ultimately revealed a previously unrecognized role for EV-delivered miR-9394b in reprogramming the host Nitric Oxide (NO)-ornithine metabolic axis, a discovery that forms the core of this report.

Results

L. paracasei ameliorates DSS-induced colitis and restores intestinal barrier integrity via suppression of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of four Lactobacillus strains (L. plantarum (LP), L. rhamnosus (LR), LPC, and L. buchneri (LB)) isolated from breastfed infants, we assessed their efficacy in a DSS-induced colitis model. Mice were orally administered each strain during a 12-day experiment comprising a modeling phase (days 0–8) and a recovery phase (days 8–12) (Fig. 1A). During the modeling phase, all DSS-treated groups exhibited progressive weight loss, whereas the normal control (NC) group maintained stable growth, confirming successful colitis induction (Fig. 1B). By day 8, mice treated with LP or LR showed more severe weight loss than the model control (MC) group, whereas those receiving LPC, LB, or the positive control (PC) maintained higher body weights, indicating strain-specific protection against colitis progression. During the recovery phase, body weight trajectories diverged markedly. By day 12, the LPC group displayed a significantly higher body weight recovery and a lower disease activity index (DAI) than the MC group (Fig. 1B, C). Consistent with these clinical improvements, colon length was significantly preserved in the LPC group compared with the MC group (Fig. 1D, E). We next examined colonic tissue architecture and barrier integrity. Histological analysis revealed intact mucosa with abundant goblet cells in the NC group, whereas the MC group exhibited severe epithelial damage, inflammatory infiltration, and profound loss of goblet cells. In contrast, LPC administration markedly attenuated these pathological changes, resulting in only mild mucosal injury, reduced inflammation, significantly higher goblet cell counts, and improved histopathological scores (Fig. 1F, G). At the molecular level, LPC treatment significantly enhanced the mRNA expression of the tight junction proteins Occludin and ZO-1, as well as the mucin Muc2 (Fig. 1H), indicating robust reinforcement of the intestinal barrier. In line with these findings, LPC administration substantially reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) in both colon tissues and serum, while elevating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 1I, J). Given the central role of TNF-α and IL-6 in TLR4/NF-κB signaling, we probed this pathway by Western blot. The results showed that LPC significantly downregulated TLR4 and phosphorylated NF-κB p65 protein levels, while increasing IκBα expression (Fig. 1K, L), indicating suppression of the TLR4/NF-κB cascade. Taken together, these results demonstrate that LPC alleviates DSS-induced colitis by restoring intestinal barrier function, rebalancing cytokine profiles, and inhibiting TLR4/NF-κB–driven inflammation.

We next assessed whether the protective effects of LPC were associated with modulation of the gut microbiota. The Venn diagram depicts the overlap and unique operational taxonomic units (OTUs) among the groups, showing that the MC group harbored the lowest community richness, with only 502 OTUs detected (Supplementary Fig. 1). Systemic analysis revealed that DSS administration significantly reduced microbial richness, as reflected by the Chao index, whereas LPC treatment partially recovered community richness compared with the MC group (Fig. 2A). To explore potential functional relationships between microbial shifts and host parameters, we performed correlation analysis between bacterial genera and indicators of inflammation and barrier integrity. Beneficial genera such as Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Limosilactobacillus, and Dubosiella showed strong positive correlations with the barrier proteins Occludin and Muc2, and negative correlations with pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α). In contrast, Escherichia, Bacteroides, and Parabacteroides were positively associated with inflammatory markers and negatively linked to barrier components (Fig. 2E). These results suggest that LPC may alleviate colitis in part by promoting the expansion of beneficial taxa and inhibiting opportunistic pathogens, thereby partially shifting the gut microbiota toward a configuration associated with improved intestinal homeostasis, rather than fully restoring a normal microbiota state.

Taxonomic profiling at the phylum level showed that Bacteroidota and Firmicutes were the dominant phyla across groups. Compared to the MC group, LPC administration increased the relative abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteriota, while reducing that of Proteobacteria (Fig. 2C). At the genus level, the abundance of Lactobacillus was significantly higher in the LPC group than in the MC group (Fig. 2D). Differential abundance analysis further identified Bacteroides and Escherichia as enriched in the MC group, whereas Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Limosilactobacillus, and Clostridium were increased following LPC treatment (Fig. 2F, G).

Morphological, genomic, and extracellular vesicle characterization of LPC

We observed that only the LPC exhibited significant anti-inflammatory activity, whereas the other three Lactobacillus strains showed no comparable effects. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) revealed distinct budding structures on the surface of LPC, while the other strains displayed relatively smooth surfaces with only occasional protrusions. This morphological difference suggests that EVs may play a pivotal role in the biological effects of LPC (Fig. 3A). To further characterize LPC, we performed whole-genome sequencing (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 2A). The circular genome map revealed COG categories such as Cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis (M) and Extracellular structures (W), which are associated with membrane lipid synthesis, cell wall remodeling, and surface protein anchoring, thereby providing mechanistic clues for EVs budding and cell-wall traversal in Gram-positive bacteria (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses showed a high proportion of terms related to membranes and membrane transport, suggesting that LPC possesses a well-developed transmembrane transport system (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2B). To further identify the active components of LPC, LPC-EVs were isolated from the culture supernatant using a combination of differential and ultracentrifugation (Fig. 3D). FE-SEM analysis showed multiple vesicle-like protrusions on the surface of LPC cells, exhibiting typical membrane-derived structural characteristics (Fig. 3E). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further confirmed abundant vesicle-like structures within and around the bacterial membrane, and the purified LPC-EVs displayed the characteristic bilayered lipid membrane typical of bacterial vesicles (Fig. 3E). Moreover, no detectable extracellular vesicles were obtained from the other three Lactobacillus strains using the same isolation procedure.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) determined that the isolated vesicles had a mean diameter of 174.6 nm and a particle concentration of 7.1 × 10¹¹ particles/mL, confirming the successful isolation of intact EVs (Fig. 3F). Compositional analysis further revealed that LPC-EVs carry substantial amounts of nucleic acids and proteins, suggesting the presence of functionally active biomolecules (Fig. 3G). Together, these data confirm the efficient secretion of EVs by LPC and provide a structural basis for their subsequent functional evaluation.

To further evaluate the stability of LPC-EVs under in vivo gastrointestinal conditions and their intestinal distribution characteristics, we performed a vesicle-tracing experiment in DSS-induced colitis model mice. Whole-body imaging showed that, after oral gavage, the fluorescence signal remained predominantly localized to the abdominal gastrointestinal region from 0 to 24 h, indicating that LPC-EV-associated fluorescence could persist in the gastrointestinal tract for a defined period in vivo. Overall, the abdominal signal was more evident at 0 h and 6 h, gradually decreased thereafter, but remained detectable at 24 h, suggesting sustained in vivo persistence during gastrointestinal transit (Fig. 3H). Ex vivo imaging further demonstrated a clear gastrointestinal transit pattern after gavage: at 0 h, the fluorescence signal was mainly confined to the stomach; at 6 h, prominent signal was detected throughout the intestinal tract; at 12 h, the intestinal signal was reduced; and residual intestinal fluorescence was still detectable at 24 h. In contrast, fluorescence in the major organs was overall weak, with no obvious signal detected at 0–6 h, detectable signals appearing in the liver and kidney at 12 h, and residual signal persisting in the liver at 24 h. These findings indicate that orally administered LPC-EVs can persist in the gastrointestinal tract and undergo intestinal transit, reaching intestinal tissues within a defined time window, thereby supporting their relative stability and intestinal availability under in vivo gastrointestinal conditions (Fig. 3H). In addition, the weak signals detected in the liver and kidney at later time points suggest limited extraintestinal dissemination of LPC-EV-associated signals, although whether this reflects intact vesicle- or cargo-mediated delivery remains to be directly determined.

LPC-derived extracellular vesicles ameliorate DSS-induced colitis via inhibition of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway

To evaluate whether LPC-EVs recapitulate the protective effects of their parental strain, we administered purified EVs to DSS-induced colitis mice (Fig. 4A). LPC-EVs treatment attenuated body weight loss during the acute phase (days 0–8) and promoted faster recovery upon DSS withdrawal, culminating in a significantly higher body weight change rate than the MC group by day 12 (Fig. 4B). The DAI in the LPC-EVs group peaked on day 8 and declined thereafter, reaching a level significantly lower than that of the MC group by the end of the experiment (Fig. 4C). Macroscopic examination showed that colon length was better preserved in LPC-EVs-treated mice than in the MC group (Fig. 4D, E). Histological analysis further revealed that LPC-EVs mitigated inflammatory infiltration, preserved goblet cell numbers, and lowered histopathological scores (Fig. 4F, G). At the molecular level, LPC-EVs administration significantly reduced the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in both colon tissue and serum, while elevating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 4K, N). Concordantly, mRNA expression of the tight junction proteins Occludin and ZO-1, as well as the mucin Muc2, was markedly upregulated in the colon (Fig. 4H), indicating enhanced barrier integrity. LPC-EVs also increased the protein abundance of Occludin and ZO-1 in colonic tissues compared with the MC group, providing protein-level evidence that LPC-EVs preserve epithelial barrier structure in vivo (Fig. 4J, M). In addition, LPC-EVs suppressed the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, as reflected by decreased protein levels of TLR4 and phosphorylated NF-κB p65, together with increased IκBα expression (Fig. 4L, O). Collectively, these results demonstrate that LPC-EVs exert protective effects against DSS-induced colitis that are comparable to those of live LPC, mediated through the regulation of macrophage polarization, suppression of pro-inflammatory signaling, and restoration of intestinal barrier function.

Taxonomic profiling showed that LPC-EVs administration induced significant shifts in bacterial composition at multiple levels. At the phylum level, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria was significantly lower in the LPC-EVs group than in the MC group (Fig. 5D). At the genus level, we observed a marked enrichment of Akkermansia in the LPC-EVs group (Fig. 5E). Differential abundance analysis revealed significant enrichment of probiotic-associated taxa following LPC-EVs treatment, including butyrate-producing genera Ruminococcaceae and Eubacterium, along with Coriobacteriaceae and Turicibacter, while the MC group exhibited a notable increase in Escherichia (Fig. 5F, G). Additionally, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) identified Oscillospirales as a taxonomic biomarker that was significantly more abundant in the LPC-EVs group (Supplementary Fig. 3A–C). Collectively, these findings indicate that LPC-EVs alleviate DSS-induced intestinal dysbiosis by partially recovering microbial diversity, enriching beneficial bacterial taxa, and suppressing potential pathogens, thereby shifting the gut microbiota toward a more favorable configuration without fully restoring a normal microbiota state.

LPC-EVs reprogram host metabolism by modulating the ornithine-polyamine axis in DSS-induced colitis mice

To elucidate the metabolic mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of LPC-EVs, we conducted a non-targeted metabolomic analysis of colonic tissues. The score scatter plot revealed clear separations among the experimental groups, indicating distinct metabolic profiles induced by the interventions (Supplementary Fig. 4). Consistent with these global alterations, metabolomic profiling identified numerous differentially abundant metabolites among the groups (Fig. 6A). Specifically, LPC-EVs treatment significantly elevated ornithine levels and tended to increase the concentrations of polyamines such as spermidine and putrescine compared with the MC group, although the changes in spermidine and putrescine did not reach statistical significance. (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 5B). In contrast, the MC group exhibited marked accumulation of multiple arginine dipeptides (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 5A). These findings suggest that LPC-EVs redirect arginine metabolism toward ornithine and polyamine synthesis. Trend analysis of metabolite abundance across experimental groups identified 59 metabolites in clusters 7 and 8 that exhibited opposite trends in the MC group compared with the NC and LPC-EVs groups (Supplementary Fig. 6). This pattern indicates that LPC-EVs intake partially restored the intestinal metabolic profile disrupted by DSS-induced colitis. This regulation balances the synthesis and accumulation of NO and ornithine, which in turn enhances mucosal repair and improves intestinal barrier function (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. 7B). We conclude that this metabolic reprogramming plays a critical role in the recovery from colitis.

LPC-EVs deliver miR-9394b to target iNOS and reprogram the NO–ornithine metabolic axis for epithelial repair

To investigate the direct effects of LPC-EVs on intestinal epithelial cells, we co-cultured them with MODE-K cells. Fluorescent imaging confirmed efficient cellular internalization of LPC-EVs within 12 h (Fig. 7A). In an LPS-induced inflammatory model (80 μg/mL, Supplementary Fig. 8A), LPC-EVs promoted cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner (30–120 μg/mL), with a maximal proliferation rate of 84.11% (Fig. 7B). To balance efficacy and experimental operability, 100 μg/mL LPC-EVs was chosen as the optimal concentration for subsequent assays. To further assess whether the protective effect was mainly attributable to the vesicular fraction, we compared LPC-EVs with the corresponding vesicle-free supernatant (LPC-VFS) in the same LPS-injured MODE-K model. LPC-EVs significantly restored cell viability, whereas LPC-VFS did not show an obvious protective effect (Supplementary Fig. 8B). These results suggest that the protective activity in this system is mainly associated with the vesicular component. Given this protective phenotype, we examined key genes in the arginine–ornithine metabolic pathway. qPCR analysis revealed that LPC-EVs significantly downregulated inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and upregulated arginase 2 (Arg2) expression in MODE-K cells, a trend corroborated in colonic tissues (Fig. 7F, G), suggesting a shift in arginine metabolism toward ornithine production. The bioactivity of LPC-EVs was dependent on their RNA cargo, as RNase but not DNase treatment abolished their proliferative effect (Fig. 7C).

Subsequent miRNA sequencing of LPC-EVs identified 85 miRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 9B, C and Supplementary Table 4). KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to nitrogen metabolism and D-amino acid metabolism, which is consistent with the metabolic processes under investigation (Supplementary Fig. 9D, E). The most abundant species, miR-9394b (Supplementary Fig. 9A), was predicted to target the 3′UTR of iNOS (RNAhybrid; Supplementary Fig. 10B), with a visualized alignment shown in Fig. 7E. Next, to confirm the direct binding specificity of miR-9394b to iNOS, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed using reporter constructs containing either the wild-type (iNOS -WT) or mutant (iNOS -Mut) miR-9394b binding sequence within the iNOS 3′UTR. Co-transfection of miR-9394b mimics with the iNOS-WT reporter significantly decreased relative luciferase activity compared with the negative-control mimics. In contrast, this suppressive effect was abolished when the predicted binding site was mutated in the iNOS-Mut reporter. These results indicate that miR-9394b directly binds to the predicted site in the iNOS 3′UTR and that the observed repression is sequence-specific (Fig. 7H, I). Functional studies confirmed that miR-9394b mimics significantly suppressed iNOS expression, while Arg2 expression remained largely unchanged, and enhanced the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin in LPS-stimulated MODE-K cells (Fig. 7J, K). In addition, miR-9394b mimic transfection increased the mRNA levels of ZO-1 and Occludin (Fig. 7L). Western blot analysis further showed that both LPC-EVs and miR-9394b mimics increased the protein abundance of Occludin and ZO-1 in LPS-stimulated MODE-K cells, whereas the negative-control mimic did not show a comparable protective effect (Fig. 7K, N). Notably, Arg2 expression was unaffected by miR-9394b alone (Fig. 7J), indicating that other vesicular components likely contribute to its regulation. This miR-9394b–mediated suppression of iNOS directly impacted the metabolic balance: both LPC-EVs and miR-9394b mimics significantly reduced NO levels in the cell supernatant (Fig. 7M).

LPC-EVs regulate ornithine expression in intestinal epithelial cells in association with macrophage polarization

Based on the conclusion that miR-9394b-mediated inhibition of iNOS affects NO homeostasis, we sought to investigate whether ornithine metabolism was affected subsequently and what role ornithine played in this process. To address this, we measured ornithine and arginine levels in the MODE-K cell supernatant under conditions with LPC-EVs and miR-9394b mimics. The results showed that LPC-EVs and miR-9394b mimics significantly increased ornithine levels in the cell supernatant (Fig. 8A). Meanwhile, compared with the CON group, LPS stimulation disrupted extracellular arginine homeostasis and reduced arginine levels. Under inflammatory conditions, LPC-EVs treatment further decreased arginine abundance, while miR-9394b mimic transfection partially reproduced this trend, although the magnitude of the effect was weaker than that of intact LPC-EVs (Supplementary Fig. 11B). To further characterize macrophage polarization at the protein level, we performed immunofluorescence staining for CD86 and CD206 in RAW264.7 cells. Consistent with the transcriptional results, LPS stimulation markedly increased CD86 fluorescence and decreased CD206 signal, whereas co-culture with LPC-EV-treated MODE-K cells significantly reduced CD86 staining and enhanced CD206 fluorescence in macrophages (Fig. 8D). However, when RAW264.7 cells were directly treated with LPC-EVs, this effect was not reproduced at the transcript level, although Arg1 expression was partially increased (Fig. 8G, H). Immunofluorescence analysis further showed that direct LPC-EVs treatment did not substantially reverse the LPS-induced phenotype, whereas supplementation with 2 mM ornithine clearly decreased CD86 fluorescence and increased CD206 fluorescence (Fig. 8F). We therefore hypothesized that the ability of LPC-EVs to maintain ornithine production in epithelial cells contributes to macrophage modulation. To test this, RAW264.7 cells were supplemented with exogenous ornithine. The results showed that 2 mM ornithine significantly reduced iNOS expression, increased Arg1 expression, and significantly lowered IL-6 and TNF-α mRNA levels (Fig. 8I, J). Together, these results indicate that LPC-EVs regulate macrophage polarization indirectly through epithelial ornithine remodeling rather than by direct action on macrophages.

Discussion

UC is a complex disorder characterized by recurrent intestinal inflammation, the pathogenesis of which involves the interplay of genetic, immune, barrier, and microbial factors18. In recent years, probiotics and their metabolites have gained increasing attention as emerging therapeutic strategies, with accumulating evidence demonstrating the pivotal role of LAB in alleviating intestinal inflammation19,20. Our study reinforces this concept by systematically investigating a L. paracasei strain isolated from the intestinal microbiota of exclusively breastfed infants and its EVs. We demonstrated that LPC and its derived EVs alleviate DSS-induced colitis in mice by improving the gut microbiota and mucosal barrier function; mechanistically, this protective effect is closely associated with EV-mediated delivery of a specific microRNA that targets and modulates the host NO-ornithine metabolic pathway.

The therapeutic benefits of LAB in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), particularly in UC, are well-documented21. Infant-derived LAB strains possess inherent advantages for gut health maintenance, as they are adapted to utilize breast milk components and exhibit probiotic potential through pathogen inhibition22–25. Consistent with this, our in vivo results confirmed that the infant-derived LPC strain effectively attenuated DSS-induced colitis. This protective effect was associated with a partial reshaping of DSS-disrupted gut microbiota. Specifically, LPC intervention reduced the DSS-associated enrichment of Proteobacteria and Escherichia and increased the abundance of beneficial genera, including Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Ruminococcus, although the overall microbial configuration was not fully restored to that of the NC group26–28. Likewise, LPC-EVs promoted enrichment of taxa such as Akkermansia, Ruminococcaceae, and Eubacterium, suggesting that both the parental strain and its EVs improved, but did not fully normalize, the DSS-disrupted microbial community29. Although both LPC and LPC-EVs improved DSS-disrupted microbial communities, the comparable protective effects observed with LPC-EVs suggested that the benefits of LAB are not solely dependent on live bacterial colonization, prompting us to further investigate their bioactive components, particularly BEVs.

BEVs, nanoscale phospholipid bilayer structures, enable the long-distance transport of bioactive molecules from parent bacteria and have emerged as promising delivery tools30. Their physicochemical stability and ability to traverse biological barriers make them ideal for mediating gastrointestinal disorders31. In line with this, we found that LPC-EVs recapitulated the anti-inflammatory effects of their parental strain. Notably, the enrichment of Eubacterium fissicatena in both LPC and LPC-EVs groups suggests a potential indirect immunomodulatory mechanism, possibly through the production of butyrate to promote Treg differentiation32. The anti-inflammatory action of LPC-EVs, shared by other probiotic EVs such as those from Limosilactobacillus mucosae and Lactobacillus johnsonii33–35, involved a marked suppression of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway. This pathway is a central regulator of intestinal barrier integrity, and its activation by pathogens like Escherichia coli disrupts tight junctions and amplifies inflammatory responses36. Our results demonstrated that LPC and its EVs suppress p65 phosphorylation by downregulating TLR4 and upregulating IκBα expression.

A key finding of our study is the functional link between the suppression of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway and a profound reprogramming of host arginine metabolism. While the inhibition of this inflammatory signaling axis is crucial, our data indicate that the ensuing metabolic shift represents a deeper, fundamental mechanism of action. It is well-established that TLR4 activation mediated by LPS upregulates iNOS, driving excessive conversion of arginine to NO, which exacerbates nitrosative stress and intestinal tissue injury37–39. Concurrently, Arg2 catalyzes the hydrolysis of arginine to ornithine, a precursor for polyamines that are essential for cell proliferation, mucosal repair, and barrier integrity40,41. Our integrated multi-omics and functional analyses reveal that the alleviation of DSS-induced colitis by LPC-EVs is intrinsically linked to a decisive shift within the arginine-ornithine metabolic axis, a pathway increasingly implicated in both the pathogenesis and mucosal repair processes of UC42.

This LPC-EV-driven shift elevates ornithine levels, thereby promoting its conversion to polyamines, including spermidine and spermine, via the ornithine decarboxylase pathway43. The resulting increase in polyamines, including spermidine and spermine, is critically important as they are essential for maintaining epithelial barrier integrity and promoting cellular proliferation44,45. Beyond these foundational restorative roles, emerging evidence indicates that polyamines also exert sophisticated immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects. They not only directly participate in mucosal repair but can also regulate the chromatin accessibility of TLR4 pathway-related genes through epigenetic mechanisms, establishing an important negative-feedback regulatory loop46. Mechanistically, polyamines such as spermidine and spermine inhibit the activity of the IKK complex, thereby preventing IκBα degradation and the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65, which ultimately suppresses the expression of downstream pro-inflammatory genes47. Furthermore, the activation of the ornithine-polyamine axis helps to shape an immune microenvironment conducive to the differentiation of regulatory T cells and M2 macrophages, both of which are pivotal for suppressing excessive inflammation and restoring immune tolerance48. Our non-targeted metabolomic analysis, which confirmed a significant rise in ornithine and showed an upward trend in polyamine levels in the colonic tissues of LPC-EV-treated mice, provides direct metabolic evidence for this multifaceted mechanism.

The therapeutic potential of targeting this ornithine-polyamine axis is compellingly supported by emerging clinical evidence. Notably, a recent study demonstrated that gut-microbial L-ornithine synergizes with the biologic agent ustekinumab to improve treatment outcomes in Crohn’s disease49. This pivotal clinical finding provides a direct translational bridge for our mechanistic discoveries, indicating that the LPC-EV-mediated elevation of ornithine we observed is not merely a biochemical phenomenon but bears significant relevance to human disease therapy. It suggests that interventions designed to enrich intestinal ornithine levels, such as the LPC-EV-based strategy identified here, could represent a promising approach to enhancing the efficacy of biological therapies in IBD.

Further supporting the pathophysiological relevance of this axis, the accumulation of arginine dipeptides in the model control group may reflect impaired dipeptide uptake, potentially due to the downregulation of amino acid transporters such as SLC7A2 during active colitis50, a defect that LPC-EVs intervention appears to mitigate. Moreover, exogenous supplementation with putrescine or ornithine activates type 3 innate lymphoid cells, promoting IL-22 secretion and enhancing mucosal barrier function51,52. Collectively, this metabolic shift away from inflammatory NO production toward reparative ornithine synthesis underscores a fundamental mechanism through which probiotic-derived BEVs can directly promote intestinal homeostasis.

To elucidate how LPC-EVs orchestrate this metabolic reprogramming, we focused on their cargo of miRNAs, small non-coding RNAs that fine-tune gene expression and are implicated in inflammatory diseases53,54. Building on evidence that microbiota-derived EVs can modulate host pathways via miRNAs, such as miR-125b in NF-κB activation55, we hypothesized that a specific miRNA within LPC-EVs targets iNOS. This was confirmed by nuclease treatment, which abrogated the pro-proliferative effect of LPC-EVs on MODE-K cells, pinpointing RNA cargo as essential. miRNA sequencing identified miR-9394b as a highly abundant miRNA in LPC-EVs. Functional assays demonstrated that both LPC-EVs and miR-9394b mimics significantly downregulated iNOS expression in LPS-stimulated MODE-K cells. The concurrent upregulation of Arg2 by LPC-EVs, but not by miR-9394b alone, suggests that while miR-9394b is the key inhibitor of iNOS, other components in the vesicles may contribute to the regulation of Arg2 expression, perhaps as part of an adaptive host response to inflammation37. The net result of miR-9394b delivery was a significant reduction in NO production, an increase in ornithine levels, and enhanced expression of tight junction proteins, supporting a model in which epithelial metabolic remodeling is associated with improved barrier-related phenotypes. Notably, miR-9394b carried by LPC-EVs may indirectly contribute to the observed changes in microbial composition. Furthermore, to investigate potential off-target effects of miRNA, we further predicted the binding of miR-9394b to representative genes in the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, including Tlr4, Myd88, Traf6, RelA, and Stat1, as well as representative genes in the arginine–ornithine metabolic pathway, including Arg2, Slc7a2, Odc1, Ass1, and Asl. The results showed that miR-9394b was not predicted to successfully target these genes. More importantly, the direct interaction at the iNOS site was supported by the WT/Mut dual-luciferase assay, and miR-9394b mimic selectively suppressed iNOS/NO. In the present study, iNOS is the most directly validated target identified so far, whereas the broader pathway changes are more likely to reflect secondary host responses or the combined contribution of other EV cargos.

This mechanism, together with the macrophage phenotype observed in vivo after EV intervention, prompted us to further investigate how intestinal epithelial cells influence macrophages. Using a Transwell co-culture system, we found that LPC-EVs increased ornithine levels in MODE-K cells and were associated with changes in macrophage polarization-related markers, supporting a role for epithelial metabolic remodeling in epithelial–macrophage crosstalk. These findings further provide a mechanistic framework for interpreting the apparent iNOS-ARG1 balance observed in our study. Importantly, this balance is not a simple reciprocal switch occurring within a single cell type. Rather, our data support a compartmentalized process. In intestinal epithelial cells, LPC-EVs directly deliver miR-9394b to suppress iNOS and reduce NO output, while intact LPC-EVs additionally promote Arg2 expression and ornithine accumulation, thereby shifting local arginine utilization away from inflammatory NO synthesis. In macrophages, the downstream consequence of this epithelial metabolic remodeling is reflected by reduced iNOS and increased Arg1 expression in the Transwell system. Notably, this phenotype was not reproduced by direct exposure of RAW264.7 cells to LPC-EVs, but could be partially mimicked by exogenous ornithine. Such metabolite-mediated communication is biologically plausible, as previous studies have shown that metabolites such as succinate can be absorbed by intestinal epithelial cells and also influence immune cells via SUCNR1 signaling, which is consistent with our interpretation56. In contrast to previous reports in which EVs primarily act on macrophages and subsequently affect epithelial barrier function57, our findings suggest an opposite directionality, namely that epithelial metabolic reprogramming can, in turn, shape macrophage polarization. Taken together, these results indicate that LPC-EV-induced regulation is cell-context dependent and likely involves a reciprocal positive feedback process rather than a simple unidirectional effect. Consistent with our in vitro findings, LPC-EVs promoted ornithine accumulation in epithelial cells and, in the Transwell co-culture system, this epithelial-derived metabolic change was associated with a shift in RAW264.7 macrophages toward a less inflammatory phenotype. Direct ornithine treatment partially reproduced these effects, supporting ornithine as a mechanistically relevant downstream mediator linking epithelial metabolic remodeling to macrophage polarization. However, because ornithine alone did not fully recapitulate the effects of LPC-EVs, macrophage modulation is more appropriately interpreted as part of a broader epithelial remodeling process rather than an independent event. In this context, epithelial barrier improvement and macrophage polarization are likely parallel and mutually reinforcing consequences of LPC-EV-induced epithelial reprogramming.

Several limitations of the present study should also be acknowledged. First, although PKH26 labeling demonstrated that LPC-EVs can be internalized by intestinal epithelial cells and our functional data further support biologically meaningful RNA cargo delivery, we did not perform a systematic quantitative analysis of uptake efficiency or kinetics in epithelial cells or macrophages. Thus, while our current data are sufficient to establish functional uptake, they do not yet define the proportion of recipient cells that internalize LPC-EVs or the relative efficiency of uptake under different conditions. Second, the precise machinery responsible for LPC-EVs entry remains unresolved. In the present work, TLR4/NF-κB was investigated as a downstream inflammatory signaling pathway modulated by LPC-EVs, rather than as a demonstrated uptake receptor. Likewise, we did not map the relative contribution of specific endocytic routes, such as clathrin-mediated endocytosis, dynamin-dependent uptake, caveolae/lipid raft-related processes, or macropinocytosis. Third, all in vivo experiments in the present study were performed exclusively in male C57BL/6 mice. However, biological sex is an important variable in experimental colitis. Therefore, the therapeutic effects and mechanistic conclusions of LPC and LPC-EVs in the present study should be interpreted within the context of male mice only. Future studies should combine quantitative uptake analysis with targeted dissection of endocytic mechanisms, while also determining whether the microbiota-related effects and the miR-9394b-iNOS-NO/ornithine regulatory axis identified here are similarly preserved in female mice.

In summary, this study systematically elucidates the protective effects of an infant-derived L. paracasei strain and its EVs in a murine colitis model. We demonstrate that this intervention mitigates DSS-induced colitis by improving gut microbial composition and enhancing mucosal barrier function. At the molecular level, these benefits are closely associated with the suppression of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway. More importantly, we propose and validate a previously unrecognized mechanism in which LPC-EVs deliver miR-9394b to inhibit iNOS, remodeling the host NO-ornithine metabolic axis and shifting the metabolic flux from pro-inflammatory NO production to reparative ornithine synthesis. The increased ornithine served as a downstream mediator linking epithelial metabolic remodeling to macrophage polarization. (Figure 9). Collectively, our study provides the first systematic evidence supporting the “Lactobacillus EVs miR-9394b-intestinal epithelial cell-macrophage” axis, which alleviates UC-related pathology through modulation of host metabolic programs. Methods

Animals and treatment

Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–20 g were used to investigate the effects of lactic acid bacteria on DSS-induced colitis. The mice were randomly assigned to seven groups (n = 8 per group): NC (phosphate-buffered saline, PBS), MC (DSS + PBS), PC (DSS + Life Space probiotics), LPC (DSS + LPC), LB (DSS + LB), LR (DSS + LR), and LP (DSS + LP). The PC group received a commercially available multi-strain probiotic powder (Life Space), which was selected as a practical benchmark comparator because it is formulated to support gut, digestive, and immune health and contains multiple probiotic strains according to the manufacturer. Except for the NC group, all other groups were given 2.0% DSS solution ad libitum from day 0 to day 8, after which it was replaced with sterile water. From day 0 to day 12, mice in the NC and MC groups were orally administered 200 μL of PBS daily, whereas the PC group received 200 μL of Life Space probiotic suspension (1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL). The LPC, LR, LP, and LB groups were administered 200 μL of their respective Lactobacillus suspensions (1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL) daily. On day 12, all mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia, and colon, blood, and fecal samples were collected.

Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–20 g were used to investigate the effects of Lactobacillus-derived EVs on DSS-induced colitis. The mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 8 per group): NC (PBS), MC (DSS + PBS), and LPC-EVs (DSS + LPC-EVs). Except for the NC group, all mice were given 2.0% DSS solution in their drinking water ad libitum starting from day 0. The DSS solution was replaced with sterile water on day 8. From day 0 to day 12, mice in the NC and MC groups were orally administered 200 μL of PBS daily, whereas the LPC-EVs group received 200 μL of EV suspension (0.25 mg/mL) each day. On day 12, all mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia, and intestinal tissues, blood, and fecal samples were collected.

Lactic acid bacteria culture

Four strains of Lactobacillus, LPC, LB, LR, and LP, were isolated from the feces of healthy infants and stored at the Microbial Genetic Stock Center of Huazhong Agricultural University (Wuhan). The strains were cultured in de Man, Rogosa, and Sharp (MRS) medium at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by centrifugation to collect the cells, which were washed twice with PBS. The concentration was determined using culturing methods, and the bacterial cells were preserved at −80 °C in 40% glycerol.

Histological analyses

Colon tissues were subjected to H&E staining and PAS staining as previously described35,58. Briefly, colon tissues were embedded, sectioned into paraffin and frozen slices, and then stained with H&E and PAS. Goblet cells and inflammatory cell infiltration were examined using CaseViewer software. The number of goblet cells was quantified, and histopathological scoring was conducted. IHC staining of colon tissues was performed as previously described59. In brief, after section preparation, antigen retrieval was performed, followed by primary antibody incubation and chromogenic detection. Macrophages were imaged and analyzed using Aipathwell software, and the relative expression levels of CD86 and CD206 were quantified.

Cytokine assays

Colon tissues were homogenized in ice-cold PBS and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Mouse serum samples were kept on ice for 4 h and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −20 °C for subsequent assays. The concentrations of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10) in colon tissue homogenates and serum samples were determined using commercial ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from colon tissues using the Animal RNA Extraction Kit (Beyotime) and from cells using the Cell RNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN). The concentration and purity of RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the ABScript III RT Master Mix (ABclonal). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using 2× Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (ABclonal). β-Actin served as an internal control, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in Table S2.

Western blot analysis

The procedure was performed as previously described with minor modifications60. Briefly, proteins were extracted from colon tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime) and measured at 562 nm with a multimode microplate reader (BioTek Cytation 5). Proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with Rapid Blocking Buffer (Epizyme) for 15 min and then washed with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST). The membranes were incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing five times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, protein bands were visualized using an UltraSensitive Chemiluminescence Detection Kit (ABclonal) and imaged with a Tanon 5100 chemiluminescence system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. The primary antibodies used in this study included β-actin, ZO-1, Occludin, NF-κB p65, phospho-NF-κB p65, TLR4, and IκBα. Detailed antibody information is listed in Table S3.

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

Microbial community genomic DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, USA). The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified by PCR using barcoded primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806 R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Purified PCR amplicons were used to construct sequencing libraries with the NEXTFLEX® Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (PerkinElmer). Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina). Raw paired-end reads were quality-filtered using Fastp v0.19.6 and assembled with FLASH v1.2.11. High-quality sequences were clustered into OTUs at 97% similarity, and chimeric sequences were removed using UPARSE v7.1. To minimize the influence of sequencing depth on downstream diversity analyses, sequence counts were rarefied prior to alpha- and beta-diversity analyses, and all samples were standardized to 20,000 reads per sample. After rarefaction, the average Good’s coverage remained 99.09%, indicating sufficient sequencing depth for microbial community analysis. Taxonomic annotation of OTUs was performed using the RDP Classifier v2.11 against the SILVA 16S rRNA database (version 138) with a confidence threshold of 70%. Microbial community composition was analyzed across different taxonomic levels based on relative abundance data, whereas α-diversity and β-diversity analyses were performed using the rarefied OTU table. Bray-Curtis distance was used for β-diversity calculation, and PCoA was applied for ordination, group differences were further assessed by ANOSIM. Functional prediction based on 16S rRNA gene data was performed using PICRUSt2 v2.2.0.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis of colon tissues

Colon tissues were slowly thawed at 4 °C and mixed with 1 mL of pre-cooled methanol/acetonitrile/water (2:2:1, v/v), followed by vigorous vortexing. After sonication at low temperature for 30 min, the samples were incubated at −20 °C for 10 min to precipitate proteins and then centrifuged (14,000 × g, 4 °C, 20 min). The supernatants were collected and dried under vacuum, and the residues were stored at −80 °C until use. Before analysis, the dried extracts were reconstituted in 100 μL acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v), thoroughly vortexed, and centrifuged again (14,000 × g, 4 °C, 15 min). The final supernatants were subjected to liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis61.

Isolation and characterization of LPC-EVs

The procedure was performed as previously described with minor modifications62. LPC-EVs were isolated using a combination of differential centrifugation and ultracentrifugation. Briefly, LPC cultures were collected during the logarithmic growth phase and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was sequentially centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min and 10,000 × g for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm pore-size membrane. The filtrate was transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes and ultracentrifuged at 150,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C using an SW70 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter). The resulting pellet was resuspended in PBS. To increase purity, the resuspended EVs were ultracentrifuged again under the same conditions for 2 h. The final pellet was resuspended in PBS and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to obtain purified EVs. The morphology of purified EVs was examined using TEM, and their particle size distribution was determined by NTA.

Ultrastructural analysis of LPC

The surface morphology of LPC was examined using Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SU8010, Hitachi). The procedure was as follows: fermentation broth collected during the logarithmic growth phase was centrifuged, and the bacterial pellets were washed twice with PBS. The samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C in the dark for 4 h. After fixation, the samples were rinsed with PBS and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The dehydrated samples were subsequently freeze-dried. The freeze-dried bacterial samples were imaged using an SEM, and micrographs were captured for morphological analysis. For internal ultrastructural observation, LPC samples were prepared by ultrathin sectioning and examined using a transmission electron microscope (HT7800, Hitachi). In brief, bacterial pre-fixation was performed following the same protocol as that used for SEM sample preparation. Subsequently, the samples were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C in the dark for 2 h. After removing residual osmium tetroxide, the samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol–acetone series. The dehydrated samples were then subjected to resin infiltration, embedding, and polymerization. Ultrathin sections were mounted on copper grids and stained sequentially with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. After drying, the sections were examined using a TEM, and micrographs were captured for ultrastructural analysis.

Quantification of DNA, RNA, and protein in LPC-EVs

The amounts of DNA, RNA, and protein in EVs (1 × 10⁹ particles) were quantified using the Qubit™ dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen), Qubit™ RNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen), and BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime), respectively.

In vivo biodistribution of LPC-EVs

To investigate the gastrointestinal retention and tissue distribution of LPC-EVs in mice, EVs were labeled with a near-infrared fluorescent dye. Purified EVs were incubated with DiR dye in the dark, and the unbound free dye was removed by ultracentrifugation. The labeled EVs were then resuspended in sterile PBS for subsequent use. Mice were orally administered the labeled EV suspension by gavage, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS or free dye solution. Whole-body in vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h after gavage. Before imaging, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and then placed in an IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system to acquire fluorescence images. Under identical imaging parameters, fluorescence signals in the abdominal region were recorded. After whole-body imaging, the mice were sacrificed, and the major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, stomach, and intestines, were rapidly collected. After gentle rinsing with PBS, ex vivo fluorescence imaging was performed on the isolated organs.

Cell culture

MODE-K, RAW264.7, and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Cellular uptake of LPC-EVs

For the cellular uptake assay, LPC-EVs were labeled with PKH26 (Umibio) and co-incubated with MODE-K cells for 12 h. After incubation, the cytoskeleton was stained with Alexa Fluor 488–phalloidin (Invitrogen), and nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime). Images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (UltraVIEW VoX & IX81; PerkinElmer & Olympus).

Cell proliferation assay

MODE-K intestinal epithelial cells were seeded into 96-well or 24-well plates and cultured under standard conditions. To determine the optimal concentration of LPS (Sigma), cells were exposed to various concentrations of LPS for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo), and optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader. After the optimal LPS concentration was determined, MODE-K cells were pretreated with various concentrations of extracellular vesicles (LPC-EVs) for 24 h, followed by stimulation with the selected LPS concentration. Four experimental groups were established: a normal control (CON), an LPS-induced model group (LPS), EV-treated groups (LPS + LPC-EVs), and vesicle-free supernatant (LPC-VFS). Cell proliferation was evaluated using two independent approaches. In the first approach, OD values at 450 nm were obtained using the CCK-8 assay. In the second approach, cell proliferation was examined using the EdU-555 Cell Proliferation Detection Kit (Beyotime). Fluorescent images were captured with a two-photon laser confocal microscope (FV1000MP; Olympus), and EdU-positive cells were counted using ImageJ software.

Cell apoptosis assay

MODE-K cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated under the same conditions as those used in the cell proliferation assay. After treatment, cells were stained with the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime), and apoptotic cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software to assess the effect of EVs on MODE-K cell apoptosis.

DNase, RNase A, and proteinase K treatment of LPC-EVs

The procedure was performed as previously described with minor modifications63. In brief, to remove vesicle-associated nucleic acids and proteins, native LPC-EVs (final concentration, 1 mg/mL) were treated with DNase I (10 U/mL; Thermo Scientific, #EN0521), RNase I (10 U/mL; Solarbio, #R8021), and immobilized proteinase K-agarose (1 mg/mL; Sigma, #P9290). LPC-EVs were incubated with proteinase K–agarose at 37 °C for 2 h, whereas DNase I- or RNase I-treated samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The reactions were terminated by heating at 75 °C for 1 h, and proteinase K–agarose was subsequently removed by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 1 min).

miRNA sequencing and target analysis

miRNA sequencing libraries of LPC-EVs were constructed using the TruSeq Small RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina). After library preparation, sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2000/2500 platform using single-end 50 bp (SE50) reads. Sequencing and primary data processing were performed by GEMEXO Biotech Co., Ltd. Prediction of miRNA target genes was performed using RNAhybrid (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/rnahybrid) and RNA22 (https://cm.jefferson.edu/rna22/). Molecular docking between miRNAs and their predicted target proteins was conducted using the AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com/). Docking results were visualized using PyMOL version 3.1.1 (Schrödinger).

miR-9394b-mimic Transfection and functional assays

To evaluate the function of miR-9394b, MODE-K cells were transfected with miR-9394b mimics and allocated to four groups: untreated control (CON), LPS-stimulated model (LPS), LPS plus miR-9394b mimics (LPS + miR-9394b; 50 nM; Tsingke Biotechnology), and LPS plus negative-control mimics (LPS + miR-9394b-NC; 50 nM; Tsingke Biotechnology). Briefly, cells cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂) were transfected with miR-9394b or negative-control mimics using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher) for 24 h. After transfection, cells were stimulated with LPS (80 μg/mL) for 24 h to assess miR-9394b–mediated responses under inflammatory challenge. The sequences of the mimics are provided in Table S1.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To verify the direct interaction between miR-9394b and the mouse iNOS 3′UTR, an approximately 200-bp fragment containing the WT or Mut miR-9394b binding region was cloned into the pmirGLO dual-luciferase reporter vector. The pmirGLO vector contains Firefly luciferase as the reporter and Renilla luciferase as the internal control. HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates one day before transfection and grown to 60–70% confluence. Cells were then co-transfected with 1 μg reporter plasmid and miR-9394b mimics or NC mimics (50 nM) using Lipofectamine 2000. The transfection mixtures were prepared in serum-free DMEM and incubated at room temperature for 15 min before being added to the cells. After 4–6 h, the medium was replaced with complete medium, and cells were further cultured for 48 h. Luciferase activity was measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (JKR23008, Genecreate/KingKary). Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of Firefly to Renilla luciferase.

Determination of NO, arginine, and ornithine levels

The concentration of NO in MODE-K cell supernatants was determined using a nitric oxide assay kit (Beyotime). The ornithine and arginine level in MODE-K cell supernatants was quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) on a QTRAP 4000 system (AB Sciex). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The flow rate was maintained at 0.3 mL/min. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Waters Atlantis® T3 column (3 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm) using the following gradient: 65–100% solvent B (0.01–6 min), 100% solvent B (6.01–8 min), and 65% solvent B (8.01–12 min). Quantification was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The standard curves for NO, arginine and ornithine are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11A, C.

Exogenous ornithine supplementation

MODE-K or RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured overnight. The medium was then replaced with DMEM basal medium. Ornithine was added to the treatment groups at a final concentration of 2 mM, while an equal volume of PBS was added to the control groups. After 12 h incubation, LPS was added to the LPS and LPS + ornithine (2 mM) groups and co-incubated for an additional 12 h. Cells were then harvested for mRNA extraction and subsequent analysis.

Transwell co-culture assay

The Transwell co-culture system was established as previously described57. Briefly, MODE-K cells were seeded into the upper chamber and cultured for 24 h. After removal of the supernatant, DMEM basal medium containing LPC-EVs (100 μg/mL) was added to the LPC-EVs group, while equal volumes of PBS were added to the other groups, followed by incubation for 12 h. RAW264.7 cells were seeded into the lower chamber and cultured for 12 h. After completion of the upper chamber treatment, both chambers were replaced with fresh DMEM basal medium, and LPS was added to the LPS and LPS + LPC-EVs groups. Cells were co-cultured for an additional 12 h, after which macrophages were collected for mRNA extraction and analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

To evaluate the effects of LPC-EVs or ornithine on macrophage polarization, macrophages in the Transwell co-culture system and separately cultured macrophages were treated with LPC-EVs (100 μg/mL) or ornithine (2 mM), followed by immunofluorescence detection of the polarization-related markers CD86 and CD206. After treatment, macrophages were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked, and then incubated simultaneously with rabbit anti-CD86 primary antibody (Proteintech, 13395-1-AP) and mouse anti-CD206 primary antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-58986) overnight at 4 °C. After washing the following day, the cells were incubated simultaneously in the dark with R-PE-conjugated F(ab’)2 Fragment Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (Proteintech, SA00008-2) and FITC-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (Proteintech, SA00003-1), with CD86 visualized as red fluorescence and CD206 as green fluorescence. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. After mounting, fluorescence images were acquired using a two-photon laser confocal microscope (FV1000MP, Olympus), and the fluorescence signals were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The exact sample size (n) for each experiment is indicated in the corresponding figure legend.
