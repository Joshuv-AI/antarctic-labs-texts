An Introduction to Network Analysis in Plant Biology
Authors: Chamindika L. Siriwardana, Ashleigh S. Carlton, Thalia Lizeth Moncayo, Elizabeth A. O'Bier, Laura E. Bartley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis beginner's guide is intended for plant biologists new to network analysis. Here, we introduce key concepts and resources for researchers interested in incorporating network analysis into research, either as a stand‐alone component for generating hypotheses or as a framework for examining and visualizing experimental results. Network analysis provides a powerful tool to predict gene functions. Advances in and reduced costs for systems biology techniques, such as genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, have generated abundant omics data for plants; however, the functional annotation of plant genes lags. Therefore, predictions from network analysis can be a starting point to annotate genes and ultimately elucidate genotype–phenotype relationships. In this paper, we introduce networks and compare network‐building resources available for plant biologists, including databases and software for network analysis. We then compare four databases available for plant biologists in more detail: AraNet, GeneMANIA, ATTED‐II, and STRING. AraNet and GeneMANIA are functional association networks, ATTED‐II is a gene coexpression database, and STRING is a protein–protein interaction database. AraNet and ATTED‐II are plant‐specific databases that can analyze multiple plant species, whereas GeneMANIA builds networks for Arabidopsis thaliana and nonplant species and STRING for multiple species. Finally, we compare the performance of the four databases in predicting known and probable gene functions of the A. thaliana Nuclear Factor‐Y (NF‐Y) genes. We conclude that plant biologists have an invaluable resource in these databases and discuss how users can decide which type of database to use depending on their research question.

Introduction

Accelerating the pace of plant gene function research is a major research and training goal for the 21st century that can impact the environment, agriculture, medicine, industry, and basic biology (National Research Council [US] Committee on Examination of Plant Science Research Programs in the United States 1992). Advances in high‐throughput analysis of genomes, transcriptomes, proteomes, and metabolomes (i.e., omics) have led to a new paradigm in biology, with large‐scale datasets now widely available for plants (Fukushima et al. 2009; Lucas et al. 2011; Seyfferth et al. 2021; Shaw et al. 2021). Data interpretation and efficient utilization are now the limitations for high‐throughput omics data. Addressing this limitation, network analysis is a powerful tool for systems biology data integration (Kwoh and Ng 2007; Zhou et al. 2020).

The representation of interactions among genes as graphs, or networks, enables visualization of high‐dimensional relationships in biological systems, providing advantages over single‐gene analyses. Network analysis is now a common starting point for hypothesis development about gene function. In one example, Kumimoto et al. (2013) used network analysis as the starting point to analyze the interactions between transcription factor families involved in abscisic acid‐mediated seed germination (Kumimoto et al. 2013). In another example, coexpression network analysis revealed a new enzyme in plant cell wall biosynthesis (Vanholme et al. 2013). Beyond pinpointing genes for functional analysis, examining biological networks can reveal properties that explain how organisms' systems function. For example, the study of plant immunity molecular networks suggests how network topology provides a robust immune response that functions even in the presence of numerous pathogen effector molecules that block the activity of individual proteins (Mishra et al. 2021; Delplace et al. 2022).

Here, we introduce the basics of network analysis for plant biologists new to this field. Then, we compare four publicly available databases and analyze their performance for identifying experimental and novel interactions with the Nuclear Factor‐Y (NF‐Y) family of transcription factors.

Basics of Network Analysis

Networks Depict Biological Relationships Between Genes and Proteins

Network analysis can broadly apply to study many classes of data. Social network theory explains a wide range of social phenomena in the social sciences, from economics to psychology (Borgatti et al. 2009). In biological systems, macromolecular nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) and proteins form the central dogma of molecular biology, which depicts the unidirectional flow of biological information from genotype to phenotype. The biological information contained within DNA in genes is copied (i.e., expressed) to produce RNA, which provides the information (i.e., is translated) to synthesize a protein, resulting in a measurable biological effect (i.e., phenotype) (Crick 1970). In organisms, genes and proteins do not function in isolation; instead, they physically, sequentially, or indirectly interact to form complex networks. Biological network analysis attempts to elucidate and visualize these relationships. Before the turn of the millennium, the general approach to studying genes and proteins was to select one gene at a time and study its phenotypes. However, this approach does not consider that the phenotype associated with a gene is not isolated but related to other genes through complex molecular interactions, such as epistasis and gene redundancy. In the 21st century, the generation of large, complex omics datasets, including genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, has enabled the investigation of more of the biological system, leading to a better understanding of the underlying molecular events that produce the emergent properties of biological systems and organisms. Ultimately, these large‐scale studies help elucidate molecular interactions that produce genotype–phenotype relationships. However, a challenge remains in analyzing, visualizing, and interpreting multifaceted biological relationships. Network analysis offers bioinformatics tools that enable researchers to examine these complex networks. Based on terms and concepts from mathematical graph theory (Barnes and Harary 1983), networks can depict relationships between DNA (genes), RNA, and/or proteins. Common terms used to describe graphs that depict networks include node, edge, directed, undirected, weight, degree, and module.

Node

The nodes are the core elements of a network. A node typically represents a physical molecule, such as DNA (a gene), RNA, protein, or a small molecule metabolite (Saito et al. 2008). In most cases discussed here, nodes represent expressed genes or proteins. In a network diagram, nodes are represented in a two‐ or three‐dimensional shape such as a circle (Figure 1A).

Edge

An edge depicts the relationship between nodes, such as a functional connection or physical interaction. The interaction could include binding between two proteins, a protein transcription factor binding to the DNA promoter of another gene, similar expression patterns between two genes (i.e., coexpression), or a biochemical transformation of one molecule into another. In a network graph, the edges are represented by lines (Figure 1A). In addition, an edge can represent additional information about functional interactions. For example, an edge can be directed with the link in one direction, often depicted with a single arrowhead. For example, a protein binding to the promoter of a gene is an example of a directed interaction. Alternatively, an edge can be undirected, with no subject–object relationship and no edge cap (Figure 1B). An undirected example is a binding interaction between two proteins, specifically a protein–protein interaction (PPI). An edge can also be weighted, as depicted by different edge thicknesses in network visualization, or unweighted. Edge weight can be used to reflect the type of interactions or the confidence in the evidence of interaction, with interactions such as PPIs often receiving a higher weight (Figure 1C).

It is important to note that the nodes and edges of a network can have different meanings. For example, in a physical interaction network, two proteins may bind to each other, or a transcription factor may bind to a promoter. On the other hand, data integration networks might include multiple classes of data at multiple molecular levels. For example, disruption of one gene can alter the expression of another gene. The two genes could then be depicted as nodes connected by a directed (or undirected) edge.

Network Topology

The terms discussed below describe the physical topology of a network and can be used to determine the relative importance of a node in the network.

Degree and Hub

A given node can have any number of edges. The number of edges connected to a node is the degree of the node in question (Figure 1D). The degree is a critical component of data interpretation. In many networks, nodes with a high degree (i.e., hubs) are more likely than nodes with a lower degree (i.e., nonhubs) to show a phenotype and lethality with loss of function (I. Lee et al. 2010). Nodes with a high degree are sometimes referred to as hubs.

Module

A module describes interconnected nodes that have a higher density of edges with each other (within connections) and few or no connections with nodes outside of the module (between connections) (Figure 1E). Module analysis in biological networks has been described in detail previously (Alcalá‐Corona et al. 2021).

Betweenness Centrality

Betweenness centrality measures the frequency of a node being on the shortest path between other nodes/sets of nodes within the network (Figure 1E) (Hevey 2018). A high betweenness centrality of a node can indicate a key regulatory point of the biological system, even if the node has a relatively low degree (Koschützki and Schreiber 2008).

Other potentially relevant biological network topology terms, such as closeness centrality and clustering coefficient, are described elsewhere (Mishra et al. 2021).

Large‐Scale Omics Experiments Provide the Data to Reconstruct Networks

The biological relationships between nodes that are visualized, analyzed, and interpreted in network analysis are primarily derived from large‐scale omics experiments (Zhou et al. 2020). Elucidating biological relationships using omics data is primarily based on the “guilt‐by‐association” concept (Saito et al. 2008; Gillis and Pavlidis 2012). This concept asserts that genes or proteins that share edges directly or through other nodes and edges potentially share a common function. Association or interaction is derived from different sources of omics data. Common examples of data types used in network reconstruction include coexpression, physical interactions, colocalization, genetic interactions (GIs), and data transfer between organisms.

Coexpression

Gene coexpression is the most common data evidence used for constructing gene networks because data can be generated for most transcripts in a sample without targeting specific transcripts (B. Zhang and Horvath 2005). RNA transcript abundance data (i.e., transcriptomics) from frequently employed RNA sequencing or from older microarray datasets facilitate gene coexpression analysis. “Guilt‐by‐association” assumes that similar expression patterns are due to a shared common function. Methods for calculating coexpression are described below. A gene coexpression network (GCN) can operate independently or be integrated into a broader molecular network, incorporating other data types.

Physical Interactions

Gene products can interact with each other physically as proteins, through protein‐nucleic acid interactions or nucleic acid–nucleic acid interactions, among other mechanisms. For example, two proteins share an edge in a network if they are found to interact in a PPI study. These interactions can be long‐lasting, such as during the formation of protein complexes, or short‐lived, as when one protein modifies another in a phosphorylation event (Bebek 2012). Proteomics experiments with “pull‐downs,” in which one protein is targeted with antibodies or a tag, and other proteins are detected to also be associated with the protein of interest, are one type of evidence for PPIs. Yeast‐two‐hybrid (Y2H) assays are another approach for detecting PPIs that can sometimes be applied on a large scale (Xenarios and Eisenberg 2001). Chromatin immunoprecipitation with an antibody to a specific DNA‐binding protein, followed by sequencing (ChIP‐Seq), can also provide data on physical interactions (Perner et al. 2014).

Colocalization

Eukaryotic cells, or even relatively simple bacteria, have different compartments or subcellular localizations. Protein colocalization data include datasets obtained from standard light microscopy of fluorescently tagged proteins and more sophisticated methods, such as single‐molecule localization microscopy, which visualizes the subcellular distribution of proteins (Willems and MacGillavry 2022).

Gene Interactions

GIs explore functional relationships between genes (Boucher and Jenna 2013). One example is epistasis, where GIs among genes can affect phenotypes by masking or modifying the presence of one another (Boucher and Jenna 2013; Kuzmin et al. 2018; Domingo et al. 2019). For example, network analysis was used to discover epistasis between transcription factors involved in aliphatic glucosinolate biosynthesis (B. Li, Tang, et al. 2018).

Cocitation

Another type of network evidence comes from mining the literature with text analysis algorithms for the co‐occurrence of gene locus identifiers within individual publications (Qiao et al. 2013).

Transfer of Interactions Between Organisms

Transfer of interactions between organisms describes the concept in which evidence of interactions, such as experimentally identified PPIs between two proteins, can be transferred from one organism to another—based on sequence similarity of the proteins in the two organisms. For example, if two proteins are known to interact in humans, a homology network would assume that proteins with the most similar sequences to the human proteins from Arabidopsis will also interact in Arabidopsis (Walhout et al. 2000).

How Are Networks Built?

Coexpression network edges represent correlation data, describing the relationship between two or more genes/nodes. Correlation coefficients can summarize both linear and nonlinear relationships, which are also described as parametric and nonparametric correlations (Figure 2). Linear data often use either the Pearson correlation coefficient (R) or the graphical Gaussian model (GGM), which applies a partial correlation value (Y. Wang et al. 2015). The Pearson correlation coefficient, also known as the R value, describes the linear relationship between two genes. Correlation coefficients typically range between −1 and 1. Negative correlations potentially indicate negative regulatory interactions, like a transcription factor repressing the expression of a target gene. Positive correlations can indicate positive interaction, as expected for proteins that act together in a physical complex. Often, an arbitrary (or model‐derived) cutoff is applied, below which an interaction is considered less confident, and no edge is drawn. For example, only R > |0.5| might be included in a network (Higashi and Saito 2013).

Correlation coefficients can summarize (A) linear (parametric), (B) exponential (nonparametric), or (C) nonlinear relationships. Linear data assume that the population distribution follows a defined order, whereas nonlinear data do not make such assumptions.

Another approach for incorporating only confident coexpression interactions based on Pearson correlations is to order or “rank” the edges for a given node or to calculate the average mutual rank between every pair of nodes (Mutwil et al. 2011). In this case, the edge with the highest correlation coefficient for a given node (either absolute value or positive only) is given a rank of “1,” the edge with the next highest correlation coefficient is given a rank of “2,” and so on. Ranked correlation data are useful to indicate if a network edge is biologically meaningful (Yu et al. 2013). As illustrated in Figure 2, Pearson correlations, whether ranked or not, can be susceptible to outliers, which may reduce the accuracy of a network (Y. Wang et al. 2015). A GGM network uses a partial correlation value to determine the likelihood of two genes influencing each other while subtracting the influence of other genes. If a pair of genes has no partial correlation, they must be conditionally related by the influence of other factors (Ma et al. 2007). The partial correlation given in GGM data has been useful in determining many aspects of experimental networks, such as which proteins or genes directly interact with a gene of interest to nonequilibrium omics regulation (Y. Zhang et al. 2022; Borzou and Sadygov 2021).

Another option when creating a correlation‐based network that includes nonlinearly distributed data, which is typical in RNA‐Seq data, is to start by using a nonparametric correlation coefficient, like Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (Spearman's rho) or Kendall's rank correlation coefficient (Kendall's tau) (Borzou and Sadygov 2021). Nonparametric correlations can handle relationships between two genes that are not monotonic. These coefficients are useful because they are more resilient to outliers than the Pearson correlation coefficient (Figure 2) (Y. Wang et al. 2015). Spearman's rho is calculated by assigning a rank to each gene relative to the others based on the original omics abundance data. Then, a network can be assembled by calculating the sum of squared differences in the ranks of every pair of genes (Y. Wang et al. 2015; Mack et al. 2019; Siska and Kechris 2017). Another nonparametric correlation, Kendall's tau, uses ranked data to determine the tendency of the genes to increase or decrease together. Tau is calculated by summarizing how many agreements and disagreements in the rank order are in a gene collection (Y. Wang et al. 2015).

Several tools are available for researchers to build networks from datasets that they generate or download. Weighted GCN analysis (WGCNA) is a widely used approach for building networks based on sets of omics data and identifying modules of highly correlated genes (B. Zhang and Horvath 2005). First, WGCNA builds a GCN from Pearson correlation combined with the power law, a method of removing less significantly correlated nodes (de Silva et al. 2022). Then, hierarchical clustering identifies modules of genes with highly similar expression patterns. Finally, hub genes are detected by determining which nodes within modules have the highest degree (number of edges) (J. Li, Zhou, et al. 2018). WGCNA is available through a web‐based user interface through the integrated differential expression and pathway analysis site (Ge et al. 2018); the Comprehensive R Archive Network (Langfelder and Horvath 2008); and as a new Python package, which addresses some shortcomings of the R implementation (Rezaie et al. 2023). Other network creation tools include GEne Network Inference with Ensemble of trees (GENIE3), a common directed network approach for regulatory interactions (Huynh‐Thu et al. 2010), and context likelihood of relatedness (CLR), an algorithm that adapts a scoring method built from true and false positive correlations of transcriptional regulatory networks (Faith et al. 2007).

Beyond reconstructing networks from a single data type such as transcript abundance, additional considerations are necessary when integrating multiple data types into a single network. Such networks can be referred to as multiplex or multilayered networks, and different approaches have been proposed to solve this issue. Some networks, like those in GeneMANIA, described more below, display the union of networks with different data types, using colored edges to differentiate between evidence types for interactions between nodes. Bayesian networks represent a different comprehensive strategy in which multiple types of evidence are summarized with a likelihood score between each pair of genes (I. Lee et al. 2010; Su et al. 2013). A third approach for the analysis of multilayer networks stacks individual networks from different data classes or species datasets atop each other with connections (i.e., interlayer edges) across layers between nodes. Then, the multilayer network can be explored or collapsed by an algorithm with a “walker” that randomly steps along edges as if the edges were roads between nodes. The algorithm then calculates the statistical probability that any node is visited with respect to a starting node (Wen et al. 2021). This algorithm, called “random walk on multiple networks” or RWM, is a promising method of further data analysis through networks (Baptista et al. 2022).

Assessing Network Quality

Once a network is reconstructed, estimating accuracy is important. Using controlled vocabularies that describe genes as a way of scoring commonalities between connected nodes is a common approach to gauge network accuracy (Ietswaart et al. 2021; Schaefer et al. 2018; Zogopoulos et al. 2023; Johnson and Krishnan 2022). Two of the more common controlled vocabularies are “Gene Ontology” (GO) and Mapman. GO has three main categories: cellular component, molecular function, and biological process. After describing characterized nodes in a network with their GO categories, as shown with colors in Figure 3, scoring can estimate the network success rate. Typically, a true positive rate is estimated by dividing the number of nodes that share an edge of the same GO class by the number of edges that could be shared among nodes of the same GO class in a perfect network. Conversely, a false positive rate is calculated by dividing the number of edges between nodes with differing gene ontologies by the number of edges that could be shared between nodes of differing gene ontologies but are not identified (Johnson and Krishnan 2022; Moore et al. 2019). To estimate network accuracy, the true positive rate is plotted against the false positive rate with decreasingly stringent edge scores in what is known as a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (Figure 4). A randomly reconstructed network would appear as a diagonal line with a slope of one in such a curve. Networks that deviate from this with more gains in the true positive rate than the false positive rate are considered to have more accurate predictions, which can be quantified by calculating the area under the curve of the ROC curve, abbreviated the AUC. Network performance, then, can be compared with AUC‐ROC, with higher values indicating a greater ability to recall likely interactions. Finally, it is important to note that while such controlled vocabulary matching is considered the standard in biological networks, a truer gauge of accuracy is experimental analysis, with the highest standard being in planta genetic experiments, though this is typically only conducted on a limited basis.

Datasets and Databases Available for Plant Biologists

Plant biologists have a variety of datasets and databases to choose from for network analysis. Here, we discuss some websites and bioinformatic tools available to plant biologists for obtaining datasets. Other databases utilize various approaches to generate and visualize networks within the site or export them to an external visualization tool such as Cytoscape. It is important to note that both small datasets and large databases that use a catch‐all approach have drawbacks. Small datasets may lack sufficient coverage; however, including all data can decrease network performance (P. Wang et al. 2021; Uygun et al. 2016).

The beginning of the last decade saw a large increase in the availability of datasets, especially for gene expression. These datasets can be downloaded and used to create networks, and an example that is available for plant biologists includes the Bio‐Analytic Resource for Plant Biology (BAR) (Qiao et al. 2013) and the Arabidopsis Coexpression Tool (ACT), which displays gene expression similarity with hierarchical clustering or a “tree” view (Manfield et al. 2006). While most data compendium sites contain information about the model plant Arabidopsis, other plants, such as rice, are also increasingly common. For example, BAR contains information about Arabidopsis and many other plant species, including maize, poplar, tomato, camelina, soybean, potato, barley, Medicago, eucalyptus, rice, willow, sunflower, cannabis, wheat, and sugarcane (Waese and Provart 2017; Toufighi et al. 2005). Networks Are Visualized and Analyzed Within Databases or With Specialized Software

In addition to dataset compendia, numerous databases are available for network analysis. More than the dataset sites, network databases already have precomputed relationships based on multiple datasets, making them fast and accessible. Tables 2 and S1 summarize databases available for network analysis in plant biology (I. While some databases are specifically developed only for Arabidopsis or a few plant species, others harbor networks for multiple eukaryotes and prokaryotes, including many model organisms. Data collection from multiple sources has been shown to increase the accuracy of a network (Rocca‐Serra et al. 2003; Jansen et al. 2003; Lu et al. 2005; Xing and Dunson 2011). The databases contain information such as functional annotations of genes, protein interactions, coexpression, and protein localizations. For example, the TF2Network attempts to identify transcription factor binding sites in genes by integrating coexpression data and experimental protein–DNA and PPIs (Kulkarni et al. 2018).

Cytoscape app

Networks are visualized and analyzed within databases that reconstruct networks or with specialized software programs for custom visualization and analysis. For plant biologists, databases such as ATTED‐II (NetworkDrawer provides drawing and analyzing functions within ATTED‐II) (Obayashi et al. 2022) and GeneMANIA (Warde‐Farley et al. 2010) consist of both data and interactive network visualization. The performance of network visualization and analysis software has been surveyed and compared in detail (Thomas and Bonchev 2010). Two of the most common specialized software packages for network visualization and analysis are Cytoscape (Shannon et al. 2003) and Gephi (Bastian et al. 2009). Both are open‐source software programs that are freely available to users. For this publication, we used Cytoscape to visualize network maps, which will be discussed as an example of network visualization.

Cytoscape

The Cytoscape software package is a platform‐independent Java application that runs in Windows, Mac, or Linux environments. Cytoscape was developed to analyze omics data, including protein–protein, protein–DNA, and GIs (Shannon et al. 2003). Cytoscape Core, the source code, provides attractive features for network visualization. This includes the ability to control node and edge attributes to obtain desired visualizations that are suitable for publication and to support network exploration for future experiments. For example, edges can be displayed as lines or dashes, and edge weight (i.e., thickness) can be set to correspond to a scoring system. In addition, different shapes, colors, and sizes can be selected for nodes. For example, red nodes could represent an attribute such as embryo lethality. In addition, Cytoscape possesses expensive plug‐ins that extend its capabilities. The top downloaded plug‐ins include Clue GO, stringer, Bingo, Clupeids, MCODE, and yFiles Layout Algorithms. Cytoscape apps are available for network databases such as Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) and GeneMANIA. Furthermore, scientists can create their own plug‐ins to meet their unique requirements.

In‐Depth Comparison of Selected Plant Network Databases

To illustrate the strengths and weaknesses of network analysis and visualization, we selected four databases among those available for plant biologists, AraNet (I. Lee et al. 2010; T. Lee et al. 2015), GeneMANIA (Warde‐Farley et al. 2010), ATTED‐II (Obayashi et al. 2022, 2007), and STRING (Snel et al. 2000; Szklarczyk et al. 2021) for an in‐depth comparison (Tables 2 and S1).

AraNet

AraNet (I. Lee et al. 2010; T. Lee et al. 2015) is a genome‐scale functional network for Arabidopsis that utilizes the concept of guilt‐by‐association, that is, genes closely associated with each other through edges are assumed to have similar functions. Functional associations between genes are made using numerous datasets summarized into a single “likelihood” score for an interaction, based on a Bayesian statistics approach. The AraNet encapsulates 19 distinct types of data, including cocitation (MEDLINE and PubMed), coexpression derived from microarrays and RNA‐Seq data, domain co‐occurrence in proteins, and PPI (measured from high‐throughput experiments and mined from literature). This network differs from ATTED‐II in that it also transfers interactions between species into a single likelihood score. That is, data is available from both Arabidopsis and nonplant species ( Caenorhabditis elegans , Drosophila melanogaster , Danio rerio , and Homo sapiens ), which predict functional associations in Arabidopsis. AraNet currently has two versions available. AraNet v2 offers a significant improvement in both Arabidopsis genome coverage and linkage accuracy compared to AraNet v1 (T. Lee et al. 2015). In addition, there are two search options in the new version: (i) “Find new members of a pathway” and (ii) “Infer functions from network neighbors.” AraNet v2 has also expanded its plant species coverage to include 28 additional species. The AraNet v2 network has high coverage of annotated genes, with functional linkages between 84% of the Arabidopsis protein‐coding genes (22,894 genes total). Although using data from nonplant species has the advantage of predicting more edges than most other networks, it also has the potential to identify false interactions (i.e., false positives). The creators of AraNet v2 tested the network's predictive power (i.e., the true positive prediction rate based on known or published linkages) using an ROC curve (described above). They observed that AraNet v2 has high accuracy and noted that including nonplant data, thereby achieving greater gene coverage, provided an advantage in terms of greater predictive power compared to other Arabidopsis networks. The authors extensively tested the v1 and v2 networks to gauge their ability to predict novel functions. They identified novel genes associated with seed pigmentation, drought sensitivity, lateral root development, and shade avoidance response (I. Lee et al. 2010; T. Lee et al. 2015). AraNet was last updated in 2015. We encountered a challenge while developing the networks for AraNet. The network visualization was based on Adobe Flash Player, which was retired on December 31, 2020. However, a text file containing the network can be downloaded from the website and uploaded to Cytoscape for visualization.

GeneMANIA

GeneMANIA (multiple association network integration algorithm) is another functional association network that uses the concept of “guilt by association” (Warde‐Farley et al. 2010; Mostafavi et al. 2008; Franz et al. 2018). It identifies genes in publicly available databases that are functionally associated with one or more input genes. The data collected from publicly available datasets and summarized in GeneMANIA include coexpression, colocalization, GIs, physical interactions, shared protein domains, and predictions. Predicted interactions include the transfer of interactions among well‐studied organisms ( C. elegans , H. sapiens , D. melanogaster , and Saccharomyces cerevisiae), PPI predictions (Geisler‐Lee et al. 2007), and AraNet predictions (I. Lee et al. 2010). Researchers can also upload their datasets to GeneMANIA. An advantage of GeneMANIA is that the researcher can select the types of evidence to be included in constructing the network. Also, GeneMANIA's graphical user interface allows more user control than the other four databases. A researcher can move nodes and interact with the network. The data can also be exported to Cytoscape and other network visualization software, and Cytoscape has an app for GeneMANIA. GeneMANIA produces an excellent report on the analysis and saves the most recent analysis, which can be accessed when the user returns to the website. GeneMANIA was last updated in 2018 (Franz et al. 2018).

ATTED‐II

ATTED‐II ( Arabidopsis thaliana trans‐factor and cis‐element prediction database) (ver. 11.1) is a gene coexpression database (Obayashi et al. 2007, 2009, 2011, 2014, 2018, 2022; Aoki et al. 2016). Unlike most databases discussed here that rely on multiple data sources, ATTED‐II comprises only gene coexpression data. ATTED‐II vII was released in 2021 and has updated information from the original version, first published in 2007. ATTED‐II was last updated in 2024 (ver. 12.0) to include updated coexpression data. The ATTED‐II gene coexpression data is obtained from publicly available RNA‐Seq and microarray data denoted Xxx‐r and Xxx‐m, respectively, as well as a combination of RNA‐Seq and microarray data, denoted Xxx‐u. An attractive feature of ATTED‐II is that it enables users to draw networks using a tool called NetworkDrawer.

STRING

The STRING database was developed as a hub to contain all known and predicted PPI (Snel et al. 2000; Szklarczyk et al. 2011, 2015, 2017, 2019, 2021, 2023; Franceschini et al. 2013; Jensen et al. 2009; von Mering et al. 2003, 2005, 2007). PPIs in STRING include physical interactions and functional associations (i.e., two proteins involved in the same cellular process are assumed to be functionally associated) (Guala et al. 2020; Marcotte et al. 1999; Huynen et al. 2000; Enright and Ouzounis 2001). The database collects information from seven sources (termed “evidence channels”) and includes automated literature text mining (PubMed abstracts, PMC open access subset, and entries from Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man [OMIM] and Saccharomyces Genome Database [SGD]), databases of interaction experiments and annotated complexes/pathways (BioGRID, DIP, and PDB), interaction predictions based on coexpression and conserved genomic regions, and transfer of interactions between organisms. The “evidence channels” each have separate subscores and can be enabled or disabled separately. STRING networks can be visualized in a network display window on the website. In addition, Cytoscape has an app that can directly connect and query the STRING database. The network can be exported using multiple options, including bitmap image, text output, and vector graphics. The latest version of STRING, released in 2023, features significant updates to its deep learning–based relation extraction text mining model (Szklarczyk et al. 2023).

Performance of AraNet, GeneMANIA, ATTED‐II, and STRING in Predicting the Functions of NF‐Y Transcription Factors

Although many plant species have sequenced genomes, functional annotation, that is, detailed gene‐specific functional information of genes, remains scarce. The Arabidopsis genome was sequenced in 2000 (Arabidopsis Genome Initiative 2000); however, by 2021, only 13,638/27,655 protein‐coding genes were annotated with GO terms (Rhee and Mutwil 2014; Depuydt and Vandepoele 2021). Other sequenced plant species have even lower functional annotations and experimental characterization (Rhee and Mutwil 2014; Depuydt and Vandepoele 2021). Therefore, network analysis can aid in developing (testable) predictions regarding the function of genes. As discussed earlier, follow‐up studies based on AraNet predictions identified novel genes acting in stress responses and developmental processes (I. Lee et al. 2010). Similarly, a network analysis using the databases AraNet, GeneMANIA, ATTED‐II, and STRING successfully predicted gene functions in Arabidopsis (Hansen et al. 2018). Here, we demonstrate and compare the performance of AraNet, GeneMANIA, ATTED‐II, and STRING in predicting functions of a family of transcription factors, the Arabidopsis NF‐Y. We note that a previous study comparing network databases in the model species S. cerevisiae (yeast) and Mus musculus (mouse) concluded that the GeneMANIA database performed best at predicting interactions compared to the competition (Mostafavi et al. 2008; Pena‐Castillo et al. 2008).

NF‐Y transcription factors are ubiquitous in eukaryotes. The trimeric complex comprises three independent proteins: NF‐YA, NF‐YB, and NF‐YC (Sinha et al. 1996; Sinha et al. 1995). Formation of all three proteins into a trimeric transcription factor complex is required before the complex can bind DNA at conserved CCAAT motifs (Mantovani 1999; Kusnetsov et al. 1999). Although ubiquitous in eukaryotes, the number of NF‐Y genes has greatly expanded in the plant kingdom. For example, humans have one subunit each of NF‐YA, NF‐YB, and NF‐YC. In contrast, Arabidopsis has 10 subunits, each of NF‐YA, NF‐YB, and NF‐YC (Petroni et al. 2012). In plants, the best‐studied biological function of the NF‐Y is its role in regulating photoperiod‐dependent flowering. The NF‐Y subunits, NF‐YA2, NF‐YB2/NF‐YB3, and NF‐YC3/NF‐YC4/NF‐YC9, are positive regulators of photoperiod‐dependent flowering and act together with CONSTANS (CO) in regulating the FLOWERING LOCUS‐T (FT) promoter (Swain et al. 2017). The NF‐Y also has roles in regulating seed dormancy and germination, photomorphogenesis, skotomorphogenesis, early seedling development, root development, embryogenesis, seed maturation, plant primary metabolism, and abiotic stress responses including drought and salt stress and temperature stress (Swain et al. 2017; Siriwardana 2024). Most of the NF‐Y proteins can physically interact with each other; for example, NF‐YB2/NF‐YB3 physically interacts with NF‐YC3/NF‐YC4/NF‐YC9 (Kumimoto et al. 2010). However, only one trimer complex has been experimentally demonstrated to form on a promoter: the NF‐YA2/NF‐YB2/NF‐YC complex that binds CO protein on the FT promoter (Siriwardana et al. 2016). Other than interacting with each other, the NF‐Y also interacts with other proteins, including other transcription factor families such as CONSTANS, CONSTANS‐LIKE, TOC1 (CCT), and bZIP (Table S1) (Kumimoto et al. 2013; L. Li et al. 2015) (Table S1).

To examine different network database performances, here, we selected three of the best‐characterized NF‐Y genes known to form a trimer, NF‐YA2, NF‐YB2, and NF‐YC, to examine which known and novel functions each database predicts. All four networks, AraNet, GeneMANIA, ATTED‐II, and STRING, predicted some published relationships among the genes. All predicted interactions between the NF‐Y subunits (Figures 4 and S1–S4). GeneMANIA and STRING predicted interactions between the NF‐Y and CO and RGL2, while AraNet predicted the published interactions between the NF‐Y/ABF1. GeneMANIA identified interactions between all 10 NF‐YA, NF‐YB, and NF‐YC (Figure 4). An advantage of GeneMANIA, absent from the other three databases in the comparison, was that the user could select the data type for the network. The GeneMANIA graphical user interface was very user‐friendly, with the ability to analyze and edit the network within the GeneMANIA website (Figure S3). The AraNet database also contained a comprehensive list of common names for Arabidopsis genes. The tabular data generated included GO annotations about the interacting partners.

Novel interactions predicted by the databases included interactions with bZIP and CCT proteins. GeneMANIA, AraNet, and ATTED‐II predicted interactions of the NF‐Y and bZIP proteins. We expected that bZIPs would be present in the network analysis due to prior publications that demonstrate the NF‐Y and bZIP coregulate plant developmental processes (Kumimoto et al. 2013). Due to the prediction of a similar interaction by three databases, this interaction is a potential candidate for further analysis, though it could also indicate overlapping input datasets. The STRING database also identified NF‐YC3 as a potential interacting protein of TOC1, a CCT protein. This interaction is likely accurate as the Brachypodium NF‐YC orthologs physically interact with the Arabidopsis TOC1 proteins (Cao et al. 2011). Thus, the interaction between Arabidopsis NF‐Y and TOC1 is another strong candidate for further analysis.

To begin to quantitatively assess the performance of the different network databases, we applied the formalism of a contingency table based on experimentally verified interactions for NF‐YC3 to compare the networks (Table 3). We find that STRING has the highest true positive rate, recapitulating 9 of 19 known interactions when the network size is set to 50. GeneMANIA and AraNet both recapitulate the interactions with the NF‐YBs, while ATTED‐II does not contain any of the known interactions. Despite these results, we would like to point out that all these networks have been used as the starting point for experimental work and likely contain many true interactions that have yet to be studied.

Recent Developments

Two trends are having a major impact on plant network analysis—data availability and increasingly sophisticated network reconstruction approaches. There is an ever‐growing availability of data both for well‐studied species and new species. Single‐cell transcriptomics is starting to have a significant impact on network analysis, as it enables researchers to study gene expression at the individual cell level and build networks for each cell type/state. This has led to increased data sizes, with tens of thousands of individual cells being monitored for the expression for single publications. This high resolution has also led to new analysis methods such as the ability to identify separate and common expression modules across cell types (Morabito et al. 2023; Kobak and Berens 2019). Furthermore, the availability of plant species other than Arabidopsis is an important feature both within and separate from databases. It is becoming increasingly valued to study plants of economic importance and use molecular biological techniques to improve these food, biofuel, fiber, and medicinal species directly. Further, most available plant genomes have been obtained more recently than that of Arabidopsis, and as a result, their gene annotations are less complete. Using network analysis may accelerate the prediction of gene functions through comparisons with Arabidopsis and other well‐characterized species.

Increasingly, deep learning–based approaches are being developed to reconstruct molecular interactions (Cui et al. 2022). Machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) approaches use modeling to strengthen predictions from and “denoise” molecular networks and extend results to poorly characterized systems. One example is the use of random forest methods to classify genes based on expression differences; genes paired together at the end of the classification process are considered to share an edge (Chen and Ishwaran 2012). The frequency of that edge upon various means of repetition and comparison with randomized data can be used to derive edge scores. Another example, ensemble learning, is a method that combines alternative approaches to representing a problem to develop an ensemble score for an outcome, for example, a network edge. For example, F. Li et al. (2020) proposed an ensemble learning algorithm that integrates multiple “learner” representations of protein sequences and an “attention module” that selects sequence features that are most related to interactions to achieve enhanced predictive performance in PPI network inference (F. Li et al. 2020). Graphical neural networks (GNNs) have gained popularity in predicting PPIs, elucidating protein function, and advancing in silico drug discovery (Muzio et al. 2021). Diverse GNN models such as DeepWalk for undirected graph networks, graph convolutional neural networks for both undirected and directed networks, and graph recurrent neural networks all have demonstrated efficacy in the field of network analysis (Jin et al. 2021). By treating graph representation as input data and then applying convolutional operations, convolutional neural networks can extract latent features and hidden information, which is crucial for understanding biological networks (Y. Li et al. 2019).

The AI method of deep learning holds significant promise in deciphering the complexity of biological networks. An advantage over other methods is that deep learning can handle larger datasets, enabling comprehensive analysis with substantial data volumes. Recent studies have demonstrated the usefulness of deep learning methodologies in a biological context (Jin et al. 2021). Deep learning models have been used in plant biology to predict effector targets in plant–pathogen interactions and identify microbial hubs in plant–microbe interactions as the predicted key players in the plant immune systems (Shoaib et al. 2023), to predict PPIs in plants such as Zea mays with high predictive performance (Mishra et al. 2019), and in advancing plant breeding approaches (Farooq et al. 2024). Although deep learning in biological network analysis excels in classification performance and handling large datasets over other ML approaches, it has limitations regarding data interpretability and requires extensive labeled data. Despite these challenges, ongoing research and high‐throughput data advancements offer promising avenues for deep learning in addressing diverse biological network analysis challenges. On a practical level for the novice researcher, the various ML and AI networks have yet to be widely applied to plants or, to our knowledge, distributed in readily available network databases like the ones described in the rest of the article. Furthermore, we are not aware of detailed comparisons of the performance of deep learning–derived predictions and more conventional network predictions. Especially, experimental validation lags with these methods. Among the four databases we compared, only STRING has been updated to incorporate new deep learning models (Szklarczyk et al. 2023).

Conclusion

Publicly available databases are important resources for network analysis of gene functions. This paper compares four resources available for plant biologists: GeneMANIA, AraNet v2, STRING, and ATTED‐II. These networks have similarities and differences regarding the type of data used, the algorithms used to determine relationships, the type of data available for the end user, the availability of nonmodel plant species, and so on. Overall, all the databases proved useful, and the user is encouraged to decide the best database to use depending on their research question. In the future, we expect that ever‐growing data availability, ML and AI networks will continue to improve the quality of biological networks that are available for hypothesis generation and testing.
