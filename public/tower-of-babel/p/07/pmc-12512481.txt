Fire needle acupuncture for postherpetic neuralgia of the oral mucosa after initial misdiagnosis: a case report
Authors: Jingxing Li, Jiachun Xu, Zhaohua Jiao, Chuyi Zhang, Yan Li, Zhen Zhou

Background

Herpes zoster, commonly known as shingles, is a viral infection caused by the reactivation of the varicella-zoster virus. While it typically presents as a painful rash on the skin, its occurrence in the oral mucosa is indeed rare and can often be misdiagnosed as other oral conditions such as infections [1] or ulcers. This misdiagnosis can lead to delays in appropriate treatment, which is crucial for managing symptoms and preventing complications.

Moreover, the initial presentation without the characteristic vesicular rash can mimic odontogenic pain, trigeminal neuralgia, or simple aphthous ulcers, often leading patients to dentists or general practitioners who may not immediately consider herpes zoster, which emphasizes the need for a thorough differential diagnosis. In some cases, herpes zoster has been implicated in the pathogenesis of pulpal pathoses, further complicating its diagnosis and treatment [2]. This highlights the importance of considering herpes zoster in the differential diagnosis of oral pain, especially when typical treatments for common oral conditions do not yield the expected results.

In addition, herpes zoster in the oral cavity can lead to significant complications such as neuralgia, which is a persistent pain condition accompanying and even following the resolution of the rash, referred to as postherpetic neuralgia. This condition can be debilitating and requires effective management strategies, including the use of antiviral agents and pain management therapies [3], such as valacyclovir, gabapentin, or pregabalin. However, the substantial shortcomings of the conventional treatments, missed antiviral window, incomplete neuralgia prevention, inadequate and acute pain relief, slow onset of neuropathic agents, and common side effects (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache) underscore the critical need for alternative therapeutic strategies that offer improved efficacy, faster onset, and long-term neuralgia management.

Case presentation

The patient, a 40-year-old Chinese female, presented with a complaint of persistent pain at an ulcerative site on the left upper palate of the oral cavity, which developed 18 days after the resolution of blisters in the same region. She reported an episode of fever 18 days prior to presentation, followed by the emergence of painful blisters on the left upper palate postfever resolution, which she initially disregarded. At 15 days prior to presentation, her pain intensified, radiating to the left zygomatic and mandibular regions, accompanied by dysphagia. She sought treatment at an oral clinic, where a physical examination revealed extensive, confluent blisters on the left palatal aspect. Her C-reactive protein was 44.9 mg/L, with a white blood cell count of 6.43 × 109/L and a neutrophil percentage of 56.30%. She was diagnosed with an "oral infection." She underwent local anesthesia for oral mucosal irrigation and medication, which included vitamin B12 injection, lidocaine hydrochloride injection, dexamethasone sodium phosphate injection, and recombinant human interferon α2b spray. Despite this treatment, she experienced no significant relief, prompting her to visit our outpatient department. Current symptoms: an approximately 3 cm2 concave ulcer with a triangular shape and distinct margins was observed on the left upper palate (Fig. 1A). The pain was localized to the ulcer site, radiating to the left zygomatic and mandibular regions, and was exacerbated at night, impeding sleep. She reported hypogeusia on the left side of the tongue, reduced appetite, and difficulty in eating, with a visual analogue scale score of 7. In this case, the symptoms of fever, vesicles, radiating pain, taste loss, and the limited effects of the treatments made the previous diagnosis, oral infection, questionable. We thought that the neural damage was caused by herpes zoster rather than a simple oral infection, leading to secondary ulceration. Given the unilateral distribution along a likely dermatome (maxillary division of the trigeminal nerve), history of vesicles, and the nature of the burning, radiating pain, taste loss, a diagnosis of herpes zoster was made. Other differentials, such as major aphthous stomatitis, squamous cell carcinoma, or trigeminal neuralgia, were considered less likely owing to the preceding vesicular rash and the specific lesion characteristics. The pain began 18 days after the blisters, which might be early postherpetic neuralgia (PHN). Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with postherpetic neuralgia of oral mucosal herpes zoster, and fire needle acupuncture was applied. The patient was in a supine position, with her mouth open to fully expose the ulcerated area. A tongue depressor was used to gently retract the tongue, while a medium-fine fire needle (0.4 mm in diameter) was heated to red-hot over an alcohol lamp held close to the affected side. The needle was swiftly inserted into and withdrawn from the ulcer’s surface, puncturing in a circular pattern from the periphery towards the center. The needle depth was adjusted to induce bleeding, with punctures spaced approximately 0.3 cm apart. Postacupuncture, the ulcer surface displayed dark red blood, and the patient was instructed to suck until it cleared to a bright red. Treatments were scheduled twice weekly, with a 3–4-day interval between sessions. The patient was counseled on dietary regulation, emotional management, and to abstain from spicy and irritating foods.

After the first treatment at day 0, the patient revealed significant alleviation of radiating pain in the left zygomatic and mandibular regions, slight improvement in eating difficulty, and reduced sleep disturbance, with a VAS score of 5. After three treatments at day 7, the ulcer had shrunk (Fig. 1B), and the patient reported further pain relief, continued easing of eating difficulty, near-normal sleep, and a VAS score of 3. After four treatments at day 10, the ulcer had shrunk significantly (Fig. 1C), and the patient reported obvious pain relief, marked improvement in eating difficulty, normal sleep, and a VAS score of 1. After five treatments at day 15, the affected area was pain-free, with most of the ulcer surface healed. The patient’s eating difficulty had nearly resolved, with a VAS score of 0. At the 6th follow-up visit at day 17, the ulcer was nearly healed (Fig. 1D), prompting the cessation of fire needle treatment. At 1-month posttreatment, the patient’s ulcer was fully healed, and taste sensation on the affected side had normalized.

Discussion

Herpes zoster, a prevalent skin condition resulting from varicella-zoster virus infection, typically presents as unilateral clusters of papules and herpes along the peripheral nerves. The early-stage presentations of some patients can be atypical, and there is a lack of specific laboratory examination methods. Therefore, herpes zoster is very likely to be misdiagnosed [4], such as herpes zoster with delayed rash, herpes zoster without rash, abortive herpes zoster, herpes zoster in special parts (perioral [4], genital area [5]), and so on.

The incidence of herpes zoster of the oral mucosa is relatively low, and there are rare clinical reports [6–8]. It might be misdiagnosed as oral infection or oral ulcer. Oral herpes zoster is caused by the virus invading the nerve roots, resulting in lesions in the nerve innervation area and involving the oral mucosa, leading to acute skin and mucosal pain, blisters, and secondary ulcers. Nerve involvement results in pain and sensory disturbances, such as hypogeusia. Before the blisters appear, there may be symptoms such as fever, fatigue, and loss of appetite. Generally, common oral mucosal ulcers will not be accompanied by fever or taste disorders.

In this case, the patient exhibited fever, vesicles, radiating pain, and taste loss, suggesting nerve damage from herpes zoster rather than a simple oral infection, leading to secondary ulceration. Upon initial examination, a large, irregular ulcer was observed on the left upper palate, larger than typical oral ulcers, with symptoms persisting for over half a month. The localized, severe pain, worse at night, and sensory loss were consistent with herpes zoster characteristics, and it was diagnosed of oral mucosa herpes zoster.

Fire needle acupuncture is a traditional Chinese medical practice that involves the use of heated needles to stimulate specific acupoints. This ancient technique combines the principles of acupuncture with the warmth, aiming to correct imbalances in the flow of Qi (vital energy) and blood throughout the body [9]. The fire needle is heated until red-hot and then quickly inserted into the skin at designated points, which is believed to warm the meridians, dispel cold, and alleviate pain. It is used to treat a variety of conditions, including musculoskeletal disorders, neurological conditions, and certain dermatological diseases [10–13]. Research has also shown that fire acupuncture therapy has positive clinical effects in treating herpes zoster [14, 15].

Studies have shown that acupuncture demonstrated a normalization effect on default mode network to regulate descending pain processing [16]. Acupuncture could also exert antiinflammatory and analgesic effects by modulating the neuro–immune–endocrine interactions, thereby suppressing the induction and transmission of pain signals. This mechanism involves the interaction between peripheral afferent and central efferent neural circuits, particularly the neuroinflammatory interactions between spinal pain neurons and glial cells [17]. In addition, acupuncture can alleviate pain by reducing effective connectivity in neuropathic models through decreased cortico-marginal system feedback [18].

In this case, we used fire needle acupuncture for neuralgia of herpes zoster in oral mucosa, and the patient experienced pain relief and the lesion healed very quickly. As a traditional Chinese medical treatment method, fire needle acupuncture provides an effective alternative treatment option for patients with herpes zoster to alleviate pain symptoms by promoting blood circulation, improving the nutritional status of local tissues, and regulating the functions of the nervous system. We believe that the localized, controlled micro-trauma induced by fire needle acupuncture (heat and puncture) acts as a significant local stressor, which might trigger a complex neuro-immunomodulatory cascade, to stimulate the release of various neuropeptides, neurotransmitters, and immune modulators. The controlled micro-injury and subsequent inflammatory/repair response might also promote tissue repair and nerve regeneration in the affected area, potentially altering the pathological microenvironment. Furthermore, the distinct, intense sensation elicited by fire needles might constitute a potent diffuse noxious inhibitory control stimulus to inhibit pain mediated by brainstem centers.

Conclusion

Oral herpes zoster can be diagnostically challenging. This case report suggests that fire needle acupuncture might offer a potential benefit in alleviating its pain. However, as a single case study, it cannot prove causation or discount the natural history of herpes zoster (HZ). These findings are hypothesis-generating only. To robustly evaluate the efficacy and safety of fire needle acupuncture for oral HZ-associated pain, future rigorously designed studies, such as randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and appropriate control groups, are urgently needed.
